## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The ford at Hollow's mouth was the first thing travelers saw and the last thing it let them keep.

Coral had learned that early, watching from the reed-bank with her grandmother's patience: how the water slowed people, how the crossing made them look back at the road behind them instead of forward to where she waited. The river ran shallow there over pale stones, cold as a held breath, and everything a traveler carried came loose in the crossing — not their purses, which they gripped tighter, but their thoughts, which drifted like silt. The valley drank them in. The valley always drank first; Coral only skimmed what floated to the surface.

She crouched now among the reeds with her skirts darkened by dew and watched a mule train pick its way down the far bank. Three riders, four pack animals, salt smell off their goods — traders out of the low country, tired enough to be generous with their minds. She could feel them already, the way you felt weather coming: a pressure behind her eyes, faint flavors of thought. One of them was rehearsing an argument he'd lost years ago. Another was counting. They were always counting.

Market day. Maghda's scales would be polished, her ledger open, her prices insultingly fair because Maghda believed fairness was another way of owning you. Coral had until noon to fill her hands.

She closed her eyes and reached across the water.

It was not like taking. That was the lie she told herself, and she told it so often it had gone smooth as a river stone. It was more like lifting steam from a kettle — the surface of a man's morning rose into her, warm and thin, and she skimmed the top layer: the argument, the counting, a memory of bread baked wrong, a woman's voice saying *come home before frost*. Small things. Things that would not be missed, or would be missed so briefly the missing itself would fade before the travelers reached the far side of the pass.

That was the rule, her rule, older than any law in the Hollow: *never keep what you take.* Take and sell, take and sell, and hold nothing overnight. A thief who kept her loot grew heavy. A memory-keeper grew something else entirely — her grandmother had shown her what, once, though Coral had never decided whether that story was warning or boast.

The mule train splashed through the ford. The man rehearsing his old argument frowned suddenly, like a man reaching for a word and finding the shelf bare. He touched his temple. He looked around at the reeds, at the gray-green hills, at nothing.

Coral pressed herself lower and did not feel guilty. Guilt was a luxury the Hollow did not stock. There were two hundred mouths in this valley and not one field that gave more than it grudgingly yielded, no forge that burned hotter than a cookfire, no child born with a trick of light in its fingers. Magic stopped at the ridgeline like weather against glass. What the world outside called power, the Hollow paid for in the only coin it had: other people's yesterdays. The brokers fed the town on them — memories ground into tincture and thread and candle-smoke, sold back to farmers whose own pasts had worn too thin to dream on.

It was honest work. Coral repeated that too, sometimes, in the dark, in the rhythm of a prayer whose god had never answered.

*Honest work. Never keep what you take.*

The last rider crossed, and the mule train climbed away toward the market road, and the ford went quiet except for water talking to itself over stone. Coral stood to shake the dew from her hem —

— and smelled lilac.

She froze. Lilac did not grow in the Hollow. Nothing sweet grew here without being carried in, wilted, and buried. The air of the valley tasted of flint and wet clay and, in autumn, of rot-sweet apples; it did not taste of flowers. And under the lilac, unmistakable to anyone who'd spent her life around blades and the men who bled from them: iron.

Blood. Fresh blood, smelling of iron and something blooming and impossible, drifting up the streambed like smoke.

Coral turned toward the sound before she decided to turn, which was itself strange — her body moving ahead of her caution the way a dog moves before thunder arrives. Downstream, where the ford shallowed to a trickle between two leaning alders, something dark lay half in the water.

A man.

He had crawled, she saw at once. The track he'd left through the mud told the whole story in a language she read easily: he'd come down the north path — the hard path, the one traders avoided because it climbed — on foot, staggered along the bank for thirty yards or more, and fallen twice. The second fall had been the true one. He lay on his side now with his cheek nearly in the current, and the water running past him came away pink, then paler pink, then clear, as if the river were politely drinking him.

"Hey." Coral waded in. The cold bit through her boots. "Hey — don't drink the water sideways, you'll —"

Stupid thing to say. His eyes opened anyway.

They were gray, the gray of ash after a fire that has taken everything worth burning, and they found her face with terrible precision, as if he'd been looking for exactly her among all possible faces and had finally, at the end of the world, found her. He smiled. It cracked blood from his lips.

"You're," he said, "smaller than I imagined."

"I imagine most people imagine me taller." Her voice came out flat and professional, and she hated it and kept it anyway. Flat was safe. Flat was the tone of a woman doing a job. She knelt in the shallows beside him and put her hands on him because there was nothing else to do with hands, and found the wound beneath his left ribs — a puncture, deep, deliberate, angled up. Not a bandit's slash. A duelist's courtesy. Someone had wanted him to take his time dying.

His coat was good wool, travel-worn but good, and stitched at the collar with a sigil she didn't know: three interlocked circles. His blood soaked through her fingers, warm, and the smell of it filled her head — iron, yes, but underneath, rising with every heartbeat, that impossible sweetness. Lilac. As if spring itself had been wounded.

"You're not from here," she said. Idiot. Obviously. "There's a healer in the settlement, half a mile — I can run —"

"No." His hand shot out and caught her wrist with a strength that astonished her, that should not have been left in him, and his ash-gray eyes went wide and fierce. "No healers. Listen. *Listen.* There is no time. There has never been any time, we have all of us been spending it like drunkards —"

"You're feverish."

"*Thirteenth.*"

The word dropped between them like a coin down a well. Coral heard it hit bottom and echo.

"What?"

But he wasn't answering her anymore; he was rummaging, dragging himself half out of the water to get at the strap across his chest, cursing in a language that made the alders shiver — actually shiver, she saw the leaves tremble with no wind, and a cold finger traced her spine. He tore open the flap of a leather map case, long as a forearm, scarred and oiled and older-looking than any single object had a right to be, as if things had happened to it that left marks on time itself.

He pressed it into her hands.

Into *her* hands. He looked past her shoulder first — quick, practiced, the glance of a man who had been hunted long enough to check sightlines while drowning — and then he pushed the case against her chest with both palms, as if sealing a letter into an envelope of bone and breath.

"It goes to the thirteenth," he said. "Only her. Sworn and kept. If another opens it—" He coughed, and the cough was bad, wet and deep, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quieter, almost gentle, the way men go gentle when they are speaking from somewhere already far away. "Girl. What's your name?"

"Coral."

Something moved through his face — relief, grief, terror, all wearing the same mask. "Of course," he whispered. "Of course it is. They always know, don't they, the ones who send us. Thirteenth keeper. *You.*"

"I'm not a keeper of anything. I steal memories for a living. It's not as interesting as it sounds."

He laughed. It cost him terribly; she watched the laugh take payment. Then his hand found hers where it rested on the case, and folded her fingers shut over the leather, and held them there.

"Never keep what you take," he said. "That's your rule. I can smell it on you — a clean thief. Good. Because this—" his grip tightened, "—this is the one thing, girl, the *one thing*, you must break your rule for. Keep it. Keep it hidden. Keep it *kept*. Do you understand? Everything depends on a thief who finally keeps."

"I don't understand anything," Coral said, and her voice shook, and she hated that more than the blood, more than the lilac. "Who stabbed you? Who do I give this to? What is a keeper? Why does your blood smell like — like flowers, that isn't — people don't —"

"The fae," he said simply, "bleed like this. So do those they mark." He smiled again, smaller. "I was marked a long time ago. I ran longer than I should have. We always do." His eyes drifted past her, up the north path, into the gray distance where the pass climbed out of the valley, and whatever he saw there drained the smile away. "They'll follow. Something follows the keepers now. It found twelve of us."

"Twelve," Coral repeated. The number sat strangely in her mouth, familiar as a word from a dream.

"Eleven dead. The twelfth—" his breath hitched, and he pressed the case harder into her hands, hard enough to hurt, "—the twelfth was me, and I am done, and now there is only you. Thirteenth. Last. *Last*, do you hear me — whatever comes, whatever offers, whatever bargains knock on your door wearing beautiful faces — the stone must not be *claimed*. Only returned. Or ended. Promise me."

"I promise you a healer," Coral said desperately. "Hold on. Hold on, I can carry you, I've carried sheep heavier than —"

He took her chin in one trembling hand and made her look at him, and for a moment — just a moment — she felt him *reach* into her, the way she reached into others, a touch like cold silk across the surface of her mind. Not stealing. Searching. Finding.

"There you are," he murmured, wonderstruck. "Under all that locked doors. There you are."

And then the strength went out of him all at once, like a tide withdrawing, and he sagged back into the shallows, and the river took over the work of holding him.

Coral knelt in the freezing water and held the leather case against her heart and watched a stranger die at the ford where she had stolen ten thousand small yesterdays, and discovered that she knew nothing at all about how to be present for an ending. She said his sigil aloud, uselessly. She said *hold on*, uselessly. When his breathing went long and rattling, she gripped his hand, and when it stopped she kept gripping it, because letting go seemed like a kind of theft and her rule had just been shattered against the rocks and she did not yet know what rules remained.

The water ran clear past them both. Somewhere upstream, a bird sang, indifferent and lovely.

At last she laid him straight on the bank, folded his hands on his chest, and closed his ash-gray eyes with two fingers, the way her grandmother had taught her — *always close their eyes, Coral, the dead see too much otherwise* — though her grandmother had never said what the dead saw, and Coral had never asked, because asking questions in the Hollow was how you learned the answers were all for sale.

Then she sat back on her heels in the mud, dripping, shaking, and looked at the map case in her lap.

Plain leather. Old scars. A brass clasp shaped like a keyhole with no key. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. Her fingers wanted to open it the way a tongue wants to find a broken tooth, and she caught herself with her thumb already on the clasp and snatched her hand away as if the leather had bitten her.

*If another opens it—*

He hadn't finished the sentence. Of course he hadn't. Dying men economized.

The sun climbed. The ford glittered. Downstream, the settlement's smoke began to rise, thin gray threads against the green — Maghda's fire among them, Maghda's scales waiting, Maghda's ledger open to today's page. Coral had a morning's takings in her mind's pockets: an argument, some arithmetic, badly baked bread, a woman's voice saying *come home before frost*. Ordinary cargo. She could walk into town, sell it, buy flour and lamp-oil, and be home by dark with nothing kept, nothing owned, nothing changed.

The case sat in her lap like a second heartbeat.

And beneath the ordinary cargo, beneath the hollowness she wore like her own skin, Coral felt something she had never once felt in twenty-two years of taking and selling and holding nothing:

Possession. Dreadful, intoxicating possession. This was hers — given, not stolen; entrusted, not taken. For the first time in her life, something belonged to her, and it was bleeding-edged and dangerous and it smelled, faintly, when she bent close, of iron and lilac and a country she had never seen.

She got up. She hid the case inside her coat, against her ribs, over her own heartbeat.

Then she looked downstream toward home, at the smoke of the only valley magic could not enter, and understood — with the slow, cold certainty of ice forming — that she had just become the most dangerous thing in it.

Behind her, up the north path, something began to sing.
