## 4. The Inquisitor's Kindness

Wren Vael had learned, in nineteen years of service to the Crown, that fear was a blunt instrument and kindness a scalpel. He carried both into the Hollow on the morning the mist refused to lift.

He came alone, as courtesy demanded — one gray horse, one gray cloak, no soldiers at his back. The valley road wound down between hills the color of old pewter, and where the first fence post rose out of the fog, he stopped and dismounted, because a man who arrives on foot asks nothing of anyone. He walked the last mile leading his horse by the bridle, boots quiet on packed earth, letting the Hollow look at him for as long as it liked.

It looked. He felt it the way one feels weather: doors easing shut behind shutters, conversations dying mid-word, a child snatched off a doorstep by an arm that appeared from nowhere. The Hollow was a town built of held breath. Vael found it restful, in its way. Most places he went, people performed their innocence. Here they simply went silent, which was a more honest thing.

The settlement itself sat low in the valley like a stone in the palm of a hand. No temple spire, no garrison tower, no bell. Just crooked lanes, slate roofs furred with moss, and smoke rising thin and straight from chimneys because there was no wind down here — there never was. That was the first strangeness he noted, and he noted it without surprise. The Crown's maps marked the Hollow with a small annotation in faded clerk's ink: *null ground. Magic does not follow.* His superiors believed that made the place safe.

Vael believed it made the place *useful* — to someone who knew what could be hidden in ground where nothing divine or arcane could reach in and dig it out.

He tied his horse at the market square's edge, beside a trough where the water lay perfectly still, mirror-flat, reflecting a sky the valley never saw. Then he took off his gloves, folded them into his belt, and smiled at the first person who would meet his eyes.

She was a baker's wife, broad and flour-dusted, standing in her doorway with the particular stillness of prey deciding whether to bolt.

"Good morning," Vael said, and meant it. "I wonder if you might point me toward someone who sells cider. I've ridden since dawn, and I find I'm thirstier than I am official."

The word *official* did what it always did. Her chin lifted a fraction; her eyes went flat. "There's the Drowned Bell," she said. "Down the lane. We don't get much call for—"

"Cider is fine," he said warmly. "Thank you. Your loaves smell wonderful, by the way. Caraway?"

"Fennel." And then, despite herself, despite everything: "It keeps better."

"It does," Vael agreed gravely, as though this were wisdom worth carrying back to the capital, and touched two fingers to his brow in something adjacent to a salute, and walked on.

Behind him he heard her exhale.

The Drowned Bell was doing its best to be empty when he entered, and failing — a tavern at midmorning cannot fully evacuate without looking like a theater after a bad review. A dozen patrons remained, arranged with studied carelessness around their cups. The innkeeper, a heavy man with forearms like hams and the eyes of someone counting exits, watched Vael cross the room.

"Whatever you're selling," the innkeeper said, "we don't want it."

"I'm not selling." Vael sat at the bar, unhurried, and laid a silver crown on the wood. It rang softly against the grain. "I'm buying. Cider, if you have it. And a little of your time, if you'll sell that too."

"We don't sell time here."

"Everyone sells time here." Vael kept his voice gentle, almost apologetic. "That's rather what this valley is, isn't it? Time, sold by the memory. I've read the trade ledgers. Fascinating bookkeeping. You'd be astonished how far a jar of pickled recollections travels before someone drinks it."

The silence that followed had texture. Vael drank his cider slowly — it was poor, sharp with tannin, honest — and let the room decide what he was. He wore no badge. He carried no visible blade, though the cloak hung in a way that suggested weight beneath. He looked, he hoped, like what he pretended to be: a tired man of middle years, gray at the temples, kind around the eyes, the sort who asked after your children and remembered the answers.

The performance was not false, exactly. That was the trick of it. Wren Vael *was* kind. He simply refused to let the kindness interfere with the work.

"A courier passed through here," he said at last, conversationally, to the bar top. "Nine days ago, perhaps ten. Tall man, lean, rode hard. Brown hair going early gray. He would have been bleeding." He glanced up, mild as milk. "I mention the blood because it makes him memorable. Men forget faces. They rarely forget blood."

No one spoke. Somewhere near the hearth, a spoon stopped clinking against a bowl.

"He was carrying letters of some importance," Vael continued. "His name was Aldous Merrin. He had a wife in Corvale and a habit of humming when he wrote. I say 'had.'" He let that sit. "I'm told he never reached the next relay. So either he turned back — unlikely; Aldous was constitutionally incapable of turning back — or he stopped here. And either he died here, or someone helped him onward. Helped him *hide*."

The innkeeper's knuckles whitened on the tap handle. "We're traders, sir. Memories, like you said. We don't meddle in crowns and couriers."

"No," Vael agreed. "You don't. Which is why whoever helped him wasn't thinking like a trader." He turned on his stool, slow and easy, and let his gaze travel the room — not searching, exactly. *Greeting*. Each face received a half-second of genuine, warm attention, and each face flinched from it, and each flinch told him something. The miller by the window was afraid but ignorant. The two drovers by the door were afraid and hiding knowledge of something else entirely — smuggling, most likely, cheap brandy, irrelevant. The girl sweeping near the hearth—

The girl sweeping near the hearth was not afraid at all.

That interested him. She was young, maybe nineteen, with ink-dark hair escaping its braid and hands that moved the broom in perfect mechanical rhythm while her eyes stayed fixed on the floorboards. Everyone else in the room was performing innocence badly. She wasn't performing anything. She was simply *elsewhere*, wearing her body like a coat left behind on a hook.

Vael filed her away and said, pleasantly, to the room at large: "I should tell you why I ask so gently. Because I know how this looks. A crown man comes asking questions, and everyone remembers every story about crown men, and fair enough — most of them are true." He smiled, rueful. "But here is my situation. A man is dead. Possibly murdered. And the person who helped him — if someone helped him — may be in danger themselves. From the same quarter that killed Aldous Merrin. I am not hunting them. I am trying to reach them before whatever hunted Aldous does."

It was a beautiful lie. It was even, in its way, partially true — Vael genuinely did not wish the helper dead, because a living helper could answer questions, and a dead one could only leave clues. But he watched the lie land the way lies land in small rooms: softening shoulders, loosening jaws, the slow poison of *maybe he means it* working through the crowd like warmth through cold hands.

Kindness, Vael thought, sipping his cider. Cheaper than torture and twice as effective.

"The Hollow keeps to itself," the innkeeper said finally. "Always has. Whatever roads run out of here, we don't walk them."

"Of course." Vael stood, dropped a second coin beside the first, and bowed slightly. "Thank you for the cider. If you think of anything — a face, a name, a horse that came back riderless — I'll be at the ford cottage for a few days. The Crown has rented it." He paused at the door. "Oh — one more thing. Does anyone here keep bees?"

The question landed wrong-footed, as he'd intended. Frowns, shrugs, a shake of the head. "No bees in the Hollow," the innkeeper said. "Nothing much grows right. Why?"

"No reason," Vael said, and meant to leave it there, and then — because the girl with the broom had gone very still, stiller than still — he added, lightly, almost to himself: "Only that Aldous smelled of lilac when they pulled what was left of him out of the river. Lilac and iron. Strange perfume for a courier." He smiled at the room. "Good day to you all."

He heard the broom stop entirely as the door swung shut behind him.

* * *

From the shadowed mouth of the cooper's alley across the square, Coral Dunmore watched the inquisitor the way one watches a snake sunning itself on a path one must cross.

She had not planned to follow him. She had planned, in the frantic hour after dawn when the news ran through the Hollow faster than the mist could smother it — *crown man, alone, asking questions* — to do precisely nothing. Stay home. Keep her hands busy. Be nobody. She had survived seventeen years in the Hollow by being nobody with great discipline.

But then she'd seen him dismount at the valley's edge and walk, and something about the walking had snagged in her like a fishhook. Nobody walked like that. Soldiers strode. Priests glided. Beggars shuffled. This man *walked* the way her stolen memories said kings walked through plague towns — slowly enough to be seen, gently enough to be pitied, and with the absolute certainty of someone who has never once doubted that the ground belongs to him.

So she had followed, telling herself it was reconnaissance, telling herself she needed to know what he knew, and had arrived at the Drowned Bell in time to hear him describe Aldous Merrin's death with the tenderness of a man reading a bedtime story.

*Lilac and iron.*

Coral's stomach turned over slowly, like a boat coming loose from its mooring. She had smelled that scent on her own hands for nine days now, soaked into the leather of the map case, into the creases of her palms, no matter how she scrubbed. The courier's blood had smelled of lilac. She had told herself it was a fever-thing, a dying-thing, some oddity of the wound.

Now a crown inquisitor stood in the morning fog and named that scent aloud, casually, like a man identifying a wine.

*They pulled what was left of him out of the river.* Not died at the ford, then. Or not only. Someone had moved him. Someone had finished him. And the crown knew things about it that the Hollow did not.

Coral pressed herself deeper into the alley's shadow and made herself watch, because Maghda had taught her once that terror is useless unless you spend it on observation. *Fear is coin, girl. Don't clutch it. Spend it.*

The inquisitor emerged from the tavern and stood a moment in the square, head tilted, listening to the town hold its breath. He was not what she'd expected. She had expected cruelty dressed in black, all edges and threat. This man was gray and gentle-looking, with laugh lines he had apparently earned honestly, and he touched his brow to a passing grandmother as though she were his own kin. He crouched to speak to a child chasing a hoop, and the child — children being the last honest creatures alive — did not run from him. He laughed at something the child said. The laugh reached his eyes.

And Coral felt the true shape of her fear arrive, cold and complete. It was not that he might threaten the Hollow. It was that he *liked* people. You could threaten a cruel man into silence. But a kind man, a patient man, a man who genuinely loved the sound of a child laughing — that man would sit in your town for a week, buy your cider, learn your fennel from your caraway, and peel the truth out of you layer by layer while you thanked him for the company.

He began to walk the square. Not searching — *strolling*. He admired the well. He commented on the masonry to a stonemason, who found himself answering before he could decide not to. He bought an apple from a fruit cart and ate it walking, and the vendor, a woman who hadn't sold an apple to a stranger in years, watched him go with an expression of dazed pleasure.

By the third circuit, Coral understood what she was seeing, and it chilled her worse than any interrogation chamber could have. He was mapping them. Not the streets — the *people*. Who answered quickly. Who looked away. Whose doors had opened and whose had stayed shut. Every small human transaction was a thread, and he was gathering them all with both hands, weaving something invisible that by nightfall would be a net.

He stopped at the broker's row.

Coral's breath went thin. Maghda's shop sat among the six memory-brokers' establishments like a fat spider among thinner ones, jars gleaming amber in the window, each one holding a stranger's wedding day, a soldier's homecoming, a mother's last afternoon. The inquisitor stood before the window a long moment, reading the labels. Then he went inside.

Coral crossed the square through the back ways, heart hammering, and took up a position in the disused smokehouse behind Maghda's, where a gap in the boards gave her a sliver of view into the shop's front room. Through it she watched Maghda — vast, ringed, magnificent — receive the crown's man with the expression of a hostess greeting a rival.

"Madam," Vael was saying, removing his hat. "I'm told you're the finest judge of memories in three valleys."

"I'm told a lot of things." Maghda's voice was a landslide wrapped in velvet. "Most of them flattery. Yours sounds like an opening move."

"It is." He laughed — genuinely, Coral thought, or else he was better than anyone alive. "Then I'll skip to the end. I'm looking for a courier who passed through nine days ago. Wounded. He may have sold something here. A memory, perhaps. Dying men sometimes prefer to leave the pain behind."

"And if he did, it's stock. Bought and paid for. What a man sells, he's sold."

"Entirely true, and entirely beside my point." Vael leaned forward, elbows on the counter, and his voice dropped into something confiding, almost intimate. "Madam, I'm going to be honest with you, because I suspect you can smell dishonesty the way I can smell lilac. The courier was carrying something the Crown wants very much. Something that was taken from him. If it passed through this valley — if anyone here is holding it, knowingly or not — then that person is in extraordinary danger. Not from me." He spread his hands. "From the parties who killed him. I would very much like to find them first. For their sake."

Silence. Coral could see Maghda's profile, and on it played a war of calculations too fast to count. Coral knew that face. Maghda weighed everything in coin, and somewhere behind those small bright eyes, the price of a crown inquisitor's gratitude was being set against the price of whatever Coral was hiding.

"You've asked the whole town, I hear," Maghda said at last. "Doors opening, doors shutting. And yet you came to mine last."

"I saved the best for last."

"Mm. Or you already know the answer, and you wanted to watch my face when you asked." Maghda's rings clicked against the counter. "Here's what I know, inquisitor, free of charge, because I value my neck over my curiosity. A courier came through the ford nine days past, bleeding. By morning he was dead. I bought nothing from him. Whether anyone else did—" a vast shoulder rolled, "—is not something brokers discuss about other brokers. Guild law."

"Guild law," Vael repeated softly. "Yes. I've read about your guilds. Remarkable institutions. All the structure of a government and none of the accountability." He replaced his hat. "Thank you, madam. You've been more helpful than you know."

"How, exactly?"

"By lying to me," Vael said pleasantly, "so competently. It tells me exactly how frightened you are. And you don't frighten easily, which means whatever you're protecting isn't yours." He bowed. "Good day."

Coral was moving before he reached the door, ghosting out of the smokehouse, through the wet weeds behind the tannery, home. Her hands shook as she barred the door, and she stood in the dim room with the map case under her floorboards and made herself breathe.

*He knows.* Not the name — not yet. But he knew the shape of it. A helper. A broker who lied well. A town with one secret big enough to frighten Maghda. He would sit at the ford cottage and be kind, and the Hollow — soft, lonely, starved for courtesy — would unravel for him thread by thread, and every thread led eventually to a girl with dark hair who had lied for the first time in her career.

Coral sank onto her stool and stared at the floorboards over the map, and the fear in her chest did something strange: it cooled, and hardened, and set like poured wax taking the shape of a decision.

He would not leave empty-handed. She understood that now with total clarity. Men like him never did. The only question — the only choice left in the world — was whether what he carried away was her, or just the ashes of everything she'd burned behind her.

Outside, faintly, she could hear him greeting the neighbor's child by name.

He'd been in the Hollow four hours.
