## 5. Thirteen Turns

The hedge-witch lived at the crooked end of Tanner's Row, where the Hollow's houses leaned together like conspirators and the smell of lye and old fat hung in the air even in the cold months. Her name was Sef, though most people just called her the Row Witch, and Coral had known her all her life in the way you know a landmark — always there, never quite looked at.

Sef was ancient in the way of trees: bent, weathered, impossible to guess at. Her hands were spotted like thrush eggs and her eyes were the pale grey of river fog, and when Coral knocked on her door at dusk with the map case hidden under her cloak, those fog-grey eyes went straight to the shape of it before they ever found her face.

"You've brought something into my house that doesn't belong in this valley," Sef said. Not a question.

"I need you to read something."

"Did I say I would?"

"No. But everyone says you're the only one who can."

The old woman snorted and stood aside from the door. "Everyone says that about me because everyone else is dead or stupid. Come in, then. Wipe your feet. The floor's older than your grandmother and twice as touchy."

* * *

Inside, the cottage smelled of dried yarrow and woodsmoke and something underneath both — a faint green scent, like crushed stems, that Coral had never noticed in any other house in the Hollow. The walls were papered with pressed flowers gone brown as autumn, each one labeled in a spidery hand. A kettle muttered on the stove. A cat with one ear watched Coral from a shelf with the flat disapproval of its kind.

Coral set the map case on the table between them and did not open it.

"It won't burn," she said. "Or tear. And the ink moves when no one's watching."

Sef looked at her for a long moment. "You tried to burn it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because she had sat up half the night with it propped against the stove, watching flames lick across leather that refused to char, feeling the first true fear of her adult life. Because Maghda had offered her a fortune — an actual fortune, enough coin to buy the whole of Tanner's Row — and Coral had heard herself lie, smooth as water, saying she'd never seen such a case. Because possession had turned out to feel exactly like being possessed.

"Because I wanted it to be nothing," she said.

"Hm." Sef lowered herself into a chair with the care of someone whose joints were a private negotiation. "Open it, then. Slowly. Some things mind being startled."

The map came out of the case the way it always did now — too smoothly, the leather warm as skin. Coral unrolled it across the table and weighted the corners with Sef's ink pots, and there it lay: the valley rendered in fine lines, the ford, the scree pass, the roads beyond, all of it drawn in ink that seemed to breathe if you didn't look directly at it.

And along the margins, in handwriting too small to read by firelight alone, ran lines of script that Coral had taken for decoration until tonight.

Sef bent over it. She did not touch it. Her nose nearly brushed the parchment and her breath came slow and deliberate, the way Coral breathed when she was working a mark — careful, controlled, keeping herself out of the thing she handled.

"Well," Sef said at last.

"Well what?"

"This is keeper's hand." The witch's finger hovered above the marginalia without landing. "Old style. You see how the letters lean? That's not a scribe's slant, girl, that's a runner's slant. Written fast, written moving, written by someone who expected to be interrupted." She straightened up with a wince. "Where did you get this?"

"A man died at the ford."

"And gave it to you."

"He put it in my hands. He said—" Coral stopped. She had not said it aloud since that morning. "He said *thirteenth*. Just that. Then he was gone."

Sef sat back. For a while the only sound was the kettle muttering and the cat rearranging itself on its shelf with the sound of a small avalanche.

"Read it to me," Coral said. "I can't make out the script."

"I can," Sef said. "That's rather the problem."

"Then read it anyway."

The old woman looked at her — that long, weighing look again, the one that made Coral feel like a fish being assessed for market — and then she fetched a horn-rimmed lens from a drawer and held it over the margin, angling the lamp so the light fell clean.

"*To the thirteenth,*" she read. "*If you hold this, the twelfth has fallen, and you are already hunted. Do not go home. Do not go to ground where the ground is dead. They will find you fastest where magic cannot follow, because that is where we hide.*"

The words landed in the quiet room like stones dropped down a well. Coral heard them once, then again, echoing.

"That's nonsense," she said. "There's no hiding here. Nothing happens here. Nothing *can* happen here."

"Keep reading," Sef said.

"*The stone remembers what was sworn upon it, and what was broken. Twelve have carried the way to it. Eleven are graves. The hunter wears courtesy like a coat and does not tire, does not sleep, does not stop. Trust no kindness that arrives with questions.*"

Coral thought of gentle eyes and immaculate gloves and a voice like warm oil. Her mouth went dry.

"The moon completes its thirteenth turn at the turning of the season," Sef continued, tracing a line of figures coiled in the corner of the map like a nest of sleeping snakes. "See these marks? One for every full moon between the writing and—" She stopped.

"Between the writing and what?"

"Between the writing and the end of everything, depending on who reaches the stone first." Sef set down the lens. She looked tired suddenly, and very old, and very sorry. "Twelve keepers, girl. This man at your ford was the twelfth. He was killed by something that hunts his line, one by one, down through the years. And the count runs out in one season. One turn of the moon. Thirteen."

"The thirteenth what?" Coral asked, though some cold, clever part of her had already begun assembling the answer, the way she assembled a stranger's face from fragments of memory — reluctantly, precisely, unable to stop.

Sef met her eyes.

"The thirteenth keeper," she said. "You."

* * *

The cat had gone to sleep. The kettle had gone quiet. Coral stood at the window looking out at Tanner's Row in the dark, at the leaning houses and the banked fires and the distant glow of the market square, and felt the ground shift beneath a lifetime.

"There's no such thing as keepers," she said finally. "There's no such thing as any of it. Magic doesn't work here. Everyone knows that. It's the one true thing about the Hollow."

"Mm," said Sef, behind her.

"People come here to be safe from it. That's why it was founded. That's what my mother—"

She stopped.

Her mother, who had died when Coral was nine, who had held her hand in the fever and said strange things near the end, things the neighbors had blamed on delirium. *Stay inside the lines, little fish. The lines are drawn around you, not for you.* Coral had buried the words with the rest of that year, folded them away in the place where she kept nothing, kept everything at arm's length.

"The soil here is dead," Coral said slowly. "Magic-dead. Every child learns it. Nothing grows enchanted, no charm holds, no curse lands. It's protection."

"Is it," Sef said. It wasn't a question either.

Coral turned. "What aren't you saying, old woman?"

"What I'm saying," Sef said, "is that I've lived in this valley seventy years, and I'll tell you what I know about its famous dead soil. Charms don't work here, true enough. Blessings slide off. Curses fail. But you know what else doesn't work here?" She tapped her own breastbone with one spotted finger. "Gifts. I was born knowing which plants would take and which would die, born knowing where to cut and where to leave be. In the lowlands they'd have called me a greenwise woman and burned me or fed me, one or the other. Here I'm a hedge-witch, which is to say, an herb-woman with a reputation. My gift didn't die at the boundary, girl. It went to sleep. There's a difference, and the difference is a cage."

Coral's heart was beating strangely. "That's one story."

"It is. Here's another." Sef rose, crossed to the shelf, and took down a clay jar sealed with wax. From it she shook a handful of dark earth onto the table — ordinary Hollow dirt, the kind Coral had dug in all her life. "Soil from my garden. Now watch."

She pricked her thumb on a needle, let one drop of blood fall onto the earth, and murmured three words in a language Coral didn't know but recognized the shape of, the way you recognize a song heard through a wall.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. The blood sat on the soil like blood on soil.

"Now," Sef said, and swept the earth into her palm, carried it to the door, and stepped out into her dooryard — one stride, no more, past her threshold stone. She crouched, set the earth down, spoke the same three words.

Even from inside, Coral saw it happen. The drop of blood sank. The soil *moved*, a slow heave like a sleeper turning over, and something green and improbable unfurled from it in the space of ten heartbeats — a shoot, a leaf, a bud that opened white in the lamplight and closed again, all in the space of a breath.

Sef came back in and shut the door. She was breathing hard, and not from the crouching.

"One stride," she said. "One stride past the line and the land wakes up. So tell me, thief-girl. If the valley kills magic at its border, why does it start again one step outside? Why is the deadness shaped like us? Like a ring drawn around the Hollow and nowhere else?"

Coral couldn't answer. Her mouth wouldn't shape anything.

"It isn't protection," Sef said gently. "It never was. It's containment. Somebody built this valley the way you'd build a pen — walls of dead ground, high enough to keep certain things in. Or certain people." She looked at Coral with those fog-grey eyes, and for the first time since childhood Coral saw that behind the fog there was something fierce and burning. "People like me. People whose gifts sleep instead of dying. And people like you, whatever you are, that a dying man bled out to reach."

"My mother," Coral said. The words came out cracked. "She told me to stay inside the lines. Near the end. Everyone said she was raving."

"Was she?"

"She said the lines were drawn around me. Not for me. Around me." Coral wrapped her arms around herself, and was horrified to find her hands shaking. "I grew up thinking I was safe. I grew up thinking the whole valley was safe, that we'd found the one hole in the world where nothing could touch us. And it was a cage. It was a *pen*, and I was born in it, and I stole memories at the gate like a good little animal, and I never once asked why the grass outside the fence was greener because I was told the fence was mercy—"

"Girl."

"—and my mother knew. She *knew*, and she died telling me in riddles because even dying she was afraid to say it plain, because saying it plain might bring the thing that killed eleven keepers down on—"

"*Girl.*" Sef's hand closed on her wrist, surprisingly strong, dry and warm as bark. "Breathe."

Coral breathed. The cottage swam back into focus: the brown flowers on the walls, the sleeping cat, the map lying on the table with its patient, crawling ink.

"How many others?" she asked quietly. "How many people in the Hollow are sleeping?"

"I don't know. A few in every generation, I'd guess. Enough." Sef released her wrist. "Enough that whoever built this pen wanted a whole herd, not one beast. Which raises a question I'd advise you not to ask me, because I don't have the answer, and I've spent seventy years not asking it."

"Who built it?"

"There it is. The question." Sex sighed and lowered herself back into her chair. "Ask the map. Ask the stone it leads to. Ask the man who wrote the margins, if the dead take questions." She nodded at the parchment. "But ask it soon, girl. The count is running. Thirteen turns, and the last one rises at the turning of the season. Whatever comes to a head then, you'll want to be standing somewhere you chose, not somewhere you were penned."

* * *

Coral walked home through streets gone blue with moonrise, and the Hollow looked different to her, and that was the worst of it.

It should have looked the same. It was the same — same leaning houses, same banked fires, same smell of woodsmoke and tallow and the river's iron breath. But she walked it now the way you walk through a house after learning the walls are painted over a door. Every familiar thing had a second shape underneath.

The boundary stones she'd passed ten thousand times: not markers. Bars.

The magic-dead soil in every garden: not poor earth. Poured earth. Laid down deliberately, thickly, the way you'd lay mortar.

Maghda's counting-house with its warm yellow windows: a feeding trough set inside the pen, so the animals could be kept busy and paid and placated. And her own little room above the chandler's, rented with stolen years, furnished with stolen comforts — a life assembled entirely from other people's pasts because she had none of her own worth keeping.

Because she had given hers away. Or had it taken. At seven years old, some voice whispered that she almost remembered. She pushed the whisper down. It had no purchase here. Nothing had purchase here; that was the whole point of the place.

She stopped at the foot of the chandler's stair and looked back the way she'd come, down the long crooked throat of the Hollow to the ford, silver-black in the moonlight, where a dying man had chosen her out of everyone alive.

*Thirteenth.*

Grief came for her then, and she let it, standing in the street with her arms wrapped around herself, because there was no one to see and no one to steal it from her. It wasn't grief for the lie itself. Lies she understood; lies were currency here, and she'd traded in them all her life. It was grief for the child who had believed — who had lain in bed listening to the wind off the scree pass and felt *safe*, truly safe, one of the few feelings she'd ever owned outright — and who had been wrong in a way that could never be repaired, because you cannot return a childhood to the market and demand your coin back.

They had built a cage and called it shelter. They had penned her mother and Sef and who knew how many others, and called the pen peace. And she had grown up grateful for it. Grateful. That was the wound that would not close: not the bars, but the gratitude.

When the grief had wrung itself mostly out, Coral wiped her face with her sleeve, climbed the stairs, and lit her lamp.

The map lay where she'd left it, rolled tight in its case. She unrolled it on the table and looked at it for a long time — at the valley drawn small and precise, the ring of it, the roads running out beyond the boundary like threads pulled loose from a hem.

Then she looked at the marginalia, at the runner's-slant hand of a dead man, and read the line Sef had translated last, the one the old woman had repeated twice, as if making sure Coral heard it over the roaring of her own blood:

*Do not go to ground where the ground is dead.*

The ink shifted. She was almost sure it shifted — the road beyond the pass seeming to lengthen, the far edge of the parchment seeming to darken, as if the map were drawing breath.

Outside, the moon cleared the eastern ridge, round and white and indifferent, beginning its thirteenth turn.

Coral blew out the lamp and began, in the dark, to pack.
