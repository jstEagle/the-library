## 6. Leaving Is a Kind of Dying

Coral had always imagined she would leave the Hollow the way thieves leave houses: quickly, in the dark, with nothing in her hands that could be traced back to her.

Instead she left at dawn, which was the worst possible hour, because dawn in the Hollow was honest. The light came down the valley's throat in a long grey-gold pour, and it found everything — the crooked chimneys of the lower town, the frost on Maghda's shuttered stall, the wet backs of the goats already complaining their way up the terraces. There was no shadow to steal away inside. Anyone who looked out a window would see her plainly: a thin girl in a patched coat walking uphill against the light, carrying nothing but bread, a knife, and a map that refused to die.

She kept to the drainage channel behind the tannery, where the smell of lye covered her own scent, if scent mattered to whatever hunted keepers. The old hedge-witch's warning sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *Something kills them, girl. One by one. Twelve graves' worth of something.* Sera had pressed a twist of dried herbs into her palm at parting — rue and rowan and something bitter Coral couldn't name — and told her to chew it when the air began to hum. Coral didn't ask what humming meant. In the Hollow, asking questions about magic was a way of inviting it closer, like scratching at a scab.

The map rode against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of her coat where a thief keeps the things she cannot bear to fence. It had not moved all night. She had checked eleven times anyway. Ink did not crawl on its own; ink did not burn green and heal itself; ink did not whisper — she was almost sure it hadn't whispered — but she checked, because checking was the last ritual of the life she was leaving, and rituals were all she had ever owned outright.

At the edge of the tannery yard, a dog lifted its head from beneath a cart and watched her pass without barking. She knew the dog. Its owner was a cooper who had once sold Maghda three summers of his wedding day because the marriage had gone sour and grief was worth more than joy on the market. Coral had taken those summers off him herself, gently, while he wept and thanked her. She looked at the dog now and felt the familiar hollow open under her sternum — the hollow where a person's own years should sit.

*Never keep what you take.* Her rule. Her whole architecture. And here she was, walking out of the valley with the heaviest thing she'd ever carried strapped against her heart, and she had no intention of giving it back.

The scree pass began where the terraces gave up. It was not a road so much as an argument between the mountain and gravity, a spill of grey stone that clattered and shifted underfoot and made every step sound like a confession. Climbers hated it. Smugglers loved it, because nothing that lived below could follow quietly. Coral had used it twice before — once to lift a memory off a fur-trapper who thought himself safely above the world, and once, at fourteen, just to see what was up there. What had been up there was more scree, and wind, and a view of the valley rim: a line of standing stones so old their names had worn off, spaced along the crest like teeth in a jaw.

The boundary. Everyone knew the stones. Nobody talked about them much, the way nobody talks about the walls of a prison they've stopped noticing.

She climbed for two hours. The sun cleared the eastern rim and turned the scree into a field of small hot mirrors, and sweat ran down her spine, and twice she stopped to listen and heard only her own blood and the tick of settling stone. Once, far behind and below, a bell rang the morning hour in the lower town — the bell whose rope she'd braided new two winters ago, because the old one had frayed and no one else would do the work. Her hands remembered the rope's fibers. That was the trouble with leaving: your hands stayed behind whether you wanted them to or not.

Near the top, the wind changed.

It wasn't a direction change. It was a texture change. All her life the Hollow's wind had felt like the Hollow itself — flat, dry, slightly mineral, a wind that had blown over too little to carry any stories. This wind coming down from the crest was different in a way she had no word for yet, the way a deaf person might feel a drum through a floorboard before ever hearing music. It pushed against her face and seemed to push *through* her face, lightly, like fingers testing a curtain.

Sera's herbs. She chewed them. They tasted like a church floor: dust and old wood and something faintly sweet underneath, patient as rot.

The last hundred yards of scree rose toward the gap between two of the standing stones. Coral crouched in the shadow of the westernmost monolith and looked back.

Once. She had promised herself one look, because more than one look was how exiles turned into ghosts who wandered home forever.

The Hollow lay below her like a cupped hand. Smoke rising straight from forty chimneys. The brown ribbon of the river. The market square, empty now except for Maghda's awning, a smudge of faded red she could have covered with her thumb at this distance. Somewhere down there was the room she rented above the chandler's, with its one window and its mattress stuffed with straw and its floorboard where she kept — had kept — nothing, because she never kept anything. Somewhere down there were people who knew her name and none of whom knew her at all. Twenty-two years of borrowed faces traded across a counter, and not one of them hers.

She searched herself for grief and found instead a strange, terrible lightness, like a tooth coming loose. *Leaving is a kind of dying,* her mother used to say — one of the few memories Coral owned honestly, a scrap of a woman bent over a washbasin, saying it absently, the way you say things you don't know a child is keeping. *You leave a place, you leave the self that place was holding.* Coral had thought it was just talk. Standing here now, she understood her mother had been giving her instructions for a death she'd need to survive.

"Goodbye," Coral said aloud, and the wind took the word sideways, and the valley did not answer, because valleys never do.

She turned and walked through the gap in the stones.

* * *

Nothing happened. That was the first thing. She had braced for thunder, for a wall of force, for some sentience in the ancient rocks to wake and weigh her. Instead she took ten steps past the boundary line and the world simply went on being a mountainside — grey stone, blue sky, her own boots crunching. She almost laughed. All those generations huddled below the rim, telling children the outer world would boil their blood or turn their shadows against them, and it was just a slope of scree going down toward a green haze of forest.

Then she noticed the silence had changed key.

It took her another fifty steps to name it, and even then she named it wrong first. She thought: *insects.* But it wasn't insects. It wasn't sound at all, exactly. It was pressure — a fine, constant, subcutaneous vibration, as if the air itself were taut as a plucked string and someone somewhere was holding the note. The hum. *Chew it when the air begins to hum.* Well. The air was humming. She waited for the rue to kill her, since clearly it hadn't been meant as a cure for anything but nerves, and nothing happened, and the hum went on, gentle and enormous, like lying with your ear against the flank of some vast sleeping animal.

And the colors —

The Hollow had colors the way a ledger has poetry: technically present. Grey stone, brown water, the washed-out red of Maghda's awning. Here, past the stones, the greens of the forest below were so green they seemed to be lit from within, each tree holding its own private dawn. The sky overhead wasn't blue so much as *depths* — she looked up and felt the way she imagined drowning must feel, if drowning were voluntary. Even the scree under her feet had opinions: every stone a slightly different grey, veined and flecked, individual, as if color were a form of attention and everything outside the valley had been attended to.

Coral stood very still and let it happen to her. Wonder came first, whole and uncomplicated, the wonder of a child — and she was startled to find she still owned a child's wonder; she'd assumed she'd sold it years ago along with everything else.

Then vertigo hit her like a wave over a seawall.

It wasn't fear of heights. It was fear of *scale*. The Hollow was small; she had known it was small the way a fish knows the bowl is round, abstractly, without consequence. Now the bowl was gone. The world fell away from her feet in folds of forest and ridge after ridge beyond, and each ridge revealed more ridges, and the horizon wasn't a wall but a suggestion, and the hum said: *all of this is awake, and so are you, and there is nowhere in it that has ever heard of the Hollow or its rules.* Her knees actually buckled. She sat down hard on the warm scree and breathed, the way she'd taught frightened travelers to breathe while she lifted their pasts out of them. *In through the nose. You're safe. Nothing here will hurt you.* The lie had always worked on others. She tried it on herself. It half-worked. It was more truth than she'd ever given anyone, at least.

That was when her stray thought caught fire.

She had been thinking — idly, the mind wandering as minds do — *I wish I could see the sea. I've never seen the sea. I wonder if it looks like this sky feels.*

And the air in front of her, a foot from her nose, filled with a shimmer.

It wasn't quite light and wasn't quite mist. It gathered out of nothing the way breath gathers on cold glass: a soft, wavering image, roughly the shape of a horizon, a long silver-grey line meeting a long silver-grey sky, waves implied rather than shown, moving in a rhythm her body recognized before her mind did — the rhythm of something enormous breathing. Salt. She could *smell* salt, faintly, impossibly, on a mountaintop a thousand miles from any coast. The image held for three heartbeats, four, trembling like a reflection in disturbed water, and then it thinned, unraveled, and was gone, leaving only the hum and the ordinary morning and Coral sitting on the stones with her mouth open.

Her thought. Her stray, idle, worthless thought — visible. Made briefly, shyly real, like a fish surfacing in a pond.

"Oh," she said. Her voice sounded very small inside all that humming. "Oh, that's — "

That's what it's *for*. That's what the valley keeps out. Not danger. Not corruption. *This.* Magic wasn't some invading army battering at the Hollow's gates. Magic was the natural weather of the world, falling everywhere equally, and the valley was a rain-shadow, a dry country under a mountain's arm — and she had grown up believing the drought was safety. Sera's voice, cracked and certain: *it isn't protection, girl. It's a cage.* Coral had thought she meant a cage for the valley folk. Sitting here now, watching the air settle after carrying her private longing across its surface like a boat, she understood the cage had been built narrower than that. Built around *her*. Around the thirteenth keeper, and whoever had come before, and however many before them — born generation after generation into a hole cut out of the living world, so that something hunting them would find only dead ground and dead ends.

Twelve graves' worth of caution, spent on her.

Coral got up. Her legs shook, and she let them, because pretending otherwise was a Hollow habit and she was trying, clumsily, to stop. She brushed the scree dust from her coat and felt the map shift against her ribs — and froze, because it had shifted *wrong*, a slow slide rather than a jolt, deliberate as a cat turning over in sleep.

She unwrapped it right there, standing in the open with the whole waking world humming around her, propriety be damned. The leather case was warm. Warmer than her body. Inside, the map lay in the morning light, and the ink was moving.

Not crawling, this time. Flowing. The lines of road and river ran like dark water finding their level, rearranging themselves, and as she watched, a new mark surfaced near the map's upper edge, in territory that had been blank paper yesterday: a small, precise symbol like an eye, drawn in ink the color of rust and lilac. Below it, letters assembled themselves stroke by stroke, unhurried, as though written by a hand that had all the time in the world:

*She crosses. Good. Keep to the high road, keeper. The low road belongs to me.*

The hum deepened around her, and for the first time Coral could swear she felt attention in it — vast, layered, curious — like a thousand eyes opening at different depths of a lake.

She looked east, where the high road would run, a pale thread stitched along the ridgelines. She looked north, where the forest dropped away into green shadow and the low road wound out of sight.

Somewhere down in that shadow, faint but unmistakable, drifting up through the singing morning air, came the smell of iron and lilac.
