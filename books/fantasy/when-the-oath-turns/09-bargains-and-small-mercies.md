## 9. Bargains and Small Mercies

Coral ran until the lilac smell was gone from her lungs, and even then she did not stop.

The road behind her had already begun to forget them — she could feel it in the map's pull against her ribs, a gentle insistence that the way forward mattered and the way back did not. Tam kept pace beside her, his tinkering satchel banging against his hip, his breath ragged but his mouth, miraculously, still running.

"He knew your *name*," the boy said for the third time. "The tall one with the— the antlers, or whatever they were, he said *Coral Dunmore of the Hollow*, like it was written down somewhere."

"It probably is." She didn't slow. Her shoulder throbbed where Ashvael's finger had brushed her, one light touch that had felt like being read cover to cover by something with too many eyes. "Keep moving. He doesn't chase. That's what makes him worse than the inquisitor. Vael hunts. Ashvael just... waits at the places you're going to be."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be."

They left the road where it bent toward a river and took instead the track no cart had widened, up through bracken gone copper with early autumn, toward the dark green wall on the horizon that every map in the Hollow had marked with a single word and a warning: *Weeping*. The map in Coral's case tugged her that way with the patience of water finding a crack. There was no other way to the abbey country. The woods stood between her and everything, which meant they were the only place Ashvael could not simply be waiting.

She told herself that as they climbed. She told herself it again as the light went long and amber and the trees came up to meet them.

The first willow-like giant loomed out of the mist at the forest's edge, trunk silvered and split, branches hanging in curtains so dense the wind moved through them like fingers through hair. And from somewhere inside those curtains came a sound Coral had never heard from any tree: a low, wet weeping, as if the wood itself grieved.

Tam stopped dead. "Tell me you hear that."

"I hear it."

"Trees don't cry."

"Then we've found the ones that do." She checked the map one last time. The ink had crawled into a new shape since morning — a path threading between the trunks, marked here and there with small symbols she couldn't read, and along the margin, in handwriting that was not the courier's and was not hers, a single line in a language she almost understood:

*It shows you what you carry. Walk fast. Forgive slowly.*

"Charming," she muttered, and stepped under the branches.

*

Inside, the Weeping Woods were dusk-colored at noon. Mist pooled knee-deep between the roots, and every surface dripped, and the sound was everywhere — not one tree weeping but hundreds, a chorus of quiet grief that got under the skin and stayed. Coral walked with her hood up and her eyes down and tried very hard not to listen.

Because the tears weren't only water.

Wherever a drop struck the moss, it bloomed: brief, translucent images, like breath on glass. A woman's hands kneading bread. A child's shoe, small and muddy, alone on a stair. A door opening onto an empty room, over and over, the same door, the same room, the same nothing behind it. Strangers' sorrows, shed by strangers' trees, soaking into the ground to feed whatever fed the forest.

"They show you things," Tam whispered. He'd stopped beside a birch-thin sapling whose leaves trembled without wind. In the puddle beneath it, images turned like fish under ice: a burning wagon. A man shouting words too blurred to catch. "Is it doing that to us? Is it showing *us*?"

"It shows strangers their worst," Coral said. "That's what the old stories say. The trees take the memory you least want and hang it in front of you like laundry." She pulled him onward. "So don't look down."

Which was easy advice to give and impossible advice to keep. The mind goes to the puddles the way the tongue goes to a broken tooth.

For an hour she managed it. Eyes on the path, ears full of grief-song, hand on the map case, one foot and then the next. The forest thickened. The mist rose to their waists. The light went strange and sourceless, coming from everywhere and nowhere, and the weeping softened until it sounded less like sorrow and more like someone humming a lullaby badly through tears.

Then, at the edge of a clearing where a fallen giant lay bridging a stream, the trees found her.

It began gently. A drop fell from the curtain of a willow ahead of her and struck the water at her feet, and the image that bloomed there was not a stranger's.

It was hers.

A kitchen. Low ceiling, blackened beams. A woman's voice saying *you'll be good for Maghda now, won't you, little wren*, and a small girl's hand gripping a wooden spoon as if spoons were the last solid thing in the world. Seven years old. The exact age. The exact day.

Coral's whole body went cold. She hadn't thought of that day in years — she had made sure not to think of it, had buried it so deep even she couldn't dig it up, and that was the point, that was the trick she'd done to herself when she was small and clever and already a thief: she had stolen the memory *from herself*, locked it away where she couldn't reach it, because reaching it hurt more than anything the Hollow had ever shown her.

But the trees didn't care about locks. The trees showed what you *carried*, and she carried it still, buried alive and screaming all these years.

"No," she said aloud.

More drops fell. The stream itself had begun to glow, images rising off its surface like steam. The kitchen again. Her mother's face, young and tired and already planning to leave. A promise sworn over a child's head — she couldn't hear the words, only feel their weight, the oath-shape of them pressing down like a hand. Then the door. Then the road. Then the small girl standing in the doorway of the world's emptiest house, holding a wooden spoon, waiting for someone who had sworn she would come back and never did.

"Coral?" Tam's voice, far away. "Coral, you're shaking."

"I'm fine." The lie came automatically, professionally smooth, and died in her throat, because the trees were weeping harder now, all around them, and every puddle and pool and bead of water on every leaf had become the same scene, her mother's leaving multiplied a thousandfold, a cathedral of it, and there was nowhere to look that wasn't the worst moment of a life she'd pretended had no worst moments.

She sat down. It wasn't a decision; her legs simply ended. The moss was cold and wet through her skirt and she didn't care. Somewhere above her the willow curtains shivered and shed their grief directly onto her shoulders, and each drop that landed bloomed against her skin — not painful, worse than painful, *familiar*, the way an old scar is familiar — and Coral Dunmore, who had taken memories from soldiers and merchants and pilgrims and sold them without a flicker, who had built a fortress out of feeling nothing, put her face in her hands and shook.

She heard Tam move. She expected him to come closer. Instead he stepped back — deliberately, loudly, crunching away across the clearing to the fallen log bridge, putting twenty feet between them, and then sitting down on the log with his back to her, facing the stream, giving her the one thing no one in the Hollow had ever given her: privacy inside her own ruin.

He didn't ask what she saw. He didn't offer comfort in that bright, useless way people do, *it can't have been that bad*. He just sat there, a thin boy with ink-stained fingers, guarding her silence like it was something valuable he'd been entrusted with.

Coral cried until there was nothing left in her, which took longer than she would have guessed, years longer. The trees wept with her. At some point she realized the distinction had stopped mattering.

*

When she finally raised her head, the light had shifted toward evening and the mist had thinned. Tam still sat on the log, back turned, whittling a stick with the small knife he used for cleaning clockwork. He must have heard her move, but he gave her another minute before speaking, and even then he spoke to the stream, not to her.

"My dad left when I was six," he said. "Took the good mule and the good tools and left my mum a note that said he'd send for us. Never did. I used to stand at the end of our lane every market day for two years, watching the road." He turned the stick in his fingers. "So. Whatever that was. You don't have to tell me. But I'm not going to look at you like it's a wound, if that's what you're waiting for. Wounds are just stories the body keeps."

Coral laughed. It came out cracked and wet and surprised her so much she laughed again, properly this time, a sound with no hollow in it at all.

"A story the body keeps," she repeated. "You're fourteen. How are you fourteen and wise?"

"Fifteen in spring. And it's not wisdom, it's just — I've thought about it a lot, is all. On roads. Alone." He shrugged, and finally turned around. His face, when she met it, held exactly what he'd promised: nothing like pity. Just attention. Just a person looking at another person. "Did it help? The crying. Mum says it does. Says grief's like water, it finds its level eventually."

"I wouldn't know. I don't do this." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "I take feelings off other people and sell them. I don't keep any of my own. It's the rule."

"What happens to yours, then? The ones you don't sell?"

"Nothing. They just—" She gestured vaguely at her own chest. "Sit there. Getting older. Going stale."

Tam considered this with the seriousness he brought to broken hinges. "That sounds lonely."

"It is lonely." The admission slipped out before the fortress could close its gate, and once it was out, more followed, drawn up after it like water finding its level. "It's been lonely since I was seven. Since—" She stopped. Looked at the stream, where the last of the images had faded to ordinary water. "There's a day I stole from myself. When I was small. I've never let myself have it back. Not once. I built my whole life on top of the hole where it used to be, and I told myself the hole was a foundation." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Those trees just showed me the shape of it. Not even the whole thing. Just the shape. My mother leaving. Something she promised me. I still can't hear the words of it, and apparently that's the part that breaks me."

Tam slid off the log and came closer — slowly, telegraphing it, the way you'd approach a spooked animal — and sat down in the moss an arm's length away. Close enough for company. Far enough for choice.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I saw yours. Back there. Some of it leaked over while you were — while." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I didn't mean to look. But I'm glad I did, maybe. Because now I know why you're like you are, and knowing doesn't make me think less of you. It makes me think you're the toughest person I've ever met, and also the saddest, and both of those are true at once and neither cancels the other."

"You should have flinched," Coral said quietly. "Everyone flinches. It's what I'm for. People see what I am and they step back."

"I'm not everyone."

No, she thought, looking at him — this runaway boy with a tinker's kit and a head full of stories, who had followed a thief out of the only home either of them had known — you very much are not.

"Thank you," she said. It tasted foreign in her mouth, unpracticed, like a coin from a country she'd never traded with. "For turning around. No one's ever — people in the Hollow, they'd have watched. Watching is currency there."

"Well, we're not in the Hollow." He smiled, lopsided. "Out here watching's free, so I figure kindness can be too."

Above them, the great willow's curtains stilled. The weeping had softened to almost nothing, and Coral understood, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, that the forest had finished with her — had shown her the shape of her burden and then, in its strange arboreal mercy, let her set it down for a while. Not taken it. Never taken it; that was her own crime, not theirs. Just held it with her, the way the boy beside her was holding it now, without flinching.

She stood. Her legs held. The map, warm against her ribs, tugged onward and deeper.

"We should move," she said. "Ashvael doesn't chase, but he waits, and the woods won't hold him forever. Nothing holds him forever."

Tam scrambled up, slinging his satchel. "How far to wherever we're going?"

"Ask the map." She drew it out — and stopped.

The ink had changed again. The crawling lines had redrawn themselves while they rested, the path bending sharply northward, and new marginalia had surfaced in that half-familiar script, fresh as wet blood:

*The twelfth keeper sleeps ahead. Wake him gently. He has been waiting to be found.*

And beneath it, smaller, in handwriting Coral now recognized with a jolt as her own — aged, hurried, but unmistakably hers:

*Do not trust the grave that is not a grave.*

Tam leaned in over her shoulder, read it twice, and swallowed audibly.

"So," he said. "Whose handwriting is that?"
