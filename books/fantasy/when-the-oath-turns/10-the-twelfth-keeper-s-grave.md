## 10. The Twelfth Keeper's Grave

The map did not lead them forward anymore. That was the first wrongness of the morning — the second was the crows.

Coral had noticed it two days past, somewhere between the ash-wood and the river called the Sigh by people who had never heard it weep: the ink on the courier's map had begun to move backward. Roads that had once unspooled ahead of her like thread from a spindle now seemed to pull in the other direction, tugging at her eyes whenever she looked down, drawing them west and north, toward a fold of hills marked with nothing at all. Not blankness. She had learned the difference by now. Blank paper was empty. This was *withheld* — a place the map knew and chose not to say aloud.

"It's leading us in circles," Tam complained for the third time since dawn, hopping a stone wall with the easy grace of a boy who had spent his short life climbing over things that belonged to other people. "Or it's leading us nowhere. Or it's leading us somewhere terrible, which is my personal favorite theory."

"It's leading us back," Coral said.

"Back where? There's no grave marker. There's no anything. Just hills and sheep and—" He stopped. He'd seen what she saw.

The crows sat in a dead rowan tree at the crest of the slope, perhaps twenty of them, silent in a way crows are never silent. They were not cawing. They were not squabbling. They were arranged along the bare branches with the terrible tidiness of mourners who had been waiting a long time for someone to arrive, and when Coral lifted her eyes to them, every black head turned in unison, as if pulled by the same string.

"They know," she said quietly.

"Crows know everything. My da used to say—"

"Tam."

He fell quiet. He was learning, slowly, when her voice went flat like that, that it wasn't anger. It was the sound of her listening to something he couldn't hear yet.

She climbed the slope. The grass grew long here, untended, silvered at the tips by frost that hadn't quite surrendered to the season, and it bent around her boots in patterns she tried not to read too closely. Since leaving the Weeping Woods she had caught herself doing that — reading meaning into wind and water and the flight of birds, the way she'd once read meaning into the twitch of a mark's fingers before lifting his memory. The world outside the Hollow was fluent in a language she'd never been taught, and it kept trying to talk to her.

The rowan stood over a shallow dip in the earth, no more than a saucer pressed into the hillside. Someone had tried to fill it. Grass had grown over the attempt, thin and pale, refusing to take root properly, as if the ground itself remembered what lay beneath and would not dress itself up for company.

The crows watched. They did not move.

"We could walk past," Tam offered, without conviction.

"No." Coral knelt. Her knees found the frost-wet grass and the cold came up through the wool of her trousers like a hand closing around bone. "We couldn't. You know we couldn't."

He did, apparently, because he sighed and dropped down beside her and began, without being asked, to pull stones from the edge of the depression, setting them aside in a careful line. She loved him a little for that — for not asking whether they should, only how. She didn't examine the feeling too closely. Love, these days, felt like a door left open in a house she was still learning to live in.

The soil came away easily. Too easily. It had been turned recently — seasons ago, not years — and beneath its skin it was loose, aerated, disturbed. Her fingers found cloth first, then the cold wax of something wrapped in oilskin, then the edge of a boot sole, cracked and mud-stiffened, pointing up at the sky as though its owner had been walking somewhere and simply stopped, mid-stride, mid-life, and been folded down into the dark.

They dug in silence. The sun climbed. The crows stayed.

* * *

The man in the ground had been dead since late autumn, by the look of him, and the earth had been gentle with what it took and honest about the rest. His coat had rotted to lace at the shoulders; his hands, crossed on his chest by whoever buried him, had gone to leather and tendon. But someone had laid him out with care. Someone had folded his arms, closed what remained of his eyes, tucked a sprig of rowan berries — long since gone to grey mush — against his collarbone. Rowan, Tam said, was for warding. For keeping things out. Or in.

"He buried himself?" Tam asked. "Before he died?"

"Someone else buried him." Coral's voice came out strange. "And warded the grave after."

"Against what?"

She didn't answer, because she was looking at the thing beneath the man's folded hands. An oilskin packet, tied with waxed cord, small enough to cup in both palms. Beside it, a book — a real book, bound in scarred brown leather, swollen with damp, a brass clasp green with age. A journal. The kind couriers carried because maps told you where but journals told you why.

Her hands were steady as she lifted them free. That steadiness frightened her more than trembling would have. She was a thief of memories; she had spent her whole life taking the most intimate things people owned, and some part of her — the professional part, the hollow part — wanted to do it again, right now, to this dead man. To press her palm to his leathery brow and draw out the last of him the way she'd drawn the funeral from the soldier, clean and quick, a transaction.

But there was nothing left to take. Whatever the courier had been, whatever he'd carried, whatever had chased him across these hills — the earth had drunk all of it, or something else had. His skull, when the soil shifted, gaped empty and dark as a snuffed lamp.

"Coral." Tam's voice had gone thin. "Look at his neck."

She looked.

Two marks, side by side, below the jaw. Not wounds, exactly — not blade or arrow or beast. They were round, precise, deliberate, each the width of a thumb-tip, and though months of rain had washed the flesh to shadow, the shadows themselves were wrong. They were not brown, as old bruise goes. They were faintly, unmistakably *violet*. Lilac-dark, like a stain of dusk that had soaked into the skin and stayed.

Like the smell of blood she had caught at the ford, the day a dying man put a map case in her hands and said a word she hadn't understood until it was far too late to pretend she hadn't heard it.

"Iron and lilac," she whispered.

"What?"

"Nothing." She closed the dead man's collar over the marks, gently, the way you'd close a door on a room you weren't ready to enter. "Help me finish this. Properly, this time."

They worked until the light began to slant gold through the rowan's bare branches. They piled the earth high and set the stones Tam had lined up in a ring, and Tam cut a branch from a hawthorn further down the slope and drove it into the mound because, he said, nobody should lie under plain dirt with no name on them. Coral took the charcoal stub from her pocket and hesitated over the smooth bark of the stone at the head.

Then she wrote: *Twelfth keeper. He ran so that others could run after him.*

It wasn't a name. It might have been true anyway.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the hill, in the ruins of a shepherd's cot whose roof had fallen in a generation ago and whose walls still held the day's warmth like cupped hands. Tam built a fire small enough to hide and cooked nothing in particular over it — he had a genius for turning almost-nothing into supper, a skill Coral suspected would keep them alive longer than any sword. She sat apart with the journal on her knees and the firelight moving on its cover, and for a long time she did not open it.

"You don't have to," Tam said. He was sharpening his little belt-knife against a stone, an occupation that was half usefulness and half nerves. "Reading it. Some things you can leave in the ground."

"Some things follow you out anyway." She turned the journal over. The brass clasp clicked open under her thumb like it had been waiting. "He carried this map for someone. He died carrying it. I'm holding the thing he bled for, Tam. The least I owe him is to know what he was running from — or toward. Names make ghosts quieter. Maghda always said that." She paused. "Maghda said a lot of things that turned out to be cages."

She opened the journal.

The first pages were ordinary — supply tallies, weather notes, road names in a cramped, disciplined hand, the handwriting of a man who wrote quickly because stopping was dangerous. *Crossed the Sigh at the mill ford, water higher than reported. Paid the ferryman double and he still spat after me.* A sketch of a bridge, rendered with surprising tenderness. A list of inns, each annotated: safe, unsafe, unsafe but the stew is worth it. Tam leaned in despite himself, and she let him. Reading alone in the dark felt worse than reading together.

Then, forty pages in, the handwriting changed.

Not gradually. All at once, between one entry and the next, the letters grew larger, looser, pressed hard enough into the page to emboss the sheets beneath. As if the man writing had stopped being careful. As if he had stopped believing care would save him.

*They were never delivering me anywhere,* the new hand said. *I understood it tonight at the inn in Marrowgate, watching Vael's men drink downstairs. They aren't escorts. They're leash-holders. The crown doesn't want the Oathstone found. It wants the Oathstone FOUND BY THEM — recovered, catalogued, and reforged. Reforged, Coral-of-the-road if anyone ever reads this, do you understand what reforged means? It means broken apart and remade with crown-marks hammered into every face. A truce-stone turned into a throne-stone. The old kings swore the stone belonged to both courts and neither. These kings mean it to belong to one and only one, forever, and everyone who ever signed their name to peace will find their signatures burning.*

Coral's breath had stopped somewhere in the middle of the passage. She made herself start again.

*I asked my handler, straight out, at the last waystation. Asked him what the reforging was for. He smiled at me the way you smile at a dog that has learned a word too many. And that night I ran. Gods forgive me, I ran, and I took the map with me because it was the only copy and because a thing that terrible should belong to no one — not even to the people it was stolen from, if taking it back means handing one court a weapon with a treaty carved on it.*

*The twelfth keeper before me hid the map in the Hollow because magic cannot follow there. He told me the valley was a vault. He lied, or he didn't understand. It isn't a vault. It's a blind spot. And now I've passed it to a girl inside the blind spot, and the thirteenth moon is coming, and there are things in these hills that smell my blood from a county away. If you're reading this, I am sorry. I am sorry I led them to your door. I am sorry I made you the thirteenth when twelve was already too many graves. But hear the last of an old coward:*

*Do not give the stone to the crown. Do not give it to the courts. They will tell you it belongs to one or the other. It belongs to the oath itself — to every promise ever kept and broken, and that is everyone, and so it must be no one's. Find another way. Break the riddle if you must. I could not. I was tired, and they were fast, and the hills were dark.*

*If a woman finds this with thief's hands and a keeper's blood — I saw you in a dream, near the end. I don't dream true, but I hope this once. Forgive me. And be faster than I was.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The grave above them was signature enough.

Tam had gone very still beside her, his knife forgotten flat on his knee. "Vael," he said. "That's the inquisitor. The polite one."

"The polite one." Coral heard her own voice come out low and level and cold, and recognized, distantly, the tone she used on brokers when she was about to be cheated and wanted them to know she knew. "Wren Vael, who asks gentle questions and leaves flowers on tables. Who says *please* and *thank you* and means none of it. He wasn't hunting the courier because the courier stole something. He was hunting him because the courier *refused*." She closed the journal with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a room you're not ready to enter — no. She had thought that before, about the dead man's collar. This was different. This was the way you closed a door when you intended to come back through it swinging.

"The crown wants the stone reforged," Tam said slowly, working it through. "Broken and rebuilt with their mark on it. So the truce ends, and the fae owe them, or fear them, or—"

"Or kneel. Yes." Coral stood. Her legs ached from digging; she ignored them. Firelight caught her face and Tam later swore it had gone strange, lit from underneath like a mask held too near a candle. "All those years in the Hollow, I thought the brokers were the worst thing money could buy. Then I thought the fae were, with their bargains and their beautiful teeth. And the whole time, the men with flags and laws and *kindness* were building the cruelest bargain of all — and calling it order."

She looked down at the journal in her hands, then out into the dark hills, toward the west, toward wherever Wren Vael was sleeping tonight in some clean bed with a courteous conscience.

"He'll catch us eventually. You know that."

"He's polite," Tam said. "Maybe he'll listen."

"He listened to a courier beg, and put two thumbs in his throat." She tucked the journal inside her coat, against her heart, where the map rode already, and felt the two objects settle together like pieces of a lock finding each other. "No, Tam. We stop running from him. From tomorrow we run *toward* — toward the abbey, toward the stone, ahead of all three of them. Because now I know what he wants to build with it, and I know what the fae want to charge for it, and I know exactly one thing that neither crown nor court can do."

Tam waited. The fire popped. Above the ruined cot, the crows had finally risen from the rowan, wheeling once against the stars, and flown west.

"Neither of them can choose to let it go," Coral said. "But I can."

Far off, beyond the hills, beyond the dark, a bell began to toll — one clear note, repeated, from a direction where no bell should be. Neither of them moved. Neither of them spoke. And in the silence between the strokes, Coral understood that somewhere out in that darkness, something had just learned where they were.
