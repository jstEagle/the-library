## 11. City of Scales

Verrin announced itself long before we saw it — first by smell, then by sound, and finally by a light that had no business existing at the edge of the world.

The smell came on the third morning of travel: woodsmoke braided with roasting meat, hot metal, tallow, spice, and beneath it all something sweeter and stranger, like flowers rotting in honey. Tam said it was the scent of money changing hands so fast the air itself grew greasy with it. I thought it smelled like Maghda's back room multiplied ten thousand times, and my stomach tightened around our last heel of bread.

Then came the sound. Not one sound but a hundred thousand layered together — hawkers crying their wares, hammers ringing in forges that never cooled, cart wheels grinding, dogs, drums from somewhere, a woman singing high and clear over it all as though the noise were an ocean she could float above. And threaded through everything, faint but unmistakable to ears trained like mine, the murmur of memory-brokers calling their stock.

*Grief, lightly used. A wedding, whole and unspoiled. The sea, for those who've never seen it.*

I had grown up inside that voice. I knew its music the way a fish knows water. Hearing it here, three hundred miles from the Hollow, was like hearing my mother's lullaby sung by a stranger with her stolen face.

But it was the light that stopped us both on the last ridge of the road. Verrin sat in a bowl of hills where two rivers met, and at its heart rose a tower faced entirely in mirrored bronze — the Scalehouse, Tam said, where anything could be weighed and therefore bought. The setting sun struck it and the tower threw light back across the whole city, so that Verrin glowed like a coal banked in flesh, burning without being consumed.

"Anything can be bought there," Tam repeated, with the reverence he usually saved for stories about dragons. "That's what they say. Anything. Someone once bought the memory of their own birth. Someone else bought a name."

"And someone," I said, "bought me."

He looked at me sideways, uncertain whether it was a joke. It wasn't, quite. Every coin I'd ever earned in the Hollow had passed through hands that passed through hands that led, eventually, to cities like this. I had simply never followed the river upstream before.

We went down into the glow.

* * *

Verrin's streets were canyons of commerce, walled with stalls stacked three stories high, roofed in awnings dyed every color the word *color* could be stretched to mean. Scales hung from doorposts everywhere — little brass balances swinging in the draft, weighing coins, herbs, letters, tears sealed in glass. Over one doorway hung a scale large enough to weigh a child; over another, small enough, I suspected, to weigh a soul.

Tam moved through it all with his mouth open, cataloging wonders aloud — a man selling bottled thunder, a woman whose caged songbirds only sang dead people's favorite songs. I walked close behind him and let his chatter cover the work my eyes were doing: reading faces, marking exits, noting which guards wore guild colors and which wore none. Old habits. In the Hollow I had learned to move through markets like smoke through fingers. Here the fingers were more numerous, but smoke is smoke.

The map, folded against my ribs inside my shirt, had begun to warm each time we drew nearer to some purpose it approved of. I felt it now, a faint heat like a hand pressed flat between my shoulder blades, guiding. Following its pull through the tangle of streets, we climbed toward the oldest quarter, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the awnings gave way to slate roofs furred with moss.

There, in a square no wider than a threshing floor, stood a house with a scholar's sign hanging above its door: a candle carved in oak, unlit, above the words **HALL OF THE UNBROKEN WORD**.

"The clerk in Maghda's office used to talk about this place," I murmured. "Scholars who study oaths. How they're made, how they bind, how they break. If anyone can tell me what the Oathstone really is — what restoring it would actually *do* —"

"Then let's knock." Tam reached for the door.

I caught his wrist. "You wait outside."

His face did the thing it always did when I tried to protect him — the wounded look of a boy who had survived things that should have killed him and resented being treated as survivable. "Coral—"

"Tinker's boy. You're loud, you're memorable, and you have exactly one useful skill, which is running. Save it." I softened it with a crooked smile. "Watch the street. If anyone follows me in, whistle like you're calling a horse."

He grumbled, but he took up his post against the wall opposite, arms crossed, wearing his best impression of a person with nothing worth stealing. I pushed open the Hall's door.

* * *

Inside smelled of dust and lamp oil and old vellum, and it was very quiet — the first true quiet since the Hollow. Shelves rose past sight into gloom, crammed not with books but with objects: broken rings, snapped staffs, rusted keys tied with faded ribbon, each labeled in a careful antique hand. Tokens of oaths, I realized. Every vow leaves a residue, and these scholars had spent centuries collecting the husks.

A man sat at a desk in the center of it all, small and gray as a moth, mending a shattered quill with wax and thread. He did not look up.

"We're closed," he said.

"I haven't come to buy or sell." I kept my voice level. "I've come about an oath. An old one. Older than any kingdom."

The quill-mending paused. He raised his head, and I saw that his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of cloth boiled too many times. "Everyone who comes to Verrin comes to buy or sell. That is the whole of the city's liturgy." But something in him had sharpened. "What oath?"

"One sworn between humankind and the fae courts. Sealed on a stone. The stone is gone."

For a long moment the only sound was a carriage passing outside, wheels hissing on wet cobbles. Then the old scholar set down his needle with great deliberation, the way a man sets down a tool when he has decided to pick up a weapon instead.

"Sit," he said, indicating a stool. When I didn't move, he added, almost kindly: "Or run. But you've come too far to run at the first hard question, I think. You have a courier's map under your shirt. I can see the shape of the case. And there are people in this city who would pay a great deal to know it had arrived."

Cold walked down my spine on small feet. "Who told you about the map?"

"No one told me. I *inferred*. It is what scholars do instead of sleeping." He folded his hands. "Now. The Oathstone. I will tell you what I know, because knowledge is the one thing in Verrin that cannot legally be sold, only given — a loophole I have exploited shamelessly for sixty years. And then you will tell me why a girl from a magic-dead valley carries the most dangerous object in the world pressed against her heart. Do we understand each other?"

I sat.

"The stone was never merely a treaty-marker," he began, pacing now between his shelves, touching the broken tokens as he passed them like a priest among relics. "It was the *anchor*. Every oath sworn between mortal and fae — every bargain, every debt, every promise — drew its binding force from that single point. Break the anchor, and oaths become... suggestions. Courtesies. Words in the wind. Which is why the courts have hunted its restorer for three hundred years, and why your crown wants it found even more desperately than they do. Whoever holds the Oathstone does not merely keep the peace." He turned. "He becomes the peace. All bargains flow through him. Refuse his terms, and your own word turns to ash in your mouth."

"That's not peace," I said quietly. "That's a leash."

"Now you begin to see why it must never be reforged." His pale eyes fixed on me. "And yet both sides want it reforged, each certain their hand will hold the leash more gently. So. Your question, girl, before I ask mine: what do you intend to do with it?"

I thought of Wren Vael's gentle, terrible courtesy. Of Ashvael's lilac-scented patience. Of the ink crawling across the map like something alive and homesick.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I know it shouldn't belong to any of them."

The old scholar exhaled slowly, and something in his shoulders eased, as if I had answered correctly without knowing the question. "Then perhaps—" 

The door banged open behind me.

* * *

I turned with a pick already palmed — thief's reflexes die hard — and found myself looking at four men and a woman in dove-gray livery, silver scales stitched at their collars. Guild colors. The woman held a sheet of paper, and on it, rendered in surprisingly skillful engraving, was my face.

**WANTED FOR QUESTIONING,** the bounty read. **CORAL DUNMORE OF THE HOLLOW. LAST SEEN ON THE NORTH ROAD. REWARD NEGOTIABLE THROUGH THE BROKERS' GUILD.**

Maghda's seal sat at the bottom like a black sun.

"You see," the scholar said sadly, to no one in particular, "in Verrin, information travels faster than horses. Someone described you at the north gate yesterday evening. I did wonder how long we had."

The enforcers spread out with the unhurried competence of professionals. No drawn steel — questioning targets were worth more breathing. The woman spoke in a voice like syrup poured over gravel. "No trouble, girl. The guild only wants a conversation. You'll walk out again. Probably."

Probably. There it was — the whole architecture of Verrin's freedom in one word. Anything can be bought, including the difference between walking out and not.

I did the arithmetic I'd done ten thousand times in the Hollow: four exits, six bodies, one old man whose allegiance I couldn't guess, and Tam outside, whom I could not allow to see me cornered because he would do something brave and stupid and final.

So I did what I had always done. I became smoke.

The stool went over backward into the nearest enforcer's knees. The desk lamp followed, shattering, throwing oil-fire across the papers — not to burn the Hall, the flames were small, but to make every eye flinch toward light. In that half-second of universal blink I was moving, low and fast, through the gap between the woman and the shelf-wall, and then I was out the door into the square, lungs full of evening air—

—and Tam was whistling, loud as a stable call, because two more enforcers had been waiting at the far end of the street, and he had put himself directly in their line of approach, arms wide, shouting, "She went east! East! Through the fish market!" — pointing west.

They hesitated exactly long enough.

"Run!" I screamed at him, and he ran, and for a moment I believed — I *believed*, with the whole foolish weight of my heart — that we would both slip away the way we had slipped away from bandits, from Ashvael, from everything. He was fast. He knew alleys. He was laughing even as he fled, the laugh he laughed when a story was being born.

Then a crossbow bolt took him in the thigh, and the story changed.

He went down rolling, crying out, and the two enforcers behind him — the ones he'd been leading west — were on him before I had covered twenty paces. I heard them wrench his arms back. I heard him say my name, not in accusation, just in fact, the way you say the name of a place you expected to reach.

Every instinct I owned said *keep moving*. Smoke doesn't turn around. Smoke doesn't grieve in the street where the hunters can see. I had built my entire life on the principle that what you carry, you carry alone, and what falls behind you stays behind you, and if you stop to look back you will join it.

I stopped anyway.

I stood in the mouth of an alley with my heart hammering against the map case and watched them drag Tam into a covered wagon, watched the wagon pull away toward the river district where the Scalehouse glowed its pitiless bronze glow, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a wound opening, exactly what had happened.

He had been following me for the story. That was the joke we'd shared on the road, the truth we'd built our strange companionship on. But stories follow, and following has a price, and the price had just been paid in a boy's blood on Verrin cobols while I ran clean.

In the Hollow, I had one rule above all others: never keep what you take. Pass it on. Stay empty. Stay safe.

I had broken the rule the day I took the courier's map. And everything I carried had grown hooks, and the hooks had caught Tam, and now the debt was real and owed in a currency I couldn't steal or sell.

"Coral Dunmore," said a voice behind me.

The old scholar stood at the edge of the alley, breathless, his gray robes dusted with lamp oil. In his hands he carried a satchel, and thrust through its strap were three of his broken oath-tokens, as if he'd grabbed the only possessions that mattered.

"They'll burn my Hall by morning for what I almost told you," he said. "So I find myself suddenly mobile. And you find yourself suddenly famous." He held out the satchel. "The brokers' guild in this city is sister to your Maghda's — same rites, same masters, older. They will interrogate your boy to find you, and they are *very* good at interrogation. But—" his pale eyes gleamed with sixty years of shameless loopholes, "—every machine has a ledger. Even theirs. Especially theirs."

I took the satchel. Somewhere across the city, the mirrored tower caught the last of the light and burned.

"Tell me about the ledger," I said.

* * *

By midnight I had learned three things.

First: the guild keeps records — meticulous, indexed, jealous records — of every transaction, every bounty, every prisoner. Records require clerks, and clerks can be bribed, blackmailed, or befriended, and the old scholar knew the name of a clerk named Odell who had fallen out of favor and nursed his grievance like a hidden blade.

Second: prisoners taken for questioning were held in the Weighhouse cells beneath the Scalehouse, and the first interrogation was always scheduled within a day, because memories spoil.

Third: I could not do this alone, and for the first time in my life the knowledge didn't numb me. It burned.

I stood on a rooftop in the rain with the scholar's satchel over my shoulder and the map warming against my ribs like a second heartbeat, looking down at the glowing tower that held Tam, and I made a promise — silently, fiercely, in the one place no broker could reach it.

I had spent my life taking things from strangers. Tomorrow I would go and take back a boy.
