## 12. What Justice Costs Off the Books

The cell smelled of tallow and wet stone, and Tam decided, somewhere in the second hour of counting the cracks in the ceiling, that if he survived this he was going to write a very unflattering ballad about the city of Verrin.

He had been in worse places. There had been the winter he'd slept under a tinker's wagon with a wheel for a roof, and the three days in the Marshlock lockup for lifting a copper off a drunk who turned out to be a constable's cousin. But those places had been honest about what they were. This one was beneath a countinghouse — he could hear it, the soft industry of ledgers being opened above him, quills scratching like mice in the walls — and there was something obscene about being locked up by people whose whole trade was remembering things for other people.

His wrists were bound with silk. That was the detail that kept snagging on him. Not rope, not iron — silk, dyed guild-grey, soft as a lady's favor. Someone down here had opinions about leaving marks. Someone down here wanted their merchandise unbruised.

*Merchandise.* He tested the word and found it fit too well. He was not, strictly speaking, worth anything himself. He was a runaway tinker's boy with a head full of half-learned songs and a talent for being exactly where he shouldn't be. His value lay entirely in what he knew: where Coral Dunmore had gone, which road she'd taken out of Verrin, how fast she moved, whether she traveled by night or day.

Three enforcers had asked him these questions already. He had given them three performances — the ignorant boy, the terrified boy, the simpleton boy — and all three times the man called Brannick had smiled the way a smith smiles at iron that won't take an edge yet, and said, *We have time, little tinker. Memory is our trade. We know better than anyone that everything keeps.*

That was the worst of it. They weren't going to beat it out of him. They were going to wait until waiting did the work, because they understood, professionally, that all things held inside a person eventually leak.

Tam leaned his head back against the wall and thought about Coral, who was by now two days gone toward the salt flats, moving through country she didn't know, carrying a map that wanted her dead or crowned depending on who caught her. He thought about how she'd looked when the enforcers closed around them in the scholar's alley — how she'd looked at him, just once, and he'd read it plain as print: *run.* So he had run, uselessly, gloriously, for the length of one street before a net of grey silk took his legs out from under him.

He'd bought her maybe forty heartbeats. He hoped it had been enough. He told himself stories about it while the tallow burned down: that she was over the walls by now, that the map was pulling her along roads that forgot themselves behind her, that by morning she'd be a rumor even to herself.

The lock turned.

Tam sat up so fast his neck cracked. But it wasn't Brannick. The woman who came in alone, carrying her own lamp, was small and grey as the cell itself — fifty-some years, he guessed, with ink stains worked so deep into her fingers they looked like bruises that had given up healing. She wore clerk's robes, but wrong: the guild sigil at her collar had been unpicked and sewn back on upside-down, and only someone who'd spent his childhood watching his mother mend other people's finery would ever have noticed.

She set the lamp on the floor between them and folded herself down onto a stool that must have been left in the cell for exactly this purpose, and for a long moment she simply looked at him, the way you look at a page to see what condition it's in before you decide what to do with it.

"You're the boy," she said. "The one they took in the alley."

"I'm lots of boys," Tam said. "Depends who's asking."

"The honest answer," she said. "Good. Brannick got three performances and no truth. I watched him report them." She tilted her head. "You're better than he thinks you are, and he thinks you're quite good."

"Who are you?"

"My name is Odile Fenner. It was Clerk Third Rank of the Brokers' Guild of Verrin, and now it is nothing, because eleven years ago I wrote down something I was not supposed to write down, and I have been demoted into the basement every year since, and every year I keep writing things down anyway, because someone has to." She said it without heat, the way you recite your own name. "I'm asking the questions tonight, tinker's boy. You'll want to listen, because I'm the only person in this building who doesn't want anything out of your mouth except the truth."

"That's a suspicious thing to want."

"It is. That's why it works." She reached into her robe and produced a small notebook, bound in grey leather, and a pencil stub worn to the length of a thumbnail. "Let's begin with what Brannick will do to you tomorrow, so you understand the shape of your evening. There's a process called *gentle extraction*. No blades. A broker touches your temple and reads the surface of your memory — recent days, strong emotions. It leaves you sick and weepy for a week and costs the guild nothing. They'll do it at dawn unless someone gives them a reason not to. And then they'll have your face, your road, and Coral Dunmore's name attached to both, and there will be bounty sheets in every market from here to the coast by week's end."

Tam's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "And if I talk?"

"Then they extract it gently anyway, because they don't trust testimony they haven't taken themselves. Talking buys you nothing with them." She let that sit. "But talking to me buys you something else. Because I am going to leave this cell, walk upstairs, and enter into the duty log that the prisoner is feverish — bad head wound from the net, you understand, nets do that — and that extraction is medically inadvisable for three days. Possibly four. Clerks are believed about fevers. Nobody checks a fever."

"Why would you do that?"

"Because I'm buying something too," Odile Fenner said. "I'm buying your testimony. And unlike Brannick, I pay first."

* * *

She laid it out for him the way his mother used to lay out mending: everything flat, everything visible, nothing hidden in the folds.

Eleven years ago, Odile Fenner had been a rising clerk in the Verrin guild, trusted with the deep archives, the old contracts, the oaths of record. And in the course of her work she had found a pattern. Guild brokers sold memories — that was the trade, everyone knew the trade — but the guild also *held* memories. Deposits. Powerful families paid handsomely to store their worst moments somewhere outside their own heads: the bribe witnessed, the rival ruined, the child disowned in a rage and quietly bought back before anyone heard of it. And every so often, a deposit would be *altered*. A memory would come back to its owner sanded smooth, the crime filed off, the guilt excised — and the guild would charge a fee for the restoration and swear the damage was natural decay.

"I checked the decay," Odile said. "Memory decays at its edges. These had been cut at the root. That's not rot. That's surgery." Her pencil tapped the notebook. "I wrote it down. Names, dates, contract numbers. Eleven years of names, dates, and contract numbers. And when I brought it to my superiors, I learned the most important lesson of my life, which is that the powerful do not fear exposure. They fear *record*. Exposure fades in a season. Record compounds."

So she had been demoted. And she had kept writing. And she had discovered, in the basements of Verrin, that she was not alone — that scattered through the guilds and courts and countinghouses of the free cities were dozens like her: clerks, registrars, toll-keepers, a harbor master, a woman who stamped import seals at the salt gate. Ordinary people, invisible people, the people whose whole job was to notice what actually happened and write it down while the important people were busy performing. They called themselves nothing. They had no name, no banner, no oath — Odile was emphatic on that point; oaths could be broken, and what they were building had to be the kind of thing that couldn't.

They had a ledger instead. Copies of copies, hidden in plain sight — pages sewn into hymnals, entries disguised as shipping tallies, one full duplicate buried in a lead box under a tannery that had burned down twice, which was why no one ever searched it. Every broken oath by every powerful person that any of them could document. Every altered deposit. Every witness paid, every verdict bought, every promise made in private and betrayed in public.

"We can't arrest anyone," Odile said. "We can't fine anyone, or exile anyone, or do any of the things justice is supposed to do. We have no swords and no seal. All we have is paper and patience. But I'll tell you what we've learned, tinker's boy, and you can carry it out of this cell or die in it, whichever you choose: a man can survive being hated. What he cannot survive is being *known*. Hatred is weather. Record is climate."

Tam sat with that for a while. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself.

"What does this have to do with me?"

"Coral Dunmore stole a memory from Maghda's sisterhood in the Hollow — or rather, she declined to sell them the one thing they wanted, and Maghda's reach extends into this guild like roots into a shared wall. The guild wants the map. The crown wants the map. And a certain fae lord's agents have been asking after her in ways that make even Brannick nervous. She is currently the single most valuable unknown in Verrin, and every power in this city believes she will eventually need to come back through it." Odile opened the notebook to a fresh page. "When she does, she'll be walking into a web. Unless the web has moths in it. Unless some of the threads, when she touches them, turn out to be ours."

"You want me to tell you where she went."

"I want you to tell me *everything*. Where she went, why, what she carries, what she fears, what she promised and to whom. Not so we can sell it. So we can protect it. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire reason I'm sitting in a cell talking to a runaway instead of sleeping in my bed." She met his eyes. "In exchange: your whereabouts stay quiet. Not your location — your *whereabouts*. The log will say feverish, the log will say transferred to the infirmary, the log will say released to a charity house when you're well enough. Brannick will chase a boy who no longer exists in paper. And Coral Dunmore's trail goes cold in this city, because the only complete account of it will live in a ledger that has never once been sold, altered, or surrendered. Not in eleven years. Not under threat. Not for money."

"Why should I believe you?"

"You shouldn't," Odile said promptly. "Belief is for priests and fools. Check me. Ask the laundry women who brings them extra bread in hard winters. Ask the night-soil men who never get reported for the coins they skim. Ask why the widow on Tallow Street still has her house when the guild called her husband's debt three years ago. We're not heroes. Heroes get statues. We're clerks. We get *results*, slowly, and then we die, and someone else writes it down." She held out the pencil. "Now. Testimony is a bargain, and bargains should be written. Tell me what happened, from the ford onward, and I'll write it in front of you, and you'll watch every word, and when it's done you'll initial the margin — not your true name, your mother's name for you, the one nobody else knows. That way the record binds you without exposing you. That's how we do it. That's how it has to be done, if it's to survive us."

Tam looked at the pencil stub for a long moment. Then he started talking.

He talked about the ford and the dying courier, about the map that wouldn't burn, about the hedge-witch and the thirteen turns. He talked about Ashvael and the lilac smell of blood, about the Weeping Woods and what the trees had shown him — and here his voice cracked, because the trees had shown him Coral's worst memory, the one she'd stolen from herself, and he described how she'd waited for him to flinch and he hadn't, and how that moment had felt more binding than any oath. Odile wrote it all down, unhurried, her pencil never stopping, and twice she stopped him to ask a question no enforcer would ever think to ask: *what did she say, exactly? what words, in what order?*

"Words are evidence," she said. "Everything else is weather."

Near the end, he told her about the alley — how Coral had looked at him, how he'd run, how the net had taken him. And then, because it seemed suddenly important that someone besides him hold it, he said: "She didn't leave me behind to save herself. She left me behind because I asked her to. I said I'd slow them down. She didn't want me to. She—" He stopped. Started again. "She hates costing people things. It's the only thing she hates more than what she used to be."

Odile wrote that down too, verbatim, and underlined it.

* * *

It was nearly dawn when she finished. She blew on the ink, closed the notebook, and tucked it away in the lining of her robe, where Tam noticed — really noticed, for the first time — that the lining was sewn with pockets upon pockets upon pockets, a garment built over years into an archive you could wear.

"There," she said. "One boy's testimony, entered into the record of a court that has no throne, no seal, and no power except that it does not lie." She rose, joints cracking, and retrieved her lamp. "Brannick will find a feverish prisoner at dawn, and a transfer order by noon, and by tomorrow night you'll be out through the tannery route with a face that isn't yours and papers that say you're a factor's apprentice from the coast. After that, boy, you have a choice to make, same as everyone who learns we exist. You can go home to your tinkering and forget us. Or—"

"Or I write things down," Tam said.

"Or you write things down." Something moved across her face that was almost a smile. "Fair warning: it's a poor life. No glory, no reward, and the powerful never thank you. You spend decades being nobody, and the best you can hope for is that someday, when one of them stands in front of people who finally know everything, they look into the crowd for the face that ruined them, and they can't find it, because it was never one face. It was hundreds. It was laundry women and night-soil men and a disgraced clerk in a basement." She lifted the lamp. "Ordinary stubbornness, tinker's boy. It's the only justice that's ever lasted."

She was at the door when he spoke.

"The ledger," Tam said. "All those broken oaths. All those names." He swallowed. "Would it have room for a thirteenth keeper?"

Odile Fenner went very still.

Then she turned, and the lamplight made her eyes look young again, bright and sharp as a newly cut nib.

"Boy," she said softly, "the ledger has been waiting eleven years for someone to ask me that question. Get some sleep. Tomorrow we have work — because if the Oathstone is moving, then every oath in this city is about to come due, and someone had better be writing down who pays."
