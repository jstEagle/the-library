## 13. The Hunt on the Salt Flats

The salt flats began where the world forgot to be anything else.

Coral had walked three days since the Weeping Woods, and the trees had thinned first to scrub, then to nothing at all, until the land lay open like a held breath — white crust stretching to every horizon, cracked into hexagonal plates that rang faintly under her boots, as if she walked on the skin of a drum some enormous hand was waiting to strike. There was no shade. There was no cover. There was only the light, which came down without mercy and bounced up again from the salt so that she was burned from above and below at once, a moth pinned between two flames.

Tam had wanted to come. She had made him stay behind in the last stand of wind-bent pines with the horses and the map — no, not the map. She carried the map now; she could not explain why, except that it had begun to feel less like an object and more like an organ, something her body would notice the absence of. She had told him to stay because he was slow on a bad ankle and because if she did not survive the day, someone needed to carry the truth of it forward.

She had not told him the third reason, which was that she was tired of people being taken because of her.

The flats gave her nowhere to hide, and she understood, by the second hour of walking, that this was the point. Someone had chosen this ground. Someone had arranged for her to cross it.

She felt him before she saw him — a wrongness in the air, the way the Hollow used to feel wrong when a traveler crossed the boundary carrying magic in their blood like swallowed riverstones. She turned, and there he was, perhaps two hundred paces out, walking toward her across the white with the unhurried gait of a man arriving at a scheduled appointment.

Wren Vael wore no armor. He wore a plain coat the color of wet slate, dust to the knee, and his sword rode his hip like a sentence already written. His hands were empty and visible, palms slightly forward, the universal grammar of *I mean you no harm* — and Coral had learned enough about Wren Vael to know that every gesture he made was grammar, deliberate, parsed, correct.

"Inquisitor," she said when he was near enough. Her voice came out flat and dry as the ground.

"Keeper." He inclined his head. "Or should I say Coral Dunmore, of the Hollow? I find I prefer names. Titles are how we stop seeing people."

"You stopped seeing me the moment you decided what I was."

"Did I?" He looked at her with those pale, patient eyes, and the terrible thing — the thing she had been refusing to look at since the woods — was that he seemed genuinely to consider the question. "I've been reviewing my notes. Three weeks of them. And I find I still don't know what you are. That's rare. I don't enjoy it."

The sun hammered down. Sweat crawled under her collar. She kept her weight balanced, her right hand loose near her knife, and watched his hands the way Maghda had taught her to watch a customer's — not the eyes, which lie professionally, but the hands, which only know how to tell the truth a moment before the body commits to it.

"There's no one here to arrest," she said. "No witnesses. No guild. Just salt."

"No," Vael agreed. "Just salt. Which is why I can speak plainly, and why, I suspect, you'll finally answer honestly. You've been running through country full of people who'd shelter you or sell you, and either way you never have to stop. Here—" he spread one hand at the blinding emptiness— "there is nothing between us. Not even lies. Salt doesn't hold a shape."

"You didn't ride three days across a desert to talk about terrain."

"No." He sighed, and it was such a human sound, such a tired sound, that her grip on the knife loosened despite herself. "I rode it because you won't stop running, and I won't stop following, and one of us has to end it. So let me make you an offer, Coral Dunmore, and let me make it in the open, where no one can claim coercion later."

"I've heard offers. From a fae lord. They were prettier than yours will be."

"I doubt it. Fae dress wounds in silk. I'm going to hand you the wound itself." He clasped his hands behind his back — deliberately, she noted, disarming himself of even the appearance of threat — and began, in the level voice of a man reading charges, to tell her the truth.

"The Oathstone was stolen from the fae four hundred years ago. You may know this. What you don't know is what happened after. For a century, the courts raged and raided, and ten thousand people died along the borders — villages burned in retaliation for villages burned, tit-for-tat until the maps were more ash than ink. Then the Truce of Greywater: the stone stays hidden, the courts stay east of the mountains, humankind keeps its little kingdoms and its little lives. Four hundred years of peace, Dunmore. Farmers who never saw a fae raid. Children who grew old. All of it resting on one condition — that the stone remain lost, unclaimed, unspoken of."

"And now I've spoken of it."

"Now the courier spoke of it, dying, in a valley full of memory-brokers, and now a thief carries his map toward the one place in the world where its secret can't stay buried." His voice didn't rise. That was the horror of it. It stayed level as a carpenter's line. "Every faction that ever wanted the stone has heard the same whispers you have. Within a season, there will be an army on each road leading to it. And when they arrive — when the crown arrives, when Ashvael's host arrives, when the guilds arrive with their hired steel — do you know what happens to the Truce?"

"It breaks."

"It shatters. And the war that follows makes the border wars look like a tavern brawl, because this time both sides will believe the other stole first, and both sides will be right, and neither will ever stop." He took a step closer. She did not retreat, though every nerve in her legs asked her to. "Unless the stone passes to a single legitimate authority. The crown. Mine. Reforged under law, bound by charter, held in trust. Order, Dunmore. One hand on the hilt instead of twelve knives at each other's throats."

"And your hand holds it."

"Somebody's must."

The salt sang faintly beneath them, that strange ringing, as if the ground itself were tuning up for violence. Coral found she was angry — not the hot flash-anger of ambush but a cold, rising flood of it, the anger of a woman who had spent her life watching powerful people explain why theft was necessary.

"You want to know what I see?" she said. "I see a man describing a lock and calling it a door. Your order — your *charter*, your *trust* — it's just theft wearing a crown. The stone was stolen once, by desperate people, and everything good that came after was built on top of the stealing, and now you want to steal it *legally*, file the paperwork, hang the banners, and call four hundred years of quiet a *success*." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "I grew up in a town that runs on exactly that logic, Inquisitor. We call it the Hollow. We take memories from travelers because otherwise we starve, and we tell ourselves the taking is necessary, and the necessity becomes the law, and the law becomes the virtue. Order built on theft is just theft with banners. I've lived inside its shadow my whole life. I know the smell of it."

Something moved across Vael's face. Not anger. Something older and more tired.

"You think I haven't stood where you're standing?" he said quietly. "You think I came to this work believing in it? I was a border boy, Dunmore. I saw what the last fae raid left of Hallowmere — I was nine years old, and I pulled my sister out from under a beam, and she was the only one under any beam we got back whole. Order isn't an abstraction to me. It's the difference between that village and every village after it. Yes, the peace was bought with a theft. Yes, the crown is hungry and the law is a blunt instrument and men like me are the instrument's edge. But show me the alternative. Show me the world where everyone keeps their own stone, their own sword, their own truth, and tell me how long the farmers last."

"The alternative isn't everyone grabbing. The alternative is putting it down."

"Putting it where? In whose keeping? A thing like that doesn't rest. It waits." He shook his head slowly. "You're going to do something with it. I've read your trail — the broker you lied to, the woods you crossed, the boy you've collected. You're not running away from the stone, you're running *toward* it, and every step is a decision you haven't admitted to making. So I'm asking you. Give it to the law. Let me carry the weight. That's the offer. That's the whole offer, and I swear to you on my sister's grave it's the only offer anyone in this war will make you that doesn't end with you used."

For one moment — one treacherous, sun-struck moment — Coral almost believed him. That was the worst of it. Not his blade, not his office, not the cold machinery of the inquisition, but this: that he meant it. That somewhere under the slate coat and the pale patience was a nine-year-old boy under a fallen roof, and everything he had built himself into was load-bearing around that wound. She knew the architecture. She had built the same kind of house herself, over the memory she'd taken from her own childhood, and she knew how strong such walls were, and how much of the world they kept out.

"I believe you mean it," she said, and watched him flinch, very slightly, at the generosity. "That's what frightens me. Monsters I could run from. But you'd build your order on top of my bones and sleep well, and visit your sister's grave, and call it justice. And in two hundred years someone would stand where I'm standing and tell your successor the same things, and he'd mean it too."

Vael exhaled through his nose. "Then we're past words."

"We were always past words. The words were courtesy."

He drew.

She had expected speed and got stillness instead — he simply flowed into guard, blade low, weight even, an economy of motion that told her everything about how many duels he had ended quickly. Coral drew her own knife and the short blade Tam had pressed on her at the forest's edge, and set her feet in the stance of a soldier whose funeral memory she had once sold — muscle borrowed from a dead man's grief, guiding her living hands.

"I know that guard," Vael said. "Third Border Regiment. Where did you get it?"

"From a father who buried his daughter," she said. "Want to know how I got it?"

His jaw tightened. "That's the other thing about you, Dunmore. You fight dirty in ways I can't outlaw."

They came together.

The first exchange taught her she could not win. His blade found hers twice in the space of a breath, and each parry rang up her arm like the salt ringing underfoot, and he wasn't even breathing hard. She gave ground, circling, boots cracking the hexagonal plates, sweat stinging her eyes, and understood that her survival would not come from skill. It would come from the one resource she had that he did not.

So she fought him with truths.

"You pulled your sister from the rubble," she said, circling, blades whispering. "Who pulled the children out from under our beams? When the crown comes for the stone, who burns? Because it's never the men who make the order. It's the Hollows. It's the valleys. It's the places like where I was born, that exist so places like yours don't have to look at what order costs."

"Keep talking," he said, pressing, "it saves me chasing you."

"My mother sold her own wedding day to feed me. Did your charter account for her? Or was she acceptable losses?"

He struck high; she caught it and the impact numbed her forearm to the elbow. "Your mother chose to sell. That's grief, not tyranny."

"She chose because the choice was all she was left! That's what your order does — it takes everything and then congratulates you on your freedom to sell the rest!" She was crying now, she realized, and hated it, and used it, letting the tears blur her face into something younger and more wounded than she was, giving him a target for his chivalry. Men like Vael had a chivalry; it was load-bearing too.

It worked. He hesitated — half a heartbeat, a softening at the edge of his guard, the instinct of a man who did not enjoy cutting down weeping women — and she threw herself sideways off the exchange, rolled across the ringing salt, and ran.

Not away. Across.

The map against her ribs had grown warm, and she had noticed, these last days, what it did near water, near crossings, near thresholds: it *pulled*. And the salt flats, for all their emptiness, had one feature — a line of brine pools at the far edge where the crust gave way to shallow, mirror-still water, each pool ringed with crystal blooms sharp as broken glass. She ran for them with Vael's boots hammering behind her, and the sun off the salt turned the world into a white roar, and she thought absurdly of the Hollow, of market days, of Maghda's scales, of every small gray safe thing she had traded away.

Her boot went through the crust at the first pool. She fell hard, salt crystals scoring her palm, and Vael was on her before she could rise — his blade at her throat, his breath ragged, his eyes furious and something else underneath the fury.

"Yield," he said. "Yield, and I give you my word you'll be treated fairly. Hearing, counsel, a cell with a window. My word, Dunmore. I keep my word. It's the only currency I have left."

And there it was again — the conviction, naked now, stripped of courtesy. He would do it, too. He would march her a thousand miles in chains and write his report in a fair hand and lose sleep over her, and the stone would go to the crown, and the order would hold another century, and somewhere a girl in a valley would grow up selling pieces of strangers because the alternative was hunger, forever, amen.

"The last man who held me at blade-point and offered fair treatment," Coral said, "was a broker's enforcer. He also kept his word." Her hand, bleeding, closed around a shard of salt crystal the length of a knife. "You want my truth, Inquisitor? Here's all of it. I don't know what I'm going to do with the stone. I don't have a charter or a court or a plan. All I have is that everyone who's certain — you, Ashvael, the guilds — scares me worse than the uncertainty does. Maybe that's naivety. Maybe it's the only honest thing left on these flats."

"Then yield anyway," he said, quieter. "Let the law decide if you're naive."

"The law decided about my mother."

She drove the salt shard into his sword-arm, below the elbow, where the coat was thinnest. It was not deep — she felt it catch, felt him grunt more in surprise than pain — but his grip broke for half a second, and half a second was a country she had learned to live in. She twisted free, scrambled up the far bank of the pool, and ran, salt biting her sliced palm, blood warming her sleeve where his blade had opened her shoulder on the way past — she hadn't even felt it happen.

He did not chase. She heard him stop, heard his boots crunch to stillness, and when she dared a glance back he was standing among the brine pools holding his wounded arm, watching her go, and he did not shout threats or curses. He called out, once, in a voice that carried flat and clear across the white:

"Dunmore! Whatever you choose — choose it awake!"

Then the heat-haze took him, and she ran until the salt gave way to scrub and the scrub to stunted trees, and only then did she let herself fall against a trunk and shake.

* * *

She bound her shoulder with strips of shirt and sat bleeding gently in the green dusk while the pines whispered overhead. The wound was shallow. The wound in everything else was not.

She had seen him. That was the ruin of it. Not the inquisition's mask, not the crown's instrument, but a man — a border boy with a dead village folded inside him like a letter he reread every night, sincere all the way down, and sincerity, she now understood, was the most dangerous thing she had ever encountered. Ashvael she could refuse, because Ashvael wanted and wanting could be starved. But Vael *believed*, and belief could not be starved, only answered, and she had no answer yet. Only a map, a wound, a boy waiting in the trees with the horses, and a growing suspicion that the stone would not wait to be argued over.

Because as the light failed, she felt it — faint, unmistakable, coming from the direction she had forbidden Tam to travel: the map's pull, warm against her ribs, bending south.

Toward her. Toward the boy. Toward something else that had found him first.

Coral stood up, swaying, and started back into the dark at a stumbling run.
