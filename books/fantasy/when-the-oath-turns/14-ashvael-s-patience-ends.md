## 14. Ashvael's Patience Ends

The soldiers found her at dusk on the third day after the salt flats, in a fold of hills where the heather grew tall enough to swallow a kneeling woman whole.

Coral heard them before she saw them — the wrongness of hoofbeats where no road ran, the creak of leather, a voice giving quiet orders in the clipped cadence of crown troops. She pressed herself flat into the heather and did not breathe. The wound Vael had given her along her ribs had stopped bleeding but not aching, and every heartbeat reminded her of it, a small dull hammer tapping out the hours.

There were six of them. She counted by sound: six horses moving in a wide arc, the way hunters move when they already know where the quarry lies and are only being thorough about it. Her hand closed around her thief's pick, which was a poor weapon against swords but was the only thing she owned that had never once failed her.

*They will ask questions first,* she told herself. *Vael's men ask questions first.*

She was wrong.

The first soldier came through the heather ten feet from her head, and he did not ask questions. He looked down at her with the flat patience of a man who had been promised a bonus for delivery intact, and said, "Up, then. Gently. The inquisitor wants you breathing."

Coral got up because there was nothing else to do it with — no slope to roll down, no stream to break scent, no crowd to vanish into. The Hollow had taught her a hundred ways to disappear among people and almost none among empty hills. She had left the only place that ever hid her, and this was the arithmetic of that choice: six swords, one bad rib, and a map case pressing warm against her spine like a second heart.

They bound her wrists in front of her, which she noted with a professional's interest — sloppy, or arrogant, or both — and set her on a led horse. The soldier who had found her rode close and kept glancing at her the way men glance at things they've been warned about.

"You're the thief," he said. Not a question either.

"I'm nobody," Coral said.

"Nobody doesn't get two lords and an inquisitor fighting over her." He shrugged. "Doesn't matter to me. I'm paid by the mile."

It was full dark when the world ended.

Coral felt it first as a change in the horses — all six of them stopping at once, ears swiveling forward, breath suddenly loud in the silence. Then the smell: lilac, faint and cold, threading through the heather-sweet air like a needle through cloth. Her stomach dropped. She knew that smell now the way she knew her own name.

The soldier nearest her said, "What in the—" and then the light went strange.

It came up out of the ground, or perhaps down from the sky; afterward Coral could never say which. It was silver without whiteness, light the color of moonlight on still water, and where it touched the soldiers they simply *stopped* — not frozen, not dead, but paused, mid-gesture, like figures in a story someone had quit reading aloud. One man's sword arm hung in the air, half-raised. Another's mouth was open around a word he would finish, if he ever finished it, years from now or never.

Only the horses moved, and they moved sideways, away, with the deep animal wisdom of things that know when to leave.

Ashvael stepped out of the heather.

He looked, as always, as though he had dressed for a court and found himself inconvenienced by a battlefield — long coat the grey of ash over embers, hair dark as the space between stars, his face carrying that terrible courtesy that made courtesy itself feel like a threat. He surveyed the paused soldiers with mild distaste, the way a man surveys mud tracked across his floor.

"They'll wake in an hour," he said to Coral, "confused, and poorer in courage. They'll tell Lord Vael that fae took you, which is true, and he will hate it, which pleases me." His eyes came to hers, and they were the color of deep water over deep stone. "You are difficult to find, Coral Dunmore. You have gotten better at vanishing. I wonder where you learned it."

"The Hollow," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You know that."

"I know everything about you that matters." He gestured, and the ropes at her wrists loosened and slid away like living things abandoning a losing post. "Which brings us to the unpleasant portion of the evening. Walk with me."

"I'd rather not."

"Yes," he said, "I suspected. Walk with me anyway."

* * *

He did not touch her. That was the worst of it, Coral thought later — that he never once needed to touch her. He simply walked beside her up the hillside, under a sky salted with stars, and the paused soldiers shrank behind them, and the lilac smell wrapped around her like a coat she couldn't take off.

"You were cornered," Ashvael said. "Six blades, one wound, no road. In another hour you would be in Vael's tent, and he would have been gentle with you, because gentleness is his weapon the way terror is mine, and within a season the Oathstone would be reforged in a crown forge and worn like a signet ring by a king who has never kept a promise in his life." He glanced at her. "I have saved you. Say it."

"You've saved me," Coral said. "For a price."

"For a price." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a face that had one. "You learn quickly. Yes. My price is the map."

"No."

"Coral." He said her name the way other people said prayers, or curses. "You refused me once, on the river road, and I admired it. Refusal is rare in my experience; most creatures bend so smoothly they believe bending was their own idea. But I told you then what my justice requires, and I have been patient, and my patience has a shape and a measure, and we have reached the end of both." He stopped walking and turned to face her fully, and the starlight seemed to gather in him rather than fall on him. "Give me the map now, freely, and I will consider the debt between our peoples partially discharged, and you may go wherever your feet carry you. Or refuse again — and I will take what I want another way."

"Another way," she repeated. Her mouth had gone dry.

"Your memories." He said it simply, the way a merchant names a price. "One by one. I am told you know how such things are taken. You have made a trade of it, haven't you? Lifting the pasts off travelers and selling them to brokers. So you understand the mechanics better than most. The difference is only one of scale." His voice stayed soft, almost kind, which made it obscene. "I will take them slowly. I will leave you enough to walk and eat and answer questions. And when I am finished there will be a woman standing in front of me who has the map's location written somewhere in what remains of her, and no self left to grieve what I removed to find it. Nothing of Coral Dunmore will remain except usefulness."

The wind moved through the heather. Somewhere far off, a nightjar called, indifferent.

"You call that justice," Coral said. "A debt paid in human years. Your court's fine old law. And your law lets you hollow out a person like a gourd."

"My law let your ancestors' theft stand unpunished for three hundred years while my people withered," Ashvael said, and for just a moment the courtesy cracked and something older showed through, something vast and wounded and furious. "Do not lecture me on cruelty, thief. I have watched a whole court fade like frost in sun because humans could not bear to keep their word. I have earned my patience. Do not make me spend it."

Coral stood very still. Below them, the paused soldiers gleamed faintly in the strange light. Above them, the thirteenth moon — not yet risen, but coming, always coming — waited beyond the horizon like a held breath.

And in the space between those two facts, Coral Dunmore did the thing she would carry in her chest like a swallowed coal for the rest of her life.

She thought: *I can't beat him. I can't outrun him. I can't give him the truth.*

And then she thought, with the cold smooth competence that had fed her for twenty years: *But I know memories. I have handled hundreds of them. I know how they're shaped, how they're weighted, how they feel from the inside. And I know how to make a thing look true.*

"I'll give you something," she said.

Ashvael's head tilted, birdlike, interested. "The map?"

"A memory instead. A fair trade — a memory for a memory's worth of mercy. Time. Days. Enough for me to reach the stone and decide whose hands it belongs in, yours or the crown's or no one's." She lifted her chin. "You want the location. I'm offering you the next best thing: me, unharmed, moving toward it, with a debt ledger balanced on my side. Take the map by force and you get paper. Take my bargain and you get a keeper walking willingly to your prize."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lilac smell thickened. She felt him looking *into* her, not at her — felt that gaze like cold water poured slowly down the inside of her skull, reading her the way Maghda used to read the weight of a memory by holding it to the light.

"You are offering to bargain," he said at last, "with a lord of the Lilac Court. On a hillside. With bound hands an hour ago and a hole in your side." Something that was almost admiration crossed his face. "You are either the bravest human I have met in a century or the stupidest, and I genuinely cannot tell."

"Neither," Coral said. "Just a thief. We do this sort of thing all the time."

* * *

Here is what Coral had learned in twenty years of lifting memories, and here is what she used now, standing under the stars with her heart hammering its guilt-rhythm against her broken rib:

Memories are not facts. They are *textures*. A memory of grief is heavy and wet-edged; a memory of joy is bright and thin as glass; a memory of fear tastes of iron. And a skilled handler — a broker's apprentice, a valley thief — learns to feel these textures with her fingers before her mind ever parses the content. Maghda had trained her to sort stolen pasts blindfolded, by weight alone.

And Coral had carried stolen fragments for years. Discarded slivers, the leavings brokers didn't pay for — a stranger's wedding morning, a child's fear of deep water, an old man's last sight of his mother, a soldier's memory of snow on a battlefield. She had never kept any of them, per her rule. But keeping and knowing are different things, and she knew each fragment the way a seamstress knows scraps in her basket.

She could weave them. Not well — not the way a broker wove a false past for a client fleeing debtors — but well enough. Well enough for a creature who read memories the way she read lock mechanisms: by feel, by weight, by the honest lie of good craftsmanship.

She knelt in the heather, palms open, and built it.

She took the wedding morning and dyed it darker, folding in the child's fear until the joy trembled. She took the old man's dying mother and set her in a room of white stone, and around the room she laid the soldier's snow, falling silent and endless. And at the center — because every memory needs a heart, and a forgery needs its best work there — she placed the map. Not the real one. The *idea* of it, the shape of it as she had first seen it in the courier's bloodstained hands, and around that shape she wrapped the strongest thing she had: her own genuine dread, harvested honestly from this very hillside, this very hour. A memory of Ashvael himself, offering bargains, and beneath him, buried in white stone, the Oathstone — its location rendered in the vague, dream-logic way locations render in borrowed memories. North. Beneath ruins. Water overhead. True enough to survive a casual reading. False enough to buy her days.

It was, she thought as she worked, the finest piece of theft she had ever committed. And she was the thing stolen from.

"There," she said, when it was done, holding it cupped in her hands like a candle flame. "This is what I offer. A memory of where the stone lies. Take it, and consider our account opened, not settled — I'll owe you the rest when I choose to pay it. That's how bargains work in the Hollow. Both sides leave wanting."

Ashvael looked at the little light in her hands for a long time.

Then he reached out — and this time he did touch her, fingertips to her brow, cool as riverbed stones — and drew it from her.

Coral felt it leave and nearly gasped, because she had woven it too well. For half a heartbeat, as it passed out of her, she *believed* it — believed her mother had died in a white room while snow fell, believed she had seen the stone sleeping under ruins with water singing overhead — and then it was gone and the belief went with it, leaving only the knowledge of what she'd done, which was somehow worse.

Ashvael held the memory up between two fingers. Starlight moved through it. His eyes narrowed, and Coral's whole life narrowed with them, every nerve in her body strung tight as wire.

"A white chamber," he murmured. "Water above. Ruins." He turned it, considering. "Old stone. Old grief woven through it — genuine grief, I think. You have hidden the true location inside a wrapping of true feeling. Clever. Crude in places—" and here his gaze flicked up and fixed on her with awful precision, "—but clever."

Her lungs forgot their work.

Then he closed his fist around the memory, and it sank into his palm like a coin into deep water, and he smiled — the first true smile she had seen on him, and it was worse than all his courtesy.

"Days, then," he said. "I will follow the thread of this memory north, and you will travel more slowly than you wish to, because you are wounded and burdened with conscience, both of which slow your kind wonderfully. When I find the chamber empty — if I find it empty — I will come back for you, Coral Dunmore. And then we will do it the other way. Slowly. One memory at a time, until nothing remains." He stepped back, and the paused soldiers' glow began to fade behind him, and the night resumed its ordinary shapes. "Run beautifully. I do so enjoy the chase when the quarry has teeth."

The lilac smell thinned, unraveled, was gone.

The horses screamed once, all together, and bolted into the dark. Down the hillside, six soldiers woke confused in the heather, and Coral was already running — not north, not yet, but away, downhill, anywhere — with her hand pressed to her ribs and her breath tearing and Tam's name rising in her throat like a prayer to a god she hadn't believed in until recently.

Because that was the thing she couldn't outrun, the thing that ran faster than soldiers or fae lords or shame:

She had done it. She had woven a lie out of stolen pieces and sold it to save herself, and it had *fit* her hand like it had always belonged there. Twenty years of never keeping what she took, of passing the pasts of strangers through her clean and untouched — and in one hour on one hillside she had become the broker, the forger, the thing the Hollow pretended it wasn't.

She stopped at the bottom of the hill, bent double, and was sick into the heather.

When she raised her head, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand, she saw a small figure detach itself from the shadow of a hawthorn tree — a tinker's boy with a pack too big for him and his crossbow held wrong, exactly the way she'd told him a hundred times not to hold it.

"You're bleeding," Tam said. "And you look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse," Coral managed. "I've *been* one." She straightened, and the map case pressed warm against her spine, and somewhere north, a lord of the Lilac Court was following a beautiful lie toward an empty grave. "Tam. How fast can you walk?"

"How fast do we need to?"

"Faster than a lie travels," she said, and turned north, into the dark, toward the Abbey of First Light and whatever was waiting underneath it.
