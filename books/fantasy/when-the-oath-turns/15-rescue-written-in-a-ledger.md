## 15. Rescue Written in a Ledger

* * *

The cell smelled of wet stone and old tallow, and Tam Vetch had learned, in four days of counting cracks in the ceiling, that a man can get used to anything except the waiting.

He sat with his back against the wall, knees drawn up, and did what he had done every night since the guild enforcers had thrown him into this cellar beneath the Scales: he recited. Not prayers — he'd never been a praying kind — but stories. The story of his mother's cart wheel squeaking on its left axle. The story of the peddler who paid him in buttons. The story of a girl with salt-grey eyes who walked like she owed the road nothing.

If they took everything else from him, they would not take those. He would say them aloud until his voice gave out, and then he'd say them again inside his skull where no clerk could confiscate them.

The lock turned at midnight. He knew it was midnight because he'd counted the bell tolls through the floor — eleven bells, then a long silence, then one more that came late and soft, like an apology.

A woman stood in the doorway holding a shuttered lantern. She was perhaps sixty, grey-haired, dressed in the drab brown of a guild clerk, which was to say she looked like furniture. In her other hand she carried a ledger bound in green leather, fat as a Bible, corners worn round by decades of thumbs.

"You're the tinker's boy," she said. It was not a question.

"Tam."

"Quill," she said. "Formerly senior clerk of oaths, formerly respected, currently a name scratched off a roster because I asked why the bounty sheets list children." She studied him with eyes the colour of dishwater, missing nothing. "You bargained with me for her silence. Coral Dunmore. You traded testimony about the enforcers' smuggling routes so I wouldn't sell her whereabouts to the highest bidder. That was brave and stupid, and I have decided it was mostly brave."

Tam got to his feet slowly. His legs had gone strange from sitting. "Is she all right?"

"She's alive, which is more than she was three days ago when the fae lord's people found her on the flats. Ask her yourself." Quill jerked her chin down the corridor. "Walk fast, talk quiet, and if anyone asks, you're my nephew and you're simple."

"I'm not simple."

"Then act it badly and convincingly." She was already moving, and Tam had no choice but to follow, because staying meant another night of counting cracks, and because there was something in the way she held the ledger — not like a book but like a shield, or a weapon, or both.

They went up through the cellar levels past doors he'd heard screaming behind once and never mentioned again. At the ground floor, Quill paused beside a rack of ledgers, pulled one at random, and handed it to him. "Carry that. A clerk and her nephew carrying records is invisible. A clerk and a boy carrying nothing is a question."

The streets of Verrin at midnight were rivers of fog and lamplight. They crossed three courtyards where guild men stood watch, and each time Quill simply walked through their sightlines with the unhurried boredom of a woman going home after a shift, and each time the guards' eyes slid off her as though she were a wall they'd seen ten thousand times. Invisibility, Tam realized, was not a spell. It was paperwork. It was being the thing everyone had agreed not to look at.

In the fourth courtyard a wagon waited, loaded with dye barrels, and behind it a man in an apron who nodded once at Quill and said, "Ledger's clean?"

"Cleaner than most," she said. She turned to Tam and pressed something into his hand — a slip of paper, folded small, sealed with a drop of plain wax. No crest. No mark. "When you find her, give her this. And tell her—" For the first time the old woman hesitated, and Tam saw the person underneath the clerk, someone who had spent forty years recording broken promises and had finally, quietly, begun keeping one of her own. "Tell her the ledger remembers mercy too. Tell her that's why I do this."

She climbed back toward the guildhall before he could answer, brown and drab and gone, like furniture walking itself home.

The wagon rolled out through the Salt Gate before dawn, Tam wedged between barrels of indigo dye that stained his sleeves blue and made him sneeze until his ribs ached. He unfolded the paper only once, by the light leaking under the canvas. It bore a single line in a cramped, careful hand:

*The thirteenth keeper goes to the Abbey of First Light. So does everyone else. Hurry.*

* * *

Coral woke to birdsong, which was wrong, because birdsong meant trees, and the last thing she remembered was salt.

She lay in a bed of bracken beneath a canopy of silver birches, and the light came down through them in long pale blades, and every breath she drew hurt like a lesson. Her side was bandaged — good work, tight and clean — and someone had set a clay cup of willow-bark tea within reach of her hand, along with a strip of dried venison arranged neatly on a leaf, the way you feed a stranger you hope will be grateful rather than furious.

Ashvael's soldiers had come out of nowhere on the flats. Or rather, Ashvael himself had, stepping between her and Wren Vael's crossbows with that terrible courtesy, saying words she couldn't now recall through the haze of blood loss, doing something to the air that made twenty armed men forget which direction they were pointing. Then lilac-scented dark, and waking here, alone, alive, and unrobbed.

Unrobbed. She checked anyway, hands moving over her coat with the reflex of thirty years. The map case was still against her ribs. The decoy memory — the false one she'd woven for Ashvael, stitched from stolen scraps — was still hidden where she kept it, behind her teeth, in the place memories lived when they weren't hers.

And the others. She counted them the way a miser counts coins. The soldier's daughter's funeral. The fisherman's wedding. The midwife's first birth. The weaver's mother singing. Forty-one stolen lives, carried so long she'd stopped noticing their weight, the way a woman stops noticing a stone in her shoe until someone asks why she limps.

She ate the venison. She drank the tea. She got up, gasped, swore, and started walking north, because the map's ink had crawled overnight into a new configuration, roads converging on a point marked with nothing at all — a blank space, a held breath, the Abbey of First Light.

That was where the strangers began.

She met the first one at a shrine barely bigger than an oven — a leaning cairn of stones beside the road, hung with ribbons faded to ghost-colour. An old man knelt there, farming hat in his hands, mouthing something over and over. As Coral passed, he looked up, and his face was wrecked, open, the face of a man standing in a burned house.

"Lost my boy," he said, to her or to the cairn or to nobody. "Two years since. Can't remember his laugh anymore. I keep reaching for it and it's just — gone."

Coral stopped. She didn't decide to stop; her feet did it without consulting her.

She thought: *I have a laugh like that.* Somewhere in the crowded dark of her she carried a father's memory of a son laughing — stolen off a traveler on the north road three years ago, sold or not sold, she couldn't even remember which broker. She had carried it through the Hollow's market days and never once thought of it as anything but stock.

"I might be able to help you," she said, and her voice came out rusty. "I don't know how to explain it. But if you'll let me—"

She touched his temple with two fingers, the way she'd done ten thousand times to travelers who never knew, and this time she pushed instead of pulled. It felt like opening a fist she'd forgotten she'd clenched. The memory went out of her and into him, whole: a summer evening, a boy on a fence rail laughing so hard he fell off into the nettles, the specific ridiculous sound of it, the father's own helpless laughter answering.

The old man made a noise like a hinge. He grabbed her hand and pressed it against his forehead and said thank you eleven times, and Coral stood there feeling something terrifying happen in her chest, which was lightness. Actual lightness. Like setting down a pack after years of forgetting you wore one.

"It was yours all along," she told him. "I was only keeping it warm."

By evening she'd given away three more. A young widow's memory of her husband dancing badly and joyfully at their wedding. A blind fiddler's memory of dawn over the sea — given to him secondhand, and he wept and said he'd take it, borrowed light was still light. A child's memory of a dog, returned to a woman whose dog had died before she could say goodbye properly, and Coral hadn't even known whose memory it originally was, only that it was time for it to go home somewhere.

Each release left her lighter and stranger. She had built her whole life on the rule: *never keep what you take.* She had thought the rule was armor. Now, giving things away freely, she understood it had also been a prison — because keeping nothing meant belonging to nothing, and hoarding stolen goods meant owning nothing either. This was the third way, and it hurt like a wound cleaning itself.

On the fourth morning, a voice said, "You're supposed to be harder to find than this."

Tam dropped out of a birch stand, blue-stained, sneezing, grinning, and Coral hit him hard enough to stagger before her brain caught up with her arms. Then she held onto him for longer than she meant to, breathing dye-smell and road dust and boy.

"Quill says hurry," he said into her shoulder. "She says everyone's going to the abbey. Everyone, Coral. All of them at once."

"I know." She pulled back. His face had a new line to it, around the mouth — the line a person gets from four days in a cell deciding not to break. "You shouldn't have come."

"You shouldn't have gotten stabbed."

"That was strategic."

"You're bleeding through your shirt right now."

They argued companionably the rest of the day, and it was, Coral realized somewhere past noon, the easiest conversation she'd ever had, precisely because it wasn't easy at all. He knew what she was. He'd watched her worst memory walk across her face in the Weeping Woods and hadn't flinched. There was no performance left to maintain, and the absence of it was like the absence of weight: vertigo and wonder tangled together.

At dusk they reached the edge of the Weeping Woods again, and Coral stopped at the tree line and made herself look at the trees that showed strangers their worst hours. She had twelve memories left now. Twelve, and each one a debt.

"Give them away?" Tam guessed, watching her face.

"All of them. Before we get there." She flexed her fingers. "I want to walk into that chamber owing nobody. Whatever happens under the abbey, I want it to be *my* choice, not a ledger balancing itself through me."

They entered the woods, and the trees began their whispering, showing Tam his mother's face and Coral the taste of an oath sworn at seven years old — but she was learning to hold those images loosely now, to let them pass through like weather. And at the woods' far edge, where the land rose toward a black spine of hills, they saw it for the first time: a ruin on the ridge, broken towers against the rising moon, and around it, small with distance but unmistakable —

Fires. Dozens. Three separate encampments ringing the abbey like wolves around a wounded stag, their banners unreadable in the dark but their intent perfectly clear.

"They really did all come," Tam breathed.

Below the nearest fires, a trumpet sounded — thin, cold, unmistakably royal.

Coral put her hand on the map case and felt the ink crawl beneath her palm, eager, hurrying, like a heart beating faster as it nears home.

"Then we stop being smugglers tonight," she said. "We walk in through the front door."
