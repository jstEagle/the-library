## 16. The Abbey of First Light

They smelled it before they saw it: old smoke soaked so deep into stone that a century of rain had never washed it out. The Abbey of First Light rose out of the moorland like the ribcage of some enormous patient animal, its arches open to the sky, its roof long since burned or fallen or both. Grass grew in the nave. Birds nested in the mouths of saints whose faces had been worn to smooth suggestion.

Coral stood at the broken gate with the map pressed flat against her chest, and for a long moment she could not make her legs move.

*You knew this place once,* she thought, though she didn't know how she knew. The memory Tam had seen in the Weeping Woods — the one she'd stolen from herself at seven years old — still lived behind whatever wall she'd built around it, but its edges leaked. She had dreamed of these arches three nights running now. In the dreams, she was small, and someone was holding her hand, and the hand was warm, and then it wasn't.

"You're doing the thing," Tam said beside her. "The thing where you stare at something until it stares back."

"I'm deciding," Coral said.

"Deciding what?"

"Whether I want to know." She looked down at him — he'd grown an inch since Verrin, or she'd simply stopped measuring him against the boy who'd followed her out of a bandit camp whining about his blisters. Dirt on his cheek, tinker's oil under his nails, the guild clerk's ledger wrapped in oilcloth and slung across his back like a second spine. "Once I touch that stone, there's no version of me that doesn't know what happened here. Whatever happened here."

Tam considered this with the grave practicality he brought to everything, from picking locks to choosing which of his father's proverbs to ignore. "There's no version of you that *doesn't* already know," he said finally. "That's been the whole problem, hasn't it? You've just been carrying it around unopened."

She almost laughed. Almost. "When did you get wise?"

"I've always been wise. You were too busy being tragic to notice." He bumped his shoulder against her arm and started forward through the gate, because that was Tam's answer to every threshold in the world: walk through it before it could develop opinions.

* * *

Inside, the abbey was louder than a ruin had any right to be.

Not with wind, though wind moved through the broken vaulting and made the grass bow in slow waves. Not with birds, though they startled up in gray flurries as they walked the nave. With voices.

Coral stopped dead three steps past the threshold.

"—swore on my mother's grave, I swear it, I *swear*—"

"—by crown and covenant, by blood and banner—"

"—I'll come back. Do you hear me? I will come back—"

Thin as smoke, layered as a market crowd, rising from everywhere and nowhere. The vows of the dead and the long-dead, hanging in the air of the ruined church like weather.

Tam's hand found hers without either of them deciding it should. His palm was damp. "Tell me you hear that too."

"Every oath ever broken under this roof," Coral said quietly. She understood it all at once, the way she understood maps — not learned but remembered, some inheritance waking in her blood. "It doesn't let them go. A vow sworn here is heard forever. And when it breaks—" she tilted her head, listening to a woman's voice repeating *I forgive you* over and over in a register that had curdled into its opposite somewhere along the centuries, "—it stays broken. Audibly. Forever."

"Cheerful architecture." But Tam's voice had gone small, and he didn't let go of her hand.

They followed the sound deeper. Past the rotted pews, their wood silvered and soft as skin. Past the font where rainwater had gathered, dark and mirror-still, and in which Coral caught a reflection that took her a heartbeat to recognize because the girl looking back wore no expression at all — the face she'd spent twenty years building, the numb competence Maghda used to call *a professional asset*. The girl in the water looked like someone waiting to be emptied.

Past the altar itself, cracked down the middle, and beyond it a stair going down into the hill.

The voices changed as they descended. Upstairs they had been human vows — weddings, oaths of office, soldiers' promises, mothers' bargains with indifferent gods. Down here the voices were older. Heavier. They spoke in languages Coral didn't know and yet understood the way you understand grief in any tongue. Some were fae — she knew that now, knew it by the lilac-and-iron undertone, the same scent that had clung to the dying courier. Others were older than fae, older than the word *oath*, and those made the hairs on her arms rise like hackles.

And threaded through all of them, one voice repeated that was not old at all.

*"Thirteenth keeper. Thirteenth keeper. Thirteenth—"*

The courier. Dead a season, buried in his shallow grave behind them, and still his last words circled this stairway like a moth that couldn't find the window.

"He's still here," Tam whispered.

"No." Coral shook her head slowly. "He's *heard* here. There's a difference." Though she was no longer sure there was.

* * *

The chamber at the bottom of the stair had no right to exist beneath a ruin this ruined.

It was round, and whole, and dry, its walls of pale stone unmarked by the centuries that had chewed everything above them. No torches burned, and none were needed: light came from everywhere, sourceless, the color of the moment just after dawn. And in the center of the floor, set into a basin of black stone worn glossy by ten thousand years of attention —

The Oathstone.

It was smaller than she had imagined. That was her first stupid, human thought. All of this — the war gathering on three horizons, the inquisitor's gentle terrible questions, the fae lord's century of patience, the couriers dead in ditches, the keepers hunted turn after turn like mayflies — all of it for a stone no bigger than a loaf of bread, no taller than her knee.

But it was beautiful. She would give it that. It was not gemlike; it did not glitter. It was the color of deep water over deep stone, and it moved, very slowly, the way clouds move if you watch long enough, and looking at it made Coral feel the way she felt at the top of the scree pass with the whole Hollow spread below her: that she was very small, and that smallness might be a kind of freedom.

Around the walls, carved into the pale stone in spirals, ran words in a dozen scripts. She recognized fragments. *So long as the stone holds. So long as neither hand takes. Balance given, balance kept.* The original truce, written where both courts and humankind could read it. The treaty the theft had shattered.

Every broken vow whispered in this room. Here, at the source, they weren't smoke. They were weight. Coral could feel them pressing on her shoulders like wet wool — the accumulated mass of every promise ever made within the stone's reach and then betrayed. Kings' coronation oaths, snapped like thread. Lovers' vows, rotted through. A child's promise to feed a dog, breaking, breaking, breaking, small and pitiful among the rest, and somehow that one hurt worst of all.

"Do you feel that?" she asked.

"Yeah." Tam's voice was hoarse. "Like the room knows things about me."

"It does. It knows things about everyone." She stepped closer to the basin. "It's the ledger underneath all ledgers. Your clerk friend keeps paper. This keeps truth."

"And you're really going to touch it."

"I'm really going to touch it."

"Because last time you touched something old and glowing, we lost two days and you cried about a woman named Sera whose face you'd borrowed, so forgive me if my enthusiasm has limits—"

"Tam." She turned to look at him fully. He fell quiet. "If what's in this stone changes me — if I come up those stairs different, or worse, if I don't come up at all — I need you to remember something for me."

His jaw set. "Name it."

"That I chose this. Nobody pushed me. Not the map, not the moon, not the courier's ghost, not you, not the factions circling outside. Me. Whatever else happens — Coral Dunmore of the Hollow chose to stop not-knowing." Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. Twenty years of practice at feeling nothing, it turned out, made a strange kind of steadiness. "Can you hold that?"

He swallowed. Nodded. Then, because he was Tam: "Also hold this." He dug in his pocket and pressed something into her free hand — a bent tin whistle, tinker-made, ugly as sin. "So you have a reason to come back. You still owe me a duet, and I refuse to be haunted by an unfinished song. Bad enough this place is full of ghosts already."

She laughed — actually laughed, once, sharp and bright in the whispering dark — and turned to the stone.

* * *

She reached out and laid her palm against it.

The world went white, then went true.

* * *

She stood in a valley that was not the Hollow — or rather, that *was* the Hollow, before. Green. Alive. Magic lay over it like sunlight, visible now that she knew what to look for: threads of gold in the streams, blossoms opening at a passing glance, children skipping stones that skipped back. Humankind and fae shared the ford at the valley's mouth without suspicion. There was a village where Coral's market square stood, and in it, people she suddenly, unbearably recognized — broad faces, dark hair, her mother's crooked smile on strangers' mouths. Her people. Her actual, literal ancestors.

Then came the famine.

She watched it arrive like weather: three harvests rotting, the gold threads in the water thinning to nothing, the fae withdrawing up-slope into their bright courts while human children went hollow-eyed and quiet. She watched desperation do what desperation does. She watched a council of starving villagers listen to a man — clever, hollowed-out himself, a hedge-mage who had lost his own family and could no longer bear a world that kept rules while his daughters starved — tell them that the truce was a cage, that the Oathstone in its shrine on the hill held enough power to green a hundred valleys, that the fae had plenty and simply would not share.

She wanted to scream at them. She knew how it ended. She screamed anyway, inside the memory, and no one heard, because no one ever hears.

They went up the hill by night. Forty of them, with rope and hammers and a sledge. The shrine's guardians — two fae wardens, young, courteous even in betrayal — asked them once to stop, and were killed for asking. Coral felt the wardens die through the stone's own senses, felt the truce snap like a snapped bone, felt the stone carried down the hill in a blanket like a stolen infant while the sky above the valley began, very slowly, to bruise.

And then — this was the part that broke every story she had ever been told — the magic didn't save them.

She watched them try. Watched them carry the stone to the famine fields and beg it, command it, sacrifice upon it. But the stone was not a granary. It was a *witness*. It held oaths; it did not grant wishes. Its power flowed only through keeping faith, and they had founded their claim on perjury and murder, and so the stone sat in their desperate hands as silent as river rock while the famine finished its work. Half the thieves died anyway. The survivors hid the stone beneath the new-built abbey — built, in their shame, as penance, its every vow meant to ring true forever — and swore their descendants to guard it and say nothing, and the lie hardened into history, and history hardened into scripture: *the fae stole the stone; the fae are thieves; our suffering is their crime.*

The fae came for it eventually. Of course they did. And the humans, who had forgotten their own crime inside two generations, met them with righteous fury, and the long war began, and both sides raised children on stories in which they were the wounded party, and the stone passed from hiding to hiding, keeper to keeper, twelve of them, each one hunted, each one human, each one carrying a burden they had inherited from a lie—

—and the thirteenth was a girl from a valley where magic couldn't follow, who stole memories because she had once, at seven years old, been brought here by her mother and made to swear an oath she didn't understand, and who had spent her whole life believing herself empty.

* * *

Coral came back to herself on her knees on the cold pale stone, gasping, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face, the tin whistle biting into her clenched fist.

Tam was crouched in front of her, white-faced, hands hovering an inch from her shoulders, afraid to touch. "Coral. Hey. Hey. You were gone an hour. You said — you kept saying *they stole it first*, over and over—"

"They did." Her voice came out wrecked. "We did. *My* people. My valley. Tam, the Hollow — the Hollow isn't a refuge from magic, it never was. It's the shrine's shadow. It's where the thieves hid." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Maghda sells other people's pasts in the town the original thieves built to forget their own."

"The fae lord said they were robbed," Tam said carefully. "In the woods. He said it and you didn't believe him."

"I didn't want to believe him." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "Ashvael told me the truth standing in a road and I filed it away as a lure, because the truth was unbearable and the lie had better posture." She stared at the stone in its basin, dimming now, settling, having given up its secret. Every simple story lay in pieces around her. The fae were not monsters. Humans were not victims. The inquisition's order, Ashvael's justice, the guild's commerce — all of them built atop a foundation theft had poured, each faction claiming the moral high ground of a hill that had been stolen ground from the very first night.

And the famine — the famine had been real. That was the worst of it. The thieves hadn't been wicked. They'd been starving, and afraid, and lied to by a grieving man with good rhetoric, and they had done a monstrous thing for reasons that would have sounded, in the telling, almost reasonable. Evil wasn't a face. Evil was hunger plus fear plus a story that let you sleep afterward.

"Coral," Tam said softly. "Your hands."

She looked down. Faint light was moving under her skin — gold threads, following her veins, the same gold that had run in the pre-famine streams. The stone had marked her. Or woken her. She couldn't tell which, and the distinction felt suddenly, urgently important.

Above them, faint but unmistakable, carried down the stair through a thousand years of broken vows: the sound of many boots on abbey stone, and a horn blown twice — the crown's pattern, three notes shortened to two, the signal Wren Vael's soldiers used to close a cordon.

And beneath it, thinner, sweeter, impossible to mistake: lilac.

Tam looked at her. "Tell me you have a plan."

Coral got to her feet. The light under her skin pulsed once, like a second heartbeat, learning her rhythm.

"No," she said. "But I know exactly what they all want. And for the first time in my life—" she closed her fist around the whistle, "—so do I."
