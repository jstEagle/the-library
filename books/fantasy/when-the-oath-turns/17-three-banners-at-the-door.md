## 17. Three Banners at the Door

The Oathstone sang.

Not with sound — the Hollow had taught Coral that magic had no obligation to be audible — but with something older than sound, a vibration in the marrow, in the wet meat of the heart, in the thin bones of the ear. It rose through the floor of the crypt like water through chalk, and it carried voices with it.

Every broken vow ever sworn beneath this roof still lived in the stone's keeping. She had known that since she first descended the abbey stairs three days ago. What she had not understood until now — sitting cross-legged on the cold flagstones with her back to the shattered altar, watching the thirteenth moon climb toward the broken oculus overhead — was how *many* vows there would be, and how they would not quiet for nightfall.

*I will come back for you.* A woman's voice, worn to a thread. *I swear it on my mother's grave.*

She hadn't. The vow frayed and dissolved, and another took its place, decades older, in a man's voice cracked with fever: *Only this once. Only to feed them. I swear I'll stop.*

He hadn't either.

Coral pulled her knees up and pressed her palms over her ears, which helped nothing, because the voices were not coming through her ears. They came up through the stone, through her sit bones, through the long bones of her legs, settling somewhere behind her sternum where she kept the small hoard of memories that were actually hers. She had learned, over these three days, to let them pass through without catching — a skill not so different from lifting a memory off a traveler's mind, only reversed. Instead of taking, she was learning to refuse.

*You were always going to be good at this place,* Tam had said last night, before climbing up to the abbey's ruined nave to keep watch. He'd meant it kindly. It had stung anyway.

Above her, through the ragged hole where the dome had fallen in on itself centuries ago, the moon was rising. Not full — the thirteenth turn of the season did not require fullness, the hedge-witch's marginalia said, only completion — but swollen and strange, the color of skimmed milk gone faintly gold, and it threw a column of light down through the oculus onto the crypt floor like a blade laid flat.

The Oathstone sat at the center of that column. Waiting.

It was smaller than she had imagined, all things considered. A stone the size of a loaf of bread, dark as wet slate, veined with lines of pale light that moved when she wasn't looking directly at them — the same trick as the map's crawling ink, and she wondered now if the courier's map had been made from the stone, or the stone from the map, or whether the distinction even mattered for a thing that had spent six centuries being argued over, fought for, stolen, hidden, and blamed.

*Sworn and broken,* the voices murmured. *Sworn and broken. Sworn—*

"I know," Coral said aloud, and her voice sounded very small in the crypt. "Believe me. I've heard."

Her pick lay on the flagstones beside her knee. A thief's pick, bone-handled, its tip filed to a needle's point by a tinker's boy who had worked three nights by candlelight and refused, with surprising ferocity, to explain what he'd ground the temper with. Tam had handed it to her two days ago without ceremony, the way he did everything, as if offering her an apple.

"For whenever you decide," he'd said.

She looked at it now. It caught the moonlight along its edge, a single bright hairline.

Footsteps above. Tam's, quick and uneven — he'd twisted his ankle on the stairs yesterday and pretended he hadn't — crossing the nave toward the broken doorway. Then his voice, pitched low but carrying down through the oculus with terrible clarity:

"Coral. You need to come see this."

* * *

She climbed the stairs and stood in the nave beside him, in the shadow of the west wall where the roof still held, and looked out through the doorway across the abbey's drowned courtyard, and felt the bottom drop out of the night.

Three fires.

One in the meadow beyond the mill leat, burning high and clean, ringed by the orderly geometry of tents — crown troops, no mistaking them, the white-and-gold banners of House Vael hanging limp in the windless dark. She could count watch-fires and picket lines and the slow patrol of sentries whose armor caught the moon. Wren Vael had brought forty men at least. Perhaps sixty.

The second fire burned *wrong* — violet-hearted, smokeless, throwing shadows that leaned the wrong way — and around it stood figures too tall and too still to be anything but fae. They did not move like soldiers. They moved like trees deciding to be elsewhere. At their center, visible even at this distance by the silver of his hair and the pale gleam of lacquered mail, Lord Ashvael of the Lilac Court stood facing the abbey, motionless, and Coral would have sworn on any oath in that crypt below that he knew the exact moment she looked at him, because he inclined his head. A small courtesy. Across four hundred yards of ruined ground.

Her skin crawled.

The third fire was smallest and nearest, tucked into the lee of the collapsed bell tower, and it was barely a fire at all — a cook-smoke smudge, a scatter of bedrolls, half a dozen figures who might have been peddlers or pilgrims. Except that peddlers did not post lookouts in the branches of dead trees, and pilgrims did not carry crossbows, and neither wore the gray-and-iron sashes of the memory-brokers' guild.

"Hired swords," Tam said quietly. "Maghda's cousins. The clerk warned us the guild would come." He shifted his weight off his bad ankle. "They're not here to fight the other two. They're here to pick up whatever's left."

"And the other two are here to kill each other over what's left first."

"Or to make sure nobody has to fight at all." Tam glanced at her. His face in the moonlight was young and stubborn and frightened, and he was trying so hard to hide the fright that it made her chest ache. "That's the play, isn't it? Nobody fights if you just... give one of them what they want."

Below them, in the crypt, the broken vows went on murmuring like a tide.

Coral counted the hours left in her head, the way she'd once counted coins. Moonrise had been marked by the hedge-witch's almanac to the quarter-hour: the thirteenth turn completed at the moon's transit of the oculus, roughly four bells past midnight. Before then, the stone was only a stone with a sickness in it. After then — after then, whatever was written in six-hundred-year-old marginalia would come due, and every faction in that courtyard intended to be holding it when it did.

"Go wake the clerk's people," she said. "Tell them positions only. Nobody engages anyone."

"They know. Hesk says—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Hesk says it's going to come down to the door regardless. However careful everyone is. Three armies and one door, Coral."

"I know," she said. "Go."

He went, limping fast, and Coral stayed alone at the west wall with the moon climbing and three fires burning, and discovered that what she felt was not fear, exactly. It was the market-day feeling. The hollowness after. The whole shape of her life compressed into a single narrowing hour: everything she had run from and everything she had run toward, converging on a ruined abbey, and her standing at the door of it with a thief's pick and no plan worth the name.

*Everything except the choice,* she thought. That was the thing none of them had brought. Banners, armies, arguments, debts — but not the choice. The choice was hers alone, and it seemed to her suddenly, terribly funny that six centuries of war and scheming had all funneled down to a Hollow girl with calloused fingers standing in a ruin deciding whether to hand over the world or break it.

She didn't laugh. But it was close.

* * *

Wren Vael came at midnight, alone, under a white cloth held high on a spear-shaft.

Tam wanted to bar the door; Coral told him to open it. The inquisitor crossed the courtyard unhurried, boots crunching on fallen masonry, and stopped at the threshold where the moonlight ended, and waited to be invited in — which was such a perfectly calibrated gesture of courtesy that Coral felt her teeth set against it.

"You're outnumbered," Vael said. He looked tired. He had a fresh scar at his temple, salt-flat earned, and he wore it without embarrassment. "Sixty men. Fae. Guild knives. And you have a tinker's boy, a clerk, and four of the ledger people."

"You've miscounted," Coral said. "You also have yourself. That's always been your real army."

Something flickered in his face — appreciation, quickly mastered. He stepped into the nave and let the white cloth fall, and just like that they were enemies again, politely.

"I'm not here to threaten you," he said. "I've watched you refuse threats for five hundred miles. So here is something else." He drew a folded document from his breastplate — heavy paper, sealed twice — and laid it on the altar stump between them. "Full pardon. All of it. The courier, the map, the thefts in the Hollow, the broker's guild, the escape at the flats. Every charge, struck. Witnessed under the King's Own Seal."

Coral looked at the paper and did not touch it.

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, you place the stone in my hands at moonrise, alive and unbroken, and you walk away rich." He met her eyes. "I mean it. Rich. Landed. A name that opens doors. You'd never steal again because you'd never need to."

"That's what legitimacy looks like from inside a crown," Coral said. "A leash with a gold buckle."

"It's what *order* looks like," Vael said, and his voice dropped, and for a moment the polished courtier fell away and she saw the man on the salt flats again, bleeding and certain. "You think I don't understand what you want? Six hundred years of chaos, contained in one object, held by law instead of appetite. One hand. Accountable hands. Courts that answer to courts. Or—" he gestured, once, at the ruined world outside — "your way. A stone smashed, and everything it bound let loose into every hedge-witch and bandit king and desperate farmer with a grudge. Magic in every hand is not freedom, Coral. It's the famine again. It's the reason the stone was stolen in the first place."

"The stone was stolen because humans were starving," she said. "And the ones who stole it kept it for power anyway. Your own archives admit it. I've read the journal."

"My archives admit that desperate people do terrible things, and that the answer is law, not more desperation." He straightened. "You have until the moon crosses the oculus. I won't ask twice, and after tonight, no one will offer again. Whatever happens after that is on all our heads — mine included." He turned to go, then paused at the threshold, silhouetted against his distant banner. "For what it's worth — I believe you'd do it. Break it. I believed it on the flats. It's why I stopped underestimating you."

He left the pardon on the altar stump, gleaming like something freshly killed.

* * *

Ashvael did not send for her, and did not come to the door.

Instead, at some black hour past one bell, the lilac smell arrived — threading down the stairwell, impossibly sweet against the crypt's stone-and-candle reek — and Coral, descending to keep her vigil, found him already standing at the edge of the moon-column, regarding the Oathstone with an expression she had never seen on him before.

Reverence. And grief.

"You shouldn't be able to enter," she said. Her hand found the pick at her belt. "Consecrated ground."

"The consecration failed when the oath failed," Ashvael said softly. "Six hundred years ago. Nothing in this place has been holy since. Only haunted." He turned. In the moonlight his beauty was almost unbearable and entirely irrelevant; she had learned, on the road, to see past it the way you learn to see past a painted wall. "I have watched you carry my cousin's map through flood and fire and human treachery, keeper. I have offered you courtesy, and passage, and patience. Tonight my patience ends, and I find I regret it — which surprises me. You were supposed to be a means. You have become..." he paused, choosing, "...a disappointment I respect."

"I'll try to live with it."

"Do you know what I hear, standing here?" He touched the air, and the crypt's murmured vows swelled, a thousand broken promises rising like sediment in shaken water. "Every debt. Every perjury. My court's justice was built to collect exactly this. Return the stone, and every broken vow in the world becomes collectible again. Every liar pays. Every oath means something. Is that so monstrous a dream?"

"It's monstrous if you're the one holding the collection book," Coral said. "Your justice means human years, Ashvael. Human lives paid out to fae ledgers. I've read your terms."

"And I have read yours." His composure cracked, just slightly — the first true emotion she'd seen from him, and it frightened her more than all his serenity. "You intend to shatter it. Don't you? That little bone needle. You've been turning it in your fingers for three days like a woman telling beads." He stepped closer, out of the light, and his voice gentled into something worse than threat. "Then hear me, Coral Dunmore. If you break it, there will be no debt collected, no justice, no order — yes. But there will also be no *boundary*. The wall between the courts and your kind dissolves with it. Everything my people are, loose in your world, and everything yours in ours. You think the famine was bad? You have no idea what my grandmother's generation did to humanity the last time there were no walls. And I—" his voice caught, and he mastered it, and Coral realized with a jolt that it was genuine — "I would rather see the stone in a human king's fist than see those walls come down. Remember that I told you. Remember that I *asked*."

"Asked," Coral repeated. "While your host sits on my doorstep."

"Everything I have, I have brought to asking." He bowed — deep, formal, final. "At the moon's transit, keeper, I stop asking."

Lilac hung in the air long after he was gone.

* * *

The guild came last, and they did not knock.

Coral heard Tam shout from the nave, and came up the stairs two at a time to find three gray-sashed figures inside the west door — they had come through the collapsed cloister, quiet as the professional emptiness they sold — with Hesk and two of the clerk's network standing frozen over drawn blades, and the leader, a broad woman with a broker's ink-stained fingertips, spreading her empty hands.

"No steel," the woman said. "Guild rules. We're not here to fight over a rock." Her eyes found Coral, and there was something in them Coral recognized with a sick lurch: Maghda's expression, the day she'd offered a fortune for a map. Appraisal. Appetite. "We're here to make you an offer, Dunmore. Same one we'd make anyone holding stock this rare."

"There's nothing to buy."

"There's everything to buy." The woman smiled. "You think the guild wants the stone? Let the crowns and the fairies bleed for the rock. We want *you*. Thirteenth keeper. The girl who can lift a memory clean and leave no scar, who can weave a decoy so fine it fooled a fae lord. Do you know what the guilds would pay for that art, taught? For a *school* of it? Verrin, Corvassy, the island brokers — they're bidding already. Name your price. Safety through our lines tonight, whatever the inquisitor or the lilac lord intends, passage anywhere, wealth — vengeance, if you want it. We know who burned the twelfth keeper's trail. We can give you his name."

Behind her, Tam had gone very still, and Coral saw him doing the arithmetic she was doing: of all three offers, this was the only one that promised she would walk out of tonight alive and free. The crown would pardon her into a gilded cage. The fae would honor her until the walls came down. But the guild—

The guild would simply hollow her out slowly and sell the pieces, and call it a career.

"I spent my whole life selling strangers' pasts," Coral said quietly. "I'm not going to spend the rest teaching it."

The broker's smile faded by a degree. "Pity. Maghda said you'd say that. She also said you'd change your mind by moonrise, when you see what's actually coming through that door." She stepped back toward the cloister gap, and her easy confidence flickered for the first time. "Because you do know that's what all three of us are waiting for, girl? None of us trusts the others enough to move first. So we wait for the moon. And when it crosses your pretty hole in the roof, and the stone comes *due* — the marginalia doesn't say it goes to whoever holds it, does it? Read it again. It goes to whoever it *chooses*. And things that choose..." She shrugged. "Things that choose have appetites. Good luck with your needle."

They melted back through the cloister gap, and the nave was empty except for lamplight, moonlight, Tam, and the paper pardon still lying on the altar stump like a wound dressing.

* * *

Two bells past midnight. One hour, perhaps less.

Coral stood at the oculus's edge, where the moon-column struck the floor, and looked down through it into the crypt. The Oathstone's pale veins pulsed faster now, quickening like a heartbeat approaching exertion, and the murmur of broken vows had risen to something almost like surf, almost like applause, almost like a crowd.

Tam came to stand beside her. He didn't say anything for a while. Below them, in the courtyard, three fires burned, and sentries on all sides had begun to stir, and somewhere a horn called once, softly, and was answered from two directions.

"Coral." His voice was rough. "Whatever you're going to do. I want you to know I did the sums. All of them. Every offer, every outcome." He swallowed. "There wasn't one where you gave it to somebody that I could stand."

"I know," she said. "Me neither."

"So." He put his hand briefly on her shoulder — the first time he had ever touched her first — and squeezed, and let go. "Whenever you decide."

He went back down the stairs to the crypt, to his post, and Coral stayed at the rim of the moonlight with the pick cold against her hip, watching the moon slide toward the center of the broken sky.

The Oathstone's light changed.

All across its dark surface, the pale veins flared at once — and the murmur of broken vows cut off mid-word, total silence slamming down over the abbey like a dropped bell jar, and in that silence Coral heard, very clearly, from the heart of the crypt, a sound she had heard only once before in her life: the sound of something old waking up and taking an interest.

The stone began, gently, to rise from its pedestal.

Below, in the courtyard, all three camps erupted at once.
