## 18. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon rose the color of a wound gone cold, and Coral felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her sternum, like a hand pressing on the door of a house she had locked long ago.

Beneath her feet, the Oathstone sang.

Not with sound. With memory. The crypt of the Abbey of First Light had been humming since sundown, every broken vow ever sworn within a hundred leagues rising through the cracked flagstones like water through limestone. She could taste them. A sailor's promise to a wife he never went back to. A king's oath to a starving province, sworn and then quietly shelved. A mother's vow over a cradle, kept so faithfully it had worn a groove in the world. They pooled around her ankles, invisible and heavy as floodwater, and at their center, down three broken steps in a chamber shaped like a cupped hand, the stone sat on its pedestal of fused glass and waited.

It was smaller than she had imagined. Grey as riverbed slate, veined with silver, no larger than a loaf of bread. Thirteen facets. Of course thirteen.

*You could still walk away,* said the coward that lived behind her ribs. *Let them fight over it. Let them tear each other apart and take whatever's left.*

But she had seen what was left when powerful things fought over something small. She had grown up in the Hollow, where everything the valley had was what remained after someone bigger took what they wanted.

Coral crouched beside the pedestal and drew her thief's pick from the sheath at her wrist — a sliver of blackened iron, thin as a needle, worn smooth by ten years of other people's locks. It had opened shutters and strongboxes and one memorable bishop's correspondence. It had never been meant for this.

Behind her, far above, the abbey began to die.

She heard it first as a groan in the stones, then as shouting — muffled, distant, coming through three floors of ruin and a collapsed stair. Boots. Steel. The particular crystalline shriek of fae glamour tearing through mortar like wet paper. Three factions, converging, exactly as the map's last marginalia had promised: *when the thirteenth moon crowns the broken tower, all debts come due at once.*

"Come on," she whispered to the stone. "Tell me how this ends."

Warmth flooded up her arm the moment her fingers touched it.

* * *

She stood in a famine year, seven centuries deep.

A city of mud and smoke, walls too short for the hunger inside them. Humans — hollow-eyed, desperate, magnificent in their stubbornness — moving through streets where the fae markets glittered with grain that never ran out, fruit that blushed eternally, wine dark as secrets. All of it there. All of it priced. Not in coin. In years. In firstborn names. In memories, freely given and never returned.

The humans had begged first. That was in the stone's memory too — the petitions, the kneeling envoys, the fae lords' courteous refusals. *The terms are the terms.* A famine did not soften a bargain; it only thinned the customers.

So on the longest night, twelve of them had gone into the fae vaults. Twelve thieves, armed not with blades but with an oath sworn in blood and desperation — an oath so total, so absolute, that it had torn the Oathstone itself from the courts' keeping and bound its power into human hands. The stone had not been stolen by monsters. It had been stolen by starving people who had run out of every other option, who had sworn to share its power equally among all humankind —

— and then, one by one, in cellars and war-rooms, had quietly unsworn that part.

Coral surfaced from the vision gasping, her hand still on the cold facet, seven hundred years of accumulated betrayal sitting in her chest like swallowed river stones.

Every faction below her wanted the stone back into rightful keeping. But there was no rightful keeping. There never had been. The stone was an argument that had been going on since before her grandmother's grandmother: who deserved power, and how much of it, and who got to decide. The truce hadn't broken because the stone was stolen. The truce had broken because theft was the only language anyone spoke anymore.

And now they were all coming up the stairs to speak it again.

The ceiling of the crypt split open like ice on a spring river.

* * *

Light poured through — torchlight, moonglow, the sickly green of fae witchfire — and through the breach came Wren Vael first, dropping twenty feet and landing in a crouch that should have shattered his knees but didn't, because his crown had spent fortunes on enchantments and it showed. His white coat was scorched. His sword was drawn. Behind him, soldiers rappelled down on ropes, boots hammering the flags.

From the western stair came the guild's hired swords — six of them, mercenary-casual, the clerk's paper seal pinned to the lead man's chest like a license to be present.

And through the wall itself, walking through stone as though it were fog, came Ashvael.

The fae lord's beauty had always seemed to Coral like a held breath, and tonight he wasn't holding it anymore. His eyes found the stone. Then her. Something moved across his face that on a human would have been called grief.

"Coral Dunmore." His voice filled the chamber without effort. "Thirteenth keeper. Set it in my hands and I will see you safely across any border in the world for the rest of your natural life."

"The crown claims the Oathstone under lawful writ." Vael's voice was quieter and somehow louder. He was looking at her the way he had on the salt flats — with that terrible, genuine conviction. "Coral. I know what you've learned. I know what the stone showed you. Put it in my custody and we can *govern* what comes next instead of drowning in it."

The guild swordsman just grinned and spat. "Stone goes to our employer. Anyone disagreeing gets introduced to my disagreement."

Three banners. One small grey stone. And her, standing between them with a lockpick in her fist.

Tam was somewhere above — she'd made him stay topside, and for once in his life he'd listened, and she loved him a little for it and hoped desperately he was far enough away.

"The thing about oaths," Coral said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her, "is that I grew up in a place where they don't work. Magic-dead soil. Dead contracts. You could swear anything in the Hollow and it meant nothing but breath." She turned the pick over in her fingers. "Do you know what that taught me? That an oath is worthless unless you keep it when nobody's checking. And every single person in this room holding out their hands—" she looked at Vael, at Ashvael, at the mercenary, "—has already decided what happens to everyone else. Without asking."

"There is no fourth option, girl," Ashvael said gently. "Only three sets of hands."

"You're right." Coral smiled, and felt it pull at the scar on her cheek. "There isn't a fourth set of hands. So I'll use mine."

The moon crowned the broken tower. She felt it happen — a click behind the world, a tumbler falling into place, the thirteenth turn completing some mechanism older than any of them.

Every voice in the crypt rose at once. Vael lunging, shouting *wait*, and meaning it. Ashvael's hand coming up, words of binding gathering light around his fingers. The mercenary running.

Coral dropped to her knees beside the Oathstone, fitted her thief's pick against its heart-facet the way she'd fit it against a thousand lesser locks, and whispered the only oath she had ever sworn that she'd never once regretted:

*I don't keep what I take.*

She drove it in.

* * *

The world broke evenly.

That was the only way she could ever describe it afterward. Not like an explosion — explosions have centers, favorites, distances. The Oathstone shattering was fair. Light came out of it the way water comes out of a dammed river when the dam finally admits defeat, and it went *everywhere*, into everything, without asking permission or weighing worth.

Coral felt it pass through her and knew, in one vertiginous instant, what it was doing. Into the soldiers. Into Vael. Into Ashvael, who threw back his head and laughed — laughed, high and astonished, a sound with a century of dust on it. Into the rats in the walls and the worms in the earth and the dying ivy on the abbey's bones. Into Tam, topside, whatever he was doing. Into Maghda asleep in the Hollow, into every hedge-witch and broker and king and beggar, magic arriving in all of them at once, unowned, ungoverned, unasked-for, like rain.

Then the crypt remembered it had been broken for four hundred years, and agreed to lie down.

Stone became sand became silence. The ceiling came down soft, almost politely, the way snow falls, and the shockwave picked Coral up like a leaf and threw her through the collapsing dark, and the last thing she thought, tumbling weightless through the ruins of every oath ever broken, was that she couldn't feel her pick anymore, and that this was fine, and that she was —

* * *

Someone was dragging her by the armpits.

Grit in her mouth. Stars overhead — real stars, the actual sky, because the abbey's roof was gone, was *all* gone, was a fine grey powder drifting down over everything like ash over a burnt field. Her ears rang with a tone that wasn't a sound so much as an absence of all sounds, slowly letting the world back in.

"—oral. *Coral.* Blink, you idiot, blink if you're alive—"

She blinked.

Tam's face swam above hers, soot-streaked, wide-eyed, his tinker's hands shaking as he propped her against a fallen pillar. Behind him, where the Abbey of First Light had stood for four centuries, there was a crater full of moonlight and drifting dust. At its edges, figures were stirring — soldiers, mercenaries, shapes that might have been fae — all of them rising slowly, staring at their own hands, because their hands were glowing. Faintly. Equally. Every single one.

"It worked?" Her voice came out scraped raw. "What happened?"

Tam looked at her. Then at the crater. Then at his own palms, which were shimmering faintly gold, sparks crawling between his fingers like curious insects, and he started laughing — a slightly hysterical, entirely wonderful laugh.

"I think," he said, "you broke the whole world."

Coral leaned her head back against the pillar and looked up at the thirteenth moon, already sliding westward, already ordinary.

"No," she said, as the dust settled over them both like a blessing nobody had authorized. "I shared it."

Out across the crater, Wren Vael was on his knees in the ruins, staring at the light in his own hands. And Ashvael was walking toward her through the settling ash, smiling like something newly free — and Coral realized, with a lurch, that she had absolutely no idea what either of them intended to do next.
