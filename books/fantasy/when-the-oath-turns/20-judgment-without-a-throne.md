## 20. Judgment Without a Throne

They did not put her in a cell.

Coral thought that was the first strange mercy of it. After the abbey came down in thunder and white light — after Tam dragged her coughing through the collapsing dark with the shards of the Oathstone still singing in the air like struck bells that would not stop — she had expected chains, a sack over her head, a blade between her ribs while no one watched. That was how the powerful settled debts. She had spent her whole life learning how the powerful settled debts.

Instead they gave her a tent. A clean blanket. Water that tasted of snowmelt and, faintly, of lilac.

The camp sat on the scarred plain where the Abbey of First Light had stood for nine hundred years, and where it now stood for nothing at all. Three banners snapped in the wind above it: the crowned sun of Vael's king, the silver bough of Ashvael's court, and the plain grey flag of the clerk's people, who had no sigil because, as the old woman had said when Coral asked, *paper doesn't need one.* Between the banners, the ruins smoked gently, and every few hours a tremor ran through the ground as some deep-set oath finally finished unbinding itself, like knots loosening in a rope long after the hanging.

Magic was loose in the world. Coral could feel it pressing against the edges of her like weather. The guards who brought her food kept glancing at their own hands as if expecting them to glow. One boy's shadow moved a half-second out of step with him, and he flinched from it every time.

On the third morning, they came for her.

Not soldiers — or not only soldiers. Vael walked at the front in his grey coat, his inquisitor's chain gone from his neck; she noticed its absence before she noticed anything else about him. Behind him came two of his crown officers with faces like closed doors. Then Ashvael, unhurried, beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, his courtiers drifting behind him like leaves that had forgotten which tree they belonged to. And at the back, small and ink-stained and utterly unimpressed by all the finery around her, the clerk — Maghda's sister in trade but nothing else, Coral reminded herself — carrying her ledger under one arm the way a farmer carries a hen.

"This way," Vael said. His voice was careful. It had been careful since the salt flats, since she'd seen the exact moment his conviction cracked down the middle and showed him something underneath that he hadn't wanted to look at.

"Am I walking to my death?" Coral asked him, quietly, as she fell into step.

"You're walking to an accounting," Vael said. "What it becomes is not mine to decide anymore." He said it strangely, like a man describing a door that had been locked his whole life and had simply swung open overnight.

* * *

They had built the tribunal in what remained of the abbey's great hall — or rather, in the hall's ghost. One wall still stood, windowless and scorched, its stones bearing faint impressions of where stained glass had been. The others were rubble sorted into rough seats: flat slabs of fallen masonry arranged in a crescent, facing a broken pillar that someone had cleared off to serve as the place where a person stood to be judged.

Coral looked at the crescent of stone seats and understood, with a lurch, that there was no throne among them. No high seat. No chair taller than the rest. Whoever judged her this day would sit at her level, in the open air, under a sky that still smelled faintly of ozone and shattered oaths.

She was made to stand at the pillar. Her hands were left free. She almost wished they weren't; empty hands had nothing to hold onto.

Vael rose first. He had always been most comfortable standing.

"The crown's position is simple," he began, and his voice carried the cadence of a man who had argued law in cold rooms his entire adult life. "Coral Dunmore destroyed an artifact held in trust by the realm for nine hundred years. Whatever its provenance —" here his jaw tightened, because he knew the provenance now, everyone did, the truth had come out of the crypt with the shockwave like smoke from a breached fire — "the Oathstone anchored the truce between humankind and the fae courts. Truces are not truths. They are agreements, and agreements require terms, and terms require enforcement. What she did was abolish the treaty table and set fire to it while three parties sat around it."

"And replaced it with what?" Ashvael said softly, from his seat. He didn't stand. Fae lords didn't rise for human proceedings; it was a point of pride older than the abbey.

"With uncertainty," Vael said. "With a world where every border, every bargain, every sworn word must now be defended by strength alone. I have spent my career watching what happens when men are left to defend their own words by strength alone. It looks like famine. It looks like burned villages. It looks like the Hollow."

Coral felt that one land somewhere beneath her ribs.

"The crown does not ask for cruelty," Vael continued. "It asks for order. Execution would be lawful. Restitution is impossible — you cannot restore what has been scattered into every living thing. So the crown asks for judgment commensurate with the crime: the destruction of the single greatest instrument of peace between our peoples. Life imprisonment. Sealed, warded, documented. A warning written into the world so plainly that no one can pretend not to read it."

He sat down. He did not look at her while he spoke, and Coral found she couldn't decide whether that was mercy or cowardice, and suspected it might be both wearing each other's clothes.

* * *

Ashvael rose then — or rather, he simply stopped sitting, and gravity seemed to agree with him out of ancient courtesy. When he spoke, the wind quieted to listen.

"My position," he said, "is also simple. Also lawful. By the laws of my court, which are older than your crowns by the width of a mountain range, Coral Dunmore owes a debt." He turned to her, and his eyes were the pale violet of dusk over snow. "You took from me the chance to return what was stolen from us. For centuries my court planned restitution — the stone recovered, the truce renewed, the balance restored. Justice, performed properly, with ceremony. You have robbed us of our justice. In the Lilac Court, robbing someone of justice is itself a crime, and it is paid for in years."

"Whose years?" Coral asked. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her.

"Yours," Ashvael said. "Naturally." A ripple went through the fae contingent — not laughter exactly, but the memory of laughter, the shape of it without warmth. "A life for a loss. You will come to the Lilac Court, and you will serve until the debt is measured full. My clerks are very precise. It may be eighty years. It may be three hundred. Time moves differently where you would be going, which is fortunate for you, or unfortunate, depending on your perspective."

"And if I refuse?"

Ashvael smiled, and it was the smile from the Weeping Woods, courteous and terrifying, the smile of a thing that had waited a century and could wait a century more. "Then the debt stands unpaid, and unpaid debts accrue interest. Interest, in my court, is taken in memories. Yours, Coral Dunmore. All of them. One by one, beginning with the oldest." He paused delicately. "I understand you know precisely how that feels. To have your past lifted out of you and handed to a stranger who pays well for it."

The words went into her like a pick into a lock. She kept her face still. She'd had practice.

But Ashvael saw anyway — he always saw — and something flickered behind his beauty that might, in a creature capable of it, have been respect. He inclined his head a fraction and resumed his seat, and the wind remembered how to blow.

* * *

Then the clerk stood up, and everything changed temperature.

She was a small woman, sixty-odd, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles mended with wire, and she climbed onto her stone slab with the undignified grunt of any ordinary person getting up too fast. She set the ledger on her knees. It was enormous — a book bound in grey leather, swollen with inserted pages, corners soft from handling, the kind of book that gets carried through rain and war and still opens flat to the page it needs.

"I'm not going to make a speech," she said, to Coral, to the crescent of judges, to the sky. "Speeches are what people do when they don't have evidence. I have evidence."

She opened the ledger.

"Coral Dunmore," she read, "entered the record eleven years ago, age fourteen, first documented act: the theft of a memory belonging to a wool merchant named Aldous Frey. Memory sold to the broker Maghda for four marks and a winter coat. The merchant, per follow-up inquiry, forgot his mother's face." She looked up. "Do you deny it?"

"No," Coral said. Her voice was quiet in the ruined hall. There was nowhere to hide in a place like this, and besides, she had promised herself something on the road here, somewhere between the salt flats and the abbey, when she'd been returning stolen memories to strangers in exchange for bread and directions and once, memorably, a song: *if they ever catch me, I won't lie. Lying is how I got here.*

"Eleven years of entries," the clerk said. "Four hundred and six documented thefts. Some duplicated — the same memory taken twice from the same person, which is a particular cruelty, and noted as such. Sales recorded. Buyers recorded. Prices recorded." She turned pages. The sound of it filled the hall like slow rain.

Vael shifted. "Then the matter is—"

"I'm not finished," the clerk said, and Vael, remarkably, closed his mouth.

The clerk turned to a later section of the ledger, and Coral saw that the pages changed color — newer paper, different ink, entries in a hand that was not the clerk's own cramped script but a looser, younger one.

"Entries resume," the clerk read, "fourteen days ago. Not thefts. Returns. Coral Dunmore, traveling the east road, returned a stolen memory of a wedding to a woman who had been searching for it for six years. Returned a soldier's grief to a veteran camped outside Verrin — unprompted, unpaid. Returned a child's memory of drowning to a fisherman whose daughter—" The clerk stopped. Cleared her throat. Continued. "There are thirty-one such entries in fourteen days. Cross-referenced against her earlier sales wherever possible. In nine cases she sought out the original victims specifically. In the others, she gave memories back to strangers who had bought them honestly from brokers, absorbing the loss herself."

The clerk looked up over her wire-mended spectacles.

"There's more. Entry forty-four, current section: subject sheltered a fugitive tinker's boy at cost of capture. Entry fifty-one: subject surrendered herself rather than allow pursuit forces to search a refugee column. Entry fifty-eight —" and here the clerk's voice did something complicated — "subject returned, voluntarily, a memory she had stolen from a dying courier. It contained his daughter. She could have kept it. She gave it to his grave."

Silence. Somewhere out on the smoking plain, a bird sang — actually sang, a real bird doing a real bird thing, and the sound was absurd and holy in the wrecked hall.

"The ledger," the clerk said, closing the book with a thump like a door, "does not weigh intentions. Intentions are unfalsifiable. It weighs acts. And by weight of acts, Coral Dunmore's account stands thus: four hundred and six thefts, thirty-one returns, and — I count these separately, because they are a different category — seven acts of mercy performed at material risk to herself, none of which she could have predicted would ever be read aloud in front of anyone." She looked around the crescent of judges. "I have kept ledgers for forty years. Most people's books show only what they did when someone was watching. Hers shows what she did when no one was. Judge that however you like. But judge *that*."

* * *

"They'll want testimony," Vael said at last, into the silence. It wasn't quite a command. It sounded almost like hope.

And they brought Tam in.

He looked terrible. He'd been guild-held in Verrin for weeks before the clerk's network sprang him, and it showed in the hollows of his face and the way he favored his left side. But when he caught sight of Coral standing at the pillar, he grinned — that crooked, stubborn, entirely unrepentant grin — and something in her chest that had been clenched since the abbey fell loosened by one degree.

He stood where they told him to stand, which was near her, and he stuck his hands in his pockets like a boy waiting out a sermon.

"State your name and relation to the accused," the clerk said.

"Tam Wicker. Tinker's boy. Runaway. Professional nuisance." He glanced at Coral. "Friend, I'd say. If she'll have it on record."

"On record," the clerk said, writing.

"Ask him what he saw," Vael said quietly. "In the woods. Ask him what she carries."

Tam's jaw set. Coral knew what Vael meant — the Weeping Woods, the trees that showed strangers your worst memory, the night Tam had seen the thing she'd taken from herself at seven years old and buried deeper than any grave. Her secret. The oldest theft in her ledger, the one entry the clerk's book could never contain, because she'd never told a soul what it was.

Tam looked at Vael for a long moment. Then at Ashvael. Then at Coral.

"What I saw in the woods," Tam said, "was a girl who was scared of a memory so bad she'd cut it out of herself rather than carry it. And I saw that even holding a thing that awful, she never once treated me like cargo. Never sold me. Never used me." He shrugged. "People aren't their worst nights. I'm a tinker's son. I know mending. You don't judge the pot by the crack. You judge it by whether anybody bothered to mend it, and she's been mending — herself, other people, the whole rotten ledger — since the day I met her. Faster than I've ever seen anybody mend."

He turned to the crescent of judges and raised his chin.

"She broke your stone. Fine. Hang that on her. But ask yourselves what the stone was *for*, and ask what she gave everybody instead. Because I was there when it broke, and I felt it go through me like sunlight, and I'm nobody special. That's the point. She made it so *nobody's* special. Including the lot of you." He pointed a grimy finger at Ashvael, then Vael, then, apologetically, the clerk. "No offense."

"None taken," said the clerk dryly.

* * *

The judges conferred in low voices while the wind moved through the ruined hall and the light went from noon to the long gold of late afternoon. Coral stood at the pillar and let herself feel all of it — the fear, the exhaustion, the strangeness of being seen whole, acts and cracks and mends together, by strangers with no crown on any of their heads.

When they called the verdict, it was the clerk who read it, because it had been written in the ledger first, in fresh ink, dated that day.

"Not execution," she said. "Execution ends an account. This account should stay open — open accounts can accrue credit as well as debt. Not imprisonment; the world has enough cages, and we've just learned what happens when you build them around the wrong things." A glance at Coral, brief and unreadable. "Exile. From every court, every crown, every guild, every ledger-bound jurisdiction we can name. She belongs to none of us and none of us to her. She goes where she likes, answers to no banner, and lives or dies by her own conduct, which — per the record — is better than most of ours."

Vael exhaled slowly. It was not agreement. It was the sound of a man setting down something very heavy and not yet sure his back would forgive him.

Ashvael studied her across the ruined hall, and then, to Coral's astonishment, he laughed — short, startled, genuine, the first laugh she had ever heard from him and possibly, given the look on his courtiers' faces, the first in a century. "Debt unresolved, then," he murmured. "How delightfully untidy. Go, thief. Perhaps untidiness is its own justice."

The verdict was struck into the ledger. The crescent of judges rose — at her level, all of them, as they had sat.

Tam came to stand beside her, shoulder brushing hers, warm and solid and alive.

"So," he said, as the three banners snapped overhead and the broken horizon stretched out past the smoking abbey, gold going to violet going to dark. "Exile. Where does an exile go when there's nowhere she's allowed?"

Coral looked south, past the ruins, past the last marked border, to the blank space on the edge of every map she had ever seen — the space where, in the last two weeks, new ink had begun to crawl.

"Somewhere with no name yet," she said. "We'll be the first ones in the ledger."
