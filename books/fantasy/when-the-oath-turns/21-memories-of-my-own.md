## 21. Memories of My Own

The tribunal's verdict still hung in the air of the ruined abbey like incense: *exile*. Not a rope, not a blade, not the fae debt paid in years — just exile, pronounced by a disgraced clerk with a ledger full of other people's sins, witnessed by a broken inquisitor and a laughing fae lord and a tinker's boy who had stood up on a shattered crypt step and told the truth about her in a voice that cracked only once.

Exile was, Coral thought, the most honest sentence anyone had ever given her.

She spent the night after in what remained of the abbey's guesthouse, a roofless cell where the stars came down through broken vaulting and pooled on the floor like spilled milk. The walls that survived were furred with moss gone silver in the moonlight of the thirteenth turn — the moon now waning, its great work done, magic loose in the world like water from a smashed dam. She could feel it everywhere now. In the stones. In her own blood, humming faintly, a tuning fork struck against the sky.

And she could feel *it* — the last thing she carried.

It sat behind her ribs the way it always had, a small dense knot of not-her-ness, so old and so worn smooth by decades of carrying that she had stopped feeling it as foreign. That was the trick of it. That was the cruelty of it. A memory kept long enough becomes a bone. You build your whole skeleton around it and call the shape of yourself your own.

But it wasn't hers.

She had known that since the crypt, since the Oathstone had poured its ledger of broken vows through her like a river through an open door. She knew it the way you know a wall is load-bearing before you knock it down: with dread and certainty in equal measure.

Tam found her there at dawn, or what passed for dawn beneath the smoke-hazed sky — the light came gray and gentle now, gentler than it had any right to be after everything. He carried two chipped cups of something hot that smelled of rosemary and scorched barley, because the clerk's people fed everyone who passed through the ruins, exiles included. Especially exiles, maybe. Their network had opinions about who deserved feeding.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"I'm going to do something worse than not sleeping." She took the cup anyway. The warmth of it felt borrowed too, and she almost laughed at herself for the thought. "I'm going to give something back."

Tam sat down on the fallen lintel across from her, careful, patient, the way he'd been in the Weeping Woods when the trees had shown him her worst thing and he hadn't flinched, hadn't asked, had only said *that's heavy, you shouldn't carry it alone* — which had been, she suspected, the first kind words spoken over that particular wound in twenty years.

"The one from yourself," he said. Not a question.

"The one from myself."

"Do you remember doing it?"

"That's the thing." She turned the cup in her hands. Outside, somewhere in the ruins, the clerk's clerks were already cataloguing; she could hear their pens scratching like insects in the walls. "I don't. I have a hole shaped like a memory, and I've walked around that hole my whole life the way you walk around furniture in the dark. I know its edges. I know if I step wrong I'll fall into it. But I don't know what's in it."

"And you're going to find out."

"I'm going to give it back," Coral corrected quietly. "That's different. Taking it out isn't mine to do anymore. Giving it —" She stopped. Started again. "When I lifted memories off travelers in the Hollow, I never kept them. That was my rule. Never keep what you take, because the moment you keep it, you start building a life out of other people's pasts, and then you're nothing but a house other people lived in. I broke that rule exactly once. When I was seven."

Tam waited. He had gotten good at waiting. Tinker's boys, she'd learned, were raised on patience — everything in a tinkering wagon rattles unless you pack it slow.

"My mother died when I was six," Coral said, to the cup, to the stars fading into morning, to the boy. "Fever. And my father — I've told you my father drank. What I haven't told anyone is that I don't actually remember her face. I remember that I should. I remember that there was a time I could have drawn her eyes closed, her mouth, the way her hair went iron-gray early though she wasn't old. And then one day I couldn't. One day the Hollow had eaten it, or I had eaten it, or —"

She set the cup down because her hands weren't steady enough to be trusted with it.

"— or I sold it," she finished. "Or gave it away. Or buried it so deep I called it selling. Maghda used to say the best thieves steal from themselves first. Clean hands afterward. No guilt, no witnesses. Just money and a gap you learn not to look at."

* * *

The clerk — Serah Ostrow, though everyone just called her the Clerk, as though there were no others anywhere in the world, and after reading her ledger Coral half believed it — received her in the abbey's scriptorium, a room miraculously intact, its arched windows glazed with glass so old it ran with bubbles and threw wobbling coins of light across the desks.

Serah was a small woman built entirely of angles and ink, sixty-odd years old with forearms like a dockworker from hauling ledgers, and she looked up when Coral entered with the expression of someone who had read ten thousand confessions and been surprised by none of them.

"You want paper," Serah said.

"I want — how did you know?"

"Everyone who comes to me after a verdict wants something. The guilty want absolution and the pardoned want purpose, and both are paper problems." She slid a fresh sheet across the desk, and a stoppered pot of ink, and a quill trimmed for fine work. "Write it down. Whatever you're carrying. Names, dates, what was taken, from whom, and why. My ledger doesn't judge. It just remembers. There's a difference, and it matters more than you think."

So Coral wrote.

Her hand shook at first, then steadied, because thief's hands always steady when the work begins — that was the one inheritance of her trade she didn't resent. She wrote: *Coral Dunmore, of the Hollow. Age seven, the winter after my mother's death. I took a memory from myself. I don't know its content. I know its weight.* She described the knot behind her ribs as best she could, the shape of the hole, the edges she'd mapped in the dark all these years. She wrote until the light moved across the desk and the ink pot needed refilling twice.

Then she stopped, quill hovering, and looked at what she'd written, and saw it — saw the thing she'd been circling her entire life without naming.

"Why would a child take her own memory?" she asked aloud.

Serah Ostrow put down her pen. For the first time, the clerk looked at Coral not as a case but as a person, and there was something old and tired and kind in it.

"Children take things from themselves for the same reason adults do," she said. "Because keeping it hurts more than losing it. But a seven-year-old doesn't usually manage the working alone. It takes precision, that kind of theft. Surgical. Either someone taught her —" and here Serah's eyes went sharp, "— or someone *made* the taking possible. Tell me about the Hollow again. The soil. The silence."

"It's dead ground. Magic can't follow. Everyone says it's a refuge."

"Everyone says a lot of things about places people are afraid to leave." Serah pulled a different ledger toward her — thinner, older, its leather scarred with water damage — and turned pages with a scholar's economy. "During the war of courts, before your grandmother's grandmother, there were enclaves founded by oath-breakers. People who had sworn vows under compulsion and wanted out. They learned something the crowns never bothered to learn: oaths bind even where magic doesn't. A sworn word is sworn *everywhere*, dead soil or living. Magic can't follow you into the Hollow, but neither can magic release you. Whatever vow was spoken inside those borders stays spoken. Forever. Unless —"

"Unless what?"

"Unless the swearer unswears. Freely, knowingly, with the memory of the swearing intact." Serah closed the ledger and folded her hands atop it. "You see the design of it? Whoever built that cage made sure the door could only be opened from the inside, by someone who remembered locking it. And they filled the place with people whose memories of their own vows had been — removed."

The cold that went through Coral then had nothing to do with the drafty scriptorium.

"The memory-brokers," she whispered. "Maghda. The whole economy. We thought we were feeding the settlement. We were — we were *maintaining the lock*. Every traveler's past bought and sold, every stolen year traded away, and underneath it all —"

"Somebody has to keep a valley full of people from remembering what they promised," Serah said mildly. "It's honest work, if you don't look at it directly. Most evil is."

* * *

She understood then, standing in the wobbling light, why she had never been able to leave.

Not poverty. Not fear. Not love of the market square or Maghda's grudging approval or even the simple gravity of home. She had stood at the scree pass a hundred times in her childhood — she remembered that much, the wind coming down the pass smelling of pine and distance — and every time, some hand inside her chest had simply turned her around. Not dragged. *Turned.* Gently. Like a mother steering a child away from a cliff edge.

An oath, binding in dead ground, held in place by a memory she had stolen from herself at seven years old because keeping it hurt more than losing it.

She tried to reconstruct the moment. She was seven. Her mother was less than a year in the ground. Her father was drinking himself toward the grave that would come two winters later. And someone — who? Maghda was already old then; Maghda had been old forever — someone had knelt down in front of a grieving child with tear-swollen eyes and said words like *you'll be safe here, little one, you'll never be alone, you'll never have to leave* — and a child, wanting nothing else in the world, wanting it so badly her whole body ached with the wanting —

— had sworn.

*I will stay in the Hollow. I will stay in the Hollow. I will stay.*

Three times. Oaths liked three times. She knew that from the crypt, from the chamber where every broken vow echoed; she knew the architecture of promising now the way she knew lock mechanisms. Three repetitions, freely given, by a seven-year-old who didn't know that grief makes the worst bargainer in the world and that whoever was holding her hands while she swore knew exactly what they were planting.

A keeper, seeded and grown in the dark. The thirteenth keeper, harvested right on schedule.

Coral sat down slowly on the scriptorium floor, because her legs had opinions of their own, and she grieved.

She grieved for the child who had been offered comfort and handed a chain. She grieved for the woman who had built a life on top of the chain and called the rattle of it her heartbeat. She grieved for her mother's face, which might have been in the very memory she carried — might have been *why* she'd taken it, because looking at that face every day while the person who helped bury it poured wine was a hurt no seven-year-old survives intact — she grieved for the version of herself that could have left at seventeen, at twenty-five, at thirty, free, if one adult in the Hollow had ever told her the truth instead of handing her a pickpocket's kit and calling it a livelihood.

Tam found her there, eventually, sitting on the cold stone with dried tears making her face feel tight as parchment. He didn't say anything. He just sat down beside her, shoulder to shoulder, the way he had in the Weeping Woods, and let the silence be a shelter instead of a judgment.

"I know what I have to do," she said finally.

"Yeah?"

"I have to give it back. All of it. The oath and the memory together — that's the only way it works, according to Serah. The swearer unswears, freely, knowingly, with the memory intact. Which means I have to *remember* first. Remember swearing. Remember her face, probably. Remember whatever was bad enough that a child chose to cut it out." Coral laughed, and it came out ragged. "Do you know what's funny? This is what I did for a living. I took this exact thing from hundreds of people. Soldiers, widows, pilgrims, children. And I never once — not once — understood what it cost to be on this side of it."

"You understand now."

"I understand now."

* * *

They did it at dusk, in the abbey's crypt — or what remained of it, the great shattered bowl where the Oathstone had died, its fragments scattered like a burst star, each shard still faintly warm and singing to itself. The echoes of broken vows had faded from the air, mostly, though sometimes a whisper drifted through, some promise made centuries ago finally, blessedly, released.

Coral knelt at the center, where the stone's heart had been. Tam sat above on the steps, watching, present, a lantern burning beside him because she'd asked for light — she was done doing her life's work in the dark.

She closed her eyes and went looking for the knot behind her ribs.

It resisted. Of course it did. Twenty years of scar tissue had grown over it; it had become load-bearing, part of the architecture of Coral Dunmore. To pull it free was to pull out a bone. She breathed. She found the edges she'd mapped all her life in the dark, and this time, instead of walking around them, she took hold.

It came away with a sound she felt rather than heard — a soft tearing, like roots leaving soil, like a bandage off a wound kept too long. And then it opened in her like a flower forced into bloom all at once, decades compressed into a single unbearable instant:

Snow. Lantern light. The smell of tallow and fever-sweat still clinging to her own hair, because her mother had died eleven days ago and no one had let her wash. Hands holding hers — dry, papery, strong. Maghda's hands. Maghda's voice, gentle as she had never once been in Coral's adult memory: *You're frightened, little bird. You don't want to carry this hurt. Let me help you set it down.* And the church of the Hollow, the little stone chapel with its magic-dead floor, and Maghda saying, *Promise me you'll stay where it's safe. Say it three times and I'll take the hurting away.*

And her mother's face — oh, her mother's face, iron-gray hair and laugh-lines and eyes the color of the ford in autumn — her mother's face had been the *first* thing to go. Because the oath was tied to the grief and the grief was tied to the face, and Maghda had taken the whole knot, neat as a surgeon, and left a seven-year-old hollow where her heart's center used to be.

*I will stay in the Hollow. I will stay in the Hollow. I will stay in the Hollow.*

Seven years old. Snow in her boots. A stranger's hands. A bargain struck by someone too young to know that safe and caged are often the same word.

Coral knelt in the ruined crypt with tears running freely down her face and spoke into the echoing dark, clearly, in her grown woman's voice, with the memory blazing whole behind her eyes:

"I unswear it. I was a child, and I didn't know what I promised, and it was taken from me unfairly — but I unswear it *freely*, and *knowingly*, and *whole*. I am not bound to the Hollow. I was never bound to the Hollow. I was bound to a lie." She drew breath that shuddered all the way down. "I release myself. I release myself. I release myself."

Three times. Oaths liked three times. So did unbindings.

The change was not dramatic. There was no thunderclap, no shockwave — the world had had quite enough of those. It was smaller than that and infinitely larger: a sensation like a door opening in a house she'd lived in all her life without knowing it had doors. Air moved through her that had never moved before. Something that had been pressing gently, ceaselessly, invisibly against her sternum for thirty years — *stay, stay, stay* — simply stopped.

And into the space it left rushed everything she'd sealed away. Her mother's voice, singing off-key. The game they'd played with buttons on the winter bed. The exact weight of her head against her mother's shoulder. The funeral — the terrible smallness of the coffin, her father's silence like a held breath that never ended. All of it, hers. All of it, *hers* — not lifted, not borrowed, not sold, not carried as freight. Hers the way her own hands were hers.

She cried for a long time. Tam came down the steps at some point and sat with her among the shards of the broken god-stone, and she leaned against him, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying. Some mercies are silent ones.

Later — much later, when the lantern had burned to a thumb's depth and the stars had wheeled overhead through the broken vaulting — she spoke.

"I spent my whole life thinking I was empty," she said. "Numb. Hollow, like the valley. I thought that was just what I was. A person with nothing inside." She touched her own chest, wonderingly, the way you touch a healed wound. "I wasn't empty. I was *locked*. And the key was a memory I stole from a seven-year-old because someone convinced her the hurting was better gone."

"And now?"

"Now I'm full." She laughed — wet, unsteady, real. "Gods, Tam, I'm so full it hurts. But it's the good hurt. It's the hurt of a room that finally has furniture in it."

He smiled in the lantern light. Then his expression shifted, and he nodded upward, toward the broken doorway of the crypt, where footsteps sounded on the rubble — unhurried, familiar, deliberate.

Coral looked up.

Serah Ostrow stood silhouetted against the starlight, ledger under one arm, and behind her — unmistakable even in shadow, tall and pale and crowned in lilac-grey — Ashvael, Lord of the Lilac Court, wearing an expression Coral had never seen on him before and could not yet name.

"You need to hear this," the Clerk said quietly. "Both of you. Before you walk out of here tomorrow — there's something on the roads east that wasn't there yesterday. And it's following the map."
