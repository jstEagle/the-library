## 22. Where the Map Ends

The last marked border was a line of ink no thicker than a hair.

Coral had walked it three times in the past hour, pacing back and forth along the crest of the ridge where the courier's map simply — stopped. Everything beyond was blank vellum, pale as the inside of a shell, and she had come to understand that this was not the map's failure but its honesty. Cartographers drew what they knew. The world beyond this ridge had never been known by anyone who lived to draw it.

Or so the old stories said. Coral had stopped trusting old stories somewhere around the Abbey of First Light, when she learned that every simple tale she'd been told was built on a theft.

"Are you going to stare at it all morning," Tam said, "or are we going to find out what's under the ink?"

He sat on a flat stone with his boots off, rubbing his feet, which had been blistered and re-blistered since Verrin and were now toughened into something like leather themselves. His hair had grown out shaggy over his ears. He looked older than he had at the ford outside the Hollow — they both did — and younger too, somehow, the way people look once the weight of pretending has been set down.

"There's nothing under the ink," Coral said. "That's the point."

"There's nothing *drawn* under the ink." He wiggled his toes at her, irreverent as ever. "Different thing."

She crouched and unrolled the map across the stone between them, weighting the corners with pebbles. In the weeks since the abbey's crypt had come down around them like a held breath finally released, the vellum had changed. The roads behind them — the Weeping Woods, the salt flats, the free city of Verrin with its sisterhood of brokers and its ledger-keepers — remained inked and true. But new lines had begun to appear ahead of them, faint as veins under skin: a river here, a ford there, a cluster of marks that might have been a village or might have been wishful thinking.

The ink came slowly, and only when they moved. As though the map weren't showing them the road. As though they were drawing it.

Tam had noticed first. "You're doing that," he'd said one night on the banks of a nameless stream, watching her fingers hover over blank space while, an arm's length away, a thin silver line crept across the vellum to mark the crossing they'd use in the morning. She hadn't believed him until she'd tried to make it happen deliberately and nothing came — the map only filled for a life actually being lived, not for one being planned.

Magic, loose in the world now, worked that way. It didn't answer commands. It answered motion.

* * *

They went down off the ridge into country that had no name because no one had needed to give it one yet.

The grass grew tall and seed-heavy, whispering against their knees, and the air smelled the way the Hollow's air never had — of living soil and rain that had fallen recently enough to still be remembered by everything it touched. Somewhere out of sight a bird sang a phrase over and over, four notes descending, patient as water dripping through stone. Coral found herself matching her breathing to it without deciding to.

This was the other thing about the broken Oathstone, the thing the tribunal had argued over and never quite resolved: magic had not come back evenly. It pooled and eddied. Some places drank it deep and bloomed strange; some places stayed stubbornly plain, and the folk there counted themselves lucky. Travelers' tales — real ones now, not memories pried from strangers' heads — spoke of wells that told fortunes, of children who whistled up wind, of a village where every loaf baked on Sundays rose into the shape of whatever the baker most missed. Grief-bread, they called it. People came from three counties away to buy it and wept into their sleeves and called it worth the price.

Coral carried none of that power herself. She had checked, more than once, in private and with a certain grim thoroughness. Whatever she had been — thirteenth keeper, thief of years, the girl who drove a pick through the heart of an oath — she was now only a woman with quick hands and a head full of her own history, all of it hers, none of it borrowed.

It was enough. It was more than enough. Some mornings the sheer weight of owning her own past made her stop mid-stride just to stand there and hold it.

"You're doing the stopping thing again," Tam said, catching up.

"I'm admiring the view."

"You're standing in the middle of the path looking constipated."

"That's how I admire views."

He laughed, and the bird took the sound up and carried it out over the grassland, and Coral thought — not for the first time, but with less fear than before — that this was what a life felt like from the inside. Not the hollow echo of someone else's wedding day or funeral or first snow. This. Sore feet. Bad jokes. A sky with no ceiling of valley walls above it.

* * *

The river appeared on the third day before it appeared in the world.

Coral woke at dawn and saw the fresh line gleaming on the vellum, curving down from the hills ahead, and by noon they heard water. It was broad and slow and brown-gold, running heavy with spring melt from mountains they couldn't yet see, and on its far bank stood a willow so old that its trunk had hollowed into an arch you could walk through, and inside the arch someone had hung dozens of small offerings — ribbons, buttons, a child's shoe, a clay whistle — each one tied with a knot.

"A shrine?" Tam asked.

"An inn," said a voice.

An old woman sat beneath the willow on a stool worn concave by decades of sitting, mending a net. She was brown and wrinkled as a walnut and looked entirely unimpressed by two travel-worn strangers appearing out of empty grassland.

"People leave things when they cross," she said. "Safe passage, mostly. Or thanks. Or apologies." Her eyes flicked to the map case at Coral's hip with the frank assessment of a woman who had watched a great many things cross her river. "You'll want the ferry. It's a copper apiece unless you're carrying something that hums. Then it's three."

Coral went very still. "Carrying something that—"

"Maps," the woman said, returning to her netting. "Since the stone broke, maps that draw themselves want paying like anything else with a soul in it. Ferryman's rule. Don't look at me like that, girl — I don't make the rules anymore than you do. Nobody makes them anymore. That's rather the whole point, isn't it."

* * *

They paid their six coppers. On the far bank, Coral paused inside the willow's arch, looking at the offerings turning slowly on their strings in the river breeze. A ribbon faded rose-pink. A brass button polished bright by touch. The small shoe, laced neatly, waiting for a foot that had surely grown long ago.

She untied the leather cord from her wrist — the one Maghda had given her, years ago, the first payment marker of her trade, kept out of habit and then out of something she'd refused to examine — and knotted it to the willow among the rest.

"What was that?" Tam asked.

"A debt," she said. "Paid sideways."

He didn't ask more. One of the many things she loved about him — and she had admitted it to herself somewhere on the salt flats, bleeding and furious and certain she was going to die, so it seemed rude to pretend otherwise now — was that he knew when a door was closed on purpose.

* * *

Beyond the river the land began to rise, and the map began to hurry.

That was the only word for it. New ink came faster now, almost eager — roads branching, a bridge marked where no bridge could be seen, then a second line doubling back as if correcting itself. Twice the vellum shivered under her fingers like a horse scenting home.

"It's excited," Tam said that night, holding the map up to their firelight. The new lines seemed to move when he wasn't looking directly at them, the way stars do at the edge of vision. "Why is it excited?"

"Because no one's ever walked here with it before," Coral said. And then, hearing herself: "No one's ever walked here with *me* before."

The fire cracked and settled. Above them the stars were thick as spilled salt, and in the months since the breaking, travelers swore some of them had shifted — not much, a thumb's width, the sky adjusting to a world with one fewer oath holding it straight. Coral lay back on her bedroll and let her eyes lose focus until the whole vault of night blurred into a single shimmering cloth, and she thought about the Hollow, and about Maghda counting coins in her dim shop full of other people's yesterdays, and about the soldier whose daughter's funeral she had carried for an afternoon so long ago it felt like a story someone else had told her.

She wondered if he remembered his daughter now. She hoped he did. Grief was a terrible thing to own and a worse thing to lose.

* * *

On the sixth day they met the first person.

He came up the trail toward them leading a mule loaded with pots, a round-faced man in a patched coat, and he stopped ten paces off with the caution of someone who had learned, lately, that strangers might be carrying anything.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning," Tam said.

They regarded each other. Then the pot-seller said, "You wouldn't have crossed the high meadows, would you? Past the singing stones?"

"Singing stones?"

"Ah." His face fell with such genuine disappointment that Coral almost laughed. "Then you've not. They're new. Or old. Depends who you ask — my gran says they sang before the kings ever made their oaths, and everyone forgot, and now they remember again. You stand among them and hum and they hum back, only it's your mother's voice, or whoever you most—" He stopped, suddenly awkward. "Anyway. If you're headed that way, mind the third one. It lies."

"Lies," Coral repeated.

"Hums in a voice that isn't yours. Tells you things you want to hear. My cousin followed it for half a day before it led him into a bog." He shrugged philosophically. "New world. Same as the old one, mostly — some things sing true and some things sing sweet, and it pays to know the difference."

He touched his cap and went on down toward the river, mule bells chiming, and Coral stood a long moment looking after him.

"Some things sing true and some things sing sweet," Tam repeated softly.

"I spent twenty years selling the sweet kind," Coral said. "Stolen memories always taste better than they were. I used to tell myself that was mercy — smoothing the edges before handing them over." She turned back up the trail, toward the high meadows, toward stones she very much intended to visit. "It was just easier to sell."

"And now?"

"Now I'd like to hear something true. Even if it's ugly. Especially if it's ugly." She glanced at him sidelong. "You coming, tinker's boy? Or does the story end here?"

"The story doesn't end," Tam said, falling into step beside her, "until I say it does, and I haven't even found out what happens next."

* * *

The singing stones stood in a ring on a shelf of the high meadow, nine of them, gray and lichen-crusted and utterly ordinary until you stepped inside the circle and hummed.

Coral went last. Tam first — and the nearest stone answered him in a woman's voice, warm and worn, humming a fragment of a tinker's road-song, and Tam stood there with his eyes shut and tears running freely down his face and made no move to wipe them, and Coral stood guard at the ring's edge and let him have it, the way he had stood guard for her in the Weeping Woods when the trees had shown him the memory she had stolen from herself at seven years old, the oath sworn on a dead mother's grave to stay in the Hollow forever, and had not flinched.

When his turn ended he opened his eyes and nodded once, and stepped out, and did not explain, and she did not ask.

Then it was her turn, and she hummed, uncertain, and the stones were quiet a moment — and then they answered.

Not her mother. Not any voice she knew.

Her own. Seven years old, thin and fierce, singing the little skipping song the Hollow children sang on market days — *round and round the broker's door, take a year and give a score* — except the stone sang the verse after the one everyone remembered, the verse that had dropped out of the rhyme sometime in the centuries, the verse that had been waiting in the stone the way truth waits in ordinary things:

*Round and round till the oath runs free,*
*what you gave away comes home to thee.*

Coral stood in the ring with her own child-voice rising around her and understood, finally, completely, that the world had not been waiting to forgive her or to punish her. It had been waiting for her to come home to herself. All of herself. Even the seven-year-old who had been afraid. Even the thief. Even the keeper who broke the thing she was meant to keep.

The song ended. The stones went gray and silent and ordinary.

"Well," Tam said from the edge, gruffly, because his voice wasn't quite steady. "That's a tune you don't hear every day."

"No," Coral said. She wiped her face. She was smiling. She was astonished to discover she was smiling. "No, it isn't."

* * *

That evening, camped under the ring's sheltering shoulder, she took out the map one last time before sleep — a habit now, checking the day's new ink against her memory of the day itself — and stopped.

There was writing on it.

Small, neat, in the margin of the previously blank country beyond the ridge, in a hand she recognized because it was her own: words she had not written, or had written without knowing, the way the roads drew themselves.

*Here the named lands end,* it read. *What follows is unwritten. Bring nothing taken. Tell no one's story but your own. — C.D.*

Below it, fresher, the ink still wet-shining, a single new line:

*A city on the coast, where the drowned keep their names.*

Tam leaned over her shoulder. "Coast," he said. "I've never seen the sea."

"Neither have I."

"Do you think it hums?"

"I think," Coral said, rolling the map closed with care, tucking it into the case where a dying man had once pressed it into her hands and changed the shape of her whole life with one whispered word, "that we're going to find out."

Above the fire the stars wheeled, unbound and unbinding, and the dark land ahead of them waited — unnamed, unsung, wide open as the first page of a book no one had dared to begin.

Coral slept without dreams she didn't own, and in the morning they walked on, two small figures on a road that was drawing itself into being beneath their feet, and neither of them looked back.
