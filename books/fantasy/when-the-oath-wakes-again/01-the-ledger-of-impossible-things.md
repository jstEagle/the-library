## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The bottled storm had been sitting in the receiving room for three days before anyone thought to tell me about it.

I found it on a Tuesday — or what passed for Tuesday in the Guild of Rare Exchanges, where the days were named for the commodities that arrived rather than for any god. Stormday, then. The jar stood on the trestle table beneath a canvas sheet that some porter had thrown over it with the careless reverence men give to things they don't understand, and when I lifted the cloth I understood immediately why they'd kept their distance. The storm was awake.

It filled the jar the way grief fills a house: completely, and without asking permission. Rain fell inside it in tight silver spirals, striking an interior sea no deeper than a thumb's width and throwing up spray that never escaped the glass. Lightning moved through it like a thought trying to find its shape — a flicker behind the eyes, a pressure at the temples. When I leaned close, the hair on my forearms rose, and I felt the particular static hum that means a thing is *more* than it appears, that its edges are a courtesy rather than a fact.

Someone had corked it with wax and iron filings. Someone had, presumably, caught it first.

"Well," I said, to no one, because there was never anyone. "Let's have a look at you."

This is the part of my work I am best at, and I will admit it without false modesty: cataloguing. Anyone can swing a sword, bribe a magistrate, or smuggle a griffin egg across three borders — I've watched the Guild's factors do all three from the safety of my desk, and none of them could tell you afterward what they'd actually held in their hands. But to write a thing down properly, to give it a name and a number and a history in ink, is to hold it more tightly than any hand can. A ledger entry is a small act of ownership over chaos. It is the closest thing to magic I have ever practiced, and until recently I believed it was the only kind I'd ever want.

I set the jar on the brass balance scales, noted its weight against the standard weights (four pounds, eleven ounces, though the needle trembled whenever lightning flashed, as if the storm weighed hope), and opened the day's folio to a fresh page.

*Item 4,117,* I wrote, in the small, upright hand that Corvane says is worth its weight in contracts. *One storm, bottled. Provenance: the Skerry Coast, by way of a fisherman who will not be needing payment, per the attached note.* There was indeed a note, folded under the jar, written in a shaking hand that got steadier toward the end, which told me everything about how the catching had gone. Fishermen off the Skerries bottle storms the way other coasters salt herring; it's old work, older than the Guild, and the storms they catch are the small ones, the squalls that would otherwise take a single boat and be done. This one was not small. This one had taken out the whole fleet's nerve, if the note's last line meant what I thought: *She screamed the whole night. We are sorry to sell her.*

*Condition: living,* I continued. *Disposition: Vault Six, pending valuation. Valuation deferred — see note re: vocalization.*

Because the storm did vocalize. I had been writing for perhaps ten minutes when the rain inside the jar went quiet all at once, the way a child goes quiet when it decides to listen, and then something inside the glass said — not in thunder, but in a thin, exhausted voice like wind through a keyhole — a word I did not know, repeated twice.

I sat very still. My pen hovered above the ledger, leaving a small growing blot of ink, and for a moment I felt the strange vertigo of my profession: the awareness that I spent my life reducing the impossible to columns and figures so that I would never have to admit the impossible was speaking to me.

*Note,* I wrote finally. *Storm appears sentient. Recommend valuation include relocation costs. Recommend handler wear gloves.*

Then I signed it — *T. Eastwick, Keeper of Ledgers* — blotted the page, and carried the storm down to Vault Six myself, because the porters were afraid of it and because, though I would never have put this in any book, I wanted a few more minutes in its company. In the vault's cold dark, among shelved impossibilities, I set the jar on its assigned shelf between a reliquary finger that pointed at whoever lied nearest to it and a taxidermied swan that dreamed audibly on moonless nights, and I stood there a while listening to the small sea breathe.

"Sorry," I told it. It seemed the thing to say. "It's just what we do here."

It rained at me gently, which I chose to accept as forgiveness.

* * *

The stolen shadow came in at dusk, and it came in badly.

I heard the commotion from the counting room — shouting in the yard, the wet slap of boots, someone being sick against the stable wall — and I knew before I looked out the window that the Marlowe brothers had botched another acquisition. They were our best retrievers and our worst men, twins who moved like mirrors of each other and had, between them, one complete conscience, which they took turns borrowing.

They brought the shadow up wrapped in a sailcloth bundle, and even through the cloth I could see it moving. That is the trouble with shadows: you cannot cage them in anything opaque, because opacity is their native country. Sailcloth only slows them. By the time the bundle reached my desk it had already begun seeping out at the seams, thin ribbons of darkness uncoiling along the floorboards like ink dropped in water, and one of those ribbons touched my boot before I could move my chair back.

Cold. Not the cold of weather but the cold of absence — the specific chill of a place where warmth used to be. I snatched my foot away and said, with more sharpness than I intended, "Iron tray. Now, please."

The younger Marlowe fetched it. Between us we got the shadow onto the tray, where it pooled sullenly, refusing any shape at all, a puddle of deliberate nothing. Up close it smelled faintly of lavender and candle smoke, which told me whose it had been before someone took it, and I felt the familiar tightening in my chest that I have learned, over eleven years at this desk, to file under *professional detachment* and have recently begun to suspect is something else entirely.

"Provenance," I said.

The elder Marlowe wiped his mouth. He still looked green. "A lady in Copperlane. Widow. She sold it herself."

"She sold her own shadow?"

"She asked us to take it." He shifted. "Said she hadn't slept right since her husband died, because his shadow used to fall across the bed at dawn, and hers didn't reach that far anymore. Said a person shouldn't have to lie every morning in the proof of what they've lost."

I wrote that down. It is a rule of mine to write down exactly what people say about the impossible things, because the impossible things themselves rarely explain, and the human freight around them is often the most valuable part of the record. Future valuations depend on context. So does future guilt, though no one at the Guild has ever paid me for that.

*Item 4,118,* I wrote. *One shadow, adult, female, detached voluntarily. Provenance: Copperlane, widow's household. Condition: distressed. Note the scent profile (lavender, tallow) consistent with long attachment to original owner. Disposition: Vault Two. Valuation:* — and here my pen stopped, because the Guild prices shadows by weight and rarity and the shadow was neither heavy nor rare, it was only grieving, and there is no column for that.

In the end I wrote: *Valuation deferred.*

"You'll want the fee schedule," said the younger Marlowe.

"I know the fee schedule," I said. "Bring me the widow's signature. Original copy. If she signed it, I want to see the steadiness of the hand."

She had signed it steadily. That was somehow worse. The signature sat at the bottom of the contract in firm, deliberate strokes — *Maren Voss, née Aldery* — the hand of a woman who had decided a thing entirely and would not be argued out of it, and beneath it, smaller, almost hidden in the margin, a single line in the same script: *For whatever it's worth to whoever buys it — it remembers mornings. Be gentle with it.*

The Marlowes had not noticed the line. I cut it out of the margin with a knife, pasted it into the ledger beside the entry, and told myself this was archival practice. It is important, when cataloguing a stolen thing, to preserve the record of the theft — including the part where the theft was requested, and the part where it hurt anyway.

I carried the tray down to Vault Two myself, again, again alone, again telling no one. The shadow flowed off the iron reluctantly, gathering itself in the corner of its assigned case like a cat deciding whether the new cushion is an insult. As I latched the glass, it stretched once — briefly, horribly, into the shape of a woman standing in dawn light — and then collapsed back into pool-shaped anonymity.

"It remembers mornings," I told it through the glass. "I wrote it down. Whatever else happens, that stays in the record."

If shadows can be comforted, I don't know it. But it settled, after that. And I stood in the vault longer than the work required, the way I had stood with the storm, and the way I stand most nights, and if you had asked me why I would have given you a perfectly reasonable answer about security protocols and left out the truth entirely: that the vaults are quieter than my rooms above the counting house, and that the things on the shelves ask fewer questions than people do, and that a man who spends his days writing down other lives can go a remarkably long time without noticing he isn't living one.

* * *

Here is what the Guild of Rare Exchanges trades in, for those outside its doors: everything the world has agreed not to believe in. Bottled storms and borrowed years. Love potions that actually work, which is to say they work terribly. The last tooth of a dragon that never existed. Maps to places that only exist while unfolded. Names — true names, purchased from the desperate and sold to the dangerous. We have vaults beneath the counting house that go down farther than the cellar records admit, and somewhere below all of them, behind a door only Corvane opens, the oldest acquisitions sleep in the dark and are spoken of, when they are spoken of at all, as *the founding stock*.

My place in all this is small and, I used to think, safe. I keep the ledgers. Every item in, every item out, every valuation, provenance, and disposition, entered in my hand and sealed with my mark. Eleven years. Four thousand one hundred and eighteen entries as of tonight. I can tell you what the Guild owns to the ounce, and I can tell you — though this skill has never once been praised, only needed — what each of those things *cost*, in the sense that has nothing to do with coin. The storm cost a fleet its courage. The shadow cost a widow her mornings. Everything down in those vaults is a wound somebody decided to stop bleeding by selling it, and my ledgers are the record of the scars.

Corvane says a ledger-keeper with sympathy is a liability. Corvane says a great many things in that smooth voice of his, standing at my desk in the blue hour before closing, reading over my entries with hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring art he intends to buy.

"'Valuation deferred,'" he read now, tapping the shadow's page with one finger. "Twice in one day, Eastwick. The auditors will talk."

"The auditors always talk."

"Mm. You've a gift for making refusal look like diligence." He smiled. He has a good smile; I've watched it close contracts worth fortunes. "Defer them both by week's end. The storm goes to a buyer in the capital who wants weather he can threaten people with, and the shadow—" he glanced at my pasted-in scrap of the widow's handwriting, and something flickered across his face too fast to name— "the shadow has a buyer already. Someone who collects them. Don't ask. You're happier not knowing, and you *are* happier, aren't you, Eastwick?"

It was not really a question. I said yes, because I have said yes to Corvane for eleven years, and because the ledger was open in front of me and there is a particular shame in lying when your whole trade is truthfulness in ink.

He left. The lamps burned low. Around me the counting house settled into its nightly sounds — the tick of the great clock, the settling of paper, the distant drip of the vault condensation that no one has ever fixed because the sound keeps the apprentices honest, or so Corvane claims. I finished the day's entries. I ruled the columns straight. I dusted the folios, banked the fire, and locked my desk with the little brass key I have carried so long its teeth have worn to fit my hand alone.

And then, because it was late and the house was empty and I am a man of exact habits, I did the thing I do every night: I took down the audit folios — the old ones, the deep ones, the century-deep ones — to reconcile the historical index against the physical stock. It is tedious work. It is the loneliest work in the building. It is also, I have lately come to understand, the reason I was hired, though no one ever told me so, and the reason I was hired is waiting for me in a box of acquisitions dated ninety-nine years ago, on a shelf I pass every day without looking.

But I did not know that yet. Tonight I only knew the numbers wouldn't balance.

There was a discrepancy. Small — a single acquisition from the year 914, listed in the index as received, valued, and shelved, with no corresponding entry anywhere in the disposal records across ninety-nine subsequent audits. Item 12, in a hand not mine, older than my grandfather. *Acquisition: one book, bound in grey leather, contents prophetic, seller unknown, price: waived.* And in the column where I should have found its vault location, instead of a number, someone had written a single word in faded brown ink:

*Waiting.*

I sat in the lamplight with four thousand entries behind me and one impossible word in front of me, and I felt the peculiar cold that comes of realizing your meticulous life has a hole in it shaped exactly like your name — though of course I didn't know that either, not yet. I only knew that somewhere in this building was a book a century old that had never been catalogued, never been valued, never been disposed of, and that someone, a hundred years ago, had bothered to leave me — or someone like me, or possibly precisely me — a message in the margin of my own profession.

*Waiting,* it said.

Well. I have never once left an entry unfinished, and I did not intend to start tonight.

I took the lamp and went down toward the vaults to find it.
