## 2. A Date Written a Century Ago

The Guild of Rare Exchanges did not deal in the ordinary vices of commerce. There were no warehouses stacked with silks, no cellars of brandy, no strongrooms groaning under coin. What the Guild traded in was rarer than all of that: the impossible, the improbable, and the quietly illegal. A lullaby sung by a woman who had been dead forty years. The last breath of a hanged king, kept in a silver locket. A key that opened no door but every regret.

And it was my task — my singular, meticulous, thankless task — to keep the ledgers of it all.

I had been at it for eleven years, since the age of nineteen, when Master Corvane had looked at my hands over the interview table and said, "Steady hands. Good. Most of what we keep wants to move." He had not asked about my family, my schooling, or my health. He had asked whether I dreamed in color or in words. I had answered *words*, truthfully, and he had hired me before I finished the sentence.

That was the shape of my life: ink, lamplight, catalogues, and the slow accumulation of other people's wonders, none of them mine.

On the evening in question — the evening everything afterward would be measured against, though I did not know it yet — I was working late in the Audit Room, three floors beneath the Guildhall's gilded offices, where the air smelled of cedar shavings and cold iron and something older underneath, like the memory of rain on stone.

* * *

The acquisition had come in on the Tuesday barge from Greyharrow, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax that shifted color when you weren't looking directly at it — a cheap trick, common to fae-touched goods, but the manifest called the item *sensitive*, and sensitive things got audited by me and no one else.

There were three crates. The first held a clockwork nightingale that sang only in a language no living scholar could read; I logged it, weighed it, noted that its song seemed to grow one note sadder each time it finished. The second held a jar of preserved dusk, harvested somewhere, from some evening someone had thought worth keeping. It glowed faintly amber when I tilted the glass, and for a moment — just a moment — I smelled woodsmoke and heard a dog barking far away, as if the dusk remembered its own street. I put it down quickly. Sentimental residue was a known hazard of preserved time, and I had long ago trained myself not to lean into it. Leaning into other people's memories was how keepers went strange.

The third crate was heavier than it had any right to be.

Inside, packed in wool gone grey with age, lay books. Eleven of them, assorted, mostly worthless — a water-stained almanac, a bookkeeper's arithmetic primer, a devotional with every saint's face scratched out. And beneath them, at the bottom, wrapped separately in its own cloth as though someone had wanted it found last, or perhaps never: a ledger bound in green leather gone almost black, its spine cracked in four places, its pages swollen with damp and time.

The flyleaf bore an inventory number I had never seen before, and I have an excellent memory for numbers: **GX-0000**. Zero-zero. The catalogue's very beginning. Either a clerical joke, or the oldest thing the Guild owned.

I set it under the lamp and began, as I always began, with provenance.

No seller's mark. No chain of custody. The manifest entry was in Corvane's own hand — I knew the sharp downstrokes, the way he crossed his sevens — and it said only: *Old stock. Verify contents. Do not read ahead.*

*Do not read ahead.* An odd instruction for a ledger audit, which is nothing but reading ahead, page after page after page. But Corvane's oddities were part of the furniture of my world, like the cedar smell and the iron nails driven into every doorframe of the Audit Room, and I did not question them. That, I would later understand, was the whole of my failure and the whole of my making: I did not question.

I opened the book.

It was an account book, structurally. Two columns per page. Left column, debits. Right column, credits. But the entries were not sums of money.

*Paid: the color out of a wedding, 12th of Harvestmoon, 1187.*
*Received: one promise, unkept, weight approx. 4 lbs.*
*Paid: the sound of a name, spoken once, by mistake.*

Ledger-speech. The true kind. I had seen fragments of it before — the Guild's oldest contracts used a degraded version, the way modern churches use the fossilized grammar of dead prayers — but I had never seen it alive like this, whole and flowing across a hundred pages, as though the writer had thought in it natively. Whoever had kept this book had not been recording transactions in ledger-speech. They had been *thinking* in it.

The dates ran backward as I turned pages forward, which meant the book was kept in reverse — newest entry first, history unspooling toward its origin as you read deeper. A bookkeeper's affectation, or a prophecy's habit. At the time I noted it as curiosity number fourteen in my audit margins and moved on.

The handwriting changed as the years went by. Early entries — which is to say, the deepest pages, a century old — were in a hand so fine and slanted it looked like wind through wheat. Then, around sixty years back, the hand grew hurried, pressed hard enough to emboss three pages down. Around thirty years back, it deteriorated into a scrawl that made me think of someone writing while running, or bleeding.

And then, near the front — near what should have been the present day — the hand steadied again. Became careful. Deliberate. Like someone who knew exactly how much room she had left.

She. I had already begun thinking of the hand as *she*. The wheat-through-wind hand and the steady final hand were the same, I was nearly certain. Whatever else this book was, it had one author, and she had been writing in it for a very long time, and she was not done.

I reached the last written page — the most recent entry — and stopped.

The date at its head was rendered in ledger-fashion, season and moon and year rather than our Gregorian plainness, and for a moment the old reckoning meant nothing to me. Then my eye did the conversion the way it did all conversions, automatically, treacherously, and the numbers assembled themselves in my mind with a small, silent click.

The date was tonight.

Not approximately. Not in the maddening, deniable way of prophecies — *in the days when the ash fruits twice*, *when a grey horse drinks at the stone bridge*. Tonight. This evening. The twenty-third of Frostwane, in the year 1219, the very date I had written myself at the head of my own working ledger six hours earlier, in my own tidy, unremarkable hand.

I sat back. The chair creaked. Somewhere above me, the Guildhall settled and ticked through its nightly noises, utterly indifferent.

Coincidence, I told myself. Old books fall open to strange pages. Calendars repeat; cycles overlap; any date will land on some anniversary if you look hard enough. I have talked myself out of worse vertigo in this room. There was a bottled storm two shelves away that had once convinced an assistant keeper it was his dead brother, and the assistant had needed three months and a great deal of valerian. I knew how to be rational among impossible things. Rationality was, in fact, my entire profession: to stand between the impossible and the invoice, and to keep my hands steady.

So I looked at the entry itself, expecting weather or omens or the usual prophetic fog.

It was not fog. It was a debit column and a credit column, same as every other page, and it read:

*Debit: one keeper of ledgers, his eleven safe years, his steady hands, his small gray life — all forfeit, this night, to the waking of the Oath.*
*Credit: pending.*

*His.*

I remember noticing, absurdly, that the word was slightly smudged, as though the author had hesitated over it. As though even she, writing a hundred years ago, had flinched at what she was doing to me.

Below the columns, in the margin, in letters larger than any others in the whole book, was a name.

**Tamsin Eastwick.**

* * *

There is a particular quality to the silence that follows the discovery of your own name where it should not be. It is not the absence of sound. It is the presence of attention — the feeling that the room has turned its face toward you, that the dark beyond the lamplight has leaned in to see what you will do.

I did what any auditor does when an account refuses to balance. I checked my work.

I checked the date conversion twice, then a third time, walking myself through the old reckoning aloud in the empty room like a madman: *Frostwane the twenty-third, new-style equivalent, year of the common calendar 1219 — yes — yes.* I checked the name letter by letter, certain I must be misreading the archaic script, that *Tomas Eastgate* or *Tammar Estwick* lurked behind my certainty. No. The letters were clear as a bell. My father's surname. My mother's odd, unkind gift of a first name, given for her grandmother and given to no one else in any parish record I had ever searched.

I checked the paper for forgery — held it to the lamp, looked for modern fibers, fresh ink gloss, the telltale chemical brightness of recent manufacture. The page was a century old if it was a day. The ink had sunk into the fibers and oxidized to brown the way only great age browns. Whoever wrote my name had written it before my grandfather was born.

I even, in the end, checked my own hand against hers, because some stubborn clerk-part of my mind proposed that I had written this book myself in fugue, that the zero-numbered volume was mine from some lost year. But the wheat-through-wind hand was not my hand, and the pressure was wrong, and the pen had been a cut quill, not steel.

Every check failed. Every single one. The account balanced perfectly, and the balance said: *this night, Tamsin Eastwick.*

I do not know how long I sat there. The lamp burned lower. My tea went cold in its cup, undrunk, and I noticed its coldness the way you notice a distant bell — evidence of time passing without any sense of having lived it. The vertigo was not dramatic. That surprised me. I had expected — if I had expected anything — fear, or wonder, or some operatic emotion worthy of the Guild's strangest stock. Instead there was simply a slow tilting, as if the floor of my entire life had revealed itself as a painted sheet laid over a well, and I could feel, through the paint, the depth below.

Eleven years. Eleven years of steady hands and small gray hours, and it had all been — what? Preparation? A waiting room? Had Corvane hired me nineteen years ago because of my hands, or because of this book, this date, this entry? *Do not read ahead.* Had he given me that instruction knowing — knowing — exactly which page I would reach?

The margin smudge bothered me most, afterward. In a book of perfect records, one hesitation. One place where the author's certainty had trembled. She had known my name. She had known my date. And still, writing it, she had paused — as if she wished it could be otherwise. As if she were sorry.

I turned the page. Blank. Turned another. Blank, blank, blank — the future, unwritten, a run of empty columns waiting for entries. Except.

At the top of the next blank page, faint as breath on glass, letters stood in the paper the way watermarks stand — not written, *pressed*, as if a pen of enormous weight had once rested there without ink:

*Tonight. The door opens at midnight. Come to the front of the shop. You are later than you think.*

I looked up at the Audit Room's clock, an iron thing older than the building, its face cracked like drought-earth.

Quarter to twelve.

I want to say I considered my options. I want to say the rational auditor in me drew up the prudent course: report to Corvane, seal the book, log the anomaly, go home to my narrow rooms above the chandler's and my thin soup and my single window on the lane. That man existed. He had existed for eleven years, and he was, in that moment, screaming at me from behind my own eyes — *this is not your account, this is not your entry, close the book, climb the stairs, be nobody, be safe.*

But beneath him, beneath eleven careful years, something older stirred — something that had waited all this time in the gray, in the margins of other people's wonders, keeping inventory of a life that contained nothing impossible at all. It did not scream. It simply asked, very quietly, the question the book had already answered:

*If your whole life has been a ledger, when does anything get entered under your name?*

The clock gathered itself and struck the quarter-hour. In the jar on the shelf, the preserved dusk flared amber, remembering some street somewhere. The lamp guttered. And from three floors above, faint through stone and timber and eleven years of habit, I heard the front door of the Guild shop — locked, barred, and warded since nine o'clock, I had heard Corvane turn the key myself —

open.

Not forced. Not broken. Simply *unlocked*, with the soft, patient click of a door that has been expecting someone.

I closed GX-0000, and my hands — steady for eleven years, praised for their steadiness by the most dangerous man I have ever known — were shaking so badly the green leather slipped and struck the table edge with a sound like a struck tooth.

Midnight was coming. The book said the door would open, and the door had obeyed it, a hundred years after the instruction was written. Which left only the question the credit column had left pending, the question I would spend everything I had to answer:

What, precisely, was about to be paid — and what, precisely, would I receive in exchange?

I took up the lamp, and the book, and I started up the stairs toward the front of the shop.
