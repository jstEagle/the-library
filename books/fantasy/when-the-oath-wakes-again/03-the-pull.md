## 3. The Pull

Midnight came to the Repository the way water comes to a drowned thing — not all at once, but finding every seam.

Tamsin felt it before he heard it: the great clock in the counting room exhaling its last stroke, and then the silence after, which was different from all other silences in the building. Daytime silence was merely absence of noise. Midnight silence had *weight*. It settled on the shelves like dust made of attention, as though ten thousand catalogued impossibilities were holding their breath to see whether anyone still watched them.

He watched them. That was his whole life, reduced to a sentence: he watched them.

The prophecy-ledger lay open on his desk where he had left it an hour ago, unable to close it, unable to look away from it either, caught in the orbit of his own name like a moth around a lamp he already knew would burn him. The candle had burned down to a stub beside it. His tea had gone cold at his elbow. Somewhere below, the bottled storm shifted in its crate and muttered thunder against the glass, the way it did every night at this hour, dreaming of the sky it had been stolen from.

*Eastwick, Tamsin. Keeper, third grade.*

Written in iron-gall ink that had dried before his grandfather's grandfather drew breath.

He had spent the hour doing what he always did when the world stopped making sense: he worked. He checked the binding against the Guild's acquisition records. He traced the provenance — a monastery deaccession in 1131, sold by monks who claimed they could no longer afford to keep it, which Tamsin now understood differently, because you do not *afford* to keep a book like this any more than you afford to keep a fire in your lap. He measured the page against other pages and found it fractionally thicker, as if the paper itself had been waiting, fattening on anticipation for a hundred years.

And he tried, methodically, ruthlessly, to prove it was a forgery.

That was the true discipline of a keeper, after all. Not cataloguing. Doubting. Every impossible thing in the Repository was impossible right up until the moment someone proved it wasn't, and then it was inventory, and inventory was safe. A bottled storm was just weather with a cork in it. A stolen shadow was just darkness with someone's name attached. And a prophecy naming Tamsin Eastwick on tonight's date was just old ink on old paper, written by some clever charlatan with a taste for cruelty—

Except that the ink, under his jeweler's loupe, had not faded. Not one shade. A century old, and not one shade faded, while the ink of the surrounding entries had gone brown and ghostly, as though the letters of his name were being fed from somewhere. As though something kept renewing them, night after night, year after year, so that they would be crisp and black and undeniable on exactly this evening.

"Very well," he said aloud, because the alternative was saying nothing, and saying nothing meant listening to the silence listen back. "You have my attention. What precisely am I supposed to—"

The candle went out.

Not guttered. Not blown. It went out the way a word is swallowed — mid-flicker, complete one instant and absent the next, leaving a thread of smoke that rose perfectly straight in air that had no business being still.

Tamsin's hand went to the drawer where he kept the flint, which was the sensible response, which was the keeper's response, cataloguing the problem before solving it: light source extinguished, cause unknown, remedy available. His fingers found the flint. Struck it. The sparks fell and died without catching, three times, four times, and each failure felt less like bad luck and more like refusal, as though fire itself had been instructed to withhold its cooperation.

Then he noticed the light that remained.

It came from the book. From his own name, specifically. The letters had begun to glow — not brightly, not dramatically, but with the low patient amber of a banked coal, of embers that remember being a fire and intend to be one again. The glow moved as he watched, crawling letter to letter through the line, *Eastwick, Tamsin*, the way lamplight moves along a wick, and beneath it he felt rather than heard a sound beginning in the paper. A hum. Deep, subterranean, felt in the bones of his wrist where they rested on the desk, in the small bones of his inner ear, in the fluid of his spine.

"No," Tamsin said. He was surprised by how steady his voice came out. Detached. Professional. *Anomalous luminescence in archival material; recommend immediate quarantine.* "No, I don't think we'll be doing this."

The hum answered by becoming a chord.

That was the only word for it. The single note split into harmony, into three notes and then five, and each new note came from somewhere else — one from the prophecy-book, one from the dark mouth of the stacks behind him, one from below the floor, rising up through the legs of his chair into his body until his teeth ached with it. The Repository was singing. The bottled storm threw itself against its bottle hard enough that he heard the straw packing snap. Down some far aisle, the stolen shadow peeled itself off its velvet backing and stood upright in the dark, a man-shaped absence wearing his night-vision like a suit, and turned its faceless head toward him with what he swore was curiosity.

Tamsin stood up. The chair skidded back. His heart was going hard and fast and his hands — his meticulous, ledger-ruled hands — had begun to shake, and he found himself thinking, absurdly, of the inventory codes. If the storm broke loose it was shelf 9-C, hazard class third-rank, and there were procedures, there were *procedures*—

The shelves began to fold.

There was no better word. The long aisle before his desk, forty paces of oak and catalogued wonder, shortened. Not collapsed, not toppled — *shortened*, the way a road shortens when you crease a map, the far end swinging toward the near end through angles that had never been granted permission to exist in a mortal building. He saw the aisle bend. He saw its middle rise out of the plane of the floor like a drawbridge pulling itself up, and the shelves rode the fold without spilling so much as a ledger, their contents serene, their labels facing outward, everything in perfect order even as order itself was violated.

Tamsin grabbed the edge of his desk. It was absurd — the desk weighed as much as a horse — but he gripped it anyway, because it was *his*, because it was real, because the grain of its oak under his palms was the last honest fact in a world going false.

"This is not," he said, and his steadiness cracked finally, beautifully, "this is *not sanctioned*—"

The air in front of him opened.

A door appeared where no door had ever been. He knew every door in the Repository — he had keys for eleven of them and had copied two more — and this was none of them. It stood free-standing, frameless except for its own edges, tall and narrow and made of a wood he could not name, gray as driftwood, silvered as if a century of weather had washed over it in a single minute. There was no handle. There was no hinge. There was only a seam of light down its center, thin as a knife's edge, and through that seam he could see — not the corridor beyond. Not wall. Something else. Something vast and dim and moving, like landscape seen through smoked glass, like a country remembering itself.

The pull began.

He felt it first as a lean in his own blood, a tilting of everything inside him toward that seam of light, the way water leans toward a drain. Then it became physical. His boots slid an inch across the floorboards, groaning, and he braced harder against the desk and felt the desk *shift* with him, dragging with a shriek of wood on wood, because whatever wanted him through that door was not negotiating with his grip. It was negotiating with gravity itself, and gravity had apparently signed something once that obligated it here.

"The ledger," Tamsin gasped — because the keeper in him would not die, would not stop, even now, even with his heels plowing furrows in the floor — "*the ledger stays catalogued*—" and he snatched the prophecy-book off the desk one-handed as he passed, slammed it shut, and felt it fight him, felt it swell hot against his palms like a living lung refusing to breathe out, and held it anyway, crushed against his chest.

The shadow was between him and the door.

He didn't see it arrive. It was simply there, standing in the amber light bleeding through the seam, and it raised one long absence of an arm, and Tamsin had one full second to understand that it was pointing — not threatening, *pointing*, courteously, the way a servant indicates the way out — and then the floor tilted the rest of the way and gave up pretending.

He went.

Falling sideways through a door is not like falling down. Falling down has ground waiting at the end of it, an argument that resolves. This fall had no bottom that he found; instead there was passage, a throat of gray air rushing past him on both sides, and through the smoke-dark walls of that passage he glimpsed things he refused at first to catalogue because cataloguing them would make them real: trees growing downward from a rootless sky, rivers that ran uphill in slow silver ropes, a hill in the distance shaped unmistakably like a sleeping face whose lips were moving, mouthing words he could almost hear. Cold air poured over him and it smelled wrong and wonderful at once — rain on hot stone, night-blooming flowers, iron, honey, the green smell of a forest floor and underneath it all a faint burnt tang, like the memory of lightning.

*Terror* was the first feeling, and it was total. It emptied his mind of procedure and inventory and left only the animal, screaming *wrong, wrong, this is wrong.* His lungs forgot their work for three full seconds. He heard himself make a sound he had never made before and hoped never to identify later.

But underneath the terror — and this was the part that frightened him more, the part he knew even in that moment he would never tell anyone — underneath it, coiled and warm as a second heartbeat, was *exhilaration*.

Because the world through those smoking walls was *alive*. Everything in it leaned and strained and sang the way the Repository had begun to sing, everything humming that same five-note chord, and Tamsin realized with a lurch that the chord had never left him — it was coming from inside his own chest now, from the place behind his sternum where fear lived, or where fear had lived until it had been evicted by something older. His name glowed through the leather of the book pressed to his ribs. He could feel it, a brand-shaped warmth, patient as embers.

All his life he had handled wonders and filed them away. Bottled storms. Stolen shadows. A feather that fell upward, a key that fit any lock but only on Sundays. He had recorded them in a fair round hand and closed the covers and gone home to his narrow rooms and his solitary supper, and he had told himself that pride was enough, that precision was a kind of passion, that a man who kept the ledgers of impossible things stood closer to them than most men ever got.

He had been lying to himself for thirty-one years, and his body knew it, and his body was *singing*.

The passage ended the way a held breath ends.

Light struck him first — not bright light but *broad* light, light without a source, the color of the hour just after sunset when the day cannot decide to end. It came up to meet him and he came down into it, boots hitting ground that was neither stone nor soil but something between, packed earth with a faint crystalline ring to it, and he staggered three steps and stayed upright through pure dumb luck, arms wide, the book clutched to his chest like a rescued child.

Then he turned around, because the keeper in him demanded documentation, and looked back for the door.

It was closing. The gray wood swung shut with unhurried gentleness, and as it shut it thinned, and as it thinned it faded, and where it had stood there was now nothing but air and the enormous hush of a country that was not his. Behind it, impossibly, faintly, he could still hear the Repository — the clock, the muttering storm, the ordinary tick of an ordinary world — sounds from the far side of a wall that no longer had a door in it.

Tamsin Eastwick stood alone in the borderland between realms, and did the only thing he knew how to do.

He assessed.

Sky: sourceless amber, neither day nor dusk, with slow pale shapes drifting overhead that might have been clouds and might have been very high fish. Terrain: a rolling moor of that ringing earth, stitched with grass that was silver on one side of each blade and green on the other, so that the wind combed the land in shifting stripes. Distance: hills, a river's glint, and — directly ahead, perhaps half a mile off, standing alone where nothing explained why it should stand — a single milestone, old and leaning, carved with letters too worn to read.

Air temperature mild. Wind light, from his left. Immediate threats: none visible. Water: unverified. Position: unknown. Time: he glanced at his pocket watch and watched the hands spin slowly backward, unhurried, almost apologetic, and decided to close the lid on that particular datum.

Inventory, he thought, and almost laughed at himself. *One keeper, third grade, misplaced. One forbidden ledger, active. Zero flint, zero procedures, zero idea.*

His hands were still shaking. He noticed that distantly, the way he noticed weather. But his breathing had slowed, and the terror had drained down into something quieter and stranger — a taut, humming alertness, the feeling of standing on a stage before a curtain about to rise. Whatever had pulled him through had wanted him *here*, in this between-country, on this ringing ground. His name had burned for a hundred years to bring him to this mile of silver-grassed moor.

The wind changed. It came around from ahead of him now, off the milestone and the hills beyond, and it carried a scent that did not belong to the moor: ink. Fresh ink. Warm paper. The smell of a book newly opened, blowing toward him across impossible grass from nowhere at all.

And with it, thin as a thread pulled through the ear, a voice — dry, distant, amused, and unbearably sad:

"Well. You're late. Though I suppose you don't know that yet."

Tamsin's grip tightened on the book against his chest. Its warmth pulsed once, twice, like something answering a knock.

The grass parted ahead of him, blade by blade, marking a path toward the milestone and whatever waited there, and Tamsin Eastwick — who had spent his whole life recording other people's wonders in neat columns — took the first step onto a road that had no entry in any ledger ever kept.

The wind followed him, and it smelled like handwriting.
