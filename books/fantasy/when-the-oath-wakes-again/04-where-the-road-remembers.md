## 4. Where the Road Remembers

The door let him go the way a river lets go of a stone.

One moment Tamsin was falling through the folded geography of the shop — shelves passing him like the ribs of some enormous swallowing beast, ledgers shrieking open around him, their pages fanning with a sound like ten thousand startled birds — and then there was grass under his hands and sky above his head, and his body remembered all at once that it had a shape, and that the shape hurt.

He was sick onto the grass. Twice. The second time brought nothing up but bile and the sour ghost of the ink he'd been chewing absently for hours before midnight. He knelt there shaking, palms pressed into soil that felt wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name, and it took him longer than he would have liked — a man of ledgers, a man of particulars — to work out what the wrongness was.

The soil was warm. Not sun-warmed. Warm like something living, warm like the palm of a hand held just beneath the surface of the earth, and when he pulled one hand back and looked, the impression his fingers had left glowed faintly green before fading.

"Right," Tamsin said aloud, because saying things aloud had always helped him file them. "Right. Catalogue your surroundings, Eastwick. That's all this is. An audit of an unfamiliar location."

His voice sounded thin. It did not echo, which was the first entry in the new ledger: *the air here does not return what you give it.*

He stood, slowly, joints complaining, and made himself look.

There was a road. That was something. Roads meant destinations; roads meant someone had gone somewhere before you and worn down the world enough to make it easier for the next fool. This one ran from nowhere visible to nowhere else visible, a pale ribbon of packed earth winding through country that could not decide what it wanted to be. To his left rolled hills of a green so saturated it looked painted by someone who had never seen grass and was working only from an enthusiastic description. To his right, the same hills continued — but grey, leafless, their trees standing like the preserved corpses of trees, beautiful and utterly dead, under a sky the colour of old pewter.

The road itself ran between the two without choosing. Where he stood, the grass underfoot was half green and half silver-grey, each blade transitioning not along any line but blade by blade, as if even the ground here had been divided by lot.

And the light. There was no sun. There was no moon. But light came anyway, sourceless and directional, casting his shadow off to the east — except that his shadow was doing something no shadow should do. It lay on the ground properly enough, but it was *moving*, subtly, turning its head when he turned his, a half-second behind. A shadow catching up to its owner.

"Entry the second," Tamsin whispered. "*Time here may be running slightly late.*"

He checked, out of habit older than sense, that his satchel was still with him. It was — the strap across his chest, the leather scarred from fifteen years of service, the weight inside reassuringly dense. He opened the flap with fingers that only trembled a little and nearly wept with relief at what he found: three ledgers, his ink horn, six quills, his knife for cutting nibs, the stub of tallow candle he kept against power failures at the shop, and — pressed flat between the pages of the smallest book — the prophecy ledger itself, the one that had named him, its ancient vellum warm to the touch through the paper wrapping he'd bound it in.

*Eastwick, Tamsin. Tonight.*

Well. Tonight had happened. He was evidence of that.

He began to walk, because standing still felt like agreeing to become part of the landscape, and the landscape here had an appetite about it he didn't care to test. The road accepted his feet without comment. Behind him, he knew without looking, his shadow followed at its slight, unnerving delay.

He walked for what felt like an hour, though he caught himself twice counting his steps and losing the count — not forgetting, precisely, but finding that the numbers had rearranged themselves between one stride and the next, so that two hundred became three hundred became, briefly and horribly, no number at all. Each time he stopped, wrote the last certain figure in his pocket-book, and started again, anchoring himself to arithmetic the way a drowning man anchors to rope.

The country changed as he went, or failed to change in ways it should have. He passed the same leaning fencepost four times — he was sure it was the same one, marked with a rusted horseshoe nailed upside down — and on the fourth passing, the horseshoe was on the other side of the post. He passed a cottage with smoke rising from its chimney, and when he knocked, the door opened onto nothing at all, a darkness with no room behind it, and from somewhere inside that darkness came a sound like breathing that was almost a voice saying something that was almost a word. He did not knock again anywhere after that.

The road began, gradually, to remember itself.

That was the only way he could describe what he noticed. At first the path was merely dirt. Then he began to feel, underfoot, faint impressions — the ghost of cartwheels, the fossil pressure of feet. Then the impressions deepened, and by the time the pewter sky began to darken toward a dusk with no sun to end, the road bore actual ruts, actual hoofprints, some shod and some not, some distinctly *not* — cloven, or too long, or arranged in patterns no anatomy could produce. And threaded through all of it, like a melody under a drone, came the sense of all the travellers who had ever walked here, a sediment of passage, so that walking this road felt less like moving through space and more like moving through everyone who had ever moved through it before.

*Where the road remembers,* Tamsin thought, and shivered, and wrote it down anyway.

It was full dark — whatever dark was here, a darkness thick as wool but luminous at its edges, like looking into deep water from above — when he saw the light ahead. Not torchlight. Not lamplight. A cold silver glow, steady, hovering at the roadside just beyond a stone marker whose letters had been worn to suggestion.

His first thought was *campfire*, and his second was *bait*, and both thoughts arrived with equal weight, because he had read enough guild reports to know how the impossible things were usually packaged. But his feet were aching, the road behind was worse than the road ahead, and a man who has already fallen through a folding shop is poorly positioned to start being cautious about lights.

He approached. The glow resolved.

A woman sat on the milestone, reading a book by her own light.

She was translucent. That registered third, after *sitting* and *reading*, and it registered with the calm of a man noting discrepancies in an inventory: she was translucent, cast no shadow at all where every stone around her threw three, and her outline frayed gently at her edges, wisps of her rising off her shoulders like heat-shimmer in reverse. She wore clothes of a fashion Tamsin had seen only in the oldest plates in the Guild's archive volumes — high-collared, severe, a century out of the world. Her hair was pinned in a style requiring more patience than he had ever possessed. She might have been forty; she might have been never. Her eyes, when she looked up at him, were the exact silver of her own glow.

"You're late," she said.

Tamsin's mouth, which had been dry for hours, somehow produced words. "I don't— I don't know you."

"No," she agreed, and returned to her book. "You wouldn't yet. Sit down, Eastwick. Your feet have been bleeding for the last mile and you've been pretending not to notice because you think noticing is a concession. It isn't. Sit."

The use of his name went through him like cold water through cloth. He looked at the milestone. He looked at the road back, featureless and remembering. He sat, because his legs made the decision and his pride simply failed to lodge an objection in time.

"How do you know my name?"

"It's written down," she said. "In several places. One of them is this." She held up the book she was reading, and Tamsin's stomach dropped through the warm earth and kept going, because he recognized the binding — the cracked leather, the brass corners gone green, the burn mark on the lower spine shaped like a moth. It was the prophecy ledger. The same book that was, at that moment, wrapped in oilcloth in his satchel, six inches from his hip.

"That's impossible," he said. "I have that book."

"Yes," she said. "You do. I know. I put it there."

"You—"

"Wrapped it in oilcloth. Bound it with twine in a grocer's knot, because you learned the grocer's knot from your father and you've never trusted any other. Tucked it under the third ledger from the top, because you always keep the important thing under the third ledger from the top; you think nobody's noticed the pattern." She closed the book on one finger, keeping her place, and regarded him with an expression he would spend a long time trying to classify and eventually file, privately, as *pity wearing patience as armour*. "I know your habits better than you do, Tamsin Eastwick, because I've had a hundred years to learn them."

"A hundred years," Tamsin repeated. The words felt borrowed from someone else's mouth.

"I wrote that book," she said. "Every entry. Every date. Including tonight's. Including yours." She opened the book again and turned it toward him, and there it was in ink that seemed to move very slightly, the way water moves under ice: *Eastwick, Tamsin. Tonight.* His own name in a hand that was elegant, assured, and — he realized with a vertigo that had nothing to do with the shifting hills — familiar. He had seen that hand before. He had spent his life among ledgers; he knew hands the way other men knew faces. He had seen that looping ascender on the G, that particular way the crossbar of the T hooked leftward—

"In the Guild archive," he said slowly. "The acquisition ledgers. The early ones. There's a hand like yours on the—"

"I did the indexing," she said. "For eleven years. Before I understood what the Guild was *for*." Something moved across her translucent face, a weather front of anger or grief, gone as fast as it came. "But that's a later conversation. Possibly a much later one, if you keep sitting here arguing with a ghost instead of walking."

"Then you're—" Tamsin's mind performed the arithmetic and rejected the result twice. "If you wrote it, and the book is a hundred years old, then you're writing from—"

"From now-plus-a-hundred," she said. "Yes. Or I did. Do. Will. The grammar is genuinely difficult and I've had decades to notice how badly 'was' serves. I am Vespera. I was a keeper of ledgers, like you — they always choose a keeper, did you know that? The covenant wants tidy minds. It's a joke among us. All twelve of us were accountants of one impossible thing or another." Her silver gaze held him. "I bound myself to the book so that the warning would survive me. So that someone would come. And here you are, late, bleeding, and arguing about grammar."

"Why me?"

Vespera closed the book fully, and for a moment her light dimmed, and the darkness leaned in around them like a crowd.

"Because your name is in it," she said quietly. "Because the covenant is broken and bleeding out at every seam, and the shards are scattered, and the ones who broke it — the ones who *let* it break, who profit by the bleeding — will do anything to keep a swordspeaker from ever standing at the seam again. Because the road brought you here, and the road only brings those the oath remembers." She rose from the milestone without appearing to move through the intervening postures; one moment seated, next standing, a cut in the film of the world. "And because I need your help, and you need mine, and neither of us can afford the luxury of trusting anyone else."

"I don't trust you," Tamsin said.

"Good," said Vespera, with the first thing approaching warmth she had shown. "Distrust carefully. Check my claims against the world. That's the whole discipline, Eastwick — the ledger never asks you to believe, only to verify. Now stand up. We have miles to walk before the road forgets us, and it forgets quickly after dark."

"Before we go anywhere," Tamsin said, and was surprised by the iron in his own voice, "you'll verify something for *me*. You want an alliance? Then answer plainly: what happens to me? It's in your book. You've read ahead. What does the book say I do?"

For a long moment the ghost was silent, and her silence had texture, had weight, had the specific gravity of a thing withheld rather than a thing unknown. Around them the borderland waited — green hills and grey, the dead trees and the living, the road with its sediment of a thousand thousand passages — and Tamsin watched a woman from a hundred years hence choose, visibly, how much truth a man could carry on his first night in the world-between.

"The book says," Vespera said at last, "that you will hold every word of the old Oath in your hands. And that at the seam of the worlds, you will speak."

"Speak what?"

"That," said Vespera, turning to walk, her silver light spooling out along the remembering road like thread from a spindle, "depends entirely on which page you stop reading at. Come along, Eastwick. We're expected nowhere, which means we must be everywhere before anyone knows to look."

Tamsin got to his feet, took one last inventory — satchel, knife, quills, book, ghost — and followed her into the borderland, telling himself that he was not trusting her, merely auditing her.

It was the first lie of many, and he wrote none of it down.
