## 5. The Broken Covenant

I have been dead a hundred years, and in that hundred years I have learned this: the living do not understand what they carry until it is taken from them. They walk through their hours like misers through a vault, counting and hoarding and never once looking at the gold.

The boy — Tamsin Eastwick, though he did not yet know what that name meant, though I knew and said nothing, which is its own kind of wound — the boy stood at the edge of the road that remembered him, and he looked at me the way the living always look at ghosts. As if I were the impossible thing.

He had not yet understood that *he* was.

*You wrote this,* he said again, holding the prophecy-book out before him like a shield, or an accusation. Its pages fluttered in a wind that touched nothing else in the borderland — no grass bent, no cloud moved — because that wind came from *me*, from the small remainder of will I had left after a century of being bound between two covers. *This is my handwriting's cousin. This is nearly my hand.*

*I told you. I write in your family's script because I learned it from your family.*

*That's not an answer.*

No, I thought. It never is. Answers are a currency, and I had spent so long among the Guild's ledgers — first in life, then haunting one particular volume of them — that I had forgotten how to give anything away for free.

So instead I turned and walked, if you can call it walking, when your feet stir no dust and cast no shadow, down the road that remembers, and I let the road do what roads do in that place between realms: it showed him.

* * *

The borderland has no proper name, or rather it has eleven, one for each of the swordspeakers who died sealing it, and none of those names is spoken anymore because speaking them hurts. The common folk on either side call it the Seam, or the In-Between, or simply *the grey*. It is a country of borrowed weather and secondhand geography. Hills roll there that are made of mortal stone but fae light. Rivers run backward through valleys that remember being gardens. And everywhere, everywhere, the scars.

Tamsin saw the first scar ten minutes into our walk and mistook it for geology.

A canyon opened at his left, deeper than any river had cut in the age of the world, and its walls were not rock. They were *word*. Compressed, fossilized language, layer upon layer of it, oaths and clauses and bindings pressed so hard together over centuries that they had taken on the density of stone. Here and there whole phrases had sheared away and tumbled to the canyon floor, where they lay glinting dully, half-buried promises eroding in wind that smelled of rust and rain.

*What is that?* he asked, and his voice had gone quiet in the way voices do in churches and sickrooms.

*That is the Oath,* I said. *Or what's left of it where it cracked.*

He stopped. He was a ledger-keeper, this boy; give him credit for that. He read the walls. His eyes moved across the compressed strata of language and I watched comprehension arrive in him slowly, the way dawn arrives in a valley — not all at once but slope by slope.

*These are... contracts?*

*One contract. The contract.* I drifted closer to the edge, close enough that the old words seemed to lean toward me, recognizing a voice that had once helped speak them. *Twelve hundred years ago, the worlds were not separate. Do they teach you anything true in your guildhouses? No. Of course not. Truth is bad for trade.*

I will tell it now as I told him then, and I will tell it plainly, because plainness is the only honesty left to me, and because I have carried this account alone for a hundred years and a ghost's silence grows heavy as a drowned man's coat.

* * *

Once, there was one world, and it was not peaceful.

Mortals and fae shared the same ground the way wolves and winter share a forest — in competition, in terror, in occasional terrible beauty. The fae were older and stranger and did not distinguish, as mortals do, between cruelty and appetite. Mortals were numerous and inventive and burned whatever they could not understand. There were wars whose names survive only as curses. Whole kingdoms went under. A third of the mortal world's map was marked, in the oldest atlases, with a single word: *fae*, meaning here be things that will unmake you, and meaning also, if you read the marginalia of those brave or mad cartographers who went and looked: *here be wonders we cannot bear to live without.*

Into that long ruin came twelve people.

Not kings. Not archmages. Twelve swordsmen and swordswomen — farm girls and hedge knights, a nun, a bandit, two siblings from a fishing village whose names I will come to, oh yes, I will come to them — who discovered independently what only a handful in every age discover: that a word spoken with the whole soul behind it can cut. That language, at its root, is a blade, and most people spend their lives handling it by the wrong end.

Swordspeech, we called it later. Then we called it nothing. You do not name a thing while you are inside it, the way you do not hear your own scream until it ends.

They fought. For thirty years they fought, each alone and then together, drawn to one another the way iron filings know a magnet, and when the last war threatened to end both worlds at once — the fae queen Celedhriel had begun unmaking the mortal coastlands thread by thread, and the mortal emperor Aldric had answered with fires designed to burn the Otherworld's roots — the twelve met on neutral ground, at dawn, in a place so grey and empty that neither world claimed it.

That place is where Tamsin stood. That place is the borderland. We made it. We made it out of the space between our twelve intentions, and we swore the Oath there, and it held for nine hundred years.

*Held what?* Tamsin asked, and his ledger-keeper's mind was already drafting columns, already wanting figures. *Held how?*

*A boundary,* I said. *Two worlds, folded apart along a seam of words. The Oath did not banish the fae, whatever your priests say, and it did not cage magic, whatever your scholars say. It did something more delicate. It made the two realms *neighbors* instead of *wounds in each other*. Crossing required passage. Passage required consent. Consent — that was the heart of it, the hinge-word, the load-bearing clause. Neither realm could touch the other except by agreement, freely given, renewed at the seam each turning of the year.*

*And the twelve?*

*Maintained it.* My voice, I am told by those few who can still hear me, goes very flat when I speak of this. Like a ledger line. *A covenant is not a wall that stands once built. It is a fire that must be fed. Each of the twelve carried a shard of the spoken Oath — a fragment of the original utterance, crystallized, alive — and each shard was a word. Together the twelve words were the sentence that held the worlds apart. Apart, but touching. Apart, but able to trade, to visit, to fall in love across the boundary if they were stubborn enough, and some were, and I watched them, and it was beautiful.*

*Was,* Tamsin said.

*Was,* I agreed.

* * *

Here is what killed it, and I will say it quickly because quick is the only way I can.

Nine hundred years is long enough for vigilance to become ritual and ritual to become sleep. The twelve grew old. Swordspeech is written in the speaker — I will teach the boy this eventually, God help him, when he has already learned it in his body the hard way — and so the power passes down bloodlines and bonds, imperfectly, like a story retold at too many removes. The shards passed to heirs. The heirs were not always worthy. Some words weakened. One speaker in the fourth century used her shard-word for private vengeance and spent half her sentence doing it. By the ninth century the covenant was an old dog: loyal, half-deaf, dreaming by the fire of the wolf it once was.

And then came the night it died.

I was there. Understand that — I do not report this from archives. I stood on the seam with the others, and I felt the eleventh word fail in Maren of the Ford's throat mid-renewal, felt it go out of her like a lamp going out of a window, and for one heartbeat the worlds were one world again, raw edge to raw edge, and everything that had been politely separated for nine centuries remembered its neighbor all at once.

Magic bled through. Not the gentle magic of trade and treaty — the *wild* kind, the deep water kind, pouring through the tear in the sentence like flood through a broken dam. Forests on the mortal side woke up hungry. Rivers in the Otherworld forgot their courses and ran uphill through mortal cellars. Things that had slept since the founding — hollow things, hungry shapes, the dead who had been promised rest by the old words — sat up in their graves and barrows and found themselves unpromised.

And the seam itself began to scream. I mean that literally. The borderland, our beautiful grey compromise, tore along lines no map has ever recorded, and where it tore, the two worlds ground against each other like continental plates, and the friction produced monsters the way friction produces sparks.

Eleven of us stood in the ruin of the twelfth word — the twelfth speaker had not come to the renewal that night; I will not yet say why; my hands, such as they are, shake even now — and we did what we could. Eleven shards against the flood. We spoke ourselves nearly to nothing, sealing the worst of the wounds, buying the worlds a ragged century of slow leak instead of instant drowning. Ten of the eleven died of it within the year, their selves spent entirely, their names worn smooth as sea glass. I lived on, diminished, and when I finally died — badly, stupidly, twenty years later — the covenant I had given my life to patch was still bleeding.

It bleeds now. You have seen the leaks, Tamsin Eastwick, even if your ledgers filed them under other names. The storm in the bottle on your guild's shelf. The shadow someone stole from a man who will never feel sunlight warmly again. The cattle born with too many legs in villages near the grey. TheGuild prices these things. It does not ask why they exist.

I told him all of this as we walked, or most of it, and he listened the way good listeners listen, which is to say his face stayed still and his hands did not. At the end of it he stopped on the remembering road and asked the question I had been dreading, because it was the correct one, and correct questions from clever bookkeepers are more dangerous than knives:

*If the covenant failed a hundred years ago, why did the book wait until tonight to summon me? Why me, tonight, now?*

And because I am a coward even in death, and because the answer involves a smudged name and a sister's grave and a crime I helped commit, I gave him the true-but-incomplete reply I had rehearsed for a century:

*Because the leak has become a flood,* I said, and gestured with a ghost's hand at the horizon.

He followed my gesture. Far off, where the grey country met a mortal skyline he almost recognized — the smokestacks and spires of the city nearest his guildhouse — the sky was wrong. It hung in shreds, like fabric worried by moths, and through the gaps I could see the Otherworld's forests moving with intent, and below, in the mortal streets, lights flickering in patterns that were not electricity but attention. Something on the far side had noticed the thinning. Several somethings.

*How long?* he asked.

*Seasons. Perhaps less. The seam fails faster every day, and there are those on both sides who profit from the failure, and one of them keeps your ledgers, boy, and signs your wages.*

He was still absorbing that — I watched it land on him like cold water — when the road remembered something ahead of us, and the something drew its sword.

* * *

I should explain about the hollowed ones, and I will, but first let me describe what Tamsin saw, because his seeing matters more than my knowing.

Down the road, perhaps fifty paces, stood a knight in armor the color of old teeth. Nothing was inside the armor. That is not poetry — the visor was up, and the space behind it was dark in the specific way that a house is dark when no one has lived there for a very long time. Yet it moved. It moved with the memory of movement, joints flexing in rhythms some dead man had practiced for decades, and its sword was a real sword, mortal-forged, pitted with grave-dirt, and it was pointed at us.

*Vespera,* Tamsin said, very evenly, the way men speak when their bodies have gone ahead of their courage. *What is that.*

And I looked at the hollowed knight — at the dead swordspeaker wearing its own corpse like a puppet's costume, at the Guild's iron collar fused around its neck-plate, at the shard-glint buried somewhere in that ribcage I could see through as easily as paper — and a hundred years of grief rose in me at once, grief and guilt and fury, all of it crowding my ghost-throat, and what came out was not the explanation he needed.

What came out was: *Run.*

He ran. But the road that remembers runs both ways, and behind us, from the direction we had come, I heard a second set of footfalls begin — patient, armored, empty — and I knew then what I had suspected since the moment the prophecy-book chose its hour:

The Guild had known about Tamsin Eastwick longer than he had known about himself.

And they had sent their dead to collect him before the living ever could.
