## 6. First Blood on Old Ground

We walked for what felt like a day and no time at all.

That was the borderland's first lesson: distance here was a rumor. The road beneath our feet was old — I could feel its age through my boot soles, a deep worn grooving like the track of a river that had flowed since before rivers had names — and yet every horizon we approached slid politely away from us, the way a dream rearranges itself when you try to read the writing on the wall.

Vespera drifted at my shoulder without touching the ground. I had stopped flinching at this. Mostly.

"You're limping," she observed.

"I'm favoring my left foot because the right one stepped in something that smelled like regret," I said. "There's a difference."

"It's the same thing, where you're going." She turned her face toward me. Ghost-light moved under her skin like fish under ice. "The borderland reads intention, Tamsin. It gives back what you carry."

"Then it should be giving us ledgers and tea."

"It gave you a door out of your shop. That should have told you something about what you carry." Her voice had that quality I was learning to distrust — gentle on the surface, with arithmetic underneath. "You walked through a place that shouldn't exist and your heart rate barely rose. You catalogued the impossible within an hour of meeting me. Most people scream. You asked me to spell 'prophecy' twice so you could transcribe it correctly."

"That's called professionalism."

"That's called a man who has never once been permitted to feel anything loudly." She said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "It will matter later. What wakes quietly wakes hungry."

I did not answer. Ahead of us the road bent around a stand of trees that were not quite trees — their trunks were grey as ash, their leaves the color of old pewter, and they made a sound when the wind moved through them that I can only describe as held breath. Beyond them, the land opened into a shallow valley, and in the valley there were stones.

I knew stones. Stones went in ledgers. Boundary markers, waystones, cairns, menhirs — the Guild had acquired four standing stones over the years, each with provenance papers longer than my arm. I looked at the valley the way I looked at everything: as inventory awaiting description.

Twelve stones stood in a ring. Eleven of them leaned, cracked, or had fallen entirely, lying in the grass like teeth knocked from a jaw. One still stood upright at the ring's center, though it was blackened down one side as if lightning had spent a long, patient afternoon with it.

"Oh," said Vespera, very softly. And then, quieter still, in a voice I had not heard from her before — small, and young, and grieving: "Oh, no. Not here."

"What is this place?"

"The moot-ground." She had stopped drifting forward. Ghosts, I was learning, could be reluctant too. "Where the twelve stood when they swore the Oath. Where they—" Her hand closed in the air, grasping at something a hundred years gone. "This is where it broke."

* * *

I should have turned back then. I want to be honest about that, in whatever ledger this account eventually lands in. Every instinct I owned — the careful ones, the ones that had kept me alive and employed in a guild that traded in things better left unowned — all of them said: not this. Not the broken heart of the thing itself. Not on your first night.

But the ground did something to me when I crossed the lip of the valley. The air changed taste. It went from the green-copper flavor the borderland carried everywhere to something older and more specific — iron and rain and woodsmoke and, underneath it all, faintly, absurdly, beeswax. Candle wax. The smell of a room where someone has sat up late writing.

My hand ached.

Not hurt. *Ached*, deep in the bones of it, the way a limb aches before weather. My sword-hand, though I hadn't held a sword in eleven years, not since the Guild assessed me at fifteen and pronounced my aptitude "clerical" with a smile that managed to be both pitying and relieved. I remembered that smile now with sudden heat, standing in a dead circle of prophecy-stones, my right hand throbbing like a second heartbeat.

"The upright one," I said. "What is it?"

"The First Stone. Ysolde drove it herself." Vespera had recovered her composure, mostly, but her edges flickered when she spoke the name. "It holds the master-word. Or it held it. Everything else is—" she gestured at the fallen stones, the cracks, the rot in the grass where nothing grew, "—downstream of that."

I knelt beside the nearest fallen stone. Habit. You assess before you acquire; you describe before you deal. The stone was taller than me even lying down, and carved along its length with letters that refused to sit still in my memory. I would look away and look back and they would have rearranged themselves, patient as ants.

"There was a word here," I said. "A big one."

"A shard-word. One of the twelve that braided into the Oath." She crouched opposite me, her nearness making the air go cold and sweet. "When the covenant shattered, the words didn't die. Words like that don't die. They broke loose and scattered, and they've been buried in things ever since — chapels, trees, hearts. Looking for hands that might speak them again."

"And if nobody speaks them?"

"Then both realms keep bleeding." She nodded at the grass, the grey trees, the wrong-colored sky. "You've seen the edge of it already. Hollow things crossing over. Magic leaking out like marrow from a split bone. It accelerates, Tamsin. Slowly. But everything slow is also inevitable. That's practically the definition."

I put my palm flat against the fallen stone, because I am a fool who catalogs things, and I wanted to know its temperature for the record.

The stone knew my name.

I didn't hear it. I felt it — a vibration coming up through my arm like a struck bell deciding whether to ring, a vast attention turning over beneath the surface, sluggish and half-asleep and *recognizing*. The letters under my fingers warmed. Somewhere far away, or perhaps directly behind my eyes, something drew breath.

"Tamsin." Vespera's voice, sharp now, all the gentleness burned off it. "Take your hand off the stone."

"Why? What does it—"

The valley answered before she could.

It came out of the treeline on the far side, and my first thought — God help me, my first thought, the ledger-keeper's reflex — was *inventory*. Tall figure, armored, moving with a smooth wrong gait. Second thought, arriving a heartbeat later and considerably less useful: *that armor has a man inside it, and the man is empty.*

The hollowed knight came across the moot-ground like a debt being collected. Its plate was ancient, blackened, furred with moss along the shoulders, and it did not shine because there was nothing left in it to reflect light properly. Through the open visor there was no face. There was a dark, and in the dark, two points of pale light that might once have been eyes and were now only sockets remembering them. Where its heart should have beaten, I saw — I don't know how I saw, the breastplate wasn't transparent, but I saw anyway — a dull red ember, caged in ribs, pulsing out of rhythm with anything alive.

"What," I whispered, "is that."

"Hollowed knight." Vespera had risen, and her form had gone strange — stretched thin, translucent, a candle flame deciding whether to stay lit. "A swordspeaker who died with their oath unfinished. Something wears the body afterward. Uses it." A pause, and then, in a smaller voice: "Some of them wore their own oaths out deliberately, so there'd be nothing left to hold. I knew two of them. Before."

"Can it be killed?"

"It's already dead. The question is whether it can be *stopped*, and the answer is—"

The knight drew its sword.

The sound of it was obscene. Steel clearing scabbard is supposed to sing. This one *groaned* — a long low exhalation, like the last air leaving a lung, and the blade came up trailing not light but shadow, a smear of darkness that clung to the metal like oil clinging to water. The grass died in a slow spreading fan wherever the knight stood. I watched frost crawl across a fallen oath-stone three yards from its feet.

Then it looked at me. Both socket-lights fixed on my chest, on my right hand, on the place where my blood was suddenly very loud.

And it *recognized* me too.

"*Speaker,*" it said.

The word came out of the helm like wind through a ruined house. I felt it in my teeth. Behind me, Vespera made a sound I had never heard a ghost make — fear, plain and human, from something that had supposedly left humanity behind.

"Run," she said. "Tamsin. *Run.*"

I ran. I am not ashamed of it. I ran the way clerks run, badly, with my ledger-sense of the terrain cataloguing uselessly — low wall, good cover, poor exit — while the ground shook behind me with footsteps that fell too evenly, metronome-perfect, a machine wearing a man's grave-goods. The grey trees whipped past. Something cold breathed against the back of my neck, and I understood with total clarity that it was the sword, and that the next breath would be my last, and that my life would end in a dead valley between worlds holding nothing but other people's inventory.

And my mouth opened, and I spoke.

I don't know where the word came from. That is the truth and I will write it plainly: I do not know. It was not in any language I have studied, and I have studied seventeen, professionally. It was not a word I remembered learning. It was a word that had been waiting in me the way a splinter waits under skin, patient, foreign, *mine* — and it came up my throat and out past my teeth with the force of a drawn blade.

"*Caeth.*"

The world cut.

There is no other honest way to say it. The air between me and the knight parted along a line, a straight bright line hanging in space like a seam of white fire, and everything on the far side of that line was simply — elsewhere. The knight's descending sword met the line and stopped, shrieking, sparks that were not quite sparks spraying off it, colors I had no names for bleeding from the wound in the air. The shock of it slammed backward into me. I went down hard on the wet grass with my ears ringing and blood running warm from my nose, and the line of light hung there another full second before it guttered out like a candle pinched between fingers.

Silence. Even the held-breath trees had gone still.

The knight stood ten feet away, motionless, its blade stopped mid-arc, its socket-lights burning on me with what I can only call — and I hated myself for the word even as I reached for it — *interest.*

"*Speaker,*" it said again. Slower. Savoring. "Little speaker. Late speaker."

"Vespera," I said. My voice came out shredded. "What did I just do."

She appeared beside me — how she moved I'll never know, ghosts have their own physics of desperation — and hauled at my arm with hands that passed almost through me, almost cold enough to burn. "Get up. Get *up*, that bought us seconds, not safety—"

"It spoke. Knights don't—"

"These ones remember words. That's the cruelty of hollowing. Everything goes except language." She got me vertical by main force. "It remembers what it was. That's worse than a monster, Tamsin. Monsters don't grieve."

The knight began to walk toward us again. Unhurried. The dying grass curled brown in its wake. And I stood there with blood on my lips and a word still echoing in the roots of my teeth, and I made the second-worst discovery of my life, which was this:

I wanted to speak again.

Not the sane, reasonable want of a man defending himself. A deeper want than that. The word had gone through me like liquor through a starving man, and some part of me — some part I did not recognize, some part older than my whole careful clerk's existence — had *opened* to it, the way dry ground opens to rain. My hand ached with wanting. My throat itched with unspent syllables. Somewhere below thought, below fear, I understood that there were more words where that one came from, twelve of them braided together into something vast, and that I could have them, that they were *owed* to me—

"Tamsin." Vespera, gripping my sleeve, her voice cracking like ice on a pond. "Whatever you're feeling — the pull, the hunger — listen to me. Listen. The shard-words are not free. Nothing in either realm is free, least of all words that cut the world. Every time you speak one, it speaks you back a little. Do you understand? It takes payment in *self*. I watched better people than you fall in love with that feeling, and I watched what was left of them afterward, standing in fields, smiling, empty as—"

"—as him," I said.

The knight had stopped. Ten feet away, sword lowered, head cocked at an angle no living neck permits. And in the dark of its visor, the two pale lights dimmed, and steadied, and I would swear on every ledger I ever balanced that what moved behind them, in that moment, was sorrow.

"I had a word," it said. Almost gently. "Once. Mine was *sorrow* too, little speaker. We are all of us sorrows, in the end." The ember in its chest pulsed, guttered. "Give them back. Give the words back, and it stops hurting. It stops, I promise you, it stops—"

It lunged.

No more speeches. The grief folded away like a letter and the machine underneath came out, fast, impossibly fast for something so heavy, and this time there was no clever word waiting in my mouth because I had spent the only one I had and my legs were water and my nose was still bleeding and Vespera was screaming my name in a voice that tore straight down the middle—

So I did the only thing left. I grabbed for the word the way you grab a rope thrown to a drowning man, blindly, greedily, and I spoke again.

"*Caeth. Caeth. CAETH.*"

Three cuts. Three lines of white fire, crossing, closing — I didn't plan it, I couldn't have planned it, my hand drew shapes in the air and the world obeyed them, and the cage of light snapped shut around the knight with a sound like a cathedral door. The impact threw me onto my back. Above me the knight hammered against the inside of the fading lattice — once, twice — and then the light failed, and it stepped through the ruin of my working as easily as a man stepping through cobwebs, and raised its shadowed blade over my chest.

"*Foolish,*" it said, almost tenderly. "Words spent wildly. Now you are empty, and I am not."

Vespera hit it.

I don't know what a ghost's substance weighs, or whether rage has mass, but she struck the knight full-on and the collision lit the whole valley — a silent flash of white that bleached the grass, the stones, my own upturned face. When my vision cleared, she was on her knees in the trampled ground beside me, flickering like a failing lamp, half of her simply *gone*, and the knight had staggered back three paces with frost blooming across its armor where she'd touched it. Its socket-lights guttered.

It considered us. I felt the consideration like weather.

"Not tonight," it said at last, and the voice had gone distant, receding, a tide going out. "The words wake early in you. That is someone's design, little speaker. Ask *who wrote you*." The helm tilted. Somewhere inside it, something laughed — a short, awful, human laugh. "Ask the ghost what she wrote."

Then it simply walked backward into the treeline, and the grey trees closed around it, and the frost of its passing melted as if embarrassed.

* * *

For a long time neither of us moved.

The moot-ground settled around us. Wind returned to the pewter leaves. Far off, something that lived in this borderland resumed whatever it had paused to watch. I lay in the dead grass with my heart slamming and my right hand burning like a brand, and I stared up at a sky the color of tarnished silver, and I took inventory, because inventory was the only prayer I knew.

One clerk. One ghost, diminished. Zero weapons. One word, spent three times, and no idea of its price.

"Vespera." My voice sounded like someone else's. "Are you—"

"Do not ask me if I'm all right. I'm a century-dead woman who just headbutted a hollowed knight; the question answers itself." She lay down beside me, or arranged herself in the shape of lying down, staring at the same wrong sky. Half of her was missing — her left arm, part of her hair, a crescent of her face like a bite taken from the moon. It was growing back. Slowly. Like frost forming in reverse. "Are *you* all right is the better question, and I suspect the answer is no, and I further suspect you don't know yet."

I flexed my right hand. It obeyed me. It ached. Deep under the ache, coiled and patient, the emptiness the knight had promised was already whispering — I could feel the shape of the next word waiting somewhere below my tongue, and I could feel how much I wanted it, and the wanting frightened me more than the sword had. Swords, at least, stayed in scabbards until drawn. This lived *in* me. This had been in me my whole life, sleeping, and I had spent thirty-one years believing I was nothing but a man with neat handwriting.

"Who wrote you," I said. "That's what it said. Ask who wrote you. Ask the ghost what she wrote."

"Yes."

"It knows you. It spoke like it knew you."

"It would." Vespera's remaining eye fixed on the sky. "Hollowed knights are dead swordspeakers, Tamsin. I was a swordspeaker's — I was *with* the twelve. Of course it knows me." A pause, long enough that the wind filled it. "And it's right about one thing. Someone designed your waking. Words don't choose their speakers by accident. There's a hand in this. There's always a hand."

I sat up. My nose had stopped bleeding; my hand had not stopped burning. Across the moot-ground, the eleven fallen stones lay in their postures of collapse, and the twelfth stood blackened and alone, and I found I couldn't look at Ysolde's stone without my palm itching to touch it again.

"We need to get out of this valley," I said. "Before it comes back with friends. Before I—" I swallowed. "Before I say anything else."

"Agreed." She rose, patchwork and pale. "There's a rift. Unstable, but it opens near here — a tear where the borderland presses close against the mortal side. If we reach it, you can step back through. Briefly. Gather supplies. Think."

"Think," I repeated. "Yes. Thinking. That's the thing I'm best at."

I got my feet under me, and we started up out of the valley, away from the graves of the Oath, and I did not look back — mostly because I was afraid that if I did, I would see my own reflection in the First Stone, wearing a smile I had never learned.

The rift was exactly where she said, a hanging shimmer in the air like heat above a forge, and stepping through it felt like diving through a page. The world folded. Cold became lamplight; the smell of iron and rain became dust and vellum and tallow —

— and I stumbled out into the Guild's back archive, into the familiar dark that had been my whole life until midnight, and the lamps were lit.

Every one of them. And sitting at my own desk, in my own chair, with my own ledger open before him and a decanter of the good brandy poured into two glasses, was Master Corvane.

He did not look up from the page.

"Sit down, Eastwick," he said pleasantly, and turned the ledger toward me so I could read my own handwriting in it — entries dated years before I'd ever written them. "We should discuss the prophecy. And precisely how much of it the Guild has known, and for how long, and what we paid to keep it quiet."

He slid the second glass across the desk.

"You've had quite a night. Drink. You'll want your hands steady for what comes next."
