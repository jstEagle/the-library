## 7. The Guild Knew

The rift opened where a door had been.

That was how Tamsin understood it, in the first disoriented heartbeat after he stepped through: not that a doorway had appeared, but that the shop had *remembered* being a doorway, and had obligingly become one again. The borderland's grey light peeled away from his shoulders like wet cloth, and then he was standing on the worn boards of the Rare Exchanges' back room, among the smell of beeswax and cold iron and the particular dust that gathered only around things that did not wish to be catalogued.

His legs nearly went out from under him. The borderland clung — its silence, its too-wide sky, the way distance there was a suggestion rather than a law. For three days he had walked a road that remembered every foot that had ever crossed it, and now the floorboards under his boots were merely wood, honest and dumb, and some traitorous part of him grieved for it.

*Three days.* Had it been three days? Time in the borderland pooled and eddied like water finding its level. His body said three days; his bones said a year.

Behind him, the rift was already closing — a seam of pale gold unzipping itself along the grain of the air, patient as a healing wound. Through it, for one last moment, he could see the road: the long grey ribbon of it running between hills that were more memory than stone, and Vespera standing at its edge, her outline flickering like a candle flame deciding whether to stay lit.

"Sunrise," she had told him, when he'd made the decision. "There is a place where the road touches your world again — an old crossing, thin as paper. Go back. Find the book. There are names in it you will need before we go further, and I—" her voice had done something complicated then, grief folded four ways and pressed flat, "—I cannot say them myself."

Now he stood in the back room of the Guild of Rare Exchanges, and the book was forty feet away through two locked doors, sitting in its case in the vault where he himself had shelved it a hundred and six years ago.

A hundred and six years ago. He had written that acquisition date in his own neat hand, and until three nights past he had never once wondered what kind of man wrote tomorrow's date beside a prophecy about himself.

Tamsin Eastwick leaned against the wall and let himself shake. It seemed only professional to get it out of the way now, before anyone could see.

* * *

He had expected the shop to be alarmed. Locked, warded, humming with the quiet defensive spells the Guild paid half its profits to maintain. Instead the wards recognized him and parted like curtains, and the lamps lit themselves as he passed, warm and welcoming, and this courtesy felt worse than hostility. The building trusted him. The building had always trusted him. Eleven years of ledgers, eleven years of arriving before dawn and leaving after dark, eleven years of ink-stained fingers and perfect columns — the shop knew nothing else of him, and loved him for what it knew.

He moved through the front room slowly. The bottled storm sat on its shelf where he'd left it, swirling in its glass like a thought no one would finish. Beside it, the stolen shadow lay coiled in its cedar box, darker than the darkness around it, restless. Ordinary inventory. Impossible things, priced and shelved and waiting for buyers whose names Tamsin recorded but never learned.

He had believed, right up until the prophecy woke, that this work meant something. That keeping meticulous accounts of the impossible kept the impossible *accountable*. That somewhere in the balance of debits and credits there was a kind of justice, or at least a kind of order, and that order was a wall against chaos, and walls were good.

Vespera's voice, dry as old vellum: *"Ledgers, little keeper? You think the realms run on arithmetic?"*

"They run on something," he'd answered. "I've never seen anything run on nothing."

She had looked at him with eyes like frost forming, and said, "Then you have never seen the borderlands," and would say no more.

The prophecy-book lay in its display case in the inner vault, exactly where he'd left it. *Acquisition 4471. Provenance uncertain. Contents: apocalyptic verse, marginalia in an unknown hand. Value: negligible.* He had appraised it at twelve crowns. Twelve crowns, for the book that had spoken his name across a century.

His hands were steady as he unlocked the case. That steadiness frightened him more than trembling would have. Somewhere in the last three days, some load-bearing part of Tamsin Eastwick had quietly stopped caring about ordinary dangers, because it was busy bracing against the extraordinary ones.

He lifted the book free. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as skin, and beneath his fingers it warmed — faintly, unmistakably, the way a sleeping animal warms when touched. The marginalia, the unknown hand that was supposedly Vespera's future hand, stirred at the edges of his vision. He did not open it. Not yet. Sunrise, she'd said. He had hours yet before the crossing would bear his weight again, and there was a thing he needed to do first, a small human thing, almost embarrassingly so:

He wanted tea. He wanted to stand in the Guild kitchen and drink tea from the chipped blue cup he'd used for eleven years, and pretend, for the length of one cup, that he was still the man who had come to work yesterday morning.

* * *

The kitchen was not empty.

Corvane sat at the table with a pot of tea already steeping and two cups poured, and Tamsin's heart performed a slow, terrible somersault, because Master Corvane should have been in the capital, should have been weeks away, should have been anywhere but here at — Tamsin checked the clock out of pure habit — twenty minutes to sunrise.

"Sit down, Tamsin." Corvane's voice was mild. It was always mild. In eleven years, Tamsin had heard him raise it exactly once, over an apprentice who had mislabeled a jar of bottled thunderstorms, and the mildness afterward had been worse than any shouting. "You look dreadful. Sit. The tea's still hot enough to be worth drinking."

Master Aldous Corvane was sixty-some years old and built like a question no one wanted asked: spare, precise, silver at the temples, hands that were always either folded or doing something useful. He wore the Guild's grey the way other men wore armor. His eyes, when they settled on Tamsin, were the color of ledger-lines — ruled, straight, designed to catch deviation.

Tamsin did not sit. Every instinct that had kept him alive in the borderland for three days was screaming that standing was better, that standing meant a door at his back and a clear line to the window. But this was Corvane. This was the man who had taught him penmanship and provenance, who had once stayed up all night with him when the fever took him, cooling his forehead with a wet cloth and reading aloud from the shipping manifests because Tamsin found them soothing.

"You're supposed to be in Vael Corrin," Tamsin said. "The Meridian auction. You left nine days ago."

"I did." Corvane turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the handle with the table's edge. "And while I was there, a message reached me. From our night clerk. A junior keeper entered the vault without authorization, removed Acquisition 4471 from its case, and then vanished through a door that does not exist." He looked up. Mildly. "I confess I came back faster than I have ever traveled in my life."

"The wards let me in."

"Of course they did. They're keyed to you." Something moved behind the ruled grey eyes — not quite warmth, not quite regret. "They've been keyed to you since you were nineteen, Tamsin. Did you really never wonder why a guild of rare exchanges needs a ward-protocol keyed to a single junior clerk? We are careful people. We do not spend that kind of magic on penmen."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the sky was going the color of pewter at its eastern rim — not sunrise yet, but rehearsing for it.

"The book," Tamsin said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "You knew what it was."

"I knew it was important. I have known since I was younger than you are now, when my own master showed it to me and made me swear the same oath every Guildmaster has sworn for a hundred and six years." Corvane poured the second cup anyway, unhurried, steam rising between them like a veil. "We call it the Custody, informally. The formal term is longer and duller. Its substance is this: the Guild keeps the book. The Guild reads the book once a generation, to confirm the date has not changed. And the Guild ensures that the man named in the book"— the quarter-turn of the cup again, handle aligned, aligned, aligned —"*never learns his name is in it.*"

The floor tilted. Or perhaps it was only the world, rearranging itself around a new weight.

"Why?"

"Because of what you did three nights ago, presumably." Corvane nodded toward the back room, toward the seam in the air that had already sealed itself to invisibility. "Because the wards registered a crossing. Because a door that shouldn't exist opened inside my shop, and my keeper of ledgers walked out of it looking like a man who has seen where the world ends." He sighed — genuinely, Tamsin thought, though he was no longer certain he could trust himself to read this man at all. "I hoped I would have another thirty years. The date has held stable since the book was acquired. One grows optimistic."

"Corvane." Tamsin's hands had closed into fists without consulting him. "Why keep it secret? If the covenant—if the Oath—if all of it is real, why hide it? Why hide *me*?"

For a long moment the master simply looked at his apprentice, and the lamplight caught the lines of his face and deepened them, and Tamsin saw suddenly how tired he was, how long he had carried whatever he was about to set down.

"Because the Guild eats," Corvane said quietly, "from the broken places."

* * *

He talked for a long time. Tamsin stood with his back to the cold stove and let him, because interrupting would have meant admitting he could bear no more, and some stubborn accountant-core in him insisted on hearing the whole entry before closing the column.

The Guild of Rare Exchanges, Corvane explained, was older than its charter — older than the city, older than the kingdom's current name. It began as a company of factors trading at the seams: fae-grown silk, mortal iron, the small crossings where the two worlds brushed and bartered. When the great Oath was sworn — twelve swordspeakers binding both realms in mutual obligation, each realm owing the other passage and peace — the traders had prospered. Lawful trade, guaranteed crossings, prices fixed by treaty rather than terror.

"And then the Oath broke," Corvane said. "A hundred and six years ago. You know the histories — everyone knows the histories. The Twelve fell, the words scattered, the borders went rotten. Magic bleeds through where it shouldn't. Monsters cross where they shouldn't. Villages vanish. Rivers run backward." He lifted his cup, considered it, set it down untasted. "Do you know what else happens, Tamsin? Bottled storms sell for their weight in gold. Stolen shadows find eager buyers. Fear makes everything rare, and rarity is our entire business. A world with open, lawful crossings needs factors and clerks. A world with *wounds* needs us."

"That's—" Tamsin started, and found he had no word ready. *Monstrous* was too simple. Monstrous things at least knew they were monsters.

"It's arithmetic," Corvane said, and the word landed like a slap, because it was Tamsin's own word, his own creed, handed back to him with the blood still on it. "You taught me nothing, boy; I taught you. Order the account. Follow the numbers. And the numbers say this: a mended covenant means regulated crossings, common markets, magic returned to commons. It means the end of scarcity. It means the Guild becomes what our charter pretends we are — a warehouse concern with good handwriting." His mouth bent, not quite a smile. "Whereas a broken covenant, carefully maintained, carefully *farmed* — that is a harvest that never fails. Chaos is our crop, and we have been excellent farmers for a century. Every monster scare raises insurance premiums we hold shares in. Every blighted village produces refugees desperate enough to sell what should never be sold — memories, shadows, years of life — and we buy low. We buy *so* low, Tamsin. You know. You've recorded the purchases."

Eleven years of entries scrolled through Tamsin's mind, each line innocent alone, each line a grain, and now he saw the field. The widow in Harrowmere who sold her dead son's shadow for bread money. The ferryman who traded ten years of memory for a charm against the things in the water. He had written fair market value beside each one. He had balanced those columns to the copper.

"The prophecy," he managed. "The man named in it. Me. What were you going to do? Kill me?"

"What an imagination." Corveane sounded almost fond. "No. Nothing so crude. The Custody is simpler than murder. We watch you. We guide you — gently, over years, into a life so small and so orderly that prophecy has nowhere to put down roots. A man who catalogs bottles cannot reforge worlds. And if the date ever *did* change, if the book ever woke early—" He spread his hands, an accountant's gesture, helpless before an unfavorable figure. "There are measures. There have always been measures. My master described them to me the way I would one day describe them to you, and I remember thinking, even at nineteen: *surely it won't come to that.*"

"What measures?"

"Tamsin." For the first time, something in the mild voice frayed. "Please sit down and drink your tea, and let us discuss what happens next like the two rational men we have spent eleven years pretending to be."

"Answer the question."

Outside the kitchen window, the pewter sky had begun to blush. Minutes now. The crossing Vespera had shown him would be opening, a paper-thin place where the road remembered his world, and she would be waiting on the far side, flickering, running out of whatever ran out of ghosts.

"The measures," Corvane said, very softly, "involve a cell warded against speech. Words are how you will do whatever it is you're going to do — the book is quite explicit, and so are the fragments of older records we keep beneath the vault. A swordspeaker speaks, and reality listens. So the measure is silence. Permanent, comfortable, well-fed silence, in a room with no doors and no sound, for as long as the date holds." He met Tamsin's eyes. "You would live. I want that understood. Whatever else I am, I have never once lied to you, and I am not lying now. You would live a long time. You simply would not ever again be permitted to *matter*."

The horror of it arrived slowly, the way cold water arrives — ankle, knee, chest — and Tamsin realized distantly that his hands were shaking now, that the steadiness had finally broken, and that what was breaking underneath it was larger. Not fear. Fear he could have carried. This was the specific agony of rereading eleven years of entries and finding fraud in every margin. The fever-night and the wet cloth — had that been kindness, or husbandry? The promotion, the keys, the trust — had he been an apprentice, or an asset? A man, or a line-item the Guild had kept off the books?

"You taught me penmanship," Tamsin said. His voice came out strange, distant. "You taught me that a record is a promise. That whoever writes it owes the truth to whoever reads it."

"Yes."

"And you've been lying to me since I was nineteen years old."

"I have been keeping an account," Corvane said, "that you were not authorized to read. It is not the same thing." He rose, unhurried, smoothing his grey coat. "It is the same thing, actually. I have known that for decades. I simply chose, as we all choose, the version of the truth I could live inside." He glanced at the window, at the blushing sky, and Tamsin watched him make the calculation — saw it move behind the ruled eyes like a bead sliding down an abacus. "The crossing opens within the hour. Your ghost will be waiting. And you are going to walk out of my kitchen and through that door, and I am going to let you."

Tamsin blinked. "...What?"

"Because I am old, and because I am curious, and because — damn it all, boy — because I taught you penmanship and I would like to be wrong about how this ends." Corvane straightened his cuffs. "But understand what follows. The Guild is not me. The Guild is a hundred masters in a dozen cities, and most of them lack my sentimentality, and all of them have read the Custody. By tonight there will be hunters in the borderland. Not soldiers — we don't field soldiers. *Auditors.* Men and women trained for one purpose: to find the man named in the book and implement the measures." He picked up his hat from the table, turned it once in his hands. "Some of them, I should mention, are very old. Older than the charter. The Guild has employed certain former swordspeakers, over the century, who preferred service to death. Their words still work, Tamsin. Bent ones. Hollowed ones. You'll know them when you see them — they don't breathe unless they remember to."

The name for them surfaced from nowhere, from some page he'd catalogued and forgotten, and it tasted like ash: *hollow knights.*

"Why tell me this?" Tamsin whispered. "If you want me caught, why warn me?"

"Because I told you. I would like to be wrong." Corvane settled the hat on his head, and for one unguarded instant his face was just an old man's face, tired past the reach of arithmetic. "Reforge it or destroy it, the book says. Those are the only two outcomes it allows. But the book was written by a ghost, and ghosts, in my experience, only count the futures they can bear to look at." He paused at the kitchen door, back still turned. "There may be others. Off the page. Look for them."

Then he was gone, footsteps precise and fading down the corridor of the only home Tamsin had ever trusted, and the tea steamed untouched on the table, and outside the window the sky split open along its eastern seam like a letter being torn.

Tamsin stood in the empty kitchen for the length of one breath. Two. Then he took the chipped blue cup, drank the tea in three scalding swallows — because it was his cup, and it was his tea, and whatever else the last eleven years had been, this much had been real — and set it down, and went to answer a door that shouldn't exist.

Behind him, on the table, Corvane's untouched cup sat with its handle aligned to the table's edge.

Perfectly. Precisely. Like a man who had balanced every column of his life and found, in the final audit, nothing but red.
