## 8. Twelve Names, Eleven Graves

I had not said their names aloud in a hundred years.

That is a strange sentence to write with a ghost's hand, because a ghost has no mouth, no breath, no throat to carry sound. I speak the way ink speaks — by pressing myself into something that will hold me. For a century that something was the book. Now it was Tamsin Eastwick, walking beside me through a borderland that smelled of rain on old iron and flowers that had never learned the word *rot*.

He did not know I could feel his footsteps through the ground of the road, faint as heartbeats under wool. He did not know that when he frowned at the horizon — where the sky folded like wet paper left too long in a drawer — I felt the frown too, an itch behind eyes I no longer owned.

The road we walked remembered us both, though neither of us had ever been here before. That is what the borderlands are: memory with weather. The path bent where someone once hesitated. It widened where armies had passed, or funerals. Somewhere beneath our feet lay the fossil of a first kiss and the grave of a broken promise, and the grass grew greener over one than the other, though I never learned which.

"Vespera." His voice came out careful, the way a man speaks when he suspects the ground might answer. "You said twelve swordspeakers swore the Oath. Twelve names. You've given me eleven."

"I've given you none," I corrected him gently. "You haven't asked."

His jaw tightened. I watched it move through the translucent skin of my attention, and I felt the small stubborn pride in it, the ledger-keeper's need for completeness before he commits anything to paper. Good. Pride like that keeps men alive in places like this. It kept me alive long past the point where living should have been possible.

"Then I'm asking," he said. "All twelve. If I'm going to be hunted across the end of the world for something written in your book, I want to know whose book it is. Whose names are in it."

We stopped, then. Or rather, the road decided we would stop, curving around an ancient milestone half-swallowed by moss. Someone had carved letters into its face so long ago that the stone had begun healing over them like flesh over a wound, and only the deepest strokes still showed: two words in a script older than either realm's habit of writing things down.

*I remember.*

Tamsin knelt and brushed the moss away with his sleeve, gentle, almost tender, the way he handled the ledgers back in the shop — though he didn't know yet that I knew about the ledgers, about the bottled storm shelved between *acquisitions 4409* and *4411*, about the stolen shadow coiled in its jar like a sleeping eel. A man who catalogs impossible things with such care deserves, I think, to have some impossible things cataloged about himself. So I gave him this:

"The first swordspeaker was Halvard Ashgrave," I said. "He spoke the word that cuts. When he drew breath to swear the covenant, the air itself parted along a seam, and every blade in a thousand miles turned in its scabbard to face him. He was a blacksmith's son who never lost a duel, and he died old, in bed, which for a swordspeaker is its own kind of tragedy."

Tamsin wrote nothing down. There was nothing to write with. But I felt him memorizing anyway, filing each name into that meticulous interior archive of his, and it eased something in me that had been clenched for a century.

"Maren Duskwater," I continued. "She spoke the word that binds. A river-witch who traded her reflection for her power — she spent the rest of her life unable to see herself, and said it was a fair price. She drowned in the Sundering. Not killed. She walked into the flood deliberately, holding the seams together with her last breath while everyone else ran."

The moss under Tamsin's hand had gone cold. He noticed. Ghosts grieve coldly; it is all we have left of tears.

"Cassian Vole. The word that opens. Locksmith, thief, the only honest liar among us. He opened the way between realms with a spoken sentence, and when the Oath broke, he tried to open it again from the other side, and the way closed *through* him." I paused. "There was very little left to bury. We buried the lock."

"Eleven," Tamsin said quietly. "Keep going."

"Elian Thorne, the word that lies, God rest her and forgive me for what I'm about to tell you someday — she could make truth itself doubt its own name. Perrin Vale, the word that mends, a surgeon who sewed wounds shut with sentences. Odile Marchand, the word that remembers — she forgot everything else, gradually, year by year, until at the end she knew only the covenant and nothing of her own children, and she called it a fair price too. They all called it a fair price." My voice — my pressed-in, borrowed voice — went thin. "Sera Quill, the word that waits. Tomas Ironhale, the word that holds. Wren of the Low Fen, the word that listens. Bramble Cordwainer, the word that grows. And Ysolde—"

I stopped.

Not because I chose to stop. Because the road chose it for me. The borderland has its own opinions about what may and may not be said aloud, and grief, I have learned, is a kind of password it recognizes even better than names.

Tamsin looked up at me from the milestone. In the flat silver light of that place, his face was young and tired and sharp all at once, and there was ink on his fingers — real ink, from the ledger he'd been clutching when the shop folded around him — and something in my chest that hadn't been a chest in a hundred years ached at the sight of it.

"You were going to say a twelfth name," he said.

"Yes."

"And you can't."

"No," I said. "It isn't that I can't. It's that the name has been *taken*. Smudged. Blotted out of the record by someone who understood exactly how records work — someone who knew that a thing unwritten becomes a thing unremembered, and a thing unremembered becomes a thing undone."

Something moved across his face then — not fear, but recognition. The look of a man who has spent his life guarding ledgers suddenly understanding that erasure is not damage. Erasure is a decision.

"Who smudged it?" he asked.

"I did."

The words came out of me before I could weigh them, the way truth always does, heavy and graceless as a dropped anvil. Tamsin went very still. Even the road seemed to still, the wind holding its breath in the grass.

"Why?" he asked finally. Not accusatory. Worse — curious. Patient. The tone of an auditor who has found a discrepancy and intends to sit there until the numbers confess.

"Because the twelfth swordspeaker betrayed us," I said. "Or tried to. Or — no." I pressed the heel of a hand against eyes that weren't there, an old habit from a body I'd worn out a hundred years ago. "Because it isn't simple, and I am not ready to make it simple, and if I say the whole of it now, here, on this road, I will say it wrong, and a thing said wrong in the borderlands stays wrong forever. Do you understand? This place *keeps* what it hears. Every lie told here grows roots."

"Then don't say it here," Tamsin said. He rose, brushing moss and grave-dirt from his knees, and looked down the road to where it narrowed toward a distant line of hills that were, I knew, not hills at all but the shoulders of something sleeping. "Say it somewhere that forgives. Or don't say it at all. But give me this much now, since you won't give the name: how many shards are there?"

"Twelve," I said. "One for each word. One for each speaker. When the Oath broke at the Sundering, the covenant shattered into its components — the words themselves, crystallized, scattered like seed from a burst pod. Eleven fell where the eleven dead fell. The twelfth..."

I let the silence stretch. Tamsin waited. He was good at waiting. Ledger-men are.

"The twelfth shard was never found," I finished. "And the twelfth name cannot be read. And you should understand what that means, Tamsin Eastwick, keeper of ledgers, last of nothing in particular: the prophecy in my book does not say the covenant will be mended by a hero. It says it will be mended — or ended — by *the heir*. By blood. By inheritance. Which means whoever smudged that name knew that someday, someone would come asking after it. Someone with the right face. The right blood. Someone who might walk into a guild of rare exchanges one ordinary evening and find tonight's date inscribed in a century-old book, beside his own name."

The color left him slowly, like tide going out. I felt it leave — felt his pulse climb and steady itself, climb and steady, the discipline of a man who balances books for a living and knows that panic is just arithmetic done badly.

"You're saying the smudge wasn't cowardice," he said. "It was a seal."

"It was mercy," I said. "Or cruelty. I've had a hundred years to decide which, and I still don't know. All I know is that when I look at that page — my own page, my own handwriting, the entry I made the night the world broke — where the twelfth name should be, there is only a blur. And the blur is *warm*. Have you ever touched a warm smudge, Tamsin? Ink that refuses to dry for a hundred years? As if the name underneath is still alive in there. Still breathing. Still waiting to be legible again."

He was quiet for a long time. The road breathed around us. Far off, in the folded sky, something turned over in its sleep, and the light changed color the way light changes when a cloud passes — except there were no clouds here, only thoughts, and not all of them ours.

"Say the eleventh again," he said at last. "Ysolde. Just the name."

"*Ysolde,*" I said.

And the road shuddered.

It was a small thing — a tremor, like a horse flicking off a fly — but in the borderlands nothing is small. The moss on the milestone curled inward. The carved words *I remember* seemed to darken, wet with fresh meaning. And somewhere behind us, far back along the path we'd walked, I heard — felt — a door open. Not a physical door. A *ledger* door. The sound a book makes when a page turns in a room where no one is reading.

Tamsin heard it too. His head came up sharply, and his hand went to the sword he'd taken from the hollowed knight — the dead man's blade, still humming faintly with borrowed purpose.

"What was that?" he whispered.

"That," I said, and my voice shook, because ghosts' voices shake more easily than living ones, having less to hold them together, "was the borderland hearing a name it hasn't heard spoken in a hundred years. Names are keys here. And someone — *something* — has just checked whether Ysolde's key still fits."

We stood in the failing silver light, the two of us: a dead woman pressed into a living man's shadow, and a living man carrying a dead man's sword, standing on a road made of everyone's memories, in a world held together by a covenant that had failed them both.

"Come on," Tamsin said finally, and started walking, faster now, toward the hills that were not hills. "Whatever heard that name — I'd rather meet it on my feet."

I followed. Of course I followed. I have been following that family for a hundred years.

He didn't notice what I'd just thought. He didn't notice that I'd thought *family*, plural, as if the word applied to more than one of us, as if it had already chosen its shape in my mind and was only waiting for the courage to become a sentence.

But the road noticed. Roads here keep everything.

And far behind us, patient as ink drying, something began to follow too.

* * *

That night — if the borderland's slow silver dimming can be called night — we made camp in the lee of the milestone, and Tamsin slept badly, as I knew he would. Dreams come easy here and leave hard. I sat watch, or stood watch, or did whatever it is a ghost does instead of watching, and I opened the only place I have left to open: the pages of myself.

I reviewed the roll of the dead, as I do every night, the way he reviews his ledgers. Halvard, who cut. Maren, who bound. Cassian, who opened. Elian, who lied. Perrin, who mended. Odile, who remembered until remembering was all she was. Sera, who waited. Tomas, who held. Wren, who listened. Bramble, who grew.

And the eleventh. The one I could not say on the road. *Ysolde.* My sister. My mirror. My opposite number in every argument we ever had, right down to the last one, the one that ended with her blood on my hands and my name on her curse and a smudge of warm ink where a traitor's name used to be.

Eleven graves. I dug none of them. The world dug them, in a single terrible day, and then it asked me to keep the records, and I have kept them faithfully, meticulously, for a century — every date, every shard, every consequence — because someone must, because grief without accounting is just noise, because—

Because I could not bear to keep the twelfth record honestly. So I smudged it. With my own thumb, my living thumb, the night the ink was still wet. And the ink has never dried since, because guilt does not dry, and neither does love, and I had so much of both for the same person that the page may never close.

Tamsin stirred in his sleep and murmured something. A name, maybe. Half a syllable. The wind took the rest.

I leaned closer than a ghost should lean to a sleeping man, and I listened, the way Wren taught me to listen, the way the road was listening, the way everything in this wounded country listens now — for footsteps, for key-turning, for the sound of a family name being spoken by the wrong mouth at the right hour.

What I heard in his sleep-speech was not a word at all. It was a shape. The shape of a name I had blotted out, trying to surface through a living mouth the way a drowned thing surfaces through still water.

I pulled back, cold in all the places I no longer had.

"Not yet," I whispered to him, to the road, to whatever was following us out of the dark. "Let him walk a little farther before the ground gives way. Let him keep his ledgers balanced a little longer. There will be time enough for vertigo. There always is."

But the smudge on my page grew warmer.

And somewhere ahead, past the hills that were not hills, a market was setting up its stalls at the edge of the world — and the first price was already waiting to be named.
