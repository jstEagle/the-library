## 9. The Market at the World's Edge

The road to the market was not a road so much as an argument the borderland had with itself.

Tamsin followed Vespera through country that could not decide what it wanted to be. One moment they walked a lane of blackthorn hedges heavy with frost; the next, the same hedge bloomed with summer roses that wilted as he passed, as if his presence were a small and unwelcome winter. The ground beneath his boots changed texture like a sentence changing its mind—loam, then slate, then something warm and faintly pulsing that he chose not to examine too closely.

"Don't eat anything," Vespera said. She drifted ahead of him, translucent in the grey light, her hem dissolving into mist at every second step. She had been giving instructions since dawn, each one delivered in the tone of a woman reading a list of ways to die. "Don't drink anything. Don't give your true name. Don't accept a gift without paying for it, even if they swear it's free—especially if they swear it's free."

"You've said all of this twice."

"Because you nodded both times, which is not the same as understanding." She turned, or turned as much as a ghost turns, her face catching the light like smoke catching fire. "You are about to walk into the only place in either world where everything is honest and nothing is fair. The fae do not lie, Tamsin. They simply make truth do work it was never meant to do."

"And you're certain the first shard is there?"

"I am certain someone there knows where it is. Certainty is a currency I stopped carrying a hundred years ago." She looked away, toward the horizon, where the sky did something wrong—a slow bruising fold of color, violet bleeding into gold bleeding into a green no sky should wear. "There. See the lights?"

He saw them. Below the fold of the impossible sky, strung along a valley like beads on a black cord, hundreds of lanterns burned—not orange, but colors lanterns had no business burning: pale silver-green, deep sea-blue, one that flickered between red and the memory of red. Music reached them across the distance, thin and strange, a fiddle played by someone who had learned the instrument from the wind.

"The Market at the World's Edge," Vespera said. "It appears where the seam between realms wears thin. It has stood in forty places and none of them, and it will stand long after both worlds have finished forgetting each other." Her voice went quiet. "I sold things there once. A long time ago. When I still had things worth selling."

"What did you sell?"

"Ask me again in ten chapters," she said, and drifted on before he could decide whether that was a joke.

* * *

They came down into the valley as the sun set—or as the sun would have set, if this place acknowledged such vulgar regularity. Instead the sky dimmed out of courtesy, and the lanterns brightened to compensate, and Tamsin stepped onto the market road between two stone pillars carved with figures whose faces kept rearranging themselves when he wasn't looking directly at them.

The noise hit him first. Beneath the fiddle there were dozens of other musics, none of them agreeing: a drum made of stretched river-hide, a woman singing in a language that tasted of copper when he breathed it, the clatter of dice that landed showing emotions instead of numbers. And the smells—roasted chestnuts and woodsmoke, yes, ordinary things, but threaded through them ran scents that belonged to no kitchen in any world: rain falling upward, the inside of a seashell, the moment just after forgiveness.

The crowd parted around him the way water parts around a hot stone. He was mortal; he could feel it in how they looked at him, or rather in how they didn't. The fae glanced at him the way merchants glance at a coin of doubtful minting—briefly, assessing, already calculating what he might be worth.

"Tamsin Eastwick," Vespera murmured at his shoulder. "Keeper of ledgers."

"Yes?"

"Do not open your ledger here."

His hand had gone, without his noticing, to the satchel at his hip where the Guild's ledger rode—the book of impossible things, the bottled storm and the stolen shadow and eleven years of careful entries. He hadn't realized he'd been reaching for it the way a frightened man reaches for a wall. "Why not?"

"Because in that market, a book that records value is itself a thing of enormous value. And because—" she hesitated, and he heard, for the first time, something almost gentle in her voice, "—because tonight you may be asked to pay for something with more than coin. You'll want your hands empty for that."

The stalls crowded close on either side. He passed a booth where bottled voices hung in rows like preserved fruit—a child's laugh, a general's command, someone weeping in a language he didn't know—and another where a tailor measured customers with a ribbon that unspooled their futures, snipping off the bad years with shears of black bone. A creature with the body of a heron and the face of a tired old man tried to sell him a map of everywhere he'd never go. An entire stall was devoted to doors, standing open on nothing, each one breathing out the smell of somewhere else.

And through all of it ran the current he'd been warned about: the trading. Fae and mortals alike bent over scales that weighed things no scale could hold—an hour against a secret, a first kiss against a favor owed, a name against a name. No coins. Coins meant nothing here. Everything was paid for out of the self.

"There," Vespera said.

At the market's heart, where the lantern-light pooled thickest, stood a tent that was not a tent. It was built of stitched shadows and moon-fall, and above its entrance hung a sign written in letters that politely rearranged themselves into whatever language the reader knew best:

**THE LEDGERLESS LADY — APPRAISALS. PURCHASES. TRUTH AT MARKET RATE.**

"A broker," Tamsin said.

"The best," Vespera said grimly. "She dealt in oath-shards back when there were twelve whole ones to deal in. If anyone can point you to the Word That Cuts, it's her. But understand, Tamsin—" and now she took his sleeve, and though her fingers passed through the fabric he felt the cold of her grip settle into the bone beneath, "—she will not want coin. Coin is dead weight to her kind. She will want something alive. Something of yours. And she will be very, very good at making you believe you're getting the better bargain."

* * *

Inside the tent it was larger than outside, which Tamsin had learned to stop remarking upon. Shelves climbed into darkness overhead, laden with objects that hummed or slept or occasionally opened one glass eye to watch him pass. At a desk of driftwood and iron sat a woman who might have been thirty or three hundred, her skin the pale grey of pearl, her eyes the flat reflective silver of a mirror looked into too long.

"Well," she said, and her voice had harmonies in it, two notes sounding from one throat. "A mortal. A ledger-keeper. And—" her gaze slid past his shoulder, narrowed, and her smile thinned, "—a ghost I haven't seen in a very long time. Hello, little prophet. Still writing endings?"

"Still selling beginnings," Vespera said coldly. "We're told you know where a piece of the Oath has washed up."

"I know many things." The lady laced her fingers. "Knowledge is my stock in trade, after all. But you've come to buy, not chat. So." She leaned forward, and the lamplight bent toward her face as if drawn there. "You want the first shard. The Word That Cuts. It sits, at present, in a drowned chapel east of here, sunk in a lake that remembers being a valley. I can tell you how to find it. How to survive claiming it. What word must be spoken and what word must never be spoken in its hearing." She spread her hands. "Everything. For a price."

"Name it," Tamsin said, and hated how quickly he said it.

"Ohh." The lady's smile widened by one tooth exactly. "Eager. Eager is expensive, ledger-keeper, but I'll be generous, because I like the shape of your need." She rose, circled the desk, and walked around him slowly, and wherever she passed, the air smelled faintly of his own childhood—the specific dust of the Guild's archive stairs, the tallow-smell of his first candle, bread baking in a house he could barely picture anymore.

"You've been carrying something heavy," she murmured. "Something you've been keeping safe the way misers keep gold—not because it spends well, but because counting it is the only comfort you own." She stopped in front of him. "Show me your mother's face."

The words went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"I don't—"

"You do." Gently now, terribly gently. "I can see it in you, wrapped up tight, kept dry all these years. You were young when she went. Younger than you tell people. And you've spent your life keeping ledgers because ledgers never leave, isn't that right? Numbers stay. Entries don't die. Nothing in a book is ever taken from you." She tilted her head. "But a face, Tamsin Eastwick. A face fades whether you guard it or not. Every year a little less. You've already lost the sound of her voice—you notice, some nights, trying to hear it and finding only silence where it used to live. In ten more years you'll lose the rest. The shape of her jaw. The way her eyes did that thing at the corners when she laughed." She leaned close. "I'm offering to take what time is stealing anyway. Fair market rate for a depreciating asset. That's not cruelty, keeper. That's arithmetic."

"That's theft dressed as kindness," Vespera snapped.

"It's both," the lady agreed pleasantly. "All bargains are. Now—" and she held out one pearl-grey palm, "—the memory, and you walk out knowing where the Word That Cuts sleeps and how to wake it. Or refuse, and wander these borders until Corvane's knights find you, and they'll take far more than a face. They'll take everything down to the name."

"How do you know about Corvane?" Tamsin demanded.

"I know everything bought and sold in either world, and fear is the most liquid currency there is. Someone has been buying a great deal of it lately." She wiggled her fingers. "Choose. The market waits for no one, and neither, I think, does your ghost's century-old deadline."

Tamsin closed his eyes.

He tried to summon her—the discipline of it, the way he summoned any entry: deliberately, precisely, line by line. His mother. The kitchen with the crooked shutter. The flour on her hands. Her face—

And there it was, and God help him, there it was, faded like a photograph left in sun. He could see her and he could feel the edges of her going soft, the details slipping like sand through the fingers of his attention. Had her nose been narrow? Had there been a scar? He couldn't—there had been a laugh, there had been a laugh and a song she sang while stirring, and he could not for the life of him remember the song, only that it existed, only the hole where it lived.

She was already leaving him. Year by year, night by night, she had been walking out of his head without saying goodbye, and he had been so busy keeping other people's inventories that he'd never once noticed the most important asset running dry.

"If I give it to you," he said quietly, "what happens to it? Do you keep it? Does she—does the memory live somewhere?"

"She lives," the lady said, softening her voice in a way that was somehow worse than the salesmanship. "Memories don't die when they change hands, keeper. They're bought and treasured. Some nights I wear them, the way you'd wear a coat. I will know what it was to be loved by your mother. And you—" she smiled, "—you will be free. Lighter. Nothing weighing on you but the quest. Isn't that what heroes want? To travel unencumbered?"

"No," Tamsin said. "That's what tools want."

But he thought of the hollowed knight, of the word that had come out of him like a blade coming out of a wound, of Corvane's face in the shop, of the date in a century-old book with his own name beside it. He thought of the prophecy, and of the choice waiting at its end—reforge or destroy—and of how a man could hardly choose anything at all if he couldn't remember what love had looked like when it was given freely, before oaths, before guilds, before any of it.

He also thought: *if I forget her, then whoever she was dies twice.*

He also thought: *and if I fail, everyone else dies once more than that.*

Arithmetic. She'd called it arithmetic. Fine. He'd been doing sums since he was nine years old.

He opened his eyes and took her hand.

"I want terms," he said. "Written. Spoken plainly, in full, no cleverness, no truths doing work they weren't meant to do. You get the memory of my mother's face—all of it, the whole of her, not fragments I get to keep. In exchange you tell me where the chapel lies, how to enter it, how to claim the shard, what word wakes it, and one warning you think I'll need and don't know to ask for. All of it true. All of it complete."

The lady's eyebrows rose—one genuine expression crossing that mirror-face, gone in a heartbeat. "Well," she said, with something that in a human might have been respect. "Ledger-keeper indeed. Terms accepted and witnessed." Behind him, Vespera raised her hand, and the air chilled, and somewhere a bell that had not been ringing finished ringing.

"Now," the lady said. "Hold still. This is easier if you don't fight it. Most men fight it."

She pressed two fingers to his forehead.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the worst part—it did not hurt at all. It felt like exhaling after holding your breath for eleven years. It felt like setting down a load so old you'd forgotten it was a load and not part of your spine.

Her face went first, and it went gently, the way snow melts—no violence, just a gradual absence where a presence had been. He grasped for it. He grasped the way a drowning man grasps, and found only smooth dark water. The kitchen remained—the crooked shutter, the flour—but the figure moving through it had become a warmth without features, a presence with the face worn smooth as a river stone. Then the warmth began to go too, draining out of the scene like tide off a beach, and the kitchen grayed, and he understood with distant horror that soon he would not even miss her, because missing requires a shape to miss.

Then it was done.

He stood in the tent. The lamps burned. Somewhere outside, the fiddle played on. He felt—he took inventory, because inventory was all he had left—light. Unbearably, terribly light. There was a grief in him with no object, mourning something it could not name, a phantom ache where a limb used to be.

"There," the lady said softly, and something in her voice had changed—the merchant gone, and underneath it, briefly, something ancient and almost sorry. "You see? Painless. Time was eating it anyway; now it's safe forever." She pressed something into his palm: a small disc of black glass, cold as lake-bottom. "East of here, past the Weeping Stones, there's a lake shaped like a lung. Dive where the water is darkest. The chapel door opens for those who knock with a debt unpaid—that's your warning, the one you didn't know to ask for. The shard tests every claimant. It will offer to cut away whatever hurts you most." Her mirror-eyes held his. "After tonight, keeper, I suspect it won't have to try very hard."

He turned and walked out of the tent. He was aware, distantly, of Vespera following, of the market noise closing over him again, of lanterns burning their impossible colors. He walked until the stalls thinned, until the music faded, until he stood alone at the edge of the market road looking east at hills he could not see in the dark.

"Tamsin." Vespera's voice, low. "Look at me."

He looked at her. Ghost-light, smoke and sorrow.

"Say her name," Vespera said.

"My mother's?"

"Yes. Say it. Quickly, before—"

He opened his mouth. And found nothing. Not the name, not the shape of the name, not even the certainty that there had ever been a name to say. The memory of her face was gone, and it had gone through the name like a lock through a door, taking everything behind it. She had not said the memory alone. She had taken the key, and the room, and the house around the room.

His knees gave. He sat down hard in the dirt of the road, and the ledger fell from his satchel and lay open between them, and he stared at it—at eleven years of meticulous entries, bottled storms and stolen shadows, columns and columns of things accurately recorded—and understood, finally, with a clarity that felt like the Word That Cuts already in his chest:

Some things cannot be entered in a ledger. Some losses do not balance. He had spent his whole life believing that if he wrote everything down, nothing could truly be taken from him. And the very first price the quest had asked had proved the lie of it in a single breath.

Vespera knelt beside him—knelt, though ghosts needn't kneel—and her cold hand settled on his shoulder, resting on the fabric rather than through it, which he suspected cost her something.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I should have told you what she'd really take. I've watched that market for a hundred years and I still flinch at it." A pause. "But listen to me, Tamsin Eastwick. Listen, because this matters. You think you've lost her. You haven't. Not all of her." She tapped his chest, and her finger was ice through cloth and skin. "Memory is what the mind keeps. Love is what the keeping leaves behind. You don't remember her face anymore—but watch. Watch what your hands do when you meet someone grieving. Watch how you count the poor and the overlooked in your precious ledgers, the ones the Guild says aren't worth an entry. That's her. That's what she built in you before you could talk. She isn't in your head anymore." Her voice dropped. "She's in what you do."

Tamsin sat in the dirt a long moment. Then he picked up the ledger, closed it, and stood.

"East," he said. His voice came out scraped but steady. "Past the Weeping Stones. A lake shaped like a lung."

"Yes."

"Then let's not waste what she cost."

They walked east under the wrong-colored stars, and behind them the market sang on, spending other people's yesterdays. And in his pocket, the black-glass disc grew colder with every mile—as if the lake it came from were already reaching for him, patient as deep water, hungry as a debt.

He did not yet know what waited in the drowned chapel. He did not yet know that the shard would speak to him before he ever touched it, in a voice that sounded unsettlingly like his own.

But the water knew. And the water had been waiting a hundred years for an Eastwick to come knocking.
