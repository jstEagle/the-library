## 10. Shard One: The Word That Cuts

The chapel had been drowning for two hundred years, and it had not yet finished.

We came down to it at low tide, when the sea withdrew like a debtor leaving town and left the marsh exposed — black mud ribbed with the bones of a road that no cart had traveled since before the covenant broke. Briar went first, her bare feet finding purchase where my boots slid, and she moved across that treacherous ground the way water moves: without argument, without effort, as though the mud remembered being a riverbed and still deferred to things that flowed.

I carried the ledger.

Old habit. Even here, even now, I had bound the notes into a waxed leather case and slung it across my back, because some part of me believed — stubbornly, stupidly, comfortingly — that if I could write it all down, catalog it, enter it in neat columns under neat headings, then the world would remain the kind of place that could be audited. *One (1) chapel, submerged. One (1) shard of the original Oath, location per prophecy-book, folio 214. One (1) ghost, condition: fading. One (1) fae outcast, motive: debt.* The columns balanced. They always balanced. It was the only prayer I knew.

Vespera walked beside me, or rather drifted beside me, her feet a half-inch above the mud and trailing light the way a struck bell trails sound. She had grown quieter as we neared the coast. Not calmer — quieter. There is a difference, and I have learned to hear it. Calm is a full cup set down carefully. Quiet is a cup with something poured out of it.

"You're doing the thing with your mouth," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you recite inventory to keep from screaming."

"I don't scream," I said. "It's unprofessional."

"Ysolde said almost exactly the same words once." And she turned away before I could answer, which was becoming a habit of hers, dropping my name's history into the water between us and refusing to fish it back out.

* * *

The chapel rose from the mud ahead of us like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down to die and been too dignified to finish. Its spire leaned east, its walls were furred green-black with weed, and its windows — great lancet windows that must once have thrown colored light across kneeling congregations — were gone entirely, so that the sea had filled it like a cup and drained it again ten thousand times, and the salt had written its own scripture on every stone.

Briar stopped at the threshold, which was a doorway with no door, an arch of barnacled granite framing darkness and the smell of brine and old iron.

"It doesn't want me in there," she said simply.

"The chapel?"

"What sleeps in the chapel. The old covenant was sworn by mortal tongues, and what remains of it remembers whose side it stood on. If I cross, it will know me for fae, and it will not be gentle about knowing." She looked at me, and her eyes caught what little light the overcast sky allowed and gave back too much of it, gold shot through with green, beautiful in the way that weather is beautiful when it is about to be dangerous. "Go alone, ledger-keeper. We will hold the door."

Vespera's light flickered. "Tamsin. Before you go in — listen to me properly this time. When you find the shard, you will want to speak it. The word will be in your mouth before you decide anything. That is how swordspeech works; that is how it worked for all twelve of them. The word arrives, and it feels like remembering, and remembering feels like truth, and truth feels like permission." Her voice did something I had never heard it do. It shook. "Do not mistake any of those feelings for choice."

"And if the word arrives anyway?"

"Then speak it once. Only once. And count your cost while you can still count."

She would not say more, and I did not ask, because I was already afraid of the answer and I have always been a coward about answers that arrive early.

* * *

Inside, the chapel was a cathedral of tide.

Water stood waist-deep in the nave, black and perfectly still, holding the broken vault above it upside down in reflection so that it seemed the roof went down forever instead of up. Light came through the empty windows in long grey blades and lay across the water like laid-out steel. Along both walls, stone figures knelt in niches — saints, or kings, or the twelve, I couldn't tell; their faces had been eaten to smoothness by salt until they were all the same face, which is perhaps the most honest portrait any faith ever commissioned. Their hands were raised. All of them, raised palm-outward, as if in blessing, or surrender, or the universal gesture of *stop*.

I waded. The cold climbed my legs like a slow animal. Somewhere ahead, at the altar, something glowed.

Not brightly. The way a coal glows under ash — a warmth of light, a suggestion, as if the chapel were keeping it alive out of habit. I reached the altar steps and pulled myself up onto dry(ish) stone, and there it was.

A sword hilt, driven point-down into the altar itself, snapped off a hand's width above the guard. The blade was gone. But along the break, in the wound of the metal, something burned: a splinter of light no longer than my smallest finger, shaped like nothing and everything, like a word waiting to be pronounced.

The shard.

My hands, those meticulous, ledger-keeping hands, reached for it before I told them to.

And the chapel woke.

* * *

It did not wake loudly. That was the horror of it. No grinding of stone, no rush of water. Simply: the reflections in the flooded nave stopped reflecting. Every inverted arch, every drowned saint doubled in the black water, turned — slowly, all together — to look at me.

And the kneeling statues in their niches lowered their raised hands.

Palm by palm, finger by finger, with the patience of centuries, twelve smooth-faced figures folded their gestures of blessing into gestures of reaching, and their mouths — mouths that had been worn featureless — opened, and from each of them came a single syllable, layered and overlapping, a chord of one note sung by a choir of stone:

"*Sssss—*"

The first letter of the word.

I understood then, with the clarity that fear grants, that this chapel was not a reliquary. It was a throat. The whole building was built around the shard the way a scabbard is built around a blade, and it had been holding the word in its mouth for two hundred years, and I had just put my hand around its tongue.

"*Take it,*" Vespera's voice came faintly through the doorway, thin as thread across water. "*Tamsin, take it NOW—*"

I took it.

* * *

The world ended and began in the same instant, and I cannot fully render what happened, because what happened was not made of events but of meanings, and meaning does not survive transcription any better than music survives a fire. But I will try, because I am a keeper of ledgers, and trying is the whole of my trade.

When my fingers closed on the shard, the word entered me.

Not through the ears. Through the *name*. I felt it settle into the part of me that knows itself as Tamsin Eastwick, keeper of ledgers for the Guild of Rare Exchanges, son of a woman whose face I still owned, and it read me the way I read a column of figures — swiftly, totally, without mercy — and then it offered itself to be spoken.

And oh, it was *beautiful*. That is the part no one warns you about. Not power. Beauty. The word was the shape of a cut made so cleanly that the two halves of the severed thing do not even know they've been parted; it was the edge of morning; it was the exact line between yes and no drawn by someone who loved both. I felt what the first swordspeaker must have felt, kneeling where I knelt, and I understood why they had done it, why anyone would bind two worlds on such a word, because holding it was like holding dawn and being asked, politely, to decide whether the sun should rise.

I spoke it.

I did not choose to. I want that recorded honestly, in my own hand, in my own book: I did not choose. The word arrived, and it felt like remembering, exactly as she'd said, and remembering felt like truth, and I spoke it —

— and the chapel screamed.

Every statue, every reflection, every drop of standing water found my single syllable and multiplied it, gave it body, gave it edge. A blade of pure utterance leapt from my mouth and struck the flooded nave, and the water parted — not sloshed, *parted*, a canyon opening in the black mirror from wall to wall, floor bared, fish and silt and two hundred years of drowned leaves hanging suspended in walls of held-back sea. The broken vault above cracked along lines of light. The saints' faces re-formed for one terrible instant — I saw them, twelve faces, young and fierce and frightened, the faces of people who had once stood where I stood — and then they crumbled, and the canyon of water closed with a sound like a book slamming shut, and the shockwave of the word came back along my own arm like a debt called due.

It went through me looking for something to take.

I felt it pass through my left hand and leave the memory of learning to hold a pen. Through my shoulder and leave the ache of a fall from a roof when I was nine — the ache stayed, the *fall* left; I knew I had fallen, I could prove it from the scar, but the falling itself was gone, a room in my house with the furniture removed. Through my chest, and took — I didn't know what. That was worst. Something was missing and I could not inventory the absence, and for a man whose entire soul is a ledger, an unrecorded loss is a kind of bleeding.

I was on my knees. I was tasting copper. The shard in my fist was dark now, spent or sleeping, and cold as a coin at the bottom of a well.

"*Once,*" Vespera was saying, and her voice came from very close, from everywhere, from inside the ringing in my skull. "*You spoke it once. Stop. Stop now, Tamsin. Put it away. PUT IT AWAY.*"

But here is the thing I have not told anyone, the thing I am setting down only because a ledger owes the truth even when the truth is disgraceful:

Some part of me did not want to stop.

The word was still in me. Diminished, wounded, but coiled at the base of my mind like a second heartbeat, and I could feel the other words behind it — the ones to come, the binding word, the opening word, the lying word, the ending word — the way you feel the rest of a staircase in the dark after climbing only the first step. And I wanted them. God forgive me, sitting in the ruins of a drowned church with my memories leaking out of me like wine from a cracked cask, I *wanted* them, the way the starving want bread and the drowning want air, without shame, without thought, without self. For one whole breath — one breath in which I was nobody and nothing except a mouth that a word lived in — I was happier than I have ever been in my meticulous, balanced, lonely life.

Then I saw my own reflection in the water that had rushed back into the nave, and the face looking up at me was smiling, and it was not my smile. It was hungrier. It had my features the way a glove has a hand.

That ended it. I wrapped the shard in three layers of cloth from my coat, bound it like contraband, and shoved it deep into the ledger-case against my spine, where it lay against the waxed pages like a knife against a throat.

* * *

Briar caught me at the doorway. I don't remember crossing the nave. I remember her arms, unexpectedly strong, and her voice saying something in a language that sounded like frost forming, and then we were on the mudflats and the tide was turning and the chapel behind us was filling with sea again, patient as ever, keeping whatever was left.

Vespera hovered before me, and her light was guttering — actually guttering, like a candle in a draft, and I realized with a lurch that proximity to the spoken word cost her substance, that she had shouted into that chapel knowing it would burn her, and had done it anyway.

"How much?" I asked her. My voice came out scraped.

"Nothing that matters." Which is ghost-language for *a great deal.*

"Tamsin." She drifted closer, and for once she did not look a century old. She looked frightened, and young, and it occurred to me distantly that she had probably been both when all this started. "What did it take? Tell me exactly. Write it down if you have to. What's missing?"

I searched myself the way I search a miscounted page, line by line, and reported: "A fall from a roof when I was nine. Holding a pen the first time. Something else — I don't know what something else."

Her light steadied. Then dimmed further, which I would learn later meant she was choosing honesty over mercy.

"That's how it starts," she said. "That's how it went for all of them. First it takes little things — a fall, a flavor, the name of a street you grew up on. And you think, *I can spare that, it was only a fall.* And the word grows roots, and you start speaking it for smaller reasons, and it takes bigger things, and you let it, because by then you can't remember clearly why the big things mattered." She paused. "Ysolde kept a list. Of everything the speech took from her. She called it her bill of sale. By the end, the list was longer than her prayers, and she could still recite the list and not remember the mother it was about."

Wind came off the returning tide, cold and smelling of everything the sea had ever taken. Briar stood a few paces off, wringing seawater from her hair, watching me with those gold-green eyes that missed nothing.

"You heard it," I said to her. "The word. What did it sound like?"

She considered me for a long moment.

"It sounded," she said, "like the moment before a promise. The good kind." And then, softer, with something that was almost pity: "Which is the most dangerous sound there is, ledger-keeper. Everything cruel in both our worlds began as a beautiful promise."

We walked back across the flats as the water followed us home, and I wrote the entry that night by firelight in a copybook, in my careful merchant's hand:

*Shard One recovered. Word spoken: once. Cost: two memories certain, one unknown. Warning, for whoever reads this — including, especially, myself: the power is not a tool. It is a hunger that wears your face. Count the cost while counting is still possible.*

I closed the book. And in the silence after, in the ordinary dark of an ordinary camp, I noticed that my fingers had begun, without any instruction from me, to trace the shape of the word against my knee — slowly, hungrily, again and again, like a man touching a scar to make sure it's real.

Two days' walk south, at a crossroads inn where the fires burned slightly the wrong color, a broad-shouldered man in guildmaster's grey was already asking the locals about a clerk who passed through with a ghost and a fae.

He was smiling when they described me.

It was not a kind smile. But it was patient — and I have learned since that Corvane's patience was the most expensive thing the Guild ever owned, and that he had decided, somewhere on that road, exactly what my price would be.
