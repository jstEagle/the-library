## 11. Corvane's Offer

Corvane had dressed carefully for the occasion.

Not in the Guild's formal regalia — the silver-and-slate that made the masters look like crows at a funeral, all beak and black feather and appetite. No. Tonight he wore the clothes of a reasonable man: a coat of undyed wool, a plain shirt, boots polished but not ostentatious. A man you might meet at any respectable counting-house in Greyharrow and trust with your savings. He had learned long ago that power dressed plainly was more persuasive than power dressed richly. Rich men wanted things from you. Reasonable men only wanted to *talk*.

He sat in the little parlor above the shop on Candlewick Lane — Tamsin Eastwick's parlor, though the boy had never thought of it as anything so warm as a home — and waited for him to come through the rift.

The rift would bring him. It always did. The seams between worlds were like old wounds; once torn, they wept along their old lines forever. Corvane had watched this particular seam for three days now, since the ledger-room's wards had shrieked and spat out a boy who smelled of borderland mist and something older, something that made the Guild's oldest instruments tremble in their cases like dogs before a storm.

Swordspeech. In the Eastwick line, no less. After a hundred years of careful, patient nothing.

He poured two cups of tea while he waited. Not the Guild's bitter tannin brew but real tea, white tea from the eastern provinces, worth more by weight than the bottled storm currently catalogued in Vault Nine. He set the pot on the trivet, arranged the cups just so — one nearer his own chair, one across, angled toward the door as if its drinker were expected, because expectation was half of hospitality — and then he folded his hands and considered the room.

It told him everything about Tamsin Eastwick that the ledgers already had.

A single chair worn to the shape of one body. Books shelved by acquisition date rather than author or subject — the habit of an auditor, not a reader. A cold hearth swept clean every morning regardless of whether it had been lit the night before. One cup in the cupboard, one plate, one knife. And on the table by the window, a small brass instrument for measuring humidity, kept polished to a mirror shine, because a keeper of impossible things needed to know exactly how damp the air was when he recorded a bottled storm.

*One of everything,* Corvane thought. *A life inventoried down to the last decimal.*

The boy had spent twenty years learning to want nothing, which meant there was nothing to hold over him — and nothing to anchor him either. Such men drifted beautifully when you pulled the right thread.

The air above the hearth split with a sound like wet paper tearing, and Tamsin Eastwick came through it sideways, half-falling, one hand pressed to a shallow cut along his forearm where the seam's edge had kissed him. His eyes went first to the exits — window, door, the cold fireplace — and then to Corvane, and Corvane watched the recognition move through the boy's face like frost moving across glass.

"Master Corvane." He straightened. Even now, even bleeding, he straightened like a clerk caught late to audit. "Sir. I can explain—"

"No need." Corvane gestured to the second cup. "Sit. Your tea is getting cold."

"I didn't pour myself—" The boy stopped. Recalculated. Corvane could nearly see the abacus clicking behind those gray eyes: *two cups, poured before I arrived, therefore expected, therefore known.* "How long have you been waiting?"

"Three days. Sit down, Eastwick."

Tamsin sat. Of course he sat. Twenty years of Guild discipline did not dissolve in a fortnight of prophecy and ghosts. He took the cup but did not drink, holding it the way he held dangerous acquisitions — firmly, at a distance, cataloguing it for threat.

"You've been in my rooms."

"The Guild holds the lease on every building on Candlewick Lane," Corvane said mildly. "We have always held it. Why do you think your rent never rose?"

Something flickered behind the boy's eyes — the first hairline crack in that meticulous composure. Good. Corvane let the silence stretch, sipped his own tea, and studied the cut on the boy's arm with a connoisseur's interest. Borderland wound. Already knitting wrong, the skin pinking at the edges like a page left too near a flame.

"It will fester," Corvane said. "The wound. Borderland cuts do. They don't heal; they *remember*. In a week you'll dream of it. In two you'll start leaving pieces of yourself on that side of the seam without noticing — a word here, a memory there. I've seen it happen to better-prepared men than you."

"Why are you here?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"Because you're sitting in my chair, in my house, drinking my tea, holding one of my shards in a warded case against your ribs — yes, we know about the drowned chapel, Eastwick, the river told us, the river tells us everything — and because I am the last person in either world who is going to tell you the truth."

He leaned forward then, and let the reasonable-man mask slip just enough that something older showed beneath: not menace, precisely. *Certainty.* The certainty of a man who has done arithmetic no one else has bothered to check.

"Ask me what the Guild does with the borderlands."

Tamsin's jaw tightened. "You trade in them. Rare exchanges. Impossible—"

"We warehouse them," Corvane said. "There is a difference, and the difference is profit. Do you know what happens to a storm in a bottle? Nothing. That is its value. A storm unbound levels a county. A storm contained appreciates. Chaos, Eastwick — raw, leaking, covenant-rotted chaos — is the only commodity in history whose price only rises. Every year the Oath decays further, and every year the borderlands bleed a little more wildness into our world, and every year the Guild grows richer containing it. We do not suffer from the broken covenant. We *dine* on it."

He watched the boy absorb that. Watched the auditor's mind do what it was built to do — follow the numbers to their ugly conclusion.

"The hollowed knights—"

"Are inventory control." Corvane shrugged. "Dead swordspeakers make excellent guards. They cannot be bribed, they cannot unionize, and they cannot remember why they objected to any of this. Before you ask: no, we did not kill them all. Most of them died the way swordspeakers die, which is stupidly and young, defending a covenant that had already abandoned them. We merely... declined to let them rest. Waste not."

"And the prophecy." Tamsin's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used for counting out someone's ruin. "The book. My name in it. You knew."

"We have known for one hundred years that an Eastwick would sit where you're sitting tonight." Corvane set down his cup. "Do you imagine the Guild acquired that ledger by accident? We have bought, sold, buried, and burned eleven copies of Vespera's book across four generations, and each time the date arrives, we send someone to sit in this chair and offer this exact conversation. You are the fourth. The other three walked away. Two lived long, comfortable lives. One is buried under a very nice apple tree in the Fenmark country, and I'll thank you not to ask which fate belonged to which."

"What happened to the third?"

"He tried to steal the shards instead." Corvane smiled thinly. "The hollowed knights found him at the ford. It took them some time. He was, after all, a swordspeaker."

The fire was still cold in the grate, but the room felt colder anyway. Tamsin set his untouched tea down with a click precise enough to be contempt.

"So this is the offer."

"This is the context. The offer comes after, and it's better than you deserve." Corvane stood and moved to the window, hands clasped behind his back — the posture of a man surveying an estate rather than a street. Below, Candlewick Lane lay quiet, lamplit, ordinary. "Here is what I know about you, Tamsin Eastwick. You have no family. Your mother died owing debts the Guild quietly forgave, because we found her face useful in a bargain once and prefer our assets unmuddied by grief — ah, there it is, that flinch. Yes, we know about the market. We know what you paid, and we know you can't remember her smile anymore, only that you used to be able to, and that the absence wakes you at night like a toothache. You have no friends. The ghost doesn't count; she's using you, and poorly. You have no future except the one written in a dead woman's book, and that future ends one of two ways: reforging, or destroying. Both ways, you die. Reforgers burn up in the words. Destroyers get hunted by everything that liked the old arrangement, which is everyone with power, which is us."

He turned back. Let the boy see his face open, honest, almost kind.

"So here is the offer. Walk away. Tomorrow morning you resign your post — with honor, with full pension, with a letter of recommendation that will open doors in six kingdoms. The Guild settles upon you a stipend of four hundred crowns a year for life. We purchase you a house wherever you like — somewhere with good light for reading, I think, you strike me as a man who'd like the coast. And we perform a small procedure. A forgetting. The prophecy-book's claim on you dissolves the moment you stop being the man named in it, and we have art enough to make you *stop being him*. You'll wake remembering a quiet career, a modest competence, a mother whose face you'll finally get to keep, because we'll give you back the memory we bought — restored, pristine, hers. You'll never think of the borderlands again except as a place other people lose shipments in."

Corvane crouched slightly, bringing himself level with the seated boy, and softened his voice into the register he saved for the truly important transactions.

"You cannot win, Eastwick. Not because you lack power — you have more raw gift than any speaker in a century, and frankly it terrifies me — but because you lack *want*. You've spent your whole life wanting nothing, guarding nothing, belonging to nothing. Men like that don't forge covenants. They file reports. Meanwhile, everything that wants the covenant broken or mended wants *you* spent like coin to do it — the ghost, the fae, the underground fanatics in Greyharrow, all of them. Everyone you meet from this point forward intends to spend you. Except me. I am offering you the only thing anyone in this entire affair has offered: the chance to keep yourself."

He straightened, retrieved the teapot, and refreshed the boy's cup — a small domestic gesture, deliberate as everything else.

"Consider it as an auditor would. Two ledgers side by side. In the first, you die at the end of a sentence someone else wrote a century ago. In the second, you live to ninety in a house by the sea, with your mother's face whole in your memory and no one's name in your mouth but your own." He tapped the table twice. "I know which column I'd rather die in. Sleep on it. The Guild is patient; it's how we got rich. But the wound on your arm won't wait, the ghost will push, and the borderlands are already pulling at the loose thread of you. So decide quickly, boy. Decide while deciding is still something *you* can do."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past on Candlewick Lane, utterly indifferent.

Then Tamsin Eastwick looked up, and Corvane saw — with a satisfaction so keen it bordered on pleasure — that the boy's hands were shaking.

"You said my mother's face," Tamsin said slowly. "You said the Guild 'found it useful.' Useful *how*, Master Corvane? What did she trade it for?"

And there it was. The question Corvane had hoped he was too disciplined to ask — the one thread that, pulled, would unravel the neat little knot of resignation the boy had been tying around himself all evening. Curiosity. The auditor's disease. The Guild had cultivated it in him for twenty years like a gardener cultivates a graft, and now it bloomed at the worst possible moment.

For the first time that evening, Corvane found he did not have a prepared answer.

He recovered, of course — smoothed it over with a small smile, gathered his coat, murmured something about the Guild's records being available to valued retirees — but the boy saw the hesitation. Corvane watched him see it. Watched the shaking hands go still. Watched something settle behind those gray eyes that had not been there before: not fear, not greed, but the cold bright arithmetic of a man who has just realized his ledger has been kept by someone else, in his own name, all along.

At the door, Corvane paused.

"Sleep on it," he repeated. "But not too long. The knights are already moving, Eastwick. They can smell the shard on you. By dawn, the choice may not be yours to make at all."

The door clicked shut behind him. On the table, two cups of tea went cold side by side — one drunk, one untouched — and upstairs, in the borderland mist pressing close against the windows like a tide against glass, something ancient began, softly, to sing.
