## 12. Refusal and Reckoning

The offer hung in the air of Corvane's study like smoke that refused to rise.

I stood at the edge of his carpet — the good carpet, the one he only unrolled for clients whose coin smelled of desperation — with blood drying on my sleeve where the rift's edge had kissed me on my way through, and I listened to the most dangerous sentence ever spoken to me in that shop full of impossible things.

"Name your price, Tamsin."

He said it gently. That was the horror of it. Corvane had a voice for every occasion, and this one was the voice he used for widows and orphans selling off the last of a dead man's estate: soft, patient, kind in the way a trap is kind to the foot it closes on.

Behind him, the ledgers of Rare Exchanges stretched along their shelves, forty years of my own careful hand cataloguing bottled storms and stolen shadows and griefs decanted into amber glass. My whole life, alphabetized. I knew every spine by touch. I had thought that knowledge meant something. Standing there, I understood it meant only that I had been very useful, and that usefulness is the leash they put on you before they show you the collar.

"You want me to walk away," I said.

"I want you to *live*." He came around the desk, unhurried, a man adjusting his cuffs before dinner. "There is a difference, though I know prophecy-poisoned young men rarely see it. Walk away from Vespera's book. Walk away from the shards. Let the covenant rot as it has rotted for a century. The borderlands will bleed a little longer. Monsters will wander where monsters wander. And you will grow old behind a counter, Tamsin, with ink-stained fingers and a pension and no one hunting you."

"And the Guild keeps its trade," I said. "Chaos is good for business."

His smile flickered — not with guilt. With *pride*. That was worse.

"Order is bad for business," he corrected mildly. "A sealed border means no contraband moonlight, no bottled fae-songs, no shadow-trade. A mended one means the old enforcement, the tithe-years, the compulsion — and believe me, boy, the tithe-years were *worse* for everyone except the twelve who sat atop them. But a leaking seam? A seam that drips wonders and horrors both?" He spread his hands, a merchant displaying wares. "Every leak is a ledger entry. The world as it is pays my clerks' wages. Why would I want it fixed?"

"Because people die in the leaks." I thought of the drowned chapel, of the hollow knight's empty face, of the blighted edge Briar would later show me — but that hadn't happened yet; that night all I had was the chapel, the word that cuts still humming in my bones like a swallowed bell. "I've seen what comes through."

"Yes." He sighed, genuinely regretful, which somehow made it filthier. "Collateral. The Guild did not make the world, Tamsin. We simply keep accounts in it. Now—" he opened a drawer, and light spilled out, gold-soft, the color of safety, "—this is a purse that never empties. This is a deed to a house in a city where the Guild holds no writ. And this—" a slim black folio, bound in something that wasn't leather, "—is the last copy of your page in the prophecy-book. One match and the future stops knowing your name. You could be no one again. You'd be amazed how many men would kill for that."

And here is the shame I will carry past the end of this account: for three heartbeats, I wanted it.

Not the gold. Not even the safety. The *no one*. To open my eyes tomorrow and be merely a clerk again, meticulous and small and safe, with nothing written about me anywhere but inventory lists. Three heartbeats of wanting, long enough for Corvane to see it, long enough for him to smile.

Then I thought of Vespera — her voice like paper, her patience worn thin across a hundred years of waiting for someone to read her properly — and of Ysolde's name smudged out, erased by exactly the kind of merciful erasure now being offered to me, gift-wrapped in gold. Someone had accepted that bargain once already. Someone had let themselves become a smudge.

"No," I said.

Corvane tilted his head. "No to the gold?"

"No to being unwritten." My voice surprised me. It didn't shake. "You're right that I'm nobody. I've been nobody for thirty years and I did it *well*. But the twelfth swordspeaker was somebody, and your Guild rubbed her out so thoroughly that not even her ghost remembers her face — and I am not going to thank you for doing it to me too. Keep the purse. Keep the house. Keep your rotten, profitable leak." I heard myself add, quiet as a closing ledger-line: "I'm going to mend it. Or break it clean. Either way, it ends, and either way, it ends without you profiting from it."

The kindness left his face the way tide leaves a flat.

It didn't become rage. It became arithmetic. I watched him recalculate me — asset to liability — and I watched him arrive at the sum almost fondly, the way one regrets having to write off a debt.

"I taught you to read numbers, Tamsin," he said. "So read this one." He nodded toward the window, toward Greyharrow's rooftops gone blue with evening, toward the seam-haze that shimmered over the far quarter where the Otherworld pressed close. "There are nine hollow knights in my service. You have met one. It took two swordspeakers and a dying god to stop the knights when they were alive. Whatever you think you are becoming —" his eyes flicked, briefly, to my sleeve, where the shard-word still burned under my skin like a brand reading itself — "you are not yet worth one of them."

"Then I'll be worth more tomorrow."

"That," said Corvane, "is precisely the problem."

He didn't signal anyone. He didn't need to. The door behind me opened on hinges oiled to silence, and the room filled with the smell of cold iron and grave-dirt, and I understood that the conversation had been over since I walked in. Everything after *name your price* had been courtesy. Corvane was a great believer in courtesy. It made the killings cleaner.

I did the only thing a keeper of ledgers can do when the audit turns against him.

I ran.

* * *

Greyharrow at night is a city holding its breath between two worlds, and that night it exhaled all over me.

I went out the window because the door had a knight in it, down into the alley where the guild-house kept its rubbish and its secrets, and I ran the way I used to run as a boy through the tannery district — badly, desperately, elbows out, lungs full of knives. Behind me the alley mouth filled with grey. Not fog. *Him.* The hollow knight from the borderland, or its brother; they all wore the same armor the way corpses wear the same expression. Its feet made no sound. Its sword made less.

Shouts went up ahead — Guild men, plain-clothed, positioned at the corners like punctuation. They hadn't come for a fight. They'd come to herd me, and they were good at it, turning me left and right down streets I half-knew until I realized with cold clarity that I was running exactly where they wanted: away from the underground, away from witnesses, toward the old tannery flats by the seam-haze where the city forgot to look.

A crossbow bolt took me in the shoulder.

I want to be precise about this, because pain lies and memory lies worse. It did not feel like fire. It felt like a *correction* — like a red line drawn through an entry, like the world's editor striking out a clause it disliked. The impact spun me and sat me down hard against a wall that stank of lime and old hides, and the bolt-head was through muscle, not bone, and some detached clerk-part of my mind noted all this in neat columns even as the rest of me howled.

They came for me then, four of them, casual as collectors, and behind them the knight walked with its patient dead rhythm, saving me for itself.

I should have died in that yard. I have done the accounting since, and by any honest ledger I died there, once, the man who believed the world was mostly paperwork.

Because the shard-word woke in me the way it had woken against the first knight — not summoned but *squeezed*, forced up out of me by terror like water from a crushed cloth. I didn't choose it. I opened my mouth to scream and spoke instead, one syllable of the Word That Cuts, and the night divided.

I will not describe it fully. Words fail in the exact place they matter most, which I suspect is the point of swordspeech, and perhaps the point of everything. What I can say is this: the air between me and the four men became a line, and the line was sharp, and the men stopped being a threat and started being four separate emergencies. The crossbow fell apart mid-reload. A blade sheared through its own hilt. And the line kept going — past them, through the yard wall, through the wall beyond, a slow silver seam opening in the world with me trembling at its hinge, and I felt it drinking from me as it cut. Drinking *memories*. I lost, in that moment, the name of my childhood street. I reached for it and found a smooth blank space where a street had been, and I understood — really understood, kneeling in my own blood in a tannery yard — what the power was priced in.

That was the addiction's warning Vespera had spoken of, arriving on schedule. The word wanted to be said *again*. Bigger. The line wanted to widen. Somewhere inside me a voice that sounded like mine said: *you could cut them all down, cut the street, cut the night open and walk through, say it again, say it again—*

I bit my tongue bloody and closed my mouth.

The knight came anyway. Of course it did. It had no fear to lose, and fear was the only currency my word traded in. Its sword came down and I threw up the line one more time — weaker, thinner, a wire instead of a wall — and the impact rang through me like a bell struck by a mountain. Something in my chest tore. The world went grey at the edges, then white, then—

Then someone laughed.

Not a human laugh. A laugh like frost forming, like a branch cracking in deep winter, bright and bitter and utterly unafraid. The knight paused — actually paused, its dead helm tilting — and through my dimming vision I saw a figure drop from the tannery roof into our midst. Tall, lean, wrapped in ragged layers the color of bramble and bone, hair like a spill of dark water. Thorns moved where she walked. Actual thorns, pushing up through the flagstones, curling toward her ankles like dogs greeting their mistress, and where they touched the knight's armor they *bloomed*, forcing apart the joints, splitting the greaves, prying the dead thing open the way frost splits stone.

"Debt," she said to the knight, conversationally, as thorns dragged it down. "One life owed to the old covenant, payable to its speakers. He counts. Barely. But he counts."

The knight fought, silently, terribly. She danced it — there is no other word — leading it onto the thorn-beds, letting its own weight drive the briars through the gaps in its plating, and when it finally went down it did not clatter. It *deflated*, like a puppet with its strings cut, armor folding around nothing, and from somewhere inside it a small grey light guttered out and was gone.

Then she turned to me, and her eyes were the green of things growing in wrong places, and they were furious.

"Well," said the fae outcast who was going to change everything I believed about her people, crouching to peer at the bolt in my shoulder with clinical disgust, "the prophecy sends me a bleeding accountant. A hundred years of debt waiting to be paid, and the covenant spends it on *you*."

"There were complications," I managed.

"There are always complications. Hold still." Her hand closed around the bolt-shaft, and her grip was cold sap and iron. "This is going to hurt. I want you to know that I find that fair, given the evening I was having."

She pulled. The world went white and then red and then honest grey again, and I heard myself make a sound I will deny to my grave, and when I could see, she was binding the wound with a strip of cloth torn from her own wrappings, working fast and rough and — I noticed despite everything — *carefully*, avoiding the tendon, knowing exactly where the muscle needed slack to heal true.

"Why?" I rasped. "Why save me?"

She tied off the knot, hard enough to make my eyes water, and looked at me for a long moment. Up close her face was sharp and strange and younger than her voice, older than her hands.

"A hundred years ago," she said, "your kind swore words over my people and chained us to a bargain we never consented to. Then the oath broke, and my folk caught the shrapnel same as yours — worse, if you ask the blighted edge, which no one does. I owe the covenant a life. One. I've been dodging the debt for sixty years hoping it would just rot." She stood, and pulled me up with a strength that had no business being in those arms, and the seam-haze parted before us down an alley that had not been there a moment before. "But tonight I watched a mortal refuse gold, refuse erasure, refuse the easy lie, and speak a word of the old speech with blood in his mouth. So." Her thorns curled around us both, closing like a gate, like a decision. "The debt gets paid. On *my* terms. Which means you don't get to die, accountant. Which means, gods help us both, you don't get to quit."

We stepped through, into the haze, into the borderland's bruised half-light.

"Where are you taking me?" I asked, swaying, clutching my shoulder, my stolen street-name aching in its blank place.

"Somewhere you can see what your broken oath cost *my* side," Briar said, and her laugh again, frost and branches, bitter as the truth. "Time you saw the blight, Eastwick. Time you saw what the pretty ledgers never entered."

The haze closed behind us like a wound deciding to heal — and far off, in the city we left, I felt rather than heard a bell begin to toll, once, twice, nine times.

Nine knights. And eight still walking.
