## 13. What the Fae Lost Too

Briar had carried mortals across the borderland before, and she had hated every one of them.

Not the way a wolf hates a rabbit — there was nothing clean in it. She hated them the way you hate a debt collector who has never seen your face, who signs his name to papers that take your house while you sleep. Mortals crossed into the Otherworld with their iron and their ledgers and their comfortable certainty that the wild places existed to be inventoried, and they left footprints that did not heal for a hundred years. She had watched them come. She had watched what followed.

So when she turned back on the road — when she knelt beside the bleeding, burned, stubborn little clerk who had refused Corvane's offer and said, *get up, we're not done* — she told herself it was debt and nothing more. The old covenant bound her people to answer need with aid; some fossil clause of it still lived in her blood like an old scar that ached before rain. That was all this was. A clause. An ache.

She did not look at Tamsin Eastwick as he limped after her through the grey grass. Looking at him made the lie harder to hold.

"You're walking wrong," she said over her shoulder. "Favor the left side."

"I'm favoring the left side *because* of the left side."

"Then you're doing it wrong anyway." She slowed despite herself, adjusting her stride to his. "Corvane's men use cold-iron shot. It doesn't just cut. It stays. You'll feel it for days — a coldness under the skin, like a coin left in snow."

"Thank you," he said, and then, after a moment, "for coming back."

"I didn't come back for you."

"I know," he said mildly. "You came back for your debt. I'm merely where the debt lives."

Briar stopped walking. The borderland grass hissed around their ankles, silver-grey and endless, and somewhere far off something sang in a register that made her back teeth itch. She turned and looked at him properly for the first time since she'd pulled him out of Corvane's reach: a thin man in a scorched coat, holding his ribs, with eyes too tired for a man his age and ink stains he'd never fully wash out of his fingers.

"Do you always answer everything?" she asked.

"It's a professional habit. I keep ledgers. Every entry wants a response or the books don't balance."

"And what do I balance against, then? In your ledger?"

He considered it with the same grave attention he seemed to give everything, and Briar found, to her considerable irritation, that she wanted to know what he would say.

"Nothing yet," he admitted. "You're an open column. Those are the most dangerous kind."

She snorted and walked on. But the words stayed with her longer than they should have, the way a splinter stays, working deeper every time you forget it's there.

* * *

They traveled two days through country that grew worse the farther east they went, though Tamsin could not have said why at first. He only knew that the singing thing behind them faded, that the grass thinned from silver to ash, that the air itself began to feel — he groped for the word and found it only on the second evening — *unattended*. Like a house whose owners had died and whose servants had drifted away. Beautiful still, in patches. But no one home.

On the third morning Briar stopped at the crest of a low ridge and said, "Before we go any further, there's something you should see. Something your Guild makes sure your kind never sees."

"The Guild doesn't make sure of anything about me. Until four days ago I catalogued bottled storms."

"Until four days ago you were a pen," Briar said. "Now you're being asked to be a sword. Swords should see what they're swung at." She jerked her chin toward the valley below. "Look."

Tamsin looked.

At first his eye read it as autumn — a whole country gone brown and brittle out of season, mile upon mile of forest standing bare and grey against a white sky. Then the details resolved, and the beauty curdled. The trees were not bare. They were *drained*, their bark split in fine vertical seams like skin stretched too tight, and from the splits seeped a slow sap the color of old candle wax. There were no birds. There were no insects. The streams ran clear and utterly empty, water without so much as an alga green in it, and where a river bent through the middle distance its banks were crusted with pale mineral blooms that glittered like sugar but cast no reflection.

And the fae.

He saw them before he understood what he was seeing: figures moving slowly among the dead trees, tall and slight and lovely even now, gathering the wax-colored sap into clay pots with hands that trembled. Some wore crowns of woven briar — real ones, living ones, the only green things in the whole valley. Others wore rags. One, close enough to see clearly, had skin like birch bark and antlers pruned down to stubs, and she moved with the deliberate care of someone conserving strength for a task that would require all of it and give back none.

"What happened here?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"The Oath broke." Briar's voice had gone flat, which he was learning was how she sounded when feeling ran highest. "Your masters will tell you the covenant was a leash on *us*. That it kept the fae penned behind the borders, kept our magic out of your fields and your cradles. And it did do those things. But a covenant is a bridge, clerk, not a wall. When it shattered, the bridge went down. Both banks fell in." She gestured at the valley with one hand, and her fingers — he noticed for the first time that they ended in thorns, small and black and neat as a lady's manicure — shook slightly. "This is our bank falling in."

"I don't understand what I'm looking at."

"No. You wouldn't. So let me tell you." She sat down on the ridge-top, folding her long limbs, and patted the ground beside her with the brisk imperiousness of someone who had decided he would sit whether he chose to or not. He sat.

"When the twelve swore the Oath," Briar said, "they didn't bind us to obey. That's the story mortals tell, because obedience flatters the teller. What they bound was *exchange*. Flow. Magic moves between the worlds the way blood moves through a heart — out and back, out and back, each world feeding the other. Your side gave us belief, and season, and story. We gave yours wonder, and growth, and dream. The Oath was the valve that kept the flow steady. Twelve swordspeakers stood at the seam and spoke the covenant alive every turning of the year, and while they spoke, both worlds breathed."

She plucked a blade of the grey grass and watched it crumble in her fingers.

"Eleven of them are dead. The twelfth is erased. No one has spoken the covenant alive in a hundred years, and so the flow has been dying by inches. Your world hoards what leaks through — the Guild bottles it, sells it, calls it rare exchange. My world starves. Look at them." Her voice cracked and she mastered it. "Look at them, Tamsin. That woman with the pruned antlers was a queen of the summer court once. Her name was Aurelian and her gardens could make stone flower. Now she boils sap for her people's supper and thanks whatever's left of the sky that there's still sap to boil."

Tamsin watched the ex-queen work. There was nothing dramatic in it, nothing monstrous. That was what undid him, he thought later — the absence of drama. Just a tall figure moving tree to tree in the silence, patient and careful, scraping sustenance out of a wound in the world.

"My mother used to tell me stories about the fae," he said. His voice came out strange to him. "That they stole children. That they lured travelers into bogs with lights. That if you ate their food you belonged to them forever."

"Some of that was true, once," Briar said. "Some of it was true the way your ledgers are true — accurate entries in service of a larger falsehood. Yes, we took children, sometimes, in the old days, when the covenant let us trade in such coin. And your people took our land, our names, our *belief* — took and took and called it settlement. The covenant was meant to end all of that. To make exchange honest. And for three hundred years it worked, and my people forgot what hunger was." She looked at him sideways. "Do you know what happens to a creature that has forgotten hunger, when hunger comes back?"

"No."

"Neither did we." She stood, brushing ash-grass from her clothes. "Come down with me. If you're going to carry five shards and speak an oath, you'll do it knowing what the oath failed. Not from a ledger. From the ground."

* * *

The valley received them without welcome and without hostility, which was somehow worse than either. The drained trees stood around them like columns in a ruined temple, and the fae working among them paused to watch Briar pass — pausing also, Briar noted with something complicated moving behind her face, to watch *him*. A mortal, walking openly in the blight. She saw them mark his iron-buckled boots, his mortal smell, the faint aura of the shard-word still lodged under his skin from the drowned chapel, humming in him like a struck bell that would not finish fading.

An old fae with moss for hair spat on the ground as they passed. Briar stepped between him and Tamsin without breaking stride. "Debt-walkers," she said loudly, using the old formula. "Bound by clause and blood. Touch him and you touch the covenant's claim."

"There is no covenant," the moss-haired one rasped. "There's no claim. There's only what's left, and what's left is ours, and that thing—" his eyes fixed on Tamsin with a hatred so old and worn it had gone smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for generations, "—that thing smells of the words that killed us."

"He carries one shard and half an education," Briar said. "If the words killed you, grandfather, ask yourself who broke them first. It wasn't the mortals. We had eleven graves of our own making before your grandmother drew breath."

The old fae held her gaze a long moment. Then he turned back to his sap-pots, and the moment closed like water over a dropped stone.

"That was kind of you," Tamsin said quietly. "Defending me."

"It wasn't kindness. It was accuracy." But she walked a little faster after that.

They came at midday to the place Briar had been leading him: a clearing where the blight stopped. Not gradually — absolutely, along a line as clean as a knife-cut. On one side, the grey and drained waste. On the other, a wood so vividly alive it hurt to look at: leaves like emeralds wet from polishing, light falling through the canopy in golden ropes, air thick with the smell of honey and rain and growing things. The border between the two states ran in a perfectly straight line for as far as the eye could see in both directions.

Tamsin stood at the line and felt it. That was the only word. Through the soles of his feet, up through his shins, a hum — the same hum he'd felt in the chapel, the same frequency as the shard sleeping in his coat pocket, but vast, continental, a single sustained note played by the whole living world. And past the line, where the dead wood began, the note simply stopped. Silence. Not acoustic silence — *substantive* silence. The sound of a heart skipping its beat.

"What am I hearing?"

"The covenant," Briar said. "Or the ghost of it. This is a root-seam — one of the great anchor-points where the Oath was spoken into the ground. The words died a century ago, but the ground remembers the shape of them, the way your mouth remembers the shape of a song you can no longer sing. Everything inside the memory keeps living. Outside it..." she spread her thorn-tipped hand at the grey waste, "...outside it, the world is holding its breath and forgetting why."

Tamsin crouched and put his palm flat against the living soil. The hum climbed his arm and settled behind his sternum, and for one disorienting instant he was certain he could almost hear *words* in it — syllables worn round by centuries, a cadence, a rhythm like twelve voices speaking in unison —

— and then it was gone, and he was kneeling in a clearing with dirt on his hand and Briar watching him with an expression he couldn't read.

"You heard it," she said. Not a question.

"Something. Almost words."

"Ysolde's descendant would." She said it lightly, tossing it away, but he caught the way her breath hitched on the name, and filed it away as he filed everything, in the open column where dangerous things lived. "Come. There's someone I want you to meet before dark."

* * *

The someone lived in the last house before the dead wood: a cottage grown, not built, its walls of woven hawthorn still stubbornly green in the blighted valley, its chimney smoking a thin thread of woodsmoke that smelled of juniper. Inside, in a single warm room, an ancient fae woman sat by the fire winding yarn the color of dusk. Her wings — vestigial, translucent, veined like frost on glass — lay folded flat against her back, and one of them was torn, a ragged crescent missing from the lower edge, healed badly long ago.

"This is Maren," Briar said. "She was there. At the last swearing."

The old woman's hands did not stop their winding. "Sit down, mortal, before you drip on my floor. You bleed through your coat; I can smell the iron in it." She nodded at a stool. "Briar. Tea."

"I'll make it."

"You'll make it badly, as always. Sit, both of you. My hands remember better than my head does, these days, and my head remembers plenty."

While Briar coaxed the stove, Maren studied Tamsin with eyes gone pale as winter water but not, he sensed, dim. "You want to know what we lost," she said. "That's why she brought you. She brings all the hopeful ones eventually, when they start thinking the covenant was a mortal tragedy." A dry rustle that might have been laughter. "It was everyone's tragedy, child. That's what a covenant *is*. Everyone's."

"What did you lose?" Tamsin asked. "Truly. Not abstractions."

Maren considered him for a long time, winding, winding.

"My sister," she said at last, simply, and the room went very quiet. "She was a river-spirit. When the flow died, the rivers were first to fail — they're veins, you see, the easiest road for magic going either way. She thinned. Year by year, like water spreading over stones. By the tenth year after the breaking she was a creek. By the twentieth, a memory of wet. I went to the riverbed where she'd lived and dug and found nothing. Not bones. Rivers don't leave bones." The yarn wound on. "So yes, your Guild profits from the chaos, your masters grow fat on broken borders, and you mortal clerks count your bottled storms. And here, on our side, a queen boils sap, an old woman winds yarn alone, and my sister is a dry ditch with my grief sitting on its bank like a stone."

She looked up at him then, and her winter-water eyes held no softness and no cruelty — only a terrible patience, the patience of someone who has outlived consolation.

"Briar says you mean to mend it. Mend it *properly*, not the half-mending your Corvane will offer — she tells me everything, the girl, she thinks I don't notice her listening at doors she's invited through. So I'll tell you the one thing worth carrying out of this room." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her eyes like fish under ice. "When you stand at the seam with your shards, mortal, and the old words rise in you wanting to be spoken — remember that they were written by frightened people trying to hold two worlds together with fear and formality. Do not restore the cage because the garden outside it frightens you. Whatever oath wakes again, let it wake *new*. Or let it stay dead, and be kinder than the thing it replaced."

Tamsin found he could not speak for a moment. He bowed his head instead, the deepest gesture he knew.

Outside, the light was failing, and along the straight cruel line between the living wood and the dead, the hum went on — a heart beating steadily on one side of a wall, and on the other side, silence, waiting.

Briar joined him at the door, arms crossed against the evening chill. "Well, clerk? Still think we're the monsters in your mother's stories?"

"I think," Tamsin said slowly, "that tomorrow I need to find the next shard. And I think I know where to look — Vespera said the second word sleeps in a living tree-court, guarded by things that show you the life you abandoned." He glanced at her. "A court. Here. In the middle of all this?"

Briar smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all — only warning, sharp and bright as her thorn-tipped fingers.

"Oh, it's alive, right enough. The Court of the Unfallen. The last green throne in the blight." She pushed open the door into the cold. "And Tamsin — whatever it shows you in there, whatever life it lays out on the table for you like a feast — you must eat *nothing*. Not one bite. Not one word. Because the tree-court doesn't tempt you with lies."

Her eyes caught the last of the light.

"It tempts you with the truth."
