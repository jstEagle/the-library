## 14. Shard Two: The Word That Binds

The tree-court did not appear so much as *arrive*.

One moment Briar was walking ahead of me through a birch wood gone silver with frost, and the next the birches had thickened into oaks the size of cathedral towers, their trunks ribbed like the flanks of sleeping animals, their crowns lost in a green-dark canopy where no sky showed at all — only a soft, sourceless light, the color of candlelight through honey. The air changed too. It grew warm and resinous, thick with the smell of sap and old leaves and something sweeter beneath it, something that made my mouth water and my chest ache with a homesickness I could not place for a home I had never had.

"Stay close," Briar said, without turning around. Her voice came out hushed, as if we had stepped into a temple mid-service. "And whatever you see in there — however real it looks — remember it is a door, not a room."

"A door to what?"

"To everything you've ever wanted." She glanced back at me then, and in the honeyed light her face looked older than I'd ever seen it, the sharp edges of her worn down to something almost gentle. "That's how the court keeps its shard. Not with guards. With gifts."

* * *

We entered through a root-arch tall enough to swallow a church, and inside, the tree-court opened like a heart cut in half and laid bare. Galleries wound up the inner walls of a single vast trunk, balconies of living wood grown smooth as banisters, hung with lanterns that were not lanterns but clustered fruits glowing amber from within. Fae moved along those galleries — I use the word *fae* loosely now, having learned how many shapes that word must stretch to hold — some tall and antlered, some small as children, some barely shaped at all, more suggestion than silhouette. None of them looked at us. That, more than anything, told me they were expected guests rather than intruders, and that the courtesy was a trap dressed in silk.

An old fae woman met us at the base of the great stair. Her skin was bark-brown and cracked like drought earth, her eyes pale as birchwood, and when she spoke her voice had the dry rustle of wind through winter branches.

"The Eastwick boy," she said. "Come for the word that binds."

I flinched at my own name spoken by a stranger in a stranger's country. "You know why I've come."

"We knew before you were born." She smiled without warmth or malice, the way weather smiles. "We knew before your grandmother was born. The court dreams long dreams, keeper of ledgers. Come. Climb. The shard will find you where it finds you."

She gestured upward, and there was nothing to do but climb.

* * *

The stair wound up and up, past galleries where the fae lived their small eternal lives — a weaver working thread of moonlight; two lovers sharing a bowl of something steaming and star-scattered; a child chasing a light-moth across a balcony rail. And every gallery I passed, the air grew heavier with that sweetness, and every step took me further from the cold birch wood and the frost and the quest, and closer to —

To a kitchen.

I stopped on the stairs because the kitchen was *there*, set into the trunk's living wall as naturally as any balcony, and it was my kitchen. Not one like it. It. The crooked shelf above the stove that I had never gotten round to straightening. The chipped blue mug on its hook. The window with the view over the guildhouse roofs where the gulls argued every morning at dawn. My ledger lay open on the table, weighted with the river-stone I used as a paperweight, and beside it sat a pot of tea, still steaming, poured into the mug that was not supposed to be here, in this world, hanging off the inside of a tree between realms.

"Tamsin?" said a voice from the pantry. "Is that you? You're late, love. The tea's gone cold twice already."

My mother's voice.

Except — it wasn't. I knew it wasn't. I had traded the memory of her face at the market at the world's edge, and the price had taken her voice's music with it, left me with only the shape of the sound and none of the song. What stood in the pantry doorway now was built from that shape, from the hollow the memory had left behind, filled in with what the tree-court imagined a mother should be. And it was *good*. It was so good my knees nearly went. She had his laugh-lines and flour on her hands and an expression of fond exasperation I felt in my ribs like a hook set deep.

"You look terrible," she said. "Sit down. Tell me about this ridiculous quest you're on, and then tell me you're coming home."

"This isn't real," I said. My voice cracked in the middle of it, which undermined the argument considerably.

"No," she agreed pleasantly, wiping her hands on her apron. "But it could be. That's rather the point, dear."

* * *

Briar found me an hour later — or what felt like an hour, though time in the court ran like honey off a cold spoon — sitting at the kitchen table with the tea going cold in front of me, talking. Just talking. About the ledger, about the shop, about the bottled storm and the stolen shadow and the thousand small impossibilities I had catalogued in my neat, narrow hand. About loneliness. I heard myself describing the flat above the guildhouse, how the floorboards announced Corvane's footsteps three rooms away and how I had learned to read his moods by their weight, and how I had never once had anyone to read *mine*, and the woman in my mother's shape listened with her chin on her hands like there was nothing else in all the worlds she wanted to hear.

"There you are." Briar's voice came from the doorway, flat and hard as a slap. "Good. You haven't eaten anything."

"It's not food," I said stupidly. "It's memory. Isn't it? All of it. The tea, the kitchen —"

"The tea is tea. The rest is bait." She crossed the room, pulled out a chair, and sat down across from the thing wearing my mother's absence. They regarded each other for a long moment, the fae outcast and the illusion, and something passed between them that I didn't have the map for. "You'll want to leave him alone now," Briar said quietly. "He has somewhere to be."

"He has *here* to be," said my mother's voice, gentle, reasonable. "Look at him, outcast. Look how tired he is. He never asked for any of this — the prophecy, the shards, the dying. He was a clerk. He liked being a clerk. He liked the order of it, the small clean columns, a world that balanced at the bottom of the page. You want to drag him up a mountain to die for strangers. I want to give him tea. Which of us loves him more?"

"That's not your question to ask," Briar said. "You're not her. You don't even know who she was. He doesn't either, anymore. He sold that. So you built her out of the hole she left." She leaned forward. "And here's what you can't counterfeit: he knows it too. Every moment he sits here, he knows. That's not peace you're offering him. It's a beautifully furnished wound."

The kitchen flickered. Just once — a stutter, like a lamp in wind — and in the stutter I saw the wall behind the pantry dissolve into raw wood-grain, vast and breathing, and the window's gulls become motes of drifting pollen. Then it snapped back, warmer than ever, and my mother turned to me with tears standing in her eyes, which was the cruelest touch yet, because I could not have told you whether my mother had cried like that or whether the tree-court simply understood that this was what would break me fastest.

"Ask me to stay," she whispered. "Just once. Ask me, and I can make it true. The court has power enough. Stay, Tamsin. Stay, and be nobody's sword, nobody's prophecy, nobody's heir. Just my boy."

And oh, I wanted to. Let the record show, in whatever ledger keeps the truth of me: I wanted it with the whole desperate weight of a man dying of thirst wanting rain. I thought of Vespera's ghost-light guttering on the borderland road, telling me the covenant was broken and bled and that eleven graves held the ones who'd tried to mend it. I thought of the drowned chapel, the first shard's word cutting through me like a blade through wet paper, the way it had sung in my blood afterward, sweet as rot. I thought of Corvane's face, patient and kind, saying *you were always meant for more than bookkeeping, boy* — and how even his kindness had been a leash.

And I thought — this was the strange thing, the thing I turned over afterward for days — I thought of the empty chair across from me. Of evenings in the flat with the kettle on and no one to pour for. Of how, if I stayed, the tree-court would keep pouring tea forever into a cup for a woman whose face I had sold, and call it love, and I would drink it, and it would taste of almost.

"I can't ask you to stay," I said finally, "because you aren't her. And she —" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "She wouldn't have asked me to stay, either. Whatever else I've forgotten about her, I know that much. Mothers like the one I had don't build cages out of kitchens. They teach you the road and wave you down it."

The kitchen went very quiet. The steam rose off the tea in a thin unwinding line.

"Say your goodbye, then," said my mother's voice, and for just a heartbeat — one flicker, one frame — the illusion let its craft slip, and underneath the borrowed face I saw the tree itself looking at me, ancient and curious and not unkind, weighing what I'd said against ten thousand other answers it had heard down the centuries.

I picked up the chipped blue mug. I drank the tea. It tasted of almost, exactly as I'd known it would, and I drank it anyway, because it was the last cup anyone would ever pour me in her name.

"Goodbye," I said. "Thank you for the tea."

* * *

The gallery dissolved around me like sugar in water, and the stair resumed, and I kept climbing, and Briar climbed beside me, and for a long while neither of us spoke.

"You knew it would offer me her," I said at last.

"I suspected. The court reads what a traveler carries. You carry her the way a stump carries rings." She glanced sideways at me. "For what it's worth — the answer you gave was the right one. Most don't. There are galleries upstairs full of mortals who sat down to tea fifty years ago. Some of them are happy. Some of them are even right to be. But they aren't yours to judge, and you aren't theirs."

"How do you know so much about this place?"

Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Because I sat down to tea once too," she said. "A long time ago. That's a story for a night with a fire in it, and we don't have a fire. Keep climbing."

* * *

The stair ended at a small round chamber near the crown of the tree, open on every side to drifting veils of leaf and light. In its center stood a table of living wood, and on the table lay the shard.

It was smaller than the first — a sliver of pale glass no longer than my finger, shot through with veins of gold like sunlight caught in amber — and it did not gleam so much as *listen*. That is the only word I have ever found for it. The first shard had blazed; this one attended. When I stepped into the chamber, I felt it notice me the way you feel someone turn to look at you across a crowded room.

"The word that binds," Briar murmured. "Ysolde's sisters called it the keeping-word. It holds things together — vows, doors, wounds, worlds. Speak it wrong and it binds you instead. Speak it right and—" She shrugged. "Well. You'll see."

I reached out. And the tree-court, having fed me illusions at the door and found me stubborn, tried one last thing: it gave me silence.

No — not silence exactly. It gave me *stillness*. The chamber held its breath. The drifting leaves hung motionless in the honeyed light. Even my own heartbeat seemed to pause, listening, and into that pause came a single clear understanding, arriving whole like a memory I'd never made: *the word cannot be taken. It can only be given. To bind, you must first consent to be bound.*

That was the test. Not the kitchen. Not the tea. Those were just the court's way of asking the question in a language I'd understand. The real test was this: every oath I had ever known — the Guild's contracts, the old covenant, the twelve swordspeakers' terrible compulsion — had been binding imposed from outside, will lashed to will. The shard waited to learn whether I could swear the other way. Whether I could kneel *willingly*.

"I consent," I said. My voice sounded very small in the vast listening tree. "Whatever you are, whatever word you carry — I take it freely, and I give myself freely with it. Bind me."

I closed my hand around the shard.

The gold veins lit. The word came up through my arm like sap rising, like spring forcing winter back down a mountainside, and I understood it whole before I could have spelled a syllable of it aloud — a word shaped like a knot tied from the inside, a word that meant *I hold because I choose to hold*. When I spoke it — and I did speak it, helplessly, the way you gasp — the chamber rang like a bell the size of the sky, and every leaf in the crown of the tree turned gold at once, and far below us, I felt it: the borderland's bleeding seams pulling tight by one stitch. One stitch, out of ten thousand torn.

Then the ringing faded, and I was on my knees on the wooden floor with blood in my mouth — I'd bitten my tongue, nothing more dramatic — and Briar's hand hard on my shoulder, and the shard lying quiet in my palm, warm as a held hand.

"Two," I rasped.

"Two," she agreed. "Three to find, if the count holds. And Tamsin—" She helped me up, and her grip lingered a half-second longer than balance required. "What you said downstairs. About mothers teaching you the road. Wherever she is, whatever the market took — I think she'd have been proud of the toll you paid to get up these stairs."

I laughed, mostly so I wouldn't do something weaker. "Careful, Briar. A man could start thinking you liked him."

"Don't be absurd." But she smiled as she said it, brief and real, the first smile I'd seen on her that wasn't armor.

* * *

We came down out of the tree-court as the false dusk of the Otherworld was settling, and the old bark-skinned gatekeeper waited for us at the root-arch, as I'd known she would.

"He took the word," she said. Not a question.

"He took the word."

The old fae studied me with her birch-pale eyes, and then she did something I had not expected: she bowed. Shallow, grudging, but bowed. "Two shards, Eastwick boy. Do you know what the courts are saying? They are saying the twelfth swordspeaker walks again." Her cracked mouth twisted. "They are saying it wrong. But they are saying it."

"What are they saying *right*?"

"That the Guild has moved." For the first time, fear touched her voice, rustling like wind through dead leaves. "While you drank tea and climbed stairs, Corvane's riders crossed three seams. They burned a waystation on the Greyharrow road. They took prisoners." She paused, and the pause was a blade laid flat between us. "Among them, they say, a fae woman traveling under the name of Thorn. Small. Dark-haired. Sharp-tongued."

The world tilted. "That's not — Briar is right here—"

"I know which of you stands before me, boy. I am asking whether you know *who else* travels your road. Who else carries your name, your cause, your scent. Think, swordspeaker. If the Guild cannot buy you, what do you imagine they will hunt instead?"

Behind me, Briar had gone very still. When I turned, her face had drained to the color of the frost-bound birches, and her hand had gone to the knife at her belt — not for me, I realized. For herself. For the old debt, the old covenant, the price her people paid for oaths sworn and broken long before I was born.

"They'll interrogate her," Briar said quietly. "Salt and iron. They'll try to strip what she is." Her eyes came up and met mine, and there was no frost in them at all, only fire banked hard against blowing away. "Tamsin. We need to reach Greyharrow before they finish breaking her. And we need to move tonight."
