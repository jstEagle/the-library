## 15. The Smudged Name

There is a particular cruelty in being remembered by a book.

I have been bound to these pages for a hundred years — no, that is not true, and I am tired of the lie even in my own head. I have been bound to them for less. For moments. For the length of an ink drying. Time moves strangely through me now, like water through a cracked jar; I carry it, and I leak it, and I cannot say how much remains. But I know what I did. The book knows. The book remembers everything I wrote into it, including the things I thought I was writing around.

Tamsin sat across from me on the cold stone of the drowned chapel's steps, turning the fifth shard over in his hands as though it might bite him. It had not bitten him. That was the trouble. It had *chosen* him, and he had not yet asked the question that mattered: why.

"You've gone quiet," he said. "Quieter than usual, I mean."

"I'm deciding what to tell you."

"Is that different from deciding whether to tell me?"

"Everything is different," I said, "when you are the one who wrote the ending."

He looked up at that. He has his mother's eyes — had, I mean, since she is dead, though I never met her, and yet I know her face better than my own hands, because Ysolde drew it for me once, laughing, in the margin of a ledger, before there were margins to write prophecies in. No. Stop. I am getting ahead of myself again. Guilt does that. It runs forward to escape looking back.

* * *

Let me tell you about the twelfth swordspeaker properly, because Tamsin deserved the truth and got instead eleven names recited over graves like coins dropped down a well.

Twelve stood at the First Seam when the world was younger and the border between realms was a wound that would not close. Twelve spoke the Oath together, word by word, each of them carrying one shard of the covenant in their chest like a swallowed star — for that is what the words became, living words, lodged in flesh, sustained by flesh. Kill the speaker and the word falls out of them still burning. That is how you get shards. That is how we got shards. That is how I—

No. In order. I owe him order. Order is all I have left that resembles honesty.

The twelve had names. I gave them to Tamsin already: Aldous of the Cutting Word, Merrin of the Binding Word, Sable of the Opening Word, and so on down the line, each syllable of the covenant housed in a mortal throat. Eleven of those throats went silent within a generation — some to age, which the covenant permitted, and some to worse, which it did not. And the twelfth name on the page, the name I smudged with my own thumb while the ink was still wet, was not smudged because I forgot it.

I remember it perfectly. I will remember it until whatever passes for my end.

* * *

Her name was Ysolde Eastwick.

She was my sister. Not by blood — blood was cheap where we grew up, in the fen-country villages where children were traded between houses like borrowed tools — but by oath, sworn when we were nine years old with cut palms and stolen honey wine, the kind of oath children make because they do not yet know oaths have teeth. She swore she would always tell me the truth. I swore I would always stand beside her.

We both kept our oaths. That was the horror of it, in the end. We kept them exactly.

Ysolde was the youngest of the twelve, chosen last, chosen almost as an afterthought, and she became the strongest of them anyway, because she understood something the others refused to learn: that a covenant built on compulsion is only a cage with good manners. The Oath bound the realms together, yes. It stopped the bleeding between worlds, yes. But it bound them the way a master binds a servant — with words the fae never agreed to freely, spoken over them by mortals who had decided they knew best. Every fae who crossed the border under the old covenant crossed it leashed. Every mortal who touched magic touched it through a collar someone else had fitted.

The other eleven called this peace. Ysolde called it a debt accruing interest.

"They'll rise against it," she told me, the night before everything. We were on the roof of the chapterhouse in Greyharrow, before Greyharrow was grey, when it was still Harrow and the seam ran bright beneath it like a vein of dawn. "Maybe not today. Maybe not for a hundred years. But every binding breeds its breaker, Vespera. Every leash teaches the throat under it to hate."

"So we tighten the leash," I said. I believed it then. Gods forgive me, I believed it with my whole heart, because I had seen what the border was like before the Oath — the bleeding, the blended creatures screaming themselves inside out, the villages on both sides drowned in each other's weather. Compulsion had bought us quiet, and I loved quiet. I loved it the way starving people love bread, without asking what it cost anyone else.

"No," she said. "We ask. We unmake it and we ask. Consent, freely given, realm to realm. A new oath, sworn new, in new words." Her eyes were bright and terrible. She was twenty-six years old and she was right, and I helped kill her for it.

* * *

Here is the part I have written around for a hundred pages and cannot write around anymore, because Tamsin holds her shard now and the shard knows its history and will not stay silent much longer.

The other ten — two were already dead by then, their words passed to successors — the ten surviving swordspeakers convened in secret. I know because I was there. Not as a swordspeaker. As the record-keeper. I was the youngest daughter of a fen-family too, taken into the covenant's service because I had a memory like a ledger and a hand that could keep it. I wrote the minutes of the meeting where they decided to murder my sister.

They did not call it murder. They called it *preservation*. They said the covenant could not survive being unmade — that between the unmaking and the new asking, the border would tear wide open, and thousands would die in the gap. They may even have been right about the gap. I have had a hundred years to wonder whether being right about the gap excuses what they did in it.

They needed a way to reach her. She trusted no one else's counsel, but she trusted mine absolutely, because of the honey wine and the cut palms and thirty years of kept oaths. So they sent me to her with a lie folded up small in my mouth: that the others had come around, that they wished to hear her proposal, that there would be a conclave at the First Seam where the twelve — or their words — could speak together and consider remaking the covenant by consent.

She walked into that conclave believing me.

They took her at the seam itself, all ten words speaking at once against her one, and do you understand what that means, what it *cost*, what it sounded like? Words are blades in a swordspeaker's mouth, but ten against one is not a duel, it is an execution, and the seam itself screamed. The border tore along its whole length for three days. Blended creatures died in the hundreds. A village in the Otherworld simply ceased, folded shut like a book nobody meant to close. And in the middle of it, held down by the covenant she had tried to set free, my sister looked at me across the stones and did not curse me.

That was the worst of it. She did not curse me. She said, "You kept your oath. You stood beside me," and I did not understand her until later, until the trial, until the smudging — she meant that standing beside someone includes witnessing what is done to them. She forgave me by making me watch. She made me the record-keeper of her own death, because the covenant demanded a record, and I was the only one whose hand did not shake.

Almost the only one. My hand shook enough to smudge.

* * *

When it was done, when her word came out of her still burning and they sealed it in lead and buried it with her body in an unmarked grave because even her grave felt like an accusation, the survivors made their final decision, and it was worse than the killing. They decided to erase her. Not just the woman — the *name*. If people knew a swordspeaker had turned against the covenant, others might follow. If others followed, the covenant might actually be tested. And a covenant that has killed once to preserve itself will kill again, and again, more cheaply each time. That is the arithmetic of compulsion. I learned it at her trial and I have never unlearned it.

So I opened the prophecy-book — the great ledger, the master record — and I found her entry, and I pressed my thumb into the wet ink of her name until it blurred into nothing, and the book took the erasure into itself and made it prophecy. That is the secret of the book, the thing I have never told anyone living or dead: it does not foretell. It *records intentions so strong they bend what comes after*. I wrote in grief and guilt and fear, and the book drank all three, and out of that drinking came the line that waited a hundred years for tonight:

*On this date, the heir of the smudged name shall find the first page.*

Not "the heir of Eastwick." The book could not say it. I had smudged it too well. But books are honest in their bones even when their keeper lies on their surface, and the prophecy bent itself around the truth I had tried to bury: that someday, someone of her blood would stand where she stood and be offered the same choice.

And I bound myself to the book to wait for him. Voluntarily. Do not mistake it for penance nobly borne — it was cowardice with a long horizon. I could not bear to die and leave the lie standing, so I made myself unable to die until it fell.

* * *

Tamsin was watching me. He had stopped turning the shard. Somewhere above us, water dripped through the drowned chapel's broken roof, counting seconds neither of us wanted counted.

"The twelfth name," he said quietly. "It wasn't just erased. You erased it."

"Yes."

"And the twelfth swordspeaker tried to break the covenant. On purpose. To remake it by consent."

"Yes."

"And you—" His voice caught on the edge of it. He is not a cruel young man, which is precisely why the question hurt him to ask. "You helped kill her."

"I held the door open," I said. "I carried the invitation. I wrote the minutes of her execution and then I destroyed her name so no one would ever follow where she pointed." The chapel dripped. "And now her shard has chosen her blood, and I have spent a hundred years guiding that blood toward the choice I murdered her for making. Do you see the shape of it, Tamsin? I did not guide you despite my guilt. I guided you *because* of it. Everything I have told you that was true, I told as a liar. Everything I have hidden, I hid to protect myself from this exact hour."

He was silent a long time. Water dripped. The shard in his hand pulsed faintly, warm as a sleeping bird, and I wondered if it could hear us, if somewhere in its burning word it recognized the voice that had written its keeper's condemnation.

"She was my sister," I said. "In every way that mattered. And I chose the quiet over her, because the quiet was comfortable, and comfortable things always ask you to pay in someone else's coin." I finally looked at him straight. "Your grandmother's grandmother's mother died asking the realms for consent. The Guild calls her the traitor. The prophecy-book calls her the smudged name. I call her by her name every day inside my own skull, and every day it is not enough, and nothing ever will be, and I am telling you this not for absolution — I do not want absolution, absolution is another comfort bought cheaply — but because you cannot choose rightly until you know what choosing wrongly looks like. And I am what it looks like. I am what it looks like after a hundred years."

Tamsin looked down at the shard. Then at me. And when he spoke, his voice was very soft, and very steady, and it frightened me more than any anger could have, because it sounded like hers.

"What was her word?" he asked. "The twelfth swordspeaker's word. Everyone else's word is named — cutting, binding, opening. What was hers?"

And I felt the book shudder against my ribs where I carry it, felt the pages turn by themselves deep in my chest, because that was the one thing left I had never written, the one truth the smudge had protected all this time.

I made myself answer. "Consent," I said. "Her word was the Word of Consent. The word the covenant could not afford to contain — because a covenant that included it could be dissolved by agreement. They didn't just kill her for wanting to use it. They killed her because as long as her word existed, the Oath was never truly binding at all."

The shard in Tamsin's palm flared white.

And far above us, through the chapel's broken roof, the sky over the borderland went the color of a page being torn.
