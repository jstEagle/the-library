## 16. Hollow Knights Rising

The rain in the borderland did not fall so much as remember falling — great slow sheets of it drifting sideways through the air, hanging like grey curtains between the crooked birches, and when we walked through them we came out the other side wet, though the drops themselves had never touched us. I had stopped trying to understand this place three days ago. Understanding was a luxury of people whose world stayed put.

"Left," Vespera said.

I went left. My boots had learned to obey her before my head finished processing her words, which was either trust or surrender, and I no longer cared which.

We were four days out from Ysolde's grave, two days past the tree-court where Shard Two had nearly eaten me alive with kindness, and somewhere behind us — I could feel it now, the way you feel a storm building behind hills — Corvane had stopped being patient.

It began with the birds.

The borderland had birds, or things that performed the duties of birds: pale, silent, punctual as clerks. They scattered at dusk on the fourth day, every one of them at once, streaming east like smoke blown through a keyhole. Briar, walking ahead with her hood up against the remembering-rain, stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into her back.

"They're wrong," she said.

"They're *leaving*," Vespera corrected. Her voice came thin and cold off the book strapped to my chest; she could only speak clearly near it, so I had taken to carrying it against my sternum like an iron heart. "Birds leave when something enters the forest that even they won't watch."

I looked back down the trail we'd cut through the birches. The light was failing in that particular borderland way, not darkening but going *thin*, as if someone were diluting the day. And far off — very far, maybe half a mile, maybe ten feet, distance here being a suggestion rather than a law — I saw them.

Three figures. Armored. Walking.

Not toward us. Not away. Just *walking*, in perfect unison, their footfalls landing together with a sound like one drum struck by one hand.

"No," Briar said. She said it quietly, the way you say a word you've been dreading for a long time. "No, those are dead."

"They're knights." Vespera's voice had gone strange. Older. "Hollowed ones."

"Hollowed?"

"The Guild found a way to do the worst thing imaginable with swordspeech," she said. "They found a way to make it *obey* after death. A swordspeaker swears words into steel, Tamsin. Words don't stop being true just because the mouth that spoke them stops breathing. The Guild learned to reach into that truth and pull. Like puppeteers working strings made of oaths."

The three figures turned their heads. All at once. In unison.

Then they started running, and the forest ran with them.

* * *

We ran. There is no more honest word for what happened next than that: we ran, all night, through birch-woods that folded themselves into marshes and marshes that unfolded into stands of black pine, over ridges where the stones hummed old songs, down into hollows where our own shadows got ahead of us and waited, panting, until we caught up.

The knights did not tire. That was the first lesson of the night, and it cost us everything to learn it. We would gain an hour on them through some trick of the terrain — Briar knew paths that only existed if you believed in them hard enough, and she believed with a ferocity I envied — and then we would crest a hill, lungs burning, legs shaking, and there they would be below us. Three armored shapes moving at a pace no living soldier could hold for a minute, let alone hours. Their armor was wrong, too. Old make, mismatched, each suit assembled from different centuries — a greave from the Age of Warring Houses, a breastplate I recognized from the effigies in Greyharrow's crypts. Dead men dressed by committee.

"Why three?" I gasped, somewhere past midnight, hauling myself over a fence that had grown through a fallen church. "Why not send twenty?"

"Because three is enough," Vespera said. "And because every hollowed knight they spend is a shard-word they can't recover later. Even the Guild counts costs." A pause, and then, quieter, in the voice she used when the truth had teeth: "Also, those three are not random. Look at their shields."

I looked the next time the moon cooperated, which the borderland moon did grudgingly, sliding out from behind clouds like a debtor avoiding a creditor. Each knight bore a shield blazoned with a device worn nearly smooth. But I had spent five days studying the prophecy-book, and I had spent last night staring at the page of twelve names, and I knew heraldry the way a ledger-keeper knows anything: thoroughly, obsessively, without love.

"Those are—" I couldn't finish. The hill took my breath, or the knowledge did. "Those are original swordspeakers. Those are *hers*. Ours. The twelve."

"Eleven graves," Vespera said. "Nobody promised the graves stayed full."

Briar made a sound low in her throat that I would learn, over the coming days, meant she was afraid and intended to keep fighting anyway. "Can they speak? The words?"

"No mouths left worth speaking with," Vespera said. "But they carry the words in the steel. When they strike, the strikes will be *true*. Understand me, Tamsin — a blow from a hollowed knight isn't a wound, it's a sentence. It says something about you, and reality makes it so."

"What kind of sentences?"

"You'll find out. Run faster."

* * *

The first engagement happened at dawn-that-wasn't-dawn, in a field of grass gone silver as coin. We'd found a river — a real one, thank every merciful power, wide and fast and smelling of actual water instead of memory — and Briar said the knights' oath-weight would sink them, that dead steel hates crossing living current. We had maybe an hour. Maybe less. I wanted to use it to rest. Briar wanted to use it to argue.

"We should split," she said.

"And how does that help?" I was sitting in the shallows, letting the current try to take my boots and failing, because everything about me had become stubbornly attached. "There are three of them and three of us."

"There are two of us and one ghost," she said, with fae precision. "If we split, they must choose. Or divide. Either way, slower."

"And if they divide?"

"Then you fight one alone." She crouched in front of me. Her eyes were the amber of resin, and they held mine without mercy. "You have two shards, Eastwick. You've spoken each of them exactly once, both times by accident, both times screaming. Sooner or later you are going to have to do it on purpose, while calm, while aiming. Better it happens against one knight than three."

"She's right," Vespera said, and there was something raw under her agreement. "And I hate it. But she's right."

So we split. Briar took the north bank, drawing two by waving her arms and shouting insults in a language that made the grass flinch — apparently fae profanity is structurally offensive to dead things. I took the south, alone, with a river at my back and a ghost in a book against my chest and a sword I'd bought in a market at the edge of the world, paid for with my mother's face, its blade still carrying a hairline seam of light where Shard One had sunk into it.

The first knight came out of the treeline at a walk. It had stopped running. I understood why a moment later: it didn't need to run anymore. The chase was over. The killing was the easy part.

Up close, the hollow was worse than the rumor. The visor was empty — not dark, not shadowed, but *empty*, a hole in the shape of a face, and through it I could see the far bank of the river, undistorted. Nothing lived behind that visor. Something wore the shape of having lived.

"Tamsin Eastwick," it said.

I want it recorded, in whatever ledger finally gets written about this, that the voice was the worst part. It wasn't a corpse-voice. It was a *clerk's* voice. Dry, precise, faintly bored. It was the voice of the Guild's counting-houses, and hearing it come out of that empty helm told me exactly who held these puppets' strings.

"That's me," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated myself for the crack.

"You are in possession of covenant material. You will relinquish it."

"No."

A pause. The empty helm tilted, very slightly, the way a man tilts his head when a ledger line doesn't add. "That response is not anticipated."

"Well." My hand found the hilt. The seam of light pulsed once, twice, like a pulse. "Get new ledgers."

It drew its sword, and the sound it made was the sound of a word being unsheathed — I know how that sounds, I know how impossible that sounds, but I was there, and the blade came out singing a single syllable in a language older than the borderland, and the syllable hung in the silver grass like frost.

It attacked.

I have fought exactly three times in my life before that morning, and all three were tavern incidents resolved by other people's intervention. What saved me was not skill. It was the shards. Shard Two, the Word That Binds, woke in my blood when the first blow came, and I heard myself shout — not a scream, a *word*, a binding-syllable that hit the knight's sword mid-arc and made the arc *argue with itself*. The blade slowed. Not stopped. Slowed, as though swinging through deep water, and I threw myself sideways and the edge that should have opened me shoulder-to-hip instead grazed my arm, and the graze *said something*.

I felt it land. That's the only way I can describe it. The cut was shallow, barely bleeding, but a sentence went in with it, and the sentence was: *you are slower than you think you are.* And suddenly I was. My limbs dragged, my thoughts pooled, my whole body became an argument for standing still. That's what Vespera meant. The wounds tell truths, and your body believes them.

"Shard One," Vespera shouted from the book, and her voice tore across the field like a banner snapping. "The Word That Cuts! Speak it AT him, not around him!"

"I nearly died speaking it!"

"You'll certainly die NOT speaking it!"

The knight raised its sword again, unhurried, inevitable. Behind the empty visor, nothing considered me. Nothing weighed mercy. Somewhere north, Briar was holding two of these at bay with tricks and fury, and I could hear the clangor like bells rung by a drowning man, and I understood, with the terrible clarity that exhaustion grants, that no one was coming. That this was the shape of the rest of my life: alone, outmatched, holding words too heavy for me, in a country that didn't obey rules.

Good, said some small hard thing in me that I didn't recognize. Good. Then let's find out what I'm for.

I reached for Shard One. Not groping this time, not panicking. Reaching, the way you reach for a tool you've finally decided to pick up. And the shard rose in me like a fish rising toward light, and I felt its edge everywhere at once, in my teeth, in the roots of my hair, in the space between my heartbeat and the next, and I spoke the Word That Cuts directly at the hollowed knight, at the emptiness inside it, at the strings I could suddenly *see* — fine bright lines running up out of its shoulders, up into the sky, up toward a counting-house in a city that sat on a seam.

The word cut the strings.

The knight dropped. Not collapsed — dropped, like a marionette whose puppeteer has set it down, gently, almost respectfully, and stood there, empty helm bowed, sword point-down in the silver grass. For three heartbeats it simply knelt in the posture of a man released.

Then the armor fell open like a door, and there was nothing inside at all, and the wind came through the empty shell and made a sound almost like relief.

I stood shaking in the shallows, my shallow wound whispering its lie about slowness, and I watched the strings dissolve, and I thought: *they're still attached at the other end. All the way to Corvane.*

He had felt that. He knew, now, exactly where I was.

* * *

We ran again. Of course we ran again. The remaining two knights regrouped with a horrible administrative efficiency, abandoning Briar's harassment the moment their third fell, and came south along the riverbank at their dead, tireless pace, and we went the other way, and the borderland, which had seemed vast and labyrinthine, revealed itself to be small — a box, a trap, a territory shaped precisely like a hunt.

By the second day I understood what the pursuit really was. It wasn't meant to catch us. It was meant to *spend* us. Corvane had read the same prophecies I had. He knew the shards fed on their speaker, that swordspeech wrote itself in the speaker's self. Every time I spoke, I lost something. Already the name of the innkeeper in my home city had gone soft in my memory, like a word said too many times. Already I had to concentrate to recall whether my mother's eyes had been grey or brown — and panic seized me every time, because that face was *paid for*, sold at the market, and what remained was a debt I couldn't afford to default on.

"He's grinding you down," Vespera said on the second night, as I sat with my back against a stone that hummed, watching Briar sleep — truly sleep, deeply, the way the fae do, like a cat in sunlight, trusting nothing enough to be defenseless anyway. "Each word costs. Each mile costs. He doesn't need to beat you. He needs you to arrive at the third shard already hollowed. A hollowed swordspeaker is easier to steer."

"Than what?"

"Than anyone," she said. "Believe me. I've seen it done."

I wanted to ask. She wanted me to ask. Neither of us did. Some doors you leave shut because you can hear breathing on the other side.

* * *

The end of it came at a place the borderland maps — all three of the maps Briar carried, drawn on bark, on skin, on a scrap of sky — agreed to call the Weeping Stair. A cliff-face where groundwater broke through in a hundred springs, so the whole rock face streamed, and steps had been cut into it by hands unknown, ascending into cloud. Beyond it, Briar said, lay the drowned chapel country, and the first of the deeper shards. Beyond it also lay open ground — a plain of white grass where nothing hid and nothing sheltered, a killing floor if ever the Guild wanted one.

The two knights reached us at the base of the stair, at what passed for dusk.

I was so tired by then that fear had burned down to embers, and embers, I discovered, are useful. I was done running. My body had filed a formal objection and my pride had countersigned it. When the knights came out of the treeline — unhurried, inevitable, their clerk-voices reciting my crimes in advance ("in possession of covenant material; refusal anticipated; force authorized") — I turned to face them on the lowest step of the Weeping Stair, with the water running cold down my neck like a benediction, and Briar at my shoulder with her thorn-knife, and Vespera burning in the book like a coal wrapped in paper.

"You're tired," Vespera said softly. "So am I. Isn't that a fine thing to share?"

"It's the first thing we've shared," I said.

"Speak with me, then. Both words. At once. Bind them and cut them, together, and let's see what a hundred-year-old ghost and a bankrupt ledger-keeper can do to two dead kings of the old oath."

The knights charged, and the water of the Weeping Stair bent around them, refusing to touch the hollow, and I raised my sword with arms that shook and a heart that, for the first time since a date inscribed a century ago had broken my tidy life open, did not shake at all.

I spoke. Both words. The world went white and loud and true.

When I could see again, both suits of armor lay open and empty on the white steps, and the strings that had trailed from them were severed, curling in the springwater like burnt hair, and I was on my knees, and I could not remember —

— I could not remember my own street. The one I'd lived on. The one with the ledger-house at the end of it. I knew I had lived on a street. I knew there had been a door. The street itself was gone, scooped out, a blank line in the record of me.

"Tamsin." Vespera's voice, urgent now, frightened. "*Tamsin. Say your name.*"

I opened my mouth.

Far across the white plain, where the killing ground met the horizon, a light appeared. No — many lights. Lanterns, ranks of them, orderly as inventory, advancing in perfect formation out of the dark. And above them, borne on poles like banners, hung shields bearing devices I now knew better than my own vanished street: the arms of dead swordspeakers, dozens of them, a whole company of the hollowed dead marching in Corvane's name.

"Say your NAME," Vespera screamed.

Across the plain, the lanterns kept coming, and I clung to the one line in my ledger that remained legible, and said it:

"My name is Tamsin Eastwick. And I am not finished writing."
