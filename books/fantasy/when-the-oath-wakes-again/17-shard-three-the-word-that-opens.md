## 17. Shard Three: The Word That Opens

The hollow knight had been following us for three days.

I knew it the way you know a tooth is rotten — not by sight, but by the low persistent ache of its presence, a wrongness threaded through the borderland's ambient hum. The Otherworld's edge had a music to it, if you learned to listen: wind through reeds that whispered in no human tongue, the distant chiming of the fae market's bells that never quite stopped, the slow pulse of the seam itself like a heartbeat buried under miles of stone. And beneath all of it, for three days now, a second heartbeat. Slower. Colder. Out of rhythm with everything alive.

"It's herding us," Briar said on the third evening. She crouched at the lip of a ravine where the grass grew silver and rang faintly when the wind moved it, like thousands of tiny bells mourning something. Her fox-mask was pushed up on her head, and in the failing light her face looked young and very tired. "Corvane's knights don't hunt. They *drive*. It wants us somewhere specific."

"Where does it want us?"

She didn't answer at first. She reached down and pulled one blade of the silver grass, held it to her ear, and listened the way I might listen to a seashell — except what she heard made her flinch.

"The Chapel of the Drowned Bell," she said quietly. "Or what's left of it."

Vespera flickered into visibility beside me, which she did only when something frightened her. Ghosts have their own body language, I'd learned; hers was a kind of thinning, as though the world were trying to erase her even as she stood in it. Tonight she was thinner than usual.

"That's where the third shard sleeps," she said. "In my book it's written as a sleeping place. A resting place." Her voice did something strange on the word *resting*, folded over itself like paper creased twice. "Tamsin. If the Guild knows where shards sleep—"

"Then the Guild put something there to wake up when we arrived." I finished the thought because someone had to, and saying it aloud made it real in the way ledger-entries are real: fixed, undeniable, waiting to be balanced.

We went anyway. That was the shape our days had taken — knowing the trap and walking into it, because every road led somewhere worse, and this particular somewhere held a piece of the covenant that might, if we gathered enough of them, let me unmake Corvane's careful rot. Five shards, Vespera had said once. Five words, and the Oath could be spoken whole again. We had two. The word that cuts. The word that binds. Both of them lived in me now like swallowed coals, warm and hungry, and both of them had already taken payment from the account of who I was.

I could no longer remember the smell of my mother's bread. I knew I had known it. I knew there had been a kitchen, an apron, flour to the wrists. But the smell itself was gone, spent like coin, and some nights I caught myself reaching for it and finding only smooth nothing where a memory should be.

That was the price of swordspeech. The power wasn't cast *from* you. It was written *in* you, each word carved out of your own substance, and I was beginning to understand that by the time I gathered five shards I would be a man-shaped hole with a sword where his heart used to be.

Which was ironic, given what waited in the chapel.

* * *

The Chapel of the Drowned Bell sat at the bottom of a valley that had flooded a century ago and never drained, though the water had receded enough to leave the structure standing in a marsh of black mud and drowned trees. The bell tower rose from the mire at a drunken angle, and the great bronze bell hung silent within it, furred with moss, its mouth filled solid with earth and roots. A bell that could never ring again. Even the birds avoided it.

"This was a mortal church once," Vespera said as we waded the shallows. Her feet didn't disturb the water, which still seemed to offend Briar on principle. "Before the Sundering bled the border through it. Half the congregation woke up one morning able to hear their dead grandmothers singing hymns. Half the rest turned into herons." A pause. "It's why the shard came here. Places where the seam runs shallow collect things that don't fit anywhere else."

"Like ghosts?"

"Like ghosts," she agreed softly, and would say no more.

The doors had rotted to lace. Inside, the nave stretched long and dim, moonlight falling through the collapsed roof in pale columns, illuminating drifting motes that might have been dust or might have been tiny souls. The mud floor had hardened into ridges like frozen waves. At the far end, where an altar should have been, stood a figure in armor.

It was kneeling.

I had seen hollow knights before — glimpsed them across battlefields, fled them through burning orchards — but always moving, always terrible, dead men puppeted by Guild-iron and stolen oath-fire. This one knelt with its helm bowed and its gauntlets folded, in a posture so perfectly, so deliberately reverent that my skin crawled. It had come here, day after day, and knelt before an empty altar where a shard it could not touch slept in the stone.

Or — and the thought arrived cold and complete — it hadn't been guarding the shard at all.

"It's praying," Briar breathed beside me. "Gods below. Look at it. It's been *praying*."

"Don't," Vespera said sharply. "Don't give it that. Hollowed knights are empty. The Guild scoops everything out and pours obedience in. What you're seeing is habit, nothing more. Muscle-memory of faith in meat that doesn't remember why."

But her voice shook, and I had learned by now that when Vespera shook, it was usually because the truth was worse than the lie she was offering instead.

I stepped forward. "I am Tamsin Eastwick," I said, using the formal cadence Vespera had taught me, the old address of one oath-bearer to another. "Keeper of the ledgers. Speaker of the waking word. I claim the shard that sleeps here, and I mean no harm to what remains."

The knight's head lifted.

The helm was old-fashioned, its visor a lattice of slots gone green with age, and through them I saw — nothing. Not darkness. Nothingness, the way a window at night shows you nothing when the room behind it has been emptied of everything, including the dark. Then, deep in that absence, a light kindled. Small. Blue-white. Steady as a votive candle.

And it was looking directly at me.

"*Eastwick,*" the knight said.

The voice was ruin — rust and grave-dust and something underneath that had once been capable of laughter. My name in its mouth sounded like a sentence being read aloud. Behind me I heard Briar's blades clear their sheaths and Vespera's sharp intake of breath that ghosts shouldn't need.

"You know me?" I managed.

"I know the *name*." The knight rose, joint by joint, and the sound it made was like a ship settling into deep water. It did not draw its weapon — a great two-handed blade still slung across its back, untouched. "A hundred years I have knelt here. A hundred years they have worn me like a glove and made me do their killing, and always, between uses, they bring me back to this chapel and set me down before the altar, and the shard in the stone hums against the shard in my chest, and for a few minutes—" The light in the helm flickered. "For a few minutes I remember being a man. His name was Aldous Vane. He liked honey in his tea. He had a daughter with a laugh like breaking ice."

"Stop," Vespera whispered.

"He was a swordspeaker," the knight continued, inexorable. "One of the eleven graves. One of the twelve names. And when he died they took his body and his shard and they made him into this, and the worst part, boy, the part you need to carry — is that the shard keeps the soul. That's how the binding works. The Guild couldn't puppet a truly dead man. So the shard holds what's left of him, right here, behind the iron, and lets them drive the body. He is in here. He has *always* been in here. Watching his hands do what they've done."

The nave was silent except for the drip of marsh-water and my own pulse.

"The third shard," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, because inside I was anything but. "It's fused to your heart."

"Not my heart." The blue light guttered, almost — almost — like laughter. "My heart stopped a century ago. The shard *is* my heart now. It beats for me. It remembers for me. And you want it."

"Yes."

"To mend the covenant. To finish what we started." The knight — Aldous, I had to make myself think of it as Aldous, a man with a name and a daughter and tea taken sweet — stepped closer, and this close I could see the fine engraving on his breastplate, worn nearly smooth: a bell, a sword, and twelve stars. "Then you must open my chest and take it. And when you take it, everything it holds goes with it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? There is no surgery here, keeper. The shard doesn't sit in me. It *is* me. You will reach into my chest and pull out the last living piece of Aldous Vane, and what remains will finally, truly die."

"Twice," I said.

"Twice," he agreed. "First death is cheap. Everyone gets one. The second is the one that matters — the second is the one where you stop waiting to see what happens next."

Behind me, Vespera made a sound I had never heard from her, small and cracked, and when I glanced back she was staring at the knight with an expression I couldn't read — grief wearing recognition's face, or the other way around.

"Aldous," she said.

The blue light swung to her. Held. Something moved through the nothing behind the visor, something vast and slow, like a whale passing beneath a boat.

"You're the ghost from the book," he said. "The one the Guild fears. They speak of you in the dark places. The prophet who writes backward." A long pause, and then, gently, horribly: "You look like your sister when you grieve. Did you know that? Ysolde made exactly that face, the last time I saw her alive."

Vespera vanished. Not left — *vanished*, snuffed like a candle, which I hadn't known ghosts could do, and the space where she'd stood stayed cold for a long moment after.

Briar's hand found my shoulder. "You don't have to," she said quietly. "We can find another way. There's always another way."

"There isn't," said the knight, "and it's cruel of you to pretend otherwise, fox-child. Every hour I remain is an hour Corvane can call me back into his hand. Every hour is more killing done by these hands while I watch from behind the glass." The blue light steadied, and I understood suddenly, completely, that what I was looking at was hope. A hundred years of horror, ending in a kneeling chapel, asking a stranger for a knife. "You came for the shard. Take it. Take *all* of it. That isn't violence, boy. That's the first mercy anyone has offered me since the bells drowned."

* * *

I want to record, in whatever internal ledger remains to me, exactly how it happened, because I refuse to let it soften into legend in my own telling.

There was no spell for opening a hollow knight. Swordspeech could cut steel and bind rivers and unmake lies, but it could not — would not — do this gently. So I did it with tools. Briar pried the breastplate's seals with her knife-points while I braced the pauldrons, and the armor came away in pieces, and beneath it there was no body as such. There was a shape. A man-shaped arrangement of grey tissue and old scar and Guild-iron wiring, fused at every joint, and in the center of the chest, where the sternum should have been, the shard.

It was larger than the others. It pulsed. Blue-white light beat within it in a slow rhythm — the rhythm I had been hearing for three days, the second heartbeat, the out-of-step one — and around it the grey flesh had grown like bark grown around a nail, integrated, interwoven, *necessary*. To take the shard was to take the heart. To take the heart was to end the man.

"I remember," Aldous Vane said conversationally, watching my hands work, "the day the covenant broke. We felt it go, all twelve of us, like a bell cracking inside our ribs. Ysolde screamed. I've never heard a person scream like that — like the sound was coming out of the wound in the world and using her throat to do it." His head tilted back against the altar steps. "They told us afterward it was sabotage. An enemy. We hunted the enemy for years. It was only near the end I understood there was no enemy. The Oath was flawed from its making. It demanded, and demand curdles, and curdled oaths shatter. She saw it first. Ysolde saw it first, and we called her a traitor for it."

His chest lay open beneath my hands. The light spilled up over my face, warm as a hearth, and I could feel the other two shards in me leaning toward it, recognizing kin.

"Tell me about your daughter," I said. Because someone should ask. Because a hundred years of kneeling deserved one question asked kindly.

The light flared — and steadied, softer. "Maren. Maren Vane. Laughed like breaking ice, I told you. She'd be dead of old age now, most likely, or descended, or..." The pulse stuttered. "Take the shard, keeper. Before I remember anything else that hurts."

"How do I do it without—"

"You don't. Without is not on offer." And then, in the tone of a man giving final instructions about a household: "Pull straight up. Don't twist. And when it comes free, say the word written on it. Say it *quickly*, because for a moment the shard will hold both of us — me and you — and the word decides which way the door opens. Say it with mercy in your mouth and I go through clean. Say it with fear and I drag you halfway along, and believe me, boy, neither of us wants that."

My hands were shaking. In forty chapters of my life I had catalogued bottled storms and stolen shadows and the teeth of extinct saints, and my hands had never shaken, because ledgers don't require courage, only accuracy.

"Tamsin." Briar, low. "Whatever happens — I'm here."

Vespera reappeared at the edge of my vision, thin as breath on glass. She said nothing. She had her sister's face, Aldous had said, when she grieved. She grieved it now.

I reached in.

The shard was warm. Warmer than warm — it was *alive* in the way a heart is alive, and the moment my fingers closed around it the boundary between it and me dissolved, and I was inside Aldous Vane's remembrance. Honey in tea. A daughter's laughter. The weight of an oath sworn young among twelve stars. The crack. The screaming. A century of kneeling in the dark, watching his hands, *watching his hands*, and the single stubborn ember of self he had kept lit through all of it by repeating one thing, over and over, in the vault of his ruined skull:

*Maren. Maren. Maren.*

I pulled.

It came free with a sound like the world's largest door unlocking, and the light in the helm began to fail, and I brought the shard to my lips and spoke the word written in fire across its face — the word that opens — and I said it the way you say a name over a grave, with everything gentle that you have.

*Aldous Vane died the second time with his own name in his ears.*

The armor collapsed inward like a tide going out. The blue light rose once, twice, three times from the empty helm — three slow pulses, a farewell, a bow — and scattered into motes that drifted up through the broken roof toward a sky that had, for just that moment, no seam in it at all.

I knelt in the wreckage holding a dead man's heart, and it beat against my palm twice more before going still, and I found I was crying, which was inefficient, undignified, and absolutely necessary.

Briar crouched beside me and said nothing for a long time. Then: "Mercy as violence," she murmured. "That's the whole covenant, isn't it? That's what the old swordspeakers never understood. You can't compel kindness. You can only choose it, and choosing costs."

"The word," I said. My voice was wrecked. "The word that opens. It's in me now. Three of five." I looked down at the shard, pulsing gently, patient as a seed. "Two left, Vespera. Where—"

I stopped.

Because Vespera was standing over the fallen armor, and she had gone utterly still, and she was looking at the inside of Aldous's breastplate where centuries of proximity had etched new lines into the old engravings — lines that hadn't come from any craftsman. Scratched from within, by fingers that had a hundred years to wait and nothing else to do. Words, cut letter by letter into the iron from the inside of the tomb he'd worn.

She read them aloud, and her ghost-voice carried strangely in the drowned chapel, and every word landed like a stone:

*"If anyone finds this — the twelfth name is not lost. It was smudged to hide it from the Guild. The twelfth swordspeaker lives. Ask the ghost whose book it is what she did to her sister. Ask her what Ysolde's bloodline owes. The oath wakes in the heir."*

The chapel was silent.

Slowly, terribly, Vespera turned to look at me — at the name I carried, at the shards beating in my chest like extra hearts — and for the first time since I'd met her, the prophecy-ghost looked afraid of me.

"Eastwick," she said. "Your name is Eastwick."
