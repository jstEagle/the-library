## 18. The City That Sits on the Seam

We smelled Greyharrow before we saw it.

That was Briar's doing — she made us stop on the ridge while the light was still the color of old pewter, and she said, *Breathe. Learn it first.* The wind came up off the valley carrying woodsmoke that was not quite woodsmoke, something sweeter underneath, like resin burned in a chapel, and beneath that the mineral cold of deep water, and beneath even that a smell I could only call *other* — green and metallic at once, the scent of a place where grass might remember being sword-blades.

"Two realms," Briar said, "sharing one set of chimneys."

Then the dawn came, and Greyharrow revealed itself, and I forgot to be tired.

The city lay along a river that ran in two directions at once. That is not poetry. I watched it happen: the water flowed east toward the mortal lowlands, gray and sensible, barges poled by men in oilskins; but in the middle of the channel, along an invisible seam, the current split, and west of that line the river ran *backward*, uphill into a country that did not appear on any map I had ever catalogued — a country of towers grown rather than built, pale as antlers, veiled in mist the color of mother-of-pearl. Where the two currents met they did not churn. They braided, silver over gray, gray over silver, like two hands laced together.

And the city sat across both banks as if sitting were the simplest thing in the world. Mortal quarters of slate roofs and crooked gables crowded the eastern shore. On the western edge, fae structures — if you could call them structures — spiraled out of the living rock, translucent, lit from within by no fire I could name. Between them, bridging seam and river both, sprawled the true city: streets that began in brick and ended in birchwood that had never been cut from any tree, lamps that burned oil on one side of a threshold and moonglow on the other. Markets spilled across the boundary line without ceremony. A woman sold apples from a cart that stood precisely half in each world; on her mortal side the fruit was red, and on her fae side — I squinted until my eyes ached — the same fruit gleamed faintly gold, and she charged different prices for each and cheated neither customer.

"It's real," I said, and hated how small my voice came out.

"You sound disappointed," Briar said.

"I sound—" I searched for the word. "I've spent three weeks walking through a dying borderland. Hollowed knights. Blighted groves. Rivers running backward through ash." Vespera's ghost-light flickered at my shoulder, saying nothing, which from her was agreement. "I started to believe the covenant's fall meant the end of everything. And here's a city that simply — refused."

"Don't romanticize it yet," Briar said, but gently, the way you correct a child so they'll be right later instead of happy now. "Greyharrow isn't a miracle. It's a negotiation that has been renegotiated every single day for four hundred years. Nobody here trusts anybody. They've just decided that distrust is not a reason to stop trading bread." She started down the slope. "Come on. If we enter before the morning bells, we can pass for pilgrims. After the bells, everyone entering looks like a refugee, and refugees get questions."

"We *are* refugees," I said.

"Yes. So walk faster."

* * *

The gate was a bridge, because of course it was — everything in Greyharrow was a bridge or wanted to be. The Seamgate spanned the river at its braided center, and it had been built by no single hand: the eastern arch was quarried granite carved with mortal saints whose faces time had softened into anonymity; the western arch was grown bone-white timber, still faintly warm, and when I laid my palm against it as we crossed I felt a pulse, slow as a sleeping heart. Between the arches the road itself changed underfoot — cobblestone becoming root-woven planks becoming cobblestone again — and my boots reported each transition like a translator stumbling between languages.

There was a tollhouse at the exact midpoint. Two officials sat in it. One was human, gray-whiskered, reading a newspaper. The other was fae — tall, silver-eyed, her skin patterned with the faint lichen-marks of the blighted edge, though hers looked older, deliberate, like tattoos earned rather than symptoms suffered. Between them on the desk sat a single ledger.

A ledger. My profession found me even here.

"Business in Greyharrow?" the human asked, without looking up.

"Pilgrimage," Briar said smoothly. "To the Chapel of Both Waters."

The fae official glanced up, and her gaze went past me to the air above my left shoulder, where Vespera hovered in her dim violet ember-glow, visible only to those who knew how to look. The official's expression did not change, but her pen stopped moving.

"Ghost-bound?" she asked quietly.

"Chronic condition," Briar said.

The pen resumed. "Toll is two coppers or one truth. Truths are non-refundable."

I fished in my coat and found one copper and a button. The human official sighed at our combined poverty and waved us through, but the fae leaned out of the tollhouse window as we passed and said, very softly, so that only I could hear:

"The book you carry writes itself, swordspeaker. Be careful what it writes about this city."

My hand went to my satchel — to the prophecy-book, Vespera's century-old ledger, its pages sometimes turning on their own these days, sometimes showing me lines I hadn't read yet. When I checked, it was closed and innocent. Vespera's voice came thin and cold beside my ear: *"She's right. And she knows more than a toll-collector should. Remember her face."*

I remembered it. Silver eyes, lichen-marks, and pity — pity aimed at me like something thrown.

* * *

Inside, Greyharrow swallowed us whole, and I let it.

We came down into the Weaver's Quarter, the district straddling the seam itself, and I will spend the rest of whatever life I have failing to describe it properly, but I will try. The streets here were paved with stone on their northern edges and moss on their southern, and children played hopscotch across the line, hopping between worlds as casually as hopping between squares. A smithy stood at a corner where the seam passed directly through the forge: the smith worked iron on the mortal side, and his partner — a fae with arms like birch branches — sang to the metal as it cooled, and the blades that came out of that forge held an edge no mortal steel could keep. Above them hung a sign: TWO HANDS, ONE WORK.

An apothecary's shop displayed its wares in a window that was half glass and half ice that never melted, mortals' remedies on one side, fae simples on the other, and in the middle shelf, priced highest of all, medicines that worked in both worlds — brewed, said the card, *on the line*.

People argued in the street. That was the thing that undid me, more than the architecture, more than the river running two ways. A fishmonger and a fae ferryman were shouting at each other about mooring rights, loudly, rudely, with gestures that would have gotten them arrested anywhere civilized — and then they finished, spat on their palms, shook hands, and the ferryman gave the fishmonger's daughter a candied plum off his cart. The argument had been real. So was the plum. Neither canceled the other.

"Do you see it?" I asked Vespera. "Do you see what this is?"

"I see a city," she said, but her ember-light had gone soft, the way a face goes soft. "I saw cities like it once, before the breaking. There were eleven of them, sitting on eleven seams, and we swore the Oath in the twelfth, and every one of those towns believed what you believe right now. That proximity would become trust. That trust would become permanence." Her glow guttered. "Eleven ruins, Tamsin. Love this place. But love it with your eyes open."

"Briar loves it."

"Briar grew up on the blighted edge watching everything she knew starve for want of exactly what this city has. Of course she loves it." A pause. "So do I. That's why I'm frightened."

Briar, ahead of us, turned and walked backward a few steps, grinning — actually grinning, the first full grin I'd seen on her since the night she pulled my broken body out of Corvane's reach. "You're both staring like farm boys at a cathedral. Come on. I know a house where we can sleep behind a real door, and a woman who owes me nine favors and cooks like sin."

* * *

The house belonged to a hedge-witch named Odile who had a mortal father, a fae mother, and the permanent exhausted expression of someone who had inherited debts from both. She took one look at Briar, one look at my bandaged ribs, one look at the empty air where Vespera drifted, and said, "Three beds and a bath. And you're telling me everything over stew, because if trouble followed you across the Seamgate, I want to know its name before it knocks."

The stew was mushroom and barley and something golden that tasted the way the smell of the city had promised. We told her most of it — the prophecy, the shards, the hollowed knights, Corvane. Not all of it. Never all of it. But Odile listened with her spoon stopped halfway to her mouth, and when Briar described the blighted edge, Odile closed her eyes and murmured something in a language that made the fire lean toward her.

"So the Guild wants the border sealed," Odile said at last. "Or broken entirely, depending on which lie pays better that week. And you're collecting the pieces of the oldest contract in the world to stop them."

"To *replace* it," I said. "Eventually. If there's anything left to replace it with."

Odile looked at me for a long moment. "You know what Greyharrow is, don't you? Really?"

"A city on the seam."

"A city on the seam that *shouldn't exist*." She set down her spoon. "When the covenant broke, every other crossing point collapsed or turned poisonous overnight. Eleven seams died. Why do you suppose this one didn't? Four hundred years, and the Guild has never managed to close the Greyharrow crossing, and the fae courts have never managed to swallow it, and every treaty, every war, every purge has rolled over this city and left it standing." She tapped the table, twice, hard. "Because people stayed. Thousands of them, mortal and fae and everything between, year after year, generation after generation, choosing — daily, boringly, stubbornly choosing — to live together. You want to know what holds a covenant together, swordspeaker? It isn't words spoken by twelve heroes a thousand years ago. It's this." She swept her hand at the room, the city beyond the shuttered window, the whole improbable fact of Greyharrow. "It's a smithy with two names on the sign. It's a toll paid in truths. Your five shards are impressive. This is what they're *for*."

Nobody spoke for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the evening bells rang from three towers in three slightly different harmonies, and the sounds braided together the way the river did.

"That's the hope, then," Vespera said quietly, from the corner where her light barely reached. "Not the shards. The city."

"That's the hope," Odile agreed. "The shards are just the key. Greyharrow is proof the door is worth opening."

I slept that night in a bed, behind a real door, in a city that ran on stubbornness, and I dreamed — for the first time since my mother's face was taken from me — of something other than loss. I dreamed of the ledger I kept for the Guild of Rare Exchanges, and in the dream I opened it and found every impossible thing catalogued inside it — bottled storms, stolen shadows, rivers running two directions — and beside each entry, where the acquisition price should have been written, someone had written the same word over and over in my own handwriting: *possible.*

* * *

Morning arrived with the smell of baking bread and the sound of Briar arguing with a milk-seller, which I understood was simply how she said good morning. We went out into the city to learn it — Vespera insisted, and for once Briar agreed with her, which frightened me more than any knight had.

"Corvane will come here eventually," Vespera said as we walked. "If he doesn't already have people here. A city like this is either his greatest obstacle or his greatest prize. Find out which before he does."

So we learned Greyharrow. We learned that the magistrates' court on Warrant Hill flew the Guild's scales-and-key above the city crest, and that people spoke of the magistrates in the lowered voices people use indoors near a sleeping dog. We learned that the seam-river ferries were licensed by a Guild charter, that the two-hands forge paid a "harmony tax" that no one could explain and no one dared refuse, that fae who came through the Seamgate without papers disappeared at a rate the newspapers called regrettable and the taverns called regular.

And we learned, from a laundress with forearms like cable and no patience for strangers, that when the law failed people here — when a magistrate ruled for coin over justice, when a fae was seized on a technicality, when a mortal family was evicted from a house that straddled the seam — there was another court. An underground one. She wouldn't say where. She only looked at Briar for a long moment, taking in the lichen-marks, the blight-scars, the way Briar stood like someone who had already lost everything worth guarding, and said: "Ask at the drowned chapel bell on Fetter Lane. Third knock. Tell them Marta sent you, and tell them she still remembers what they did for her son."

Briar and I exchanged a glance. The drowned chapel — I had claimed the first shard from a drowned chapel, miles back in the borderland, and my skin still crawled remembering the word that cuts. But names repeat. Cities echo each other. And a court that met below the law, in a city where the law wore the Guild's colors, sounded very much like a door we needed open.

We were walking toward Fetter Lane when the bells changed.

All of them at once — the three towers, the chapel carillons, the little cracked bell over the apothecary with the frozen window — shifting from their morning harmonies into a single repeating note, flat and urgent. In the streets, people stopped. Carts halted. The fishmonger put down his knife. The fae ferryman poled his barge to the wall and held it there, motionless, listening.

"Herald bells," Odile had told us the night before, and now I heard her voice in memory: *They only ring them for two things. A death in the old bloodline, or an announcement from the Magistracy. Pray it's the second.*

Criers appeared at the corners, men in the gray-and-crimson of the Magistracy, unrolling proclamations with the crisp violence of people who enjoyed the unfolding. The nearest one lifted his voice over the hush, and it carried down Fetter Lane and into the square and into my chest like a blade slid between ribs:

"By order of the Magistracy of Greyharrow, in concert with the honorable Guild of Rare Exchanges: the fugitive Tamsin Eastwick, oath-breaker and agent of the borderland unrest, is declared *persona non grata* within city walls. Any citizen harboring him shall answer to the court. Any fae sheltering him shall answer to the *severance*. Reward of five hundred crowns—"

The crier went on. The crowd around us had begun, very slowly, to turn — heads lifting, eyes moving across the square, looking for a face they'd just been taught to fear.

Beside me, Briar had gone very still. Ahead, at the far mouth of the lane, two gray-and-crimson guardsmen were already walking our way — unhurried, professional, checking faces against a paper one of them held.

"Tamsin," Vespera whispered, her ember-light drawing tight around her like a fist. "Walk. Don't run. Walk toward the river."

I walked. Around me the city that had filled me with hope all morning became, in the space of a hundred heartbeats, a city of shutters beginning to close — and somewhere beneath the herald bells, faint but unmistakable, I heard the pages of the prophecy-book turning on their own, writing down what happened next before I lived it.

The third knock at the drowned chapel bell, I thought. Let's hope the underground keeps stranger hours than the law above it.

Behind us, the guardsmen quickened their pace.
