## 19. Justice Outside the Law

Briar had learned long ago that a city could lie with its stones.

Greyharrow wore its honesty on its face — the seam-glow along the cobbles where the Otherworld bled through, the lanterns that burned blue on one side of a street and amber on the other, the market stalls where mortals haggled with fox-masked fae over jars of bottled laughter. A city like this seemed to have nothing to hide. That was precisely what made it such an accomplished liar.

Briar walked Tamsin through it in the grey hour before dusk, when both realms' light mingled and neither could claim the sky. She kept her hood up. Even here — *especially* here — a fae's bare ears drew eyes, and eyes drew magistrates, and magistrates in Greyharrow drew wages from the Guild of Rare Exchanges. She had counted the coin-strings herself once, before she'd learned to count anything at all. Every magistrate's chain of office bore a small lead seal shaped like an open ledger. She used to think it was a symbol of transparency. It was a symbol of ownership.

"You're quiet," Tamsin said.

"I'm counting," Briar said. "Watch the third lamp-post ahead."

He watched. The lamp-post was iron, older than the paving around it, and as they passed it Briar saw the moment he saw: the way the flame inside the glass bent *away* from them, thinning like a held breath. Ward-lamp. Guild-work. It tasted every soul that passed beneath it and sang a silent note into the network if it caught something with too much Otherworld in its blood.

"Does it see you?" Tamsin murmured.

"It sees me. It doesn't know my name yet." She touched his elbow and turned him down a crooked lane where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators. "That's the difference between being watched and being hunted. The day it learns my name is the day we stop walking and start running."

"And how does a lamp learn a name?"

"Somebody writes it down." She let the bitterness show in her teeth rather than her voice. "Your Guild keeps very good ledgers, Eastwick. I'm told you would know."

He didn't flinch. She'd come to respect that about him — the human habit of absorbing a blow and filing it away for later, like a debt entered in a book. Everything with this one came back to ledgers eventually. He thought in columns and balances even now, even with prophecy burning holes through his life. Perhaps that was why Vespera's ghost had chosen him. Or perhaps it was why she'd been forced to.

The lane opened onto a tanner's yard, long abandoned; the pits were dry and furred with moss, and the smell of old lime still haunted the walls like a grief that refused to leave a house. Briar crossed to the far wall, where a drainage arch stood half-sunk in earth, and pressed her palm flat against the stone. Beneath the skin of mortar, warm as a pulse, something answered.

*Knock twice,* the old wards said, in the voice of someone who had died decades ago. *Knock true.*

She knocked twice, and then — because the words demanded truth and she had promised herself she would never lie to this place — she laid her hand back against the stone and gave it her name. Not the name the Guild had entered in their registers. Not the name her captors had shouted across a border-field when they took her sister. Her *true* name, the one that tasted of birch-smoke and river-ice, the one she had not spoken aloud since the covenant broke and names became dangerous things to spend.

The arch exhaled. Stone unknit itself into a doorway without a door, and warm air breathed out, smelling of candlewax, wet wool, and — faintly, underneath everything — of iron and salt, which meant someone down there had been bleeding recently.

"After you," Briar said.

Tamsin looked at the dark. "Is this legal?"

"That," Briar said, stepping through, "is the entire point."

* * *

The courts of Greyharrow's underground lived in the bones of the city, in cellars and cisterns and the drowned foundations of buildings that had burned a century ago and never been rebuilt. Briar led Tamsin down stairways cut by hands that hadn't bothered with level or plumb, past niches where candles guttered in dishes of tallow, past walls scratched with tallies — days served, debts paid, children lost, each mark a small act of insistence that someone, somewhere, remembered.

The chamber they finally entered had been a wine cellar once, and before that, if you believed the oldest stories, a fae barrow-moot, back when the two peoples still argued in person instead of through treaties and tariffs. Its ceiling arched low and black. Its benches were rough planks on barrels. And its people —

Its people were everyone the city above had decided did not matter.

Briar stood at the back, hood down now — down here, among these people, hiding felt like an insult — and watched Tamsin take it in. The dockside widow whose husband had been seized as a hollow knight and returned to her as something else, something that didn't know her name. The fox-fae child with burn-scars where a Guild warden's salt-whip had taught her that her magic was contraband. The human stonemason who'd built three of the seam-gates and been paid in promissory notes the Guild refused to honor. An old man with a soldier's bearing and no shadow at all — a stolen shadow, Briar realized, one of the impossible things, sold through your guild's catalogues, Eastwick, priced and itemized like a jar of storm.

At the front of the room stood a woman of perhaps sixty, silver-streaked, dressed in a magistrate's black robe with the sleeves torn off at the shoulder — deliberately, Briar knew, the way a freed prisoner wears the scars of irons. She held no gavel. She held a wooden spoon, which she used the way other judges used gold.

"Maren Thistle-down," Tamsin breathed beside her. "That's — she presided over the assizes. Her verdicts are taught at the Inns. She retired—"

"She was *retired*," Briar corrected, "the way a tooth is pulled. Eleven years ago she ruled against a Guild seizure of fae households in the Weft Quarter. Within a season her writs were being ignored, her clerks reassigned, her court docket filled with cases she was forbidden to hear. When she ruled anyway, they stripped her office. So she went underground. Literally." Briar's mouth curved without warmth. "The magistrates above serve whoever signs their wage-seals. Maren serves the law. It turns out those are different employers entirely."

Below, Maren struck her spoon against a barrel-head, and the room settled into silence the way water settles after a dropped stone.

"Bring in the next complaint," she said.

They brought in a fisherman, a mortal, hands ruined by rope and cold. His story came out haltingly, in the flat voice of a man rehearsing grief until it stopped cutting: the Guild's assessors had declared his daughter changeling-touched — half-mortal blood, one fae grandparent — and under the Statutes of Seamed Commerce, changeling-touched persons could be "held for assessment." Indefinitely. At the Guild's discretion.

"How long has she been held?" Maren asked.

"Fourteen months."

"What cause has been shown?"

"A paper. They showed me a paper that said there was a paper."

A sound went through the room — low, collective, the growl of a pack deciding whether tonight was the night. Briar felt it move through her own chest too, that old hot coal, the one the Guild's whips had banked but never smothered. Fourteen months. Somewhere in a gate-prison a girl was sitting in salt and iron right now, learning that her grandmother's blood was a crime, while the lawful courts above stamped papers about papers.

"The writ of *habeas corpus*," Maren said, and her voice was iron wrapped in wool, "is the oldest promise this city ever made. Before the covenant, before the Guild, before either realm drew its borders — there was a promise that no person would be held without cause shown. Do you understand what they've done, master fisherman? They haven't broken that promise. Breaking it would at least be honest. They've made it *unreachable*. They've buried it under procedure until digging it out costs more than most people have to live on." She set down the spoon. "This court finds the detention unlawful. This court orders the Guild's assessors to produce the girl within nine days. And this court notes, for the record—" here she raised her voice, though there was no record, no clerk, nothing above them but earth and root and stone— "*for the record*, that a law which cannot be appealed against is not a law. It is a leash. And leashes, gentlemen of the Guild, are made to be slipped."

The room came up off its benches. Not cheering exactly — something rawer than cheering, the sound of people who had been told so long that they were powerless discovering a room where the word had no meaning. The fox-fae child clapped with her scarred hands. The shadowless soldier stood at attention as if before a general. And Tamsin Eastwick, keeper of ledgers, last of the swordspeakers, stood very still with an expression Briar couldn't read — until she could.

Shame. He was ashamed. She watched him do the arithmetic she knew he couldn't help doing: the Guild's accounts, tidy in his own handwriting, every entry balanced, and beneath all those neat columns this — this cellar full of people the balances had erased.

"They don't know," he said quietly. "Most of them. The clerks, the wardens. They think the Guild keeps order."

"Order for whom?" Briar asked. "I spent three years in a gate-prison listening to guards apologize to me. They were sorry, truly sorry, every time. They had families. They believed they were keeping *peace*." She turned to face him fully. "The rot isn't in the hearts of the little men, Eastwick. It's in the structure. A building can be full of kind tenants and still be built on a grave."

* * *

Later — after the fisherman had been given a advocate's name and a safehouse address written in cipher on a scrap of oilcloth; after a fae tinker presented a dispute over a stolen song (a literal song, humming in a brass locket, and Maren heard expert testimony from three musicians before awarding damages in the currency of favors); after the session dissolved into the murmur of neighbors comparing wounds and remedies — Briar found Maren Thistle-down by the cold wine-racks, drinking tea that smelled of willow bark and stubbornness.

"You brought a stranger," Maren said, without looking up. "A Guild man, by the ink on his fingers. I should have you flogged, Briar-of-the-Briars."

"You should. You won't. You're sentimental."

"I am *thorough*." Now she looked up, and her eyes were the pale grey of a winter morning — the eyes of a woman who had sentenced men to hang and slept well afterward, and hated herself for sleeping well, and done it again anyway. "Who is he?"

"He's the one the prophecies keep writing about."

Maren snorted. "Prophecies. Half the misery in both worlds comes from people believing in prophecies, and the other half from people *writing* them."

"That," said Tamsin, emerging from the dark between racks, "is the most sensible thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

"Then your weeks have been wasted." Maren set down her cup and studied him the way she must once have studied defendants: weighing, measuring, finding the seams. "You want something from my court, Guild man. Nobody walks down four flights of bad stairs for sightseeing."

"I want to understand why it works," Tamsin said. "This place. There's no enforcement. No gaolers. If someone defied a verdict, what would you do?"

"Nothing," Maren said pleasantly. "Nothing at all. And everyone in this room would know it, and no one would ever bring me another complaint, and the court would die of it — quietly, in a fortnight, without a funeral. Which is why," and here she smiled, and it was not a comforting smile, "no one defies the verdicts. Not because we punish. Because we *remember*. Upstairs they have irons and gallows and they are defied daily, because fear only holds people who are alone. Down here we have memory, and memory holds everyone." She picked her cup back up. "The Guild sells protection, boy, and calls the selling justice. We give away justice, and somehow it protects better than what they sell. Work out the economics of that and bring me the answer. I've been trying for eleven years."

Tamsin bowed — actually bowed, awkward and sincere, a ledger-clerk's bow to a judge — and moved off toward the benches, where the shadowless soldier had begun telling him, in a low furious voice, exactly how a man's shadow could be taken and what remained behind.

Maren watched him go. Then, softly: "You believe in him."

"I believe in what's chasing him," Briar said. "It makes belief simpler."

"Briar." The old woman's voice changed, dropped its courtroom weight, became just a tired human voice in a cold cellar. "When this ends — however it ends — the courts upstairs won't reform themselves. Power never surrenders out of embarrassment. Whatever new covenant your swordspeaker means to swear, swear one with teeth for people like the fisherman's daughter. Promise me the next oath remembers the small grievances. Big ones make ballads. Small ones make revolutions."

Briar thought of the blighted edge of the Otherworld, of the withered orchards and the silent bells, of everything her own people had lost and refused to mourn aloud. *Small ones make revolutions.* Yes.

"I promise," she said, and discovered she meant it.

* * *

They left by a different door than they'd entered — the underground courts kept a dozen mouths into the city, and Maren's wardens rotated them nightly — and came up through the back of a chandler's shop into streets gone fully dark, the seam-glow in the cobbles the only light, blue-green and restless as deep water.

Tamsin walked a while in silence. Then: "The Statutes of Seamed Commerce. The changeling-touched clauses. Those were drafted—"

"Eighty years ago. By the Guild's own advocates. Yes." Briar glanced at him. "You audited the acquisition records. You really didn't know?"

"I knew the Guild traded in difficult things. Bottled storms. Stolen shadows." His jaw worked. "I told myself difficult meant *strange*. Not—" he gestured backward, toward the hidden stairs, the whole buried architecture of grievance— "not this. Not people filed under *assets pending assessment*."

"The first shard," Briar said quietly, "was called the Word That Cuts. You told me it nearly unmade you. Do you know why the first swordspeakers needed a word that cuts? Not for monsters. For *institutions*. For the great soft grinding machines that devour people politely, with paperwork, one clause at a time. Steel can't reach that. Only speech can." She kicked a pebble from the cobbles into the gutter and watched the seam-glow ripple around it. "Corvane knows it too. That's why he wants you broken or bought. A swordspeaker loose in a city like this — one who's seen what we saw tonight — is worth more than every vault in the Guild."

Ahead, where the street bent toward the river, a crowd had gathered around a lit archway — a preaching-stage draped in violet cloth, and atop it a woman in white, arms raised, her voice carrying that peculiar honeyed resonance Briar recognized and despised: the trained timbre of someone selling certainty by the ounce.

"—and I tell you, beloved, the visions do not lie! I have *seen* the border sealed! I have seen the fae driven back beyond the veil and the streets of Greyharrow clean and bright and *safe*! Bring me your fears and I will read them true—"

Briar stopped. Beside her, Tamsin had gone rigid, his breath fogging the cold air too fast.

"You feel that?" he whispered.

She did. Underneath the preacher's voice — threaded through it, wound into it like a wire through wax — ran a familiar hum, the taste of old power badly handled. Shard-light. One of the Oath's fragments, worn openly, wielded like a stage-lamp, its true word bent into parlor trickery and crowd-craft.

"That woman," Briar said slowly, "has no business holding what she's holding."

"No," Tamsin agreed. His hand had drifted, without his seeming to notice, toward the satchel where five shards' worth of knowledge sat coiled in his memory like sleeping serpents. His eyes had the look she'd begun to dread and admire in equal measure — the look of a man reading an entry that didn't balance.

"She's lying to them," he said. "With a piece of the Oath itself."

And Briar, watching the violet banners snap in the night wind, felt the old coal flare hot again — anger, yes, but also something colder and more useful: the precise, patient fury of a witness.

"Well then," she said. "Let's go hear a false prophet lie."
