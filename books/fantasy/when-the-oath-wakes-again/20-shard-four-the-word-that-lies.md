## 20. Shard Four: The Word That Lies

The district of Candlewick had no candles anymore. It had something better, according to the woman who ruled it — it had Sight.

We came in at dusk, when the fog off the seam rolled down the gutters like milk poured too slow, and every lamp in every window burned behind shutters shaped like praying hands. Briar walked close at my shoulder, her hood up, her antlers bound flat beneath oiled canvas so that she passed, in that light, for a tall mortal woman with a bad stoop. Vespera drifted ahead of us both, a smear of silver in the fog that only I could see clearly, and even for me she was thinner now than she had been in the borderland's green dark. Each week she spent something of herself keeping pace with the living. Each week I pretended not to count the cost.

"There," she said, and her voice was wind through a keyhole. "Do you feel it?"

I did. I had learned to feel them now, the way a sailor learns the sea's moods in his knees before his mind admits the storm. The shard of the Oath sat somewhere ahead of us, under the fog and the shuttered lamps, and it did not hum or sing as I might once have imagined a holy thing would do. It *itched*. It sat in the world like a splinter in a thumb, and everything around it had grown red and swollen trying to push it out or swallow it whole.

Candlewick had swallowed it.

"Three streets east," I said. "Under the Spire."

"You're certain?" Briar murmured.

"I'm carrying four-fifths of an oath in a satchel against my ribs. It knows its siblings." I adjusted the strap. The shards rode against my chest night and day now, wrapped in oilcloth and warded with three layers of guild-grade containment script that Corvane himself had taught me, back when he was still the man who taught me things. They were warm. They had started being warm after Greyharrow, after the underground courts, after the night I'd held the Word That Lies' sister-shards and felt them lean toward the missing piece like dogs scenting home. "And it knows we're coming. It's been restless since dawn."

* * *

Mother Sarrastine had come to Candlewick nineteen years ago, the story went, out of the burning south, with ash still in her hair and a prophecy on her lips. She had foretold the flood of '61 to the hour. She had named the plague-year's dead before they died, in order, alphabetically, and when the list proved true the district had fallen on its knees. She had raised the Spire from the bones of a granary — no mortar, they said, only prayer — and from its needle-thin tower she issued the Words: monthly, then weekly, then daily, pronouncements on who should marry whom, who should trade with whom, who was blessed, who was blighted, whose door the fog must never touch.

The fog obeyed. That was the part the neighboring districts whispered about with envy and fear. When Mother Sarrastine declared a house unclean, the seam-fog pooled against it like a moat until the family fled or starved. When she declared a merchant honest, his ships found fair winds in impossible seasons.

"She's a fraud," I said. We crouched in the doorway of a cooperage two streets from the Spire, watching pilgrims climb the tower stairs with offerings — coins, letters, a live hen with its feet tied, a child's shoe. "A con artist sitting on a shard of the covenant and milking it."

"Is she?" Briar asked quietly.

I looked at her.

"You've seen what the shards do," she said. "They cut. They bind. They open. This one—" she nodded toward the Spire, where a bell was tolling the hour of visions, "—what would you expect a shard of lying to do, in the hands of someone clever? Lie. Beautifully. Verifiably. Prophecies that come true because the shard makes them come true. She may not know what she holds. She may believe utterly in her own divinity. The best liars always do." Briar's mouth twisted. "My people invented that trick, mortal. Do not assume the liar knows she lies."

Vespera had gone very still, there at the edge of the lamplight where the fog thinned. Ghosts have few expressions left to spend, and the one she wore then cost her something.

"I knew a woman like that," she said. "Who believed her own lies because half of them came true. It is the most dangerous kind of believer there is." She did not say the name. She never said the name. But I saw her hand curl, the ghost-memory of a fist, and I wondered — not for the first time — what my ghost had buried in her century of silence.

"So how do we take it from her?" I asked. "She has a fortress full of believers. I can't speak swordspeech in a crowd of innocents — the words don't aim, they *insist*. And if she's got any working control of the shard, she'll have seen us coming in her scrying or whatever passes for it."

"Oh, she's seen you," Vespera said. "She's seen you for days. A man walking toward her with five pieces of a broken god in his bag, trailed by a fae exile and a ghost. You are the most interesting thing to happen in Candlewick in nineteen years." Her silver eyes fixed on me. "Which means she will not wait for you to make the first move. Liars hate leaving moves on the table."

She was right, of course. She usually was, which was one of the many uncomfortable things about being guided by a prophecy-ghost who claimed to have written your life a hundred years before your birth.

The knock came at the cooperage door at moonrise. Three soft raps, and a voice like warm oil: "Tamsin Eastwick. Mother Sarrastine bids you ascend."

* * *

The Spire's interior was all white stone and spiral stairs, and the walls were covered in writing.

Not scripture. *Ledgers.*

I stopped dead on the fourth landing, my keeper's eye catching what the pilgrims streaming past me had apparently never read. Column after column, floor to vaulted ceiling, in a small precise hand: names, dates, sums. *Martha of the Nine Fields, wed to Aldous per the Vision, dowry received, 4 crowns.* *House of Pell declared blighted, property reassigned to the Church of the Sight, grain sold, 31 crowns.* *Fog directed against the Kettle Row holdouts, ninth day; rents collected.* It was a business. It had always been a business. Nineteen years of prophecy and extortion rendered in the same tidy double-entry I kept for the Guild of Rare Exchanges, and I felt something sour rise in my throat, because the hand was beautiful and I recognized the discipline in it, and hating her suddenly felt uncomfortably like looking into a mirror angled wrong.

"The Mother will see you now," said the acolyte, and opened the door at the top of the world.

The vision chamber was round and empty except for a brazier, a chair of white wood, and the woman in it. Mother Sarrastine was smaller than I expected, old in the way of river stones, with cloud-pale eyes that did not track quite right and hands folded in her lap like sleeping birds. On a stand beside her chair lay a sliver of light wrapped in silk — and beneath the silk, unmistakable now, the itch and burn of the fourth shard. The Word That Lies, resting on a lectern like a relic.

"Tamsin Eastwick," she said. Her voice filled the room without rising. "Keeper of ledgers. Bearer of burdens. Come all this way to rob me."

"Mother." I bowed, because flattery costs nothing and liars respect craft. "You have me at a disadvantage. You know my name and my trade. Most people only learn the trade."

"Most people don't walk out of a century-old book." Those unfocused eyes turned toward the corner where Vespera hovered — and *focused*, which sent a jolt through me, because mortals could not see her. "Sit, ghost. You too, horned thing at the door — yes, I see the shape of you under the canvas. Sit, all of you. I've been waiting nineteen years for this conversation, and I intend to enjoy it."

Briar pushed back her hood. Her antlers rose into the brazier-light like branches at dawn. "You see more than Sight, then," she said carefully.

"I see what my gift shows me." Sarrastine smiled, and the smile was terrible because it was gentle. "That is the truth of Mother Sarrastine, told freely to thieves. Will you tell me your truths? Or shall I start?"

"Start with the shard," I said. "Where you found it. What it showed you first."

"It found *me*." For a moment the oil-warm voice cracked and something older looked out. "Nineteen years ago I was a village wise-woman with a talent for cold reading and a bad harvest coming, and I dug it out of a churchyard ditch where it had fallen, I think, from a greater ruin. And it spoke to me. Not words. *Certainty.* It reached into my mouth and gave me sentences, and when I spoke them, the world arranged itself to make them true. Do you understand what that is, ledger-keeper? To be ordinary and mediocre and *poor* all your life, and then to open your mouth and have reality kneel?"

I understood more than I wanted to. The Word That Cuts had done something similar, that day in the drowned chapel — had poured itself into me and made me briefly a thing of edges. I had liked it. I had dreamed about it after. That was the part I never said aloud.

"And so you built this." I gestured at the walls, the ledgers, the whole kneeling district below us. "Blighted houses. Reassigned property. A child's shoe carried up four hundred stairs."

"The shoe is devotion," she said mildly. "Devotion is not extortion. They bring it freely."

"They bring it because you drowned a family's house in fog until they fled."

"Fog is weather." She unfolded her hands. "But let us not waste courtesy, Master Eastwick. You came to take my god from me, and I have lived nineteen years knowing someone would come. The book told me." She nodded at Vespera, and my ghost went taut as wire. "Oh yes. Your prophecy-book names this night, swordspeaker. It names you, and it names the shard, and it says a man will come to decide whether the old covenant lives or dies — and it says, in the same breath, that whatever he chooses, someone pays. I have read my ending a hundred times. Shall I recite it? *The bearer comes in fog. He speaks with a borrowed tongue. He wins by the word that lies, and loses by the truth he tells after.*"

The chamber went very quiet. The brazier popped. Below us, faintly, the pilgrim-bell tolled again, and I heard Briar shift her weight, and Vespera said, low and urgent: "Tamsin. Don't play her game. She's telling you your ending so that you'll perform it."

But Sarrastine wasn't finished. She leaned forward, pale eyes catching brazier-light like frost catching sun.

"So here is my offer, since you seem fond of offers. Walk away tonight, and I give the district truth instead of prophecy. Grain stores, flood warnings, honest rents — I can rule well without the shard, I always could, that was never the point of it. Keep your covenant-mending clean of my sin." Her smile returned, thinner. "Or reach for my god, and learn what nineteen years of certainty does to the hand that holds it. Your other shards will whisper to you, swordspeaker. They already do, don't they? At night. Telling you how much good you could do, if only you spoke more, burned brighter, gave more of yourself. Take the fifth voice into that chorus and see what remains of Tamsin Eastwick by spring."

And God help me, the offer tempted me. Not the walking away — never that — but the arithmetic of it. One district's freedom, purchased by delay. A clean conscience preserved. My mother's face, still mine to remember. The shards against my ribs pulsed warm, as if listening, as if considering.

That was when I understood, with a lurch of self-disgust so hard I nearly swayed, that she had told the truth precisely to make me lie to myself. That was the Word That Lies' opening move. Not deception — *invitation*. Every shard tested its claimant in its own nature. The Word That Cuts had tested whether I would bleed. The Word That Binds had shown me the life I abandoned and asked if I meant it. And this one offered me a deal so reasonable that accepting it would feel like wisdom.

It was waiting to see if I would lie.

* * *

"No," I said, and stood.

Sarrastine tilted her head, curious, a bird watching a worm make its own way to the hook.

"You're right about me," I said. "I hear them at night. I dream in edges. Some mornings I can't find the line between what I want and what the shards want, and that terrifies me more than Corvane does." I let the words come plain, no performance, because I had finally seen the shape of the trap and the only way through it was through. "So no bargain. No clever exchange. Here is the truth, free of charge, the truth you just wagered I couldn't afford: you were never a prophet. You were a lonely, mediocre woman holding a piece of a broken promise, and it lied to you so long you mistook its voice for your own greatness. Everything you built — the ledgers, the fog, the kneeling district — it's a monument to a lie you needed so badly you fed real people to it. Martha of the Nine Fields didn't love Aldous. House Pell starved. The Kettle Row holdouts died in weather you aimed. That's not Sight. That's just cruelty with good penmanship."

Her face did not change. That frightened me more than rage would have.

"And here is the second truth," I went on, my voice steadier than I deserved, "the one your shard didn't show you, because it can't: it doesn't belong to you and it never did. It belongs to the promise twelve people swore to keep each other honest. And the proof—" I reached into my satchel, unwrapped the oilcloth, and let the four shards show themselves to their sibling for the first time in ninety years, "—is that it has never once stopped calling for them. Have you heard it? At night? That thin sound under the floor, like someone knocking? That's not ghosts, Mother. That's your god, homesick."

The silk on the lectern moved.

Just a breath of movement, silk over silver, like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice. And Mother Sarrastine — nineteen years of certainty, nineteen years of a kneeling world — watched her god turn away from her, and her face broke.

It did not break into rage. It broke into grief, sudden and total, the grief of a woman hearing, after two decades, the sound of her own ordinariness coming home. "It never loved me," she said, very small. "All those years. It never once—"

"It used you," Briar said gently. "As the Guild uses. As the old covenant used all of us. That is what compulsion is, lady. It does not matter how sweetly it sings."

Sarrastine looked at the silk for a long moment. Then she laughed — a dry, ruined, honest laugh, the first honest sound I think she'd made in nineteen years — and swept the silk aside herself.

"Take it, then, thief. But you should know what I know, since you value truth so dearly tonight." Her ruined eyes lifted to mine, and there was nothing of the oracle in them anymore, only a tired woman's warning. "Your book says you win by the word that lies and lose by the truth you tell after. You've won. So mind what you say next, swordspeaker. The shard is listening. It takes the truth personally."

The Word That Lies slid from the lectern into my palm, and it was cold — colder than the others, cold as a coin from a dead man's eye — and the moment it touched my skin, the chamber *shifted*.

The walls rippled. The ledgers rewrote themselves before my eyes, my own name appearing in column after column: *declared blighted*, *property reassigned*, *fog directed against*. The brazier flame bent sideways. And in the doorway, Briar's outline doubled, tripled — one Briar reaching to help me, one Briar drawing a blade, one Briar simply walking away down the stairs, and all three equally real to my eyes.

"Out-lying a liar," Vespera murmured, at my ear, sounding almost proud and almost afraid. "You did it, Tamsin. Now put it in the bag before it starts deciding which of us is real."

I closed my fist around the cold. The whispers began immediately — soft, reasonable, *true-sounding* — telling me which Briar was which, telling me exactly what Sarrastine would do next, telling me how much good I could do with five voices instead of four.

I stuffed it into the oilcloth and tied the knot twice.

* * *

We descended the Spire through a district waking to the absence of its god. Pilgrims knelt in the fog-filled streets, wailing or praying or simply staring at their palms. Someone had already thrown a stone through the Church of the Sight's front window. By morning, I knew, there would be looting, and settling of scores, and a hunger for someone to blame — and Mother Sarrastine would still be alive up there in her white room, holding nineteen years of receipts.

"Will they hurt her?" I asked.

"Perhaps," Briar said. "Perhaps worse — they'll stop believing her. For a woman like that, isn't that the same thing?"

Behind us the Spire's bell tolled once and went silent forever. Ahead, the fog parted along seams I couldn't see, and far off, at the world's ragged edge, the borderlands waited with the fifth shard hidden in an unmarked grave.

Vespera drifted close as we walked, and she was quieter than usual, and thinner — the chamber's strain had cost her, I could see it in the way her edges frayed like worn cloth. When she finally spoke, her voice had a strange, resigned weight, like a woman setting down something she had carried too long.

"You should rest tonight, Tamsin. Sleep while the shards are quiet. Because tomorrow I have to tell you about Ysolde." She looked at the fog, not at me. "And once I start, I will not be able to stop."
