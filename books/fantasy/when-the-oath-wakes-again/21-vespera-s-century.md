## 21. Vespera's Century

I was born in the year the rivers ran silver, and I will die — if ghosts can be said to die — in a bookshop that smelled of dust and other people's mistakes.

But before the bookshop there was a village called Aldermere, and before the prophecy there was a girl with ink-stained fingers who shared my bed and my secrets and, for one impossible decade, my whole understanding of what the world was.

Her name was Ysolde Eastwick.

You know her now as a smudge. A name scraped from a page by hands that feared it. But I knew her laughing, and I knew her furious, and I knew her the morning she stood at the edge of the Standing Stones with her sword drawn and told me she was going to unmake the world we had sworn to keep together.

Let me tell you how we got there. Let me tell you everything, Tamsin, because you carry her blood and you deserve to carry her truth as well.

* * *

We were twelve when the swordspeakers came through Aldermere, all twelve of them in their grey traveling cloaks, and the whole village turned out to gawk as though the moon had walked down off the sky. The covenant was already old then — two hundred years of it — but old things still had shine in those days. Magic was not yet a thing that leaked. It was a thing that *held*, like mortar between stones, and the twelve who maintained it were treated as half-angels wherever they went.

They came to test children. They did every generation, though no one remembered why anymore, only that they did, only that a child chosen was a family made forever proud.

They tested us with words.

Not riddles — actual words, spoken aloud into our mouths' silence, and asked whether we heard them as sound or as weight. Most children heard sound. I heard something like weight, a pressure behind the syllables, and I saw the examiner's eyebrows rise, and I thought: *this is the day my life begins.*

Then they tested Ysolde, and she heard neither sound nor weight. She heard *meaning*. She heard what the words wanted. And the oldest swordspeaker — a woman named Maren Hale whose face was a map of every road she'd ever walked — set down her cup and looked at my sister for a long, long moment, and said quietly, to no one in particular:

"Twelve again."

I didn't understand then what she meant. That the covenant required twelve speakers, always twelve, and that the number had been short since before either of us was born, and that they had been searching for a twelfth for twenty years. I only understood that my sister was chosen and I was not, and that night I cried into my pillow with a jealousy so pure and childish that even now, a century later, I can taste its shame.

Ysolde crawled into my bed anyway. She always did when I cried. She put her forehead against mine the way she'd done since we were small enough to fit together in a washbasin, and she whispered, "Whatever they teach me, I'll teach you. Whatever they give me, we'll split it. Sworn on blood."

She pricked both our thumbs and pressed them together.

That was the first oath we ever swore each other. It is the only one I never broke.

* * *

The years that followed were the best years of my life, which is a terrible thing to say about the years that ended in catastrophe, but memory does not consult the ledger of consequences.

Ysolde trained at the covenant-house in Greyharrow — the city that straddles the seam, where the Otherworld's light bleeds through the gutters at dusk — and I went with her, because she refused to go without me and because Maren Hale, in her strange wisdom, allowed it. "The bookkeeper trains beside the blade," she said. "Every speaker needs someone to write down what they say. Otherwise the speaking eats them alive and no one knows what was lost."

So I became the recorder. The archivist. The one who sat in the scriptorium copying the covenant's terms while my sister learned to make language into lightning.

And oh, she was *magnificent*, Tamsin. You have felt a shard of what she was whole. When Ysolde spoke a word of power, the air didn't bend — it *listened*. Flowers opened out of season. Rivers paused mid-current to hear her. I watched her once stop a landslide with four syllables and then sit down afterward and vomit from the cost of it, shaking, gray-faced, and laugh about it an hour later over cheap wine. "Worth it," she said. "Everything worth doing costs. That's how you know."

I wrote down everything. Every word, every price, every tremor in her hands. I filled forty ledgers in ten years. I thought I was preserving history.

I was writing the indictment.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with a question a child could ask and no adult dared answer.

We were in the covenant-house library — Ysolde reading over my shoulder while I transcribed the Oath's founding text, the original sworn words of the first twelve — and she tapped the page with one finger.

"Ves. What happened to the thirteenth?"

"There was no thirteenth. Twelve speakers, twelve shards, twelve names."

"Then why," she said, "does the binding formula account for thirteen voices? Look. Here, here, and here. The cadence only closes if there's a thirteenth term. Someone wrote this for thirteen and then cut it down to twelve."

I told her she was misreading the prosody. I was the scholar; she was the soldier; that was the shape we'd agreed on. But she didn't sleep that night, and neither, in the end, did I, because she was not wrong. I checked the mathematics of the verse seventeen times before dawn and the mathematics said *thirteen* every time.

The founders had built the covenant for thirteen voices and sealed it with twelve.

Which meant the covenant had been broken from the moment it was made. Not shattered later, not decayed through neglect. Born amputated. Born wrong. A promise made by twelve people pretending to be thirteen, and every generation since paying interest on the shortfall — the leaks in the border, the monsters in the seams, the slow bleeding-out of magic into places that couldn't hold it.

When I showed Ysolde my confirmation, she went very still, the way she went still before drawing steel.

"Who was the thirteenth?" she asked.

"That," I said, "is what I intend to find out."

* * *

What I found, over three years of digging through archives that the Guild of Rare Exchanges did not yet own but already coveted, was this:

There had been a thirteenth speaker. Her name had been struck from every record within a decade of the founding. She had been one of the original twelve's own — a woman named Sereth, according to a single surviving fragment in a merchant's private collection — and she had argued, at the founding itself, against the form the Oath took.

Because the Oath, as written, was compulsion.

Read it plainly, without reverence, and that is what it says. The covenant bound both realms — mortal and fae — to terms neither had freely accepted. It held the border shut with force. It compelled the fae to keep their bargains and compelled mortals to honor theirs, and it worked, and it was called peace, and underneath it both worlds chafed like prisoners sharing a wall.

Sereth had wanted consent instead. A covenant sworn by choice, renewable by choice, breakable by choice — messier, weaker in the short term, honest. The other twelve had called her naive. Then they had called her dangerous. Then, when she tried to speak her counter-oath at the founding ceremony itself, they had done something to her that the records refuse to describe, striking her name and her voice and finally her existence from the record, folding her shard into the other twelve, and swearing themselves to silence.

Twelve voices performing the work of thirteen. A lie sung in harmony for three hundred years.

I brought all of this to Ysolde on a night in late autumn, rain hammering the scriptorium windows, and I laid my pages before her like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"They erased her," I said. "The whole foundation is built on an erasure. The Oath isn't sacred, Ys. It's a crime scene."

I expected her to be horrified. I expected grief, or denial, or the long slow anger she was famous for.

Instead she smiled — a wide, bright, terrible smile, the smile she wore going into battle — and said:

"Then we finish what Sereth started."

And I want you to understand, Tamsin, that in that moment I loved her more than I have ever loved anything, and that love is exactly what damned us. Because I should have said *wait*. I should have said *let us think*. I was the careful one; caution was my whole craft; the ledgers were mine. But she looked at me with that smile and thirty years of sworn blood rose up in me, and I heard myself say:

"How do we start?"

* * *

For six years we worked in secret — she the blade, I the quill, gathering proof, gathering allies, gathering the fragments of Sereth's forbidden counter-oath from the corners of two worlds.

And for six years, the schism grew between us and the other eleven.

It did not begin as hatred. Maren Hale listened to our evidence with tears in her eyes and said, "I have wondered for sixty years why the binding aches." Three of the others wavered. But the rest — the rest had spent their lives maintaining the Oath, spending their bodies and years on its upkeep, burying friends killed by the border-leaks, and they could not bear the thought that the work was rotten at the root. To admit the covenant was a crime would mean their sacrifices had been offerings to a lie.

People do not forgive you for that. People do not forgive you for making their suffering meaningless.

The arguments grew sharper. Then political. Then, in the covenant-house halls, cold. Ysolde spoke openly now — she had never learned subtlety, it was her flaw and her glory — and she stood before the eleven and said the words that broke us:

"The Oath must be broken so a truer one can be sworn. Not mended. *Replaced.*"

And Maren Hale, oldest and wisest and most afraid, answered with the words that broke her:

"If you unmake the binding, sister-speaker, you unmake the only wall between the realms. You will loose the fae on the mortal world and the mortal world on the fae, and the war that follows will drown both. A flawed peace is still peace."

"A prison is not peace," Ysolde said.

"It is the only peace anyone alive has ever known."

"And that," my sister said, quiet as a drawn breath, "is the saddest sentence I have ever heard spoken aloud."

* * *

Here is what I have never told anyone, ghost or grave: I saw the collision coming, and I did nothing to stop it.

I could feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound — the eleven hardening, Ysolde accelerating, the middle ground eroding month by month. I was the archivist; I read the pattern in the marginalia of a decade. And I said nothing, because saying something meant choosing, and choosing meant losing one of the two things I loved: my sister, or the order I had given my life to preserving.

Cowardice wears very good handwriting, Tamsin. Remember that about me.

The end came at the Standing Stones on the night of the long dark, one hundred years ago this winter. Ysolde had decided — without telling the eleven, without telling *me* until the last hour — that she would speak Sereth's counter-oath at the seam's heart herself, unmaking the old covenant and opening the space for a new one sworn by free voices. She believed, with her whole incandescent heart, that once the old compulsion fell away, both realms would rise to meet each other honestly.

She believed the world was better than it was.

The eleven came to stop her. Of course they did. And I came too — I tell myself it was to stand with her, but the truth is I came because I could not let her do it alone and could not bear to watch her do it at all, and I stood in the rain between my sister and the people we had trained beside for thirty years, holding the ledger that contained everything, and I begged her.

"Ys. Don't. Not tonight, not like this, not without them — wait, build consensus, give me five years to find another way—"

"We've waited three hundred years, Ves. Everyone who ever waited is dead."

"Then *be patient with the living!*"

And she looked at me — rain in her hair, sword bare, the seam-light of the stones turning her face silver — and she said the sentence I have carried through a century of being dead:

"You too? You of all people? I thought your ink was braver than that."

It was not cruelty. It was worse; it was disappointment, from her, which had always been the only weapon that ever truly wounded me.

And the eleven advanced, and she raised her blade, and I — gods forgive me, I will say it plainly because a confession half-spoken binds no one free — I stepped back. Out of the line. Toward them. One step, maybe two. Enough that when Maren Hale spoke the binding-word, when the eleven spoke together the great compelling phrase of the Oath itself and turned the covenant's own power against its twelfth speaker, my feet were on their side of the rain.

They did not mean to kill her. I believe that still; I need to believe it still. They meant to bind her voice, to seal her swordspeech, to stop the counter-oath in her throat. But Ysolde was already speaking — she had begun the unbinding, the words pouring out of her like a dam breaking — and when the two workings met, hers and theirs, the meeting-place tore.

You know what tore. Everything. The covenant cracked down its spine. The shards scattered. The border began to bleed. Eleven speakers died in the backlash or in the years of chaos after, and the twelfth —

The twelfth they erased. Her name, her deeds, her grave's location. Not out of hatred, I think, but out of the same instinct that made the founders strike Sereth: fear that the truth would grow if it had a name to grow from.

And I — I who stepped aside, I who held the ledger, I who alone survived with the whole record in my hands — I helped them do it. They asked for my help and I gave it, because she had looked at me with disappointment and I could not carry that look and her name at the same time.

I struck the smudge myself. My hand. My ink. My sister.

* * *

Do you understand now why I bound myself into the prophecy-book?

Not to haunt you. Not to steer you. I died within the decade — the border-bleed took me slowly, as it took so many recorders and healers who worked too close to the seams — and at the threshold between living and otherwise I was offered what ghosts are always offered: dissolution, or purpose. And I chose purpose, because dissolution sounded like mercy and I had not earned mercy.

I wrote the prophecy backward from my guilt. I found the thread of Eastwick blood — yes, Ysolde had a daughter, conceived in Greyharrow with a bookbinder she adored, a child raised on stories of a mother who traveled — and I followed it forward a century to a night when the stars would stand in the same configuration as the night of the breaking, when a descendant would stand in a shop full of impossible things, and I wrote his name into the oldest ledger I could reach.

I wrote *Tamsin Eastwick* where my sister's name had been smudged.

I told myself it was redemption. Perhaps it was. But hear the rest of it, Tamsin, because this is the part that matters, the part I will spend whatever substance I have left to make you hear:

Ysolde was right that the Oath was compulsion. And Maren was right that breaking it carelessly would drown both worlds. They were both right, and they destroyed each other rather than hear it, and the reason they destroyed each other is that neither one had someone brave enough to stand in the rain and refuse to choose — someone who would say *you are both half-correct and I will not move until you finish the sentence together.*

I was supposed to be that person. I moved instead.

So when I guide you, when I push you, when I withhold some truth because I judge the hour unripe — weigh me carefully. Love me, if you can, the way I loved her: completely and with open eyes. Because I have been a coward once at the Standing Stones, and a traitor once with the ink, and I will not be either again.

Tomorrow I will take you to the unmarked grave on the moor above Aldermere, where the villagers buried her in secret when the eleven weren't watching — the grave whose location I alone preserved, hidden even from myself inside verses I pretended were only poetry.

Her shard is there. So is her curse.

She swore it with her final breath, and I have heard it whisper from that hilltop for a hundred years: *let any betrayer who comes seeking my bones be judged by their own worst hour.*

I am coming with you to that hilltop, Tamsin.

God help me — I am coming to be judged.
