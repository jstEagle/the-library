## 22. Ysolde's Shard

The map to Ysolde's grave was not a map at all. It was a sentence.

Vespera spoke it three times before I understood that the words themselves were the road — a string of Old Tongue syllables that made the borderland rearrange itself around them like iron filings answering a lodestone. We walked through a forest of white birches whose bark peeled back in long curls to show writing underneath, and every time Vespera repeated the sentence, the trees leaned a little further in our direction of travel, as though the land itself had been waiting a hundred years for someone to ask the way.

"Say it again," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't. Her voice was thinner each time she used it. Ghosts, I was learning, were not inexhaustible. They were candles burning at both ends, and she had been burning since the night the prophecy-book first opened under my hands.

"No," she said. "We're close enough now that it can hear us coming."

"It?"

"The curse." She did not look at me when she said it. She had not looked at me properly since the confession — since chapter by chapter, mile by mile, she had unpicked the seam of her century and shown me what was sewn inside: her sister's name, her sister's death, her own hands in both. "Ysolde wrote it with the last of her swordspeech and the last of her blood, and curses written like that don't sleep. They wait. There's a difference, and you'll feel it."

I felt it before she finished speaking.

The birch forest ended all at once, cut off along a line so straight it could only have been drawn on purpose, and beyond that line lay a meadow that should have been beautiful and was instead terrible. The grass grew silver. Not grey, not frosted — silver, each blade polished and ringing faintly in the wind, so that the whole field hummed like the inside of a struck bell. At its center stood a single hawthorn tree, black-barked, in full bloom out of season, its white flowers so densely packed they looked like snow that had decided to stay. Beneath the tree, the earth rose in a low swell no bigger than a bed.

No stone marked it. No fence, no cairn, no carved name. Just the tree, keeping watch over the person who had planted it there with her own dying.

"She didn't want to be found," I said.

"She wanted to be *found correctly*," Vespera corrected. "There's a difference, and this is the second one tonight." At last she turned, and even half-dissolved, even worn to a smear of light and memory at her edges, her gaze still had weight. "Tamsin. Before we cross that line, you need to hear me plainly, because once your foot touches the silver grass, plain speech will be the first thing it takes from you."

The wind moved through the meadow and the humming changed pitch, and I would swear — then and always — that it sounded like a word being tried out in many mouths.

"Ysolde's curse does not strike betrayers down," Vespera said. "It makes them *understand* them. That was her genius and her cruelty, and they were always the same thing with her. Anyone who comes to her grave having betrayed an oath, or broken faith, or sold a trust — the curse opens them up and lets them feel exactly what their betrayal felt like from the other side. Every wound they dealt, dealt back, but not with steel. With *knowing*. You stand in the place of everyone you ever wronged, all at once, until you either go mad or go honest. Most go mad." A pause. The wind turned her edges to smoke. "Some of us are simply not permitted the mercy of madness."

"And you're going to walk in there anyway," I said. It wasn't a question. It hadn't been a question since the drowned chapel, since the tree-court, since the moment in Greyharrow's underground court when she'd first said my great-great-grandmother's name aloud and flinched as if the syllables had teeth.

"I'm going to walk in there," she agreed, "and I need you to do something much harder than walking."

"Which is?"

"Stay behind the line. Whatever you see. Whatever I become." Her voice cracked on the last word, or perhaps the wind took it; with ghosts it was hard to tell where grief ended and dissolution began. "If you step onto that grass while the curse has hold of me, it will count you as mine. My ally, my heir, my — whatever you are to me. And it will open *you*, Tamsin. Everything you've buried. Your mother's face, which you sold and cannot get back. The ledger-keeper's coldness you've carried like armor since you were a boy. All of it, all at once, from the inside of everyone you've ever failed." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing I had seen in a story already thick with sadness. "You've spent this whole quest learning to speak words that cut. Let me spend five minutes learning whether I can survive being spoken to."

* * *

I stayed behind the line.

It was the hardest obedience of my life, and I include the years I spent cataloging bottled storms without ever once uncorking one just to hear the thunder.

Vespera crossed into the silver grass alone, and the moment her insubstantial feet touched the blades, the humming stopped.

The silence that followed was worse. It was the silence of a room where someone has just said something unforgivable — the whole meadow holding its breath, the hawthorn's white blossoms going very still, the mound beneath them seeming somehow to rise a fraction of an inch, the way a sleeper's chest rises before waking.

"You know I'm here," Vespera said, and her voice carried across the field with terrible clarity, as if the air itself had been tuned to deliver it. "Of course you know. You've known for a hundred years. That's the worst part, isn't it, sister? That I couldn't even surprise you at the end."

Nothing answered. But the black hawthorn shivered, blossom against blossom, and I understood that the answer was coming, and that it would not come as words.

"I brought him," Vespera went on, and her voice began to shake, and she kept talking anyway, which is the definition of courage as far as I've ever been able to determine. "Ysolde — I brought the Eastwick boy. He has four shards. He has your stubbornness and Corvane's ledgers and none of his own history, because we — because *I* — made sure of that. I smudged your name out of the book and I smudged him out of his own line and I told myself it was protection, and it was, but it was also punishment, and I have had a hundred years to learn the difference and I learned it too late—"

The curse took her mid-sentence.

I saw it happen and I still cannot fully describe it, because it did not happen to her body — she barely had one — it happened to her *substance*. The light of her went inward, all at once, like a lamp being swallowed by its own flame. She arched — a ghost, arching, in pain that had no nerves to carry it and found others anyway — and then she was standing somewhere else, everywhere else, and I knew it because her mouth kept moving and narrating things her body could not possibly see.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, this is — this is the night. This is the night I came to her door with the Guild's writ in my hand. This is her face when she opened it. This is how small her kitchen was. She'd been making bread. She was making *bread* when I came to arrest her, and she set it aside, and she never once raised her voice—"

She was reliving it. Not remembering — the curse doesn't permit remembering, remembering is done from outside, safely, in the past tense. She was *standing in it*, in every betrayal she had ever committed, wearing the eyes of the betrayed. And Ysolde had been betrayed more thoroughly than any soul I have since heard of: by her sister, by her order, by her covenant, by her own student, by the world that used her oath-shards as currency and her grave as a secret.

"There were eleven of us," Vespera whispered, walking now, stumbling across the silver grass though her feet left no prints, chasing a ghost of her own guilt in circles around the grave. "Eleven who voted to mend the Oath and one who saw what it really was, and we called her traitor for seeing clearly. I held the writ. I read the charges. My voice. She listened to my voice read the list of her crimes and she looked at me the whole time like I was the one being hurt, and Tamsin, she was *right*, she was right about everything, the decay, the bleeding borders, the Guild fattening itself on the wound — she was right and we killed her for it and I helped dig—"

Her light guttered.

And the hawthorn bloomed harder.

I watched the flowers open — actually watched them, petal by petal, faster than any flower has a right to move — and from the heart of each white blossom came a thread of silver, and the threads wove together in the air above the grave, and they became, briefly, unbearably, a figure.

She was taller than I had imagined. She wore swordspeaker's grey gone soft with moss and decades, and her hair was the dark red that runs in my own family, the color I see every morning in my reflection and had never once thought to wonder about. Her face —

Her face was my face, forty years older, worn by a life I had never lived.

*Tamsin,* said Ysolde Eastwick, and her voice was the meadow's hum given shape, and hearing it felt like reading a ledger entry in my own handwriting that I had no memory of making. *So the book finally found you.*

I stepped forward before I remembered I wasn't supposed to. My boot touched the silver grass.

The curse hit me like cold water finding every seam in a coat.

Not pain. *Knowing.* I was suddenly standing in the guildhall at fourteen, watching old Master Hedwin take credit for my first flawless audit while the other apprentices laughed, except now I was also Hedwin, feeling the small cowardly relief of stolen praise, and also the apprentices, tasting the sour pleasure of excluding someone. I was my mother, in a memory I no longer owned, feeling what it had cost her to smile at a son too careful to hug her back. I was every debtor whose ruin I had recorded in neat columns without once wondering what the numbers did to the people between them. A hundred small betrayals, none of them worthy of the name, all of them suddenly enormous, all of them mine, all of them *felt from the far side*—

"Tamsin." Vespera's hand closed on my arm — solid, warm, impossibly solid, the curse spending its power to make her real enough to hold me. "Breathe. It's testing you, not condemning you. There's a difference."

"There it is again," I managed, "that difference—"

"Her favorite phrase." Vespera was crying, or doing whatever light does when it grieves. "She used to say it about everything. Look at me. *Look at me.*"

I looked. Behind her shoulder, the figure of Ysolde was fading, spending itself, and I understood with a lurch that she had only ever had this much — a hundred years of saved bloom, one appearance, one message, and the message was mostly delivered.

But not entirely.

The last threads of silver gathered above the mound and pressed downward, into the earth, and the ground opened — gently, the way a hand opens — and revealed a hollow lined with woven rushes, and in the hollow lay a sword-hilt wrapped in oiled cloth gone stiff with age.

No blade. Just the hilt, and set into the pommel, glowing with a patient amber light, the fifth shard.

*Take it,* said Ysolde's voice, everywhere and nowhere, already thinning. *But know what it is, child. It is the word that names. When you speak it, you will not cut anything. You will say what things truly are — and be bound by what you say. My betrayers feared it most of all the twelve words. I leave it to you because you are the only one left who might use it honestly.*

"Ysolde—" Vespera started forward. "*Please.*"

The fading figure turned. For a moment, sister faced sister across a century and a grave, and the curse hung suspended between them like a held note.

*I know,* Ysolde said softly. *That's why it hurts. If you hadn't loved me, it would just be cruelty, and cruelty fades.* The light of her smiled, and it was Vespera's smile, and mine. *Mend what I couldn't, little sister. Both realms. Or nothing.*

Then she was blossoms again, falling, and the hawthorn stood ordinary and silent, and the silver grass dimmed to mere silver, and the wind resumed as though a hundred years of waiting had been only an afternoon.

Vespera knelt at the edge of the open grave for a long time. When she finally reached in and lifted out the wrapped hilt, her hands passed through it twice before the third attempt took — as if the shard were deciding whether she deserved to touch even the wrapping.

She carried it to me. She held it out. And here is the thing I will remember when I have forgotten everything else this quest cost me: her hands were shaking, and she did not pretend otherwise.

"He named you heir," she said. "In front of me. On purpose."

"He named *an* heir," I said quietly. "Heir to what, Vespera? Five words of a twelve-word oath? Half a covenant?" I thought of Corvane's smiling offer in Greyharrow, the easy peace he'd peddled to the crowds: seal the border, trap the fae, call it safety. Four shards had already been enough for men to build that argument on. Now I held five. "Your sister gave me the word that names things truly. So let me use it once, right now, on myself, before I'm tempted to use it on anything smaller."

I unwrapped the cloth. The amber light washed over my hands, and I spoke the shard-word — not shouted, just spoken, the way you'd state a fact in a ledger.

"*Eastwick.*"

The word settled over me like a coat cut perfectly to my measure. And for one blinding instant I knew myself completely: the orphanage ledger-stamp on my intake papers, the Guild's quiet purchase of a boy with the right bloodline, Corvane's long patience, the smudged page, the whole architecture of a life designed to deliver me, unknowing, to this exact hillside. I was not a keeper of ledgers who had stumbled into prophecy.

I was an entry. Written a century ago. In Vespera's hand.

The knowing hurt less than I expected. What hurt was turning to her and seeing that she already knew I knew.

"They'll have felt that," Vespera said, looking east, toward Greyharrow, toward the great seam. "A naming-word spoken over an Eastwick. Every ward the Guild owns just rang like a bell." Her form was flickering now, badly, the curse's payment still being collected. "Tamsin — how much of it did you see?"

"All of it."

"And?"

I looked down at the fifth shard in my palm, warm as a living thing, and thought about my mother's face, which I had traded away and which this word had briefly returned to me — not as memory but as *knowledge*: that she had loved me clumsily and completely, and that some prices are not paid once but forever.

"And," I said, "the Guild didn't make me. It just found me and waited. Which means whoever I choose to be is still mine to choose." I closed my fist around the shard. "Corvane wants an heir he can control. Ysolde wanted an honest one. Let's go show them both the difference."

Far off across the silvering hills, horns began to sound — Guild horns, three notes, the signal I had transcribed a thousand times in a thousand ledgers without ever asking what it summoned.

This time, I was what it summoned. And I was no longer theirs.
