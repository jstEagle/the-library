## 23. Forgiveness Is Not Forgetting

I have been dead a long time, and in that long time I have learned that the dead do not get to choose their weather.

Ghosts carry the climate of their dying with them. Mine is autumn — the particular grey-gold of an afternoon when Ysolde Eastwick turned from me on this very hill and said, *Then we are finished, sister,* and meant every word of it, and I meant mine when I answered. The borderland has kept that afternoon alive for a hundred years. I have walked through it ten thousand times, the way a tongue returns again and again to a broken tooth.

Tonight I walk it with company, which is worse.

Tamsin stands at the lip of the hollow where they buried her — buried her without a name, without a stone, because the eleven who remained agreed that a grave would be a monument, and monuments invite pilgrimage, and pilgrimages invite heresy. They dug the earth themselves. I watched them do it. I stood at the edge of the pit with my hands folded like a woman at a funeral who has already decided what she will say to the mourners afterward, and inside me there was nothing. That is what I cannot forgive myself for: not the knife-work, not even the words we spoke over her — the unmaking, the smudging, the slow erasure of her name from every ledger and memory and stone. It was the nothing. The clean, professional nothing of a woman doing her duty to the covenant.

Ysolde's final working rises around us now as we descend into the hollow, and I feel it the way the living feel a change in pressure before a storm. Tamsin feels it too; I see him shiver and press his palm flat against his sternum, where the shards he carries sit against his heart like coals banked under ash. Five of them now. Five of twelve. And the sixth —

The sixth is here, in the ground, in the dark, wrapped in the last curse my sister ever spoke.

"Tell me what to expect," Tamsin says. His voice is steady. He has learned steadiness the way soldiers learn it — by being afraid so often that fear becomes furniture.

"You don't want to know," I say.

"I've stopped wanting things," he says. "It's simpler."

I almost laugh. He has Ysolde's humor — dry as old paper, arriving a half-beat late, always slightly surprised to find itself alive. I did not tell him that. I have not told him anything about why his face keeps snagging on something behind my eyes, why I sometimes look at him and lose the thread of what I was saying. There will be time for that, or there won't. Ghosts are poor stewards of *later*.

"The curse," I say instead, "is hers, and she was the best of us with words. Better than me. She could make a sentence stand up and walk." The mist at the hollow's edge thickens as I speak, as if the land itself remembers her diction. "It isn't a trap, exactly. Traps are for strangers. This is a *testimony*. It will ask whoever comes here to account for themselves, and it will weigh the answer, and it does not accept lies. It doesn't punish lying, mind you. It simply stops listening. And a curse that stops listening—" I spread my hands. My fingers are translucent tonight, more than usual. Spending myself in Greyharrow cost me; I am thinner than I was, a candle burned down to its collar. "—leaves you alone with everything you refused to say. Forever. That's what it did to the others who came looking. The Guild sent three men over the last century, treasure-seekers, oath-mongers. Their bones are down there somewhere among the roots. Not because she killed them. Because they couldn't bear the company of their own unsaid things, and their hearts gave out."

Tamsin looks at me for a long moment. The lantern he carries paints one side of his face amber and leaves the other in the blue of the Otherworld's leaking light. "And what happens," he says carefully, "to someone who has a great many unsaid things?"

"That depends," I say, "on whether she says them."

* * *

The descent into the hollow is a descent through seasons, which is Ysolde's signature all over it. She loved the turning year the way misers love coin. First the air goes cold and green-smelling, spring-thaw and cut grass, and the roots around us put out pale tentative shoots. Then summer presses in — heavy, honeyed, the drone of bees somewhere above though there is no sky, only darkness and the lantern's small jurisdiction. Then the leaves come down. They fall upward here, spiraling past us toward whatever passes for heaven in a dead woman's grudge, and each leaf carries a fragment of light like a stained window breaking itself gently all the way home.

By the time we reach the bottom, it is winter. Of course it is. It was winter when we killed her.

The grave is not marked, but it is not hidden either. The earth lies open — has lain open, I understand suddenly, for a hundred years, waiting, refusing the pretense of rest — and in the center of the pit stands a single thorn tree, black-barked, leafless, its branches hung with strips of something white. Paper, I think at first. Then, as the lantern light reaches them, I see they are pages. Ledger pages. Hundreds of them, torn from hundreds of books, and on every page, written over and over in a hand I would know anywhere, in ink that has never faded because ink made of true words never fades:

*I was right.*

"She wrote it out," I whisper. "Every day. Every day for however long she lasted, she wrote it out and hung it on the tree." My voice does something I have not permitted it to do in a century. It breaks. "Oh, Ysolde. Oh, you stubborn, brilliant, unbearable woman. You were right, and you died alone in a hole in the ground writing *I was right* on scraps, and I helped them dig the hole, and I helped them burn your books, and I told myself it was for the realms, for peace, for the covenant, and it was none of those things, it was because you were right and I could not live in a world where you were right—"

"Vespera." Tamsin's hand passes through my shoulder, the way it always does, but the intention of the touch lands anyway. Living people don't know how much warmth survives the failure of contact. "What is happening?"

"The curse," I say. "This is the curse. Not teeth, not fire. Just—" I gesture at the tree, the pages, the open earth. "Just the truth, waiting to be picked up."

The moment I say it aloud, the tree wakes.

There is no other word for it. The black bark flushes faintly red, like embers breathing, and the hanging pages stir though there is no wind, and a voice comes out of the winter air — her voice, her actual voice, the low wry instrument of it that I have heard in my memory ten thousand times and never once gotten exactly right until now, until it is everywhere, in the roots, in the frost, in my own skull, saying:

*A speaker comes. A speaker and a shadow. Speak, shadow, if you have anything left to say. The ground is patient. The ground has heard worse.*

"I'm not a shadow," I say, and my voice sounds young, sounds twenty-six years old, sounds like the night we fought. "I'm your sister."

*My sister is dead,* the voice says, gentle and terrible. *She fell on her sword defending a lie she didn't believe. I watched her do it. I wrote it down.* A pause, and the pages rustle like a held breath released. *But something wears her shape and her guilt, and guilt is a kind of truth. So speak, ghost. Account for yourself. The ground listens once.*

Tamsin turns to me, and I can see him understanding, and I can see him wanting to spare me, and I love him for both and neither will save me.

"What do I say?" he asks me quietly.

"Nothing." I step forward, past him, to the lip of the open grave. Below, in the black soil, something glimmers — a sliver of light the color of dawn on steel, the sixth shard, cradled in the roots like a seed. "This isn't yours. She'd have hated for it to be yours. She'd have wanted—" I stop. Correct myself. "No. I don't get to decide what she'd have wanted anymore. That privilege died with my honesty."

I look at the tree. At the pages. At the hundred years of *I was right* swaying above me like a congregation.

"My name," I say, "is Vespera Lune. I was the eleventh swordspeaker of the Oath. I was the keeper of records, the one who wrote things down, and I want you to know that I knew. That's the first thing. Before anything else, you should know that I knew."

The winter holds still. Even Tamsin's breathing seems to go quiet, as if the whole hollow is leaning in.

"We were twelve," I say. "Twelve voices, twelve blades, one covenant, and it worked — gods forgive me for saying it, it *worked*, the borders held, the magic ran clean, mortals and fae traded and quarreled and married and mostly did not kill each other, and it worked because we compelled it. Because underneath all the lovely language of balance and harmony, the covenant was a chain, and we were the ones who forged the links, and every link was a word, and every word was a wound we pressed shut and called healing. Ysolde saw it first. She always saw things first — that was her gift and her affliction, seeing. She came to the rest of us, one at a time, and she said: *we built a cage and called it a garden, and someday the gardeners will be gone and the cage will still be here, and no one left alive will remember how to open it.*"

The tree's ember-light pulses. Listening.

"And she was right. And I knew she was right. I want that written down somewhere that isn't inside me, because inside me it has been rotting for a hundred years. I knew she was right, and she wasn't wrong the way the others said — she wasn't mad, she wasn't corrupted, she hadn't gone over to the fae or been seduced by chaos or any of the elegant reasons we invented for each other over wine and worry. She had simply done the arithmetic we had all refused to do, and she arrived at the only honest sum: a covenant of compulsion will end in catastrophe no matter when it ends, and the only question is whether it ends on our terms or the world's."

My voice is shaking. Ghosts shouldn't shake. We are past all that. But grief, I have learned, does not check credentials.

"So I did what keepers do. I managed the record. When she moved to break the covenant — truly break it, unmake the compulsion, set the realms free to find each other or fail on their own — I went to the others first. I carried her plans to them in confidence, sworn confidence, sister's confidence, and I told myself I was giving her a chance to be talked out of it, but that was a lie and the curse can taste it, I can feel the words going sour in the air, so let me say it plain: I betrayed her. I organized her death. I chose the eleven of us over the one of her, I chose the known cage over the unknown sky, and when she stood on this hill and drew her blade rather than be taken, I was the one who spoke the binding-word that opened her guard. Me. The keeper of records. I filed my sister under necessary losses, and then I sat with the Guild masters and we decided that a martyr was more dangerous than a traitor, and so we erased her. Name, deeds, grave, books. I burned her letters myself. I remember the smell. I have smelled it every day for a hundred years."

The pages on the tree have gone very still. The ember-light dims to a coal-red patience.

"And here is the last of it, the part I have never said, not to the dark, not to myself: she was right about the ending, too. Look at the borderlands. Look at the bleeding seams, the monsters, the blight, the hollowed knights, theGuild growing fat on the rot. Everything she prophesied has come to pass, only slower and meaner than she promised, because half-measures always are. I did not save the world. I delayed its bill, and added interest." I kneel at the grave's edge. The frost bites through nothing, because I am nothing, and yet I kneel as if I could still feel it, because some postures are prayers regardless. "I don't ask forgiveness. Forgiveness is for debts that might be paid. This one can't. I ask only to be heard, once, completely, by the only person whose hearing ever mattered to me. Ysolde Eastwick, I confess. I was wrong. Not in what I feared — in what I served. I served my own comfort and called it the covenant. And I have haunted the consequences ever since."

Silence.

The kind of silence that has weight, and temperature, and intent.

Then the tree speaks again, and the voice is different now. Not the curse-voice, not the grave-voice. Smaller. Wearier. Hers at the end of a long day, hers when the lamplight was low and she would read to me from her forbidden notebooks while I pretended to disapprove.

*You always did write beautiful confessions, Ves. Your pen was always braver than your spine.*

A sound comes out of me that I have not made since I died. It might be laughter. It might be the other thing.

*Here is what I know that you don't,* the voice continues, and the ember-light begins to move, flowing down the black trunk, pooling in the roots, gathering around the glimmering shard. *I didn't leave the shard here to punish anyone. Curses are just doors with bad manners. I left it here because I knew they would erase me, and I knew the erasure would need a witness, and I knew — I hoped — that someday someone would come who deserved to inherit the argument instead of the verdict. Someone who would stand where you're standing and hear everything and still choose. That was always the point, little brother. Little heir. Choice. The old covenant failed because we took it away from everyone, including ourselves.*

The light rises out of the roots.

It hangs in the air between the tree and me, between the tree and Tamsin, a splinter of dawn, humming with a word I almost recognize — the word that mends, the word that binds, Ysolde's word, the one she spoke at the covenant's forging when she was nineteen and believed the garden was real.

*Come and take it, ghost,* the voice says. *Take up your sister's work. Finish it. Mend what we broke, or break what we mended — either way, do it in the open, with your name attached. That's all I ever asked of anyone. Say it and mean it and sign it.*

I reach out my hands.

They are translucent as smoke. The shard drifts toward my palms — drifts, hesitates — and I feel it testing me the way she used to test me, playfully, mercilessly, pressing on the bruise to see if I'd flinch. And I don't flinch. I hold my empty hands steady and I wait to receive my penance and my purpose in a single burning piece, and it comes within a hair's breadth of my skin—

—and stops.

*No,* the voice says, and there is wonder in it, and something else, something that makes the whole hollow go taut as a bowstring. *No. That's not right. That was never right.*

The shard swings away from me like a compass needle finding its true north, and it crosses the grave, and it crosses the frost, and it comes to rest in front of Tamsin Eastwick.

He goes very pale. In the lantern light I watch him understand a half-second before the voice says it, watch the blood leave his face in stages, watch his eyes come up and find mine with a question in them that I cannot answer because I am only now asking it myself.

*Eastwick,* the tree says. *The name smells like Eastwick. Blood remembers what ledgers burn. Take it, boy. It was always going to be family. Take it, and take her too —* the ember-light flicks toward me, almost fondly, almost cruelly — *she'll be useful, and she owes you a hundred years of service she doesn't know how to count yet. And when you've taken it, ask her about the seventh shard. Ask her what she did with the word that opens. Ask her why she never told you the ledger she found had two names on tonight's date, not one.*

"Vespera," Tamsin says slowly, and his hand closes around the shard, and it sinks into his palm like a key returning to its lock, and he doesn't cry out, though I can see it costs him. "What is he talking about?"

The hollow waits. The tree waits. My sister, dead a century, waits — wearing the patience of the vindicated.

"There were two names," I say. My voice comes out thin as paper ash. "On the prophecy page. Yours was the second. The first—" I close eyes I no longer technically have. "The first was Corvane's."
