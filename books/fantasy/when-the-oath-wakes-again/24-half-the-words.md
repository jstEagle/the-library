## 24. Half the Words

The shards did not sleep quietly.

I had thought, when I gathered the first of them from that drowned chapel, that they would sit inert in their wrapping like any other rare thing I'd catalogued — a bottled storm, a stolen shadow, an egg of green glass that dreamed aloud if you held it too long. Rare things rested. Rare things waited to be sold. That was the whole grammar of my old life: everything has a price, and everything waits for one.

But the shards spoke to each other at night.

They lay in the bottom of my satchel — five of them now, wrapped in oilcloth and wool and, on Briar's advice, a lining of salt-iron thread that itched against my hip like a conscience — and past midnight I could feel them murmuring through the leather. Not words. Not yet. More like five voices humming the same song from different rooms, each slightly out of tune with the others, each waiting for a sixth voice to bring them into accord. The Word That Cuts. The Word That Binds. The Word That Opens. The Word That Lies. And Ysolde's shard, which had no name anyone would give me, only a warmth like a hand pressed flat against my sternum whenever I came near it.

Five shards. Vespera hovered above me as I sat cross-legged on the floor of our safehouse — a defunct dye-works in Greyharrow's lower terraces, all copper vats and windows glazed so thick with old pigment that the moon came through green — and she looked at the satchel the way a surgeon looks at a wound she knows how to close but not how to heal.

"Half," she said.

"Half," I agreed.

"With five, you could mend enough." Her voice was smoke given syntax; it always sounded like it was apologizing for existing. "The seam would hold. The border would close. No more hollowed knights crossing over. No more blight bleeding through into your fields."

"And no more fae crossing either." I turned the satchel's buckle under my thumb. "Briar couldn't go home. The exiles in the undercourt would be trapped on this side forever. Whatever's left of the Otherworld beyond the seal would rot behind a door we nailed shut because we got tired of holding it."

"Yes," Vespera said simply. She never dressed the truth up. It was one of the few things about her I trusted absolutely, and one of the many things about her that made her unbearable. "That is what half a covenant does. It is a wall with your name carved into the mortar. My sister understood that. She died understanding it better than anyone alive."

I didn't answer. There was nothing to answer with. Five shards hummed in my bag like a choir missing its final note, and somewhere across the city, Corvane was preparing to tell ten thousand frightened people exactly what I had just told myself.

* * *

We heard about the gathering before we understood what it was.

Greyharrow's criers went out at dawn, bells in three tones — mortal bronze, fae silver, and the cracked third bell that had rung for both realms since before anyone living remembered why. *Assembly at the Seam-Gate square,* they cried. *The Guildmaster speaks. The Guildmaster offers peace.* By midmorning the square was filling, and by the time Briar and I worked our way down through the terraces, disguised as dyers' porters with our collars high and my satchel strapped flat against my spine where it burned like a brand, the crowd had swallowed the whole of the square and was spilling down the streets toward the river like water finding its level.

I had stood in crowds before. I had catalogued them, even — *one crowd, assembled, value negligible* — back when I believed that everything could be entered in a ledger and therefore mastered. But I had never stood inside one that wanted something this badly. Want has a smell. It came off the crowd in waves, sour and sweet at once: fear of the blighted fields east of the city, fear of the things that walked out of the borderland mist, grief for the dead of the hollow knights' raids, and underneath all of it, so thick you could chew it, hope. Hope is the heaviest thing a crowd carries. Heavier than weapons. Heavier than bodies.

"They've come for an answer," Briar murmured beside me. She had woven a glamour over her ears and eyes, dulling the leaf-green of her irises to river-mud brown, but she couldn't disguise the way she moved — too fluid, too quiet, a willow pretending to be an oak. "And someone's going to give them one. Pray it isn't him."

It was him.

Corvane rose onto the seam-gate platform at noon, when the sun stood directly over the great gate and the light fell through it in a blade. The gate itself was older than the city — two pillars of fused stone, mortal granite on one face and fae glass on the other, the seam between realms running through the gap like a vein of mercury. The Guild had sealed it years ago, ostensibly for safety, and their sigil — a set of scales weighing a coin against a key — hung across it now in black-and-gold bunting, obscuring the oldest inscription in Greyharrow. I had catalogued that inscription once. *Neither realm is the door. The word between them is.*

Corvane looked well. That was the first obscenity of it. He wore dove-grey, unadorned, the modest dress of a man who wants to seem like a servant of order rather than its owner, and his voice carried without effort to every corner of the square — I knew now that this too was swordspeech, or something adjacent to it, the Guild's own debased dialect of power, words shaped to open hearts rather than doors.

"People of Greyharrow," he said. "Children of both sides of the seam. I will not waste your morning with comfort. Comfort is cheap, and you have paid enough."

The crowd made a sound like the sea deciding something.

"You have seen the blight creep westward. You have buried your dead. You have watched the borderland mist come down off the hills some nights and not gone out again until dawn. For generations the Guild has told you this decay is natural — that broken things stay broken, that the old covenant is a story, that the monsters are weather." He paused, and let the pause do his work for him. "The Guild lied. I am here today to stop lying to you."

Beside me, Briar went very still. "He's confessing," she breathed. "Why would he confess?"

"Because a confession buys more than a denial," I said, and hated how much I sounded like him when I said it.

"There is a way to mend the covenant," Corvane continued, and the crowd's hope rose like floodwater. "There are relics — shards of the original Oath, scattered when it broke. In the hands of the right speaker, they can bind the seam again. Close the border. End the blight at its source, end the raids, end the fear. Your children would grow up in a world with no mist in it. A world with walls that hold."

*In the hands of the right speaker.* I felt Vespera shift against my shoulder before I saw her — she had taken to riding there in daylight hours, thin as heat-shimmer, visible only to me and, apparently, to anyone looking for her. Her voice came cold against my ear. "He doesn't have the shards. He can't have more than fragments — the Guild hoarded scrapings, splinters, secondhand pieces. He's describing my work. Our work. He's going to steal the shape of it and sell it back to them."

"He doesn't need the shards yet," I said. "He needs permission."

And Corvane gave himself his permission, wearing the crowd's face while he did it. An old woman near the front called out — I heard her clearly, everyone did, Corvane's acoustics saw to that — *"What about the fae folk among us? What about the ones already on this side?"*

And Corvane spread his hands, sorrowful, reasonable, a man burdened with arithmetic no one should have to do alone.

"No one here will be harmed. No one within these walls will be touched. But I will not lie to you as others might: a mended border is a closed border. Those of the Otherworld already among us may stay — under the Guild's protection, registered, accounted for. But the rest—" he let his gaze travel east, toward the hills, toward the blight, and ten thousand heads turned with it like iron filings following a magnet, "—the rest will be behind the seal. Forever."

"That's not peace," Briar whispered. Her glamour had slipped; her eyes were leaf-green again and burning. "That's a cage with good lighting."

"It's what they want to hear," I said.

"It's what they *need* to hear. That's worse."

Because that was the obscenity at the heart of it, the thing I kept turning over like a coin I suspected was shaved: Corvane wasn't lying. Not really, not in any way a magistrate could charge him with. Half the words were true. Five shards *could* close the border. The blight *would* stop. The raids *would* end. Every promise he made from that platform was a promise I could keep, with the satchel on my back and the sixth shard still unfound. He had taken the truest arithmetic of my quest and priced it honestly, and the horror was not that he was wrong. The horror was that he was right, and that being right was the cruelest thing available to either of us.

I had spent weeks learning what the first swordspeakers paid. I had read their graves. I had watched a hollow knight die twice rather than serve the thing its own heart had been nailed into. And now the sum total of all that learning was being auctioned in a square, discounted, gift-wrapped in dove-grey, offered to ten thousand exhausted people as *safety* — and the worst part, the part I will carry longer than the scars, is that standing there in the press of bodies with my mother's memory already half-spent and my own name beginning to feel like something written in water, some tired cellar of me *wanted it*. Wanted to walk up those steps, put the satchel on the platform, say *he's right, I'll do it, let everyone sleep*. The easy wrong doesn't look like evil. It looks like rest.

Vespera must have seen it in my face. "Tamsin." Just my name, in that smoke-syntax, and it landed like cold water. "You're doing the thing you do with ledgers. Balancing him. Don't. Some columns don't balance. Some entries just say *no*."

"The crowd—"

"The crowd is afraid. Fear is honest. It's allowed to want the wall. *You* know what's behind the mortar." She drifted forward, half out of my shadow, a woman-shaped absence in the sunlight. "Ysolde stood in a square once too. Smaller square. Fewer people. Same speech, different mouth. And she said the thing no one wanted said, and it cost her—" she stopped. Recalibrated. "It cost her everything. But she was the twelfth speaker, and she was right, and being right nearly broke her. You'll have to decide whether it breaks you."

On the platform, Corvane had raised one hand, and the crowd hushed the way a congregation hushes.

"I ask for no oath today," he said gently. "No banners, no levies. I ask only for your assent — a show of hands, here, now — that Greyharrow stands behind a full mending of the border. Sealed. Safe. Final. Raise your hands, people of the seam, and tell the world that this city chooses peace."

Hands went up.

I want to be fair to them, even now. Even knowing everything I learned after. They were dockhands with blight-burned lungs and grandmothers who locked their doors against the mist, and a child had died on Cobbler's Row three nights before, and the mist had taken the east orchards entire that spring. When you have been drowning long enough, you will grab the first thing that floats, and you will not ask what it's made of or who else it's keeping under. Hands rose in their hundreds, then thousands — calloused hands, small hands, hands with mourning-bands, hands that had fed fae neighbors across back fences last winter and were rising anyway — and the sound they made was not a cheer. It was quieter than a cheer and much heavier. It was the sound of a city exhaling its doubt.

Briar's hand stayed down. So did mine. We stood in that forest of arms like two stones in a wheat field, and I understood suddenly, viscerally, what Ysolde must have felt — the specific loneliness of being the only person in a square who knows what the wall is built from.

Then Corvane looked directly at me.

Across the whole of that square, through ten thousand bodies, his gaze found mine with the ease of a man finding a marked page. He didn't point. He didn't name me. He smiled — a small, private, dreadful smile, the smile of a ledger-keeper closing a column — and he raised his voice just enough to carry one further sentence over the crowd, pitched so precisely that it could reach no ears but mine:

"Come and see me, Master Eastwick. Bring the inventory."

The crowd roared its assent around us, and no one else heard a word.

* * *

We did not go straight back to the dye-works. Briar pulled me down alleyways, through the tanneries' reek, along the canal path where the water ran slow and green and the seam-gate's reflection shivered on the surface like a wound deciding whether to close.

"He knew you'd be there," she said. "He counted on it."

"Of course he did. I'm the only other person in this city who can do what he's promising. His offer isn't real until I stand next to him and make it real. He doesn't want my shards." I stopped walking. The canal stank gently of indigo and old blood — tannery runoff, I told myself, tannery runoff. "He wants me to hand them over publicly. To make his mending legitimate. Or failing that, he wants the crowd to see me refuse — to watch the last swordspeaker turn his back on their safety. Either way I climb his steps."

"Then don't climb them."

"If I don't, he takes the square anyway. Ten thousand raised hands don't unraise themselves, Briar. He owns their fear now, and fear is collateral. He'll borrow against it for years." I looked at my reflection in the canal — a gaunt man in a porter's smock with shadows under his eyes deep enough to lose things in, and behind his shoulder, faint as breath on glass, a ghost with my jawline and someone else's century. "But if I do go up there, and I speak the truth — the *whole* truth, what half a covenant costs, what the first twelve paid —"

"You'll sound like a madman defending the plague."

"I might." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Gods, Vespera, I might. That's the trap, isn't it? He's offering them a wall, and I'm offering them a history lesson. Fear beats footnotes every time."

Vespera solidified a little more, which she only did when she was spending substance she could not afford, and fixed me with eyes the grey of old paper. "Then don't offer a history lesson. Offer them what he can't. He's selling a finished thing — a sealed border, done, safe, over. You have something no mender ever had before you." She paused, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "You have a choice about *how*. Ysolde wanted to break it. The first eleven wanted to restore it. Nobody, in a hundred years, has stood where you stand with five shards and asked the crowd what *they* would swear to. He's giving them a verdict. Give them a question."

Above us, on the far side of the canal, the seam-gate caught the afternoon sun, and the Guild's black-and-gold banner snapped in the wind over the oldest inscription in Greyharrow — *neither realm is the door* — hidden, as it had been hidden for years, as it would be hidden tomorrow unless someone tore the cloth away.

And beneath the wind and the water and the fading roar of the square, I felt the five shards shift in their wrappings against my spine, and hum.

Not discordantly, this time. Not the five separate rooms, five separate songs. For one clear moment they found a single note — incomplete, unresolved, a chord missing its root — and through that chord I felt something I had no ledger-category for: attention. As if the broken Oath itself, the old covenant lying shattered across two worlds, had heard ten thousand hands rise for its half-restoration, and was waiting — patiently, terribly — to learn what the rest of Greyharrow would choose, when someone finally gave them the whole of the question.

The sixth shard was still out there. And somewhere in this city, tonight, Corvane would be counting his borrowed fear like coin, certain the arithmetic was finished.

It wasn't finished. I was the last entry in that ledger, and I had not yet decided which column to write myself in.
