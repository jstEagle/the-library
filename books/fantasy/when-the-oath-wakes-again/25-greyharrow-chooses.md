## 25. Greyharrow Chooses

The underground court met where the law could not follow: beneath the tannery district, in a cellar that had once stored salt for preserving meat and now preserved something rarer — the idea that justice belonged to everyone.

Tamsin felt it before he saw it: a hush like held breath, rising up through the floorboards of the world. Briar led them down a stair cut from the seam-stone itself, each step humming faintly underfoot, as though the city's two natures argued in the rock. At the bottom, torchlight opened into a chamber that should not have fit inside Greyharrow's bones — the borderland leaking through, widening what mortals had built, until the cellar became a hall and the hall became something closer to a cave grown kind.

Benches in rough semicircles. Mortals on some, fae on others, and — this was the thing that caught in his throat — together on more than a few. A lamplighter with soot still on his knuckles sat shoulder to shoulder with a willow-thin woman whose hair moved in a wind that did not reach the room. A one-eyed ferryman. A pair of twins who were, Tamsin realized after a moment, one person split across the seam, half in each world, both halves present here because here was the only place both halves could stand at once.

Above them all, carved into the living stone, hung the court's only ornament: an old set of scales, their pans long since broken away, leaving only the beam and its chains. Weighed nothing anymore. Held everything anyway.

"They call it the Salt Court," Briar murmured. "Because salt keeps things honest, and because it used to keep bodies. Both jobs matter, down here."

Vespera drifted at Tamsin's shoulder, thinner than she had been even a week ago, her edges fraying like cloth worried between fingers. The rescue at the seam-gate had cost her. She said little now, but when she looked out over the benches her expression was one he had not seen on her before — something almost like fear.

"I've watched a hundred years of people choosing," she said quietly. "Mostly they choose wrong. Mostly they choose small."

"Then watch tonight," Briar said. "Watch them choose big."

* * *

The magistrate of the Salt Court was a former Guild archivist named Odile Fenn, which meant, Tamsin understood within minutes of her speaking, that she had once spent her days cataloging impossible things exactly as he had, until one day she had read too carefully and could no longer unsee what the ledgers were *for*. She was sixty, sharp-faced, with ink stains permanent as tattoos, and she called the court to order by striking a salt-crusted bell that made a sound like a word being unsaid.

"We are not here to be fair," Odile announced, to a ripple of uneasy laughter. "We are here to be *honest*, which is harder. Corvane has offered the city peace. Half a covenant — enough shards mended to seal the border forever. No more bleed-throughs. No more hollowed knights crossing into your streets. No more fae taking your children's shadows, if you believe the broadsheets. No more mortals logging your forests, if you believe the other broadsheets." She let the laughter die. "And in exchange, every fae born east of the seam is locked behind it. Forever. And every mortal gift that touches the Otherworld goes quiet. Forever. And the Guild becomes the lock, the key, and the doorkeeper, paid in perpetuity by both realms."

She turned. "I've heard the arguments in the lanes for six days. Tonight you make them here, where they count. Speak. I'll keep the tally."

A woman stood first, from the mortal benches — broad-shouldered, a fisherwoman by the nets still looped at her belt, hands scarred from lines that had bitten deep. Her name, when she gave it, was Maren Cobb.

"My brother went through eleven years ago," she said, without preamble, the way people speak when they have rehearsed against grief. "Wandered off the tide-flats on a night the mist came in wrong. The Guild searched a week and declared him dead. He wasn't dead. He was *taken* — pressed into service in one of the Otherworld's courts, because the covenant broke and the old protections died with it, and anything with legs can be stolen across a border that doesn't hold." Her voice did not shake. That was the terrible part. It had been sanded smooth by repetition. "Corvane's seal would mean no more Marens. I have buried my mother and my father, and I will tell you honestly: if the vote had come last year, alone, at night, I'd have taken the seal. I'd have locked the whole Otherworld out and slept like a stone."

She looked around the benches, at the fae faces, at the willow-haired woman beside whom she had apparently chosen to sit weeks ago or years ago, Tamsin couldn't tell.

"But my brother came back," Maren said. "Last spring. Walked out of the mist on the same flats, thin as wire and talking to himself, but *back*. And do you know how? A hedge-witch over the water — a fae woman I will never meet and cannot thank — spent three years weaving a finding-song to pull him home. Because the border leaked. Because things cross both ways." She spread her scarred hands. "Seal it, and the next family gets no song. Seal it, and we're safe from everything except ourselves. I say no. Not because I'm brave. Because I know exactly how much cowardice costs."

The tally-scratch of chalk. Tamsin watched Odile mark it and wondered which column.

* * *

A fae spoke next. He was old in the way old trees are old — you could not count it from his face but from his stillness, the economy of every movement, as though motion itself were a currency he had learned to hoard. His skin held a faint bark-grain. His name was Sorrel, and when he rose, several mortals leaned away despite themselves, and he waited — patiently, visibly patiently — until they leaned back.

"I am exiled," he said. "From my own court, for the crime of trading with humans during the lean years. Understand what Corvane offers me personally: the seal would strand me here permanently, cut off from everyone I loved before my exile, unable ever again to smell the rain in my own country. If I voted my wound, I would vote against him."

He folded his hands. "But hear what I have seen. When the covenant first cracked — I was young; yes, even I was young — the blight came to our western marches. You have seen it, some of you, at the edge of your farmlands: ground that grows gray grass, streams that run backward, birds that forget their songs mid-flight. That is the covenant dying. It dies on both sides. It does not die evenly — the fae suffer slower, because we are made of magic and poison takes longer in us — but slower is not *never*." His stillness broke, just slightly, just enough. "Half a mend would stop the bleeding on your side entirely and leave ours seeping. Corvane calls that peace. In my country we have an older word for a wall built on someone else's suffering, and I will not say it in mixed company, but you can find it in any ledger the Guild keeps."

A murmur ran the benches. Odile's chalk scratched.

* * *

They spoke through the night, and Tamsin sat among them and listened the way he had once listened to ledger entries: for what was true underneath the figures.

A cooper named Aldous Vay stood and said, flatly, "My daughter's fever broke when a fae midwife sang over her. I don't care about covenants. I care that the woman who saved her would be locked on the wrong side of a wall. No seal." And an old soldier, Halvard, missing two fingers and most of his patience, stood and said, "I fought the bleed-throughs at Millrow. I carried what was left of my company home in buckets. You want to talk about songs and midwives, fine — talk about them to the buckets. Seal it. Seal it tonight. Half a wall is better than no wall, and anyone who says otherwise has never buried friends by the shovelful." And the chalk scratched both ways, and neither side won, and that, Tamsin slowly understood, was the point of the place.

A child spoke. Eleven, maybe twelve, a miller's son with flour in his eyebrows, standing on a bench because otherwise no one would have seen him, and the court let him, because down here the rule was that anyone who climbed to speak was heard.

"The Guild posted a man outside our mill," the boy said. "Handing out papers. The papers say if the border stays open, monsters eat children in their beds." He paused. "Our dog sleeps in my bed. Nothing's eaten him yet." Laughter, tired and grateful, rolling down the benches. "My da says the papers are true, though. Some. Sometimes things do come over." He looked down at his own hands. "But my ma says the Guild's papers never mention that the monsters started coming *after* the Guild started breaking things. So maybe ask who lets the monsters out." He sat down to applause that Odile had to ring the bell to quiet.

Vespera leaned close to Tamsin's ear. "That boy audits better than you did," she whispered, and there was, buried under the dryness, something raw. "A hundred years I watched this city sleepwalk. Now look at them. Look at them *counting*."

* * *

It was past the deepest hour when a woman took the floor whom Tamsin recognized with a jolt — one of the freed hollow knights, the second one, the one whose chest he had opened with mercy and a spoken word. She wore plain wool now instead of rusted plate, and she moved like someone relearning that bodies could be gentle. The benches went very quiet. Everyone in Greyharrow knew what a hollow knight was. Everyone had reason to be afraid.

"I was Ser Alenna Duskwarden," she said. "Four hundred years ago, give or take the parts I don't have back. I was one of the twelve." A sound went through the room like wind through wheat. "Then I died, and the Guild dug me up, and for four centuries I was their hand — puppeted, sent through the cracks their broken covenant made, breaking things further so the breaking would justify itself. I killed people you may have loved. I remember all of it now, and that is the Guild's cruelest work: they leave the remembering intact." Her voice held steady the way Maren's had — sanded smooth by something worse than repetition.

"Corvane offers you half a covenant. Let me tell you what lives inside the half he isn't offering. The five shards he means to use — the words of binding, cutting, opening, lying, and my sister Ysolde's word of witness — those are five voices of twelve. A chorus sings true with twelve voices. With five, it screams. I have *been* a fragment of the Oath, kept alive unnaturally in dead flesh while the rest rotted. I know what partial mending sounds like from the inside. It sounds like a border screaming. Ask the blended creatures at the eastern seams — ask the poor things Corvane's early experiments have already made — whether half a covenant is peace." She looked out over them, and her eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "You are being asked to choose between a wound that bleeds and a wound that is cauterized. I understand why the cautery tempts you. But there is a third option, and it is standing in this room, and it is terrified of it, and it is going to do it anyway."

Every face turned. Tamsin felt the attention land on him like a physical weight, torch-warm, unbearable.

Alenna sat down. The silence held.

* * *

Odile Fenn struck the bell. "The tally stands near even," she said. "Which means the city has not decided. Which means—" 

"Which means it falls to the claimant."

The voice came from the stair. Corvane did not enter like a man entering a room; he entered like weather. Four Guild wardens flanked him, and behind them, filling the stairwell, the grey light of dawn and something less natural — the shimmer of a seam-gate already folding open aboveground. Tamsin's stomach dropped. *He knows where the court is. He always knew.*

"You've had your democracy," Corvane said, descending, his boots unhurried on the salt-white steps. "Admirable theater. Truly. I attended three nights of it from the gallery before you found my listeners." He reached the floor and turned, addressing the benches as though they were shareholders. "But covenants are not sworn by crowds. They are sworn by speakers. And there is precisely one speaker left." His gaze found Tamsin, and it was almost gentle, which was worst of all. "So the question was never whether Greyharrow chooses. The question was whether Eastwick does. And I have taken the liberty of beginning the mending myself — the counterfeit holds, for now, and every hour it holds, the borders tighten. By nightfall the gate above this room seals permanently. By week's end, all of them do." He smiled at Odile, at Maren, at Sorrel, at the boy with flour in his eyebrows. "Vote all you like. The vote ended when I picked up the shards."

Briar was on her feet, chains of some old binding glinting faint at her wrists — remnants, warnings. "You'll arrest half this room first."

"Half of it, certainly," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Starting with the fae. Wardens—"

And the Salt Court, democracy of the desperate, did what desperate assemblies have always done. It did not flee. It overturned the benches.

* * *

What followed was not a battle so much as a flood in reverse — the room pouring itself upward, bodies and torches and fury jamming the stairwell while the wardens waded in with irons. Tamsin lost Briar in the crush, lost Vespera, lost the floor beneath his feet, and found himself borne along in a current of strangers who had, an hour ago, been arguing with each other and were now, without a word agreed upon, fighting on the same side. Maren Cobb swung a boat-hook. The boy with flour in his eyebrows bit a warden. Sorrel simply stood in the doorway and became, briefly and terribly, the tree his grain remembered being, roots bursting through the salt-floor, and three wardens learned why you do not corner an exile with nothing left to lose.

Tamsin surfaced into the pre-dawn street to find the sky over Greyharrow already changing — the counterfeit mending visible now even to untrained eyes, a seam of hard light drawing itself across the heavens like a scar being sewn shut, and beneath it, in the east, the Guild's horns.

Odile Fenn appeared beside him, breathless, ink-stained, alive. "The tally," she gasped, pressing a scrap of salt-paper into his hand. On it, in her precise archival hand: *No seal. 312. Seal. 309.* "Three votes. Three. That's the closest thing to a mandate this city's ever given anyone." She gripped his arm. "Whatever you're going to do, swordspeaker — do it before nightfall. After that, there's nothing left to vote on."

Above them, the seam of light tightened another inch, and somewhere beyond it, Briar screamed.
