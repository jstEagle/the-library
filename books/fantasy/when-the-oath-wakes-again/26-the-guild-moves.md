## 26. The Guild Moves

Corvane had always believed that power, properly understood, was a matter of paperwork.

Swords were loud. Spells left scorch marks and witnesses. But a decree signed at the right hour, stamped with the right seal, delivered by the right trembling clerk — that changed the world quietly, the way frost changes a window. No one saw it happen. Everyone woke to find the glass patterned.

He stood at the dawn window of the Guildhall's high tower and watched Greyharrow wake beneath him, the city straddling its seam like a sleeper sprawled across two beds. On the mortal side, chimneys had begun to breathe their grey smoke. On the fae side — visible only to those who knew how to look, which he did — lanterns of living moth-light guttered out along streets that curved in directions geometry disapproved of. Between them, the seam itself ran through the city's heart like a vein of mercury under cobblestone, and at six points across that vein stood the seam-gates: arches of fused stone and iron, older than the Guild, older than Greyharrow, humming with the covenant's dying current.

By nightfall, all six would be his.

"Master Corvane." Serel, his undersecretary, hovered at the door with a ledger bound in black calfskin. She was a careful woman, Serel. Careful women lasted in the Guild. "The writs are drawn. All six districts. The magistrates have countersigned."

Of course they had. He had dined with three of them last week; he had paid for the fourth's daughter's wedding; the fifth owed him a debt so old it had accrued interest in the form of grandchildren. The sixth magistrate was honest, and so had been retired from office the previous spring with a generous pension and a cottage far from any gate.

"The timing?" he asked.

"Proclamation read at the noon bell. Gates sealed an hour before, while the markets are full — maximum witnesses."

Corvane smiled without warmth. Serel had learned well. *Seal the gates before you announce why* — that way fear arrived first and explanation second, and explanation always sounded like comfort when it came after fear. A lie told early was called deception. A truth told late was called leadership.

"And the Eastwick matter?"

"He was seen at the drowned chapel district two nights past. Then nothing. The trackers lost him near the tree-courts." Serel's mouth tightened; she disliked reporting failure even secondhand. "The fae are shielding him."

"The fae are shielding a mortal who carries four shards of the Oath and has been seen speaking with a ghost." Corvane turned from the window. "They aren't shielding him, Serel. They're auditioning him. Which is worse."

He crossed to the great table where a map of Greyharrow lay pinned beneath lead weights, and set one finger on the seam where it passed beneath the Saltgate — the largest of the six arches, and the one through which most of the city's cross-border trade flowed. Grain went east. Moth-silk and moon-iron went west. And through it all, quietly, invisibly, went the thing the Guild actually traded in: instability. Every smuggled wonder, every impossible thing Tamsin Eastwick had once catalogued in his neat little ledgers, every flare of wild magic along the fraying border — each one was a commodity, and scarcity was the Guild's oldest inventory.

A mended covenant would end that. A half-mended covenant, sealed forever, would be almost as bad — a border frozen shut, the market strangled. No. What Corvane required was what the Guild had enjoyed for a century: decay, managed like a vineyard. Pruned. Harvested. Never cured.

And for that, he needed the gates.

"Signal Warden Hesk," he said. "The Saltgate garrison rotates at the seventh bell. I want the new watch already wearing our colors when the old watch walks away."

"They'll notice, Master."

"They'll grumble, collect their severance, and discover how expensive bread has become this season." He drew the lead weight off the map's corner and let the paper curl. "Fear is not noticed, Serel. Fear is *inhabited*. Now go. Noon waits for no one, and neither does history — though history, unlike noon, can be rewritten. That is rather my specialty."

Serel bowed and withdrew. Alone, Corvane looked down once more at the sleeping city, and allowed himself, briefly, the luxury of honesty.

Eastwick should have taken the offer. The boy had been given wealth, safety, erasure — a clean slate, the rarest commodity in any realm — and he had chosen instead to bleed in ditches with fae outcasts and dead women's ghosts. Sentiment. Corvane had seen it a thousand times in a hundred agents: the moment a tool decided it had opinions. It never ended well for the tool.

But there was more in it than that, and Corvane was too honest a bookkeeper — even of his own soul — not to record it. There was something in the boy that reminded him of Ysolde.

He dismissed the thought the way one dismisses a draft under a door: noted, sealed, forgotten. Ysolde was ash and a smudged name. Whatever she had been, her blood had come back around a century later wearing a clerk's ink-stained fingers, and blood, Corvane had found, could be redirected like any other current. You simply built the channel deep enough.

At noon, the bells rang, and the Guild moved.

* * *

Briar did not run. That was the first thing the arrest reports agreed on, and the first thing that unsettled Corvane when he read them.

The warden's account described it precisely: the fae woman had been eating breakfast at a stall in the Mothmarket — on the fae side, but barely; the stall straddled the seam like everything in Greyharrow worth having — when twelve wardens in Guild colors closed the street. Ordinary procedure would have scattered the crowd and produced either a chase or a corpse. Instead, Briar had finished her tea, set down the cup, folded her hands, and said, loud enough for the whole market to hear:

"I am arrested under whose law? Yours ends at the cobbles. Mine ended a century ago. Show me the law that owns this ground, and I'll walk to your cell myself."

And the crowd — mortals and fae together, shopkeepers and smugglers, the whole braided population of the Mothmarket — had gone very quiet, and then very still, in the way of people deciding simultaneously whether they were spectators or witnesses.

The warden had produced the writ. Briar had read it upside down, which Corvane noted with a flicker of professional respect, corrected a clerical error in the date aloud, and then walked to the wagon between her escorts as though inspecting their accommodations.

No struggle. No curse flung. No appeal to the gathering crowd — or none beyond the question itself, which had done its work better than any spell. By evening, Corvane's informants reported, the question was being repeated in taverns on both sides of the seam. *Whose law owns this ground?* It had the sound of a thing that would grow if left unwatered.

Which was, of course, exactly why she had been arrested. Not because Briar mattered — a debt-bound outcast, exiled from her own court, useful to Eastwick but replaceable — but because arrests were sermons. Every prisoner was a text, and the crowd was the congregation, and the lesson Corvane wished preached was simple: *the Guild's hand reaches everywhere, and resistance is a location we will visit.*

Except the sermon had misfired. She had made dignity contagious.

Corvane sat in his study above the Saltgate — he had taken rooms there personally, because a commander who administered from a tower was a rumor, and a commander who administered from the gate was a wall — and read the reports again by lamplight. Below him, the seized gate hummed. His engineers had wrapped the ancient arch in iron bands etched with suppression script, choking the covenant-current to a trickle. Travelers still crossed, but now they crossed under Guild seal, paying Guild tolls, answering Guild questions. Four more gates would fall to the same treatment within the week.

It was working. Everything was working. That was the trouble with machinery — when it ran true, you stopped hearing it, and it was precisely then that a loose gear could bring the whole works to screaming halt.

"Briar," he said aloud, tasting the name. The prisoner waited in the salt cells beneath the gate, in a room designed for fae guests: walls of packed salt, iron filigree in the mortar, no shadows deep enough to hide a whisper of glamour. Interrogation would begin tomorrow. He intended to conduct it himself, which surprised even him. He told himself it was efficiency.

He told himself a great many things. He was a very convincing audience.

There came a knock — Serel again, pale in the lamplight, carrying a broadsheet fresh from the printing quarter. "Master. This is on every corner from the Saltgate to the tanners' row."

Corvane took it. The Proclamation of the Oath-Breaker, in letters tall enough to read from horseback:

*LET IT BE KNOWN. TAM SIN EASTWICK, LEDGER-KEEPER OF THE GUILD OF RARE EXCHANGES, HAS STOLEN FROM HIS GUILD, CONSORTED WITH THE UNQUIET DEAD, AND STOLEN THE SHARDS OF THE OLD OATH TO WORK HIS OWN ENDS. FOR THESE CRIMES HE IS NAMED OATH-BREAKER, OUTLAWED IN BOTH REALMS. REWARD: FIVE HUNDRED CROWNS, OR ITS WEIGHT IN MOON-IRON. SURRENDER HIM ALIVE.*

"It's a good lie," Serel said hesitantly. "Most of it's even true."

"That is what makes it good." He smoothed the broadsheet flat. "Note what's absent, Serel. No mention of the covenant. No mention of mending. The public doesn't want a theology lesson — they want a thief. Give them a thief and they'll hang him before they ask what was stolen." He tapped the reward line. "Five hundred crowns is deliberately excessive. Half the city will decide no one can be trusted at that price. Neighbors will lock their doors against each other. That's not a flaw in the proclamation. That's the proclamation."

She studied him. "And if he surrenders?"

"Then we try him publicly, beautifully, over three days, and hang him on the fourth with full honors, and the shards pass into Guild custody as evidence." He said it the way he recorded inventory: weight, measure, condition. "And if he doesn't surrender, the city delivers him piecemeal — an informer here, a betrayed hiding place there — until there's nowhere left in two worlds where a man can stand without someone counting the crowns his capture is worth."

Serel lingered. He knew the look. She had been his undersecretary eleven years; she knew the difference between a plan and a performance, and she was trying to determine which she was watching.

"You think I enjoy this," Corvane said.

"No, Master."

"No. I don't. Enjoyment is for amateurs." He rolled the broadsheet and handed it back. "I grew up on the wrong side of a border that ate people, Serel. I watched the old covenant fray and I watched what came through the gaps — I've told you. Chaos is not romantic from underneath it. Someone must hold the machine. Someone must decide, coldly, so that no one else has to decide hotly, in terror, in mobs. That is the entire moral architecture of the Guild, and it is not hypocrisy. It is *load-bearing*."

"Yes, Master."

"You may go."

When she had gone he sat a long while with the lamp burning low, listening to the suppressed hum of the Saltgate below, feeling it through the floor like a caged animal turning in its sleep. Somewhere out in the dark city, Eastwick was moving. Gathering things. The underground courts were stirring; the magistrates reported whispers of a ragged host forming beyond the walls — fae exiles, freed knights, justices with no court to sit in. Absurd. A mob of the dispossessed against the machinery of both realms.

But machines, Corvane thought, looking at his own reflection ghosted faintly in the dark window — machines had been brought down before. Not by armies. By small stones, dropped in the right gear, by people who had nothing left to lose and therefore nothing the machine could threaten.

He would have to make sure Eastwick had something to lose after all.

His gaze drifted down to the reports on Briar. To the salt cell. To tomorrow.

*Everyone* had something to lose. One merely had to find what it was, and apply pressure with a craftsman's patience.

Below, the gate hummed. Above, Corvane began, in his precise ledger-hand, to write tomorrow's questions.

The first one was simple, and it was not really a question at all. It was a sentence he intended to have her repeat until she understood it was the only mercy on offer:

*Tell me where Eastwick sleeps, and I will let you keep your name.*
