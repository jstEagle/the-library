## 27. Prison of Salt and Iron

The first thing they took was her name.

Not with violence — Briar had expected violence. She had imagined iron and fire and the slow arithmetic of pain, and she had rehearsed her silences the way a singer rehearses scales. But when they threw her into the cell beneath the seam-gate, the first thing they did was speak.

"Briar of the Thornwood," said the interrogator, a grey man with ink-stained fingers and the patient voice of a clerk reading a bill of sale. "That is what you call yourself. It is not what you are."

She sat on the stone floor and said nothing, because she understood at once what kind of prison this was. Not one that held the body. One that held the shape of a person and pressed on it, slowly, like a press squeezing oil from seed.

They had built it well. She could feel the architecture of the place in her teeth: walls mortared with salt, floor salted too, a fine white dusting that got into everything, into the creases of her knuckles, into the small green places under her fingernails where her sap ran closest to the skin. Iron bands ran through the ceiling like ribs. And threaded through all of it — this was the Guild's craft, their particular genius — were words. Carved into the stones, painted in the mortar, whispered by the guards on rotation until the syllables wore grooves in the air itself.

*Thornless. Rootless. Nameless. Hollow.*

A fae was not flesh dreaming it was spirit; a fae was *story* wearing flesh the way a tree wears bark. Strip the story and there was nothing left to stand upright. The Guild knew this. The Guild had always known this. It was why they kept fae out of Greyharrow's courts and out of its contracts and out of its histories — not from hatred, or not only from hatred, but because an unrecorded fae was a half-faded fae, and a half-faded fae signed anything.

Briar pressed her back against the salted wall and began, silently, to recite herself.

*I am Briar. I am of the thorn-hedge that grew along the western marches before there were maps. I grew through a shepherd's crook once and made it bloom out of season, and he wept, and I have never forgotten his face. My hair is the color of autumn's last argument. My hands remember every door I ever opened for someone who was lost.*

She would do this for as long as she had to. She had watched trees survive girdling. She knew how life clung to a narrow strip of living wood and refused, refused, refused.

* * *

They came for her on the second day, or what she judged to be the second day; there was no window, and the lamp above her cell never changed its yellow indifference. The interrogator brought a chair this time, and a small table, and on the table a ledger.

Of course. A ledger. Everything in this war came back to ledgers eventually.

"Do you know what this is?" he asked.

"It appears to be a book," Briar said. Her voice sounded rougher than she liked. The salt was already working at the soft parts of her — her voice, her scent, the faint shimmer that lived in the air around her like heat off summer stone. "You people are very fond of them."

"This is your file." He opened it with something almost like tenderness. "Ninety years of entries. Sightings. Bargains witnessed. Debts owed and unpaid. You have been observed by our agents since before you had this face, madam. We know more about you than you do."

"Then you know I don't know where Tamsin Eastwick is."

"I know you were seen speaking with him in the borderlands. I know you fought beside him at the market at the world's edge. I know you owe the old covenant a debt—" he turned a page delicately, "—and debts, among your kind, are not metaphors. Which means you will protect him. Which means you are lying."

Briar smiled. It cost her something, but she spent it anyway. "Among your kind, master clerk, truth is also not a metaphor, though I notice you use it rarely."

He did not smile back. He turned the ledger toward her, and she saw that the pages were not paper. They were vellum, and the vellum had a grain she recognized, and she looked away before she could identify whose skin had been asked to hold a fae's history in a Guild hand.

"Here is what happens now," he said, in his reasonable voice, his voice like water finding the level. "Each day, you will be asked where Tamsin Eastwick is hiding, who shelters him, what he intends. Each day, if you do not answer, we will take a piece of your story. Not your memories — those are mortal coin, and crude. Your *story*. The things that make you you. By the end of the week, you will be a woman sitting in a cell who cannot remember why the word *thorn* makes her heart ache. By the end of the month, you will sign whatever document we put in front of you, gladly, because you will have no self left to refuse with."

"And if I answer?"

"Then you keep yourself. All of it. You walk out of here whole." He folded his ink-stained hands. "We are not monsters, Briar of the Thornwood. We are bookkeepers. We want balance, nothing more."

*You want the covenant broken,* she thought. *You feed on the bleeding edge between worlds. Every monster that slips through, every miracle that leaks out — it passes through your ledgers first, and you take your tithe.* But she did not say it. She had learned, in ninety years of living under other people's rules, which truths were weapons and which were gifts, and she gave neither away cheaply.

"I don't know where he is," she said. "That is the truth. He didn't tell me, because he is kinder than either of us."

"Then this will be brief," said the interrogator, and rang a small brass bell, and the walls began to whisper.

* * *

The whispers were the worst of it, at first. Worse than the salt, worse than the iron, worse than the lamp that never dimmed and never brightened. The words soaked into her the way damp soaks into plaster. *Thornless. Rootless.* She felt them working at her like beetles at dead wood, patient, blind, thorough.

So she fought them the only way a fae can fight alone: she told herself louder.

In the dark between interrogations, chained to the wall by iron that burned cold rather than hot — the Guild had learned that cold iron hurts longer — she built herself a country out of memory and walked its roads all night.

She walked the Thornwood in the year of the great frost, when she had carried embers in her cupped hands for three days without letting them die, because a human child was lost in the snow and would not survive a cold hearth. She walked the riverbank where she had traded riddles with a miller's daughter for a whole summer, and lost on purpose in September, because the girl's laugh when she won was worth any wager. She walked the night of the covenant, before the breaking — she had been young then, young the way oaks are young at a hundred years, still supple — and she remembered the twelve swordspeakers standing on the seam, and how the air had *sung*, how both realms had leaned toward each other like dancers waiting for the music.

That was what the Guild wanted to unmake. Not her obedience. Her *witness*. A fae who forgot the covenant was a fae who could not testify that things had ever been otherwise, that doors had opened honestly, that two worlds had once held each other's hands without one squeezing.

"They cannot take what I give away," she murmured to the dark, to the salt, to the blind yellow lamp. "Every memory I spend tonight is a memory they will never own. I am giving myself to me. Let them audit *that*."

And she began, deliberately, recklessly, to give. She told the darkness about her mother, who had been a hedge-witch of the elder line and had taught her that a bargain freely made was the only magic that never curdled. She told the darkness about the debt she owed the old covenant — how her grandmother's grove had stood untouched through three wars because a swordspeaker had planted his blade in the earth beside it and spoken one word of protection, and how that word had cost him, she later learned, the memory of his own garden. She told the darkness about Tamsin Eastwick, not his location but his *face* — the way he held a pen like a man afraid of it, the way his eyes went distant when the shards spoke through him, the way he had looked at the blighted edge of the Otherworld and had not flinched, had not made it small, had simply *seen* it and grieved.

Let the salt drink. Let the iron listen. She would pour herself out faster than they could drain her.

But by the fourth night — or what she believed was the fourth night — she caught herself stumbling. She reached for the miller's daughter's laugh and found the summer it belonged to gone soft at the edges, like fruit past its season. She reached for her mother's lesson and could remember the *words* but not the voice that spoke them. Small losses. Almost nothing. Almost.

Almost was how it started. She knew that. Girdle a tree an inch at a time and it looks healthy until the year it doesn't leaf.

She pressed her forehead against the cold iron and laughed, quietly, because the alternative was something she refused to do in front of an audience of walls.

* * *

"You're fading," the interrogator observed on the sixth session, with clinical satisfaction. He set down his terrible ledger. "Your color. When you arrived, there was a — shine, shall we say. It's nearly gone. Do you understand how close you are? Another week and there will be no Briar of the Thornwood to interrogate. Just a woman who wandered in from somewhere and will believe anything we tell her about herself. Including, incidentally, that she remembers where Tamsin Eastwick is. We'll build the memory for you. You'll confess joyfully. And your confession will be worthless, and you'll have lost everything for nothing."

It was a good cruelty, Briar thought. Precise. It offered her a way to surrender that felt like mercy — *give us nothing true, since soon you'll have nothing true to give* — and thereby made surrender look like the only rational act.

"That is the most honest thing you've said to me," she rasped.

"I am always honest. Honesty is efficient." He leaned forward. "Where is he?"

And here was the thing — the thing she would carry out of this place if she carried nothing else, the thing she gripped now the way a drowning woman grips a root: she *still didn't know*. Tamsin had not told her. Vespera had not told her. The underground justices who spirited him through Greyharrow's attics and cellar-chapels had not told her, because they were careful people and she was a captured person, and everyone around her had done the cruelest, kindest thing possible: they had trusted her enough to leave her ignorant.

Her ignorance was her armor. They were trying to strip her story from her, and the one piece of story that mattered — the piece they actually wanted — had never been hers to lose.

So she laughed. It hurt; laughing hurt now, her throat raw with salt, her voice down to embers. But she laughed, long and low and genuinely, and the interrogator's composure cracked for just a moment, a hairline fracture of uncertainty across his clerk's face, and she treasured it.

"What," he said flatly, "is amusing?"

"You've built," she said, "the most elaborate machine in two realms — salt and iron and carved stone and ninety years of files — and its entire purpose is to extract information I do not possess. You are torturing an empty drawer, master clerk. You are burning a house down to find a guest who left before you knocked. Somewhere in your ledger, there must be an entry for wasted effort. Write it large. This page deserves it."

His jaw tightened. Then, with admirable discipline, it loosened again, and he rose, and gathered his ledger, and paused at the cell door.

"The thorn-hedge," he said, over his shoulder. "On the western marches. We cut it down in the year the covenant broke. Did you know that? Your mother's hedge. It made excellent fence posts."

The door closed. The lamp hummed. And Briar sat alone with the newest wound, the deepest one, the one they'd saved for last because they'd finally found something she couldn't laugh at.

She sat with it for a long time. Then she straightened her spine against the salted wall, closed her eyes, and went back inside herself, to the hedge, and rebuilt it — every branch, every blackthorn flower, every winter it had held the field against the wind. If they cut it in the world, it would grow here. If they erased it from her, she would plant it again tomorrow. That was the whole of fae resistance, in the end; it fit in a single sentence:

*What is truly given cannot be taken, and what is truly rooted grows back.*

* * *

On the ninth day, the lamp changed.

It was such a small thing. The yellow flickered — once, twice — and steadied at a slightly different pitch, a shade warmer, as though somewhere far above, in the administrative floors of the gate-prison, a circuit of power had been rerouted. A guard's boots in the corridor stumbled mid-step and resumed. A door slammed that should not have been open to slam.

Briar lifted her head. Nine days of salt and whispering had pared her down to wire and will, but wire knows when something vibrates along its length.

Then she heard it, faint through the stone, threading down through iron and mortar and ninety years of Guild certainty: voices. Many voices. Not the disciplined cadence of guards — the ragged, urgent, various music of ordinary people refusing. And underneath the voices, unmistakable even muffled, even distant, a sound she had heard only once before in her long life and had never forgotten:

Words becoming blades. Swordspeech, waking in the world again, ringing through the seam-gate like a bell struck in a cathedral of doors.

Briar began, slowly, painfully, to smile. Iron bit her wrists. Salt glittered in the lamplight like a field of tiny hostile stars. She was faded, half-drained, stripped of her shine, uncertain of her mother's voice and her friend's laugh.

But her name — her name she had guarded like the last coal of a fire, cupped and shielded and fed breath by breath through nine endless days — her name was hers. Briar. Of the Thornwood. Debtor to the old covenant. Friend, perhaps, of the last swordspeaker, who was at this moment tearing the Guild's fortress open above her head because he had not, after all, abandoned anyone.

She got her feet under her. The chains pulled taut. Above, the shouting grew nearer, and somewhere in it she was almost certain she could hear a voice she knew — rough with exhaustion, cracking at the edges, saying words that cut.

"Hold on," she told the empty cell, the salted walls, the listening iron. "I'm coming. Slowly, mind you — dignity demands it — but I'm coming."

And far overhead, with a shriek of tortured metal, the first iron band of her ceiling split from wall to wall.
