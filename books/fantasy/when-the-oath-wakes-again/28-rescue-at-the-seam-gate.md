## 28. Rescue at the Seam-Gate

The rain over Greyharrow tasted of iron that night, which was fitting, because everything the Guild touched turned to iron eventually — gates, laws, hearts.

Tamsin crouched on the roof of a tannery three streets from the seam-gate prison and watched the water run silver down the shingles, pooling in the gutters where it caught the light of the gate-lanterns below. The prison had been a customs house once, back when goods crossed the seam openly and no one thought twice about a fae merchant paying toll in songs or years. Now its windows were barred with cold-iron lattice, its doors banded with salt-white oak, and above the entrance hung the Guild's new seal: a closed ledger, stamped in lead.

*Oath-breaker wanted,* the broadsheets said. *Tamsin Eastwick. Do not engage. Report.*

They'd printed his face well. That stung more than the word *oath-breaker*. Someone had drawn him from memory — the set of his jaw, the way his hair fell over his left eye — and whoever it was had known him long enough to get him right.

"Stop looking at your own ugly mug," muttered Sef, the tanner's daughter who led this cell of the underground. She was small and quick and smelled permanently of lime and hide-scrapings, and she had lost two brothers to Guild magistrates before she turned nineteen. "You'll want that face later. Faces are useful for running."

"I'm not running."

"No." Sef's grin had no warmth in it at all. "That's what worries everyone."

Below them, the prison yard lay open to the rain, and in the yard stood six of the Guild's watch in oiled cloaks, pikes grounded, bored in the way only men guarding something they believed unbreakable can be bored. Behind them rose the gate-prison proper: a squat block of grey stone with one door facing the street and — Sef swore it, and her sworn word was the only currency the underground traded in — one door facing the seam itself, a hairline crack in the world that the Guild had walled up with iron and prayer and called a holding cell.

Briar was behind that wall. Briar, who had pulled him bleeding from the mud after Corvane's knights opened him shoulder to hip; Briar, who had shown him the blighted edge of the Otherworld and never once asked what he would do with what she showed him; Briar, who owed the old covenant a debt and had paid it ten times over before the Guild ever put chains on her.

"They interrogate at night," Sef said. "Salt water and iron filings, in a basin, while they ask questions. The fae call it *unmaking tea.*" Her voice didn't shake. Tamsin wished his own did less. "Third night now. Whatever they're trying to peel off her, they haven't got it yet. But there's only so much of anyone, Eastwick."

He knew. He knew better than almost anyone alive what it was to have pieces of yourself taken as payment. The shards had taught him that lesson in his mother's laughter, in the color of a door he'd grown up behind, in whole years of his boyhood gone soft and grey as wet paper. Every word of swordspeech he spoke wrote itself in his own flesh, and lately, when he caught his reflection in shop windows, he sometimes had to count his features to be sure they were all still his.

"We go in through the coal chute," Sef said, tapping the map scratched into the lead flashing beside her. "Cellars first, then the corridor, then the seam-cell. Twelve minutes if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?"

Sef looked at him the way people had started looking at him since the shards — the way you look at a blade someone else is holding. "Then you do the thing you do with words," she said, "and we all try not to die of it."

* * *

Vespera drifted beside him through the coal chute like a rumor of moonlight.

She had been fading for weeks. Ghosts were made of story and stubbornness, and hers had been spent — spent shielding him from hollowed knights, spent holding shut the door in Ysolde's grave, spent simply refusing, night after night, to thin into the general grey of dead things. Where she walked now, the rain fell through her without sound. Once she had cast a shadow when the lanterns were right. He had checked, without meaning to, and found none.

"You should leave me out of this part," she said quietly, as they dropped into the cellar dark among sacks of coal dust. Her voice was still beautiful; ghosts kept their voices longest, like singers keep their songs after their faces go. "I'm not much use anymore, Tamsin. I weigh nothing. I stop nothing."

"You talked me past the curse on Ysolde's grave."

"I talked. I've noticed that's mostly what I'm good for." A pause, and then, with the dry ghost-smile that was the last thing left of whatever woman she had been: "Well. And prophecy. But the prophecy's nearly done, isn't it? We're in the last pages now. I can feel the book getting lighter."

"Don't say that."

"Why not? It's true. And you've never once thanked me by pretending otherwise." She circled him slowly, insubstantial as breath on glass. "When this is finished — whatever this becomes — don't carry me around in you like a stone, Tamsin Eastwick. I've watched you collect griefs the way other men collect debts. Put me down when I'm done."

"Not yet," he said, because it was the only honest answer, and because somewhere under the fear he was already grieving, and grief is a coward that always arrives early.

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the watch changing shift — boots, keys, a yawn. Somewhere deeper in the building, muffled by stone and salt and iron, something that might have been singing. Fae songs carry through walls the way water carries through cloth. He couldn't make out the words. He didn't need to. Briar sang when she was hurting, and she sang the songs of home, and every note of it came through the stone like an accusation.

"Now," Sef whispered at his shoulder, and the cellar filled with the underground — eleven of them, tanners and lamplighters and a disgraced magistrate with a scarred lip, all of them armed with the tools of their trades and the particular fury of people who had finally stopped asking permission.

Tamsin put his hand to the shard-pouch at his belt — four words against the world, four words written in his dwindling self — and went up the stairs toward the sound of Briar's song.

* * *

The corridor fight lasted nine seconds and felt, afterward, like a year folded small.

It began well. Sef's people took the two watchmen at the stairhead without a sound — a garrote, a sap, the practiced violence of the desperate — and for eleven steps Tamsin believed in the twelve-minute plan. Then the lamp at the corridor's end guttered green, and he understood, a heartbeat too late, that the Guild had learned to ward its lamps, and the lamp had already begun to scream.

Not the lamp. The alarm woven through it.

Doors burst along both sides of the corridor. Watchmen boiled out in half-fastened mail. And behind them, walking through the smoke of the screaming lamp with rain still steaming off their shoulders, came the hollowed knights.

Three of them. Dead swordspeakers in rust-bright armor, puppeted by the Guild's iron collars, moving with that terrible wrongness the dead have when something else is doing their walking. Their chests glowed faintly where the fourth shard's absence had healed over wrong — Tamsin had freed that shard from one of them in another life, it seemed now, another man's chest, another man's blood. They drew their blades in perfect unison, and the sound of it rang down the corridor like the opening note of an execution.

"*Eastwick!*" Sef shouted, and shoved him sideways as the first knight cut the air where his head had been. The blade took a doorframe instead, and the doorframe — solid oak, iron-hinged — fell in two pieces, cut so cleanly the surfaces shone.

Words. He needed words. But every word cost memory, and he could feel the account of himself running low — he'd spent a summer afternoon just that morning, buying a shield-word against the magistrates' hounds, and now he couldn't remember the name of the dog he'd owned at nine years old, only that he'd loved it, only the shape of loving it with the middle scooped out.

So instead of swordspeech he did the ordinary human thing. He fought.

Steel met steel. The corridor was narrow, which favored the living; the dead fought like machines, and machines need room. Sef's lamplighter went down with a cry Tamsin would hear for the rest of his life. The disgraced magistrate killed a watchman with a magistrate's ink-knife and took a pike through the thigh and did not stop fighting. And Tamsin — Tamsin parried, and slipped, and gave ground, and gave ground, and hated how much ground there was to give, until his back met the seam-cell door and there was no ground left anywhere in the world.

The first knight raised its blade. Under the helm, where eyes should have been, was a soft grey glow, and inside the glow, briefly, horribly, he saw a flicker of something that might once have been a person — recognition, maybe, or pleading. *I know you,* the dead knight's emptiness seemed to say. *We wore the same oath.*

"Briar!" he shouted at the door behind him, hammering it with his fist. "Briar, are you there?"

Through the iron, weak but unmistakably amused: *"Took you long enough. There's a lock. It wants a word. I'd give it one of yours, but my hands are full of chain."*

One word. One more line written in his vanishing flesh. He reached for the pouch—

And Vespera stepped between him and the descending blade.

"Ghosts weigh nothing," she said. "I told you that. I lied."

She burned.

There was no gentler word for it. She flared white from the inside out, a century of hoarded story and stubbornness spent in a single breath, and where she stood the air itself went bright as noon. The knight's sword stopped — not blocked, *stopped*, as though it had struck the foundation stone of the world — and the shock of it ran backward up the dead arm and shattered the iron collar in a spray of sparks. The knight staggered. The grey light in its helm flickered, guttered, and for one instant went clear, and clear-eyed, and human.

"Oh," the knight said, in a voice like dust settling. "Oh, I remember—"

Then Vespera's fire took the rest of the corridor.

Tamsin threw his arm across his eyes and felt, rather than saw, the white rush pass over and through him — cold, impossibly, cold as deep water, though it lit the stones like a furnace. When he could see again, the two remaining knights were down, their collars slagged, their armor empty and quiet. The watchmen were fled or unconscious. The corridor smelled of ozone and old parchment, the smell of a book burning.

And Vespera stood in the middle of it, translucent no longer but *thin*, worn like cloth worn to gauze, and she was looking at her own hands with an expression he had no ghost-catalog for.

"That," she said, "was most of me."

"Vespera—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and the hand trembled, and even her trembling had gone faint. "Don't spend your gratitude on me yet, and don't spend your pity either. I'm not done. A story doesn't end just because it's short on paper." She turned to the seam-cell door, and her voice firmed the way a dying fire firms when you feed it the last chair. "The lock, Tamsin. Pay it. We don't have the time you think we have."

He paid it. One word — a word of opening, the third shard's gift, bought once from a drowned chapel at the price of his grandmother's kitchen, her bread, her hummed lullaby, all the warm particulars gone now except the fact of having lost them. The word left him and the lock heard it, and the salt-white oak sighed open like a door deciding, at last, to forgive.

Inside, ankle-deep in a basin of salt water and iron filings, wrists chained above her head to the wall, Briar lifted her head. Her skin was grey where the iron had touched it, and her eyes had gone the flat black of a winter pond, and when she saw him she smiled with cracked lips and said:

"You're late, oath-keeper. Help me down, and I'll consider forgiving you in about a hundred years."

* * *

They came out through the yard under a sky going grey toward dawn, and the rain had stopped, and somehow that was worse — the silence after rain, the city holding its breath. Sef carried her wounded on doors torn from hinges. The magistrate limped and laughed and limped. Two of the eleven had not come out at all, and Tamsin catalogued them the way he catalogued everything now, entries in the ledger behind his eyes: *lamplighter, brave; cooper's son, braver.* The Guild would not record them at all. That was what the Guild did with people. It entered them nowhere and erased them everywhere.

Vespera drifted at the column's rear, keeping pace by will alone. Twice on the walk to the safehouse she simply wasn't there for a moment — a gap in the air, a skipped beat — and then was, looking startled, as though she'd caught herself falling asleep standing up.

At the safehouse door, in the lamplight, she stopped him with a hand that passed an inch into his sleeve before she corrected it.

"Tamsin." Her voice had thinned too, gone papery at the edges, a page handled too often. "What I spent tonight — I want you to understand what it bought. Not the corridor. Not the knights. Those were cheap." She looked at him with eyes that were becoming more light than gaze. "It bought time. Yours. And I need you to promise me you won't waste the change."

"I don't understand."

"You will. Soon. Sooner than either of us wants." Something moved behind her fading face — not a smile, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman reading the last chapter of a book she wrote herself. "Go inside, Eastwick. Your fae needs binding and your people need counting, and you're no good to anyone swaying on your feet. And when you sleep tonight — if you sleep — hold on to your name. Say it to yourself. However many times it takes."

"Why my name?"

But she was already thinning into the pre-dawn grey, and the last thing he heard, faint as a song through a wall, was:

"Because Corvane has the hoard. All the shard-pieces the Guild ever stole. And he's begun to speak."

Far off, beyond the city, beyond the seam itself, the horizon flickered — once, twice — like a page turning in a very large book, or a border deciding whose it was.

And every shard in Tamsin's pouch grew warm against his hip, all four together, like hearts starting to race.
