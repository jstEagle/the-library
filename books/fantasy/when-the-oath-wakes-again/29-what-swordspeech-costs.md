## 29. What Swordspeech Costs

The fire in the safehouse guttered low around midnight, and Tamsin noticed the gap.

It was a small thing at first — a hole in the evening like a missing tooth. He had been telling Briar about the night he'd first catalogued a bottled storm for the Guild of Rare Exchanges, how the lightning had pressed its face against the glass and mouthed his name, and how he had written *one storm, minor temperment* — misspelling it even then, he remembered now, remembering too late — when he reached for the color of the ledgers' spines and found nothing there.

He knew they had been green. He knew it the way you know arithmetic: as fact, not feeling. But the green itself was gone. Somewhere between the drowned chapel and the seam-gate raid, between *the word that cuts* and *the word that opens*, someone had reached into the cabinet of his mind and taken down one small green volume, and left no dust ring where it had stood.

"Green," he said aloud, testing it.

Briar looked up from sharpening her knives on a whetstone that hummed faintly, being fae-made and opinionated. "What's green?"

"The ledgers. At the Guild. They were—" He stopped. His mouth wanted to say *green* but his memory offered him only the shape of the word, an empty coat hanging on a hook. "They were some color."

"You're pale," Briar said, which was not an answer, and was also the truest thing anyone had said to him all day.

---

He should have known. Some part of him, he realized, *had* known — had been keeping its own ledger of losses and hiding it beneath the daily arithmetic of survival. The shard-words were not spells a speaker cast and then set down. Vespera had told him once, early, in her voice like paper turning: *The Oath is not spoken from the self, Tamsin. It is spoken with it.* He had nodded the way you nod at warnings meant for other people.

Now he sat by the dying fire in a smuggler's loft above Greyharrow's tannery row and took inventory, because inventory was the one discipline they could never take from him. He was Tamsin Eastwick. He was keeper of ledgers. He had a mother who —

He had a mother who —

The thought opened onto darkness. Not emptiness — worse than emptiness, because emptiness is at least honest. This was a room with the furniture removed and the rug-marks still showing. He could see the outline of where she had been: a warmth, a smell like bread and rain, the sense of hands that had held his face. But the face itself would not resolve. When he reached for it his mind produced a smear, a portrait painted over with grey primer, and beneath the primer, faintly, the pressure of a kiss on his forehead that belonged to no one he could name.

His breath went thin. The loft tilted. Briar's knife stopped humming.

"Tamsin." She was across the room faster than a mortal should move, crouching, her amber eyes level with his. Up close she smelled of cold iron and crushed thyme. "What did you lose?"

"How do you know I lost something?"

"Because you have the look." She said it flatly, without pity, which was the only mercy he could have borne. "Fae know that look. We invented it. Someone asks you about home and you smile at a wall where a door used to be."

"My mother," he said. The words came out steady, ledger-steady, and he hated how steady they were, hated the professional calm that had carried him through every catastrophe since a book of prophecies had opened its mouth and said his name. "I can't— there's a place where she should be and it's just gone. Not faded. *Removed*. Clean edges. Like a page cut out of a bound volume."

Briar was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the tannery district breathed its chemical stink, and somewhere below a cart passed, wheels crying on wet cobblestones.

"The shards," she said finally. "How many words have you spoken? Truly spoken — not practiced, not mouthed. Spoken."

He counted. It was the kind of counting that made his stomach drop as it went. "Cut. Bind. Open. Lie. And pieces of others — fragments, half-syllables, the mending-work on the seam-gate. Dozens of small speakings. Maybe more. I stopped tracking."

"You stopped *tracking*," Briar repeated, and for the first time since he'd known her, real fear moved behind her eyes. Fear, he noted distantly, aimed at him. "You're a swordspeaker, and you stopped tracking your own expenditures."

"I've been slightly busy being hunted by dead knights."

"This isn't a joke, Eastwick." She took his hand — Briar, who touched no one uninvited — and turned it palm up. Her thumb traced a line across his skin, and where she touched, faint light rose, silver-green, like foxfire under ice. Except the light wasn't smooth. It was broken. Stitched. Scars of light running through him in jagged seams, each one exactly the width of a spoken word.

"What am I looking at?" His voice sounded far away to himself.

"You," she said. "What's left of the original pattern. Every word you spoke wrote itself into you — into this — and it had to take the ink from somewhere." She looked up. "Swordspeech doesn't come out of the air, Tamsin. There is no ambient power in a mortal. The covenant put a well in twelve bloodlines and filled it with *themselves*. The first swordspeakers didn't channel magic. They *were* the fuel. Each word burns a piece of what you are to light the syllable."

"And the shards?"

"The shards are worse. A shard isn't a battery. It's a *key* — and keys don't carry their own weight. They open you wider so the old covenant can pour through. Every time you spoke one, you let a century-dead oath write a line of itself across your living self, and the ink it wrote with was yours." She closed his fingers over the glowing scars. "I've seen this before. In the hollowed knights. This is how they started."

---

He didn't sleep. He sat by the ashes with the five shards laid out on a cloth before him — cut, bind, open, lie, and Ysolde's shard, the one that had named him heir, which hummed at a lower register than the rest, patient as a judge.

Up close, in the dark, he could hear them differently than before. Earlier — how much earlier? A week? Two? Time had gone soft at the edges too — earlier they had sung like struck glass. Now they murmured. And the murmur was familiar. Not the way a voice is familiar. The way a *room* is familiar. He leaned close, careful not to touch, and listened, and heard in the layered whisper of the shards things he knew: the exact creak of the third stair at the Guild annex. The smell of Corvane's office — beeswax and old vellum. The rhythm of his own pen moving across ledger lines, *one bottled storm, minor temperment.*

The shards weren't whispering prophecy. They were whispering *him*. Or rather: they were full of him, the way a sponge is full of water, holding everything he'd fed them — and he understood, with the slow cold turn of a lock deep inside, that the traffic ran both directions. What went into the speaking came out of the self. And what lived in the shards could be drawn back out again, or overwritten, or simply *spent*.

He tried to find his mother's face one more time, methodically, the way he'd audit an inventory. Eyes: unknown. Hair: unknown — brown? the word brown surfaced without any image attached, a label on an empty jar. Voice: gone. Name.

Her name.

He searched, and searched, and found the space where a name should be filed, and the space was clean. Swept. No dust ring. No rug-mark. Just a small neat absence shaped exactly like the most important word in his childhood.

Tamsin Eastwick sat alone in a dark room above a tannery and put both hands over his mouth so Briar wouldn't hear the sound he made.

* * *

Dawn came up the colour of tarnished pewter. Vespera arrived with it, or perhaps had been there all along — ghosts kept their own hours, and hers had grown strange since the seam-gate, since she had burned so much of herself shielding them. She was thinner these days. Translucent at the hem. When she stood near a lamp he could see the shelf behind her, and it frightened him in a way he refused to examine, because if she went out entirely he would lose the only person alive — or otherwise — who understood what he was becoming.

"You felt it," she said. Not a question.

"I lost her name, Vespera. My mother's name." He was sitting exactly where the night had left him, shards before him like a hand of terrible cards. "And I need you to tell me it's temporary. I need you to lie to me, and I will know it's a lie, and I will accept it anyway."

Vespera drifted closer. Ghost-light pooled in her like water in a cup. "You know I can't lie. It's in my binding."

"Then tell me the truth slowly."

She settled across from him, folding herself down with the care of someone whose substance might tear. For a while she said nothing, and the silence had texture — wool, ash, the particular quality of grief that has been carried a long distance.

"When Ysolde began speaking the shard-words against the Guild," Vespera said at last, "she changed. I told myself it was the war. Everyone changed in those years; the borderlands were bleeding, we were all starving on hope. But it wasn't the war." Her hands, translucent, pressed together in her lap. "She forgot our childhood games first. Then the name of the river we swam in. Then — this is the part I have never said aloud — she forgot *me*. For three days, Tamsin. She looked at her own sister with courtesy and asked my name, and I gave it to her, and something behind her eyes reached for it and came back empty. She apologized. She apologized beautifully. That was the worst part — that the forgetting didn't make her cruel. It just made her *less*."

"And then?"

"Then she swore the final word, the forbidden one, the word that ends — and whatever was left of Ysolde after that, it wasn't enough to be a person anymore. The Guild called her a traitor and erased her name from the histories. But the truth is she'd already begun erasing herself, word by word, buying power with the only coin she had." Vespera lifted her gaze, and it was unbearable, because it wasn't pity. It was recognition. "The smudge on the twelfth name in my book isn't ink, Tamsin. It's a scar. I smudged it myself, because I couldn't bear to watch her name fade twice."

The shards murmured on the cloth between them. Patient. Hungry. Full of him.

"So what do I do?" he asked. "Stop speaking? Let Corvane finish his counterfeit mending? Let the borders scream?" He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down a seam. "Every path costs. You taught me that. The market taught me that. I paid a memory for a shard once already — I thought that was the price, a single toll, paid at the gate. I didn't understand I'd opened an account."

"No," Vespera said softly. "You didn't."

"Briar says the hollowed knights started like this. Stitches of light. Gaps." He spread his own palm, and the dawn caught the silver-green scarring there, five bright seams and countless hairline fractures, a map of everywhere he had spent himself. "Is that my future? Standing in armor with nothing inside, doing the Guild's will because someone else holds my strings?"

"It is a future," Vespera said. "Not the only one." She extended a hand toward the shards without touching them — ghosts learn caution around objects that remember people. "There is another way, and it is why I have stayed past my own ending. But I cannot show it to you tonight, and you cannot unspend what you have spent. Some pages don't come back, Tamsin. What you can do is stop the bleeding."

"Tell me."

"Economy." Almost a smile, ghost-thin. "You were a ledger-keeper. Be one again. Every word you speak from this hour must be weighed like gold leaving a vault. No fragments. No flourishes. No speaking in anger — anger spends carelessly. And never—" her voice dropped, and the lamp flames leaned toward her as though listening, "—never speak the word that ends unless the world itself requires it. That word takes a whole volume. Not a page. A *volume*. Ysolde proved it."

He looked down at the shards. Five keys. Five doors he had already opened in himself, and wind blowing through him from each one, carrying off small irreplaceable things — a stair's creak, a river's name, bread and rain and a kiss on the forehead belonging to no one.

"I'm disappearing," he said. "Aren't I. Word by word. I'm becoming a hollowed knight at civilian speed."

"You are spending yourself," Vespera corrected. "Spending can be survived. Dissolving cannot. The difference is whether anything is left that chooses." She rose, drifting, and at the window the grey dawn made her nearly invisible, a woman-shaped absence with eyes. "Which is why, tomorrow — today — we go to the last place your memories might be recoverable. The shards hold everything you've spent, Tamsin. Everything. They keep accounts better than you ever did."

Hope flared in him, sudden and dangerous. "You can get them back?"

"I can get you *into* the account," Vespera said. "Whether anything comes back out — whether you come back out — depends on an answer I've been avoiding giving you since the night we met." She was fading now, dawn taking her the way it took stars. "Because the account has two names on it, and the second name was smudged for a reason."

The shards murmured. Somewhere in their layered whisper, faint and unmistakable, Tamsin heard his mother's voice — reading a list, patient and warm — and could not for the life of him remember whose voice it was.

"Hurry," Vespera whispered from the vanishing point. "Before the last page turns."
