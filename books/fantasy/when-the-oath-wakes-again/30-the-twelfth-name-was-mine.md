## 30. The Twelfth Name Was Mine

I have been dead for a hundred years, and in that time I have learned that ghosts do not forget. We only misremember the order of things. The living think a ghost is a person who cannot leave. They are wrong. A ghost is a person who cannot *arrive* — who stands forever at the threshold of a moment they refused to face, knocking on a door they themselves locked from the other side.

For a century, my door was a smudge of ink.

I knew it was there. Do not let anyone tell you a haunting is passive. I sat inside my own prophecy-book like a moth inside amber, and every time a reader's thumb passed over that ruined line — the twelfth name, the one I had blurred with my own grieving hand — something in me flinched. I told myself it was shame. Shame is a comfortable garment; it fits everyone and excuses everything. It took Tamsin Eastwick, with his ledger-keeper's patience and his terrible habit of reading things *all the way through*, to show me it was not shame at all.

It was fear. And beneath the fear, something I had not permitted myself in a hundred years of drifting: hope.

* * *

It happened in the ruin of the old scriptorium beneath Greyharrow, where the underground justices kept their archives among salt-stained shelves and candles that burned with a green fae-light that gave no warmth. Briar was standing watch at the door, her thorn-crown dimmed, her hands never far from the knife at her hip. Tamsin sat across the table from me — or rather, across the table from the book, because I had learned long ago that the living find it easier to speak to a book than to a woman made of candlelight and regret.

The book lay open to the final page of the prophecy. My page. My handwriting, though the hand that wrote it had been flesh, and the ink had been bought with coin, and the night I wrote it had smelled of tallow smoke and Ysolde's blood still under my fingernails.

"Read it again," I said. "All of it. Every word."

Tamsin looked up. He had lost things since I'd first met him — I could see the gaps now, little absences worn into him like holes in a coat. He had given memories to the shards, piece by piece, and each loss had made his face simpler and stranger, as if someone were sanding him down toward some essential grain. But his eyes were unchanged. Ledger-keeper's eyes. Eyes that could not walk past an unbalanced column.

"The last part is just a smear," he said. "It's been smeared for a hundred years. Water damage, probably."

"It was not water." My voice came out thinner than I intended. Ghosts are mostly voice; you would think we would learn to spend them well. "Read what remains. Then read the marginalia. There is a symbol beside it — three lines crossed by a fourth."

He bent over the page. I watched him the way I had watched a hundred scholars, thieves, and Guild auditors handle this book, and for the first time in all those years I wanted to snatch it back. Not to keep my secret. To spare him. And I understood, in that moment, that sparing him was its own kind of theft — that I had stolen his choice from him before he was ever born, simply by being too much a coward to write the truth plainly.

"The symbol," he said slowly. "That's a house-mark. A merchant mark — you see them on old ledgers, wax seals, that sort of thing. Three lines crossed by a fourth." His finger hovered above the ink. "Eastwick."

The word fell into the room like a stone into a well. I heard Briar go still at the door. I heard the candle-flames lean, though there was no wind underground.

"My mother used to press that mark into sealing wax," Tamsin said. "On letters. On the ledgers she kept for our village. Three lines and the cross-stroke. She said it came down from her grandmother's grandmother, and further back than that, but nobody remembered why it mattered. It was just... a mark. A thing you put on wax." He laughed — one short, disbelieving breath. "It's beside the twelfth name."

"Yes," I said.

"Tamsin," he read, tracing the smear. "T-a-m-s—" The rest was drowned in my century-old blur. He looked up at me, and his ledger-keeper's mind was already doing the arithmetic I had dreaded for so long. "Vespera. The prophecy names a swordspeaker born of the Eastwick line. The twelfth name isn't smudged because of damage. You smudged it. Why would you—"

"Because it was my sister's name," I said. "And then, a hundred years later, it was yours."

* * *

Let me tell it properly now, since I have run from it properly long enough.

Ysolde Eastwick. Twelfth of the twelve swordspeakers. Youngest, and the best of us — I say that without humility because humility would be a lie, and I have told enough lies in ink to last two eternities. She was my younger sister, born eighteen months after me in a farmhouse whose beams still bore the Eastwick mark burned into them like a blessing. When the Oath was first sworn, we were both chosen — I as keeper of the words, the one who wrote them down so the covenant could be renewed each generation, and she as the blade that spoke them.

You must understand what the Oath was, before it broke. It was not a wall between the realms. It was a *sentence*, spoken by twelve voices in unison, binding mortal realm and Otherworld into a single grammar. Compulsion, yes. The first swordspeakers bound the fae whether the fae consented or not, and bound mortals equally, and called it peace. And for six generations it held, and the holding was called justice, and no one asked what the sentence cost the ones who had to keep speaking it.

Ysolde asked.

She asked when the blight crept along the Otherworld's edge and the fae courts sent emaciated envoys who were turned away. She asked when the compulsion began to fray and the hollowed knights started appearing — dead swordspeakers puppeted by the strain of a covenant too heavy for its own corpse. She asked, finally, the question that ended everything: *what right did we have to bind two worlds by force, just because force was easier than asking?*

She decided the Oath should be broken. Not mended. Broken, deliberately, cleanly, so that something new could be sworn — something by consent. She needed all twelve voices to unmake what twelve voices had made, and eleven of us were too frightened, too comfortable, or too certain that order purchased with compulsion was better than freedom purchased with chaos. So she went alone, on a night in autumn, to the seam at Greyharrow, carrying five shards and her own name like a drawn knife.

And I helped kill her for it.

Not with a knife. With a word. The swordspeakers' art works on speech, and I was the keeper of speech. When she stood at the seam and began to speak the unbinding, I spoke against her — the counterword, the silencing, the thing we had sworn never to use on one another. She faltered. The half-spoken covenant tore her apart instead of tearing the Oath. The shards scattered. The border bled. Everything that has haunted these hundred years — the monsters, the decay, the Guild feeding fat on the wound — everything flowed from the moment my voice broke my sister's.

She died looking at me. That is the detail I have carried through death like a coal in cupped hands. Not angry. Worse than angry. *Understanding.* As if she had already forgiven me, which was the cruelest thing she could have done, because forgiveness offered before repentance is not mercy — it is a debt placed in your keeping that you can never repay.

They buried her in an unmarked grave because I could not bear for her name to be spoken. And then I took up my pen, and in the prophecy I wrote the coming of the one who would finish or undo her work — and beside it I wrote *Eastwick*, because the gift ran in the blood and would wake again somewhere down the generations, and then I dragged my thumb through the wet ink until the name itself was gone, because if I could not say her name, I could not bear to see it either.

Cowardice preserves nothing. It only pickles the pain.

* * *

"You knew," Tamsin said. He was very quiet. Around us the green candlelight seemed to have contracted, drawing in close to hear. "When you found me in the shop. When you said the prophecy named me. You knew about the mark. You knew what line I came from."

"I suspected," I said. "From the first day. Your handwriting — you hold a pen the way she held a sword, low-gripped, patient, waiting for the exact right word. And when your swordspeech woke on the old ground, the accent of it — the *grammar* of it — was hers. Power runs in families like water runs in channels. The channel was dug a long time ago, Tamsin. By people with your surname."

"So this was never chance." He stood, and his chair scraped back, and the sound was enormous in the archive. "The date in the ledger. The door folding open. All of it. I wasn't chosen. I was *bred toward* it — no, worse than bred, I was *waited for*. Like a parcel. Like an entry in a ledger marked due." His voice rose, and the shards at his belt hummed in sympathy, five small voices wanting to be words. "Did you ever consider — even once — that I might have wanted a different life? That I liked my ledgers? That I was *good* at being nobody?"

"Yes," I said. "Every day for a hundred years. That is exactly what Ysolde said to me, the last time we argued. She said she had wanted to be a farmer's wife. She had wanted ordinary rain. And the blood did not care what she wanted, and neither, in the end, did I."

He flinched as if I had struck him. Good. Let him feel it. One of us should.

Briar moved away from the door — slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal or an armed one. "The prophecy says he will finish or undo her work," the fae said. "Finish or undo. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," I agreed. "They are not. Ysolde meant to undo the Oath and swear something new. The Guild means to finish it — counterfeit it, crown Corvane arbiter of both realms, turn the covenant into a leash with a man's hand on it. And there is a third road, the one I feared most of all. Mending the old covenant whole. Sealing the border forever. Binding the fae behind a wall of compulsion and calling it peace." I looked at Tamsin, and my voice — a ghost's voice, a voice made of nothing but the refusal to stop speaking — went soft. "Or swearing a new one. In your own name. By consent. Which will cost you everything you are, because that is the price of speech here. Words written in the speaker's self."

"And you waited a hundred years to tell me this," Tamsin said, "because?"

"Because telling you felt like choosing for you. And I had done enough of that." I drifted closer, close enough that my cold should have touched him, though ghosts cast no chill except the kind that lives in sentences. "But you deserve the whole truth now, and the whole truth includes this: the prophecy does not command you. It never commanded Ysolde either. Prophecy is only memory that arrived early. What it describes is a crossroads, not a cage. Ysolde chose her road knowing it would kill her, and I chose mine knowing it would damn me, and neither of us was compelled. Whatever you do next, Tamsin Eastwick — mend, break, refuse, or burn — you will do it freely. That is the one inheritance the blood gives you that cannot be taken away."

He was silent a long time. Outside the archive, somewhere in Greyharrow's bones, bells rang the hour — or perhaps it was the seam itself groaning, these days it was hard to tell. When he spoke again, his voice had changed. Steadier. Sanded down to that essential grain.

"Her grave," he said. "The fifth shard. You know where she's buried."

"I have always known where she is buried," I said. "I put her there. And I wrote a curse over it — her curse, her dying words, which I transcribed faithfully because I was a faithful coward if nothing else. It guards the shard against betrayers." I paused, and the oldest weight I carry settled onto me like snow. "Which means, Tamsin, that I am the one who must go down to her grave and face what I did. You cannot take the shard by force or trick. It must be *released*. And it will only release to hands it judges clean — or at least, to hands that have confessed."

"And if it doesn't release?" Briar asked quietly.

"Then my sister's judgment stands a second time," I said, "and this time I will not argue with it."

Tamsin looked at the book — at the smudged name, at the mark of three lines crossed by a fourth, at the family signature pressed into prophecy the way his mother pressed it into wax. Then he looked at me, and I braced for hatred, because hatred was what I deserved and I have always been punctual in collecting my debts.

What he said instead was: "Then we go tonight. Before Corvane finishes his counterfeit. You'll show me where she lies, and you'll say her name out loud, at her own grave, for the first time in a hundred years." A pause. "Ysolde. Say it. Practice."

And oh, the name cracked something in me — hearing it in a living mouth after a century of silence, hearing it shaped almost exactly as she had shaped it herself, with that same low-gripped patience.

"Ysolde," I said. It came out ragged, a ghost's voice fraying at the edges. "Ysolde. Ysolde."

"There," Tamsin said, closing the book gently, the way he closed ledgers at the end of a long audit — with respect for what was written and resolve for what remained. "First entry repaid." He shouldered his pack, and the five shards sang faintly against his belt like struck glass. "Now show me the way to my grandmother's grandmother's grave. I want to know what she cursed, and I want to know what she saved."

We climbed out of the archive into the grey light of a city holding its breath, and I went before them, the smallest I had ever felt and the lightest — because a secret carried for a hundred years is a mountain, and set down, it is only a stone. Ahead lay the unmarked grave, the curse, and whatever judgment waited in the dark earth for the sister who betrayed her.

Behind us, far off toward the great seam, the air shuddered once — a deep, wrong note, like a word being spoken by the wrong mouth. Corvane's counterfeit was growing teeth.

We walked faster.
