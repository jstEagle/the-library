## 32. Gathering the Unlikely

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone, and it was full of people who had no business trusting me.

I stood at the head of a table that had been dragged up from somewhere — a tavern table, scarred with rings from a hundred mugs and one deep gouge I chose not to ask about — and looked out at the faces gathered around it, and felt the ledger-keeper in me reach instinctively for columns that did not exist. *Assets: uncertain. Liabilities: considerable. Total value of the enterprise: unpriceable.*

Briar sat at my right hand, her wrists still ringed with the pale bands the salt-and-iron cuffs had burned into them. She had refused every salve offered. "I want to see them heal wrong," she'd said, when I asked. "So I remember what they cost." Fae logic, or Briar logic — I had stopped distinguishing.

To my left sat Magistrate Odile Ferrant, late of Greyharrow's underground courts, her gray braid bound with copper wire and her hands folded like a woman about to conduct an audit of the universe. Behind her stood six of her justices — clerks and advocates and one former executioner who had turned in his axe along with his oath to the Guild. They carried their case-books the way soldiers carry shields.

And ranged along the far wall, filling the room with the smell of cold iron and grave-dirt, were the two knights.

Ser Aldric Vane and Ser Maren Cole. Hollowed for forty years by the Guild's puppetry, their hearts fused around stolen shards, freed three nights ago when I cut those shards loose and let them die the death they'd been denied since before I was born. Except they hadn't died. That was the part nobody had explained yet — least of all them.

"We are not what we were," Ser Aldric said. He spoke slowly, as if each word were being lifted out of deep water. His armor was rusted through in places, and beneath it he was more memory than flesh, translucent at the edges where lamplight passed through him instead of stopping. "The Guild took our names first. Then our oaths. Then everything else." He turned toward me, and there was nothing behind his visor but a faint blue glow, patient as a candle. "But you gave us back the ending we were owed. An ending is a kind of name."

"He means," said Ser Maren, whose voice was dry as autumn leaves, "that we're yours now. Don't make that face, swordspeaker. We watched you hesitate over the mercy-knife. We know what you are."

*What am I?* I almost asked it aloud. The question had been circling me for weeks, closer every day, like a hawk that hadn't yet decided to strike. Since the raid on the seam-gate, since Vespera burned most of herself to ash shielding us, since I'd begun finding holes in my own past — my mother's face gone first, then the name of the village I grew up in, then the taste of the bread she used to buy on market days — I had stopped trusting the answer.

"I'm not asking anyone here to be mine," I said instead. "That's rather the point."

Odile's eyebrows rose a fraction. In the underground courts, I had learned, a raised eyebrow from Odile Ferrant constituted an ovation.

"Then say your piece," Briar murmured, "and let them decide."

* * *

I had rehearsed this speech. I want that recorded somewhere, in some honest ledger: I had written it out three times, refined it, stripped it of anything that sounded like prophecy or destiny, because I had learned that destiny was Corvane's favorite currency and I refused to spend it. And then I stood up and none of it came out.

What came out was this:

"The Guild has spent a century teaching both realms that the covenant can only work one way. Compulsion. Chains. A border enforced by fear, administered by men who profit from every crack in it. Corvane is assembling a counterfeit binding right now — you've seen what his false mendings do. Borders screaming. Things that shouldn't exist dying in the spaces between." My voice held. I was almost surprised. "He'll offer Greyharrow peace. Half a covenant, sealed forever, the fae locked behind a wall of someone else's words. It will sound reasonable. It will sound *safe*. Safety has always been the most expensive thing the Guild sells."

I laid the shards on the table.

Five of them. Five pieces of the broken Oath, each one humming faintly against the wood, each one a word that had once helped hold two worlds together: the Word That Cuts, the Word That Binds, the Word That Opens, the Word That Lies, and Ysolde's shard, the last one, still warm from a grave that had finally forgiven its occupant. Lamplight bent strangely around them. The shadows in the corners of the room leaned inward, listening.

"These don't belong to me," I said. "They never did. Ysolde Eastwick swore the old covenant with eleven others, and it broke because it was built on compulsion — because the twelfth of them tried to refuse, and the rest killed her for it. I'm her blood. Which makes me her heir, and heirs inherit debts as often as treasures." I looked down the length of the table. "There's a sixth shard. Corvane has it, or pieces of it, and he means to speak a covenant in his own name. If he succeeds, both realms become his property. If I try to stop him alone, I fail, and then he succeeds anyway. So."

"So," Odile said, into the silence, "you're asking the lawless to make law."

"I'm asking the dispossessed to write terms."

Briar made a sound low in her throat — not quite a laugh. "Careful, keeper. That sounds dangerously like hope."

"It sounds," I said, "like a ledger with a blank page. And I've learned to trust blank pages."

* * *

They didn't answer at once. Of course they didn't. I had spent enough evenings in the underground courts to know how the dispossessed deliberate: slowly, suspiciously, testing every offered hand for hooks.

It was the fae exiles who spoke first — not with words but with arrival. They had been drifting in all evening, one and two at a time, the way snow drifts against a door: a moth-winged courier whose wings had been clipped by a Guild tax-collector; a river-wight who had been legally declared *weather* so that her home could be dammed; a hedge-witch of the Unseelie fringe with frost in her eyelashes and a warrant nailed to every post between here and the Thornwood. They came and stood along the walls, and their standing was itself a vote.

"You should know what you're asking them to fight beside," Briar said quietly, to the room. "Mortals burned the Thornwood court before most of you were born. Fae drowned Greyharrow's eastern quarter twice. There's grief on both sides of that seam, and grief makes poor bedfellows." She lifted her bandaged wrists. "I owed the old covenant a debt. I've paid it. What I owe now, I choose to owe. That's the whole difference. That's the entire argument."

The river-wight spoke then, her voice like water over stones. "The Guild declared me weather. Weather cannot own property. Weather cannot testify. Weather cannot *grieve*, in the eyes of the law." Her eyes were the green of deep pools. "If the new covenant is sworn by consent — if it asks rather than compels — then I would be asked. No one has ever asked me anything."

"Aye," rumbled the former executioner. His name was Garrick, and he kept his folded axe on his back like a penance. "I executed twelve fae and three mortals under Guild writ, and every one of the sentences was legal, which is the shame of it. I'd like, before I die, to serve a law that has to look people in the face."

One by one, then all at once — it happened the way a tide happens, no single wave deciding it — the room turned. Odile rose and pressed her signet into wax on the table's edge: the mark of the underground justices, crossed quills over a broken seal. "The courts of Greyharrow-under-the-city stand with the swordspeaker," she said formally, and then ruined the formality with a dry smile, "for whatever that's worth, which is less than it was last week and more than it will be after Corvane's criers finish with us."

"They're calling you oath-breaker already," one of the young clerks said. "Broad-sheets on every corner. You shattered the hollowed knights' binding, they say. Freed monsters into the city."

"Did you?" Odile asked mildly, though she plainly knew the answer.

"They were dying by inches for forty years and begging without mouths," I said. "Yes. I broke it. I'd break it again."

Ser Maren laughed — a terrible, wonderful sound, like a gate swinging on hinges long unoiled. "Hear that, Aldric? We're monsters now. Forty years a Guild instrument and only monstrous once free."

"There are worse company," Ser Aldric said. He knelt — the whole room flinched, because a knight kneeling sounds like a tomb closing — and laid his great gauntlet flat on the boards. "Swordspeaker. I held a shard in my chest for four decades. I remember what the old Oath felt like from inside the machinery of it. It did not feel like justice. It felt like being *used*. If you mean to swear something new — something that asks — then I will stand at your left shoulder until there is nothing left of me to stand." The blue light in his helm flickered, and I understood suddenly that it was meant as an expression, that this dead man was smiling. "And Maren takes the right, because she'll argue with you less from that side."

"I'll argue with him exactly as much," Maren said. "More, probably. Distance improves my aim."

* * *

We worked through the night, because armies are not rallied; they are *accounted for*, fed, armed, and told where to stand, and that last part is the hardest. I fell back into the only fluency I have ever truly owned. Ledgers. Columns. Names.

Odile and I built the rolls together at the scarred table while Briar moved among the fae exiles translating grievances into strategy and strategy back into grievance, which is the native language of every rebellion ever raised. Two hundred and eleven souls by dawn, though numbers lie and ours lied generously — perhaps sixty who could fight in a pitched line, another fifty who could fight in the ways that actually win battles, and the rest cooks and couriers and healers and a one-eyed tinker who claimed he could open any lock the Guild had ever forged, a claim I entered in the ledger under *probable exaggeration, strategically useful*.

Vespera hovered at the edge of the lamplight, thinner than smoke now. After the seam-gate, after she had poured most of her remaining substance between our bodies and a volley of crossbow bolts, she was translucent in daylight and barely a suggestion by morning. She had not told me what remained to her. I had not asked. Some accounts you keep in a separate book.

"You're doing it again," she said, drifting close while the others argued watch-rotations.

"Doing what?"

"Making lists to keep the world at arm's length. Your grandmother did the same thing. Ysolde wrote everything down — every debt, every favor, every small kindness anyone ever did her — as if paper could hold a life steady." Vespera's voice went soft and strange. "It couldn't. Nothing can. That's rather the discovery of being alive."

"She's not my grandmother," I said, because I had to say it to somebody. "Ysolde died childless. The bloodline came down through her brother's house. I'm — what, four generations removed? Five? A name in the margins of her life."

Vespera looked at me for a long moment, and there was something in the look I couldn't read — pity, or guilt, or the particular tenderness ghosts reserve for the living who haven't yet learned their own weight. "Blood doesn't dilute, Tamsin Eastwick. It *concentrates*. Ask the Guild; they've been trying to breed it out of existence for a hundred years and failing." She began to fade, and her last words came from everywhere and nowhere, the way ghost-voices do. "You gather strays because you know what it is to be one. That's not a weakness of leadership. That's the whole secret of it."

* * *

An hour before dawn, when the safehouse had finally gone quiet — fae sleeping upright in corners, justices snoring over their briefs, the two hollow knights standing sentinel at the door like monuments that had decided to be useful — Briar found me on the roof.

Greyharrow spread below us, the treaty-city, the city that sits on the seam. From up here you could see the line of it: mortal roofs giving way by degrees to architecture that hurt to look at directly, chimneys that smoked upward in two directions, streets that were honest cobblestone on one end and moonlight on the other. Somewhere in that middle distance, Corvane was fitting his counterfeit covenant together, and the seam glimmered faintly wrong, like a scar that itched.

"Two hundred and eleven," Briar said, settling beside me on the cold slate. "Is that enough?"

"For what?"

"That's the question, isn't it. Enough for what." She pulled her cloak tighter. Her wrist-bands caught the pre-dawn gray. "When the Guild took me, I thought I understood chains. Salt and iron taught me I hadn't. But I learned something else in that cell, keeper. Every hour they questioned me, I thought about who was coming. Not whether — *who*. And I couldn't picture anyone, and that frightened me worse than the irons." She glanced sideways at me. "Then you came through the gate with magistrates and grave-knights and a moth-girl carrying a stolen banner, and I thought: ah. So that's who I am, that people like *these* come for me."

"Briar—"

"Don't ruin it with gratitude. Gratitude is a debt and I'm retiring from debts." She stood, brushing frost off her knees. "I'm telling you why they follow you. Not the shards. Not the prophecy, whatever's left of it. They follow you because you look at the discarded things and see inventory worth keeping. It's a ledger-keeper's curse, seeing value where everyone else sees waste." A pause, and then, drier: "Also you feed people before you ask them to die for you. That helps."

Below us, in the street, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm bells — the *seam* bells, the ones Greyharrow rang only when the border itself shivered. The sound rolled up through the stones and into our bones, and every window in the district lit candle by candle, and along the middle distance where the city touched the Otherworld, I saw the air split open like a healing wound torn fresh.

Briar went very still. "That's the great seam," she said. "That's *Corvane's* fortress."

And rising above the fortress walls, visible even from this distance, hung a light that was not fire and not lightning but something older and more arrogant — the color of a covenant being spoken by the wrong mouth.

The bells kept ringing. All across the rooftops of Greyharrow, two hundred and eleven unlikely souls began to wake, and dress, and sharpen, and gather — the lawless and the dispossessed, the declared-weather and the erased, the knight who had died twice and the ghost running on fumes and the keeper of impossible ledgers with five words in his pack and holes where his childhood used to be.

"March order," I heard myself say, and my voice sounded steadier than my hands. "We move within the hour."

Briar grinned, sharp as winter. "The blank page," she said, "is writing back."
