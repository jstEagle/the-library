## 33. March on the Seams

They say the great seam runs beneath Greyharrow like a scar beneath skin, but that is a lie told by people who have never stood on it. A scar heals. The seam does not. It is a wound that was made to stay open, and the Guild built its fortress on top of it the way a tick builds itself into flesh — mouth buried deep, body fat with what it steals.

I could feel it through my bare feet as we came down out of the ash-hills in the hour before dawn. Fae are made of borders; we live in thresholds, doorways, the space between one breath and the next. And so I felt the seam the way you would feel your own name spoken by a stranger's mouth: intimately, wrongly, with a shiver that was not entirely fear.

Two hundred of us, maybe more. Fae exiles with their glamour burned away by years of hiding among mortals. Greyharrow's underground justices in their stolen magistrate coats, buttons mismatched, faces set. Two hollow knights walking at Tamsin Eastwick's shoulders like tall pale lanterns — dead swordspeakers given back their own bodies, moving now by choice instead of puppet-strings. I had not thought I would ever be glad to see such things walk, but the world has been full of surprises lately, and most of them have teeth.

And there was Tamsin himself, ahead of me on the road, wrapped in his long ledger-keeper's coat with five shards bound against his chest in a sling of grey cloth. He walked like a man carrying water across ice — every step deliberate, every breath measured, terrified of spilling something that could drown the world.

He kept glancing sideways at the empty air just over his left shoulder, where Vespera drifted, visible only when the seam-light touched her. She was thinner than when I'd first seen her. Ghosts burn what they are to stay in the world, and she had been burning for weeks.

"You're limping," I said, falling into step beside him.

"I'm favoring," he corrected. "It's different. Limping is involuntary."

"Fae wisdom: pain is information."

"Then I'm drowning in knowledge." But he smiled, briefly, and the smile did more for the two hundred souls around us than any speech could have. They saw him smile and they remembered that they were not marching toward death but toward a door, and doors open from both sides.

* * *

We stopped on the last ridge before the fortress, and I looked down at the thing we had come to break.

The Guild's stronghold sat astride the seam like a rider on a horse. It had no beauty in it at all — mortal stone and iron salted with fae-binding runes, squat towers, walls studded with the antlers and horns of things the Guild had hunted for profit. Seam-gates yawned along its base, barred gates of black iron through which the border between worlds ran like a river under a bridge, and at each gate stood Guildwardens in grey lacquer, pikes crossed, lanterns burning salt-fire that stung fae eyes at half a mile.

Beyond the wall, faint even to my sight, something pulsed. A wrong light. Corvane's counterfeit mending, already working, already warping the seam into shapes it was never meant to hold.

"Four gates," said Justiciar Hallowell beside me, an old woman who had spent thirty years dispensing justice in cellars because the law upstairs belonged to the Guild. Her voice carried the flat calm of someone who had rehearsed her own courage. "We take two. The east gate is ours — Briar leads the storming party. The north gate is a feint; Wren takes forty souls and makes enough noise to raise the dead, then withdraws."

"And if the feint becomes real?" asked one of the freed hollow knights. Its voice sounded like wind through a ribcage. Even now I could not look at them too long.

"Then it becomes real," Hallowell said, "and we win faster or lose louder. War is honest that way, at least."

Tamsin raised his hand and the ridge went quiet — two hundred people holding their breath together makes a silence you could pour into a bottle and sell at the Guild's own market. He didn't give a speech. I loved him a little for that. He only said:

"The Guild has spent a century telling both our peoples that this is impossible. That mortals and fae cannot stand together. That the border must be either wall or wound." His voice was quiet, but the seam beneath us carried it, and the seam carried it further, up through boot-soles and bone. "Every one of us here is proof they lied. Let's go be very loud about it."

The ridge emptied like a cup tipped forward, and the night drank us down.

* * *

I will tell you what no ballad will ever get right, because the ballads are all written by people who watched from windows. Battles do not feel like thunder. They feel like weather changing — a pressure drop, a taste of iron, the animal certainty that the sky is about to do something to you.

We hit the east gate at a run, and the first thing that met us was the smell: salt-fire and cold iron, the twin poisons of my kind. My eyes streamed. My skin crawled where the gate's binding-runes reached for me like grasping hands. Behind me, three young exiles — children of Otherworld courts that no longer existed except in memory — began to keen, high and thin, the sound fae make when their bodies remember being hunted.

"Don't listen to the runes," I called back to them. "They're only words. Words can't hold you unless you agree to be held."

"That's not true," one of them gasped. "Words held you. We saw the chains."

Ah. Yes. The chains.

I had been trying, very successfully, not to think about the chains.

When the Guild took me at the seam-gates — a lifetime ago now, though the calendar would call it six weeks — they had not used rope or iron alone. They had used oath-cuffs, relics of the old covenant's coercive power, forged in the first swordspeakers' age: bands of cold metal that spoke the compulsion-oaths directly into a fae's blood. Wear them long enough and you stop fighting them. Wear them long enough and your wrists forget they were ever anything else.

I wore them still. There had been no key, no forge-hot blade, nothing in Briar's prison-cell arsenal that could part them, and Tamsin had looked at them once with an expression like a man reading a debt he couldn't pay and said, *when we take the fortress, I'll find the countersign*, and I had believed him the way you believe in spring — not because you've proven it, but because the alternative is unbearable.

So I led the storming party with my hands still manacled, and I had learned, in those grey interrogation weeks, to fight anyway. You would be amazed what you can do with chained wrists once you stop treating them as broken and start treating them as a weapon. I hooked a warden's pike and dragged him off the wall. I swung my linked hands like a flail and felt iron meet lacquered helm with a sound like a bell tolling for someone else. Salt-fire scorched my shoulder; I bit through the pain and bit through the arm behind it, and fae teeth are not gentle things.

We broke the east gate in eleven minutes. Hallowell counted. She counts everything; it's how she prays.

* * *

Inside the walls, the battle stopped being geography and became music — ugly, improvised, desperate music. The underground justices fought in cellar-brawl style, low and close, using the fortress's own corridors against its garrison. The fae exiles fought like weather: mist-shapes and sudden gusts, thorn-slick shadows, a girl from the Blighted Edge whose touch made armor rot. The hollow knights simply walked forward through everything, unhurried, unkillable in the ways that matter, drawing fire and fear away from the living.

And above it all, the wrong light pulsed stronger. Through a courtyard arch I saw it clearly for the first time: the great seam-gate at the fortress heart, and Corvane's working crawling over it like frost made of script, lines of stolen covenant-language knitting shut a border that was never meant to be a wall. Where the script touched, reality blistered. I watched a hound-thing of the Otherworld — some poor blended creature caught mid-crossing when the mending seized — scream itself inside out at the gate's threshold. Its two halves could not agree on which world to die in, so it did both.

That sound. I carry it still. I expect I always will.

"Onward," I heard myself say, and my voice did not shake, which surprised me. "Onward, onward."

* * *

It went wrong, as battles do, at the inner keep.

We had driven the wardens back to the second courtyard when the Guild loosed its reserve, and the reserve was not men. It was bindings — great nets of woven covenant-script, hurled from the towers like fishing-cast, and where they fell on fae they *tightened*. I saw the thorn-shadow girl go down under one, saw the script eat her glamour in seconds, leaving her small and grey and human-vulnerable. I saw the mist-shapes condense into screaming flesh. The Guild had learned, somewhere in its century of parasitism, exactly how to make a battlefield hostile to everything I am.

A net came down over me before I saw it cast.

The script kissed my skin and my blood sang the old coercion-oaths — *serve, obey, be bound* — and my knees buckled, and the chains on my wrists, my faithful hateful companions, chose that moment to speak loudest. Cold spread up my arms. The net's weave found the oath-cuffs' frequency and harmonized with it, and suddenly I was not lying in a courtyard in a fortress at war; I was back in the prison of salt and iron, and the interrogator's voice was saying *your name is whatever we write down*, and the ceiling was close, and the dark was patient—

*Briar.*

Not aloud. Through the net, through the script, through all of it, someone had said my name like it meant something worth keeping. I thrashed toward the sound and found Tamsin's face above me, cut across the cheekbone, haloed in torch-smoke, one hand pressed flat against the net as though he could hold back the whole Guild by stubbornness alone. Around him the battle churned — Hallowell bellowing orders, a hollow knight tearing script-netting with fingers that smoked where the covenant-writing touched them, exiles and justices forming a ring of bodies around their fallen.

"The cuffs," Tamsin said. "Briar — listen to me. Vespera says the cuffs answer to the old covenant. Compulsion. They can't compel what's freely given." His eyes were wild and certain at once. "The oaths only bind the unwilling. That was always the flaw in the old Oath, and it's the flaw in everything built on it. So *want* something. Right now. Want it so loudly the script can't find the shape of you to grip."

"That's your strategy? *Want harder?*"

"It worked on me," he said, "and I'm the most stubborn man alive," and the fool actually grinned, blood on his teeth, shards glowing through the cloth at his chest like coals under ash.

The net tightened. The script poured its litany into my veins — *serve, obey, be bound, serve, obey* — and the prison-dark rose up again, salt and iron and the slow erosion of a self.

And I thought: *no.*

Not cleverly. Not strategically. No animal cunning, no court-trained trick of the fae. Just the oldest word in any language, the first word of every free creature ever born, rising in me like fire finding a draft.

No, I had not served. No, they had not written my name. No, I was not the thing the chains insisted on. I wanted — oh, I wanted so many things, I wanted them so loudly — I wanted the Blighted Edge green again. I wanted the thorn-girl breathing. I wanted tea in Hallowell's cellar-court while the old woman read out verdicts on thieves and wife-beaters and Guild informants, and everyone got exactly the justice they deserved, which is rarer than magic. I wanted Tamsin Eastwick to finish his impossible book. I wanted my own name, mine, in my own mouth, spoken in my own accent, in the language of a court that no longer existed except that I was standing in it right now, because a court is wherever its people stand—

The oath-cuffs cracked.

Not gently. Not with ceremony. They split like river ice in thaw, like a lie interrupted, and the sound went through the courtyard and through me and through, I think, the seam itself — a single clear *crack* of consent refusing compulsion, and the script-net shriveled off me like paper in flame, and I came up off the stones with my broken chains swinging from my wrists like the shed skins of a snake.

For a heartbeat nobody moved. Even the battle seemed to lean closer.

Then the thorn-girl, grey and half-eaten under her net, looked at my wrists and started to laugh — a ragged, disbelieving, glorious laugh — and shouted it to the whole courtyard in three languages: *"The chains broke! Look! The chains BREAK!"*

I have fought in four skirmishes and one proper war since, and I will tell you honestly: I have never seen anything turn a fight like hope arriving late but arriving. The exiles surged. The justices roared. A hollow knight picked up a Guild banner-pole and used it as a staff with terrible grace. The warden line, which had held for a century against everything the borderlands could throw at it, folded like wet paper, and we poured through the second courtyard toward the heart of the fortress where the wrong light burned.

* * *

We reached the inner keep's threshold together — Tamsin and I, Hallowell, the two hollow knights, Vespera a pale shimmer barely holding her shape, so thin now I could see the torchlight through her chest.

Through the great doors' iron lattice we saw the seam-gate, and Corvane's counterfeit covenant crawling across it, and the Guildmaster himself standing before his work with his arms raised and his face lit with the particular ecstasy of a man who believes he is becoming God's editor.

And we saw something else.

The gate's script was *finished*. The last line closed even as we watched, sealing with a sound like a struck bell, and the border screamed — a long, subsonic shriek that rose through our boots and our bones and set every fae tooth in my head aching — and beyond the gate, in the Otherworld, the blighted horizon began visibly to move. To *spread*. Corvane's false mending, half-made, was strangling both worlds at once, and the creatures caught at the crossing point were dying in ranks.

Tamsin's hand found the sling at his chest. Five shards, glowing through the cloth, answering the seam's scream note for note.

"There's no plan past this door," he said quietly. It was not despair. It was inventory.

"There never was," I said. "There was only onward. It's served well enough."

He looked at me — really looked, the way fae look, seeing the whole shape of a soul — and nodded once. Then he put his shoulder to the doors of the keep, and two hundred voices behind us rose like a tide, and the great gates of the Guild's fortress swung inward onto a light that was devouring the world.

Vespera's voice, thin as frost on glass, came last through the closing dark behind us:

"He knows we're here. He's known all along."
