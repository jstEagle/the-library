## 34. The Counterfeit Covenant

The seam-fortress did not look like a fortress from the inside. It looked like a counting-house that had grown ambitions — ledgers stacked to the vaulted ceiling, ink-stained clerks bent over desks of black oak, and above it all a hum like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Tamsin felt the sound in his teeth before he understood what it was. He had spent eleven years listening to the Guild's ledgers whisper their impossible arithmetic: one bottled storm, two stolen shadows, three years of someone's grief sold by the ounce. This was different. This was not bookkeeping.

This was a covenant being written badly, in real time, with the world as its page.

"Keep your hood up," Briar breathed beside him. Her chains were gone — she had torn them off at the gate in a shriek of iron and salt — but she still moved like a prisoner, low to the ground, weight on her heels, every sense aimed forward. "The air in here is wrong. Don't breathe it deeper than you have to."

Tamsin nodded and did not answer. Speaking felt dangerous now. Ever since the word-that-ends had torn through him — *shattering* Corvane's first working and taking his mother's face with it, then his own name from the front of his tongue — words had felt like coins he could not afford to spend. He kept them hoarded. When he spoke at all, he spoke small.

They moved along the clerks' gallery, between the desks, past men and women who never looked up. That was the horror of it, Tamsin thought. Not cruelty. Diligence. These people were *working*. Quills scratched. Abacuses clicked. Somewhere a clerk murmured figures under her breath like a prayer — *four hundred and six seams stabilized, ninety-one collapsed, variance acceptable* — and none of them flinched when, through a high window, the sky outside turned briefly the color of an old bruise and something screamed.

Not a person. Not an animal. A *border*. The line between the mortal realm and the Otherworld, screaming because a man was sewing it shut with thread pulled from its own flesh.

* * *

He saw the first blended creature in the shadow of a stairwell.

It might once have been a fox; the fae-blood in it had given it antlers of pale glass and eyes like winter pools. Now those eyes were clouding. Its coat was coming apart in patches — not shedding, *unweaving*, mortal fur on one side, shimmering otherlight on the other, and where the two met the creature's body simply disagreed about what it was. It lay against the wall, breathing in long shudders, and each breath came out as two sounds: a fox's whine and a bell-tone, half a beat apart, as though the animal existed slightly out of step with itself.

Briar knelt. Tamsin watched her hands hover over it, not touching. "This is what Corvane's mending does," she said softly. "It doesn't join the realms. It presses them together without letting them agree. Anything made of both — anything born on the seam, anything that crossed and stayed — gets caught in the press."

The fox-thing looked up at Tamsin. There was intelligence in the fading of it. Recognition, even. As if it knew what he carried — five shards of the true Oath, warm against his chest in their wrappings, humming like a chord held too long.

"I'm sorry," Tamsin said. His voice came out rusted. "I'm sorry. I'm going to stop this."

The creature exhaled — both sounds at last finding the same beat, for just one breath — and closed its eyes. Whether it died or simply stopped being where they could see it, Tamsin could not say. The space where it had been smelled like rain on stone and snuffed candles.

He stood too fast. The gallery tilted. Vespera caught him — or tried to; her hands passed through his arm these days, cold as well-water, more suggestion than grip. She had burned so much of herself at the seam-gate prison that she was thinner than thin now, a figure drawn in gray ink, visible only when the light was wrong.

"You cannot grieve all of them," she said. "There will be too many before this night ends."

"Then I'll be grieving fast," he said, and kept moving.

* * *

The heart of the fortress was a hall no ledger should have contained.

They came down a stair of white granite into a chamber that was larger inside than the building outside could hold — the Guild's oldest trick, borrowed from the fae themselves, though the fae had never used it for this. The ceiling was lost in darkness shot through with threads of light: gold, silver, and a sickly greenish-white that hurt to look at directly. Those threads converged, hundreds of them, funneling down toward the center of the floor.

At the center stood Corvane.

He had changed since Greyharrow. Or perhaps Tamsin was only finally seeing him clearly. The Guildmaster's fine clothes were gone, replaced by robes stitched with ledger-script so dense it read as darkness, and around him hovered the Guild's hoarded shard-pieces — fragments, slivers, filings scraped from the true Oath over a century of quiet theft — suspended in rings that slowly turned, grinding against one another with a sound like teeth.

And threaded among them, stretched taut from wall to wall to unseen ceiling, hung the borders themselves.

That was the only way Tamsin could describe what he saw. The seam between worlds made visible: a vast fabric of woven light and dark, mortal grain and fae shimmer interlaced, and it was *alive* — pulsing, breathing, singing in a key just below hearing. Into that fabric Corvane had driven needles of greenish light, and from the needles ran his counterfeit threads, hauling whole regions of border tight, fusing realm to realm at points where neither wished to touch. Where the false stitches held, the land beyond the walls would be changing already. Rivers running backward into the hills. Orchards fruiting out of season and out of substance. Mortal fields going luminous at midnight; fae hollows growing walls, doors, debts.

"He's not sealing the border," Tamsin whispered. "He's *tailoring* it."

"Yes." Vespera's voice, beside his ear, was almost not there at all. "A covenant is a promise both sides make freely. What he is building is a harness. And a harness only fits if you break the wearer first."

On a dais beneath the hanging fabric, clerks worked at a single enormous open ledger, its pages taller than a man, recording each stitch. Tamsin recognized the hand that had designed the columns. He had invented half those column-headings himself, years ago, as a junior keeper: *variance*, *yield*, *acceptable loss*. His own tidy script, multiplied across a hundred desks, cataloguing the death of a world's edge.

Righteous anger is supposed to feel clean. Tamsin had imagined it, in the nights of flight and hiding, as a sword-light in the chest, simple and burning. What he actually felt was colder and more complicated: fury braided with shame, because the machinery below him ran on habits he had helped perfect. Every clerk here was him, ten years ago, content to keep beautiful records of ugly things.

"Briar," he said. "The others?"

She touched his shoulder — real pressure, real warmth; whatever else the Otherworld had done to her, her hands remained solid — and pointed along a gantry that hugged the eastern wall. Far above the dais, silhouetted against the woven border, small figures moved: Sera Duncastle of the underground justices, her magistrate's chain worn openly now like a declaration of war; the two freed hollow knights, Aldric and Maren, moving with the terrible economy of the dead given back their purpose; behind them a scattering of fae exiles whose shapes refused to sit still in the eye, and Greyharrow dockworkers with pry-bars and salt-irons.

"They're going for the anchor-pillars," Briar said. "Four of them, one at each corner of the hall. Cut the needles' feed and the whole working sags. Then your shards can do the rest."

"And Corvane?"

Briar's mouth curved, sharp and humorless. "Corvane is busy being a god. Gods rarely notice the mice until the mice are in the granary."

* * *

They climbed. The gantry was iron lacework, old, groaning, and every step rang faintly against the hum of the great working, so that Tamsin fancied the fortress itself was announcing them — but the noise below swallowed everything. The closer they drew to the hanging border-fabric, the louder the singing became, and the stranger. It was not one song. It was thousands, layered: every river that marked a boundary, every hedge, every treaty-line and churchyard wall and threshold ever sworn between here and there, all of them sounding at once, and underneath the music a grinding dissonance where Corvane's green stitches forced notes together that had hated each other for a thousand years.

Halfway along the gantry, the border screamed again — close this time, right overhead — and Tamsin stopped dead, because he *heard words in it*.

He had swordspeech. Even gutted by the word-that-ends, even missing his mother's face and his own name from his own mouth, some part of him still translated. The scream resolved, syllable by agonizing syllable, into a voice like a forest arguing with itself:

*—was sworn, was SWORN, we were promised, we consented, WHO CONSENTED TO THIS—*

"It's asking," Tamsin said aloud, appalled. "Vespera. The border is asking who gave permission."

"No one did," Vespera said. "That is the entire point of everything we have fought. Consent is the covenant's spine, and he has snapped it, and the border knows, and it cannot do anything but scream." A pause; when she spoke again her voice had gone strange and soft. "My sister heard it scream once. A hundred years ago. It is why she chose what she chose. I told myself she was mad. I told myself the Oath could not suffer, because it was only words." A ghost's laugh, thin as paper. "Only words. I was a woman made of nothing else, and I did not know."

Below them, Corvane raised his arms, and the rings of shard-filings spun faster, and a new needle of green light drove home into the fabric with a sound like a nail entering a coffin lid. The whole hall shuddered. Dust sifted down. Somewhere in the dark of the ceiling, something large and patient shifted its weight.

Tamsin tore his eyes away and counted pillars. Four corners, four anchors of black stone veined with the same green light, each wrapped in chains of script. Sera's team was already at the nearest. He saw Aldric wade into a knot of Guild guardsmen — not fight them so much as *endure* them, walking forward through spear-points with the calm of a man who had already died once and found it survivable — while Maren shattered the pillar's chain-lock with the flat of a hollow knight's blade, and Sera pressed her magistrate's seal to the stone and pronounced, in a voice built for courtrooms, the words Tamsin had waited his whole life to hear from an officer of the law:

"This instrument of unconsented binding is hereby declared unlawful. Strike it."

The first pillar cracked like ice on a spring pond.

The effect was instant and everywhere. The great fabric overhead sagged on one side, its green stitches popping one after another with reports like distant artillery. One region of border — Tamsin would learn later it was the marshlands east of Greyharrow — sighed back into itself, mortal and fae unstitching from each other gently, the way sleepers untangle limbs. In the marshes, Tamsin somehow knew, blended creatures were lying down in the reeds and breathing easier, their two heartbeats finding separate rhythms again.

But the other three pillars drank deep, and Corvane turned around.

* * *

Up close, the Guildmaster's face was the worst thing in the hall, worse than the dying fox-thing, worse than the screaming sky. Because it was serene. Corvane looked up at the sagging, shrieking, bleeding border-fabric the way Tamsin had once looked at a beautifully balanced ledger: with the profound satisfaction of a craftsman whose work was nearly done.

His eyes found Tamsin on the gantry. Of course they did. They had always found him; that had been the point of keeping him all those years, the keeper's boy with the swordspeaker's blood, a tool polished and stored against just this night.

"There he is," Corvane called, and his voice carried effortlessly over the din, pitched like a father's over a crowded table. "My best student. Come down, Tamsin. You can still see it finished properly. You of all people know what happens to unfinished work."

Tamsin did not come down. He gripped the railing and made himself look — really look — at what the counterfeit mending was doing, because someone had to witness it accurately, and witnessing accurately was the one craft he had never abandoned. On the far side of the hall, where three pillars still fed the needles, the border-fabric was *festering*. Regions of it had fused wrongly, mortal grain and fae shimmer melted into masses that pulsed and swelled, and from those masses shapes were trying to crawl free — things that had been places once, a crossroads and a standing stone and, gods help him, a wedding-boundary where a marriage oath had soaked into the land over fifty faithful years, all of them congealed now into a single writhing knot of displaced meaning. It moaned in several voices. It reached toward the gantry with what might have been roots or fingers.

It was dying. Places were not supposed to die. Tamsin watched it die anyway, and the anger that came up through him then was not cold anymore. It was the hottest thing left in him, hotter than fear, hotter than grief, and it put words in his mouth that he did not plan and could not afford — but he said them anyway, quietly, and the shards against his chest took the cost gladly:

"*I witness this. I witness what you have made.*"

The old power woke in the syllables. His voice went out across the hall carrying more than sound: carrying the *fact* of what Corvane had done, laid upon the air like a brand laid on hide. Every clerk at the great ledger looked up at once. Several of them, Tamsin noted with a fierce flickering pride, set down their pens.

Corvane's serenity flickered — just once, just at the edges. "Words," he said. "Always words with you people. Words are cheap, boy. I have bought every word worth owning in two realms. Do you know what I am making? Peace. Permanent peace. No more leaks, no more monsters in the hedges, no more fae stealing names at the ford. One border, sealed, administered, *legible*. Your little underground courts, your ragged army — they want chaos with better manners. I am offering order."

"I've read your order," Tamsin said. "It's written in that creature's corpse by the stairwell. It's written in a wedding-boundary drowning in your needle-work. Order that requires the world to stop consenting isn't peace, Master. It's just a very tidy cage."

For a heartbeat, the hall held its breath — clerks, knights, ghosts, the wounded border itself — all waiting to see which word would rule the room.

Then Corvane smiled, and turned back to his work, and spoke a word of his own; and the three living pillars flared green, and the needles drove deeper, and the counterfeit covenant lurched forward like a beast scenting home. Above, in the dark of the unreachable ceiling, the patient shifting thing began at last to descend.

"Finish your tantrum quickly, Tamsin," the Guildmaster said, not looking back. "I'm nearly done."

Tamsin's hand went to the shards at his chest — five of them, warm, humming the true Oath's broken chord — and Briar's hand closed over his wrist, and far across the hall Sera's team reached the second pillar, and the thing from the ceiling unfolded wings that blotted out the ledger-light.

"Down," Briar said. "*Move!*"

They ran.
