## 35. Duel of Ledgers

The counterfeit covenant screamed as I walked toward it.

That was the only word for the sound — a scream made of borders, of two worlds pressed together by hands that had never asked them whether they wished to touch. The great seam beneath the Guild's fortress had become a wound in the shape of a doorway, and through that doorway poured light that was not light: gold gone sour, green gone feverish, every color arguing with its neighbor. Reality near the seam had begun to blister. A moth flew past me with feathers instead of wings. A fountain three streets back had been flowing upward into a sky the color of an old bruise.

And at the center of it all, on a platform of black stone raised above the screaming seam like a pulpit above a congregation, stood Corvane.

He had heard me coming. Of course he had. I had come through his front gate at the head of an army of exiles and freed dead men, and even if half my host was still fighting its way up through the lower halls — Briar's voice rising over the din like a war-horn made of thorns — the Guildmaster of Rare Exchanges would have known my footsteps among ten thousand. He had taught me to walk quietly through ledgers. He had simply never imagined I would one day walk loudly.

"Tamsin." He said my name the way he said everything: precisely, pleasantly, as though the world were a document he had already proofread. "You're bleeding."

"I've been told it happens in wars."

"Not to you. Not until recently." He turned from the seam to face me fully, and I saw what the counterfeit mending was doing to him even as it fed him. His left hand had gone translucent at the fingertips, the bones visible beneath skin that was forgetting itself, and where his shadow should have fallen there were two shadows, overlapping, quarreling. Power written in someone else's hand always shows its forgery eventually. "You look terrible. Sit down. We have perhaps twenty minutes before your friends breach the vault floor, and I have wanted this conversation for eleven years."

"There's nothing you can offer me."

"Everyone says that," Corvane said, "right before they hear the number."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened in the next few minutes, because honesty is the whole point of what came after, and because I have spent my life around people who rewrite their own ledgers until they believe the entries.

Corvane did not threaten me. That was the horror of it, looking back. He offered me tea.

The platform was set like his office above the shop — the same impossible desk of dark wood, the same chair angled just so, the same brass lamp burning oil that smelled faintly of cardamom. He gestured, and two chairs appeared where no chairs had been, drawn out of the seam-light like coins from a conjurer's sleeve. Somewhere below us the battle thundered against the Guild's inner doors, and here was Corvane, pouring from a pot that steamed with the smell of my childhood kitchen.

"You take it bitter," he said. "Three sugars refused, always. You've done it since you were nine years old and decided bitterness was sophistication." He handed me the cup. I did not take it. He set it on the desk between us anyway, and the steam rose between us like a small ghost. "Sit. Stand. Whatever posture helps you feel righteous. But listen, because I am going to say something no Guildmaster has said aloud in four hundred years."

"Corvane—"

"The covenant was a cage." He said it flatly, without theater, and that flatness was worse than any speech. "Not a treaty. Not a sacred bond. A cage, built by twelve frightened people with swords that spoke, locking two worlds together because they could not bear the thought of either one changing without permission. And every generation since has inherited the lock and called it holiness." He sat, unhurried, folding his ruined hand into his lap. "You've read the first ledgers now. You know I'm right."

I had read them. In Vespera's book and in Ysolde's grave-dust and in the shards themselves, which remembered everything they had ever cut, I had read the founding words of the Oath — and underneath the majesty of them, yes, I had felt the iron. *Neither realm shall pass, neither realm shall turn, until the words themselves consent.* Consent, in the old tongue, meant the consent of the words. Never of the worlds.

"You think I don't know that?" I said. "You think I haven't stood exactly where you're standing and hated it?"

"No," Corvane said. "I think you've stood where I'm standing and flinched. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire history of our guild." He leaned forward. The seam-light caught the translucence of his fingers and lit them from within, so that his hand looked like a lantern made of dying man. "Here is my last offer, Tamsin, and I will make it only once, and then we will do this the expensive way. Partnership. Equal shares. You hold five true shards and the name Eastwick, and I hold the hoard — forty years of splinters my predecessors pried from graves and gutters and the teeth of hollowed knights. Together we can finish what I started. Not the counterfeit — the real thing. A new covenant, sworn by both of us, binding both realms under terms we write ourselves. We would be the arbiters of magic for a thousand years. No more decay. No more monsters bleeding through. No more Greyharrow drowning in the overflow of two careless worlds. Order, Tamsin. Real order. The kind that lasts because someone strong enough agreed to carry it."

"And the price?"

"The price is that it must be *us*. Two names on the ledger. Two signatures. Not a parliament of exiles voting on whether the sky may rain. Not fae courts haggling over every threshold. Two minds, aligned, permanent." His voice softened, and that was the cruelest instrument he owned. "You would never lose another memory. I can anchor you — the hoard has enough raw word-stuff in it to rebuild everything the shards have eaten. Your mother's face. Your ninth winter. Your own name, when the time comes. All of it restored, guaranteed, in writing." He smiled, and for one unguarded moment the smile was almost young. "You'd keep yourself. Isn't that all you've ever wanted? To keep yourself?"

And here is the honest part: for three full seconds, standing on a platform above a screaming seam while my friends bled for me in the halls below, I wanted it.

I wanted my mother's face back — I had traded it away in a market at the world's edge and spent every night since reaching for it in the dark and finding a smooth blank place where she should have been. I wanted my name, which I could feel thinning at the edges like wet paper. I wanted to stop being afraid. Corvane knew all of it, because Corvane had read my ledger longer than I had known I had one.

Three seconds. Then I looked past him, at the seam.

The counterfeit mending had welded a strip of Otherworld directly onto our side of the border, and along that strip things were dying that had never had a chance to live. A tree grew there with roots in both worlds, and it was screaming too — slowly, silently, in the way trees scream — because each world pulled its wood toward a different truth. A river ran through the weld and came out the other side as glass. Small creatures lay along the join-line, blended past saving, breathing with lungs and gills at once, suffering the arithmetic of two natures averaged into none.

"You showed me this once," I said quietly. "When I was nineteen. A smuggled salamander that was half smoke. You kept it in a jar and fed it candle flames, and it lived for six days, and on the sixth day you let me hold it while it died. Do you remember what you told me?"

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — quick, buried, genuine. "I told you the border does this to things."

"You told me the border does this to things, and then you built a machine that does it to everything." I set the untouched cup down. It rang against the desk, a small clear note, absurdly gentle. "You didn't offer me partnership, Corvane. You offered me co-authorship of the same lie, with my name printed larger."

"My name wouldn't appear at all."

"That's what frightens me about you. It's true."

* * *

He came off the chair the way winter comes off a mountain — no anger in it, just inevitability, mass, descent.

I had spoken three shard-words in my life and lost pieces of myself to each, and I felt the remaining shards wake against my ribs now like a fistful of sleeping birds startled open. But Corvane did not draw steel first. Corvane drew *words*, because he had learned, across forty years of hoarding splinters, that the duel between two speakers is fought in language before it is fought in anything else.

"*Stay,*" he said.

It was not a suggestion. It was a command in the old grammar, backed by the weight of the hoard, and it hit me like a wall of held breath. My boots locked to the stone. My jaw sealed. Every muscle in my body received the word as law and prepared to obey it forever. This was how the hollowed knights had been made — not broken, just *spoken to*, thoroughly enough that obedience became anatomy.

I had one advantage, and it was the advantage I had carried since the night a century-old ledger wrote my name beside tonight's date: I had spent my life reading the fine print.

"*Stay* binds the body," I forced through my locked teeth, "but the old grammar requires a term. A duration. A condition of release. Say the word without one and the word eats *you* —"

"I know how the grammar works; I helped write the modern recension—"

"—so you'll release me in a breath or bleed your memories into the floor, and I've seen how attached you are to those."

For a heartbeat he hesitated. A master's hesitation, calculating cost — but hesitation nonetheless. And in that heartbeat I did the thing no swordspeaker had done in four hundred years, the thing Ysolde had died attempting and I had promised Vespera I would understand before the end: I answered his compulsion not with a stronger compulsion but with a *question*.

"*Why?*" I said, in the old grammar, in the speaking-voice, and the shard at my heart turned the question into a blade of pure inquiry.

Words are blades, but questions are words too, and a question cannot be parried — it can only be answered or fled. "*Why* struck Corvane's *stay* like a wedge struck a knot, and the command split down its own grain. My body came free. He staggered, and I saw the cost of his sloppiness at once: a memory leaving him visibly, a fine gray thread unwinding from his temple and dissolving into the seam-light. Something small. Something he would notice missing tomorrow and never find again.

"That was a mistake," he said, and now the pleasantness was gone, and what remained underneath was older than pleasantness. "You know what I gave up to build this? Do you have any conception—"

"Yes," I said. "That's why I'm sorry."

And I meant it. God help me, I meant it, and meaning it made my next word sharper than any hatred could have.

* * *

We fought then, properly fought, across the black platform and along the edge of the screaming seam, and I will set down what it was like because no one else who saw it survived unchanged enough to describe it.

It was a duel of ledgers. That is the only true name for it. He spoke and I answered; I spoke and he countered; and every exchange cost us both entries from the books of ourselves. He commanded *Kneel* and I paid a summer afternoon — my eleventh birthday, the taste of burnt honey-cake — to fuel the counter-word *Rise*, and the summer left me even as I used it, so that I defended myself with a memory and destroyed it in the spending. He threw *Blind* at me and I bought my way clear with the smell of the shop's back room, ink and beeswax, twenty years of it, gone in a single breath like a snuffed candle. Each of us was spending himself to strike the other, and the seam drank the spillage eagerly, growing fatter and louder with every wasted soul-piece.

He was stronger. I have to write that plainly. Forty years of hoard against my five hard-won shards; a lifetime of practice in the crueler grammars; a mind that had never once flinched from a balance sheet. Twice he put me down. Once he nearly ended it, standing over me with a word of unmaking gathering in his translucent hand like stormlight in a jar — and in that moment, with the end of me assembled and ready, he paused.

He paused to make a final offer. Even then. Even with victory in his mouth, he needed me to *choose* him.

"Last chance," he said, breathing hard, his two shadows writhing behind him. "Stand up as my partner or stay down as my cautionary tale. Those are the only entries left in your column, boy. I have read your whole ledger. There is nothing else in you worth anything but compliance."

And lying there on the black stone, tasting blood and burnt honey-cake that was no longer anywhere in the world, I understood him completely — and understanding is the beginning of pity, and pity is a weapon no compulsion can disarm.

"You're wrong about the ledger," I said. "You've been reading it wrong since the day you found me. You see a column of debts." I got one knee under myself. "I see a column of payments. Everything I've lost, I *spent*. On purpose. On people. You've hoarded every scrap of yourself for forty years, Corvane, and do you know what your ledger shows? Nothing. No debits. No costs. A life with nothing written in it, because you never once paid for anything you believed in." I stood. My legs shook. The shards sang. "You didn't offer me partnership because you respect me. You offered it because you're the loneliest man in two worlds, and you wanted someone to countersign."

The word left him like a wound opening.

He came at me with everything — the hoard, the hate, the forty years, a chain of commands braided into a single rope of absolute grammar, *Obey obey obey*, a word with no condition, no term, no mercy in its structure, the kind of speaking that unmakes the speaker — and I did not block it. There was no blocking it. Instead I opened my ledger to him entirely, the way I had finally learned to do, and let him see the page.

He saw the empty space where my mother's face had been. He saw the smooth blank where my ninth winter was. He saw the edges of my own name going soft as wet paper. He saw what the power had already taken and what it would yet demand, itemized, initialed, *true*.

And Corvane — Corvane, who had built an empire on the principle that no one pays what power truly costs, who had spent his whole life believing the bill could always be sent to someone else — Corvane looked at my ledger and flinched. One instant of naked horror at the mathematics of the thing he had devoted himself to. One instant of seeing himself in it.

His braided command wavered. Wavered is enough. I stepped inside its reach and spoke the counter-entry, quiet as a clerk closing a day-book:

"*Paid in full.*"

The hoard-shards in his hand went dark as doused coals. He fell to his knees on his own black platform, staring at his translucent fingers as though they belonged to a stranger — as, in a way, they now did. Below us, the doors burst open and Briar's host came flooding into the seam-chamber like dawn breaking into a cellar.

I crouched in front of him. I took his ruined hand. It was cold, and lighter than a hand should be, as though most of him had already been spent and only the spending remained.

"It didn't have to be this way," he whispered. And the terrible thing, the thing I will carry longer than my scars, is that he wasn't lying. He genuinely believed some version of this had been inevitable since before I was born. He had chosen the cage so long ago he had forgotten there had ever been a door.

"No," I said. "But it was always going to be some way, and you chose this one, entry by entry, year by year. Nobody made you sign."

The seam screamed louder. Above us, the counterfeit covenant lurched toward completion, and the light went from sour gold to a white that had no warmth in it at all, and Corvane lifted his head with the last of his pride arranging itself around him like a coat.

"You still have to stop it," he said. "And you know what stopping it costs. You've seen the final word. You've felt it waiting." His mouth curved — not triumph, nothing so warm. Just arithmetic. "Say it, and you lose her face forever. Lose your name. Possibly everything. Refuse, and my work finishes itself the moment I stop holding it back." His eyes, for the first time all night, were perfectly clear. "So tell me, Eastwick. What's your final entry going to be?"

The seam tore wider.

And somewhere beyond the white light, in the place where the border had worn thin as parchment, I heard Vespera's voice — faint, frayed, spending herself to reach me — saying only my name, over and over, as though she were trying to make sure I still had one to lose.
