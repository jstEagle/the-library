## 37. Who Speaks the New Oath

### Briar

The seam's heart was not a place. It was an agreement the world kept forgetting to keep.

Briar felt it in her sap and marrow the moment they descended the last stair — a hollow carved beneath Greyharrow where the border ran so thin that mortal torchlight bled gold and fae shadow bled grey, both colors trading places with every breath. The walls were neither stone nor bark but something between: limestone laced with living root, veined with light that pulsed like a slow heart. Here the covenant had first been spoken, eleven voices and a twelfth that history had swallowed, and here, if the shards spoke truly, it could be spoken again.

She carried Tamsin's left arm across her shoulders. He weighed almost nothing now, and that frightened her more than blood would have. A body should resist. His simply *consented* to be carried, the way a coat consents, the way an empty house consents to wind.

The others came down behind them in a ragged procession: the two freed hollow knights walking side by side with the strange, careful dignity of things learning again how to be alive; three of Greyharrow's underground justices with their ink-stained fingers and their faces stripped of everything but resolve; a handful of fae exiles whose names had been salted out of them in Guild prisons and who walked therefore as half-presences, more rumor than form. And at the rear, thin as morning frost, Vespera.

Briar had seen ghosts before. Every fae had. They were echoes, habits of the dead, harmless as old perfume. Vespera was not that. Vespera was a wound in the shape of a woman, held together by a century of stubbornness and one unfinished sentence, and Briar could see through her to the far wall of the chamber — see through her, and see how much thinner she had grown since the seam-gate. Since she had burned herself as a shield for people who could not even see what saved them.

"You're staring," Vespera said, without turning her head.

"I'm counting," Briar said. "You have maybe a night left in you. Less, if you spend anything."

"Then I'll spend carefully."

"That's not how you spend anything. I've watched you."

Vespera's smile was a crack in porcelain. "No," she said. "I suppose I've never once done anything carefully. That's rather the point of me."

They reached the center of the hollow, and Briar set Tamsin down upon the dais — a low ring of black glass, older than the city above it, warm under her palms like the flank of a sleeping animal. Five shards lay there already, arranged on the glass in the order the old liturgy demanded, each one singing a different silence: the Word That Cuts humming faintly like a wire pulled taut; the Word That Binds breathing slow; the Word That Opens flickering with doors that weren't there; the Word That Lies saying, in a voice like honey over a well, *trust me*; and Ysolde's shard, the fifth, which made no sound at all and never had, only watched.

Tamsin's hand crept toward them and stopped. He stared at his own fingers as if they belonged to a stranger he'd been introduced to too quickly.

"Briar," he said. His voice was frayed down to threads. "What did I just say?"

She knelt beside him. "You don't remember."

"The duel." His eyes moved, searching a room that wasn't there. "Corvane. I remember standing over him. I remember — I remember speaking. Words coming out of me like teeth. And then—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "There was a woman singing. My mother sang like that. Did my mother sing like that? Or is that something I'm borrowing?"

Briar looked up at Vespera across the dais, and for once neither of them pretended.

The shards had been fed. Swordspeech was written in the speaker's self, Vespera had told him long ago, and Tamsin had spent himself like coin in a burning house — memories first, then skills, then names of streets and tastes of food and finally whole years, going out of him word by word. He had lost his mother entirely somewhere in the fortress corridors. He had lost his childhood home two days before that. This morning he had asked Briar, gently, twice, what his own handwriting looked like.

And the covenant still needed speaking.

* * *

### Corvane

He came down the stair unbound and unguarded, because no one had thought to guard him, and perhaps that said everything about them and nothing worth saying about himself.

Guildmaster Aldous Corvane had walked with power his entire life the way other men walked with canes, and now the cane was kicked away and he discovered he could still walk — discovery, not defeat. The duel had unmade his counterfeit working. It had not unmade him. There was a difference, he reflected, watching the ragged assembly part around him like water around a shark's fin, between losing a war and being lost.

The shards' song reached him at the bottom step and he stood very still inside it.

He had spent thirty years assembling pieces of that song in vaults beneath the Guildhall. He knew its harmony better than anyone breathing. He knew what a mended covenant would sound like when it closed over the world like a lid — and what it would sound like if someone else's hand closed it instead of his.

"Tamsin Eastwick," he said.

Tamsin lifted his head. Even diminished, even scraped nearly hollow, there was something in his face that Corvane had spent the whole pursuit pretending not to recognize: the particular steadiness of a man doing arithmetic he does not enjoy. Corvane had always respected arithmetic.

"You should kill me," Corvane said. "It would be tidier. But you won't, because you've realized what I realized the moment I heard the shards sing in your hands, weeks ago, in a vault you thought was empty."

"Don't," Briar said, low.

"He doesn't know yet." Corvane spread his hands — the gesture of a man presenting a bill. "None of you know, or you'd have stopped smiling. The new covenant needs a voice. Not five voices, not a committee, not the will of the crowd you've dragged along behind you like ballast. One voice. One speaker. And swordspeech, Master Eastwick, is the only tongue in which a covenant can be sworn — which means the speaker must *have* swordspeech. Whole. Undiminished. Yours has been pouring out of you since the drowned chapel like wine from a cracked jug. You couldn't swear a grocery list now, let alone bind two worlds."

Silence took the hollow. Even the roots in the walls seemed to lean in.

"So the choice," Corvane continued softly, savoring it, "is mine after all. Or hers." He nodded at Vespera, and something in his voice curdled. "Or nobody's. You've won the battle, Eastwick, and assembled the instrument, and discovered at the last hour that the musician is gone. I could speak it. I still have fragments enough in me from thirty years of handling shard-glass — enough to carry a voice, though it would cost me what remains of my humanity, and make me arbiter of both realms forever, which I confess appeals. But you'd have to *let* me. And you won't. So instead we shall all stand here in your beautiful ruin and watch the seams bleed, together, as equals."

He smiled then, and it was the truest thing he had ever worn.

* * *

### Vespera

She had known. She had known since the gate, since she poured herself out like smoke from a broken jar, since she watched Tamsin trade his mother's face for a stranger's life and understood that the ledger was balancing itself against him line by line.

She had known for a century, actually. That was the joke of it. That was the punchline she had carried alone since the night Ysolde died.

*The oath must be sworn whole,* Ysolde used to say, sharpening her sword by candlelight while Vespera wrote at the desk beside her, their two shadows making one shape on the wall. *That's why there are twelve of us and one covenant. No single voice should hold it all.* And then, later, in the terrible last year, when Ysolde had begun to talk of breaking instead of mending: *But if it ever comes to one voice, Vespera — if it ever comes down to one person holding every word — then that person pays the whole price. Not part. All. Swordspeech is written in the self. To speak a new oath is to give the self away entire.*

Vespera had helped kill her for less than this.

No — that was the lie she told herself on bad nights. She had helped kill her for exactly this. Because Ysolde had meant to do it. Had meant to take the twelve words into herself, break the old compulsion, and pay whatever the payment was, and Vespera — young, terrified, certain that her sister's sacrifice was really her sister's suicide dressed in noble grammar — had gone to the others and said *she means to destroy us all,* and eleven swordspeakers had come to an unmarked field, and Ysolde had died before she could finish the sentence she'd spent her whole life composing.

And Vespera had lived a hundred years knowing the truth, which was this: her sister had been right about the price, and right about the necessity, and wrong about exactly one thing — the timing. The world hadn't been ready. It was ready now, or near enough, and the person standing bleeding-light at the edge of the dais was not Ysolde, would never be Ysolde, and that was good. That was the whole point.

"Tamsin," she said.

He turned to her. Ghosts do not have voices; they borrow the listener's memory of voices, and she borrowed, deliberately, their mother's tone — no, *his* mother's tone, the one he could no longer recall, and she hated herself for using it, and used it anyway, because gentleness was a tool like any other and he deserved gentleness tonight.

"There is a way," she said. "There has always been a way. Your power isn't gone. It's *spent* — and spent power leaves a channel. The shards know the way back to the hand that paid for them. For a few minutes — perhaps only moments — you could gather yourself. Every word you gave away, called home. Whole. Complete. Enough to speak a covenant that would outlast continents."

Hope went around the room like a lit match through dry grass. She watched it catch on Briar's face, on the knights' hollow eyes, on the justices' hungry mouths.

"And then?" Tamsin asked quietly.

Vespera held his gaze.

"And then the oath takes it back," she said. "All of it, and the interest besides. The covenant binds by consent, so it can only be sealed by sacrifice — the speaker surrenders swordspeech forever, and with it everything the speech is anchored to. Not just the power. The self that carried it. What you are afterward would be..." She paused. In a century of prophecy-writing she had learned every rhetorical trick, and here, at the end, she refused every one of them. "I don't know. No one knows. No one has ever done it. Ysolde died before she could try."

"And the alternative," Tamsin said, "is Corvane. Or nothing."

"Those are the ledgers," Vespera said. "Yes."

* * *

### Tamsin

He had expected terror. What he found, sitting with the five shards arranged before him and his own emptiness mapped out like a city under siege, was something worse: relief.

That frightened him. He examined the feeling the way he had once examined acquisitions — bottled storm, stolen shadow, impossible things — turning it in the light of long habit. Relief. Because the choice, at last, was simple. He had spent months drowning in complications, in prophecies and betrayals and wars, and here at the bottom of everything was an entry so clean it could have been written in his own neat hand: *One voice speaks. Everything is given. The realms are bound by consent.*

He thought about consent. He thought about the ledgers, and how he had loved them — really loved them — not the Guild's greed they served but the act itself: naming a thing precisely, weighing it honestly, writing down what was owed. His whole life had been practice for a document. Every careful column, every verified weight, had been rehearsal for the moment when someone must stand at the world's seam and write, in the only ink that mattered, terms both realms could live under.

And he thought, with a grief so vast it was almost peaceful, of his mother's face — the blank where it had been. He had already paid that price without being asked. The rest of him was merely outstanding debt.

"It has to be me," he said, and the room began to speak all at once —

— Briar's voice sharp as holly, *you've given enough*; the justices murmuring about procedure and precedent; one of the hollow knights, a woman named Serre whom Tamsin had freed from her puppet-strings in the running battle, saying in a rusted voice, "I was made to speak words that killed me once. Let me carry this one"— and Tamsin raised his hand, and because he was still, even hollowed, the man who had kept the ledgers, they fell quiet.

"No," he said. "Listen to what you're asking. Any of us who isn't a swordspeaker can't hold the words at all. Serre — your speech was taken with your death; there's nothing left in you to spend, and the covenant would know a coerced voice, it would twist around it. Corvane—" he looked at the Guildmaster, and found, to his own surprise, that the pity he'd felt over crossed steel was still there, banked but alive, "—would make of it a crown. He's told you so himself. That's the most honest thing he's ever said."

Corvane inclined his head slightly, as if receiving a compliment.

"And me." Tamsin looked down at his hands. "I was made for this before I was born. A name smudged in a book. An inheritance I didn't choose. My whole life I've been a thing the prophecy wrote — a character in someone else's entry. If I speak this oath because destiny demands it, then nothing changes. Compulsion wears a kinder face, that's all." He looked up. "So I'm not going to speak it because I was written to. I'm going to choose it. That's the difference. That's the *whole* difference. The old covenant bound by force and called it fate. The new one binds by consent — and consent means someone has to look at the full price, written out plainly, and sign anyway."

His gaze found Vespera. She had gone very still, the way she did when a line of her own prophecy-book turned back on her.

"You knew it would end here," he said. "A hundred years ago, you wrote tonight's date in a ledger and put my name beside it. Did you write this page too? The last one?"

Vespera's mouth opened. Closed. When she answered, her voice was smaller than he had ever heard it.

"I wrote it," she said, "the night Ysolde died. I wrote it so that someone, someday, would have the truth in front of them — the whole price, itemized. I didn't know who would read it. I prayed it would be no one. I prayed it would be everyone." Her edges trembled like a candle flame. "Tamsin, I have watched you lose your mother's face and your childhood and your own handwriting, and I let it happen, because I am a coward twice over and I wanted the future I wrote to arrive more than I wanted to spare you. If you do this — if you stand on that glass and call the power home — I want you to understand that I will not stop you. I will help you. And I will hate myself with my last breath for being able to do both."

"I know," Tamsin said. "That's why it has to be you who anchors me."

Her head came up sharply.

"You're almost gone," he said gently. "Briar counted. You have a night, maybe less. But ghosts are made of memory, Vespera, and memory is exactly what I'm losing. When I speak the oath and the covenant takes me — I'll need something to hold my shape. A hand on the ledger. Someone who remembers who I was, even the parts I've forgotten, especially those. Spend the last of yourself on that. Not on shields. On *me*."

For a long moment the ghost of a hundred-year-old crime said nothing at all. Then she laughed — a broken, wet, human sound — and Briar saw that Vespera was crying, which ghosts should not be able to do, and understood that whatever Vespera had spent at the seam-gate, she had saved one last coin, and had just decided its purchase.

"You manipulative little bookkeeper," Vespera whispered.

"I learned from a prophet."

* * *

### Briar

They cleared the dais. Corvane, remarkably, did not move — he stood at the chamber's edge with his arms folded and his eyes bright, watching, and Briar realized with a chill that he intended to witness the price in full, to carry the sight of it back into the world as proof that power was worth any cruelty. She filed his face away. Fae keep ledgers of their own.

The justices withdrew to the stairs. The exiles formed a ring, old as ritual. The two freed knights drew their blades and reversed them — points down, an honor guard for the dying — and Briar took her place at the foot of the dais with her hand on the black glass, close enough to catch him if catching him were possible, far enough not to touch what he was about to become.

Tamsin Eastwick knelt in the center of the ring of shards. He rolled up his sleeves like a man preparing to reconcile accounts. His face, in the root-light, was terribly calm.

"For the record," he said, to the room, to the shards, to the listening dark between worlds, "my name is Tamsin Eastwick. I want that written down somewhere. Before I go, I want it remembered that I chose."

Vespera drifted to his shoulder, thin as a breath, and laid her fading hands above his hands without touching them.

"Call it home," she said.

The five shards began to sing.

The sound was not music. It was the sound of a name returning to its owner — every word he had ever spoken in power coming home across the borderlands at once, out of drowned chapels and tree-courts and dead men's chests, streaming back into him like rivers running uphill, and Briar watched Tamsin's emptied body begin to fill, watched color climb his throat, watched his eyes widen with the unbearable flood of everything he'd given away and everything he was about to give again, freely, in his own words, in his own name—

The lights in the walls guttered. The seam itself seemed to hold its breath.

And high above them, through a thousand feet of stone and street and uneasy city, every bell in Greyharrow began — without a hand anywhere near them — to ring.
