## 38. When the Oath Wakes Again

The seam's heart was not a place so much as a wound that had learned to sing.

We stood on nothing — or nearly nothing: a shelf of pale stone no wider than a chapel floor, suspended in the meeting-place of two worlds. Above us the mortal sky frayed into ribbons of grey cloud; below, impossibly, the Otherworld's green dark turned and turned like water in a bowl. Between them ran the seam itself, a seam of light and wrongness, a scar in the fabric of things that pulsed with every heartbeat of every creature in either realm. I could feel those heartbeats now. The shards had given me that much, and taken more.

Five shards hung before me in the air, arranged in an arc like the points of a broken crown. They did not glow, exactly. They *listened*. Each one was a word made solid — the Word That Cuts, the Word That Binds, the Word That Opens, the Word That Lies, and Ysolde's own, the Word That Remembers, which hummed at a frequency just below grief. And around them, faint as breath on winter glass, drifted the ghost-shapes of the other five words. Corvane's counterfeit working had scattered them, but scattering is not unmaking. They had come home to the seam, drawn by the same gravity that drew me.

I had lost my mother's face three days ago. I knew this the way you know a room you've never entered — by its outline, by the shape of its absence. There was a hole in me shaped like a woman singing, and I could not even remember what song. When I reached for her, my hands came back empty. When I reached for my own name, sometimes it hesitated before answering.

*Tamsin*, I told myself, standing at the edge of everything. *Tamsin Eastwick. Keeper of ledgers. Sworn of the Guild of Rare Exchanges — no. Not anymore.* Even that was fraying. The Guild's ink had written itself so deep into me that pulling it out pulled threads of *me* with it.

"You're stalling," Briar said gently. She stood at my left, thorn-crowned and battered, her sap-black blood still crusted at her collar from the march. Her hand found mine — bark against skin, warm anyway. "That's allowed. But the seam won't wait for permission."

"I'm not stalling." My voice sounded far away, as if someone else were borrowing it. "I'm trying to remember what I'm going to say."

"Then don't," said Vespera.

She stood opposite me, across the arc of shards, and she was almost transparent now. Where once she had been a figure cut from candlelight and old paper, she was thinner than smoke, a sketch of a woman drawn in the last inch of a pencil. The rescue at the seam-gate had cost her most of what remained; holding the hollow knights off while we carried Briar out had cost her the rest of her reserves. She should have been gone already. Ghosts do not linger past their substance. But she had anchored herself to me, thread by thread, and I understood with a cold, rising horror exactly what she intended to do with those threads.

"Vespera—"

"Don't." Her smile was the old wry one, the ledger-keeper's smile, the one that said *I have counted this and the arithmetic holds.* "You're about to give up the only thing keeping your name attached to your bones. Someone has to hold the other end of the string."

"There has to be another way."

"There is always another way," she said. "It's worse. Shall I list them? Ysolde and I spent forty years listing them, Tamsin. We got very good at it. It's how we learned that all the other ways end with either a wall between the worlds or a crown over both." She looked at the shards, and something moved across her fading face — her sister's name, perhaps, spoken silently. "You are going to do what neither of us could. You're going to swear it *differently*. And I am going to make certain that when the power leaves you, it doesn't take *you* with it. That's the whole of my plan. It's not elegant. It's enough."

Behind us, crowded onto the narrow stone or hovering at its edges, waited everyone the old world had no use for. Fae exiles with moth-wing scars. Greyharrow's underground justices in their plain grey coats, ink-stained fingers linked. Two hollow knights who had chosen, in the wreckage of their puppeting, to stand guard over the man who had freed them — Sir Aldric and the one who called herself Maren, dead women walking upright by will alone. A hundred others besides, the lawless and the dispossessed, silent as a congregation.

Corvane's counterfeit covenant still screamed somewhere below us. His fortress at the great seam had cracked when I spoke the Word That Ends, his false mending torn open — but torn open things bleed, and the borderlands were bleeding light and shadow in equal measure. What I had broken had to be replaced tonight, while the wound was open, while consent was still possible. By dawn the old habits of power would come crawling back, ours or anyone's.

Briar squeezed my hand once and let go. "Whenever you're ready, swordspeaker."

*I was never a swordspeaker,* I wanted to say. *I was a clerk. I liked columns. I liked the way numbers behaved.*

But that was the lie I had lived inside for thirty-one years, and the shards knew better. The shards had always known better.

* * *

I stepped into the arc.

The words rose to meet me. Not eagerly — carefully, the way old dogs rise for a master they loved and feared in equal measure. Each shard pressed against some part of me as I passed it: the Word That Cuts laid itself along the line of my jaw like a blade being weighed; the Word That Binds coiled warm around my wrists, familiar and horrible, the sensation of every chain ever justified; the Word That Opens slid beneath my breastbone and *prised*; the Word That Lies whispered sweetly of how easy it would be, even now, to swear a beautiful false thing and keep the power and call it mercy.

And Ysolde's shard — the Word That Remembers — simply looked at me. Out of the whole arc, only it felt like family. It had been waiting a century for Eastwick blood. It knew the shape of the hole where my mother sang.

"The old covenant," Vespera said, and her voice carried through the seam the way bells carry through fog, "was sworn by twelve upon compulsion — bound realms to obedience, obedience to pain, pain to perpetuity. Speak now the new one, Tamsin Eastwick, in your own words, or hold your silence forever."

"That's not how oaths go," I said, my mouth dry.

"It is now," she said. "That's rather the point."

I closed my eyes. I tried to find myself to stand on. There — *Tamsin*. Faint. A candle two rooms away, guttering. Around it, absence where a mother's lullaby should be. Absence where the smell of ledger-ink and beeswax should be. Absence, growing, where my own reflection should be. The shards had been paid for all along, and the price had been paid in me, coin by coin, and now the account stood ready to be emptied entirely.

*Let it be emptied,* I thought. *Let it all go — but let what remains choose.*

I opened my eyes and began.

"I will not bind you."

The words came out small. Then the seam caught them, and they grew.

"I will not bind the fae to mortal fear, nor mortals to fae hunger. I will not speak a covenant of compulsion, for compulsion is the root of every rot I have cut out on this road. Hear me, both worlds: I offer instead the harder oath. The oath that asks."

Light gathered along the seam. Below, the bleeding slowed. Everything in either realm that had ears — and that night, everything had ears — leaned in.

"Let passage be a door, not a wound. Let crossing be asked, and answered, and asked again. Let any soul of either realm say *no*, and let that *no* be a wall no power can pass — no guild, no crown, no hungry god, no desperate keeper of ledgers, no swordspeaker, not even me. Especially not me." My voice cracked, and I let it crack, because an oath without cracks is only performance. "The old Oath held because twelve were forced to hold it. This one will hold because *everyone must choose it, every day, freely, or it fails* — and I think, I have seen, that you will choose it. I have marched with the ones who choose."

The first shard flared. The Word That Cuts dissolved into sound, into the idea of clean division, honest endings, and poured itself into the seam like water finding a channel. The scar-tissue of a hundred years of forced borders began to soften.

"Let there be courts where the wronged may speak and be heard, in either world, under whatever sky they claim. Let debt be honored but never inherited as bondage. Let hospitality be sacred again — bread shared, thresholds honored, names given freely and kept safely."

The second shard released. Binding became promising; chains became handshakes. Somewhere far below, I heard Greyharrow's bells begin to ring, though no hand had touched them, and beneath the bells, deeper, stranger, the green dark of the Otherworld rang back.

"Let magic wake where it will and rest where it must, owing service to none and kindness to all. Let the monsters at the borders be met with questions before blades." I thought of the hollowed knight in whose chest the third shard had slept like a swallowed star; I thought of letting her die twice, and of the peace that had been in it after all. "And let the price of power be paid openly, in daylight, by consent — never secretly, out of the substance of the powerless."

The fourth shard went. Then, gently, as if handing over an heirloom, the fifth — Ysolde's — which did not blaze but *warmed*, and in its warmth, for half a heartbeat, I heard a woman singing a song I could no longer name, and I wept without shame, and the seam took my tears too, and used them.

Five words given. Six remained, drifting loose — the scattered halves Corvane's fall had shaken free, including the Word That Ends, the forbidden one, the one that had eaten my mother's memory whole. They circled me now like patient birds.

"And the rest," I said, "the words of ending and opening and unmaking — I release them. Not destroyed. Released. Let them return to the space between speech and silence, where no guild can hoard them, no prophet pretend them, no frightened clerk spend them. Power hoarded is power stolen. Let these be unhoarded forever."

The six shards rose, trembled, and came apart into light — and the light went *everywhere*, sinking into stone and sap and salt water and the marrow of every witness on that narrow shelf, so that thereafter no one could say precisely where the covenant's strength resided, which was the whole design. A covenant that lives in no single vault cannot be robbed.

And then the bill arrived.

It came not as pain, at least not first. It came as *unbuttoning* — as if someone were patiently undoing me from the outside in. Swordspeech unwove from my muscles. The shards' borrowed sight dimmed; the seam's roaring harmony collapsed to a thin whine, then to ordinary wind. I felt my hearing of heartbeats fold shut like a book. Good, I thought. Good. This is the price. This is fair—

And then it reached for my name.

*Tamsin* flickered. The candle two rooms away guttered hard. I felt the great soft current of the released power pull at the last fixed point in me, because a departing flood takes the loose soil with it, and I had made myself very, very loose. My hands did not know themselves. The word *Eastwick* sat in my mouth like a foreign coin. I tried to remember why I was standing here, and found the question turning in empty air, and understood with perfect, terrible clarity that in a moment I would not even know to be afraid.

"No," said Vespera.

She crossed the shelf of stone — a ghost, spending the last of herself with every step, and each step cost her a year of lingering, a memory of her own, a degree of *her* — and she put her insubstantial arms around whatever was still recognizably me, and she held on.

"Tamsin Eastwick," she said, clear as a struck bell, clear as the first entry in a fresh ledger. "Born in Harrowmere in the spring of the comet. Son of Maren Eastwick, who sang while she mended nets, and sang badly, and was loved for it. Hired by the Guild of Rare Exchanges at nineteen because you corrected an error in their shipping manifest and couldn't bear to leave it. Kept the ledgers true for twelve years even when truth was inconvenient. Afraid of deep water. Generous in ways you'd deny under questioning. Stubborn as a rusted hinge. You bought a bottled storm once and set it free on purpose and told no one. You traded your mother's face to save a stranger's child, and you have never once regretted it where it counts, in the place regret cannot reach."

Each fact she spoke, she paid for. I watched her thin as she gave them — her voice losing its music, her edges dissolving into gold dust that the seam drank. She was reading me back to myself out of the book of her own extinction, and it was working, oh God, it was working; each detail landed in me like a keel dropped into floodwater, and I steadied, and my name came back, and stayed.

"Vespera," I gasped. "Stop. You'll — there won't be anything left—"

"There was never supposed to be anything left," she said, and her smile was Ysolde's smile now, or maybe it had always been, sisters learning expression from each other across a shared childhood a century drowned. "A hundred years ago I helped kill a woman who wanted to give the worlds exactly what you've given them tonight. Everything since has been interest on that debt. Let me pay it, Tamsin. Let me *finish*."

She spoke the last facts softly, one by one, like coins laid on a counting-house table:

"You like your tea bitter. You apologize to furniture. You keep your left bootlace double-knotted because of a superstition you invented yourself. Your mother's song went 'down by the water, down by the weir' — you lost the words, Tamsin, but you never lost that much, and no one can take it now, because it's sworn into the seam itself. You chose your own words tonight. No one made you. That means it was yours. *You* were yours."

The flood withdrew. The last light settled. The seam, no longer a wound, breathed once — slow, deep, doubled, two worlds inhaling together — and went quiet as a closed ledger.

Vespera's arms were the last thing I felt. Gold dust, and the pressure of an embrace, and a voice almost gone:

"Ysolde would have—" She stopped. Started again, choosing her final words the way she had once chosen entries, with a keeper's care. "Tell them both worlds chose. Tell them a Whitlock— tell them an *Eastwick* finished it. And Tamsin — when they ask who swore the new covenant —"

"Vespera—"

"—tell them it was no one special," she whispered, delighted with her own last joke, "just a keeper of ledgers, who was very, very good with dates—"

And she laughed, and the laugh came apart into light, and the light went everywhere, and did not come back.

* * *

Dawn found us on the shore of the seam, if a place between worlds can be said to have a shore. The stone shelf remained — wider now, gentler, a headland looking out on two horizons instead of a knife-edge over an abyss. The borderlands around it had gone still and strange and clean, the way a house goes after a long illness finally breaks.

Briar sat beside me with her arm through mine and did not say anything, which was the correct thing. The hollow knights stood watch at the headland's edge, dead and free and solemn as statuary. Behind us, the exiles and justices were beginning, quietly, in the way of practical people, to argue about what came next — doors and tariffs and courts and who would carry messages between worlds — and the argument was loud and imperfect and utterly alive, and it was, I realized, the sound of the covenant working. It was meant to sound like this. Like bickering neighbors. Like consent, rehearsed daily.

In my hands I held the only thing the morning had left behind: a slim book, bound in grey leather, that had not existed until the sun touched it. Vespera's book — the prophecy-book, the one she had written a hundred years from now and sent sliding backward through time to find the one name that could answer it. Its pages were blank now, every one, the prophecy spent, fulfilled, forgiven.

Except the last page.

On the last page, in handwriting that faded even as I watched — a ghost's ink, a final extravagance — stood a single line:

*The twelfth grave lies empty at last. Go see what grew there.*

I looked up. Briar followed my gaze toward the far headland, where in the new light I could just make out a marker that had not been there yesterday — a simple standing stone, pale as bone, rising from the grass at the borderland's edge. And beside it, unmistakable even at this distance, planted crooked in the earth the way she would have wanted it:

An old Guild ledger, its cover scarred, its clasp undone, pages lifting and falling in the wind between worlds like something breathing.

Someone had written on the stone. The letters were freshly cut, sharp-edged, recent — cut within the hour, though no living hand on our headland had moved.

And they spelled a name that was neither Vespera's nor Ysolde's nor mine.
