## 39. After the Binding

The morning after the world changed, I woke in the crook of an alder tree above Greyharrow and did not know what season it was.

That sounds like a small confusion for one of the Fair Folk, but understand: my people *are* our seasons. We carry them inside us like lanterns. For three hundred years I had carried only winter — the blighted edge of the Otherworld had seen to that, seeping its grey rot into my sap until even my dreams came down in ash. But when I opened my eyes on that first morning and looked down at the seam-city glittering under a sky with no seam in it at all, something green moved in me. Something I had thought pruned away past all regrowth.

Spring. It was spring inside me.

I wept, which for my kind is a serious business — sap and honeyed light, dripping from the branches, startling the birds — and then I climbed down and went to see whether the mortals had managed to break anything overnight.

They had not. That was the miracle I could not stop testing, the loose tooth my tongue kept returning to. The binding held. The new covenant, sworn in Tamsin Eastwick's own name, in his own words — words no god or guild or ghost had put in his mouth — held the way a bridge holds: quietly, without drama, bearing weight.

* *

You must understand what the old world was, to grasp how strange the new one looked at dawn.

For a thousand years the border between the mortal realm and the Otherworld was a wound dressed as a wall. The old covenant — the Oath of Twelve — had bound the realms together by compulsion, sword-speech hammered into the fabric of both worlds, and when it broke, the border became a bleeding place. Things leaked through in both directions. Mortals woke with their shadows gone missing. Fae dried into husks at the edges of our own country. And into that misery stepped the Guild of Rare Exchanges, selling safety on one side of the seam and salvage on the other, growing fat on the chaos they had spent generations cultivating. They bottled storms. They stole shadows. They kept ledgers of impossible things, and every entry was somebody's grief priced by the pound.

I knew the Guild best from the wrong end — from the holding cells beneath their fortress at the great seam, from salt and iron and questions designed to peel a fae out of her own name. I owed the old covenant a debt once; I paid it to Tamsin, and I would have paid it twice over for what he did next, which was to take the whole rotten architecture of destiny and rebuild it out of consent.

But that is his story to tell, if he can still tell it. This part is mine.

* *

Greyharrow changed first, because Greyharrow had always been the rehearsal.

The city sits astride the seam — has always sat astride it — half its streets in the mortal realm, half spilling over into the Otherworld, its gutters running with water from two skies. Before the binding, living there was an act of daily negotiation conducted in whispers: which door opened where this week, which district the Guild's magistrates owned outright, which wells ran with water you could drink and which ran with water that remembered being somewhere else.

Now the seam ran quiet and bright, a river of light down the middle of the city like any other river, except that if you leaned over the railings of the Wether Street bridge you might see your reflection blink a half-second late, or wear a crown you didn't own. The magistrates' hall flew no banners at all; the Guild's had come down in the night, torn by citizens who were remarkably thorough about finding every last scrap of green-and-silver cloth.

And into the vacuum walked the underground courts — the ones Briar of the Alder-court, exile and outcast and former prisoner of salt and iron, had shown Tamsin in the days when we were still gathering shards and losing pieces of ourselves to do it. The justices of the low rooms. The wronged and the lawyers-of-last-resort. People who had spent years administering justice outside the law because the law belonged to the Guild, and now found themselves, blinking and bewildered, *being* the law.

I went with them to their first open session, three days after the binding, held in the same cellar off Cooper's Row where the courts had always hidden, because tradition dies hard and because the benches were already carved with names.

The first petitioner was mortal: a cooper's widow whose husband's shadow had been confiscated by a Guild assessor eleven years ago, filed as *unclaimed goods*. Under the new covenant, with the border open and the realms in treaty-talk, such things could be returned. The justices heard her case, weighed it, and voted unanimously — mortal and fae sitting side by side, which drew a murmur from the gallery even now — to send word across the light-river to the Otherworld's own new arbiters, requesting the shadow's release.

It took four days. When the widow's shadow came back to her, crossing the seam on its own small legs like a dog that had wandered too far and too long, half the city turned out to watch. She knelt down in the street and embraced it, and it wrapped around her shoulders like a shawl, and I stood at the back of the crowd with tears in my eyes again, sap and honey, embarrassing everyone.

That was when I understood what Tamsin had actually built. Not peace — peace is a mood, and moods pass. He had built *procedure*. A way for two worlds to argue without killing each other. A table where grievances could be laid down instead of sharpened.

The Guild had never understood this, even at the end. Especially at the end.

* *

Ah, the Guild.

I take a certain vicious pleasure in this part, so forgive me if I savour it.

Corvane was dust — unmade by his own counterfeit covenant, pulled apart by the borders he had tried to weld into a throne. But an organization does not die with its master. An organization dies the way an elm dies: slowly, from the extremities inward, one dead branch at a time, until someone leans a ladder against it and the ladder goes through.

The branches began dying within the week.

The bottling-houses closed first. With the realms in open contact, a bottled storm was worth exactly what it had always been worth — nothing — because anyone who wanted weather could simply walk across the seam and ask the Otherworld's storm-wardens, politely, and be answered. The market for stolen shadows collapsed the same day the first repatriation was announced; who buys a stolen thing when the theft itself is about to become a crime prosecuted jointly by two worlds? The Guild's ledger-clerks, those meticulous cataloguers of impossible things — trained, some of them, by the very masters who had hunted Tamsin through the borderlands — began burning records in the yards behind their exchange-halls, and the smoke over Greyharrow smelled of ink and panic.

Some of the masters fought. There was an ugly night at the Saltgate exchange, when three senior factors barricaded themselves in the vaults with the last of the hoarded shard-pieces — fragments of the old Oath, the ones Corvane hadn't claimed — and threatened to speak them raw, to tear a new wound in the border out of pure spite. They were talked down, of all things. Talked down by a deputation from the underground courts, mortal and fae, who stood outside the vault doors and explained, patiently, at tedious length, precisely what would happen to them under the joint statutes, and offered them the one thing the Guild had never offered anyone:

A hearing.

Two of the three surrendered. The third tried to cut his own name out of the world with shard-speech and succeeded only in cutting his tongue, and he lived, mute, to face the tribunals. I visited him once, in the weeks after. I am not sure why. Perhaps because I remembered the cell he had once signed papers about, the one with the salt in the walls, and I wanted to look at him through bars and feel — something. Triumph, maybe.

I felt mostly tired. And a little sorry. He had spent his whole life keeping accounts of impossible things, and in the end the only impossible thing left to him was the possibility of being forgiven.

The Guild's monopoly died not with a battle but with a price list. Once travel across the seam was free — truly free, governed by treaty and consent rather than toll and terror — everything the Guild had ever sold became either worthless or contraband, and usually both. Within a month, their exchange-halls stood empty, their doors chalk-marked by children playing games with names the children didn't fully understand yet. *Corvane's ghost lives here,* said one scrawl I passed on my walks. Beneath it, in another hand: *No he doesn't. Nothing lives here.*

From the mouths of babes, as the mortals say.

* *

Not everything healed cleanly. Let me be honest about that, because hope that ignores its wounds is just decoration.

The hollow knights — the dead swordspeakers the Guild had puppeted for a century — did not simply lie down when their strings were cut. Most did, thank the roots: freed by Tamsin's mercy in the borderlands, they got the death they had been denied, and we buried them with honours from both realms, twelve graves dug beside the old eleven, the whole sad company finally assembled and finally at rest. But two survived as free beings, awake in bodies that had been dead for decades, and waking is its own kind of wound. One of them, a woman named Serane who had been a knight of the second oathing before any living soul was born, took to walking the seam-road at dusk, relearning how to hunger, how to tire, how to belong to a body instead of merely operating one. I walked with her sometimes. She said the strangest thing to me, one evening, watching the light-river go gold.

"I spent ninety years being used," she said, "and I remember all of it, the way you remember a book you read. At one remove. Now I'm *in* it — in the pain and the cold and the wanting supper — and honestly? I wouldn't go back to the remove. Being used doesn't hurt. Being alive hurts. I recommend it."

Mortals say the Folk cannot understand mortality. Mortals are frequently wrong.

And the Otherworld had its own convalescence. The blighted edge did not bloom back overnight; blight is patient and healing is slow, and there are places at the far border where the grey will stay for a hundred years, monuments to what compulsion cost. But the *spread* stopped. The land drank the new covenant the way drought-crust drinks rain — greedily, with cracks and groans, green returning along the watercourses first and working outward. My own court, the Alder-court that had cast me out three centuries ago as cursed, sent envoys to find me in the spring, standing awkwardly under my alder above Greyharrow with formal apologies and offers of reinstatement.

I told them I would consider it. I am still considering it. There is a particular satisfaction in making the proud wait.

* *

By midsummer, Greyharrow had a new name on the maps, though nobody had officially voted for it. The treaty-city. Delegations came from both realms — mortal chancellery men in their grey wool, fae envoys in bark and moth-wing, each side convinced the other was going to be impossible, each side discovering, with visible surprise, that the other was merely *difficult*, which is a much more workable problem.

The negotiations happened everywhere and nowhere: in the magistrate's hall, in the cellar courts, in taverns on the Wether Street bridge with one foot in each world. There were shouting matches. There were standoffs over water-rights and dream-tithes and the proper legal status of a mortal who has eaten Otherworld fruit (a question with a longer answer than you would believe). There were days when I despaired entirely, listening to two delegations argue for nine hours about the definition of the word *border*, and then there were evenings when something small and enormous got settled — a shared ferry schedule, a joint school charter, an agreement that stolen memories would be traced and returned regardless of which realm's thieves had taken them — and I would walk home under two skies feeling the future arrive not with trumpets but with paperwork.

Paperwork. The great beast slain by paperwork. Somewhere, Corvane is spinning.

Tamsin appeared at the negotiations sometimes. I say *appeared* because that remained the honest verb for him. After the binding he was — diminished is the wrong word; *concentrated* is nearer it. He had poured his swordspeech into the new covenant, and with it, nearly, himself; Vespera had burned her last substance anchoring his identity while he swore, and what came out the other side of that transaction was a thin, quiet man with a ledger-keeper's stoop and an occasional look of reaching for something that wasn't there anymore. His mother's face was gone from him entirely; I watched him learn that, and I do not write down what it did to me. Some prices should not be itemized.

But he came to the sessions, when he was able, and sat at the back, and listened, and occasionally — this was the part that undid people — he was asked for his opinion, and gave it, and it counted, not because he was the oath-sworn saviour of two worlds but because he was good at ledgers. He understood accounts. He understood that every agreement is a balance, every concession a debit against some future need, and that trust is simply the credit both parties extend each other between entries.

Once, a fae envoy — young, arrogant, fresh from a court that still measured worth in centuries — sneered openly at him during a recess. "That one," the envoy said to me, nodding at Tamsin bent over his notes. "He gave up his power. Gave up everything. For *this*." A gesture at the treaty-table, the scattered papers, the bickering.

"Yes," I said.

"Was it worth it?"

I looked at Tamsin. He had caught us watching and raised his head, and he smiled — tired, wry, entirely himself, whoever exactly that was now — and went back to his columns.

"He'd tell you," I said, "that worth isn't something you're given. It's something you enter into the book yourself, in your own hand, and stand behind later. But between us? Watch how the room listens to him. He has less power than anyone in this building and more authority than anyone alive. You work that out, envoy, and you'll have learned the whole lesson of the war we didn't have."

The young one frowned. I like to think it took root.

* *

Autumn came, the first autumn of the new world, and I felt it turn inside me alongside the spring I still carried — the strange doubled season of a fae who had been winter for three hundred years and was now, absurdly, verdant. I split my days between my alder and the city. I took a position advising the joint tribunal on fae customary law, which mostly consisted of telling mortals that their instinct to put everything in writing was adorable and their instinct to swear oaths casually was going to get someone killed, and being listened to, which after centuries of exile was a drug I did not intend to quit.

And on an evening in late October, with the light-river running amber between the lamplights of two worlds, I climbed my alder one more time and looked down at the treaty-city, and let myself feel it — not analyze it, not ledger it, just feel it — the weary, earned, unglamorous hope of the thing.

Smoke from mortal chimneys mixing with the blue pollen of Otherworld hearths. A fae child and a mortal child arguing over a game on the Wether Street bridge, using a language made of both tongues and neither. The dark windows of the old exchange-hall, chalk-scrawled, already becoming just a building, which is the kindest fate I can imagine for it. Somewhere below, Tamsin Eastwick lighting a lamp in a small office over a stationer's shop, where — he had told me, shyly, the week before — he meant to open a new kind of establishment. Not a rare exchange. A common one. Ledgers, blank ones, for anyone who wished to keep accounts of ordinary things: debts entered honestly, forgivenesses recorded, terms written by the people who had to live under them.

He had asked me, haltingly, if I would come to the opening. As a friend. He used the word *friend* the way a man uses a tool he hasn't picked up in years — carefully, checking it for rust.

I had said yes before he finished the sentence.

So there I sat, in my doubled season, watching the lamps come on across two worlds that now had each other's addresses, and I confess I indulged in the most dangerous activity available to someone of my age and history. I imagined tomorrow. A whole shelf of tomorrows. Negotiations and harvests and funerals and festivals, arguments about borders and definitions, the endless unheroic carpentry of peace — and I wanted all of it. Every mundane plank.

Which was, of course, when I saw the light.

Not in the city. Beyond it — out along the seam-line, where the border ran away eastward into the hills, a point of white burning in the dusk like a star fallen and refusing to lie down. It pulsed. Twice. Three times. A rhythm I almost recognized, a rhythm like speech, like a word being said over and over into the empty country where the old Oath used to live.

Nothing should have been speaking there. The shards were gathered, the covenant sworn, the swordspeech silent forever.

I slid down from my alder, heart hammering green sap, and went to find Tamsin — because whatever had just lit a candle in the corpse of the old Oath, I had a terrible, rising certainty that it was speaking to him.
