## 40. The Empty Ledger

The morning came to Greyharrow the way all mornings came now — from both directions at once.

I stood at the window of my rooms above the old counting-house and watched the sun rise twice: once over the mortal rooftops to the east, gilding chimney smoke and gull wings, and once, fainter and stranger, through the seam-glass in the west, where the Otherworld's light filtered through like memory through water. Two dawns. One city. It had taken three months for anyone to stop flinching at it.

My hands were empty.

That was still the first thing I noticed, every morning. Not the scar where the drowned chapel's word had cut me, not the pale line across my palm where Ysolde's shard had kissed me before passing on. Just the emptiness — a hollow where swordspeech had lived, the way a missing tooth keeps its own geography in the mouth. I would reach for a word out of habit, some sharp syllable that could become an edge, and find nothing there. Nothing but Tamsin Eastwick, keeper of ledgers, a man of no particular power and, for the first time in his life, no particular debt.

I put the kettle on. Small rituals had become sacred ones.

* * *

The Guild's counting-house had been sold at auction for less than the cost of its own locks. I bought it with the last of the money Corvane had once paid me for cataloging bottled storms — a fitting irony, Briar said, that I should buy my freedom with wages earned keeping his books. The vaults beneath were still scorch-marked from the counterfeit mending; I had them sealed rather than repaired. Some wounds are better remembered than erased.

But the front room, with its long cedar counters and its high windows, was sound. And into that room, on the first grey morning of autumn, I carried a single new ledger bound in undyed calfskin, its pages blank as fresh snowfall.

Briar found me there at midday, running my thumb along the paper's edge the way I used to test the temper of a blade.

"You've started," she said. It wasn't a question. She moved through mortal spaces more easily now, though she still kept her thorns — they'd grown back softer since the blight receded from the Otherworld's edge, more ornament than armament. "The whole city's been asking when the famous Eastwick would open his doors."

"The famous Eastwick has nothing to sell."

"Good." She settled onto a counter, scattering dust motes into the twin light. "Worst thing you could do is sell something."

"It's a ledger house, Briar. Selling is rather the point of ledgers."

"Is it?" She picked up the pen I'd laid out — goose-quill, mortally ordinary — and turned it in her fingers. "What did Corvane use his ledgers for? Recording what things were worth so he could take them. What did the Guild record? Debts. Obligations. Who owed whom, who could be squeezed." She set the pen down with deliberate gentleness. "You told me once that a covenant sworn by compulsion isn't a covenant at all. Maybe a ledger isn't a ledger either, depending on what you write in it."

I looked at the open book. The first page waited, white and patient.

"And what do I write in it?"

She shrugged, thorns glinting. "That's the terrifying part, isn't it? Nobody's going to tell you."

* * *

Vespera came at dusk.

She was thinner now — I don't know how else to say it. A ghost is made of substance the way a candle is made of wax, and she had burned most of hers shielding us at the seam-gate, and more again anchoring me while I swore the new Oath and felt my own name slipping loose from my grip like water. What remained of her was a figure drawn in silver ink on grey paper: visible clearest at twilight, faintest at noon, and lately — though I hadn't said this to anyone — translucent at the edges even in her best hours.

She materialized by the window, where the second sunset was pooling, and looked at the ledger without surprise. Of course she wasn't surprised. She'd read the whole story a hundred years before any of us lived it.

"You wrote the ending differently," I said.

"I wrote *an* ending." Her voice had always sounded like pages turning; now it sounded like pages turning somewhere else in the house. "Prophecy is just a draft, Tamsin. I thought you'd have learned that by now. You rewrote mine thoroughly enough."

"I couldn't have done it without your book."

"My book is done." She said it simply, and something in my chest folded. She saw it fold, because she saw everything — she'd been reading me since the borderland, since the road that remembered, since the night a terrified clerk walked through a door that shouldn't exist with nothing but a stolen shadow's bill of sale in his pocket. "Don't make that face. Books end. That's what makes them books and not prisons. Mine ended the way I wanted it to: wrong, mostly, and then corrected."

"Ysolde would be proud of you."

It was the first time I'd said her sister's name aloud in Vespera's presence since the grave. The ghost went very still, the way candles go still when a door closes. Then, softly: "Ysolde would say I took far too long about it. She'd be right. A hundred years of trying to mend what we broke, when the mending was only ever going to come from someone willing to write a new thing entirely." She drifted closer to the ledger. "May I?"

"It's your house too, in a way. You haunted it long enough."

She laughed — a thin sound, but real — and extended a hand made of dusk toward the first page. Where her fingers passed over the paper, they left no mark. She had nothing left to write with. We both understood what she was showing me, and neither of us said it.

"I came to close the account," she said instead. "The prophecy-book. It's finished — truly finished, the binding is coming apart, the words are fading off the pages like frost in sun. When a prophecy completes or fails, the book wants to rest. I want to let it." She looked at me. "But it's yours by inheritance. The twelfth name. So it's your hand that closes it, if it's to be closed."

"Where is it?"

"In your strongroom. Where I left it the night the Oath woke." A pause. "Tamsin. When you do it — don't read the last page. I wrote it a century ago and it's embarrassing. All prophets are embarrassments at their own endings."

* * *

I went down to the strongroom alone, carrying a lamp, because some errands should not be witnessed even by fae friends.

The prophecy-book lay on the shelf where it had rested through everything — through the hollow knights and the market at the world's edge, through Corvane's counterfeit covenant screaming reality into knots, through the duel fought with spoken words as much as steel. It was smaller than I remembered. A humble object: cracked leather, brass corners gone green, the spine worn soft by a ghost's century of anxious handling. On its cover, the title she'd given it had faded to almost nothing: *When the Oath Wakes Again.*

I sat on the strongroom floor with the lamp between my feet, and I did not obey her.

I read the last page.

The ink had gone brown and ghostly, but the hand was unmistakably Vespera's — the same precise clerk's script she'd used to inscribe tonight's date beside my name in a ledger a century dead, the script that had pulled my orderly life apart at the seams. The words were few:

*And the last swordspeaker will refuse both mending and breaking, and will swear a new thing in his own name, and the realms will hold — not because they must, but because they choose to. And he will be ordinary afterward, which is to say free. And I, who watched him fail and falter and fear everything, will rest, having been — at the very last, against all my expectations — proud.*

Below it, smaller, newer-looking, one more line in shakier letters:

*He never follows instructions. Good.*

I closed the book. My hands were steady. That surprised me; grief and peace feel similar in the hands.

"There," I said aloud, to the empty room, to the fading presence above it. "Account closed."

I carried the book up into the twilight, and together — Vespera watching, Briar pretending not to watch from the doorway, the two freed hollow knights standing solemn as mourners in the corner where they still lingered sometimes, uncertain how to be men again — we gave it to the fire in the counting-house hearth. It burned slowly, the way true things burn. As the pages blackened, the words rose off them in sparks: twelve names, eleven graves, one smudged line that was never smudged after all but only waiting. Ash that had been prophecy settled among ash that had been oak, indistinguishable, finished.

Vespera stood before the fire a long moment. "Well," she said. "That's that."

"That's that," I agreed.

"Tamsin." She turned. In the firelight she was almost solid — the brightest I'd seen her since the seam-gate, and I understood, with a cold clarity I didn't permit myself to voice, that she had spent the last of her substance to be this present for this hour. "I need to tell you what happens to ghosts when their accounts close. Before you argue with me. Because I know you, and you'll argue."

"Don't—"

"Hush. Let me finish; I spent a hundred years being interrupted by better people than you." Her smile was the dry, wry blade it had always been. "A ghost is a sentence the world hasn't finished hearing. When the sentence ends, the speaker rests. There's nothing tragic in it. I watched my sister die badly and I watched her work live past her, and I have spent a century learning the difference between an ending and a completion. This—" she gestured at the embers, the settling ash, the quiet room, the two dawns' worth of light dying gently into evening "—this is a completion. Don't you dare waste it on mourning."

Briar had gone to stand by the door, head bowed. Even the former knights had lowered theirs.

"What am I supposed to do," I asked, "with all these blank pages, if everyone who taught me to write keeps leaving?"

"Write," Vespera said. "Obviously. Honestly, Tamsin, for a keeper of ledgers you ask remarkably stupid questions." She stepped forward and laid her dusk-made hand against my cheek, and it weighed nothing, and it weighed everything. "You were never made to be a swordspeaker. That was Ysolde's blood doing what blood does. But the ledgers — the care of accounts, the belief that every small thing deserves to be recorded truthfully — that was always yours. Yours alone. No prophecy touched it. No oath required it. Go be what you already were."

She stepped back into the firelight, growing finer and fainter, like a watermark surfacing on paper and sinking again. The last I clearly heard of her was laughter — genuine, unburdened, the laugh of a woman finally setting down a hundred years of carrying — and then the fire flared once, green and gold, and settled, and the room held only the living.

On the hearthstone, where she had stood, lay a single pressed flower from the Otherworld's recovered spring, silver-petaled, unfading. Briar picked it up and put it in my hand without a word.

* * *

The ledger house opened on the first of Wintermark, in snow.

We called it the Registry of Free Accounts, though Greyharrow immediately shortened it to "Eastwick's," because cities name things the way rivers name valleys — by wear. Its purpose defied easy explanation, which was precisely why it thrived. Anyone could come and record anything truthfully: debts entered *and* forgiven in the same hand, treaties between mortal fishwives and fae river-keepers drafted in duplicate, apologies witnessed, inheritances disputed and resolved, the terms of marriages and of partings, the names of the dead spoken properly at last — including two former hollow knights who spent their first free winter writing down everything they could remember of the men they'd been before the Guild hollowed them, so that someone besides themselves would know those men had existed.

No fees. No obligations. Only rules, three of them, carved into the lintel where all who entered had to pass beneath:

*Nothing written here binds anyone.*
*Everything written here belongs to everyone named in it.*
*No entry may be erased — only answered.*

Corvane's surviving masters had hated it on sight, which Briar considered its finest recommendation. The Guild itself was dissolving the way old ice dissolves — not dramatically, but steadily, its monopoly broken by open contact between the realms, its warehouses of impossible things parceled out to the markets of two worlds, its hoarded shard-pieces surrendered to the joint custody of Greyharrow's treaty-council and buried, at Ysolde's grave, under a stone that finally bore her full name. Corvane himself I did not think about often. Pity had come to me easily enough in the duel's aftermath, but pity is not the same as company, and I did not keep his account anymore.

Some evenings I still reached for a word that would become a blade. Habit, only habit. And each time, the emptiness answered, and each time the emptiness felt less like loss and more like a door left open — the shape of a choice I had made, holding.

On the eve of the registry's first year, I stayed late after closing. Snow ticked at the high windows. The twin lights — mortal lamplight, fae gloamlight — mingled on the cedar counters the way everything mingled now in Greyharrow, imperfectly and on negotiated terms, which is the only way anything true ever mingles.

I opened the second ledger. The first was nearly full: a year of the world recording itself, in forty languages and at least six that had no mortal name, entries crossed out and answered and argued over in margins, the great treaty itself copied out in full alongside a child's drawing of her fae neighbor with too many eyes and a smile. A world writing its own terms, in its own hands, one page at a time.

The second ledger lay blank as the first had lain, a year ago. Blank as I had once been, before a date in a dead book woke and called my name.

I dipped the quill. And because I was, at the last and at the best, a keeper of ledgers — because someone ought to record it, and I had earned the right to be that someone — I wrote the opening entry of the new book, in a plain hand, with no power in it but attention:

*Wintermark 2nd. All accounts current. Nothing owed. The realms hold by consent, renewed daily, witnessed here.*

*Entered by: Tamsin Eastwick. No title. None needed.*

I set down the pen and looked a long while at what I'd written — at the name, my name, chosen and kept and mine — and then, from somewhere in the rafters where the smoke gathered, faint as a watermark rising on old paper, I heard it: pages turning. Once. Softly. Like approval. Like a story, well and truly done, beginning to be read by someone else entirely.
