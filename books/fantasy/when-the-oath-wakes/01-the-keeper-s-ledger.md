## 1. The Keeper's Ledger

The trouble with guarding a door that never opened was that nobody ever thanked you for it.

Paloma Zhou had been Keeper of the Hollow Gate for six years now — the youngest in the Order's history, a distinction Bram liked to mention at every opportunity and she had learned to mention never. Six years of nightly rounds. Six years of sealing hairline cracks with mortar mixed to her own recipe (three parts lime, one part ash, one part mountain rue, and if the old manuals disagreed with her, the old manuals could file a complaint). Six years of cataloguing smuggler trails that appeared on the eastern scree like mushrooms after rain, and being thanked by no one, because the only person who read her ledger was Bram, and Bram's praise ran along the lines of *hm* and *adequate* and once, memorably, *you spelled "cairn" correctly this time.*

She wrote it all down anyway. That was the job. The ledger came first.

She sat cross-legged on the keeper's stone — a flat slab worn smooth by a century of keepers' backsides before hers — with the ledger open across her knees and the lantern turned low so its glow wouldn't carry down into the valley. The Hollow Gate rose above her like a held breath: an arch of grey stone twice the height of a man, set into the shoulder of Mount Cael, its passage packed with rubble and sealed mortar and, according to the oldest records, three iron bars that had rusted into legend. No light came through it. No sound. It was the most patient thing Paloma had ever known, and she had spent six years trying not to think about what it was waiting for.

*Entry forty-one,* she wrote. *New trail, east scree, below the split boulder. Boot prints, two sets, fresh since last night. One walker heavy on the left heel. They're using the goat path again, which means they've talked to someone who knows the goat path, which means I need to have a word with the Ferrows about their hired hands.*

She paused, considered, and added: *Also the mortar patch on the third course is holding well. For the record. Since apparently I am the only one who keeps records.*

The wind came up the valley smelling of pine and coming snow, and somewhere below, a nightjar called its two-note question into the dark. Paloma pulled her coat tighter and let herself do the thing she was not supposed to do, which was look at the Gate the way you'd look at a locked room in your own house — the way you look at anything you've been told, firmly and repeatedly, not to wonder about.

What was behind it? Everyone knew what was behind it. The fae dominion, the fading courts, the wild country where the seasons had gone strange. Everyone knew why it was sealed: the covenant, sworn a hundred years ago, closing the passage for the good of both realms. Mercy, the wardens called it. Kindness. The gate closed so that neither side could hurt the other again.

It was a beautiful story. Paloma had grown up on it, the way other children grew up on lullabies.

She just wished, some nights, that anyone would tell her where the story's edges were. What exactly had been broken? Who had sworn, and who had witnessed, and why did the restricted folio in the archive have pages cut out with a knife rather than torn with haste? Why did every keeper before her sign the same oath without asking what, precisely, they were keeping?

*You guard the door,* Bram always said. *You don't have to be the door's biographer.*

But someone had to be. If not her, then who?

She heard him coming before she saw him — the particular arrhythmic crunch of a man whose left knee had opinions about stairs. Bram came up the switchback trail with his lantern swinging and his coat buttoned wrong, as usual, one end higher than the other, so that he looked perpetually mid-argument with his own clothing.

"You're late," Paloma said, not looking up from the ledger.

"I'm punctual. You're early. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of us has sense." He lowered himself onto the keeper's stone with a grunt that suggested the knee had won tonight's argument. He peered at her page upside down. "Hm."

"That's two *hm*s in one week. I'm framing them."

"Forty-one trails this season?" He tapped the entry with one blunt finger. "That's up from thirty-three."

"Thirty-eight," Paloma said. "And yes. Someone's organizing them. Smugglers don't coordinate like this on their own; they're lazy by trade. Somebody's selling routes."

Bram grunted — a third *hm*, practically a sonnet — and said nothing, which was itself an answer, though she couldn't yet read it. He did that. He answered questions with silences shaped almost exactly like answers, and then acted wounded when she noticed.

They sat together while she finished the entries. This was the ritual of it, most nights: her writing, his silence, the wind, the Gate. She had thought, when she was young and newly oathed, that companionship would be the least part of the work. Now she knew it was most of it. A post like this didn't run on duty alone. It ran on somebody being willing to sit in the cold next to you and hand you the wax when the seal stick ran short.

Which made what she was about to do feel, unpleasantly, like picking a lock in front of the locksmith.

"Bram," she said, carefully casual, which he would see through in a heartbeat, "the archive stove's been smoking again. I went up to check the flue today."

"Mm." He was watching the Gate. He always watched the Gate, even now, even after forty years, as though it might do something. As though hoping it would, or fearing it — she'd never decided which.

"The flue passes right over the restricted shelf."

The silence that followed had a shape. She'd known it would.

"Did you find anything warm enough to burn your fingers on?" he asked mildly.

"One book." She kept her eyes on the ledger. "Older than the others. Bound in grey leather. The prophecy folio."

Bram exhaled through his nose, slow, the way he did when the knee complained or when she mentioned her mother. "Paloma."

"It's falling apart. Nobody's conserved it. Nobody's even dusted it. If we're going to keep it locked away like a relic, we could at least keep it like a *maintained* relic—"

"No."

"—because right now we're keeping it like something we're ashamed of, and I'd like to know which it is."

He turned to look at her then, and in the lantern light his face was doing the thing it did when he was deciding how much truth a situation could hold without spilling. She had seen that face over smuggler reports and warden inspections and the time she'd fallen through the ice on the cistern pond. It was the face of a man measuring rope.

"The folio is restricted," he said, "because the Order restricts it. That's the whole of my knowledge and the whole of my instruction, and I'll thank you not to make me choose between my oath and my fondness for you, because you will not enjoy the outcome."

"You've read it, though."

A pause. "I've *seen* it."

"You've seen all of it?"

Another pause, longer. The nightjar called again, closer, impatient.

"There are pages missing," Bram said at last, heavily, as though each word cost a coin. "There have always been pages missing. Before my time. Before my teacher's time. And the Order does not grieve them, which tells you everything, and I have spent forty years telling myself it tells me nothing. That is the arrangement, child. The arrangement holds."

"The arrangement holds," Paloma repeated. She looked at the Gate — grey, silent, sealed, patient. "Do you ever wonder what it costs us, the arrangement holding?"

"Every day." He said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse than any argument. Then he reached over and closed the ledger gently on her fingers. "Finish your rounds. Check the north crack — the frost's been working at it, I could hear it ticking from the trail. And Paloma." He waited until she met his eyes. "Stay out of the archive. Not for the Order's sake. For yours. Some doors are easier closed than opened, and some are easier never questioned at all."

"You say that to a woman whose entire job is a door."

"Yes," said Bram, standing with the groan of a man twice his age, which was nearly accurate. "I know exactly what I married you to."

"You *apprenticed* me to, and I was fourteen and flattered, which makes it your fault entirely."

He snorted, and some of the weight went out of the air between them, the way it always did, the way she suspected he engineered. He took the switchback down toward the keeper's cottage with his lantern bobbing, and Paloma watched him go and felt the familiar tangle of love and irritation and suspicion that she had long ago stopped trying to untangle.

* * *

The north crack ticked.

She heard it before she saw it — a small bright sound, like a fingernail against slate, repeating at intervals as the frost expanded inside the seam. Up close, the crack ran along the Gate's northern jamb, thin as a hair, weeping a rime of white. It hadn't been there last week. Or it had, and smaller, and she had been too busy counting bootprints to notice it growing.

Paloma knelt and put her palm flat against the stone. Cold, but not dead-cold. Stone-cold. She told herself that firmly, the way you tell yourself the noise in the night is the house settling.

*Mortar,* she ordered herself. *Mix it. Seal it. Write it down. That's the whole of the world's problems you can solve tonight.*

She worked by feel more than sight, pressing the grey paste deep into the seam with a bone spatula worn smooth by four generations of keepers. Her grandmother had used this spatula. Her grandmother had knelt on this exact stone, sealing this exact arch, and had never once — according to any record Paloma could find — written down what lay behind it. A hundred years of keepers, and not one of them curious, or not one of them permitted to be, and Paloma genuinely did not know which possibility frightened her more.

The crack stopped ticking as the mortar filled it. Small victory. She logged it: *Entry forty-two. North jamb, hairline fissure, sealed. Frost active. Recommend inspection in three days.*

Then, because the night was clear and the work was done and no one was watching, she allowed herself the indulgence she allowed herself perhaps once a month: she climbed the maintenance stair to the top of the arch and stood with her hand on the keystone.

From here she could see both countries. South, the human realm of Thornfell: valley smoke, lamplit windows, the black stitch of the river, winter coming down off the peaks in visible sheets of wind-blown snow. North, beyond the Gate, nothing — just the mountain's shoulder falling away into country no map had charted honestly in a century. But on nights like this, when the wind came from the north, she could smell it. Green things. Water over stones. A season that wasn't winter, leaking faintly through a hundred years of mortar, like a letter slipped under a door.

The fae realm was dying. Everyone said so. The courts gone quiet, the seasons tangled, the old powers fading like coals. And everyone said it was sad, the way you say a distant war is sad, and everyone agreed the covenant was mercy, and nobody — *nobody* — would answer the only question that mattered:

If the gate was closed for both realms' good, why did only one of them seem to be starving?

The keystone was cold under her palm. Warmer than the jambs, though. She'd noticed that years ago and never written it in the ledger, because there was no column for it, because there was no sensible explanation, because some observations you keep in the place where you keep the memory of your mother's voice and the smell of the house you grew up in — the place where things go that don't fit anywhere else.

The keystone was warm, and the Gate was patient, and tonight, for the first time in six years, Paloma felt the patience as something directed. Not a wall waiting. A door waiting. Waiting for *something*, on a schedule she couldn't see, and the frost ticking in the seams sounded suddenly less like weather and more like counting.

She laughed at herself, a short breath of a laugh, and climbed down. Six years of solitary posts will do that to a person. Start hearing intention in masonry. Bram would tell her to sleep more, and he'd be right, and she'd ignore him, and the arrangement would hold.

* * *

Back in the keeper's cottage, she banked the fire, hung her coat, and set the ledger on its shelf between its fifty-nine predecessors — a century of nightly entries, spine after identical spine, the whole history of the Hollow Gate reduced to weather, mortar, and bootprints.

Except.

She stood there a long moment, firelight moving on the spines, listening to the wind test the shutters.

Except that the restricted folio was still in the archive, and the archive stove was cold now, and Bram was asleep, and she had promised — not in words, but in the shape of her silence — to stay out.

Paloma Zhou had kept every oath she'd ever taken. It was, she sometimes thought, the only interesting thing about her. She guarded doors. She kept ledgers. She sealed cracks. She did not open things she'd been told not to open.

She got the folio anyway.

Not tonight to read it — she told herself that, and mostly believed it — but to conserve it. To dust it, to check the binding, to do the work of preservation that nobody else would do. That was keeper's work. That was defensible. That was a story she could tell Bram with a straight face, and the fact that she was already composing the story proved, on some level she declined to examine, that she knew exactly what she was doing.

The archive was cold and smelled of paper and lamp oil. The folio sat where she'd found it, grey leather gone soft as cloth, its clasp a plain iron thing with no lock — because who would steal a book no one wanted?

She carried it to the lamp, opened it on the reading table, and began, professionally, gently, to turn the brittle pages.

Prophecies, it turned out, were mostly boring. Lists of signs. Weather omens. The sort of verse that sounds profound until you realize it would fit any decade you pointed it at. She skimmed, half-relieved, half-disappointed, conserving diligently, until near the back she found a page whose margins were crowded with annotations in at least four different hands — keepers, past keepers, people like her who had clearly also told themselves they were only dusting.

And there, boxed in red ink by some long-dead clerk as though the date itself were contraband, she read the line that stopped her breath:

*On the longest night of the hundredth winter, the gate wakes, and the keeper opens.*

Below it, in a different hand, older, pressed hard enough into the paper to be read from the back:

*God forgive us. It gives the date.*

Paloma turned the page with fingers that had gone suddenly clumsy.

The date was written there. Day, month, year. In a book a century old.

It was tonight.

She sat very still in the cold archive, listening to the wind, to the tick of cooling stone, to her own heart conducting its business far too loudly. Outside, the Hollow Gate stood sealed and patient under the coming snow, and somewhere south of it a nightjar sang its two notes, and none of it — none of it — sounded like weather anymore.

The first shudder came up through the floor like a knock.
