## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The ledger did not care that it was her birthday, and neither, officially, did Paloma Zhou.

She sat at the keeper's desk with a cup of tea gone lukewarm at her elbow, the Hollow Gate's nightly report open before her, and wrote in her small, exact hand: *Crack along third seal-stone, eastern face. Width of a wheat stalk. Sealed with lime mortar at dusk. Will recheck at dawn.*

It was not a thrilling entry. Most entries were not thrilling entries. In eleven years of keeping the ledger she had recorded, in order of excitement: one bear (asleep), two smugglers (lost, grateful for directions out), forty-one hairline cracks, and a single night when the gate had hummed — *hummed*, low and sweet, like a struck bell too deep to hear properly — for the space of an hour before going silent again. She had written that one up in careful language and received back from the Order a form letter thanking her for her vigilance and reminding her that the gate was inert stone and could not hum.

Paloma had kept her own copy of that entry anyway. She kept a lot of things the Order would rather she didn't, folded into the back pages of her personal journal: pressed flowers from the fae side of the pass, though no flower should grow there; a rubbing of a symbol on the gate's western face that matched nothing in any warden manual; and now, upstairs, under a floorboard she'd pried loose herself, a certain restricted folio she was absolutely, definitely not supposed to have.

Bram said she worried too much. Bram also said the gate couldn't hum, so Paloma had long ago stopped taking his reassurances at face value.

*Will recheck at dawn,* she wrote, and blotted the entry, and allowed herself one sip of cold tea in celebration of another quiet day at the end of the world.

The keeper's house clung to the mountainside just below the Hollow Gate itself, a squat stone cottage that had been added onto so many times it resembled a cairn built by someone who kept forgetting where they'd left off. It had one room for warmth, one room for ledgers, one room for sleeping, and a cellar full of mortar, salt, and Bram's truly appalling home-brewed cider. Paloma knew every board of its floors by sound. So when, at half past nine by the clock in the ledger room, she heard the front door open without a knock, she didn't reach for a weapon.

"Bram," she called, "if you've come to drink my tea and tell me I read too much, I want you to know I've prepared remarks."

No answer.

She frowned. The door opened again — no, that was wrong, the first opening had been the wind catching the latch; the second was boots on the step, heavy ones, and then Bram himself filled the doorway, snow in his white beard and his face doing something complicated.

"You're up late," he said.

"It's my birthday. I'm celebrating with paperwork." She tipped her head at him. "You're up later. You only climb up here after dark when you've done something you feel guilty about, or when you want me to do something you feel guilty about asking."

Her mentor hung his coat on the peg with exaggerated care, which was itself an answer. Sixty years of keeping the gate had made Bram a man of deliberate motions, but this was deliberation stretched over dread, like a man crossing ice he already knew was thin.

"I brought you a gift," he said.

"At half past nine. With snow still on your shoulders."

"It's a small gift."

"Bram."

"It's a book," he said, and Paloma's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth. "A boring book. A book about drainage."

"There is no book about drainage in this valley that you would carry up the mountain through snow."

He set a wrapped parcel on the desk between them, and his hands stayed on it a moment longer than they needed to, as if it might bolt. "Your grandmother gave it to me," he said quietly. "Thirty years ago, with instructions I've followed every day since. And today of all days, I find I can't keep following them anymore." He pushed it toward her. "Happy birthday, Paloma. I'm sorry in advance."

"That is the worst birthday greeting I have ever received, and once a goat ate my boot."

But her hands were already working at the twine.

Under the oilcloth was a book bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth with age, unmarked save for a symbol stamped into the cover — the same symbol she'd found rubbed into her journal, the one that matched nothing. The pages inside were dense, close-written script in faded brown-black ink, and the whole thing smelled of ash and cedar and somewhere far beneath both, faintly, of winter.

"The prophecy folio," she breathed. "The real one. Not the sanitized copy in the archive."

"There was never a sanitized copy," Bram said. "There was only this, and the lie we told everyone else. Read the seventh leaf. Then decide how angry you are, and whether you'd rather be angry at me or at the dead."

Paloma turned leaves with fingers that had gone suddenly clumsy. One. Two. Three. The script was archaic, the spelling older still, but she'd spent years deciphering smuggler marks and boundary inscriptions, and the words came slowly, then all at once—

*And in the hundredth year the gate shall wake,*

*and the keeper who opens shall stand upon the threshold,*

*and the debt of winter shall come due,*

*and the thief's blood shall pay.*

Below, in a different, newer hand — her grandmother's hand, she realized with a lurch, she knew that slanted, impatient capital F — someone had written a date.

Tonight's date. The day, the month, the year. Inked a century past by a woman who could not possibly have known it, precise as a tax record.

The room went very quiet. Outside, the wind worried the eaves. Somewhere below the floor, beneath the cellar, beneath the mountain's roots, the Hollow Gate sat in its frame of living rock and said nothing at all.

"This is a forgery," Paloma said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because everything behind it felt like standing on a ledge. "Someone added the date later. That happens. Prophecies get edited."

"Turn the page."

"Tell me it's a forgery first."

"Turn the page, Paloma."

She turned the page. On it, in her grandmother's hand again, was a single line:

*I have checked the arithmetic four times. I pray I am wrong.*

"She was your grandmother," Bram said softly. "She was also the last keeper who read this book whole. Whatever she found in these pages frightened her enough to lock it away and spend the rest of her life making sure no one else looked." He rubbed his beard, dislodging a final clot of snow. "I thought if tonight passed quiet, I'd take it back down and we'd never speak of it. If it didn't—" He spread his hands. "Well. Happy birthday."

Paloma stared at the date. Tonight. Her ordered little world — rounds, cracks, mortar, tea, the gate that could not hum — sat around her like furniture in a flooded house, floating loose off the floor.

"It's ink," she said. "It's just ink. People write dates in books all the—"

The gate shuddered.

Not hummed. *Shuddered.* The floor kicked under her chair like something enormous turning over in its sleep; the teacup rang against its saucer; frost on the window cracked in a spreading fern pattern. Dust sifted from the rafters. Down the mountain, dogs in Corrow would be barking, if Corrow's dogs were still capable of barking and not simply sitting very still and staring north.

Paloma was moving before she'd decided to move, ledger abandoned, folio thrust at Bram, lantern snatched off its hook. "Stay here," she said, already at the door. "Or don't. But don't follow me fast."

"Paloma—"

The second shock hit as she crossed the threshold, and this one wasn't an earthquake. It was a sound. A long, groaning exhalation from the pass above, like a door that had been shut for a century remembering what hinges were for. Snow slid from the rocks in small white whispers. The lantern flame bent toward the gate, though there was no wind — bent toward it, and burned blue at the edges.

She climbed the switchback path at a pace that would have earned her a lecture from herself in calmer times. The moon was three-quarters and generous, and by its light she saw the Hollow Gate ahead: the twin seal-stones, each tall as three men, leaning together like conspirators, the seam between them sealed with a century of lime and iron and oath-work. And along that seam, glowing a faint, impossible silver, ran a crack.

Not the width of a wheat stalk. The width of a thread. But *glowing*.

"Oh no," Paloma told it conversationally, because shouting seemed undignified. "Oh no, you don't. You are inert stone. There is a form letter about this."

She got within ten paces of it before she saw the figure coming down the path on the far side of the seam — no, *through* the gap in the rocks beside it, the old smuggler cut she'd sealed twice and been defeated by thrice, a figure staggering, lurching, half-crawling, trailing darkness that even in moonlight was clearly not shadow.

Then the moon shifted, and she saw him plainly, and every story she'd ever been told stood up inside her and shouted at once.

Tall. Too tall for a man, willow-thin, with skin the pale grey-green of lichen and ears that swept back past his skull. His coat had been fine once — she could see the ghost of embroidery along the hem, worked in thread that caught light wrong, silver where it should have been gold — and it was ruined now, soaked dark from shoulder to hip. One eye was swollen shut. The other fixed on her, and it was the color of deep water under ice, and it was terrified.

"Fae," she said stupidly, because her training had failed her entirely and left her with nouns.

"Keeper." His voice was wrecked, cracked down the middle, but underneath the ruin of it she caught an accent like bells dropped in snow. He took another step and his legs resigned their commission all at once; he went down on one knee on her threshold stones, caught himself on a hand that left a smear of something too dark for blood, and looked up at her with that single burning eye. "Keeper of the Hollow Gate. Please. I know what I am. I know what you are sworn to do. But they are behind me, and they do not ask."

"Who's behind you?"

"Wardens." The word came out with a bitter twist, a man spitting out a pit. "Your wardens. Four of them, crossbows, and a captain whose name begins with V and ends in a great deal of pain."

Somewhere upslope, a bowstring sang.

The bolt took the lintel stone a hand's width above the fae's bowed head and shattered into sparks that fell blue and slow, like the gate itself had opinions about being shot at. Paloma stood between a wounded exile and the people she'd served for eleven years, on a threshold she had guarded every night of her adult life, holding a lantern that was burning the wrong color.

Down in the ledger room, she knew, lay a book with tonight's date written in a dead woman's hand. *The keeper who opens shall stand upon the threshold.*

She looked at the crack in the gate, glowing silver as a vein of moonrise. She looked at the fae bleeding on her stones. She looked up the path at the shadows resolving into men with drawn bows, and beyond them, at a tall figure in a warden captain's cloak walking unhurried through the snow.

Everything she had ever believed was true was currently lying in pieces around her, and she hadn't even finished her tea.

"Well," said Paloma Zhou to no one in particular, and knelt, and put the wounded exile's arm over her shoulders. "Happy birthday to me."

* * *

Getting him down the switchback was a study in poor decisions executed quickly. He was heavier than he looked — fae bones, apparently, were dense as riverstone — and taller than she was by half a head, which meant her shoulder fit under his armpit the way a fencepost fits under a barn. He tried, gallantly, to walk on his own leg, which he could not do, and to apologize, which he would not stop doing.

"You needn't—"

"If you say 'needn't trouble yourself' one more time I'm leaving you for the wardens, and I'll write it up honestly in the ledger."

That, remarkably, silenced him. Perhaps it was the threat of documentation. She'd found it effective on smugglers too.

She got him through the kitchen, across the flagstones, and paused at the cellar door, heart hammering. Everyone knew about the cellar. What everyone didn't know was what lay behind the second wall of the cellar, the false one, the one the smugglers had built a hundred years ago and her predecessors had pretended not to notice: a crawl-tunnel running south under the mountain, dry, hidden, and known to exactly one living person, because Paloma had never written it down. Not even in the journal. Some things you keep in your body alone.

She put him in the dark behind the false wall, propped him against the tunnel mouth, and lit no lamp, because lamplight under a door was how hiding places died. His one good eye gleamed at her out of the blackness.

"Why?" he rasped. "You don't know me. Your Order pays you to hand me over. I watched you kneel to that stone every dawn from the ridgeline for three nights — you love your post, keeper. I could see it from a mile off. Why betray it for me?"

Paloma crouched in the dark, her knees aching, her mind refusing to sit still. Above them, faintly, boots were entering her house without knocking, and Bram's voice was rising in welcome, pitched carefully loud.

"Because," she said finally, "an hour ago a book told me tonight was important, and I haven't decided yet whether to believe it. And because whatever's happening, I'd rather it happen with me choosing something than with me obeying." She stood, joints cracking. "Don't die down there. It'll ruin my ledger."

She closed the false wall over him, and went upstairs to answer her door, and found Captain Ilsa Vane of the Warden Order standing in her kitchen shaking snow from her cloak as if she owned it, while Bram hovered by the stove looking like a man watching a fire start in a library.

"Keeper Zhou," said Vane pleasantly. "We need to talk about what climbed your mountain tonight."

Paloma looked at the captain's level grey eyes, at the four hunters fanning out through her house with the ease of men who had done this before, at the stove where her kettle was beginning, absurdly, to sing.

"Tea?" she heard herself say.
