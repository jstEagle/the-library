## 3. What the Wardens Burn

The wardens came up the mountain road at dawn, and Paloma knew it before she heard the hoofbeats, because Bram stopped humming.

Her mentor hummed the way other men breathed — tuneless, constant, a low gravel of old ballads while he sharpened blades or argued with the stove or pretended not to notice her reading things she shouldn't. In nine years at Hollow Gate she had never once heard silence come off him like weather.

"They're early," he said to the window.

"Who's early?"

"The Order sends its quarterly inspection at midsummer." He turned from the glass. His face had the particular grayness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "This is not midsummer."

Paloma looked down at the folio open on her desk — the restricted prophecy, the one she'd been told three times in as many years not to read, and had read anyway, because that was rather the whole point of her. Tonight's date sat there in faded ink, a century old: *the keeper who opens.* She closed it and slid it under the loose hearthstone on instinct, then felt foolish, then felt less foolish when Bram nodded approvingly.

"Good instinct," he said. "Keep it."

The hoofbeats arrived. Six riders in slate-and-iron, and at their head a woman whose posture made the mountain look slouched.

* * *

Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane dismounted without touching her horse's flank, which was how Paloma knew the animal was trained to bite. The captain's cloak carried the road-smell of three days' hard riding and something else beneath it — ash, faint but unmistakable. Wardens burned what they confiscated. Everyone at the gate knew that much. Nobody talked about it.

"Keeper Zhou." Vane's voice was dry as ledger paper. "You look surprised."

"You look like you've ridden through the night, Captain."

"We have. May we come in? My men will inspect the grounds."

"Of course," said Paloma, who had learned young that the fastest way to keep people out of a room was to invite them into all the others. "Mind the third step in the hall, it complains."

Vane did not smile. Vane, as far as Paloma could tell, had never smiled, though there were rumors among the older keepers about a harvest festival some years back where she'd allegedly laughed for four full seconds. Paloma privately doubted them. Some people were built like doors that only opened outward, and only from the inside.

In the great room, Vane stood over the fire and said, without preamble: "There is a fae in your keeping."

The words landed in Paloma's chest like a dropped stone. She thought of Sef — bleeding on her threshold two nights ago, gray-skinned and shaking, whispering that hunters wore slate and iron — currently wrapped in a blanket behind the false wall of the root cellar, eating the last of the smoked trout and complaining, in a hoarse whisper, about the state of human cuisine.

"I don't know what you mean," Paloma said.

"Keeper." Vane turned. For the first time she looked tired, and the tiredness frightened Paloma more than any threat could have. "I have been doing this work for twenty years. I have stood where you stand, guarding this gate, believing the covenant held because I held it. I am asking you — once, keeper to warden — do not make me search."

"And if I say no one's here?"

"Then I search, and I find him, and everything after that happens worse."

Bram cleared his throat from the doorway. "Captain, the girl's kept this gate clean for nine years. If there were fae sign on the mountain, she'd have logged it."

"There is fae sign on the mountain," Vane said flatly. "Blood on the north threshold stone. Fresh. Yours?"

"A stag," Paloma said. "Caught in the wire."

"In the wire." Vane looked at her for a long moment, and Paloma understood, with a lurch, that the captain did not believe her — that the captain had never believed anyone in her life, and had simply built a career out of acting as though she did until the paperwork agreed. "Fetch the ledger, Keeper. All of it."

Paloma fetched the ledger. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside her, something was moving furniture around, quietly panicking, while the rest of her stood in the lamplight being the competent keeper everyone expected. It was an odd sensation — watching herself lie well and hating how well she lay.

* * *

They searched for two hours. Paloma walked Vane through the rounds herself, narrating smuggler trails, hairline cracks, the hairline crack she'd sealed just last week with mortar and prayer. Behind them, six wardens moved through the gatehouse like a slow tide, opening cupboards, tapping walls.

The false cellar wall was Bram's idea, forty years old, older than Paloma. It had hidden deserters during the Border Wars. It hid a wounded exile now, and if a warden tapped it right, it would answer hollow.

So Paloma did the only thing she could think of: she got in front of it.

"—and this is the cellar," she said, too loudly, standing squarely between Vane and the wall. "Root vegetables, mostly. Turnips. We're very proud of the turnips."

Vane looked at her.

"Turnips," said Vane.

"The mountain soil is generous."

"Keeper Zhou, are you standing in front of that wall on purpose?"

"No," said Paloma, sweating. "I'm standing here because it's the next stop on the tour."

Behind Vane, one of the younger wardens raised his knuckles toward the wall — and Bram saved them, or damned them, by dropping an entire tray of tankards in the kitchen with a crash like the end of the world. Every head turned. By the time it turned back, Paloma had shifted half a step, casually, and the young warden rapped the wall twice and heard nothing but solid stone, because fate, apparently, still owed her small favors.

"Hollow," Vane said suddenly, from across the room. She'd stopped at the hearth. At the loose hearthstone.

Paloma's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth.

"That stone sits wrong," Vane said, crouching.

"It's always sat wrong," Paloma said. "It's a bad stone. I've complained about it for years. There's a whole entry in the ledger."

It was true. There was. Paloma had never loved a ledger entry more.

Vane pried it up anyway. And there, beneath it, lay the folio — because Paloma had panicked, and panicked people hide things in the worst places, and the hearthstone was the worst place, and she watched twenty years of warden discipline cross Ilsa Vane's face as the captain opened the century-old prophecy and read tonight's date in ink older than either of them.

The room went very quiet.

"Where did you get this?" Vane asked softly.

"The restricted shelf," Paloma said. "Where else does one get restricted books?"

"I asked you to stop reading it. Twice. In writing."

"Yes."

"Do you know why we burn these?" Vane held the folio up. Her voice stayed level, but underneath it Paloma heard something else — not anger. Fear, worn smooth by decades of practice into something that looked like procedure. "Because every time a fool reads a prophecy, someone dies making it come true. Because this—" she tapped the page, "—is not history. It is a loaded weapon with tomorrow's date on it."

"So it's true," Paloma said. Her own voice sounded far away. "The date. The Order has known."

Vane closed the book. "The Order has known for eleven years."

Eleven years. Paloma counted backward without wanting to — eleven years ago she was fourteen, newly apprenticed, memorizing gate-laws by candlelight while somewhere in a warden archive men she'd never met read her future and decided what to do about it.

"What happens now?" Bram asked quietly. "At the date. What's the plan?"

Vane put the folio inside her coat. "Permanent sealing. Mortar, iron, consecration. The gate closes forever, the prophecy dies unfulfilled, and Thornfell keeps its peace for another thousand years."

"And the keeper?" Paloma asked. "On the sealing night. Where does the keeper stand?"

Something flickered across Vane's face — quick, quickly buried, but Paloma caught it, the way you catch movement in snow. Pity. Or its cousin.

"The keeper stands wherever the Order requires," Vane said.

*Inside*, said the flicker. *The rite requires a keeper's blood in the mortar. You know that. You've known since you took the post.*

Paloma had not known. But she believed it instantly, completely, the way you believe a trapdoor when it opens — and what astonished her most was not the betrayal itself but the fact that she wasn't even surprised. That some part of her, some quiet corner that had spent nine years guarding a door she was never allowed to question, had always suspected the door would someday be nailed shut over her.

"Well," said Bram into the silence, cheerfully, terribly, "anyone want tea before you execute my apprentice?"

* * *

They didn't find Sef. Not that day.

Vane's men searched until dark, and found blood, and found the wire with grey fur in it, and found nothing living. Vane stood in the courtyard at dusk and gave orders for a garrison — twelve wardens, permanent posting, the gate effectively under military occupation starting tomorrow — and told Paloma, in front of everyone, that the keeper would be relieved of independent patrol duties henceforth.

"Understood," Paloma said.

"You'll be safer," Vane added, and it was almost gentle, which was somehow worse.

That night, after the wardens bedded down in the barracks wing, Paloma went down to the cellar with a lamp and a plate of bread and cheese, and knocked the pattern on the false wall — three, pause, two — that Bram had taught her when she was fifteen, for reasons he'd never explained and she'd never asked about. Smuggler signs. Keeper's hands. The wall swung.

Sef sat in the dark among the turnips, gray-faced, ears sharp as folded knives, eyes catching the lamplight like a cat's.

"You brought food," he whispered. "You absolute lunatic. They're crawling all over this place."

"Eat the cheese before I change my mind." She crouched beside him. Up close he smelled of cold iron and pine sap, and his wound had crusted wrong; he needed a healer, real rest, none of which a garrisoned gatehouse could offer. "Listen carefully. Tomorrow they seal the tunnels on the south face. Day after, they'll sweep the whole pass. You can't stay."

"I gathered." He tore the bread with his teeth. "You could hand me over. Save yourself. They'd probably promote you."

"Don't tempt me, it's been a long day."

He laughed — a soft, startled sound, like something breaking ice — and then winced and pressed a hand to his side. Paloma looked at him, this stranger, this enemy-by-every-law-she-had-ever-sworn, shivering among her turnips, and felt the strange vertigo of a world tilting. Eleven years they had known. The prophecy, the date, the plan to bury her alive in mortar and call it duty. Everything she'd guarded faithfully, guarded *for*, had been planning around her the entire time.

"Bram knows the old smuggler roads," she heard herself say. "Through the mountain's spine. He taught me all of them. Every one."

"Why tell me?"

"Because in three days, when they sweep the pass, they'll be looking for a fae traveling alone." She stood, and picked up the lamp, and found her voice had gone very steady — steadier than it had any right to be. "They should be worried about the pair of us instead. Can you walk by dawn?"

Sef looked up at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop smiling.

"Keeper," he said slowly, "you don't understand what you're carrying me toward."

"Probably not," Paloma admitted. "But I'm done being the only one at this gate who isn't told anything. Dawn, tunnel three. Bring the blanket, you'll want it."

She left him the rest of the cheese, closed the wall, and climbed the stairs past the sleeping garrison — past the men who would, in three days, hunt them both — feeling the strangest thing settle over her shoulders like a second cloak. Not fear. Not quite.

It felt, she realized somewhere around the third step, like the moment before a door opens. Which was, after all, her whole profession.

In the kitchen, Bram waited up, sharpening a knife that needed no sharpening.

"Tunnel three?" he said.

"Tunnel three," said Paloma. "You're coming."

Her mentor sighed the sigh of a man whose arithmetic had finally finished, and reached for his boots.
