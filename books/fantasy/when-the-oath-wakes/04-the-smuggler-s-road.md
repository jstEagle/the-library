## 4. The Smuggler's Road

The smuggler's road began behind the gate itself, which Paloma had always found either very funny or very damning, depending on the hour.

She'd known about it since she was nine years old, when old Marta — the smuggler, not the goat, though both had been equally unrepentant — had shown her the loose slab in the keeper's cellar. "Every door has a back way, girl," Marta had said, pressing a dried plum into her hand like a bribe. "Especially holy ones. *Especially* holy ones."

Paloma had never used it. That was the point of knowing a secret: you kept it whole, like an egg you never intended to hatch. For twenty years she had walked past that slab every night on her rounds and thought of it the way she thought of her own heartbeat — present, essential, ignored.

Now she was hauling Sef through it by the wrist while warden lanterns swung above them like angry moons.

"Left," she hissed. "Not right. Right goes to the cistern and the cistern echoes."

"Your cistern echoes," Sef repeated, limping. His blood left small dark commas on the stone. "Everything about this place echoes. Your whole mountain is one enormous throat."

"Then don't give it anything to swallow."

He gave her a look — half fury, half something more wounded — but he went left.

* * *

Sef had been in tunnels before. He wanted that understood, at least by himself, if by no one else. He had crossed the drowned ways beneath the Mirefen court, where the walls wept and the dark had opinions. He had walked the bone-cold galleries under the winter palace, back when the palace still bothered to be cold instead of merely dead.

But he had never been in a tunnel built by humans who were *good* at it, and there was something deeply humiliating about being rescued through a smuggler's passage by a woman who kept stopping to check her footing out of pure habit.

"You've done this before," he said, when they paused in a junction where three passages breathed different temperatures of air.

"I've *mapped* it," Paloma said. "Doing it is new."

"Wonderful. I'm fleeing for my life with a cartographer."

"Keeper." She said it automatically, then heard how it sounded, and something flickered across her face — the first crack he'd seen in all that competence. "I was going to say keeper."

"Are you?" Sef asked. "Still?"

She didn't answer. She took the middle passage, the one that smelled faintly of pine and old woodsmoke, and he followed, because the alternative was Vane's crossbows, and because — though he would have chewed off his own wing before admitting it — following her felt less like captivity than anything had felt in thirty years.

That was the trouble with exile. You got so starved for direction that even a stranger's certainty tasted like home.

* * *

They came up out of the ground two hours later, into a fold of hillside Paloma called the Shepherd's Pocket — a hollow ringed with thornbushes, invisible from the pass below, with a view of the Hollow Gate's outer face glowing faintly in the dusk like a closed eye.

From here you could see what the gate really was: not a door in a wall, but a seam in the world. The stone around it was human-cut and honest. The stone *through* it was something else — older, veined with silver, humming at a frequency Paloma felt in her teeth.

Sef stood beside her and looked at it for a long time.

"It used to sing," he said quietly.

"What did?"

"The gate. Before your Order bricked it up. On festival nights the whole pass would carry the sound down to the low villages, and the human children on your side would put their ears to the stones to listen." He glanced at her. "You've never heard it, have you? Not once."

"No." Her voice came out flat. "I've oiled its hinges for eleven years. I've sealed forty-one cracks. I've memorized the covenant's wording in three languages. And I have never once heard it make a sound."

"That," said Sef, "is because it isn't a gate anymore. It's a scar. And scars only sing when they're healing."

* * *

They made camp in the Pocket because Paloma refused to travel the open pass at night with wardens hunting them, and Sef refused to go anywhere without arguing about it first, and these two positions turned out to be compatible.

She built a small fire — small enough to hide, large enough to matter — and shared out hard bread and smoked fish from her pack. Sef ate like a man who had forgotten food was a thing bodies wanted, then remembered all at once.

"The wound," she said, nodding at his side. The bandage she'd tied in the tunnel was spotted through. "It needs cleaning properly."

"It needs nothing. Fae heal fast."

"Fae bleed fast, too. Shirt."

He stared at her. She stared back, with the particular patience of someone who had spent a decade arguing with weather.

He took the shirt off. Underneath, the exile's brand sat over his heart — a circle broken by a line, seared into the skin — and below it, the hunter's arrow-wound, ugly and ordinary.

Paloma cleaned it in silence. But her eyes kept snagging on the brand, and finally she asked the question she'd been carrying since the tunnel.

"Why does the Order want you so badly? Smugglers they fine. Zealots they burn. But Vane came up that mountain like the world was ending. What are you?"

Sef was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"You won't believe me."

"I watched a book that hasn't been opened in a hundred years name tonight's date six hours before you fell through my door. My belief is currently accepting all applicants."

So he told her.

* * *

He told it badly at first — too fast, in the cadence of someone who had rehearsed an accusation until it wore smooth — and then, when she didn't interrupt, better. Slower. Like the truth was heavy and he could finally set it down in pieces.

"There was no invasion," he said. "There was no betrayal by my people. There was a theft. A hundred and eight years ago, a party of humans came through the gate under a flag of parley — your grandmother's banner, keeper. Zhou colors. They were welcomed. There was a feast. And while the court slept off the wine, they went down into the heart-vault of the winter palace and took the winter-heart itself."

Paloma's hands had gone still over the fire.

"The winter-heart isn't a jewel," Sef went on. "It isn't a treasure. It's the *season*. It's the engine of our winter — the slow, deep cold that lets the land sleep and wake, sleep and wake. Every realm needs its rest. Yours gets it naturally; ours needed the heart. And without it—" he gestured upward, at the sky, at the thin mean snow beginning to fall again, "—the seasons stopped turning. Our winter doesn't end, keeper. It just gets longer. Year by year. Court by court. We froze from the edges inward. And your side—"

"My side has had mild winters," Paloma said slowly. "Good harvests. Eleven years of them."

"Eleven years of stolen spring. But the debt is coming due. The realms are one body, whether your Order admits it or not. When one side hoards the warmth, the other side pays in frost — and eventually the ledger balances itself. The long winter falling on Thornfell now isn't an attack. It's arithmetic."

"And the prophecy," Paloma whispered. "*The keeper who opens.*"

"Is not a warning," Sef said. "It's an instruction. Someone of your blood carries the debt. Someone of your blood can repay it. The heart has to be returned — willingly, by a descendant of the thieves, at the origin-stone — or both realms freeze. Ours first. Then yours."

The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope, a fox screamed, short and startled.

"You're lying," Paloma said at last, but her voice had no conviction in it, and she knew it, and he knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third traveler.

"Am I?" Sef pulled his shirt back on, wincing. "Then explain the date in your book. Explain why Vane wants me dead before I reach anyone who'll listen. Explain why your Order has spent a century sealing cracks in a gate that — if your covenant were honest — should never have cracked at all. Honest doors don't leak, keeper. Guilty ones do."

Paloma looked at the Hollow Gate, glowing faintly in the dark, and for the first time in eleven years she let herself see it as it was: not a mercy. A muzzle.

"My grandmother," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a wound opening.

"Led the theft. Yes. I'm sorry. I've had thirty years to hate your family and I find I don't enjoy doing it to your face."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and Sef pretended to sleep, Paloma sat awake and did the arithmetic of her whole life.

Every kindness the Order had ever shown her had come with a lock attached. *You may read the ledger, not the folio. You may guard the gate, not question it. You may belong, as long as belonging means obedience.* She had thought that was what trust looked like. She had thought everyone's family had rooms they weren't allowed into.

And here was this fae, this branded, bleeding stranger, who had handed her the worst truth of her life wrapped in nothing — no bargain, no price, no leash. Just set it down in the firelight and let her pick it up or leave it.

She hated how much that was worth.

Across the coals, Sef was not sleeping either. Exiles don't, mostly. He was watching her watch the fire, and thinking: *she guards a door she was never allowed to open, and she still oils the hinges.* Thirty years among his own kind had taught him that cruelty wore many faces, but this — this stubborn, dutiful tenderness toward a lie — was not one of them.

She was alone the way he was alone. Not abandoned. *Assigned.* Given a post instead of a people, and told the post was love.

The snow thickened. Neither of them spoke. But somewhere in the dark between the fire and the gate, two lonely things quietly decided not to run from each other yet.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, and that was when the sky told them the debt had grown impatient.

The snow coming down the pass was wrong — too dry, too fast, falling in absolute silence, as if the air itself had been muffled. Paloma had lived her whole life in mountains and had never seen snow fall like it was afraid of being heard.

"That's not weather," she said.

"No," Sef said softly. "That's the heart being missed. The closer we get to midwinter, the louder the absence gets." He pointed east, along the high ridge where the boundary ran thin. "The origin-stone is three days' walk. Two, if we take the ridge road."

"The ridge road is forbidden. Even the smugglers won't take it. Marta said the dead talk up there."

"Marta," said Sef, "was right."

Paloma looked at the silent snow, and at the gate below them — her gate, her post, her whole life sealed behind her like a room she'd finally been locked out of — and made the decision the way she did everything: quickly, competently, and with no idea what it would cost.

"Then we'd better hope the dead," she said, swinging her pack onto her shoulder, "are better company than the living have been."

Behind them, far down the valley, a horn sounded. Once. Twice.

Warden horns. And they were not behind the hunt anymore.

They *were* the hunt.
