## 5. Bram's Silence

Bram was mending a kettle.

That was the first thing wrong with the picture, and Paloma noticed it before she noticed anything else — before the shuttered lamp, before the untouched supper going cold on its plate, before the way he sat with his back to the door of his own cottage like a man who no longer trusted entrances. Bram did not mend things at night. Bram said, with the gravity of scripture, that mended things needed daylight to set properly, and that anyone who soldered by lamplight deserved the leaks they got. He had said this to her when she was nine, holding up a botched repair of her slingshot as evidence, and he had never once deviated from it in the fifteen years since.

Tonight he sat hunched over the little copper kettle from the gate-house shelf, soldering iron in hand, working in a pool of yellow lamplight as though the light itself were something he could hide behind.

"Bram," Paloma said from the doorway.

He didn't startle. That was the second thing wrong. Bram startled at everything — at owls, at weather, at his own knees cracking when he stood too fast. He had spent forty years at the Hollow Gate flinching politely at a world that had mostly stopped surprising him, and Paloma had long since catalogued his flinches the way she catalogued smuggler trails: by depth, frequency, and direction of travel.

Now he simply set down the iron, very carefully, and said, "You're letting the cold in, girl."

"The cold's already in. It's been coming in for a hundred years." She stepped inside and shut the door behind her, and the latch sounded louder than she meant it to, a small decisive click that seemed to change the shape of the room. "Sef told me everything."

"Ah." Bram looked at the kettle. The kettle offered no support whatsoever. "The fae lad."

"The fae man whose name you knew."

"I didn't say I knew his name."

"You called him 'the fae lad.' You haven't met him. You were three miles away being suspiciously not-at-home when Vane came asking questions, and yet when I mentioned him this morning you asked if he was 'still limping.'" She crossed the small room and sat down across from him, on the stool she'd occupied through a thousand suppers, a thousand lessons, a thousand evenings of Bram teaching her the ledger codes and the crack-sealing resins and the proper way to scold a lock until it confessed. "I checked the old rosters today. The ones you told me were water-damaged and not worth my eyes. They're not water-damaged, Bram. They're *inked out*. Someone went through them with a steady hand and a bad conscience, striking names. One of the names left half-legible is *Kavanagh*. Your father's."

Bram closed his eyes.

The fire ticked in the grate. Outside, the wind came down off the pass with that new note in it, the one that had arrived with the frost and refused to leave — a sound like something vast turning over in its sleep, getting comfortable, preparing to stay.

"There was a famine winter," Bram said at last, still not opening his eyes, "the year your mother was born. Nineteen years ago now. Before your time, but you'll have seen the ledgers' shadow of it — the requisition entries that don't add up, the grain bought from towns that shouldn't have had grain to sell." His hands, resting on the table, had developed a tremor she had never seen in them. Bram's hands could thread resin-soaked cord through a hairline crack in living stone without spilling a drop. "Thornfell was starving, and the lowlands weren't much better, and there was a story going round the wardens' mess that the fae realm was hoarding. That they had a thing — a heart of winter, an ember of the old season — that kept their fields green while ours browned, and that the covenant forbade us nothing so much as it forbade us *that*."

"And?"

"And a group of keepers decided that a covenant was a rule, and rules were made by people, and people could be *outvoted*." He opened his eyes at last, and they were red-rimmed, and terribly tired. "Your grandmother was among them. So was my father. So were eleven others whose names I struck from those rosters myself, twenty years back, with the best ink money buys, because I was young and loyal and the High Warden told me that peace depended on nobody ever learning what had been done in Thornfell's name."

Paloma's hands had gone cold in her lap. She kept them there, folded, keeper-still, because some deep-buried habit of discipline was all she had to hold onto. "You knew. All this time."

"I helped bury it." The words came out flat, like stones laid on a grave. "I was the one who carried the false ledger back into the archive. I was the one who taught you — " and here his voice cracked, actually cracked, on the word — "that the covenant was mercy. That our family had guarded the gate for six generations because we were trusted. Trusted!" He laughed, and it was the worst sound she had ever heard from him. "We were not trusted, Paloma. We were *paid*. The Kavanaghs keep the gate closed because the gate is where the evidence is buried underneath it."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The kettle sat between them on the table, half-mended, its seam still open, a bright raw line of exposed metal running around its middle. Paloma found she couldn't stop looking at it.

"Why tell me the covenant was mercy?" she asked finally. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Fifteen years, Bram. You taught me the rites. You taught me that the seal was a kindness — that the fae wanted it, that both realms slept better with the door shut. I built my whole life on that. I turned down the Corrow posting because I thought this place *needed* me. I—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Was any of it true?"

"Yes," Bram said fiercely, leaning forward. "Yes, girl, listen to me. The rites are true. The sealing works. The cracks you patch every night are real cracks, and if you stopped patching them the cold would come through them whether or not anyone stole anything a hundred years ago. None of what I taught you was a lie about *how to do the work*. Only about why."

"That's the whole of it, though, isn't it. The why."

"The why is heavy," Bram said. "The how will keep you alive."

She almost laughed. It was exactly the sort of thing he said — practical to the bone, wisdom delivered like instruction on boot maintenance — and under any other circumstances it would have warmed her. Tonight it felt like watching a beloved house discover its own rotten joists: everything familiar still standing, and nothing trustworthy beneath it.

"Sef says the long winter is the debt," she said. "He says the winter-heart belongs to the fae realm, that it keeps both seasons turning, and that Thornfell stole it and hid it, and that everything dying now — the orchards, the river ice forming in high summer, the pass closing early — is the bill arriving." She watched his face. "He says it's real, Bram. Not a story. Not fae boasting. Real, and close, and *here*."

Bram was quiet for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — dropped its defensive bark and gone soft, pleading, an old man's voice asking for something he knew he had no right to ask.

"Then let it lie."

Paloma stared at him.

"Let it lie," he said again, leaning forward, hands out as if he could physically hold the words between them. "Paloma. Listen. What happens if you pull that thread? The Order falls — good, perhaps, the Order has earned worse. But the lowland towns riot, because their whole history is a lie and people do not forgive being lied to about themselves. The zealots get their holy war, the ones who already worship the sealed door like it's the tomb of a saint. And the fae—" He hesitated. "The fae have a century of grievance stored up behind that gate, and grief makes monsters of gentler folk than them. If the gate opens on anger instead of justice—"

"It's already opening on anger," Paloma said. "Anger and frost. That's what the long winter *is*. We don't get to choose whether the debt comes due. We only choose whether we're honest when it arrives."

"At what cost?" He reached across the table and took her hands, and his grip was strong despite the tremor, strong the way it had been strong when he pulled her out of the crevasse on the north trail when she was twelve, strong the way love is strong when it is frightened. "Listen to me. You are the best keeper this gate has had in six generations. I don't say that as your teacher. I say it as a warden who has read every log since the founding, and I am telling you that whatever you think you owe to truth, or to that fae boy, or to your grandmother's ghost — you owe more to the thirty villages between here and the lowlands who are alive tonight because somebody kept the gate. Let the old lie stand. Let it stand for them. Peace is not a beautiful thing, Paloma. It's an ugly thing, held together with ugly stitches, and every soul warm tonight in Thornfell is warm because of those stitches. Don't you dare start picking at them because the truth would be tidier."

"The truth isn't tidy," Paloma said quietly. "It's a wound. But wounds close, Bram. Lies only fester. You've been carrying this for twenty years — look at you. You're soldering kettles at midnight because you can't sleep. When did you last sleep a full night?"

"Don't turn this on me."

"Somebody has to turn it somewhere! You buried it, and it's been eating you alive from the inside ever since, and now it's eating the *world*, and you're sitting here asking me to let it keep eating because digging it out might hurt?" She gently freed her hands from his. Her own were shaking now; she looked at them rather than at his face. "I loved you my whole life. That hasn't changed in the last hour, which frankly annoys me. But I can't carry the lie for you. I won't inherit it. My grandmother stole a heart, Bram. Somebody in this family has to give something back."

* * *

He didn't argue further. She had expected shouting — Bram could shout, when the occasion demanded, usually about damp in the archive — but instead he sat very still for a long moment, looking at her the way he had looked at her once before: the day she passed her keeper's trial, when he'd stood at the back of the hall with an expression she'd then read as pride and now understood had always contained this moment, foreseen and dreaded, waiting all along like a coin at the bottom of a well.

Then he stood, joints cracking — he flinched at that, faintly, out of pure habit, and something in her chest twisted at the small familiar absurdity of it — and went to the great oak chest in the corner. His traveling chest. The one she had been forbidden to open her entire childhood, on pain of chores, which for young Paloma had been the most effective prohibition ever devised.

He lifted from it a ring of keys.

There were perhaps thirty of them, great and small — iron and brass and two of some pale metal she didn't recognize, each one worn smooth at the bow by decades of Bram's thumb. He had jangled that ring outside her window every morning of her apprenticeship. It was as much a part of the gate as the gate itself.

"The water-damage story," he said, holding it, not yet giving it, "was mine. I invented it the week after I struck the names. A lie within the lie — one lie to protect another, and then a third to protect the second, and so on, until you're twenty years down and you can't remember what the original truth even smelled like." He turned the ring slowly, and the keys caught the lamplight, one after another, like a tiny passing procession. "The deep keys are on the inner loop. The ones nobody's used since the sealing. There's a door under the gate, Paloma. Under and *behind* — the old keepers' records call it the Undercroft, and the newer ones call it nothing at all, because they've been taught to walk past it without seeing it. I've walked past it ten thousand times. Every keeper has. That's what training is, in the end. Learning not to see the door in your own cellar."

"Bram—"

"I'm not giving you permission," he said. "Understand that. I still think you're wrong. I think you're gloriously, catastrophically wrong, and that you'll break the world, and that you'll be right to do it, and I have never in my life hated a sentence more than the one I just thought." He came around the table, slow, an old man crossing ice, and pressed the keyring into her hands. Her hands. Cold, shaking, younger than his by forty years and about to be burdened with all of it. "I'm giving you the keys because you'll find the doors anyway — you were finding doors before you could walk — and because if it must be done, then it must be done by someone who patches cracks at night and counts the smuggler trails twice and loves this stupid frozen border more than she loves her own ease. That's you. God help both of us. That has always been you."

Paloma looked down at the keys. They weighed nothing. They weighed everything.

"What happens to you?" she asked. "When Vane finds out you gave me these?"

Bram smiled. It was a terrible smile, and a free one, and she understood suddenly that she had never seen him truly smile before tonight, because a person cannot smile freely while holding a twenty-year lie in their mouth.

"Oh, I'll be in trouble," he said mildly. "But I'm seventy-one, I make a scandalous excellent plum wine, and the warden cells in this region have stonework I helped lay, so I know for a fact they're dry." He patted her cheek, once, the way he had when she was small. "Go. Take the south road to Corrow — the north passes will be closed within the day, and you'll want supplies and you'll want them unwatched. Old Mother Ilse still owes me for the roof I fixed in '48; tell her the kettle's finally done and she'll feed you." He glanced back at the table, at the little copper kettle with its bright unfinished seam, and laughed — softly, helplessly, at himself. "And take the kettle too. Apparently I mend things at night now. New era. Everything's different."

At the door, wrapped in her cloak with the keys hidden against her ribs, Paloma paused. The wind had risen again, and beneath it — faint, unmistakable, coming not through the air but up through the soles of her boots, through the stone, through the deep bones of the mountain — she felt the gate shudder.

A hairline crack, opening, somewhere in the dark.

"Bram," she said. "How far under the gate does the Undercroft go?"

He had picked the soldering iron back up. He looked at it, and at the kettle, and then at her, and his face was grim and calm and older than the mountains.

"Far enough," he said, "that you'll need more than my keys. Far enough that whatever's down there has been keeping the winter-heart company for a hundred years." He turned back to his work, and the lamplight caught the tears he was too proud to let fall. "And far enough, girl, that I'd wager the Order's deepest vault sits closer to it than any of us ever dared measure. Go to Corrow. Find out what Ilse knows about the old survey tunnels. And Paloma — "

She waited.

"When you open the cellar door," Bram said, "whatever looks back at you — remember that it has been alone in the dark with our family's sin for a very long time. Be kind to it. That's not keeper's advice. That's just a grandfather talking."

She walked out into the falling snow, and the gate shuddered again beneath her feet — closer this time, and hungrier, like something below finally hearing footsteps on the stairs.
