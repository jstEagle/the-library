## 6. The Market of Small Betrayals

The town of Corrow smelled of wet wool, woodsmoke, and the particular desperation of a place where winter had arrived three weeks early and everyone was pretending it hadn't.

Paloma pulled her hood lower and let the crowd carry her down Market Row, Sef a half-step behind her. He'd wrapped himself in one of Bram's old traveling cloaks, which hung off him like a tent on a fencepost, and he kept tugging the hood forward as though the sky itself might recognize him and shout.

"Everyone is staring," he muttered.

"No one is staring. They're staring at me, because I'm a stranger, and you're staring at them because you've never seen a market."

"I've *seen* markets." A pause. "I've seen markets with singing fruit."

"That's not a market, that's a fever dream."

He made a small wounded sound, but she caught the ghost of a smile under his hood before he ducked it away. Three days on the smuggler's road had worn them both thin — her boots were soaked through, his ribs were still bruised from whatever the warden hunters had done to him before she'd pulled him out of the snow — but there was something almost easy in the bickering now, the way two people who have nothing left to lose start to spend their last coins on jokes.

Corrow was built the way all lowland towns were built: crooked, practical, and huddled against the weather like a dog that had been kicked too many times to trust a kind hand. The market square sat in a bowl at its heart, ringed by stalls whose awnings sagged under the weight of early snow. Merchants shouted prices that climbed by the hour. Somewhere a fiddler played a tune about a girl who drowned herself for love, which Paloma had always thought was an odd choice for a market, but Corrow had never been a cheery place.

She counted what remained of her coin purse by touch. Not enough. Not nearly enough for rope, cold-weather grease for the passes, dried food for two, and — if she could find it — a vial of marrow-salve for Sef's ribs, which he kept insisting were fine in the tone of voice people used when they were not fine at all.

"There," Sef said quietly. "The apothecary. The sign with the—"

"I see it. But salve costs more than we have, so first we solve the money problem."

"And how do we do that?"

Paloma looked across the square, to where a squat stone building squatted beneath a sign painted with crossed keys. The Gilded Cask, Corrow's only respectable inn and its least respectable back room. She knew the man who ran it. Everyone who had ever worked the Hollow Gate knew Doss Merrin, because Doss Merrin made it his business to know everyone else.

"You're going to hate this," she said.

"I already hate everything about this town."

"Good. Then nothing I say will make it worse."

* * *

Doss Merrin had grown fatter and balder since the last time Paloma had come down from the gate, but his eyes hadn't changed at all — small, quick, and warm as a fish's belly. He took one look at her, poured two cups of something amber without asking, and jerked his chin toward the back room.

"Keeper Zhou." He said it loudly enough for the taproom to hear, then dropped his voice as they passed through the door. "Word up the mountain is you died. Word down the mountain is you ran. Which lie am I drinking to?"

"The second one, apparently."

"Mm." He settled into a chair that groaned under him, and pushed a cup toward her. "And your friend? Doesn't drink. Doesn't eat. Stares at my cheese like it owes him an apology."

"He's fasting. Religious reasons."

"What religion?"

"Complicated ones."

Doss laughed, then didn't. "You want something, keeper. You always want something when you sit in that chair. Last time it was wax. Time before that it was a name. So. What is it?"

"Cold-weather grease. Rope. Food for two, ten days. And marrow-salve, if you can get it without questions."

"That's thirty silver if it's one coin. You carrying thirty silver?"

"You know I'm not."

"Then we're having a different conversation." Doss laced his fingers over his belly, which was the gesture he made when he was about to be someone's best friend or their worst morning. "Because I'll tell you what I told the last fool who came through here with empty pockets and full troubles. In Corrow, nobody gives. Everybody trades. So what are you trading, Paloma Zhou? And don't say coin."

She had known this was coming. She had rehearsed it on the road, in fact, walking through the dark with Sef snoring against a rock, turning the thing over and over until it stopped feeling like betrayal and started feeling like arithmetic. It had never quite managed the transformation.

"A route," she said. "A smuggler's route through the high passes. One that isn't on any warden map. I'll trade it for the supplies and your silence about us passing through."

Doss whistled low. "That's worth fifty silver, easy, to the right buyer. Maybe more."

"Then consider yourself getting a bargain."

"Who says I'm buying?" His little eyes glittered. "Maybe I'm just curious what the Keeper of the Hollow Gate is doing running the smuggler roads with a fae boy in tow. Maybe curiosity's free."

"Doss."

"Keeper."

They looked at each other for a long moment. Outside, the fiddler had switched to something faster; somebody was dancing badly; life went on being cheap and loud in Corrow while two people in a back room negotiated the price of survival.

"The route," Paloma said finally, "is called the Shepherd's Stair. It runs above the tree line from the Hollow Gate's western flank down to the valley below Kell's Ford. It's unmarked because the man who found it fell off it, and the man he showed died before he could tell anyone else. I'm the last living soul who knows it end to end."

"And you're giving it to me."

"I'm giving it to you. Because I need supplies, and because—" She hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, because the honest answer was ugly. "Because after tonight I won't need secrets anymore. Either I fix what's broken at that gate, or none of these routes matter, because there won't be anyone left to walk them."

Something flickered behind Doss's fish-belly eyes. Not kindness exactly. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man hearing his own funeral described in favorable terms.

"Done," he said. "Supplies tonight, packed and waiting at the kitchen door. Salve included, no charge beyond the route." He raised his cup. "To bargains."

"To bargains," she echoed, and drank, and tried not to feel the small cold weight of what she'd just done settle into her chest like a swallowed stone.

Sef said nothing as they left, but halfway down the alley he asked, "Was that true? About not needing secrets anymore?"

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters to you. You've been quiet since we walked out."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *thinking*-quiet right now. There's a difference. My mother used to—"

"Don't." She said it sharper than she meant to. "Don't tell me about your mother. Please. Not today."

Sef fell silent. After a moment, his hand found her elbow, brief and light, and then withdrew. It was, she realized, the first time he'd touched her since the road began, and somehow that tiny contact hurt worse than Vane's crossbows would have.

* * *

They bought the rest of what they needed as the light failed — candles, flint, a second blanket, a strip of dried venison that Sef pronounced "chewy but honest." Paloma kept her hood up and her face angled away from the lanterns, but Corrow was small, and small towns have long memories, and she had spent half her childhood in that square.

It happened outside the chandler's stall.

"Paloma?" A woman's voice, disbelieving, delighted. "Paloma Zhou? By the Twins, it *is* you!"

Tamsin Reeve stood in a puddle of lamplight holding a basket of candle stubs, round-faced and freckled and exactly as Paloma remembered her — except older, and harder around the eyes, the way everyone got harder around the eyes after fifteen years of Corrow winters. They had been girls together once. Tamsin had taught her to skip stones on the millpond. Tamsin's mother had mended Paloma's first cloak. For one blazing childhood summer they had sworn themselves blood-sisters with a pin and a lie about how much it hurt.

"Tamsin." Paloma's hood came down before she could stop it. "You look—"

"Old. Say it. Everyone does." Tamsin laughed, and the laugh was the same, and something in Paloma's chest cracked sideways with homesickness so sudden and stupid it made her dizzy. "What are you doing down from the gate? Is it true what they're saying? That the Order's recalled every keeper south? That something woke up in the pass?"

"Where did you hear that?"

"Oh, it's everywhere. Warden criers in every town between here and the sea. There's a woman named Vane putting out word — a description and everything. Dark-haired keeper, mid-thirties, traveling with a fae companion, considered dangerous." Tamsin's eyes twinkled. "Doesn't sound a thing like you, of course."

The cold went through Paloma like well water.

"No," she said carefully. "Nothing like me."

"Mm." Tamsin shifted her basket to her hip. Her smile stayed perfectly pleasant. Only her eyes moved, quick as Doss's, flicking past Paloma's shoulder to where Sef stood shadowed against the chandler's wall. "And who's your friend?"

"A cousin."

"Funny sort of cousin. Tall." Another pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Well. It was lovely seeing you, Paloma. Truly. You should come by Mother's house while you're in town. She still talks about you. We could catch up properly. Get warm. Have a real meal instead of—" her gaze touched the dried venison in Paloma's pack, "—road food."

"I can't. We leave tonight."

"Tonight! Of course. Always were restless." Tamsin smiled her wide Corrow smile, the one that meant a bargain was being struck somewhere underneath. "Well then. Safe travels. And Paloma —" she leaned in, kissed her cheek, and whispered, so softly only Paloma could hear: "*Run fast, love.*"

Then she stepped back, lifted her basket, and walked away into the crowd, humming.

* * *

"She knows," Sef said, the moment the crowd closed behind Tamsin. "She recognized you and she knows about Vane's notice and she's going to sell you."

"I know."

"Then why did we stand there talking about candle stubs?"

"Because I wanted—" Paloma stopped. Pressed her knuckles against her mouth. Started again. "Because when I was nine years old, that woman held my hair back while I was sick, and swore we'd be sisters forever, and I believed her. Because I haven't had a single conversation in fifteen years that wasn't about gate ledgers and crack-sealant, and for about ten seconds I got to be a girl skipping stones on a millpond again, and I paid ten seconds' rent on that by pretending not to notice she was pricing my head."

Sef was quiet for a moment. "That might be the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me. And I once watched a court forget its own name."

"Don't make it funny."

"I'm not. I'm making it *true*, which is worse." He glanced back at the square, where lanterns were coming alight one by one. "We should go. Now. Before she reaches whoever she's going to reach."

"We have twenty minutes while she decides. Corrow folk don't betray quickly. They deliberate. It's practically a civic virtue." But she was already moving, cutting down the chandler's lane toward the kitchen door of the Gilded Cask where Doss's supplies waited. "Grab the packs. We take the river road north out of town, no lamps, and we're in the hills before—"

"Paloma."

The voice came from the mouth of the alley. Not Tamsin's.

Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane stood at the end of the lane with six soldiers and a lantern, and the light caught the gray at her temples and the utter absence of surprise in her face. She looked tired, Paloma thought. Tired the way stones are tired — worn smooth by the same water falling on the same place for years.

"Captain," Paloma said. "You're a long way from your gate."

"So are you, keeper." Vane's gaze slid to Sef, then back. "Or should I say, *ex*-keeper. Desertion carries penalties, as does harboring. As does—" her jaw tightened, almost imperceptibly, "—theft of Order property. You took the folio."

"You burned the prophecy. I saved it. We can argue about who stole from whom another night."

"I'd rather not argue at all." Vane raised one hand, and the six soldiers leveled their crossbows with a sound like rain beginning. "Come quietly. Both of you. I give you my word you'll be treated fairly — treated better than fairly. There's a version of this night where nobody bleeds, Paloma. Take it."

Behind her, at the far mouth of the alley, torchlight bloomed. More voices. Tamsin's voice among them, high and bright, saying words Paloma couldn't make out but understood perfectly anyway — *yes, that's her, yes, I'll swear to it, yes, the pardon, thank you, Captain, thank you.*

Ten seconds of millpond. Priced and paid.

"Your word," Paloma said. "Like your Order's word at the covenant? Like your founders' word to the fae?"

Vane's expression didn't change. But something behind her eyes did — a door closing on a room where a fire was still burning. "My word is the only clean thing I own, keeper. Don't compare it to theirs."

"Then keep it," Paloma said, "and look away for exactly ten seconds."

"I can't do that."

"I know." Paloma's hand found Sef's wrist. "I just wanted you to have to say it out loud."

And she pulled him backward off the alley's edge into the black water of the Corrow mill-race.

* * *

The river took them fast and freezing, tumbling them under the town's foundations and out through the old culvert that some forgotten smuggler had cut a century ago — because of course there was one, there was always one; Corrow was built on bargains and bolt-holes. They came up coughing in a reed bed a half-mile downstream, soaked to the bone, gasping, alive.

Sef lay on his back in the mud, wheezing laughter that might have been sobbing. "You — threw us — into a *river*."

"You hesitated."

"I was *going* to move!"

"After you finished being outraged. We didn't have time." Paloma rolled onto her side, shivering violently, and stared up at the stars swimming above the reeds. Her whole body shook. Some of it was the cold. Most of it wasn't.

"Sef," she said. "When this is over — if this is ever over — remind me to never come home again. I don't think I actually have one. I think I've been borrowing other people's my whole life. The Order's. Bram's. Hers." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Even my betrayal tonight was secondhand. I traded a dead man's route for a dead woman's salve."

"You gave the route away," Sef said quietly. "You didn't sell it. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. A seller keeps score. A giver just wants to be done." He struggled upright, dripping, and offered her his hand. "Come on, cousin. The hills are north, the night is long, and somewhere out there is a stone that wants its heart back."

She took his hand. It was colder than hers, which she hadn't thought possible, and steadier than hers, which she had.

Behind them, faint across the water, she could hear the town of Corrow going about its evening — the fiddler still playing, the taverns filling, someone laughing at something small. A whole town of decent people who would each, given a pardon and a reason, have sold her for less than Tamsin got.

Paloma turned her back on the lights and started climbing.
