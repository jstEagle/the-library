## 7. Winter-Heart

Sef had crossed the Hollow Gate twice in his long life — once as a child on his mother's hip, when the boundary still hummed like a struck bell, and once last night, bleeding, half-frozen, and falling through it the way a stone falls through ice that has finally given up pretending to hold him.

Neither crossing had prepared him for the look on Paloma Zhou's face when she saw what lay on the far side of her door.

She stood at the threshold of the pass, breath fogging, one hand resting — out of habit, he suspected — on the gate's cold stone frame, and she stared at his country the way people stare at a house fire. Not with fear exactly. With recognition. The look of someone who has just understood that a thing she loved was dying while she kept its porch swept.

"It's *snowing*," she said.

"It is always snowing now," Sef said. "That was rather my point."

"I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "The records say the fae realm is warm. Gardens. Perpetual harvest. That was the whole justification for the covenant, wasn't it? They had plenty. We were protecting them from us."

"The records," Sef said gently, "were written by thieves."

He watched her absorb that. She did not flinch, which he liked about her. Most humans flinched. Most humans, presented with an uncomfortable truth, behaved as though truth itself had made an improper gesture at them.

"Show me," she said instead.

So he did.

* * *

They walked down into the vale of Sorrowmere, which had been called something else before the winter came — Sef had been trying for three days to remember what, and the forgetting itself felt like a splinter working deeper. The path wound between orchards whose trees stood silvered and rigid, every branch holding its exact shape, as if the frost had come so fast and so thoroughly that it had decided to become the tree.

Paloma touched one as they passed. Her fingers left no mark.

"They're not dead," Sef said. "That's the cruelty of it. Dead would be kinder. Dead things can be buried, mourned, replaced. These are only — paused."

The village below them was small, maybe forty houses, built low and round-roofed the way his people built, with chimneys shaped like sleeping birds. Smoke should have risen from those chimneys. Instead each chimney held a plume of frozen smoke, gray-white and glassy, hanging above the rooftops like banners in a windless hall.

In the doorway of the nearest house stood a woman. She was caught mid-motion, one hand raised toward a shelf where a jar sat just beyond her reach, and her face was turned slightly upward, mouth open around whatever she had been saying or singing or calling to someone in another room. Frost had taken her lashes first, then her cheeks, then everything, until she was less a person than a sculpture of a person having an ordinary afternoon.

Paloma made a sound low in her throat.

"We could carry them inside," she said. "Warm them."

"You could light a bonfire in her lap and she would not thaw." He said it more harshly than he meant to, because the woman in the doorway was his aunt's neighbor, and because he had tried the bonfire himself, two years ago, on a boy caught mid-run in a field, and had learned what the answer was. "The cold isn't weather here, keeper. It's a hunger. It ate the warmth and kept the shape."

They stood together in silence. Snow ticked softly against the frozen smoke.

"Was there a family?" Paloma asked finally. "Hers?"

"There is a family." Sef nodded up the road, where a second house stood with its door ajar. "Her daughter froze reaching for her mother's hand across the table. The husband froze standing in the yard, looking at the sky, because he went out to see whether the snow was stopping." A pause. "It was not stopping."

Paloma pulled her cloak tighter. It was a human gesture, small and practical, and it struck Sef suddenly how strange it was to watch someone do it here, in a place where nothing moved anymore except visitors and grief. She looked like a candle carried through a museum at night.

"How many?" she asked. "Like this?"

"In this valley? Four thousand. In the court cities, more than I can count, because I stopped counting." He started walking again, because if he stopped walking he would start naming names, and he had promised himself he would not do that in front of her. Not yet. "Your Order sealed the gate and called it mercy. Mercy for whom, I wonder, standing here."

"That's not fair," Paloma said, but there was no heat in it, and after a moment she added, quietly: "I don't know who it was mercy for. I'm starting to suspect nobody ever checked."

"No," Sef agreed. "Checking requires believing you might be wrong. Your Order has never once entertained the possibility. It's almost impressive. Like watching a man insist he cannot drown while standing in the sea."

That earned him a look — sharp, wounded, and underneath both, unmistakably, a flicker of agreement. He was learning to read her. It had taken him three days and one shared flight through warden crossbow-fire, which was faster than most friendships formed among his people, though slower than most funerals.

They passed a well. Frozen water stood up out of it like a fist. Someone had hung a lantern on the well-post, and the lantern's flame was frozen too — a small orange flower of glass, burning forever without giving off heat. Sef had walked past it perhaps a hundred times since the winter began. He had stopped being able to look at it some months ago, and yet he looked at it every time, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Paloma stopped beside it.

"This is what Bram wouldn't tell me," she said. It was not a question.

Sef considered several answers and discarded all of them for honesty. "This is what your whole Order works very hard not to know. There are wardens who have served thirty years on that gate and believe the other side is empty wilderness. Some of them believe it sincerely enough that if you showed them this village they'd call it a trick. Illusion. Fae glamour." He smiled, and knew it didn't reach anything. "It is easier to freeze four thousand people if you first convince yourself they are weather."

"I'm sorry." She said it plainly, without performance, and he found — annoyingly — that his throat tightened. He had expected excuses. He had rehearsed responses to excuses all the way down the mountain. Excuses he could fight. Plain sorrow simply sat down inside him and refused to leave.

"Don't be sorry," he managed. "Be useful. Come and see the worst of it, and then be useful."

* * *

The court city of Annwn-Vael — though even the name was going; he had caught himself, twice this week, reaching for it and finding only a smooth blank place where the word used to live — rose out of the valley ahead of them like a wave that had forgotten to fall.

It had been beautiful. Sef remembered it beautifully: spires grown, not built, coaxed up out of living stone by singers whose whole art was patience; bridges of woven light strung between towers; gardens that bloomed in colors human eyes technically could not see, which had never stopped human traders from trying, squinting, and declaring them "very fine anyway."

Now the light-bridges hung gray and rigid as old cobwebs. The gardens were chalices of frosted bloom. And the great doors of the high court stood open, as they always stood open, because the court had been in session when the cold came down.

Sef led her up the processional way, and Paloma said nothing for a long time, which he was grateful for. He did not think he could have borne commentary here.

Inside, the throne room held them all.

The Queen stood at the base of her own dais, one hand extended toward a herald, her face lit with an expression Sef had seen on her only once in life, decades ago, the day the treaty envoys came — fury and heartbreak braided together, the look of a monarch receiving news she already dreaded. Around her, the whole court: lords and ladies, petitioners, children in ceremonial dress who had been fidgeting when the frost took them and were fidgeting still, forever, mid-squirm. Near the great doors, two guards leaned against their spears sharing some joke whose punchline would never arrive.

And everywhere, everywhere, the silence. Not empty silence — *occupied* silence. The silence of a room full of people waiting for someone to speak next.

Paloma's hand found his sleeve. He let it stay there. He was cold too, though he'd never admit it; exile's blood runs thin.

"What were they saying?" she whispered. "The Queen. What sentence was she in the middle of?"

"I don't know." His voice came out rougher than intended. "No one knows. That's the thing about being frozen mid-word, keeper — you don't get to finish. Whatever she was telling that herald — a warning, an apology, an order to evacuate — it died in her mouth, and we've spent years inventing versions of it. Every faction remembers it differently. Conveniently differently."

"And you?" Paloma asked. "What version do you remember?"

He hadn't meant to answer honestly. But the throne room pressed on him, all those waiting faces, and honesty came up like blood from a cleaned wound.

"I remember that she was looking at the herald with love," he said. "Whatever she was saying, her face was full of love. So either she was warning him tenderly, or forgiving him tenderly, or — " he stopped.

"Or thanking him," Paloma said softly.

"Or thanking him. For something he'd done that cost him everything. Which, if you ask me, sounds a great deal like the sort of thing a herald does when he has just told his queen that the humans stole the winter-heart." Sef exhaled slowly, watching his breath hang and fade — the only moving thing in the room besides the two of them. "Though of course I would say that. I'm the traitor who believes it."

"You're not a traitor."

"I'm an exile who ran to the enemy's side of the mountain and survived on smuggler roads. Where I come from, keeper, that is the *definition*." He turned away from the Queen before his eyes did something embarrassing. "Come. There's one more thing to show you, and then we should move, because Vane's hunters cross passes faster than they cross consciences."

* * *

He took her to the nursery garden on the eastern terrace, and immediately regretted it, and did not turn back, because turning back was how countries died — not all at once but one avoided sight at a time.

Here the frost had come later. Here the children had been playing.

A game of hunt-and-hare stood suspended across the whole lawn: six children frozen in full flight and pursuit, a girl mid-leap over a hedge with her arms spread like wings, a small boy clutching a stick-sword and laughing — laughing, forever — as he rounded a statue of some forgotten hero. Their nurse stood near the gate with a blanket over her arm, caught in the act of deciding whether to scold them or join them.

Sef heard Paloma breathing carefully beside him. Controlled breathing. The kind you learn when you're holding something together by will alone.

"How old?" she asked.

"The youngest is four."

She was quiet for ten steps. Twenty. Then she said, in a voice sanded nearly flat: "My grandmother helped steal from these people. My grandmother, who taught me to whittle boats and swore at the porridge pot and died believing she was a good woman."

"Yes."

"She *knew*. She must have known what—"

"She knew what she was told," Sef said. "Which is the same thing your Order knows now. Which is the same thing, I suspect, that Captain Vane knows and refuses to say aloud." He glanced at her. "Thieves rarely think of themselves as thieves, Paloma. They think of themselves as collectors. As reclaimers. As people taking back what was owed. Your grandmother probably went to her grave certain the winter-heart was rightfully Thornfell's."

"But it wasn't."

"It was ours. It was also, and here is the part your Order never understood, *everyone's* — that was its nature. It was not a treasure to be owned. It was the hinge of both winters. The warm season of our world and yours drew from the same root, and the root was the heart. Take it across the border and both realms begin to starve — ours fast, yours slow. Ours visibly, yours comfortably." He smiled without humor. "You've had a hundred gentle years while we froze. That was the theft's design, I think. Make the crime profitable enough that no one audits it."

Paloma had stopped walking. She stood among the frozen children with her jaw set, and Sef watched her arrive somewhere — watched it land in her the way bad news lands, in stages, denial first and arithmetic last.

"Then where is it now?" she asked. "If it keeps both realms alive, where has it *been* for a hundred years? Thornfell's summers have been ordinary. Good harvests. My ledger says good harvests."

"Because it still works," Sef said. "Badly. Fading. A stolen hearth still gives some heat — that's what makes theft bearable to the thief. But it's guttering. You'll have noticed your own winters lengthening. First by days. Then weeks. This year—" he gestured at the sky, at the snow coming down thick and purposeful over Thornfell's peaks behind them, "—this year the debt comes due. The heart is failing, and when it fails entirely, both realms go under. Yours simply gets the courtesy of a longer runway."

"So we return it. Reforge the covenant. Fine." She said it briskly, practically, and he could have kissed her hand for it. "Where is it? Who guards it? How do we take it back?"

And there it was. The question he had carried down the mountain like a stone in his boot, the question he had saved for last because everything else — the villages, the court, the children — had been, in the end, preamble. Persuasion. Building the floor beneath the drop.

"You can't take it back," he said. "It isn't in Thornfell. It isn't in any smuggler cache or warden fortress or king's treasury."

"Sef—"

"It's beneath your gate, Paloma." He watched her face as he said it, and hated that he needed to watch, and watched anyway. "Beneath the Hollow Gate. Under the origin-stone your family has guarded for five generations. The deepest vault of your own Order holds the heart of both worlds, and the woman who keeps the door above it has spent her whole life standing on top of the reason mine is dying."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, snow slid off a frozen roof with a sound like a sigh.

Paloma looked east, toward the pass, toward the gate she had swept and mended and defended every night of her adult life, and Sef saw her lips move faintly — counting, perhaps. Counting floors. Counting locks. Counting the distance between the keeper she was and the thief's descendant she would have to become.

"Bram has keys," she said at last.

Something in her voice had changed. It was quieter and harder at once, the way steel is quiet and hard at once, and Sef thought: *there she is.* The keeper who opens.

"He has keys," she repeated, "and he owes me a truth. Come on. We climb tonight."

She started down the terrace at a pace that suggested the climbing had already begun in her head. Sef lingered one moment longer, looking back at the nursery garden — at the girl with her arms spread like wings, frozen at the top of her leap.

"Hold on," he told her softly, in the old tongue, the way you tell a story to keep it alive. "Someone is coming to wake us."

Then he turned and followed the keeper down into the snow.
