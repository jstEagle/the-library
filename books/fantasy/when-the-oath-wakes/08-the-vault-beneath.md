## 8. The Vault Beneath

The keyring was heavier than it had any right to be.

Bram had pressed it into her hands two nights ago without a word of ceremony — just a look she was still trying to decipher, somewhere between *forgive me* and *don't come back* — and ever since, the thing had sat in her pack like a sleeping animal. Eleven keys. Iron, brass, bone-white something she preferred not to name. She had spent her whole life in the keeper's station above the Hollow Gate and had never once seen Bram use more than three of them: the door, the storeroom, the lantern cabinet.

Which meant eight keys existed for doors she did not know.

"You're doing the face," Sef said from his perch on the third stair. He had recovered most of his color since the tunnels, though he still moved like a man whose body had been borrowed by someone careless and returned slightly dented. "The one where you look like you're arguing with an object."

"The keys are arguing back," Paloma said. "I'm losing."

Below them, the gatehouse cellar breathed its usual cold — stone-cold, honest cold — but beneath that, when she held the ring up to the lamp, she could feel a second cold coming off the floor seam in the northeast corner. A cold that had *opinion*. She had swept this cellar a thousand times. She had mopped around that corner seam a thousand times, assuming it was where the mountain wept through the mortar like every other crack in Thornfell's architecture.

It was not a crack. It was a door, and it had been waiting under her broom for longer than she'd been alive.

"Third one," Sef said. "The little crooked one."

"How do you know?"

"Because I can smell iron going old." He flexed his bandaged hand. "Fae ironwork sings even after it's locked away from us. Yours hums. That seam is older than your Order, keeper, whatever your books say."

She fitted the small crooked key — it had no business fitting, being half the size of the lock plate, but locks and lies apparently kept their own geometry — and turned. The seam sighed open along a line no eye could find, exhaling air so cold and clean it tasted like the inside of winter itself, like snowfall before anything has walked on it.

Lamplight reached down a stair that had not been swept in a century.

"Well," Paloma said, because her voice needed something to do while her courage caught up. "At least they dusted."

They hadn't. But the stairs were otherwise perfect, each step worn in the center by generations of descending feet, and that wear frightened her more than dust would have. This vault had been visited. Regularly. By keepers who smiled at her across the breakfast table and said nothing.

Sef went first. She noticed he chose to go first, and understood it as a gift: whatever waited below, he intended it to meet him before it met her.

* * *

The descent took longer than the cellar was deep, which should have been impossible, and by the twelfth turning of the stair Paloma stopped counting turnings and started counting other things instead — the way the wall stones changed from Thornfell granite to a paler, veined rock that seemed to hold the lamplight rather than reflect it; the way frost grew in patterns too regular for weather, spirals within spirals, like handwriting practiced for a thousand years.

"Your people built this," she said quietly.

"My people built the *upper* part," Sef said. "The lower part built itself. Or grew. There's a distinction the scholars used to fight about." A pause, and then, softer: "Used to."

She heard what the *used to* cost him and didn't answer it. There were things you could say to a man about his frozen homeland and things you couldn't, and most of them were the latter.

At last the stairs ended in a chamber that made her forget how to breathe properly.

It was round, and vast beyond the stair's explanation, and its walls were painted.

Not decorated — *painted*, floor to unseen ceiling, in colors that had somehow survived a hundred years of dark and cold: deep blues, fox-fur reds, a green like the first reckless week of spring. Lamplight climbed them slowly, and as it climbed, the images assembled themselves into story.

Paloma had grown up on murals. The gatehouse chapel had one — the Covenant of Sealing, all solemn wardens and grateful farmers, the fae retreating behind the closed gate with expressions the painter had generously interpreted as peaceful. She had stared at it through a thousand dull sermons.

These walls told the same events in a different language entirely.

Here: a fae hall full of light, and at its center, on a pedestal of woven silver, a shape the painter had rendered as a bloom of white fire — heart-shaped only if you squinted at it sideways, the way you squint at a word until it admits what it means. Courtiers around it in colors that hurt. A queen with her hand raised in blessing or farewell.

Here: the same bloom, carried in a box, and the carriers were not fae.

Paloma's lamp hand began, very slightly, to shake.

The carriers wore grey. Wardens' grey — the same cut, gods help her, the Order still sewed today, right down to the collar clasps. They moved through a night landscape, furtive, hunched over their stolen light. And at their head, rendered larger than life the way painters render the people they are told to honor, walked a woman with a lantern jaw and braids bound in warden's silver —

— and a face Paloma shaved around every morning of her childhood. A face that had taught her to whistle. A face carved, smaller and kinder, onto the memorial stone in the gatehouse yard: *MAREN ZHOU, KEEPER, WHO GUARDED WELL.*

"No," Paloma said. It came out very reasonably, almost conversationally, the way you decline a second helping. "No, that's — murals decay. Painters flatter. Someone commissioned —"

She ran out of commissioning excuses. The woman on the wall looked back at her across a century with her own eyes.

Sef had gone still beside the central panel. He didn't say *I told you.* He didn't say anything, which was worse and kinder than *I told you* would have been, and Paloma found herself absurdly grateful for the gentle humor of the moment he offered instead: "You have her chin. Also her habit of standing like the room owes her money."

"She was keeping us alive," Paloma said, and heard how young she sounded, how much like a girl defending a grandmother against the obvious. "The realms were failing. Everyone says — the histories say the fae hoarded the seasons, that the covenant was *mercy* —"

"The histories," Sef said, "were written by the people carrying the box."

* * *

She should have taken the winter-heart and run. That was the mission; those were the stakes; the long winter was already gnawing the lowland orchards white. Instead she stood in front of a wall like a child at a funeral, reading panels she didn't want to read, because her feet had decided that understanding came before saving.

The next panel showed the theft's aftermath. The fae court dimming, season by painted season — spring panels thinning to winter ones, the courtiers' bright clothes going grey at the edges like breath fading from glass. And threaded through the dimming court, unmistakable now that she knew to look, ran a second figure. Smaller. A fae youth with Sef's narrow build, holding the empty pedestal's shadow.

"They blamed him," Paloma said. Her voice had gone flat, which was how she knew she was close to tears. "Your exile. They said *he* let it be stolen."

"He was my age," Sef said. "Nineteen. He'd gone to the human side to trade songs for salt — there was a girl." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "When he came back, the heart was gone and the gates were closing, and a court that is dying needs someone to be angry at more than it needs the truth. So."

"So they cut you out and called it justice, and my lot wrote hymns about mercy." Paloma lifted the lamp higher. The final panel filled its light: the origin-stone, whole and unbroken, the winter-heart being sealed beneath it by joined hands — human hands and, she saw with a lurch, *fae* hands. Some of the thieves had been fae. Some of the fae had helped.

"Someone on your side sold it too," she whispered.

"Every crime has accomplices," Sef said. "That's usually how they afford to happen."

And there — she almost missed it — a line of text curling beneath the final image, in a script that predated the Order's letters but not her family's stubbornness, because her grandmother's hand was on it, added later, in charcoal: a warning, or an instruction, or possibly both:

*THE HINGE MUST BE LIVING. THE STONE ALONE WILL NOT HOLD WHAT WE HAVE TAKEN.*

Beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *Forgive me. I did it anyway. — M.Z.*

Paloma laughed. It was not a nice laugh, but it was hers, and it startled the frost spirals on the ceiling into glittering.

"She apologized," she said. "Look at that. She stole the beating heart of another world, doomed a kingdom to freeze, lied to four generations of her own family, and she wrote *forgive me* in charcoal like she'd broken a teacup."

"That's what thieves do best," Sef said gently. "They leave notes."

* * *

The winter-heart sat at the chamber's center, exactly where the mural promised, and it was nothing like the paintings.

The paintings had shown fire. The truth was quieter and far more arrogant: a crystal the size of a folded cloak, resting in a cradle of black iron roots that had grown — *grown*, she was done pretending otherwise — up through the chamber floor to hold it. Inside the crystal, slow as a heartbeat in deep sleep, light moved. Not flickering. Circulating. Winter's warmth, banked and hoarded, the whole furnace of the fae seasons locked in a jar while both worlds shivered around it.

Up close, it hummed. Old iron, Sef had said. It hummed the way a struck bell remembers ringing.

"It's beautiful," Paloma said, hating that she meant it.

"It's *ours*," Sef said, and there was no gentleness in that at all, and she didn't ask him to soften it, because looking at the dimming painted court behind her she thought she would feel precisely the same and say it precisely that way.

She reached out. The cold coming off the crystal bit at her fingertips and then, strangely, steadied — as if it recognized her hands, as if theft left a scent on a bloodline and the heart knew its thieves' kin on touch. She would think about that later. There would be a great deal of time, in the years ahead, to think about what the winter-heart knew about her that she didn't.

She got it free of the cradle. It weighed less than grief, which felt like a joke at her expense.

And that was when the lamplight behind them doubled, and a voice she knew better than her own conscience said:

"Step away from it."

* * *

Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane stood at the foot of the stairs with a drawn blade and six wardens at her back, and Paloma had one long, lucid heartbeat to take inventory of everything ruined. The tunnels' exits — Vane had found them. Bram's silence — bought or broken. The flight across the pass, the market at Corrow, the friend who had sold her — all of it converging here, at the bottom of the world, where Paloma Zhou stood holding the most stolen object in either realm like a sack of laundry.

"Sergeant Kell," Vane said, without taking her eyes off Paloma, "take the fae prisoner. Keeper Zhou — put the heart down. Slowly."

"Captain," Paloma said, and was distantly proud that her voice held, "before you arrest me, I want you to look at something. It'll take ten seconds. Then you can do whatever you came to do."

"Paloma—" Sef warned, low.

"Ten seconds," she repeated, and turned her lamp to the wall.

Vane's jaw set in the way that meant she intended to refuse. Paloma watched the refusal assemble itself behind the captain's eyes — duty first, questions never, that was the Vane she'd known all her life, the Vane who had once made her recite the covenant from memory as a girl and ruffled her hair when she got it word-perfect.

Then the lamplight reached the panel with the grey-clad carriers, and the woman with Maren Zhou's face, and Paloma watched ten years of discipline encounter a painting it had no drill for.

Vane stepped forward. One step. Two. Her sword arm lowered by degrees, the way a flag comes down.

"That's not possible," she said. It was barely aloud. "The founding records say the fae surrendered the heart. Under treaty. There are *signatures* —"

"Signatures are easy," Sef said. "Painters need to eat. Liars only need ink."

"Show me," Vane said, and it wasn't an order anymore; it came out stripped, almost young. She crossed to the theft panel and stood where Paloma had stood, and Paloma watched her trace the collar clasps on the painted wardens — the same clasps gleaming on the real wardens behind her — with one gloved finger, once, like a woman checking whether a wound was real.

The chamber was silent except for the winter-heart's slow hum and the breathing of six soldiers who could see their captain's face and were rapidly revising their evening.

"Captain?" Sergeant Kell said carefully. "The orders say the vault is to be sealed. Permanently. With the keeper inside, if —"

"I know what the orders say, Sergeant." Vane hadn't moved. In the painted night, her grandmother-figure — no, Paloma's, theirs, some ancestor shared by oath if not by blood — marched on toward a horizon that would spend a century paying for her. "I've obeyed what the orders say for twenty-two years."

She turned. And Paloma saw something shift in her face that frightened her more than the drawn sword had: not anger, not duty, but a kind of vertigo — the exact expression Paloma had worn three nights ago, at her own threshold, when the ordered world had cracked open under her feet.

"Twenty-two years," Vane said again, softly. Then, louder, in her parade voice, to her soldiers: "Kell. Take the men back up. Seal the cellar door behind you and wait for my signal."

"Captain, protocol says —"

"*Wait for my signal,* Sergeant."

Kell hesitated exactly as long as a career allows, then saluted and clattered up the stairs with the others. The lamplight dwindled. The door boomed shut.

And Paloma and Sef and Ilsa Vane stood alone in the dark with a stolen heart and a hundred years of painted evidence, and the captain of the Hollow Gate wardens said, in a voice Paloma had never heard her use — quiet, level, and terribly awake:

"Now. You're going to tell me everything the Order actually did. And then I'm going to tell you why I can't let you leave — even though I no longer believe one single word I was sworn to protect."

Behind her, faintly, from the top of the stairs: the sound of a signal whistle.
