## 9. Vane's Oath

Ilsa Vane had been a warden for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one lesson above all the others: the Order did not ask its officers to be right. It asked them to be certain.

She stood at the mouth of the vault beneath the Hollow Gate, torch guttering in air that tasted of old stone and older lies, and watched Paloma Zhou lift the winter-heart out of its cradle of iron and velvet like a woman lifting a sleeping child she had no right to hold.

The murals on the walls were still burning themselves into Vane's memory. She had seen them ten minutes ago and she would see them every night for the rest of her life: her Order's founders, painted in honest colors by some artist who had not known his work would be bricked behind a false wall for three generations. The founders, riding into the fae realm under banners of friendship. The founders, leaving with the winter-heart wrapped in oath-cloth. The founders, sealing it beneath the gate while frost bloomed behind them across a world they had promised to protect.

Paloma's grandmother was in the third panel, front and center, her hands on the chains.

Vane should have drawn her blade already. Protocol was very clear about what one did when keepers went traitor in the vault. She had written half of those protocols herself, in fact, during her years as Warden-Captain of Discipline, and there was a particular irony in that which she did not have time to appreciate.

Instead she said, "Set it down, Zhou."

"No." Paloma turned, and the winter-heart threw pale light up across her face. She looked exhausted, frostbitten along one cheekbone, and absolutely certain — which was, Vane noted with something like grief, the exact expression the Order trained into all of them. "You've seen the murals. You know what this is."

"I know what my orders are."

Behind Paloma, the fae exile — Sef, thin as a winter branch and bleeding through the bandages on his side — shifted his weight against the wall. "Captain," he said, and his voice had a rasp in it that suggested he'd been arguing with someone all the way down the mountain, possibly the mountain itself, "you're standing in front of the proof that your entire Order is built on theft. Wouldn't this be the moment for a crisis of faith? I've heard humans enjoy those."

"Sef," Paloma said quietly.

"What? I'm dying, I'm allowed to editorialize."

Vane raised her sword anyway — not to strike, but because her hand wanted the weight of something familiar. Thirty-one years. She had joined the wardens at sixteen because her mother had been a warden, and her grandmother before that, back through a line of women who had stood at doors and held them shut and never once asked what was behind them. Certainty. That was the inheritance. Not truth. Never truth.

"Zhou," she said. "When you were nine years old, you fell into the cistern at the waystation at Cold Ford."

Paloma blinked. "What does that have to do with—"

"Nothing. I'm stalling." Vane lowered the sword a fraction. "I'm trying to remember why I shouldn't do what my orders say, and I'm finding that the reasons are all personal ones, and personal ones have never counted for anything in the Order. You fell into the cistern. I pulled you out. You bit me."

"You were carrying me wrong," Paloma said. A small, stunned note had entered her voice.

"I was carrying you correctly. You were a feral child." Vane let out a breath that fogged in the vault's cold. "I have carried you correctly ever since, more or less. Kept you at the gate, kept you ignorant, kept you safe inside the lie. And now you're standing in front of the heart of it, and I find that the only thing between you and freedom is thirty-one years of doing what I was told."

* * *

Here is what Vane had never told anyone, in the way that secrets kept so long become part of the body, like an old break that aches before rain:

There was a document in the Order's archive called the Founders' Addendum. It was sealed. It was catalogued under a deliberately dull number — she had checked, once, twenty years ago, when she was young and ambitious and still believed archives rewarded curiosity rather than punishing it. She had picked that lock with a hairpin and read eleven pages by candlelight, and then she had relocked it, walked back to her quarters, and been sick into her washbasin.

The Addendum was the founding generation's own accounting of the permanent-sealing order. Not the public version — the public version spoke of *stability* and *the sanctity of boundaries*. The real one laid it out plainly, in the tidy hand of First Warden Aldous Crane, who had beautiful penmanship and no soul whatsoever:

*It must be understood by whoever succeeds us that full sealing is not a containment measure but a sentence. Cut off from the winter-heart, the far realm will fade within a generation or two. This is regrettable. It is also necessary, for a fading neighbor cannot invade.*

*Regrettable,* Vane thought now, watching Sef press his hand to his wounded side, *is a word men use when they mean profitable.*

The fae realm was not merely dying of the stolen heart. It had been *meant* to die. Slowly, deniably, over decades, so that no single generation of wardens would have to watch it happen — so that each keeper could inherit a gate and a duty and a comfortable fog of ignorance, and pass both along intact. The long winter now burying Thornfell wasn't a debt come due. It was the plan, arriving precisely on schedule, and the Order had spent a century sharpening it into doctrine.

And she had obeyed it. Every rotation, every report, every smuggler dragged off to Corrow in irons, every hairline crack sealed and logged — all of it in service of a slow murder dressed up as guardianship. She had even, she realized with a fresh wave of nausea, *enjoyed* parts of it. The order of it. The way a well-run gate felt like music. She had loved her work the way people love a song, without ever reading the lyrics.

"You know," Sef said into the silence, "for a woman composing her defection, you're very still."

"For a dying spy, you talk entirely too much."

"I'm told it's charming."

"He's lying," Paloma said. "It's not charming."

"It's a little charming," Sef said.

Vane almost laughed. That was the horror of it — here, in the belly of her Order's great crime, with treason on one side and duty on the other, she *almost laughed*, because these two bickered like an old married couple and some starved corner of her had apparently been waiting thirty years for exactly this kind of foolish honesty.

She put the laugh away. She put the sword away too, sliding it home in its scabbard, and watched Paloma's shoulders drop an inch.

"Here is what happens now," Vane said. "You will listen, because I will only say it once, and then we will never speak of it again."

* * *

"When I was twenty-two," Vane said, "I found the Founders' Addendum. I read what the sealing order actually does to the far realm. And then I locked it away again, and I told no one, and I served for twenty-nine more years." She kept her voice level, the tone she used for casualty reports. "Do you understand what that makes me?"

"A survivor," Paloma said softly.

"An accomplice. Every day I didn't act, the plan worked a little better. Every crack I sealed, every patrol I ran — I was the mechanism, Zhou. The Order doesn't kill the fae realm. The Order *holds the door* while the cold kills it, and holding doors is the only thing I have ever been good at." Her jaw tightened. "My mother held this same gate. So did hers. None of us asked. Asking was the one sin the Order couldn't forgive, and I was a good daughter, and I didn't ask until it was nearly too late, and then I asked and did nothing anyway. Because certainty is easier than truth. Because the cage is comfortable if you never measure it."

Sef had gone quiet, which on him seemed to cost effort. When he spoke again, the flippancy was gone. "My people think the wardens are monsters," he said. "I'll tell them instead that they're prisoners who guard their own cell and call it a fortress. I don't know which is worse for you."

"Neither. Both." Vane shook her head. "It doesn't matter what's worse for me. What matters—" she pointed at the winter-heart in Paloma's arms, its light pulsing faintly, like something breathing very slowly, "—is that. It goes back. Tonight, if the roads allow it, at the origin-stone above the high pass. That's where the covenant was first sworn; your prophecy folio says as much, and the folio has been more honest than my Order so far."

"You're letting us go," Paloma said. Not a question. Testing the words, the way you test ice before stepping.

"I am giving you a head start," Vane corrected. "Which is different in the ledger, and identical on the road." She reached to her belt and unclipped the second item there — not a weapon but a horn, curved black bone on a silver chain, the warden's summons. Paloma's eyes fixed on it immediately, because Paloma had always been quick, quicker than the Order ever deserved.

"If you're letting us go," she said carefully, "why are you still wearing that?"

"Because," Vane said, "when you reach the origin-stone — and you will reach it, because you are stubborn and lucky and you bite — the Order will come after you with everything it has. Lieutenants Hark and Osei, who are good soldiers and believe completely. Two hundred garrison troops. And the Sealed Door sect, who are not soldiers at all but believers, and who will do worse than kill you if they catch 'the keeper who opens' standing where their god is buried." She met Paloma's eyes steadily. "Someone has to warn you when they come. Someone has to slow them. And someone, when the moment comes, has to stand at that stone and choose — in front of everyone — whether thirty-one years of obedience means anything at all."

"That sounds like a defection," Sef observed.

"It sounds," Vane said, "like a warden doing her job. The job I was actually given, before the Order confused guarding with burying. My grandmother's grandmother took an oath at that same stone, according to the family stories — an oath to keep both realms safe from each other. *Both*, Zhou. Somewhere between her and me, the word *both* got lost, and I intend to find it."

Paloma was crying. She was trying not to — she had the Zhou gift for crying angrily, chin up, daring anyone to notice — but her eyes were bright and her voice came out rough. "Why didn't you arrest us? Protocol says—"

"Protocol," said Vane, "says a great many things. Here is what I say." She stepped aside, clearing the passage back up toward the smuggler tunnels, and made a short, formal gesture — the one wardens used to open a gate for authorized crossing. Using it for this felt like blasphemy and like prayer at once. "Go. Take the north gallery, not the south — the south passage flooded last month and Bram never fixed the map. Move quickly and quietly and do not, whatever you do, let the sect's scouts see the heart's light. Wrap it in your cloak. It glows when it's happy and right now everything makes it happy."

Paloma hesitated at the threshold. Of course she hesitated. She was the best of the keepers, the one the gate itself seemed fond of, and good people always hesitated at doors. "Vane. Come with us."

"No." It came out gentler than she expected. "My place is here. Someone has to misfile the pursuit orders, lose an hour's march, and generally be incompetent in ways that take decades of expertise to fake. Go *now*, Zhou."

Paloma went. At the tunnel mouth she paused once more, silhouetted against the heart's glow, and said, "Cold Ford. You carried me wrong."

"I carried you perfectly," Vane said. "You were feral. Go."

And they went — the keeper and the exile, footsteps fading up the north gallery, the light dimming, and then the vault was just a vault again, cold and mural-scarred and terribly quiet.

* * *

Vane stood alone for a while. She gave them a fair count — four hundred heartbeats, the traditional grace period, though tradition had never required her to count slowly and she counted very slowly indeed, savoring each beat like the last coins of a purse.

Then she took out the horn.

Her hands did not shake. She noticed that with distant interest. Thirty-one years of drills had wired her too thoroughly; the body knew its business regardless of what the soul had decided. She thought of her mother, who had died at her post with her boots on and her questions unasked. She thought of Aldous Crane's tidy handwriting. *Regrettable.* She thought of a girl in a cistern, biting the hand that pulled her out, and how maybe the girl had been right to bite.

Loyalty, she understood now, was not a virtue. It was a room. You could furnish it or starve in it, but you could not leave it by pretending it wasn't a room. She had spent her life calling the bars *duty* and the lock *honor*, and the only way out was through the one door nobody used: telling the truth out loud, in front of witnesses, and paying whatever it cost.

But not yet. Not quite yet.

Because the truth was, if she sounded the alarm *now*, the garrison would muster in minutes and run the north gallery down before Paloma cleared the tunnels. If she waited — if she misfiled, delayed, lost an hour the way she'd promised — the fugitives might yet reach the ridge road. And when the Order finally came, it needed to find her at the stone, standing where the choosing could happen. A warning from her was worth ten of anyone else's. A betrayal from her was worth a hundred.

So she did the last piece of warden's work she would ever do with a whole heart: she walked up out of the vault, through the gate-house halls where her authority lived in the very stones, and she set about being magnificently, deliberately incompetent. She sent Hark's company to check the south passage — flooded, useless, an hour wasted. She demanded a full inventory log before any muster, citing regulations she herself had drafted. She brewed tea for the duty sergeant and talked, at length, about drainage. Somewhere in the middle of describing the south culvert's many failings she saw Paloma and Sef, tiny as ants, emerge onto the ridge road far above, and something in her chest unknotted and immediately retied itself tighter.

Then there was nothing left to delay. The sun was going down. The long winter's storm was building in the peaks like an army assembling. And somewhere above, two small stubborn figures were carrying the world's stolen heart toward the origin-stone, and behind them the entire machinery of her life was winding itself up to hunt.

Vane climbed the gate-tower stairs. At the top, wind tore at her cloak and the horn chain cut cold against her palm. Below, Thornfell's valley lights were coming on, hearth by hearth, all those small warm fires burning on top of a buried theft. Above, the fae realm's frost gleamed white under the rising moon, beautiful and dying and blameless in the way of victims.

"Forgive me," she said — to the valley, or the mountains, or her mother, or perhaps to the woman she had been at sixteen, taking an oath she hadn't understood. "Both realms. Forgive me. I'm coming to do it properly at last."

She raised the horn to her lips, and blew.

The sound rolled down the valley and up the passes — three long notes and a fourth cut short, the warden's call for *fugitives at the gate, full pursuit, spare nothing* — and in the towers below, lights bloomed and boots hit floors and doors banged open across the garrison. In minutes the Order would be moving. In hours the sect would follow. By midnight the mountains would be full of armed certainty hunting two truths with swords.

Vane lowered the horn, slung her shield onto her arm, and started climbing. The origin-stone lay six hard hours above, through the coming blizzard, past the ridge where the dead were said to whisper. She intended to arrive first.

She was, after all, still the fastest warden in the garrison — and for the first time in thirty-one years, she knew exactly why she was running.
