## 10. Flight Across the Shattered Pass

The avalanche began as a whisper.

Paloma heard it before she saw it — a sound like a giant clearing his throat somewhere above the tree line — and her body knew what it meant before her mind caught up. Ten years of keeper's rounds had taught her the mountain's moods the way a shepherd knows a dog's: the settled rumble of ordinary wind, and this other thing, this wet, gathering *hush* that meant the snowpack had decided, all on its own, that it was tired of holding still.

"Down!" she shouted, and shoved Sef toward the rock overhang.

He went willingly enough — fae reflexes were quick even when the rest of him was half-frozen — but he had the presence of mind to grab the strap of her pack as they dove, which meant she didn't lose the winter-heart's wrappings, or the bread, or Bram's keyring digging its familiar teeth into her hip. Small mercies. She'd learned to count them.

Then the mountain came down.

It wasn't like anything she could have described to someone who hadn't heard it. Not loud so much as *total* — a white ocean pouring off the shoulder of the pass, swallowing the switchback trail they'd climbed since dawn, swallowing the pines, swallowing the little cairn of stones she'd built at the last rest stop because building cairns was a habit she couldn't seem to break. The air went white and then gray and then silent in that ringing, cottony way that followed catastrophe.

Paloma lay under the overhang with snow sifting down her collar and listened to her own heart hammering, and thought, with a strange detachment: *the warden trail is gone.*

"Are you alive?" Sef asked from beside her. His voice came out muffled through a faceful of scarf.

"Barely."

"Good. Barely is my favorite condition. It leaves room for improvement."

She laughed — an actual laugh, startled out of her, absurd and breathless — and then heard what he heard a half-second later: voices. Shouting. Carrying up the valley from below, thin and sharp as knife-points.

Crossbow bolts began to strike the snowfield where the trail used to be, punching neat black holes in all that pristine white.

"They're shooting at an avalanche," Paloma said flatly.

"They're shooting at *us*," Sef corrected. "The avalanche is just doing their work for them. Very efficient, your wardens. I almost admire it."

"You would."

"I would," he agreed cheerfully, "and I do."

* * *

They crawled. There was no dignified way to put it. They crawled along the base of the cliff on their elbows and knees while bolts hissed past overhead, and Paloma catalogued her bruises the way she once catalogued smuggler trails, because cataloguing kept panic in its box. Left shoulder: bruised. Right knee: possibly bleeding. Dignity: unrecoverable.

The lower road was buried. The mid-road was covered by crossbows. That left exactly one route, and Paloma had been avoiding looking at it since dawn, the way you avoid looking at a bill you can't pay.

The ridge road.

It ran along the spine of the pass itself — a knife-edge of ancient paving, older than Thornfell, older than the Order, older probably than the covenant, though nobody official would say so. The wardens called it forbidden. The smugglers called it the Whisperway. The maps in the gatehouse showed it as a single dotted line with three words beside it in Bram's careful hand: *Do not use.*

"Why is it forbidden?" Sef asked, when she told him. "Besides the obvious falling-off part?"

"The not-obvious part," Paloma said, hauling herself up onto the first slab of paving stone, "is that the boundary between realms runs thin along it. Thin enough to hear through." She paused, testing the stone with her weight. It held. "Smugglers who took the ridge road came back wrong. Or didn't come back. The Order's records say the dead talk up there, and the living listen too long."

Sef was quiet for a moment. Then: "My people have a word for roads like that. It translates roughly as 'the path that asks questions.'"

"And what do you answer?"

"Nothing," he said. "You keep walking. That's the whole trick of it. Roads like that only take you if you stop to chat."

"Charming," Paloma muttered, and started climbing.

* * *

The wind up on the spine was a living thing with opinions. It came off the fae side of the pass carrying cold that felt older than weather — a cold with intent behind it — and it tried, with great persistence, to peel them off the ridge like burrs from a coat. They moved bent double, roped together with smuggler's line Paloma had bought in Corrow back when buying rope had been her most dangerous act, and she clung to the memory of that woman — the one who'd only needed rope — the way you cling to a railing.

Below and behind them, the wardens regrouped. She could see them through the spindrift, tiny dark figures at the edge of the avalanche field, gesturing. Arguing, maybe. Vane's alarm had gone up two days ago; every garrison in the high pass was hunting them now. But no one, she noted with grim satisfaction, was willing to follow them onto the ridge road. Even zealots drew lines somewhere.

"So," Sef shouted against the wind, "the dead. Tell me more. For morale."

"That *is* morale?"

"For me, yes. I'm curious by nature. Terror and curiosity feel identical in my chest, so I've learned to pick whichever interpretation helps."

Paloma almost smiled. Almost. The ridge narrowed ahead until it was barely a hand's width wider than her boots, and beyond that the world simply dropped away on both sides — Thornfell's gray valleys to the south, and to the north something else. Something paler. The light over the fae side of the pass had been changing for days, she realized. Less like daylight. More like memory of daylight, secondhand, slightly faded.

And then the voices started.

At first she thought it was wind whistling through a crack in the paving. Then she thought it was her own blood in her ears. But the sound resolved, gradually, the way a face resolves out of fog, into speech — dozens of voices, hundreds, murmuring just beneath the threshold of words, rising out of the stone itself, out of the air, out of the terrible pale distance to the north.

*Sef,* she started to say, and then stopped, because he had gone rigid on the rope, and his ears — she'd never noticed before how very slightly pointed they were — had turned toward the sound like a deer's.

"They're here," he whispered. "All this time I thought the pass was empty. It isn't empty. It's *full.*"

"Don't stop to chat," Paloma reminded him, and kept walking.

But the voices were already finding her.

They came gently at first, the way sleep comes — a phrase here, a name there, half-heard. A man complaining about the price of oats. A woman singing three notes of a lullaby and forgetting the rest. Someone laughing, far away, about nothing. The border-dead, she understood suddenly. Everyone who had ever died on this pass or near it, everyone whose lives had brushed the thin place and soaked into it. Smugglers and wardens and travelers and fools, all of them still murmuring along the ridge road like guests who'd never been shown the door.

A child's voice, close to her ear: *"Is it morning yet? Nobody will tell me if it's morning yet."*

Paloma's throat closed. She kept walking. One boot. Next boot. The trick of it, Sef had said. Don't stop to chat.

*"Keeper,"* said another voice, deeper, and she knew it instantly, the way you know a bell you were raised under.

Her grandmother.

Maren Zhou had died when Paloma was nine, and Paloma remembered her mostly in fragments: hands smelling of lamp oil, a laugh like a door slamming in a friendly way, the way she'd stood at the Hollow Gate at night with her coat collar up, listening to it. Paloma had built her whole life in that shadow without ever examining it — the way you don't examine the ground you stand on.

Now the ground was talking back.

*"Little owl,"* her grandmother's voice said, warm and unrepentant, using the childhood name Paloma hadn't heard in twenty years. *"You found it, then. Good girl. Stubborn as our line. I knew you would."*

Paloma stopped walking.

That was the mistake. The moment her boots stilled, the voices swelled around her like water filling a footprint, and the ridge road tilted — or seemed to tilt, or perhaps truly did, the boundary being thin here, thinner than any map dared say — and suddenly she was not entirely on the ridge anymore. She was standing in a kitchen she remembered, lamplit, smelling of cedar smoke, and her grandmother sat by the fire with a ledger open on her knees, young in death as she'd been old in life, and looked up at her with bright, unashamed eyes.

*"You're angry,"* Maren Zhou observed. *"I can tell. You walk angry. Always did, even at six."*

"You stole it," Paloma said. Her voice sounded small in the lamplight. "The winter-heart. You led them. The murals — your face is on the murals, Grandmother. You broke the covenant and let the fae side freeze and lied about it for a hundred years, and you *knew*, and you let us keep the gate, keep the lie, generation after generation—"

*"Yes,"* her grandmother said pleasantly. *"All true. Would you like tea? You're shivering."*

"I don't want tea! I want—" Paloma's fists shook. "I want you to be sorry."

Something flickered across the dead woman's face — not guilt. Regret's cousin, maybe, the kind that mourns consequences rather than crimes. *"Little owl,"* she said, softer, *"when the famine came, the winter-heart fed Thornfell through three starving winters. Your grandfather lived because of it. Your mother. You. I chose my people. Every thief in history has made that same choice and called it love, and every one of us would make it again. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. You deserve better than pretense — you always did. It's why I liked you best."*

"I was nine."

*"And already asking the wrong questions beautifully."*

Somewhere far away, Sef's voice was calling her name through the storm, growing hoarse. The kitchen flickered at its edges like a candle in draft. Paloma stood in the warmth of a fire lit with stolen wood and understood, finally, with a clarity that hurt worse than the cold: there would be no absolution here. No confession. Her grandmother had weighed the worlds and chosen, and would choose again, and the dead did not revise.

Which meant the choosing fell to her.

*"You'll fail, you know,"* Maren Zhou said, watching her turn to go. Kindly. Horribly kindly. *"The rite needs a descendant who gives the heart back gladly, and no one gives back what saved their family. I couldn't have done it. Your mother couldn't. You're made of the same stubbornness, little owl, and stubbornness holds on."*

"Maybe," Paloma said. And then, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter after all: "But you held on to a *lie*. I'm holding on to the truth, and truth weighs less. I checked. I've been carrying both, and the truth is lighter."

For the first time, something like surprise crossed the dead woman's face.

Paloma walked out of the kitchen into the screaming wind, and the ridge road was under her boots again, and Sef was hauling on the rope with both hands, shouting her name with a crack in it.

* * *

"—don't you *ever*," he was saying when she could hear him properly, shaking her by the shoulders, snow crusted in his eyebrows, his composure cracked clean through. "Don't stop on this road. Don't you ever stop. I felt you go. The line went slack and then it went *elsewhere*—"

"Sef."

"—I was going to cut the rope and follow you, do you understand? Into whatever it was. I don't even know what it was, and I was going to follow you anyway, which is either loyalty or stupidity and I refuse to find out which—"

"*Sef.*"

He stopped. Breathed. His hands were trembling on her shoulders, and not from cold.

"What did it show you?" he asked quietly.

"My grandmother." Paloma looked back the way they'd come, at the murmur of the dead trailing behind them like a tide going out. "She wasn't sorry. She'd do it again. She thinks I'll fail because I'm made of the same stubbornness she was."

Sef considered this. "Are you?"

"No." She pulled the rope tight between them and turned north, toward the pale light and the frozen court and the origin-stone waiting somewhere beyond both. "She was stubborn about keeping things. I'm stubborn about giving them back. It's a different animal entirely."

And the strange thing — the thing she would remember all her life — was that terror didn't leave her. It stayed right where it was, a live coal in her chest. But resolve arrived and sat down beside it, and the two turned out to burn hotter together than either did alone.

Behind them, faint through the wind, her grandmother's voice laughed — a door slamming in a friendly way — and said something Paloma chose not to hear.

Ahead, past the ridge's end, the fae realm lay open under its faded light. And on the horizon, small as a thumbnail, dark as a wound: torches. Dozens of them. A column of figures moving along the frozen road toward the court — wardens' formation, but wrong, ragged, chanting something she could feel through her boots before she could hear it with her ears.

The sect had reached the pass. And they were marching toward the origin-stone, singing, carrying something on a bier between them that glinted like frost.

Sef saw it a heartbeat after she did. "Is that—"

"They have a heart," Paloma said slowly. "Or something shaped like one. And if they reach the stone before we do—"

The wind took the rest of her sentence and scattered it northward, where the dead murmured it along the ridge road, all the way down into the listening dark.
