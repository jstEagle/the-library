## 11. The Court That Forgot Its Name

The last mile into the court, Sef stopped talking.

Paloma noticed. She had gotten used to his chatter the way you get used to a river — a background music of complaints, riddles, and unsolicited opinions about which berries were trying to kill them. For three days he had narrated everything: the geology, the weather, the tragic history of every crooked pine. Then the trees thinned, the snow turned blue-white in the failing light, and he went quiet as a held breath.

"What is it?" Paloma asked, breath fogging.

"Home," Sef said, and the word came out wrong, like a coin bitten and found to be lead.

They crested the ridge together, and there it was.

The Court of the Winter Bough had been beautiful once. Sef knew this the way you know your own face — not from memory exactly, but from the shape of the longing. The palace had been grown, not built: pale spires of living silverwood, bridges of woven frost that sang when courtiers crossed them, gardens where the snow itself bloomed into flowers that lasted a season and a day. His mother had been born under those bridges. His grandmother had planted half those gardens.

Now it stood like a candle burned down to the wick.

Frost sheathed everything, but not the friendly frost of winter. This was frost with intent, frost as a lid on a pot. The great gates hung open, locked mid-swing by ice a foot thick. A fountain in the forecourt was frozen mid-leap, water arced and glittering like a thrown necklace no one would ever catch. And in the courtyard beyond, figures stood in the snow — dozens of them, scores, arranged in loose groups as if caught between one sentence and the next.

A courtier with her hand raised, mid-gesture, lips parted around some word ninety years unspoken.

Two guards facing each other over a game of stones, neither having moved since before Sef's father was born.

A child, small, frozen mid-run toward something out of sight.

Sef's legs simply stopped working. He sat down in the snow without deciding to.

"Sef?" Paloma crouched beside him. "Hey. Talk to me."

"That's Lissabet," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. He pointed at the running child. "She was four. My aunt's girl. She's still four." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Ninety years old and still four. She'd be older than me now."

Paloma put a hand on his shoulder. She didn't say anything, which was, he was learning, one of her better qualities. The human keeper had a talent for silence that most fae would have traded a wing for.

* * *

They walked into the courtyard because there was nowhere else to go, and because Sef had spent eleven years telling himself he was coming home, and because some cowardly part of him wanted to get the disappointment over with all at once, like pulling a splinter.

The cold here was different. On the pass it had been an enemy, sharp and active, trying to kill them. Here it was passive, patient, a museum's climate control. The cold wasn't attacking the court. It was preserving it.

He touched the nearest figure — an old steward named Corvin who had once fined Sef two copper leaves for sliding down the banister of the west stair. Corvin's sleeve crunched under his fingers but did not give. Behind the frost on the old man's face, his eyes were closed, his expression mild, almost amused. As if he'd fallen asleep waiting for something.

"They're alive," Paloma said quietly. She had her keeper's eye narrowed, reading the ice the way she read the gate — for cracks, for movement, for signs of what lived underneath. "There's a pulse. Slow. Very slow." Her gloved fingers rested against Corvin's wrist. "One beat every..." She counted. "Every few minutes."

"The winter-heart kept them," Sef said. He heard his own voice go flat and scholarly and was grateful for it; flat was survivable. "It wasn't just power. It was the season itself. It turned winter into a rest instead of a death. When my people took it—" He stopped. Corrected himself. *When the humans stole it.* Careful. Careful. "When it was taken, the winter stopped being a season and became... a pause. Everyone here is paused."

"Including whoever did the taking," Paloma said. "The thieves were human. They left."

"Yes." He looked at her. "And everyone who stayed has been holding their breath for a century."

She absorbed that. He watched her absorb it — watched the keeper's composure hold for three full seconds and then crack along a hairline, exactly like the gate she'd spent her life mending.

"My grandmother," Paloma said. "Did she know? What it would do?"

"She knew." Sef hadn't meant to say it so bluntly. But they were standing in a courtyard full of sleeping children, and gentleness felt like a luxury purchased with someone else's blood. "Everyone knew. That's why they did it at midwinter, during the Long Rest, when the court couldn't fight back. Your ancestors didn't rob a fortress, Paloma. They robbed a bedroom."

Paloma turned away from him. Her shoulders were very straight. After a moment she said, in a voice sanded down to bare wood, "Show me the rest."

* * *

The throne room doors were frozen ajar, and they slipped through sideways.

Inside, the great hall stretched up into shadow, its ceiling lost somewhere above the dark. Braziers stood unlit along the walls. And at the far end, on the living-wood throne that had grown up through three generations of queens, sat the Queen of the Winter Bough.

Half-thawed.

That was the horror of it, Sef thought. Not the stillness. The *incompleteness*. Frost covered the Queen like a second gown, but around her face and hands it had receded — whether from her own fading strength or from some quirk of the theft, he didn't know — and beneath it she was moving. Barely. Her chest rose once every long minute. Her fingers twitched against the armrest, slow as tree sap, as if even in her prison she was still signing decrees, still reaching for a daughter who—

Who was looking directly at Paloma.

"Oh," said the Queen.

Her voice shouldn't have carried. It was a dry whisper, a sound like pages turning. But the hall was built for a queen's voice, and it carried the way grief carries — further than it should, into places you can't defend.

"Oh," she said again, and her eyes, gray as thaw-water, fixed on Paloma's face with an intensity that made Sef's skin crawl. "You came back. You *came back*."

"I—no," Paloma started. "Your Majesty, I'm not—"

"You cut your hair." The Queen's hand rose, agonizingly slow, frost flaking from her knuckles like ash. "You always cut it too short. I told you. I told you the court would talk." The whisper frayed into something almost like laughter, almost like weeping. "Come here. Come here, little rootling, come away from the door, it's cold by the door—"

"I'm not your daughter," Paloma said. Firm. Keeper-firm. The voice she probably used on smugglers.

But she stepped forward anyway — one step, involuntary, pulled by the gravity of being mistaken for someone loved — and Sef saw her face do something complicated and terrible. Because whatever else was true, no one had ever looked at Paloma Zhou like that. He'd known her six days and he could see it. She stood in the beam of that desperate, maternal, ninety-years-late love like a woman standing in sunlight after a life underground, and she couldn't decide whether to bask or burn.

"Your Majesty." Sef moved to stand beside Paloma, partly to shield her, mostly because his knees were unreliable. "It's Sef. Sef of the Autumn Door line. Ilin's son."

The Queen's gaze slid off him like rain off glass.

Then slid back.

"...Sef." Something flickered behind the frost. Recognition, maybe, arriving at glacier speed. "Little Sef. You were going to be a gardener. You were always in the beds, digging where you shouldn't." Her whisper cracked. "You ran. You got out. Why did you run, little gardener, why are only the children allowed to leave—"

"The heart was stolen," Sef said. His voice shook. "From Thornfell side. Humans took it, and the winter never ended, and I ran to find help, and the help never came, and I'm sorry. I'm sorry I didn't come back. I'm sorry it took me eleven years."

"Stolen." The word moved through the hall like wind through wheat. And then, horribly, from the shadows along the walls, other voices began to stir — the half-thawed ones, the strongest ones, courtiers whose faces had broken free enough to speak. They'd been standing in the dark listening. How long had they been listening?

"Stolen," echoed a woman near the eastern pillars.

"They took it," rasped a man whose uniform marked him as a captain of the guard, his jaw working against the ice like a man surfacing from deep water. "They took it and they sealed their door and they lit their fires with our summer—"

"Why?" That voice came from everywhere and nowhere, thin and multiplied, a chorus of the nearly-waking. "Why did they hate us? What did we do? Someone tell me what we *did*—"

"We didn't do anything!" another voice, young, cracking. "I've been trying to remember! Ninety years I've been trying to remember what we did, and I can't, because we didn't do *anything*, we just—"

"We were wronged," the captain said, and it came out like a creed, worn smooth by repetition. "We were wronged. We were wronged. We were—"

"*Stop.*"

Paloma's voice cracked across the hall like a whip. Every frozen face turned to her. Even the Queen stilled.

Sef stared at his companion. The human keeper stood in the middle of a hall full of the wronged dead-not-dead, her fists clenched, her chin up, wearing the expression of someone about to arrest an entire courtroom.

"You want to know why they fought you?" Paloma said. "I'll tell you. Because my ancestors stole from you and needed you to be monsters so the theft would look like war. That's why. There was no reason. You're looking for a reason because *we* needed one, and you inherited the search." Her voice broke on the last word and she pushed through it. "The humans who did this are dust. Their grandchildren are dust. One of their granddaughters is standing right here, and I am telling you the truth, which is more than any of them ever gave you: you were wronged by cowards, and the story they told was a lie, and I'm going to spend whatever's left of my life unpicking it stitch by stitch if I have to."

Silence. The vast, ringing silence of a hall full of people who had waited ninety years for exactly that and never believed they'd hear it.

Then the Queen began, very softly, to laugh.

It was not a pleasant sound. It was the laugh of someone whose mind had been circling the same frozen pond for decades and had finally found a crack in the ice.

"A truth-teller," the Queen whispered. "My daughter always lied beautifully. And here comes a human who tells the truth like it's a weapon she found lying in the road." Her thawed gray eyes glittered. "Oh, little rootling. Oh, keeper. You'll do. You'll do *nicely*."

"Your Majesty," Sef said carefully, "we've come because there may be a way to wake you. All of you. If the winter-heart can be returned—"

"Returned." The Queen's head tilted, slow as a shifting iceberg. "Yes. Bring it home. Bring it home and set it in the origin-stone where the first oath was sworn, truth for truth, gift for gift—" Her voice slurred, sank, the tide of her brief waking going out. "Bring it... and bring my daughter..."

Her eyes drifted shut. The frost crept back over her face like a blanket drawn up by a careful nurse.

In the quiet after, Paloma exhaled shakily. "She called me rootling."

"She calls everyone that," Sef said automatically. Then: "No. No, she doesn't. She only ever called—" He stopped.

The hook of it went in deep and twisted. Because he had spent six days watching Paloma — the shape of her ears, the stubborn set of her mouth, the way frost seemed to *like* her, curling decorative ferns on her sleeves while it tried to kill him outright. Six days of noticing things and filing them under *coincidence*.

And now a queen half-mad with frozen time had looked at a human woman and seen her stolen daughter.

"Paloma," he said. "There's something I need to tell you. About the prophecy. There's a line I haven't read you yet, and I should have read it days ago, and I'm a coward, and—"

The far doors of the throne room boomed.

Once. Twice. A third time, deliberate as a heartbeat — and underneath the boom came a sound that did not belong to the court at all. A human sound. Metal, and boots, and the thin high whistle of crossbow bolts being fitted to strings.

Paloma and Sef turned together toward the doors.

Through the gap where they stood ajar, torchlight spilled into the frozen hall — orange, ordinary, horribly familiar.

Wardens. They had crossed the pass.

And they were not alone.
