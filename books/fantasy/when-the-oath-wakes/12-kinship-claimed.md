## 12. Kinship Claimed

The frozen court had a sound to it that Paloma could not stop hearing even after they left it behind: the creak of a thousand throats trying to remember how to speak.

They made camp that evening in the lee of a shattered watchtower, one of the old boundary forts from before the covenant, its stones furred with frost that glittered like sugar. Sef built a fire with a flick of two fingers and a handful of dried rowan berries — fae craft, small and practical, nothing like the grand sorcery Paloma had imagined — and they sat warming their hands while the light died violet over the pass.

Paloma had not spoken much since the court. She kept seeing the Queen's face, half-thawed, ice melting down her cheek like tears that had started a hundred years ago and simply never finished falling. The Queen had looked at Paloma and said *my daughter*, in a voice like wind through a keyhole, and Paloma had felt something crack in her chest that she suspected would not knit cleanly.

Sef turned a berry over in his fingers. He was quiet too, but it was a different quiet — the quiet of a man arranging words in order of cruelty, she thought, deciding which to say first.

"Say it," she said at last. "Whatever you're chewing on. Say it or spit it out."

He looked up. In the firelight his eyes were that strange pale color, winter-sky gray, and for the first time since she'd met him bleeding on her threshold, he looked afraid of her.

"You'll want to hit me," he said.

"I've wanted to hit you since chapter — since the tunnels." She waved a hand. "Get on with it."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't. He set the berry down on his knee, precisely, the way he did everything, as if even grief deserved good posture.

"There's a second line in the prophecy," he said. "The folio your Order keeps locked away. The part they burned out of every copy they let anyone read."

"The keeper who opens," Paloma said. "I know. It names tonight's date. It named me, apparently."

"That's the line they left in." His voice dropped. "There's one after it."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, ice calved off a rock face with a sound like a distant door closing.

"Tell me," Paloma said.

Sef took a breath. "The covenant can be reforged only at the origin-stone. Only by returning what was stolen. And only—" his jaw tightened, as if the words physically resisted coming out, "—only by blood of both realms. Not blood spilled. Blood *shared*. One person who carries both. A child of Thornfell and of the court, together in one body, standing at the stone and choosing."

Paloma waited for the rest. When it didn't come, she laughed — short and sharp, surprised out of her.

"Well, we're fortunate there, aren't we," she said. "Because no such person exists. I'd have noticed. I've spent my whole life on that border, Sef. I know every face in every village from the gate to Corrow, and not one of them has pointed ears under their hat."

"No," he agreed softly. "But you have pointed ears somewhere in your tree."

The laughter died in her throat.

"Don't," she said.

"Your grandmother," Sef said, "was Maerin Kavanagh."

The name landed in the snow between them like a dropped knife. Paloma knew that name. Of course she knew that name — it was carved on the keeper's memorial stone inside the gate-house, *Maerin Kavanagh, First Warden of the Sealed Peace*, the woman who had supposedly negotiated the covenant, the ancestor whose portrait hung in the entry hall with a stern mouth and a medal on her breast. Paloma had grown up beneath that portrait. Had straightened her spine in front of it every morning of her apprenticeship, trying to stand the way a hero stood.

"What about her," Paloma said. Her voice came out flat and careful, the way you walk on river ice.

"She wasn't human," Sef said. "Not wholly. Her mother was of the winter court — my great-aunt, Liriope, third daughter of the Queen who is frozen in her own throne room right now because of what your family carried away. Maerin was raised on the human side after the first skirmishes, passing. Passing so well that when the theft was planned, she was the perfect agent. No one guards against their own."

"You're lying." It came out very quietly, which frightened her more than shouting would have.

"I'm telling you why the vault keys answer to your family's blood," Sef said. "Why Bram — who knew Maerin personally, who helped hide the heart — trusted *you*, specifically, with a gate no ordinary warden could hold. Why the gate hums when you touch it. You've felt it. Don't tell me you haven't. Every keeper feels the gate, they say. But you *hear* it, don't you? Like it's talking."

Paloma's hand had gone, without her permission, to the hollow of her throat where, all her life, she had felt a faint cold pulse whenever she stood too near the Hollow Gate. She had assumed it was the mountain wind through the pass. She had assumed a great many things.

"Even if any of that were true," she said slowly, "it wouldn't make us kin. It would make my grandmother a thief and a liar, which I have recently learned anyway, thank you, I got the mural tour. It doesn't make you my cousin."

"Maerin had a sister," Sef said. "Born on the fae side, raised in the court. My mother."

The fire cracked. Paloma stared into it and saw, absurdly, the ledger from her first night of rounds — her neat handwriting cataloguing smuggler trails, the ordered little world she had guarded without question for eleven years. It seemed very far away now, and very small, like a dollhouse.

"You're saying I'm half fae," she said. "Me. I'm twenty-nine years old. I've been eating bread my whole life. I get cold. I sunburn. Last summer I had a splinter for three days."

Sef did smile then, briefly, helplessly. "Half-bloods run human, mostly. The old blood sleeps unless it's woken near a threshold. That's why the prophecy waits for a keeper — someone who has lived pressed against the gate until it soaked into them like dye into cloth." He leaned forward. "Paloma. I didn't come over the wall that night by accident. I came looking for the descendant of Maerin Kavanagh, because you are the only person alive who can carry the winter-heart back to the stone. Not just anyone. *You.* Blood of both realms, standing at the origin-stone, choosing—"

"*Choosing*," Paloma repeated, and something in her chest went from cracked to molten. "That's what this is. This whole road. You didn't find me by accident, you didn't save me from Vane's hunters out of kindness — you've been herding me toward a ritual like a sheepdog. Is that what I am to you? A *component*?"

"It isn't—"

"Because I have to tell you," she said, standing, her voice rising now, echoing off the broken tower, "that is the *story of my entire life*. Everyone needs something from the keeper. The Order needs me to hold the door and ask nothing. The smugglers need me blind. The zealots need me holy. Bram needed me obedient. And now you need me *ancestral*." She flung a hand toward the darkened fae side of the pass. "You need me to be your cousin so the prophecy works, and you didn't even have the decency to let me be your *friend* first!"

Sef rose too, hands open, palms out. "I am your friend."

"You're a man with a mission who found a useful stranger!" She heard her own voice crack on the word stranger and hated it. "Do you know what I did yesterday? At the court? I held the Queen's hand and told her I wasn't her daughter, and some traitor little piece of me *wanted* to be. Because she looked at me like I belonged somewhere. And you knew. You stood there and you *knew*, and you let me feel that, because it makes the ritual easier if I'm already half in love with the idea—"

"That's not fair."

"No," she said. "What's not fair is being born a hinge and having everyone in two realms decide what I swing for."

She snatched up her pack. It was stupid — night was falling, the whiteout season was coming, the ridge road was death without company — and she knew it was stupid, and she did it anyway, because her feet needed to be moving faster than her thoughts or the thoughts would catch her.

"Paloma." Sef's voice followed her, rough. "Wait. Please. I should have told you at the tower. I should have told you at the court. I kept waiting for the right moment and there isn't one, there never is, and I'm sorry — *I'm sorry* — but don't walk into the high passes alone, the storm comes down the ridge an hour after dark—"

"Then I'll walk fast."

"Paloma—"

She stopped. Turned. The firelight caught them both, ten paces apart, two people shaped by different winters wearing identical expressions, and that was somehow the worst part — that she believed him, that some deep animal part of her had believed him from the moment he said *Liriope*, because it explained the pulse in her throat and the way the gate sang and the hole in her life she'd never been able to name, the feeling of being a guest everywhere including her own skin.

"If you're my cousin," she said, "then you'll understand this better than anyone ever has."

She saw him brace.

"I need to be alone," she said, "and I need you to let me be. Not chase me. Not protect me. Not shepherd the precious prophecy-fulfilling cousin through the snow. Let me be alone, Sef, the way nobody in either realm has ever let me be anything — freely."

His jaw worked. Everything in him visibly wanted to argue; she could see the counterarguments queuing behind his teeth. But he had crossed a dying realm to keep a promise, and promises were the one language they shared.

"One night," he said finally. "Follow the ridge east till you reach the split boulder — there's a smuggler cache there, blankets, tallow, the good stuff, Corrow brandy if it hasn't leaked. Take it. It's yours. At dawn—" his voice caught, steadied, "—at dawn I'll find you at the stone, or not at all, whichever you choose. That's the thing about choosing, cousin. It only counts if you could have chosen otherwise."

It was, infuriatingly, exactly the right thing to say.

Paloma walked east into the dark.

* * *

The split boulder was easy to find even by starlight, because it was less a boulder than a small mountain that had lost an argument with lightning sometime in the last century. The cache was exactly where smuggler lore said caches always were — under the flat stone, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as a sermon. Blankets. Tallow candles. A tin of hardtack. And yes, a clay bottle of Corrow brandy, perhaps two-thirds full, tasting faintly of pine pitch, which was how all Corrow brandy tasted and was considered part of its charm.

Paloma sat with her back against the split stone, wrapped in a smuggler's blanket, drinking smuggler's brandy by smuggler's candlelight, and laughed at herself until the laugh turned into something else and she let it, because there was no one to see and no one to be, for one blessed night, except tired.

Half fae.

She turned her hands over in the candlelight. Ordinary hands. Keeper's hands, calloused in the pattern of lockwork and ledger pens. She tried to feel different and couldn't. She tried to feel betrayed and succeeded instantly, but underneath the betrayal ran another current, colder and stranger: relief. Terrible, guilty relief. Because all her life there had been a question she hadn't known how to ask — *why do I not fit, what is wrong with the shape of me* — and tonight someone had finally answered, and the answer was *nothing is wrong with you, you were only ever measured with the wrong ruler.*

And the same answer meant her grandmother had lied with her whole body, had passed, had hidden, had helped steal the beating heart of a world and then accepted a memorial stone for it. Paloma thought of the mural in the vault, Maerin's painted figure turning away with the heart in her arms, and understood for the first time that the turning-away hadn't been shame in the artist's imagination. It had probably been a portrait. Painted from life.

She slept badly and woke to gray light and the smell of snow on the wind — the real smell, the long-winter smell, sharper than it had any right to be this far south. The storm Sef had promised was coming down the ridge right on schedule, a white wall swallowing the peaks to the west.

She packed the blanket, pocketed the brandy, and stood looking east down the long descending road toward the Hollow Gate — toward home, if home was still a thing she owned. Two days' walk. Maybe three in snow. Behind her, somewhere north, Sef would be keeping his word, waiting for dawn that had already passed, honoring a choice she'd demanded he respect.

Her throat pulsed, cold and steady, like a second heartbeat. The gate calling its keeper. Or its cousin. Or its hinge. She was going to have to decide what she was before she arrived, because everyone else had decided for her long enough.

She took one step, then another, and was three miles down the road, the storm chewing the skyline behind her, when she saw the smoke.

Thin, black, wrong — woodsmoke didn't burn black — rising from the notch ahead where the road bent around the shoulder of the mountain. And threaded under the wind, faint but unmistakable to ears that had spent eleven years listening for it, came the sound she had catalogued a hundred times in her ledger without ever expecting to hear aimed at her:

Bells. Warden bells. And voices singing the hymn of the Sealed Door.

Paloma stepped off the road into the rocks and pulled the smuggler's blanket over her head, and began, quietly, to plan.
