## 13. Alone on the Long Winter Road

The quarrel with Sef had lasted three days in her head before it lasted five minutes on the ridge, and even now, walking away from him through the knee-deep snow of the high pass, Paloma could hear both sides of it replaying like a coin spinning on a table.

*Blood of both realms,* he had said. *Your grandmother took more than the heart, Paloma. She took a daughter. My mother's sister. Which makes us—*

*Don't.* She had said it too loudly. The frozen pines had shed their snow at the sound, a soft white collapse all down the slope, as if the mountain itself flinched. *Don't you dare hand me a family like a bill of debt.*

And then she had walked. Because that was what she knew how to do. Twenty years of rounds along the Hollow Gate had given her legs that did not ask permission and a spine that did not negotiate, and she put both to work now, marching south and east along the forbidden ridge road with her breath tearing out of her in flags.

It was not until the light began to fail — early, the way winter light failed here, like a candle pinched between wet fingers — that she admitted she had no plan beyond *away from him*.

"Well," she told the empty road, because talking to herself was another thing twenty years alone at a gate had taught her, "this is going well."

The ridge road was called the Widow's Spine on no map that officially existed. Smugglers had cut it generations ago, a shelf of track stitched along the mountainside where the border between realms wore thin enough to hear through. Bram had forbidden it. The wardens pretended it did not exist. And Paloma had walked it more times than she could count, because the smugglers who used it had never once asked her to pretend they didn't, and there was a kind of honesty in that which the Order's ledgers had never managed.

She heard the dead within the first mile.

That was the thing about the Spine nobody wrote down: the wind carried voices. Not ghosts exactly, or not only. Echoes that had gotten loose, conversations from both sides of the gate pooling in the thin places like water in a stone cup. Tonight they murmured under the wind in fragments — a woman counting sheep in a language Paloma almost knew, a man laughing, someone very far away singing a lullaby with the melody worn smooth as river stone.

"Keep your voices to yourselves," she muttered, and pulled her collar up, and walked on.

* * *

The storm came for her two hours past dark.

She felt it before she saw it — the particular hush that fell when the mountain drew breath to spend it. The murmuring voices went quiet all at once, the way birds do, and Paloma stopped so abruptly she nearly sat down in the snow.

Whiteout. She'd read about them in the old smuggler journals Bram pretended he hadn't given her, back when she was fourteen and hungry for anything the Order wouldn't teach. *When the mountain goes quiet,* one weathered hand had written in the margin of a route-map, *you have about the time it takes to say three prayers. Spend them wisely. First prayer: shelter. Second prayer: rope. Third prayer: don't be clever.*

She looked around. The world was already going woolly at the edges, snow rising off the ground to meet snow falling from the sky, erasing the difference between up and down. In a quarter hour there would be nothing to see at all. In half an hour, nothing to stand in.

Shelter. Rope. Don't be clever.

Her eyes found it the way they never would have found it six months ago: a slight wrongness in the slope below the trail, a shadow with the wrong texture, snow drifted against something that wasn't rock. A boulder overhang — or near enough. The smugglers called features like that *grandfather stones*, and every journal she'd ever read marked them with the same notation: a circle with a tail, like a fishhook. She'd thought it quaint once. Decoration. Superstition.

Now she scrambled downslope toward it with the storm chewing at her heels.

The overhang was real, a lip of granite thrust out over a hollow floored with dry pine needles and old ash — someone had sheltered here before, many someones, over many years. There was even a cache: a flat stone set into the wall, and behind it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, a coil of rope, half a candle stub, a tin of rendered fat, and a note.

The note said: *Take what you need. Leave what you can. Nobody asks whose.*

Paloma sat back on her heels and stared at it while the storm arrived overhead like an avalanche deciding to happen slowly.

*Nobody asks whose.* No name. No sigil of the Order. No ledger line, no entry, no accounting. Just the plain grammar of people who survived the same mountains by agreeing to be generous to strangers they would never meet.

She thought of the vault beneath the Hollow Gate, and her grandmother's face painted tall and triumphant on the mural, and the careful beautiful lie of the covenant folio. Everything the Order touched came stamped and sealed and signed. Ownership. Credit. Blame.

This tin of fat belonged to no one, and because of that, tonight, it belonged to her.

"You sentimental fool," she said aloud, to herself, to her grandmother's ghost, to the storm. "You've been an outlaw your whole life and you're only now noticing."

* * *

She did not sleep so much as she held vigil over her own body, waking hourly to move her toes and fingers and feed the tiny fire she built in a ring of stones left precisely for that purpose. Somewhere in the black hours the storm reached its full voice, and the hollow roared like the inside of a bell, and Paloma discovered that a whiteout is not silence. It is the loudest nothing in the world.

In one of the gaps between sleeps, she caught herself doing the thing Sef did — cocking his head, listening past the wind. The thin place was close here; even muffled by storm she could feel the boundary like a scar that ached before rain. And beneath the roar, faint as a memory of a voice rather than a voice itself:

*—the keeper walks alone, then. Stubborn as her line. Good. Stubbornness is the only inheritance worth having—*

She sat bolt upright, heart slamming.

"Grandmother?"

Nothing. Wind. The storm laughed at her in its wordless way, and after a while she lay back down and told herself firmly that grief hallucinated voices in storms, that exhaustion did it, that she was not going to start taking directions from a dead thief.

But she lay awake a long time anyway, doing arithmetic she couldn't stop doing.

If her grandmother's ghost walked the ridge road — if the dead of both realms pooled in these thin places — then her grandmother had been crossing this border in death for sixty years without a gate, without a key, without anyone's permission. And somewhere out in that white roar were other voices. Fae voices. Human ones. All of them mingling in the snow like spilled ink in water, impossible to tell apart anymore, which side of the border any of them had come from.

Maybe that was the truth under everything. Maybe the border had always been thinner than the oath pretended. Maybe realms were not walls but watersheds, and you could steal the heart of one and dam the passage between them, but you could not stop the seep.

The third prayer, according to the margin-writer, was *don't be clever*. Paloma smiled into the dark and broke it thoroughly.

* * *

Morning came gray and blind. The storm had spent itself sometime before dawn, leaving the world remade — the trail erased, the sky and slope one seamless pearl, the sun a brighter smudge behind cloud that gave light without direction. A post-storm day, the journals called it. The kind that killed more travelers than the storm itself, because survivors woke up confident.

Paloma was not confident. She was methodical.

She ate a strip of dried elk from her pack and melted snow in the tin for water, and while she waited for it, she got out the oldest of Bram's smuggler maps — the one she'd taken from his keyring bundle along with the keys themselves, though she suspected he'd slipped it in deliberately, the sly old badger — and studied it by the flat light.

Here was the thing about the Spine: it could not be seen in fresh snow. But it could be *read*. The smugglers' lore was full of tricks she'd once filed under colorful nonsense. Snow that lay in long ridges like plowed furrows meant wind-scoured stone beneath — safe footing. Snow that domed smoothly, like bread risen over a pan, hid hollows and drop-offs. And the cairns — the little stacked stones that dotted the route — had a grammar all their own. Three stones upright: the path continued straight. Two upright, one tipped: turn at the next landmark. One stone alone, placed atop a taller one like a hat: danger, go around, trust nothing.

Bram had made her memorize it when she was sixteen, drilling her across the kitchen table with walnuts and corks. *"A keeper who can't read the smugglers' marks,"* he'd said, *"is a keeper the mountain will eat."* And then he'd made her swear never to tell the wardens she knew, because the wardens used the knowledge to set ambushes, and Bram's loyalties had always been a house with many doors.

She found the first cairn forty minutes out, mostly buried, its top hat-stone just breaching the drift like a periscope.

Danger. Go around.

She went around, wide, testing each step with her staff, and twenty yards later her staff punched through a crust into nothing — a crevasse mouth hidden under new snow, deep enough that the dropped pebble she tossed in never reported landing.

Paloma stood at the edge of that invisible hole with her heart going like a drum and understood, finally and completely, what she owed and to whom.

Not the Order. The Order had taught her liturgy and lock-drills and the proper way to fold a flag. Useful things, honorable things, and every single one of them useless on this road.

Everything keeping her alive had come from the people the Order despised. The smugglers' maps. The smugglers' cairns. The grandfather stones and the cached rope and *nobody asks whose.* The weather-sense Bram had drilled into her over stolen card games. Even Sef — arrogant, wounded, kin-claiming Sef — had taught her how to walk on fae-frost, how to read the wrongness in the air before the ice betrayed her, how to listen past the wind.

She had spent her whole life believing she stood apart from the borderlands' outcasts — their warden, their watcher, their respectable opposite. And all the while she'd been their student, their heir, their creature. Her competence was a quilt sewn by every hand the covenant had pushed to the margins.

"I owe everybody," she announced to the pearl-colored morning. It came out less like despair than she expected. Almost like relief. Debts, at least, were a kind of belonging. "Fine. Noted. Add it to the ledger."

She walked on, reading the snow like scripture.

* * *

Two days she traveled thus, and the road taught her its lessons gladly, the way old teachers do once the student stops arguing. She learned to navigate by the voices in the thin places — not trusting them, but noting them, because a voice that repeated in the same spot twice was a landmark of sorts, and the lullaby-singer appeared to be permanently stationed above the second switchback like a resident ghost with a repertoire of one. She learned which drifts held and which lied. She learned to sleep in short banked shifts, to travel the blue hours when the crust firmed, to rub fat into her boots at night against the wet.

On the second evening she found another cache, and this one held something unexpected: a child's mitten, knitted in the lopsided way children knit, tucked into the oilcloth like an offering. Someone had sheltered here with a kid. Someone had made it through, or hadn't, and either way had left the small red mitten behind as a marker — *we were here, we were human, remember us kindly.*

Paloma held it a long time. Then she set it back exactly as she'd found it, added a strip of elk jerky to the cache, and left before her eyes could do anything embarrassing.

*The keeper who opens.* That was what the prophecy called her, and she was beginning to suspect the old writers had understood something she hadn't: that opening was not a thing you did once at a gate. It was a thing you practiced, daily, in caches and cairns and mittens left for strangers. The whole borderlands had been quietly practicing her prophecy for a century. She was just the last one scheduled to notice.

She was still turning that over when she smelled woodsmoke.

Wrong direction for it, too. There were no settlements on the Spine, no legal ones, and the smuggler camps she knew had all been a valley south. This smoke came from ahead, upslope, threading through the pines in a thin gray ribbon that the evening wind was bending toward her like a pointing finger.

Paloma doused her own fire and went forward low, using the drift shadows, staff silent, all those borrowed skills moving through her body like a congregation saying words they'd known since childhood.

She heard them before she saw them: voices raised together, rhythmic, chanting. Not smugglers. Smugglers sang bawdy songs and argued about card debts. This was a liturgy, cold and clean, dozens of voices in unison —

*The Door is Holy. The Door is Shut. Blessed are the hands that keep it shut. Cursed is the keeper who opens—*

Through the trees, firelight. Below her, in a bowl of the mountainside, a camp of white-robed figures knelt in ranks around a bonfire, faces upturned, and on a crude scaffold of pine poles they had raised a wooden effigy of a woman with a keeper's chain around its neck, and they were burning it.

At the edge of the fire, wrapped in robes too large for her, standing very straight with a convert's fever-bright eyes, was Marta. Marta of Corrow, who had sold her pardon with a smile, who had shared plum sweets with Paloma under a market awning when they were nine years old.

Marta lifted her face to the smoke and said, clear across the snow: "She comes. Brothers, sisters — the prophecy walks toward us on her own feet. The keeper who opens is coming to the stone. And we will be there to meet her."

Paloma pressed herself flat against the cold earth of the ridge, her breath shallow, her mind already running the map — the camp sat square across the only pass down to the origin-stone, and there was no way around it that the storm had left open.

Every skill she owned, and not one of them, she realized, for getting past a hundred kneeling believers and a friend turned zealot.

Above her, faint through the pines, the lullaby-singer's voice rose once more — and this time, unmistakably, it was singing a warning.
