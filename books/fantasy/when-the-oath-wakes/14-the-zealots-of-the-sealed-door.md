## 14. The Zealots of the Sealed Door

They came out of the snow like a judgment.

One moment Paloma was following the old drover's cut along the river gorge, counting her steps the way smugglers did when visibility failed — *three hundred to the leaning birch, four hundred to the ford* — and the next there were lanterns everywhere, six of them, maybe eight, held by grey-robed figures who had been standing in the drifts so long that snow had built up on their shoulders like small patient hills.

She never even reached for her knife. That was the humiliation of it. Eight against one, in the open, and her body simply understood what her pride refused to: this was not a fight. It was an appointment she hadn't known she was keeping.

"Keeper of the Hollow Gate." The tallest figure stepped forward and pushed back his hood. He had a long, gentle face, the kind you'd trust to tell you bad news kindly, and eyes that burned with the particular brightness of a man who has found an answer and intends to share it whether or not anyone asked a question. "We have prayed for you by name."

"That's flattering," Paloma said, "and also slightly alarming."

He smiled as though she'd said something holy. Behind him, the others began to move, closing around her with the unhurried confidence of people who believed the universe was handling logistics for them.

*Well,* she thought, as someone took her knife, her pack, and — after a brief theological debate about whether her boots counted as worldly attachment — very nearly her boots. *This is what I get for walking alone on a road I only half-remember.*

The last thing she saw before they drew the hood down over her head was Sef's tracks, three days cold now, leading off toward the high pass without her.

She'd told him to go. She'd all but shoved him onto the road. *You'll be faster alone,* she'd said, which was true, and also a lie, because what she'd meant was *I need to be alone*, and the mountain had heard both and punished her for the second.

* * *

They called themselves the Order of the Unbroken Seal, which, Paloma reflected as the sledge jolted her over frozen ruts, was at least honest advertising. The hood smelled of tallow smoke and dried herbs, and beneath it she could hear them singing — a low, droning hymn in which every verse ended with some variation of *the door is shut, blessed be the shut door, may it be shut forever.*

It was, she had to admit, extremely catchy. By the fourth hour she had caught herself humming it and stopped in horror.

When they finally removed the hood, she was inside a chapel carved into the mountainside itself — no, not carved, she realized; *hollowed*. A cave system behind a fallen watchtower, lit by hundreds of candles arranged in a pattern she recognized immediately and would have recognized drunk, blindfolded, and dead: the seal-marks of the Hollow Gate, reproduced at forty times their size across walls and ceiling, glowing gold in the candlelight.

"Oh," she said quietly. "You've built a shrine to my front door."

"Your door," said the gentle-faced man, "is the last fingerhold of chaos upon the world. We have watched it for sixty years. We have starved for it. We have buried our children within sight of it rather than let them grow soft enough to wonder what lies beyond." He pressed his hand to his chest. "I am Father Aldous. And you are the keeper written of in the Ash Prophecy — *the one who opens.*"

"I'm also the one who locks it," Paloma said. "Daily. There's a whole ledger."

"The ledger is a veil." He said it with such serene certainty that she felt a chill walk up her spine that had nothing to do with the cave. "You were chosen, Keeper. Marked. The prophecy does not say *the one who guards.* It says *the one who opens.* And we have read the rest of the line, the part your Order keeps locked away from its own faithful."

"And what part is that?"

Aldous smiled again, gently, terribly. "*And the gate shall drink of the opener, and be satisfied.*"

The candles flickered. Somewhere deep in the cave, water dripped into a pool with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

"That's not—" Paloma started, then stopped, because she had actually read the folio, more thoroughly than anyone alive, and the line was real. She'd always taken it as metaphor. Gate-drinking, covenant-blood, the usual florid nonsense prophecy traded in. But Aldous's face said he had never once considered the possibility of metaphor in his life.

"You intend to sacrifice me," she said. Not a question. Just checking the arithmetic.

"We intend to *free* you," Aldous corrected warmly. "Your whole life has been service, hasn't it? Rounds, ledgers, cracks sealed before dawn. No family. No rest. No love but a door." He shook his head with genuine sorrow. "We offer you glory instead. The gate drinks, the world is safe forever, and you become the holiest thing this age has produced. Honestly, Keeper, I'd have thought you'd say yes."

And that was the worst part — the truly unforgivable part, the part that made her want to spit at him — because for half a heartbeat, standing in the golden light of a shrine to her own prison, some exhausted corner of her *did* consider it. Sixty years of faith. Warm fires. People who looked at her and saw purpose instead of a woman who talked to a lock.

Then she thought of Bram, who had lied to her for love and given her his keys anyway. Of Vane, whose oath was a cage but who had hesitated at the murals. Of Sef, bleeding on her threshold, calling her cousin.

"No," she said. "But thank you for asking. Nobody ever asks."

* * *

They kept her in a cell that had once, judging by the rusted bracket still bolted to the wall, been a warden way-station storeroom. The sect had repurposed everything: ration crates became altars, a duty roster became scripture, scrawled-over with commentary in three colors of ink until the original text was mostly theoretical.

Paloma catalogued it all, because cataloguing was what her hands did when her mind needed somewhere to stand. Two guards outside, rotated hourly. One window, barred, facing the gorge. Her captors took her boots — they *had* eventually decided on the boots — which meant escape planning would proceed barefoot, which meant it would proceed cleverly or not at all.

On the second day, they brought her supper, and Paloma looked up into the face of Maren Collier.

Maren had grown up three doors down from the warden barracks in Corrow. She had once stolen honey cakes for Paloma when Paloma was sick, and eaten most of them on the way, and sworn with tears in her eyes that it was the fever making the bag lighter. She had freckles and a gap in her smile and, as of four days ago, a pardon signed by Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane, purchased with Paloma's location.

"Maren," Paloma said.

"Don't." Maren set the bowl down fast, not looking at her. "Don't do the thing where you look at me like that. I know the thing. Everyone in Corrow knows the thing. You look at people like you're putting them in the ledger."

"You sold my position to Vane's informants for a piece of paper."

"A pardon!" Maren's voice cracked upward. "My brother was in the warden cells for smuggling, Paloma. They were going to send him to the ice quarries. You don't— you were never going to understand, because you never needed anything from anybody. You had your door and your duty and your precious Order, and the rest of us had *choices*, ugly ones, and you got to judge them from up on your mountain."

Paloma sat with that. It stung precisely because it wasn't entirely wrong.

"I'm not judging you," she said finally. "I'm just sad. Those are different things. I'd have thought you knew the difference, after the honey cakes."

Maren flinched as if struck. For a moment something moved behind her face — the old friend, the girl who'd shared a blanket during the Corrow floods — and then it was smothered, deliberately, under doctrine.

"I found the truth here," Maren said stiffly. "The Seal protects us. It's always protected us. If the door opens, everything ends — the fae come through, the winter comes through, *everything*. The Father says the sacrifice will hold it closed for a thousand years. A thousand years, Paloma. Isn't that worth one life?"

"Whose life?" Paloma asked softly. "Say it plainly. Whose life are you buying your brother's freedom and your clean conscience with?"

Maren stared at the floor. "Yours," she whispered.

"And does the Seal protect *me?*"

Maren left without answering, and Paloma ate the stew, because martyrdom on an empty stomach was just dying tired.

* * *

The days developed a rhythm, and rhythm was dangerous, because rhythm was how captivity became ordinary. Morning hymn. Midday interrogation — gentle, endlessly patient, Aldous asking the same questions in different clothes: *What did the fae promise you? What did they show you? What did they steal your heart with?* Evening prayers, sung so beautifully that Paloma caught herself humming again and bit her tongue until it bled.

She learned things, too. That was the trap of zealots — they were generous with information, because information was gospel to them, and gospel was meant to be shouted. She learned that the sect had been watching her gate for decades, funded by tithes from frightened lowland villages. She learned that they had copies of the Ash Prophecy older than the Order's own restricted folio, and that Aldous quoted passages Bram had never mentioned. She learned that the ritual at the origin-stone required the keeper to be *willing* — or at least, Aldous said, appearing to believe it, prayer could make her willing, given time.

Time, she noted, was the one resource they weren't worried about spending.

"They're waiting for the solstice eve," she said to herself on the fifth night, pressing her forehead to the cold window bar. "That's tonight. Or tomorrow. I've lost count, and the count matters."

Because the prophecy named a date. Because the long winter was deepening — she could feel it even here, a pressure in her chest like weather, the stolen heart's debt coming due. Because if the gate woke while she sat in a cave singing counterpoint to a shut door, everything she'd climbed out of the mountains to prevent would happen anyway, just with worse company.

She tried the window bars again anyway. They didn't care about her schedule.

* * *

On what she had calculated — optimistically — as the sixth day, Aldous came to her cell wearing ceremonial grey instead of working grey, and carrying a small wooden box that made Paloma's teeth ache to look at.

Inside, nested in wool, lay a shard of black stone shot through with silver veins.

"Do you know what this is?" he asked.

"Origin-stone fragment," she said. "Taken from the site generations ago. It's why your hymns sound almost right and slightly wrong — you're harmonizing with a broken piece of the covenant."

Something flickered across his face — irritation, quickly mastered. "You are knowledgeable. Good. It makes the rite easier when the vessel understands her purpose." He knelt so they were eye to eye, and his gentleness was at full power now, and it was awful. "Tomorrow night, at the stone, the gate will try to wake. The prophecy promises it. But the prophecy also promises us the means to stop it — permanently. Your blood, willingly given or otherwise, poured into the origin-stone, and the door shuts forever. No more fear. No more fae. No more winter creeping over the pass. Your grandmother's generation stole to protect us, whatever the liars say. We will finish what they started."

"My grandmother," Paloma said carefully, "stole the winter-heart, and the winter you're afraid of is *her bill coming due.* Sealing the gate doesn't cancel the debt, Aldous. It just makes sure nobody's home to pay it."

"The Father does not accept revisionist history," said one of the guards, and Aldous raised a hand.

"He's frightened," Aldous said to her, kindly, as if explaining a child to a guest. "We're all frightened, Keeper. That's what faith is for. Tomorrow night, you can be frightened with us, or you can be frightened alone. Either way, the Seal holds."

He left the box on her shelf, deliberately, where she could see it. A reminder. A promise. An invitation.

Paloma waited until his footsteps faded, then put her back against the wall and laughed — a short, cracked, unamused laugh that echoed strangely in the stone room.

*Willingly given or otherwise.* So much for the willing part. And tomorrow night — solstice eve, gate-waking night, the date written in ash a hundred years ago — she would be trussed up at the origin-stone like an offering, surrounded by a hundred singing believers, with no knife, no boots, and no Sef.

No Sef. She pressed her eyes shut. She'd sent him away. She'd chosen loneliness like it was a discipline, and now the discipline was going to kill her, and the worst part — the genuinely unbearable part — was that Maren would probably sing at the ceremony. Beautifully. In harmony. With feeling.

She was still sitting there, doing the arithmetic of despair, when she heard it.

At first she thought it was the drip of water in the deep pools. Then she placed it: a tapping. Three slow knocks, a pause, two quick ones. From the ventilation shaft near the ceiling, the one too narrow for any human shoulder.

Paloma went very still.

Three slow. Two quick. Old border code — smuggler code, the kind she'd disdained her whole career and memorized anyway, because Bram had taught her everything he knew including the things he pretended not to know. It meant exactly one thing:

*I'm inside. Don't scream.*

Her heart slammed against her ribs. She crawled to beneath the shaft and knocked back, fingers shaking, the reply she'd known since childhood: *Who's there?*

A pause. Then, muffled, unmistakable, in a voice hoarse with cold and effort and what sounded suspiciously like relief:

"The cousin you sent away. And I brought the keyring's little sister — Bram's spare keys were on the same ring as his flask, Paloma, honestly, the man labels nothing."

Paloma pressed her hand over her mouth. Above her, in the dark of the shaft, something shifted — and with it came a faint, cold shimmer of light, and the smell of frost and pine and home that wasn't hers yet but might be.

"Now," Sef whispered through the grate. "About these bars. How attached are you to a quiet rescue, versus the loud one? Because I should mention — I may have already started the loud one."
