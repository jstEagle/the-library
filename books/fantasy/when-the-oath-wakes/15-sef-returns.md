## 15. Sef Returns

**Paloma**

They fed her twice a day, which she supposed was generous of them, considering they intended to kill her.

The porridge was terrible — over-salted, under-cooked, the kind of meal made by people who believed suffering improved flavor. Paloma ate it anyway, sitting cross-legged in her cage of woven hawthorn branches while the zealots of the Sealed Door sang their evening hymns around the fire. The hymn had four words repeated forty times. *The door stays shut. The door stays shut.* She'd counted. There wasn't much else to do.

"You're not eating," said Maren.

Maren sat on a stump outside the cage, wrapped in a gray wool robe that had once belonged to someone smaller. She had been Paloma's friend in Corrow, back when friendship meant sharing stolen plums off Dossiter's cart and daring each other to touch the cold iron of the gate road markers. Then Maren had sold her name to Vane's informants for a pardon. And now here they both were: one in a cage, one on a stump, watching each other across the gap where a friendship used to be.

"It's very salty," Paloma said.

"It's penance food." Maren hugged her knees. "Salt draws out sin."

"That's not salt's job."

"Everything has its proper job now, Paloma. That's the whole teaching." Maren's voice did something complicated on her name — guilt and fondness tangled together like yarn a cat had been at. "You should eat. Father Ellam says the sacrifice must be strong for the rite."

"The keeper who opens must be strong," Paloma corrected. "I've read your doctrine. It's badly organized, by the way. The prophecy quotes contradict each other in three places."

Something flickered across Maren's face — the old Maren, the one who'd laughed when Paloma pointed out errors. Then it hardened again. "Faith isn't about organization."

"No," Paloma agreed. "It's usually about being afraid of something and deciding the fear is holy." She ate another spoonful of terrible porridge. "Who taught you that?"

Maren didn't answer. She got up and walked toward the fires, and Paloma watched her go and felt, unexpectedly, sad rather than angry. That was the worst part of the last two weeks. Anger kept wearing out, and what was underneath it was just grief with nowhere to put itself.

She looked up at the sky through the hawthorn lattice. Snow clouds, low and bruised-colored. Two days' walk south, if her sense of direction could be trusted through the drug they'd fed her in Corrow, was the origin-stone. Father Ellam intended to sacrifice her there at moonrise tomorrow, opening the gate just long enough to prove it *could* be opened, then sealing it forever with her blood as the hinge. A demonstration faith. A martyrdom with an audience.

Paloma had been turning over escape plans all day, and every one of them ended the same way: alone, on foot, in the mountains, in winter, without supplies, pursued by forty zealots who knew the passes better than she did. She was a keeper, not a miracle worker. Keepers guarded doors. They didn't walk out of cages through forty singing believers.

So when the camp's third watch changed and she heard — very faintly, from the tree line, pitched exactly the way Sef pitched things when he was trying not to be noticed and failing magnificently — a sound like a large man stepping on a small owl, she felt something she hadn't felt since Corrow.

Hope. Annoying, reckless hope.

*Oh no,* she thought. *He came.*

* * *

**Sef**

Sef had infiltrated many places in his long, unlucky life: a duchess's masquerade, a smuggler kingpin's counting house, one memorable funeral where he had been mistaken for the deceased's illegitimate son and given a very good horse as inheritance. He considered himself good at infiltration.

He was not good at infiltration.

The problem, he reflected as he lay flat in a snowbank with a zealot's boot six inches from his ear, was that fae were built for beauty, not stealth. His ears caught light. His cheekbones cast shadows in starlight. And his left knee, which had been frostbitten on the ridge road and never quite forgiven him, chose this precise moment to announce itself with a crack like green wood splitting.

The boot stopped.

Sef held his breath. Above him, the sentry hummed the four-word hymn off-key — everyone sang it off-key, it was apparently part of the devotion — and shuffled onward into the dark.

Sef exhaled into the snow and permitted himself a moment of self-pity. He had crossed half the shattered pass on a knee that hated him, following the sect's trail by their fires and their terrible singing, telling himself the whole way: *she refused you, cousin or not, she looked at you like the winter itself and walked away, why are you doing this?*

Because she was going to die alone on a stone tomorrow, that was why. Because the Queen — half-thawed, half-mad, calling him by his dead brother's name — had gripped his wrist and said, *the hinge is ours, bring our hinge home*, and for the first time in a century of exile Sef had understood that he still had a family, and that family included a stubborn human woman with salt-cracked hands who had saved his life before he'd earned it.

Also because the alternative was sitting in the frozen court listening to his kin try to remember why they hated humans and getting it wrong.

He wriggled forward. The camp was laid out in a rough ring around the central fire, cages and tents and a shrine cart bearing a painted image of the Sealed Door — the painter had gotten the proportions wrong, which offended Sef professionally. Paloma's hawthorn cage sat on the ring's eastern edge, near the horse lines. Of course. Near the horses, because Father Ellam planned to move her to the origin-stone at dawn.

Two guards at her cage. Both awake. One was sharpening a knife with the meditative slowness of a man who loved his knife more than his god.

Sef took stock of his assets: one knife, borrowed; one vial of thaw-balm, nearly empty; one knee of dubious loyalty; and the accumulated guile of a hundred and thirty years, most of which had gotten him exiled.

He began to crawl.

* * *

**Paloma**

The trouble started, as trouble so often did around Sef, with a noise.

It came from beneath the cage — a soft, strangled sound, like a cat being stepped on by a polite man. One of the guards frowned. The other stood up, knife out, and peered into the dark beyond the firelight.

Paloma did the fastest thinking of her life.

"Heard something?" she called out cheerfully, rattling the hawthorn bars. "Probably a fox. We get foxes. Terrible thieves, foxes. Once one stole an entire smoked ham off my windowsill at the gate — I chased it a quarter mile in my nightshirt. Bram never let me forget it."

Both guards turned to look at her, because everyone always turned to look at the prisoner who talked, and in that half-second of diverted attention there was a scuffle below the cage floor, a muffled grunt, and then a hand — pale, long-fingered, familiar — thrust up through the gap between the woven branches and the ground, holding a knife handle-first toward her.

Paloma took the knife.

"I'm cutting you out," Sef whispered through the wicker, his voice strained. "Don't scream."

"I never scream. I catalog. Cut faster."

The blade sawed through the hawthorn weave, and Paloma worked at the bindings from her side, tearing at them with fingers gone clumsy in the cold. Below her, Sef whispered a running commentary that was either reassurance or a prayer: "Almost through — don't mind the guard, I've handled the guard —"

"You haven't handled anything," hissed the second guard, whom Sef had in fact not handled, rising behind him with his cudgel raised.

Later, Paloma would have difficulty reconstructing the next thirty seconds. There was a crash as her whole weight went through the weakened cage wall, hawthorn splintering, and she landed on top of Sef, which knocked the wind out of both of them and also knocked him directly into the path of the descending cudgel. She rolled him aside — he was lighter than he looked, or she was stronger than she knew — and the cudgel struck the frozen earth where his head had been.

Then everything happened at once. The first guard shouting. Fires flaring as sentries ran. Maren's voice rising above the din, high and cracked, crying the alarm. And Sef, flat on his back in the snow, laughing breathlessly.

"You *did* save me," he wheezed. "I want it noted. In the ledger. Officially."

"Noted. Run."

They ran. Behind them the camp erupted — torches kindling, horns blowing, forty voices finding their four words at last and screaming them like a war cry instead of a lullaby. Paloma grabbed Sef's arm and hauled him upslope, away from the horse lines — too far, too many men — into the trees, along a game trail she'd marked mentally from her cage window over two days of nothing better to do.

Which was when the crossbow bolt took him in the shoulder.

* * *

**Sef**

Pain, Sef had learned, was mostly a negotiation. You offered it attention, and it demanded more.

This bolt declined to negotiate. It simply arrived, high in his left shoulder — the right shoulder would have been insulting but survivable, the left ruined his grip on the slope — and spun him sideways into a pine trunk. The world went white at the edges. Somewhere behind him the hymn had become a hunt, torchlight bobbing through the trees below, and above him Paloma's voice cut through the roar in his ears:

"Sef. *Sef.* Look at me. Stay upright."

"I'm famously upright," he managed. "It's my defining trait."

"Lean on me. We're going down to the streambed — the water will kill our scent — move *now*—"

He moved. He was not proud of how he moved. It resembled less running than a controlled avalanche of one fae, propped against a human woman half his elegance and twice his stubbornness, crashing downhill through brush while bolts thudded into trunks around them. His knee gave out twice. Both times she caught him, grunting, swearing in fluent borderlands dialect that would have made his grandmother faint, hauling him back into motion.

At the streambed the cold hit like a blessing. They plunged in — thigh-deep, brutal, the current shoving at them like a rude stranger — and stumbled downstream thirty yards, fifty, until the torchlight behind them fractured and dimmed among the trees.

Paloma pulled him under an overhang of bank, into a hollow the floodwaters had carved, and only there let him collapse.

"Bolt's got to come out," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, checking the wound, were shaking. "It didn't go deep. Lucky angle. Hold still."

"Wait—" He caught her wrist. "Before you do. I need to say it now, because if you pull that bolt out of me I may say nothing coherent ever again."

"Then say it fast."

"The prophecy line I didn't tell you. Blood of both realms reforges the covenant. I told you I thought you were my cousin because of your grandmother. That was true. But it wasn't the whole reason I followed you." His breath was ragged; the cold was reaching his bones now, past the pain, a dangerous warmth. "I followed you because when you refused me — properly refused me, with excellent insults — it was the first time in a hundred years anyone told me the truth to my face. My court forgot how. My exile forgot how. Everyone lies kindly or cruelly, and you just — refuse. Honestly. It's the rarest thing I know."

Paloma stared at him. Torchlight flickered far upstream. The bolt's fletching trembled with his pulse.

"So," he said, "cousin or not. Kinship claimed or kinship earned. Whatever we are — I'm asking to be it. Formally. On the record."

Above them, the hunt spread along the banks. Below them, the water ran black and freezing. And Paloma Zhou, keeper of the Hollow Gate, looked at the wounded fae exile bleeding in a river hole and did the thing she had walked away from once already on a frozen road.

"Cousin," she said. "There. Witnessed by a stream and several idiots with crossbows. Now hold *still*."

* * *

**Paloma**

Getting the bolt out was worse than either of them pretended afterward. She made him bite down on his own belt, braced his shoulder against the bank, and pulled in one hard motion while he made a sound like a kettle achieving enlightenment. Then she packed the wound with snow to slow the bleeding, bound it with strips of his shirt, and fed him the last of the thaw-balm, which he protested was needed for frostbite and she overruled on grounds of seniority, having known him longer, she argued, than frostbite had.

"There," she said finally, sitting back against the frozen bank. "You'll live. Unfortunately."

"Your bedside manner is legendary."

"I learned it from wardens." She was quiet a moment. The hunt had moved off downstream, drawn by some misdirection of tracks she'd laid on the way down — old smuggler lore, Bram's lore, the lore she'd spent years pretending was beneath a keeper's dignity. "Sef. Why does the camp smell like smoke? Real smoke. Not cookfire."

He lifted his head, nostrils flaring — fae senses, sharper than hers even battered and bloodied. Then his eyes widened.

They climbed the bank together, slowly, his arm over her shoulders, and looked back north through the trees.

Father Ellam's camp was burning. Not attacked — burning *itself*. Torches had been carried tent to tent, robes to robes, the shrine cart with its wrongly-painted door roaring like a pyre, and in the middle of it all the zealots stood in a circle, singing. Not the alarm hymn anymore. Something new, four different words, voices rising ecstatic into the snowfall: *the door will open, the door will open.*

"They think you escaped because the door *willed* it," Sef breathed. "Their prophet's cage couldn't hold their prophet's sacrifice. They're burning the profane world behind them and walking to the origin-stone tonight. All of them. Ahead of schedule."

Paloma watched the flames climb, watched Maren's small gray figure pass before the blaze, and felt the cold settle into her in a way that had nothing to do with the stream. Forty zealots, marching through the night, straight toward the stone where the covenant waited to be reforged — arriving, perhaps, at the exact hour she did.

"They're not chasing us anymore," she said. "They're *racing* us."

Sef winced as he shifted his bandaged shoulder, and grinned at her anyway — that crooked, infuriating, thoroughly earned grin. "Then I suggest, dear cousin, that we run."
