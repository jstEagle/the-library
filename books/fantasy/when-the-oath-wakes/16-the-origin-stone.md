## 16. The Origin-Stone

The storm broke at dawn the way a held breath breaks — all at once, and everywhere.

Paloma had climbed through three days of whiteout on smuggler lore and stubbornness, and when the clouds finally tore open above her, she saw where they'd arrived and nearly sat down in the snow to laugh, or weep, or both.

She knew this place. Of course she knew it. She had known it since she was seven years old, before she knew what knowing meant.

The Hollow Gate stood behind her now, half a day's climb down the eastern shoulder of the mountain — but the origin-stone was here, up in the saddle between two granite peaks, where no keeper's patrol ever went because there was nothing to guard. A ring of standing stones, nine of them, weathered smooth as river pebbles, arranged around a flat slab of black rock that older maps called *the First Table*. Bram had shown it to her once, from far below, pointing with his pipe.

"That's where they signed it," he'd said. "The first covenant. Before your time or mine."

He hadn't said who *they* were. He never did.

Now she stood in the ring itself, snow to her knees, and Sef came up the last of the slope behind her, moving like a man made entirely of bruises and determination. He stopped beside her and looked at the stones, and his breath went out of him in a long white plume.

"It's smaller than I imagined," he said.

"Everything is," said Paloma. "That's how they get you."

* * *

They worked in the silence of people too tired for speeches. The winter-heart — wrapped now in three layers of wool and one of Sef's cloak, because it had begun, in the last day, to hum — sat in the center of the First Table while they cleared the snow away from the carved channels that radiated outward from it like the spokes of a wheel. Paloma traced them with bare fingers, numbness be hanged; some things you had to touch skin to stone.

"They're oath-channels," Sef said quietly, crouched across from her. His fingers were blue at the tips and he didn't seem to notice. "I've seen their like in the court vaults. When a covenant is sworn, power runs along these — back and forth, realm to realm. Like blood in a body." He looked up at her. "Like blood between kin."

"Don't start," said Paloma, without heat. It had become almost companionable, this refrain. *You're my cousin.* *I'm not discussing it.* They had been having the argument in shorthand for two days, and somewhere in the whiteout it had stopped being an argument and become a kind of rope they both held.

"I wasn't going to," said Sef, which was a lie, and they both let it lie there in the snow.

The carvings around the table's edge told the story plainly enough once you wiped them clear: two figures, one crowned in antlers, one crowned in wheat, hands joined over a stone. Between them, a third figure — smaller, hooded, holding something that glowed. The channels ran from the glowing thing outward, to both crowns equally.

Paloma stared at the hooded figure for a long time. Then at the wheat-crowned human, whose face had been deliberately chiseled away sometime in the last century. Deliberately. The gouges were old but confident, done by someone with tools and permission.

"Someone couldn't bear to look at her," she murmured.

"Or wanted everyone else to stop looking," said Sef.

She pulled the folio from inside her coat then — the prophecy book, water-spotted, ash-edged, the thing that had started all of this — and found the page again by feel. She had read it so many times the words lived under her eyelids now.

*When the heart returns to the stone of first swearing, let it be carried not by force nor by purchase nor by pity, but willingly, by the hand of the thief's own line, and the debt shall be paid in kind.*

"By the hand of the thief's own line," she read aloud anyway, because saying things aloud made them either more real or less, and she wanted to find out which. "Willingly. That's the trick of it, isn't it? It can't be taken from me. It can't even be *given* reluctantly. It has to be—" She flexed her stiff fingers. "It has to be mine to give. Freely."

Sef was watching her with an expression she was learning to dread, the one that meant he was about to say something kind.

"You could still turn back," he said. "Carry it down the mountain and bury it in a glacier and let both realms freeze arguing about whose fault it was. No one would follow you. Vane might even thank you."

"No, she wouldn't." Paloma tucked the folio away. "Vane would hunt me into next spring for wasting her sacrifice. And you'd freeze mid-sentence like your Queen, and Thornfell would go under snow by midsummer, and I'd spend the rest of my life being very warm and very ashamed." She looked up at the pale sky. "I've been cold my whole life, Sef. Cold like a house where nobody says the true thing. I'd rather be properly frozen doing the right thing than comfortably thawed doing nothing."

He smiled — the first real one she'd seen from him since the pass. "You sound like your grandmother."

"I know," said Paloma grimly. "It's the worst part."

And that was when she saw the smoke.

Not campfire smoke. Too thin, too disciplined, rising from the lee side of the southernmost standing stone where someone had built a fire small enough to feed a bird. Someone who knew exactly how much smoke a signal could afford.

Someone warden-trained.

* * *

"Sef." She didn't move her lips much. "Behind the third stone. Don't run."

"Running had occurred to me."

"Don't. We're on open ground and whoever it is chose their spot better than we chose ours." Paloma rose slowly, hands visible, the way you approached a gate-crack you suspected was more than a crack. "Hello the stone!"

For a moment, nothing. Then a figure stepped around the granite, unhurried, stamping snow from her boots, and Paloma's stomach performed a maneuver she associated with falling off cliffs.

Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane looked terrible. Not defeated — Paloma doubted the woman had a defeated setting — but *spent*, in the way of a blade that had been sharpened past its steel. Her grey coat was scorched at the shoulder. There was a bandage at her temple gone brown at the edge, and frost in her eyebrows she clearly hadn't bothered to brush out. In her right hand, casually, the way other people carried walking sticks, she carried a warden's maul — the heavy ceremonial hammer used for breaking seals, and, if it came to it, everything else.

"Keeper Zhou," said Vane. Her voice was hoarse, as if she'd spent several days shouting orders into wind, which Paloma supposed she had. "You're late."

"You're supposed to be dead," said Paloma, because her filter had frozen off somewhere around day two of the climb. "The sect burned the bridge at Corrow Gap. Half the Order thinks you went into the gorge with it."

"The other half knows better." Vane's eyes flicked to Sef, to the bundle on the table, back to Paloma. Calculating. But slowly, she noticed. Slowly, for Vane. "The zealots burned my bridge, killed four of my wardens, and preached a sermon over the wreckage about the holiness of sealed doors. I've decided I disagree with their theology." She planted the maul head-down in the snow like a standard. "So here is where I make my stand."

"This is where you make your stand?" Paloma gestured at the empty mountaintop, the ring of stones, the enormous indifferent sky. "There's nothing here to stand for. There's a rock and some weather."

"There's the rock," said Vane, "where it began. Which means it's the only place left where anything can end." She looked at the winter-heart's wrappings, and something moved behind her eyes — hunger, grief, relief, Paloma couldn't sort them, possibly Vane couldn't either. "You brought it."

"We brought it."

"Willingly?"

"That," said Paloma, "is between me and the stone."

Something that was almost a smile touched the corner of Vane's mouth and died of exposure. "Good," she said. "Because if you'd dragged it here chained to a sledge, none of this would work, and I'd have wasted the last good climb of my life getting here first." She jerked her chin at the fire. "Sit down, both of you, before you fall down. You can't swear an oath on shaking knees. Believe me. I've watched men try."

* * *

They sat around the little fire, which was absurd, and drank tea, which was more absurd, from Vane's battered tin kettle, which was the most absurd thing that had happened yet, and somehow it was also the first sane hour Paloma had spent since the night the gate shuddered.

"How did you get here before us?" Sef asked. He had positioned himself with a stone at his back and an escape route at his shoulder, and Vane either didn't notice or generously pretended not to.

"You took the ridge road." It wasn't a question. "I took the warden stair beneath the gate. It comes out at the tree line. Adds two days to your knees and takes four off your journey — if you know it exists." She poured herself tea with steady hands. "My Order built it, incidentally. A hundred years ago. To reach this place quickly. Care to guess why a brotherhood devoted to keeping the gate shut built themselves a secret fast-road to the covenant's birthplace?"

Paloma set down her tin. "They always meant to come back here."

"They always knew they'd need to." Vane gazed into her cup. "The founders wrote the sealing order with a loophole sewn into the hem, Keeper. *If the debt comes due, the seal may be answered at the stone of first swearing, by restitution.* They wrote it down. They hid it in the deepest archive. And then for a hundred years every Warden-Captain read those words and chose not to understand them, because understanding them meant admitting what the Order had actually been founded to protect." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and awful. "I found the loophole eleven years ago. I have carried it in my head every day since, like a coal I couldn't put down or hold."

"Why tell us this now?"

"Because you're about to do the thing I should have done eleven years ago, and you deserve to know you're not the first coward to stand on this mountain." Vane set the cup down with a small, precise click. "Also because there are forty zealots coming up the western approach, and I thought you should hear my terms before they arrive and complicate the vocabulary."

Paloma felt the cold arrive ahead of the words. "Forty."

"Give or take. They move slowly. They think suffering uphill is a form of prayer." Vane turned her cup thoughtfully. "Here are my terms. You perform the rite. All of it, exactly as the channels demand, no shortcuts, no clever smuggling tricks — I will check your work, Keeper, I've read the founder's marginalia and you haven't. In exchange, when the sect arrives to stop you, they go through me."

Sef stared at her. "Alone? Forty?"

"I was never alone," said Vane. "I was only ever *on their side*, which is different, and lonelier." She flexed her bandaged hand. "Six wardens walked out of Corrow Gap with me. They're dug in along the western approach with the last of our crossbow bolts and my entire remaining faith in institutions. They'll slow the sect. They won't stop it. Nothing stops the truly convinced except the truly certain, and I am gambling, Captain-to-Keeper, that you two are more certain than they are devout." She raised her eyes to Paloma's. "Are you?"

Paloma looked at the winter-heart, humming gently under its wool like a sleeping animal. She looked at the chiseled-away face of her grandmother's generation, and the antlered king, and the hooded figure holding light between two worlds. She thought of Bram's keyring heavy in her pocket, of her friend Marta's face among the zealots' torches, of a hundred years of cold kept warm by a beautiful lie.

"Yes," she said. And found, examining the word as it left her, that it needed no amendment.

"Then we have an accord." Vane stood, joints cracking like green wood, and picked up her maul. "Rest while you can. Eat. Check your ritual." At the edge of the firelight she paused, back to them, and added in a voice pitched low enough that only the three of them and the stones heard it:

"When my order comes down that slope, Keeper, and it will — they'll call me traitor. Let them. But whatever else happens on this rock today, I want it written down somewhere afterward that Ilsa Vane stood *here*, at the beginning, and chose the world over the door." She rolled her shoulders once, like a woman settling an old weight more comfortably. "Even if the world doesn't thank me. Even if the door is all that survives."

* * *

They checked the ritual.

The channels demanded, in order: the naming of the theft, spoken aloud, unsoftened. The return of the heart, willing, by the thief's line. The witness of both realms — one voice of fae blood, one of warden steel, which explained, with a click Paloma felt in her teeth, why Vane's presence here felt less like coincidence and more like architecture. And last, the price. The channels went dark at the final carving, worn blank, as if the stone itself had declined to write it twice.

Paloma ran her thumb over the blank space and did not like the shape of the silence there.

"It's missing the last term," she said.

"Old covenants hide their prices," said Sef softly. He was standing at the table's edge, and the wind had dropped all at once, the way it does before something listens. "Mine certainly did."

Above the western approach, faint as a struck match, a signal flare went up — red, then red again. Vane's people. The warning.

Down in the valley below, torches began to move.

Paloma lifted the winter-heart from its wrappings. Up close, unwrapped, it wasn't a jewel or a coal at all — it was a knot of light and cold, turning slowly, like weather deciding. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. It hummed against her palms and she understood, finally, what it had been humming all this way: not a song. A question. Asked a hundred years ago and never once answered.

*Will you give me back?*

Snow began to fall, straight down, unhurried, like the mountain settling in to watch.

"Keeper," said Vane, hefting her maul, eyes already on the slope. "Whatever you're going to say to that stone — say it soon."

Paloma opened her mouth to answer —

— and the origin-stone answered first, waking under her hands with a voice like a door opening at the bottom of the world.
