## 17. Three Oaths, One Stone

Ilsa Vane had stood vigil at the origin-stone for three days, and in that time she had learned two things: the mountain was dying faster than the Order's reports admitted, and her knees were older than her pride.

The stone sat in a bowl of granite above the tree line, a squat black monolith furred with frost, older than Thornfell, older than the Order, older — if the murals beneath the gate were honest — than the lie she had served for thirty years. She had come here knowing what the splinter sect intended to do at this place. She had come here intending to stop them.

She had not expected Paloma Zhou and a half-frozen fae exile to walk out of the whiteout on the third evening like a pair of ghosts arguing about whose fault the weather was.

"—all I said was that the ridge road would have been faster," Sef was saying.

"You said it *confidently*," Paloma snapped. "There's a difference between a route and a dare."

Vane drew her sword anyway, because thirty years of habit did not care about banter, and because her hands needed something to do while her heart decided what it believed.

They saw her and stopped. Snow gathered on their shoulders. The exile's breath came out silver; the keeper's came out ragged. Between them, wrapped in oilcloth against Paloma's chest, Vane could see the shape of something that made the frost on the stone seem warm by comparison — a glow like moonlight trapped under ice.

The winter-heart. They had actually done it. They had gone into the vault, past Bram's keys, past her own hesitation, and taken the thing her Order had guarded through theft disguised as duty.

"Captain Vane." Paloma's voice was steady. Her hands were not. "You let us leave the vault."

"I did no such thing," Vane said. "I was delayed by an inconvenient conscience. It has since been resolved."

"Resolved how?"

"I'm deciding right now." She leveled the blade. "Put the heart down, Zhou. Step away from the stone. I'll write in my report that I found you frozen and the heart lost to an avalanche. You can live out your life somewhere the Order will never look. That is the last mercy I have in me, and I am offering it before I talk myself out of it."

Paloma didn't move. Of course she didn't. Vane had read her file. Stubborn as her grandmother, and look where that had gotten everyone.

"No," Paloma said.

"No?"

"No. I'm going to reforge the covenant. And I think you already knew that, or you'd have sounded the alarm from the vault door instead of standing up here alone in a blizzard waiting for us."

Vane felt the truth of it land like a blow she'd been rehearsing for years. Alone. Yes. That was the word for what she'd been doing on this mountain, though she'd dressed it up as strategy. Three days of watching the sect's fires crawl closer through the passes, three days of composing reports she would never send, three days of holding a sword over a stone and asking it questions it did not answer.

"The Order's directive," Vane said, "is permanent seal. The founders wrote it themselves. It is the only order I have ever been given that matters."

"And the murals?" Sef said quietly. "Did the founders paint those too? Your grandmother's face, Zhou, carved into the vault wall with the winter-heart in her arms?"

"Sef." Paloma's warning tone.

"What? She's seen them. I watched her see them. She looked at them the way you look at a wound you've been pretending isn't there."

Vane's grip tightened on the hilt. "You know nothing about what I—"

"I know the sealing order kills my people," Sef said. "All of them. Slowly, then all at once. I know your founders knew it when they signed. And I know that you've known for — how long, Captain? Since the vault? Since before?"

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below, faint as memory, the sect was singing.

* * *

Here was the thing Vane had told no one, not in thirty years, not in confession, not in the privacy of her own skull until the vault forced it into daylight:

She had been nineteen when she first read the founding charter. Not the sanitized version they gave recruits — the original, which the Order kept locked away because even then someone had been ashamed. She'd picked the lock with a hairpin and a grievance, because that was the kind of recruit she'd been: too clever, too angry, too hungry for certainty.

The charter's final clause was beautiful. *Let the gate be sealed forevermore, that neither realm may harm the other.* She had wept reading it. She'd been so young.

It took her another year to find the marginalia — the founders' private annotations, cramped and honest in a way the main text was not. One of them had written, beside the clause: *The fae cannot survive a sealed gate. This is understood and accepted. Their fading is the price of our safety, and I sign it gladly.*

*I sign it gladly.*

Nineteen-year-old Ilsa Vane had closed the book, put it back, and told herself she had misread it. Then she'd spent thirty years making sure she never had to read it again — rising through the ranks, enforcing the seal, burning smuggler roads and fae relics and the occasional inconvenient scholar, building a life so loud with duty that the quiet sentence underneath couldn't be heard.

But sentences don't die. They just wait.

At the vault, three days ago, she had stood before the murals with her sword drawn and her orders clear, and Paloma Zhou had looked at her — not with hatred, which Vane could have fought, but with recognition — and said, *"You've read the charter. Haven't you. The real one."*

And Vane had hesitated. One heartbeat. Two. Long enough for a thief and an exile to slip past her into the dark, carrying the heart of a world.

She had told herself it was tactical error. Standing on this mountain now, she finally admitted it had been the truest moment of her career.

* * *

"Here is my problem," Vane said, and her voice surprised her by being level. "I have one oath. One. Everything I am was built on it. If I set it down, I don't know what's left standing."

"Then keep it," Paloma said.

Vane blinked. "What?"

"Keep your oath. Read it again. *That neither realm may harm the other.*" Paloma stepped forward, snow swirling around her boots, the winter-heart glowing against her chest like a small stubborn star. "Your oath doesn't say seal the gate. It says protect both realms from harm. The sealing is just — it's the method your founders chose. And their method is killing one realm entirely. Which means the method broke the oath. You haven't. You never did. You've just been serving the method instead of the promise."

Vane stared at her.

Thirty years. Thirty years of doctrine and drill and sleepless vigils, and this exhausted, hunted, twenty-something keeper had just untangled it in four sentences on a mountainside.

"That's sophistry," she said weakly.

"It's grammar," said Sef. "Fae are very good at grammar. Words are load-bearing where we come from."

And Vane laughed. She couldn't help it — a short, cracked sound, startled out of her like a bird from cover. When had she last laughed? She genuinely could not remember. That was perhaps the saddest data point of her career.

"If I let you do this," she said, "the sect will kill me. If I try to stop you, one of two things happens: I win, and my people freeze to death slowly behind a pretty wall while I die old and decorated. Or I lose, and die here. There is no version where I come home."

"There might be," Paloma said, "if you fight properly."

"*Properly?*"

"Not to stop us." Paloma set down the oilcloth bundle, unwrapped it, and the winter-heart's light washed across the stone, across the snow, across Vane's astonished face. "To test us. You want to know if my conviction outweighs your order. So weigh it. Properly. With everything you have. And if we're still standing when you're done — kneel."

The arrogance of it. The absolute gall. Vane should have run her through on principle.

Instead she pulled off her gauntlets, finger by finger, and dropped them in the snow.

* * *

They fought at the edge of the stone's circle, where the frost was thinnest, and it was — Vane would admit this later, grudgingly, to anyone who asked — the finest hour of her life.

Sef she dismissed early; he was brave and quick but running on empty, and after he'd disarmed himself twice on the ice she gestured him back with genuine sympathy. "Rest. This part isn't yours."

So it was Vane and Paloma, steel against a keeper's staff, in the blue light of the winter-heart, while the storm held its breath around them.

Vane was better. She knew it, Paloma knew it, any impartial observer would have known it. Thirty years of forms drilled into muscle and bone. She pressed the attack and drove the girl backward three steps, five, seven, felt the moment the staff work turned desperate —

— and noticed that Paloma was smiling.

"Why," Vane demanded between strikes, "*are you smiling*—"

"Because—" parry, stumble, recover "—you're fighting to *lose well*. I can tell. You keep giving me angles. You've been giving me angles since the vault. You don't want to beat me, you want proof you *couldn't*."

"I could beat you in my sleep!"

"Then do it! Beat me and go home and live with it!" Paloma's eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely certain. "Or admit what you've known for thirty years, Captain. Admit it with your whole body, the way wardens do everything. Your order was wrong. Your founders were thieves. And the only oath worth keeping is the one underneath."

Something in Vane tore.

Not broke — tore, like scar tissue, like ice on a thawing river, painful and inevitable and, God help her, *relieving*. Every form she'd ever drilled, every report she'd ever filed, every smuggler's pyre and burned relic rose up in her all at once, and she saw them clearly for the first time: not duty. Debt payments. Thirty years of interest on a crime she hadn't committed but had chosen, daily, to serve.

Her blade dipped. Just an inch. A lifetime of skill, surrendering a lifetime of denial.

Paloma's staff caught her wrist, swept her legs, and Ilsa Vane went down into the snow at the foot of the origin-stone with the winter-heart blazing above her and the sky spinning gently overhead.

Silence. Wind. Sef's careful breathing.

"Well," Vane said to the clouds. "That settles the question of whether I could have won."

"You could have," Paloma said quietly, offering a hand. "If you'd wanted to. That's why I'm asking, not forcing. Get up, Captain. Nobody kneels who hasn't chosen it."

Vane took the hand. Stood. Looked at the stone — black, ancient, patient, humming faintly now that its heart was near, like a dog hearing its name after decades.

And then Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane, thirty years of service, terror of smugglers, shield of Thornfell, knelt in the snow before the origin-stone of her own free will, and began to speak.

* * *

She spoke the warden's litany — every recruit learned it before they learned to hold a blade, and she had spoken it ten thousand times without once listening to herself.

*"I swear my arm to the gate. My eyes to the border. My life to the peace between."*

The words came out different this time. Heavier and lighter at once, the way a pack feels when you finally set down the stones you'd forgotten you were carrying.

"My arm to the gate," she repeated, and laid her palm flat against the stone. It was cold, then warm, then *listening*. "My eyes to the border — both sides of it, all of it, honestly. My life to the peace between." Her voice roughened. "And I name the founders' sealing order for what it is: murder written in a fine hand. I unbind myself from it. I break my Order rather than watch it break the world."

The stone flared.

Light ran up through the frost like green fire through a dead field, and Vane felt it pass through her palm, up her arm, into her chest — not pain, exactly. *Audit.* The sensation of being read, thoroughly, by something very old and very fair. It weighed thirty years of her service, found the good faith buried inside the bad method, and — she would have sworn afterward — sighed.

When the light faded, the frost around the origin-stone had melted in a perfect circle, and three small flowers Vane had no botany for were blooming in the bare earth, out of season, out of reason, out of everything except stubbornness.

Sef made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. "It accepted you. Do you understand what that means? The stone hasn't accepted a human oath in a hundred years. It *liked* you."

"Don't be absurd," Vane said, staring at the flowers. "Stones don't like people."

"This one does. Look at it. That's practically affection."

Paloma crouched beside the flowers, and her expression went soft and complicated. "Three oaths," she murmured. "Keeper's blood, fae blood, warden's word. The rite needs all three. I thought we'd have to steal the third."

"You didn't think anything of the kind," Vane said. "You gambled on me. In the vault, and again just now. That was either great faith or great insolence."

"Can't it be both?"

"It can't be anything else," Vane said, and discovered that she meant it warmly, which was new, and survivable, which was newer.

* * *

They worked through the short grey afternoon, the three of them, preparing the stone with the odd domestic efficiency of people who have recently tried to kill each other and gotten over it. Sef chalked the fae glyphs from memory, tongue between his teeth. Paloma read the ritual lines aloud from a scrap of paper covered in her own handwriting — corrections, Vane realized, corrections to the prophecy folio itself, struck-through lies replaced with plainer truths.

Vane kept watch. It was, after all, the one thing she was still qualified for.

Toward dusk she saw them: torchlight threading up through the passes from below. Not one column. Three. The sect, singing their sealed-door hymns. Behind them, banners she knew too well — the Order's outriders, summoned by her own alarm signal, sent to finish what she had refused to do. And far off, at the very edge of sight, a shimmer along the ridgeline that was not torchlight at all, but the thawing host of a fae court waking from its long frozen sentence.

All three roads converging on one stone. All three debts coming due in one place, tonight.

"They'll be here by midnight," Vane said. "Maybe sooner. The sect will want you dead at this altar, Zhou. The Order will want the heart back in its hole. And your kin—" she nodded at the shimmer "—will want everything else."

Sef looked at the gathering lights and smiled with more courage than confidence. "Any advice, Captain? You're the expert on hopeless stands."

Vane checked her sword, rolled her shoulders, and felt — remarkably, absurdly — light. Unburdened for the first time in thirty years, even facing the worst odds of her life. Perhaps especially then.

"Yes," she said. "When they arrive, and they ask who gave us leave to do this — and someone *will* ask, there's always paperwork — you let me answer."

Below, the singing grew louder. Above, the first stars of the longest night came out, and the winter-heart pulsed against Paloma's chest like a second dawn waiting for permission.

"Now," Paloma said, stepping up to the stone. "Before they get here. Begin the rite."

And the origin-stone, ancient and patient and suddenly, unmistakably awake, opened like an eye.
