## 21. The Keeper Who Opens

The folio had lived in the restricted shelf for one hundred and twelve years, and in all that time it had been read by exactly four people: Bram, twice; Warden-Captain Vane, once, under supervision she had never reported; and Paloma Zhou, three times — once out of curiosity, once out of dread, and once to check her memory against itself, because a lie repeated often enough starts to sound like truth even to the person telling it.

Now it lay open on her desk in the keeper's cottage, and Paloma was going to ruin it.

She had cleared the desk first. That felt important. The old ledger with its columns of smuggler trails went to the shelf. The ink pots were sorted — good iron-gall for the corrections, the cheap walnut brown she'd bought in Corrow reserved for the marginalia, because some things deserved only honest, unglamorous ink. She sharpened four quills and laid them out like surgical instruments, then looked at them and laughed at herself, quietly, alone in a room where laughing alone had been the only kind available for most of her adult life.

That was changing too. The door of the cottage stood half open to the morning, which would have gotten her written up under the old regime. Through it came the smell of thawing earth and the distant percussion of the gate doing what gates did now — letting people through.

*Knock-knock-knock.* A pause. *Knock.* Then, muffled: "It's Sef. I know you can hear me. Your wards sing when I touch them. It's very rude of them."

"Come in before they bite you," Paloma called.

He entered the way he entered everything, sideways, as if the doorway might be an ambush, and set a basket on her table with the exaggerated care of someone who had learned that human furniture resented sudden movements. "Bram sent bread. He said — and I quote him exactly, because I have been practicing your language's habit of quoting elders — 'Tell her I baked it myself and if she doesn't eat while she works I'll haunt this office.' He is not dead. Is haunting a living man's privilege here?"

"It's his privilege specifically. What else?"

"He asked me to tell you the archive keyring has a duplicate cut for the restricted shelf. He says you'll want it. He says—" Sef hesitated, and the humor dropped off his face the way frost slides off a warming stone. "He says he is sorry. Still. He says it every day. I told him saying it every day is not how humans apologize."

"How do fae apologize?"

"We return what we took." Sef touched the edge of the folio, not quite touching it. "So. Today?"

"Today."

"Then I will be at the gate. If anyone crosses who shouldn't, you'll feel it." He tapped his own sternum, then pointed at hers — the gesture they'd all adopted without discussing it, since Paloma now carried the gate's pulse under her ribs the way other people carried a heartbeat. "And Paloma. Whatever you write in there—" he nodded at the folio— "write the ugly parts twice."

She looked up. "The ugly parts?"

"Bram taught me that. When you record a defeat, write it twice. Once so it's known, once so it isn't prettied by familiarity." He shrugged, suddenly awkward. "It seemed like advice for today."

Then he left, and Paloma sat with the book.

* * *

The first thing she wrote was not a correction. It was a title page.

THE HOLLOW GATE COVENANT — AS IT WAS TOLD
and beneath it, smaller:
AS IT WAS DONE

Her grandmother's name was on the original title page — not as author but as witness, *recorded by Maren Zhou, Keeper, in the year of the sealing*. Paloma had grown up thinking that was an honor. Now she understood it was an alibi, signed by the person best positioned to notice the theft and least incentivized to report it.

She began with the prophecy itself, the passage that had started everything:

*"On the night the oath wakes, the keeper shall open."*

For a hundred years, wardens had read that line as threat. Zealots had read it as scripture. Her grandmother had read it, Paloma now believed, as warning — and buried the warning so deep in restriction and reverence that no keeper after her could act on it until acting on it meant treason.

Paloma struck the line through with two clean strokes. In the margin, in walnut brown:

*This line was true. It named me. It was hidden from me by my own grandmother, who knew what her generation had stolen and feared what honesty would cost. The cost of hiding it was a century of winter. Honesty would have been cheaper.*

She sat back. Her hand was shaking slightly, and she let it. Bram's rule — *write the fear into the record or the record lies* — was one of the few teachings she intended to keep whole.

* * *

The morning wore on. Twice she stopped to press a hand flat against her sternum, where something crossed the gate and registered as warmth — a tinker with a permit signed in both scripts, going home to a mother everyone had thought lost. The sensation was new still, and she suspected it always would be. Pain she had trained for. Joy required practice.

At midday, footsteps on the path — the deliberate, boot-heavy tread of someone announcing themselves well in advance. Vane.

The warden-captain filled the doorway, looked at the chaos of quills and ink and the violated folio, and said, "You're defacing an Order document."

"I'm correcting one."

"There are procedures."

"There were procedures," Paloma agreed. "They gave us a hundred-year winter. I'm trying something less procedural." She slid the bread basket aside and turned the folio toward Vane. "I'd like your signature. Not the Order's. Yours. As a witness to what actually happened."

Vane was quiet long enough that somewhere outside, the gate chimed — a small crossing, probably a trader, nothing worth either of them moving for. When the captain finally spoke, her voice had the flatness Paloma had learned to read as effort rather than coldness.

"My founders knew the theft happened. They wrote the sealing order anyway, knowing it would kill the far realm. I obeyed that order for thirty years." She pulled out a chair and sat, which Vane almost never did, which meant something. "If you want my hand in your book, put down what I did. All of it. The years I enforced a lie, and the morning I stopped."

"The morning you stopped nearly got us all killed."

"Yes." Vane met her eyes. "Write that part twice."

Paloma laughed — surprised out of it — and Vane almost smiled, which on her face was an event of geological rarity.

* * *

By late afternoon the corrections reached the pages Paloma had saved for last, the ones she had circled around for three readings like a sore tooth: her grandmother's own account of the sealing night. Beautiful writing. That was the horror of it — Maren Zhou had been a gorgeous prose stylist, and she had used every gift she had to make a theft sound like mercy.

*"We took the heart so that no door between the realms need ever stand open again. Better one long winter than endless war. History will judge us gently, for we acted in sorrow."*

Paloma read it four times. Then she took the sharpest quill and did the hardest thing she had ever done with a pen, harder than signing her life to a gate, harder than speaking a covenant into a storm.

She did not strike it out.

Instead she copied it, entire, onto a fresh leaf in her own plain hand, and beneath it wrote:

*I have left my grandmother's words intact above, though every instinct I have as her granddaughter wants to strike them. Let future keepers see how beautiful a lie can be made to sound. Let them feel the pull of it — the comfort of believing our ancestors acted in sorrow rather than ambition. My grandmother was not a monster. That is precisely the danger. She was competent, beloved, tired, and wrong, and she dressed her wrongness so well that it took a century of frozen courts and one stubborn fae exile to undress it.*

*Here is what she left out: the court she froze mid-sentence. The children of Thornfell who died in the white winters. The vault where the heart sat in the dark while both realms starved. Here is what she left out above all: that mercy taken without consent is just theft with better handwriting.*

Paloma set down the quill. Her eyes stung. She let them.

Outside, Sef's voice drifted up from the gate, patiently explaining tariff law to someone in a tone of profound suffering, and Bram's answering rumble, and the ordinary music of a border waking up. Paloma pressed her palm to her sternum — warmth, a family crossing south with seed potatoes, hope in vegetable form — and smiled through the sting.

* * *

She worked until full dark. When she finished, the folio was no longer one book but a palimpsest, old lie and new truth interleafed, the margins crowded with names: Vane's confession in her own severe hand. Sef's account of the frozen court, written in fae script that shimmered faintly when the candle moved. Even the zealot sect got its chapter — *they worshipped the seal because the seal was all they had been given to worship; we gave people a locked door and called their devotion faith* — because Paloma had decided the record would spare no one, including the people who had tried to sacrifice her.

The last page she saved for herself.

*A note to whoever keeps this gate after me:*

*You will inherit this book and want to close it, because closing books is comfortable and I was the same. You will find parts of this record shameful, and some of it will be your own kin, and you will be tempted by the oldest temptation of the keeper's office — to smooth the record, restrict the shelf, protect the next generation from knowing what protection costs others.*

*Don't.*

*The covenant holds now because it is honest. Truth for truth. Winter shared instead of stolen. Every time someone is tempted to make this story tidier, remember: the beautiful falsehood kept the gate shut for a hundred years and froze two realms doing it. The ugly truth opened it in a single night.*

*Be the keeper who opens.*

*— Paloma Zhou, Keeper, written by her own hand, in the first spring of the new covenant.*

She blotted the page, closed the folio, and locked it on the shelf — unrestricted now, available to any keeper, any warden, any curious traveler who asked. Bram's duplicate key hung beside it, deliberately, within easy reach of anyone.

Then she banked the fire, opened the cottage door wide to the thawing night — old habits dying hard, new ones taking root — and felt, warm under her ribs, the gate chime softly with tomorrow's first crossing already queuing at dawn.

Among them, she sensed, someone carrying a letter addressed to her, sealed in wax she didn't recognize, postmarked from deep in the fae realm — from a Queen who remembered, now, her daughter's name.
