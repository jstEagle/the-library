## 22. First Crossing of Spring

Paloma woke before dawn out of habit, then lay still and remembered she no longer needed to.

The Hollow Gate did not sleep, and neither, strictly speaking, did she anymore — but the old keeper's discipline had settled into something gentler over the winter: a rhythm rather than a vigilance. She rose, wrapped herself in Bram's old wool coat, and climbed the switchback path to the gate's threshold to watch the light come.

The thaw had arrived three days early, which Sef insisted was the fae realm showing off.

"Spring is a performance," he'd said last night, sprawled on the keeper's bench with his boots on her table until she'd knocked them off twice. "In my grandmother's court they used to hold contests for it. Best blossom. Loudest meltwater. One year a river god flooded half the valley just to win."

"And your people let him?"

"He was very charming about it." Sef had smiled, and for once the smile hadn't carried the frost of exile in it. "We are learning to be charming about things again. It's going slowly."

Now, standing at the gate as the sky went from iron to rose, Paloma felt the mountain breathing. That was the only word for it — the deep, slow expansion of stone and ice and root that she had lived inside for a hundred days without quite getting used to. The winter-heart beat at the center of the Hollow Gate like a second moon sunk into rock, and through it, she felt everything.

She felt the snowpack loosening on the eastern ridge, a thousand small sighs of water finding new paths downward. She felt the fox denned under the third marker stone, dreaming its sharp little dreams. And she felt, faint but unmistakable, the first travelers gathering on the far side of the gate — warmth arriving ahead of them like lamplight under a door.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone and smiled.

*They're coming.*

The first crossing of spring. They'd talked about it all winter, argued about it in the long evenings — Vane with her lists and protocols, Sef with his courtly etiquette that sounded invented half the time, Bram hovering with his ledger and his pen ready to write history and getting shooed away from both. There had been a formal name proposed for the day. *The Concordance of Renewed Passage.* Vane had drafted it herself, in her severe warden's hand, and everyone had pretended to take it seriously for almost an hour before Sef started calling it *the Door-Opening* and refused to stop, and eventually even Vane gave up.

The sun cleared the ridge. Below, on the Thornfell side of the pass, the road was already filling.

Paloma counted them from her high perch, out of habit, and then made herself stop counting and simply look. Farmers from the Corrow lowlands in their good coats, the ones they saved for weddings and funerals and apparently now this. Two of Vane's wardens — not guards this morning, she noted; they'd left their crossbows racked by order and choice both — standing at the threshold stones with their caps in their hands like boys at church. A knot of smugglers from the hidden passes, who had materialized out of nowhere as smugglers did, wearing expressions of profound innocence that fooled absolutely no one. Old Maren from the market, who had once sold Paloma dried apples without asking questions, carrying a basket that smelled of honey-cakes baked for strangers.

And among them, nervous as foals, the fae.

Sef had brought eleven of his kin across three nights ago to wait on the human side — "so the first crossing happens *together*, cousin, not in two awkward halves" — and now those eleven stood scattered through the crowd like bright threads pulled loose from another weave. Tall and slight and winter-pale, dressed in colors that didn't exist in any Thornfell dyer's inventory. A child with hair like spun frost gripped her mother's hand and stared at the humans with open, unguarded wonder. An elderly fae gentleman in a coat embroidered with what looked like falling leaves examined a human farmer's hat with scholarly intensity, and the farmer, to his credit, lifted it off and let him.

Paloma felt each small contact between the two crowds — a glance exchanged, a hand hesitantly offered, the child's frost-hair brushing a human woman's sleeve — as warmth moving through the stone beneath her feet. Not pain. Never again pain. The old covenant would have flayed a keeper for letting so much as a moth cross unregistered. The new one simply told her, like a friend leaning close, *someone is reaching toward someone else. Isn't that good.*

It was very good.

* * *

By midmorning the threshold had organized itself into something between a ceremony and a festival, which was exactly what Sef had promised and exactly what Vane had dreaded.

Vane stood at the origin-stone with a scroll she kept unrolling and re-rolling, and Paloma watched her wrestle, publicly and losingly, with the fact that there were no rules for this. Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane had spent thirty years enforcing rules written by frightened men, and now the world had handed her a blank page, and she was holding it upside down.

"The procession will proceed in pairs," Vane announced, to the crowd, to the mountains, to no one in particular. "One of each realm. Crossings will be logged. Names given freely, not compelled—"

"Ilsa." Paloma came down the path and touched her elbow. "Let them just walk through."

Vane looked at her with the expression of a woman being asked to juggle live coals. "Unlogged?"

"Logged afterward. In the evening. Over wine." Paloma nodded toward the crowd, where the frost-haired child had now been picked up by one of the smugglers and was being shown how a slingshot worked, to the visible alarm of her mother and the visible delight of everyone else. "Look at them. If you make them stand in lines, you'll make this feel like a customs house. We've had a hundred years of doors that check your papers. Let this one just be a door."

Vane closed her eyes briefly, the way she did when Paloma said something that was correct and intolerable. Then she rolled up the scroll, tucked it under her arm, and said, in the voice she'd once used to give orders that got people killed, "Then I shall log them tonight. Over wine." A pause. "You're providing the wine."

"I'm providing the wine."

"Bram's cellar?"

"*My* cellar now. He lives in my guest room."

This was true and remained strange every time Paloma thought about it. Bram Kavanagh, Keeper Emeritus of the Hollow Gate, traitor-by-silence, keeper of the century's most careful lie, had arrived at the gate six weeks ago with a mule, two crates of books, and an apology he'd clearly rehearsed the whole way up the mountain. He had gotten through perhaps a third of it before Paloma held up a hand.

"You hid the theft. You knew what my grandmother did, and you let me grow up guarding a door built on it."

"Yes," he'd said. No excuses. She'd give him that; he'd never once offered excuses.

"I could hate you for that."

"You could," he agreed. "I'd deserve it."

"But instead you're going to be my clerk," she'd said, "because someone has to keep the ledgers honest, and I want a man who knows exactly how dishonest ledgers get written sitting where he can see them."

He'd cried, a little, and pretended it was the wind, and there had been no wind. Now he stood near the origin-stone in his clerk's sash — Paloma had made it herself, badly, and he wore it anyway — recording names as people passed, and every few entries he'd look up at her with an expression she'd decided to interpret as gratitude rather than guilt, because she could, because she'd earned the right to choose kindness on purpose instead of by accident.

"Keeper!" Bram called now, waving her over. "First pair wants to go through properly. They're waiting for you."

Paloma took her place at the threshold.

The pair waiting there were the farmer whose hat had been inspected — Tomas Ellery, of the Corrow lowlands, per Bram's ledger — and the elderly fae gentleman, whose name was Aurelian Silverfall and who bowed over Paloma's hand with a formality that belonged to a world a century dead.

"We wished to wait for you, Keeper," Aurelian said. "Sef explained what you carry. What you *are*." His pale eyes were kind and terribly old. "We thought the first crossing should pass beneath the hinge herself."

"It's not a tollgate," Paloma said, and heard the gentle humor in her own voice that a year ago she wouldn't have dared use with anyone. "Nobody needs my permission. That was the whole point."

"All the same." Aurelian smiled. "Some doors you thank on your way through."

Tomas shifted, embarrassed, and thrust out a loaf wrapped in cloth. "Baked it this morning," he muttered. "For whoever comes through. Seemed right. Bread for the road, or — bread for arriving, I suppose." He flushed. "I don't know the custom."

"There isn't one yet," Paloma said. "So you're making it. Congratulations."

Aurelian accepted the loaf with both hands, gravely, as though receiving a crown. "Bread," he said softly. "Our ovens have been cold a hundred years. Do you know, I had forgotten what warm bread smells like? No — that's not true. I remembered, but I had stopped believing I would smell it again. Those are different griefs."

Paloma felt the words land in the stone under her feet, and through the stone, in the winter-heart, as a small bright pulse. She stepped back between them, laid one hand on each of their shoulders — one warm from the oven-warmed kitchen, one cool as riverbed pebbles — and walked them the last ten paces to the archway together.

Crossing, she felt it happen. Not as a tearing, not as the old covenant's electric cruelty, but as a warmth spreading up through her boots and her bones and out through her fingertips into the two travelers, like sunlight through a window onto a cold floor. The boundary passed through them and through her and registered itself as what it had always secretly been: not a wall. A heartbeat.

Aurelian and Tomas stepped through side by side onto foreign soil, and turned around, and looked at each other, and laughed — startled, helpless laughter, the sound of two men discovering they'd survived something enormous together — and behind them the crowd began to move, and the crossings began in earnest.

Paloma stood at the center of the arch with her arms slightly out, feeling each one.

A fae weaver and a Corrow spinner, already comparing thread-thicknesses in broken sentences. A human boy chasing the frost-haired child, both shrieking with joy, their mothers following in wary parallel. Smugglers bowing elaborately to fae merchants, offering, with straight faces, to sell them maps of the very passes they'd just emerged from. Each crossing a pulse of warmth. Each one a small bell rung inside her chest.

She had expected, in the worst private hours of the winter, that binding herself to the gate would feel like drowning slowly in a crowded room forever. Some nights it had, a little — every breach attempt by desperate sect survivors, every argument at the threshold, every animal blundering through, arriving in her as pressure and ache. But the thaw had changed the register of it, the way ice changes to water: the same substance, utterly different weight. This morning the gate felt less like a wound she tended and more like a hearth she sat beside. The oath, remade, keeping her. Holding her up. Saying, in its slow stone voice: *you are not alone in here.*

* * *

At noon she found Sef sitting on the flat rock above the gate, legs dangling, eating a honey-cake he'd almost certainly stolen from Maren's basket, watching the crossing traffic with the expression of a man who had won a bet with the universe.

"You're supposed to be down there," Paloma said, dropping beside him. "Being diplomatic."

"I was diplomatic for four hours. I praised a hat. I complimented a baby. I ate something pickled to be polite." He gestured grandly at the crowd below, where his kin and hers mingled like water finally allowed to mix with water. "Diplomacy is done. Now it's just people. My favorite part."

"How does it feel?" she asked. "Seeing them cross. Your people, coming back."

Sef was quiet for a while. Below, the elderly fae gentleman was teaching the farmer a song, badly, both laughing.

"When the court froze," he said at last, "we didn't know if we were dying or sleeping. You stop being able to tell, after enough winters. And I thought — if we ever woke, it would be to ash and ruin, or to war, or to nothing at all." He turned the honey-cake over in his fingers. "Instead I wake up and a human farmer gives my elder bread and asks him to sing. And I don't have a word for it. In our language we had a word for grief and a word for joy and nothing between, because nothing between ever happened to us."

"What do you call it now?"

"Inventing one. Working title: *the thing Sef feels when the door opens both ways.*"

"That'll catch on."

"It has excellent rhythm." He bumped her shoulder with his. "Cousin."

She'd stopped flinching at the word sometime in late winter. It still landed strangely — belonging offered as a claim had shaken her once, but somewhere along the frozen miles it had transmuted into belonging chosen daily, which was the only kind worth having. "Cousin," she returned, and felt the gate hum approval under the rock, the smug old thing.

"Sef!" Vane's voice carried up from below, edged with the particular exasperation she reserved for fae. "Your weaver is attempting to trade a bolt of moon-silk for Maren's entire basket of cakes, and Maren is considering it, and this is a *logistical crisis*, because if the cakes go we have nothing for the afternoon arrivals—"

"On my way!" Sef leapt up, then paused, looking down at Paloma with sudden seriousness. "For what it's worth — the Queen sent word through me last night. She remembers more now. Almost everything." A hesitation. "She wanted you to know she's sorry. For mistaking you. And—" he smiled, crooked and real, "—for what her daughter's line cost yours. She says debts should be named even when they can't be repaid. Apparently that's a royal custom."

"Tell her," Paloma said, "that I'm keeping the name. That's payment enough for now."

He went. She stayed on the rock a while longer, in the sun, feeling the traffic of two worlds flow through her like a warm tide, until Bram came puffing up the path with his ledger clutched to his chest and his face doing something complicated.

"Paloma. You'll want to see this." He handed her the book, open to today's page. "Last entry of the morning."

The entry was written in a hand she didn't recognize — flowing, ornate, threaded with tiny flourishes like frost ferns. Beneath the name it read:

*Aurelian Silverfall, returning to Thornfell bearing bread, and a message: the Queen of the Thawing Court requests the honor of the Keeper's presence at the first feast of spring, on the far side of the gate, three nights hence. She wishes to speak with her granddaughter.*

Paloma read it twice. The wind moved. Somewhere below, the frost-haired child laughed, and the gate carried the sound up through the stone and set it ringing gently in Paloma's chest, warm as a hearth, patient as a door that had learned to stay open.

"Well," she said, handing the ledger back to her clerk, and found she was smiling at the mountains, at the gate, at the whole impossible thawing world. "I suppose I should bake something."
