## 1. The Valley Where Nothing Follows

There are three things you need to know about the Valley of Ash, and I'll tell you all three because nobody else here will.

First: nothing follows you in. Second: nothing follows you out. Third, and most important if you're the sort of person who came here on purpose: that's the entire point.

I've lived in the valley for nine years now, and in all that time the most magical thing that has happened to me is a jar of plum preserves that sealed itself. I want to be clear that it didn't. I'd simply forgotten tightening the lid, the way you forget a lot of things when your whole life is an exercise in forgetting. The valley is generous that way. It forgives you for remembering wrong.

The people here are millers and menders and one extremely opinionated farrier named Doss who has never once been right about a horse and has never once been wrong about a person, which is worse. They came to the Valley of Ash the way water finds a low place — not running toward something but away from everything else. A widow who didn't want her grief gossiped about. A debt-collector's clerk who'd developed a conscience at an inconvenient moment. Me.

They don't ask questions in the valley. Asking questions implies there might be answers, and answers imply a past, and the whole architecture of this place rests on the agreement that we don't have those. We have chores instead. Chores are excellent. Chores fill a day from rim to rim, and when you fall into bed with your hands smelling of axle grease or lamp oil or someone else's laundry, you sleep like a stone that has been assured, repeatedly, by professionals, that nothing is buried under it.

My trade — if you can call it a trade, and the ledger says I do — is mending charms.

I want you to sit with the absurdity of that for a moment. In the one place in the world where magic cannot enter, cannot pool, cannot so much as condense on the morning grass, I make my living repairing the little woven knots and knotted words that people carry out of the world and then carry back in, homesick for them even though they don't work here. A charm against fever. A knot for safe roads. A braided thread a grandmother gave a girl who grew up and married a miller and moved to the valley and still, twenty years later, sleeps with the thing under her pillow, knowing full well it's just thread now.

Because here's what I learned, mending charms that don't work: they were never really working anywhere. Not the way people wanted. What a charm does — what any hedge-magic does — is hold the shape of wanting. It gives the wanting somewhere to live besides the inside of your chest, where wanting will eat you alive if you let it. So I re-braid the frayed threads and re-tie the slipped knots, and I say the words in the old cadence even though the words are dead weight here, sound and breath and nothing else, and people take their charms home and feel better, and I charge fair prices, and everyone is satisfied, and nothing is magic, and I like it that way.

I liked it that way. Past tense. You already know this, because you know how stories work, and I know how stories work, which is why I should never have opened the book.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and in the valley getting ahead of yourself is practically a sin. We do things in order here. We mend the wheel before it comes off the cart. We fix the roof shingle before the rain. We notice the past standing at the edge of the field only when it has already crossed half the distance, and by then it's rude not to offer it tea.

* * *

The morning the book arrived started out exactly like every other morning, which is the valley's second-favorite trick after the forgetting. Fog off the millrace, thick as wool and twice as gray. Doss arguing with his apprentice about shoeing angles loud enough to wake the geese, who joined in on his side. Old Sefa on her porch shelling beans and narrating the process under her breath in a language she claims not to remember learning, which is either senility or the last ember of a very old fire, and which I have always pretended not to hear, because pretending not to hear things is the valley's native tongue and I am fluent.

I was walking the race path with my tool roll under my arm, headed to the Corvane place to look at a loom charm that had — and I quote — "gone funny." Loom charms don't go funny in the valley. Loom charms go inert, the same as everything else. But Mrs. Corvane insisted hers had "an attitude lately," and arguing with Mrs. Corvane is a longer job than humoring her, so I walked the race path in the fog with my collar up and my mind pleasantly, professionally empty.

That's when I saw it in the millrace. Caught in the sluice gate, snagged against the boards like a drowned bird.

Now. Understand something about the millrace. It runs clean off the high snowmelt, through gravel beds, past our fields, and out the far side of the valley through a gorge so narrow the cartographers — the two of us who own maps, anyway — mark it with a single skeptical line. Nothing comes down that race that wasn't already in the valley. Nine years, and it had given me a boot, half a fence rail, and once, memorably, an extremely angry duck. That's the inventory. That's what the race produces.

It had never produced a book.

I stood over it for a long moment doing the arithmetic every valley-dweller does automatically: where did this come from, what does its presence imply, and what is the smallest amount of attention I can pay it while still being able to sleep tonight. The correct answer, the valley answer, was to fish it out, dry it, put it in the lost-and-found box outside the meeting house, and let it become somebody else's problem. Possibly forever. Forever is a very popular destination in the Valley of Ash.

Instead I crouched down and pulled it out.

It was small, octavo-sized, bound in something that had been leather once and was now the color of old teeth. Waterlogged. Torn along the spine. Cold in my hands in a way paper shouldn't be cold — cold like a key left out overnight, cold like metal that remembers being held. I told myself it felt cold because it was wet. The fog was cold. Everything was cold. Cold is normal. Cold is the valley's favorite temperature.

I carried it home wrapped in my scarf like something sick, and I set it by the stove, and I did not open it, and I made breakfast, and I ate breakfast, and I washed the bowl, and I looked at the book, and I opened it.

Here's what I expected: a hymnal, maybe. A merchant's ledger. Someone's diary from some other life, which would have been uncomfortable in the way all pasts are uncomfortable but survivable. We're all refugees from a previous life here; a stranger's diary is just company.

What I got was a page of dense, slanted handwriting I didn't recognize — cramped, urgent, the hand of someone writing faster than thought — above a printed date.

Not written. Printed. Set in type, inked, pressed, the way you print a thousand copies of a thing meant to be read by many people across many years. And the date was today's.

I'm going to tell you what my first feeling was, and I want credit for honesty here, because it was not dread. My first feeling was curiosity, bright and clean and almost joyful, like a struck flint. Somewhere under nine years of ash, apparently, there was still a boy who loved puzzles, who used to beg Odile to let him decode the marginalia in her grimoires, who thought a locked box was the most exciting thing in the world provided someone else had locked it.

Curiosity lasted approximately four seconds. Then I turned the page and found the sketch.

It was charcoal, quick and confident, the kind of drawing done by someone who knows the face better than they know their own handwriting. A young man, hollow-cheeked, hair too long, a small scar through the left eyebrow that he got falling out of a cherry tree at fourteen. The sketch was looking slightly off to the side, wary, the way you look when you've heard your name called and you're deciding whether to answer.

It was me. Not similar-to-me. Not could-be-me-if-the-artist-was-generous. It was me the way a mirror is me, except mirrors don't catch the expression you wear when you think no one is watching, and this sketch had caught it perfectly.

And beneath the sketch, in that same slanted century-old hand, a single line:

*He will not want to come.*

I sat with that for a while. The stove ticked. The fog pressed at the windows. Somewhere outside, Doss had moved on from shoeing angles to the moral failings of the blacksmith guild, a topic with no natural end.

"He will not want to come," I said aloud, to the kitchen. "Correct."

Because that's the thing about prophecies, if you believe in them, and I had spent nine years arranging my life around not believing in them: they're supposed to tell you what happens. This one had skipped ahead to the interesting part, the part where the subject reads it and objects. Which meant whoever wrote this hadn't just seen me. They knew me. Knew me well enough to predict my objection, to write it down a hundred years ago, to print it, bind it, and send it swimming up a millrace in a valley where — say it with me now — *nothing follows you in*.

A book doesn't swim. A book doesn't drift through snowmelt and gravel and a gorge marked with a skeptical line. Books arrive in valleys like this the way thoughts arrive in heads: because something put them there.

I did the sensible thing. I picked it up, carried it outside to the burn barrel, and set it alight.

* * *

I'll describe what happened next, but first let me establish my credentials, because I want it on the record that I know fire. Nine years of stove-tending and lamp-trimming and one memorable season burning brush off the east field. Paper catches. Paper wants to burn. Paper is one bad decision away from ash at all times; it's the most honest material there is.

This paper declined.

It didn't resist dramatically — no blue flame, no shrieking, none of the theatrics Odile used to produce when she wanted to make a point about wards. The flames simply went around it. The pages sat in the heart of the fire, surrounded on all sides by cheerful orange combustion, and remained exactly as damp and torn and legible as before, the way a stone sits in a river. When I poked it with the poker, the poker burned. The book did not.

I stood in my yard in the fog holding a burning poker, addressing a small book in a tone I'm not proud of.

"No," I said. "Absolutely not. Whatever this is, whatever she—" I stopped. I'd nearly said *she*. Interesting, the things the mouth does when the brain isn't guarding the door.

I doused the barrel. I carried the book back inside, dripping, and set it on the table, and we regarded each other, the book and I, with mutual distrust. Then I did the second-sensible thing, which was to read the rest of the page, because if the past was going to break into my house, I was at least going to get the terms of surrender in writing.

The handwriting — that fast, slanted, urgent script — laid it out the way Odile used to lay out lessons: no comfort first, no easing in, just the shape of the thing.

*A door,* it said. *A fire that is guttering, that has been guttering for a hundred years because no one taught it anything new.* And below that, circled twice as if the writer couldn't decide whether it was a warning or a plea:

*a boy who was never taught to want anything.*

I read that line four times. On the fourth reading I laughed, which startled both of us.

Never taught to want anything. That was — fine. That was accurate. That was so accurate it felt less like prophecy and more like a letter of reference. Because that was the curriculum, wasn't it? Odile's great unspoken lesson plan, delivered across fifteen years of herbs and knots and careful silences: want small things. Want bread and dry socks and a well-tended garden. Want nothing you'd cross a boundary for. Want nothing that could follow you.

I'd thought, fleeing, that I was escaping her teaching. Turns out I'd packed it in my bags and built my whole life out of it. The valley wasn't my refuge. The valley was my graduation.

The light was going amber outside. The fog was lifting, the way it did late in the afternoon, pulling back off the fields like a curtain drawn by a stagehand who knows the act is about to change. I should have lit lamps. I should have gone down and told Sefa I'd be late with her spinning-wheel charm. I should have done any of the ten thousand small orderly things that made up a day in the Valley of Ash, the wall I'd built one chore at a time between me and everything else.

I sat at the table and read the prophecy again, all of it, every word, tracing the slanted letters with a finger that wasn't quite steady. Door. Fire. A boy who wouldn't want to come. And at the bottom of the page, smaller, almost an afterthought, the way you write the thing you most mean when you're pretending it matters least:

*tonight*

The stove ticked. The kettle began, faintly, to sing.

And then the air did something air is not supposed to do.

It shuddered. Not wind — wind moves through air, bends it, borrows it. This was the air itself flinching, all at once, everywhere, the way a sheet snaps when you shake it out. Every lamp flame in my house leaned sideways. The kettle's song bent into a note I've never heard before or since, a note with an edge on it. Out in the lane, the geese went up all together, a white explosion of outrage, and Doss's voice stopped mid-insult, and the silence afterward had texture, had weight, had *attention*.

Nine years I'd lived inside the valley's dead air. I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — by never noticing it. And in that one snapping instant I understood, the way you understand a missing stair in the dark, that the air had always been holding its breath. Holding something *out*. Holding me *in*. And something had just touched it from the other side.

Something had crossed. Or something was asking to.

I stood very still in my kitchen, in the last amber light, with a book that wouldn't burn open on my table and my heart going like a hammer on a stuck door. The sketch stared off to the side, wary, listening for its name.

*He will not want to come.*

"No," I said again, quieter this time, to the book, to the air, to whoever was listening on the other side of a boundary that could not be crossed and apparently just had been. "You're wrong about one thing. I won't just *not want to*. I won't come at all."

The lamp flames straightened. The kettle forgot its strange note. Outside, the geese settled, feather by feather, back into the ordinary evening of the Valley of Ash.

And on the table, unhurried, unburnable, patient as only a hundred years can make a thing, the book turned its own next page.
