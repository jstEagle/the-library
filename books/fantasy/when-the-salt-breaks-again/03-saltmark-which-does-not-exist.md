## 3. Saltmark, Which Does Not Exist

I woke up drowning in reverse.

That's the only way I can describe it. Not water in the lungs — the opposite of that, as if my whole body had been a lung and something enormous had exhaled me into being. I was lying on my back on a street made of white stone, staring up at towers that leaned together overhead like old men sharing a secret, and every one of them was crusted in salt.

Not dusted with salt. Crusted. The buildings wore it the way a shipwreck wears barnacles — thick, geological, patient. It ran down their faces in frozen tears and pooled at their feet in drifts that glittered under a sky the color of an old bruise.

"Well," I said to nobody, because talking to nobody is a habit from the Valley and habits keep you sane. "Either I'm dead, or I'm dreaming, or the book has opinions."

The book.

I sat up too fast and immediately regretted it, because my skull performed a brief but thorough renovation. When the ringing stopped, I checked my coat out of pure reflex, and there it was — *The Salt Testament*, damp, dog-eared, smug. It had come through with me. Of course it had. Things like that don't stay behind. Things like that are the reason you leave.

"Where am I?" I asked it. It didn't answer, which was typical, though I'd already learned not to trust its silences.

I got up. My legs worked. The air worked, sort of — it tasted of brine and cold iron and something underneath both, something faintly sweet and wrong, like flowers arranged over a grave. And there was no wind. None at all. In the Valley of Ash we had no wind either, because there was nothing alive enough to push it around, and I recognized that particular stillness the way you recognize a family resemblance in a stranger's face.

I knew this place.

That's the part I want to be honest about, since honesty is apparently the theme of whatever catastrophe I've been drafted into. I *knew* it. Not from maps — I'd never seen a map of it, and I'll get to why, and when you hear why you'll want to sit down. I knew it the way you know a song you've never heard: some low, humming certainty in the sternum. These towers were wrong the way my own handwriting is wrong, familiar-wrong. The street curved where my feet expected it to curve. A doorway three buildings down stood open at exactly the height my shoulder would have cleared.

I had never been here. I had been here all my life.

* * *

The capital — and it was a capital, you can tell capitals; they have the posture of one — was drowned. Not underwater, though there was standing brine in the lower streets, black and mirror-still. Drowned the way a name gets drowned. Everything was intact and everything was abandoned, and the salt had gotten into all of it. Salt furred the lampposts. Salt sheathed a child's wooden horse left in a doorway, turned it into a ghost of itself, white and exact. Salt filled a fountain shaped like two hands cupped together, so that the hands held nothing but a fist of mineral forever.

I walked. What else was I going to do? The prophecy book had said tonight's date and my face, and then the Valley's dead air had shuddered at midnight like a struck bell, and now I was here, wherever here was, which meant the book was a set of instructions and I was the fool following them.

The towers sang.

I want to be careful how I say that, because I'm not a fanciful man. Twenty years mending charms that don't work will beat the fancy out of anyone. But the towers sang. Low, at the edge of hearing, a note like a wet finger on a glass rim, sustained, coming from everywhere. From the salt itself, maybe. And under the singing, quieter, there were voices — or the memory of voices, the shape a crowd leaves in a square after it goes home.

"You're dead," I told the city conversationally. "You're all long dead, this is a haunted place and I'm trespassing."

But even as I said it I knew it wasn't true, and the knowing arrived with a physical lurch, like a stair that isn't where your foot expects. The place wasn't dead. Dead things don't sing. Dead things don't ache. This place *ached*. It was alive the way a man is alive after the surgeons take everything they can take and he lies in the ward breathing on his own stubbornness.

There were people. That took me another hour to understand, because they didn't want to be seen, and I respect that instinct deeply, being a man who spent twenty years perfecting it.

The first one I saw was an old woman kneeling at the base of the fountain, pressing her palm flat against the stone, lips moving. She flickered. That's the word, though I hate it — she flickered, like a candle behind smoked glass, there and translucent and there again. When she saw me looking she didn't scream. She just looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read, and then she said, in a voice worn down to thread:

"You're real." Not a question. An accusation.

"I'm—"

"You're *real*," she said again, and began to cry without any change in her face, tears running down through the flicker, and she stood and walked away into a side street with her hand still pressed to her mouth, and I let her go because chasing grieving ghosts seemed rude even by my standards.

Except she wasn't a ghost. I found more of them — eleven, twelve, scattered through the streets like people left behind after an evacuation nobody announced. They were solid when the light was good and thin when it wasn't. They didn't eat. They didn't sleep that I could see. They gathered, in twos and threes, near certain doorways and corners, and I noticed eventually what those places had in common: warmth. Faint, sourceless warmth, the kind you feel near an oven wall, and in each of those places hung a smell of smoke that wasn't quite smoke — dragon-smoke, I'd have said if I'd known then what I know now, fire with a memory in it.

They lived where the fire had been. They persisted where the fire remembered them.

And everywhere else, the country was being forgotten in real time.

I saw it happen once, and I'll never unsee it. A row of houses off a small square, ordinary houses, salt-crusted like everything else. As I watched — and I mean *as I watched*, standing right there with my mouth open — the nearest house went soft. Its edges lost their agreement with themselves. The windows became suggestions. For a heartbeat it looked like a sketch someone had half-erased, graphite dragged sideways, and then it was a mound of salt, and then it wasn't anything, just a gap in the row, just absence wearing the outline of a house. Nobody screamed. There was nobody left inside to scream. But the woman across the square — a different woman, younger, holding a broom she clearly hadn't swept with in years — turned toward the gap and frowned, the way you frown trying to remember why you walked into a room.

She'd already forgotten there had ever been a house there. The forgetting moved faster than the erasing. The erasure was just cleanup.

I sat down on a salt step and put my head between my knees and did some slow arithmetic about my life choices.

Because here is the thing I understood, sitting there, and understanding it felt like swallowing a hook: somewhere, an empire had done this. You don't get half-erased cities and flickering survivors by accident. This was policy. This was somebody's Tuesday. A whole country — and it had *been* a country, the architecture told me that, the grandness of it, the harbor dry and salted over, the customs house with its scales, the boulevard wide enough for parades — a whole country had been unwritten, and the unwriting was still going, still hungry, working its way street by street while a few hundred souls huddled around embers of remembering.

And I, Gideon Zhou, charm-mender of the Valley of Ash, keeper of no one, son of no recorded mother, apprentice of a mentor who lied to me about everything including possibly her own age — I had been summoned here by a hundred-year-old book with my face in it.

"Home," I said out loud, testing the word, and the towers sang a half-tone louder, and I flinched like it had answered.

Because that was the grief arriving, right on schedule, right behind the awe, the way grief always does — awe first, wide-eyed, *look at this place, look at these towers, look at the craft in that bridge,* and then the turn, the cold slide: *this could have been mine. This should have been mine.* I stood in a drowned capital that fit me like a coat cut to my measurements and I grieved for a childhood I never got to have in it. Grieved for a language I almost recognized in the faded signage above the shopfronts — I could read maybe one word in five, and my mouth kept trying to shape the others. Grieved for festivals I'd never danced, for a harbor I'd never fished, for neighbors who'd have known my grandmother's name and used it to tease me.

You can't mourn a home you never had. I know that. Everyone knows that. Everyone is wrong.

I sat on that step until the bruise-colored sky darkened toward a night that came down like a lid, and the singing changed key, lower, wearier, and the flickering people drifted toward the warm places like moths with excellent manners, and I thought: *Odile.*

Twenty years I'd blamed her. For the exile dressed up as an errand, for the valley where magic went to die, for the charms that fizzled and the lessons that stopped and the last thing she ever said to me — *don't follow me, don't look back, live small and live unnoticed.* I had built a whole quiet life on the foundation of resenting her, and resentment, I'll tell you, makes excellent mortar. It holds for decades.

But she had written the book. She had written it a century ago, which was impossible, which meant everything I knew about her was furniture in a house built on a lie, and the lie was big enough to hold an erased country, and I was standing in the middle of it.

Somewhere in that city there would be answers. Cities always keep their paperwork; even drowned ones, even erased ones. Especially erased ones — an empire that rewrites history always misses a drawer.

I stood up. My knees cracked. Somewhere far off — farther than sound should travel, from the direction of what might once have been a palace or a temple, a district where the warm places clustered thickest and the glow on the horizon wasn't sunset because there was no sun left to set — something burned. I could feel it the way you feel a hearth through a wall. One fire, very old, very tired, holding up everything.

Holding up everything, and guttering.

I know guttering. I'd watched a candle do it ten thousand nights in a valley where nothing else was allowed to burn. A flame doesn't go out all at once. It argues with the dark. It leans, and recovers, and leans further, and spends itself in desperate bright bursts, and every burst is smaller than the last, until one lean doesn't recover.

The horizon leaned.

And the whole city cried out — not the people, the *city*, the towers' song breaking mid-note into something raw — and every flickering soul within my sight went briefly transparent as glass, and the woman with the broom dropped it and clutched at her own arms like a woman checking she still had any.

Then the fire steadied. Barely. The song resumed, a half-step weaker.

I started walking toward the burn before I decided to. My feet had apparently joined the conspiracy ahead of the rest of me — the same feet that had known the curve of these streets, the same blood, presumably, that had known everything else I'd spent twenty years not knowing.

Halfway across the square, I heard footsteps behind me that weren't a flickering survivor's — too heavy, too deliberate, boots with a soldier's heel-strike — and turned to find the empty street lit, at its far end, by a lantern carried by someone who absolutely did not flicker, and who was walking toward me with the unhurried confidence of a person carrying a map of a country that officially did not exist.

She was mud-spattered, gray-templed, and armed with what looked like forty years of grudges rolled into the set of her jaw. Over her shoulder hung a leather tube of the kind cartographers use, and when she saw my face she stopped dead, and looked at me the way you look at a figure stepping out of a page you drew yourself.

"Oh," she said. "It's actually you. The boy with the mark."

I put my hand over my face, where the birthmark sat, where the sketch in the book had put it.

"Right," I said. "We should probably talk."
