## 5. What the Fire Remembers

I have fed the fire my mother's face.

I want to be clear about that, because in forty years of tending the Emberhold I have learned that people imagine sacrifice as something grand — a sword, a life, a last stand on a salt gate. Nobody imagines it as this: an old woman kneeling before a shrine at dawn, holding a memory out flat in her cupped hands like bread, and watching a flame no bigger than a sleeping cat lean forward and take it from her.

My mother's face went first, forty-one years ago. Then her voice. Then the smell of her kitchen, which I had always assumed would be the thing I'd keep longest, and was not. The fire is not sentimental. It takes what you give it, and what you give it is whatever you can bear to lose least, because memories worth feeding are the ones with weight, and weight is the only currency it accepts.

You cannot feed the fire trivia. I tried, in my early years, when I still believed devotion could be clever. I gave it the name of a boy I'd kissed behind the fish-market when I was fifteen, thinking a kiss was cheap enough to spare. The fire dimmed. It dimmed the way a person dims when you offer them watered wine and call it supper. So I went back into myself and found something real — the sound of my daughter laughing at her own shadow, six months old, fat fists slapping stone — and the fire brightened so violently that the whole shrine rang like a struck bell, and I understood then what I was actually doing here.

The fire eats love. That's the trade. That has always been the trade, back when there were dragons to make it unnecessary.

* * *

Let me explain what the Emberhold is, since the maps won't. There are no maps anymore; that was rather the point. Saltmark was drowned — not under water, though we kept the water for atmosphere, but under erasure. The empire of Vess looked at our country and decided it had never been, and their truth-eaters went through the world like moths through a wardrobe, eating every record, every memory outside these borders, every mention in every book. Inside Saltmark, the erasure could not reach, because dragon-fire burns hotter than lies. But fires need fuel, and dragons are gone, and so the last fire sits in a shrine in the oldest quarter of a drowned capital, and it stays alive on the memories of people who refuse to forget.

There were nine hundred of us keepers, once. Nine hundred priests and priestesses taking turns, each giving what they could spare, which was never enough, which was everything.

I am the ninth hundredth and the last, and I am running out of myself.

* * *

This morning the fire woke me before dawn, which it only does when something matters. It doesn't speak — not yet, not in words, though I have begun to suspect it remembers more language than it lets on, hoarding vocabulary the way a miser hoards coin. What it does instead is change color. A contented fire burns gold. A hungry one goes orange, then red, then the deep bruised crimson of a wound held up to light.

It was crimson when I came down the shrine steps, and it was shaped wrong. Fires burn upward. This one leaned east, toward the sea-gate, like a dog pointing.

"Someone's coming," I said, because after four decades you talk to your god the way you talk to a spouse: without dignity and without hope of reply.

But the fire did something it hadn't done since the night my predecessor died and left me alone with it. It showed me something.

I should describe how that works, or rather how it worked the one other time, because Ilsa of the Emberhold does not use words like vision lightly. The old priestess, Mother Yeva, told me the fire keeps everything it has ever eaten. Nothing is destroyed in there; it is only stored, pressed flat like flowers in a book, all the love of nine hundred keepers filed away in flame. And sometimes, if the fire wants something badly enough, it will spend a little of its hoard — show you a page from its collection — the way a starving man might spend a coin he meant to save.

It spent some now.

The heat came off the shrine in a wave and I felt it press against my closed eyes, and inside the dark there was a picture: a young man standing in a millrace, holding a book, his face lit by nothing — that was the strangest part, the light coming from the book itself, or from him, I couldn't tell. He had ink-stained fingers and the wary look of someone who has spent his whole life being careful. Behind him, gray hills and dead air. The Valley of Ash. I knew the valley the way everyone in Saltmark knows it: as the place where Odile went, the place where magic went to die, the place our last hope was buried like a seed in frozen ground.

And the fire showed me the date. Not spoke it — showed it, burned it across the inside of my eyelids in letters of ember: tonight.

Then the picture folded itself away, and the fire settled back into gold, and I knelt there on cold stone with my heart going like a trapped bird, because I knew that face.

Not from life. From the prophecy.

* * *

Every keeper learns the Prophecy of the Door. We learn it as children learn table manners — early, thoroughly, and without understanding why anyone bothers. It is a century old, written by a woman named Odile whose handwriting fills the margins with corrections, as though she were editing fate itself, which, knowing what I know now, she may literally have been doing. It says a door will open. It says a guttering fire will find new wood. It says a boy who was never taught to want anything will come out of the dead valley and choose, and the choosing is the whole point, because a door opened by force is just a breach, but a door opened by choice is a promise.

For a hundred years we have recited it at every feeding. For a hundred years the fire has eaten our mothers' faces and our daughters' names and our first loves and our last arguments, and we have told ourselves: soon. Soon the boy comes. Soon the fire stops being hungry.

Soon is a word that rots in the mouth if you say it long enough.

I am sixty-three years old. I gave the fire my wedding day at thirty. I gave it my daughter's name at fifty — I'll come to that, or rather I won't, because that is precisely the memory I cannot come to; I fed it to the fire and now when I try to reach for my daughter I find a hole shaped exactly like her, edges worn smooth from touching. I know I had a child. I know she was the best thing I ever made. I do not know her name. Some mornings I stand in front of the shrine and bargain with it: give it back, just for a minute, just long enough to say it aloud once, and I'll bring you something else, anything —

The fire never bargains. That's the difference between us. I still think like a woman who owns things.

* * *

After the vision faded I did what I always do: I counted what's left.

Keepers used to keep ledgers. I keep mine in my head, which is safer, since paper can be eaten by truth-eaters and heads can only be eaten by the fire, and the fire is polite about it. Here is the inventory of Ilsa, priestess of the Emberhold, as of this morning:

One sister, dead these twenty years — her funeral remains, vivid, available. Costly, though. Funerals are rich fare.

A handful of faces from the congregation of souls who still live in the districts the fire shelters — old Brannoc who repairs nets and complains about it; the twins Vess-and-Vessa (their parents thought it funny; the empire made it tragic) who sing salt-tower songs on the corner where the acoustics are kind. These are not mine to feed. The fire eats keeper-memories only, given freely. Congregation memories would be theft, and gods can tell the difference even when priests pretend otherwise.

And one memory I have defended for eleven years, the way a woman defends a hill she's already lost half of: the day my daughter was born. Not her name — that's gone — but the birth itself. The rain on the window. My hands shaking. The midwife saying *she's perfect, look at her, look at her* — and I have never been able to establish whether the child was a she or a he, because along with the name went everything that would tell me, and I have decided, in the way one decides to stop picking at a scab, that it doesn't matter. Love doesn't check details. That's the whole point of love. I loved a person whose name I no longer know, and I love them still, at the exact temperature I loved them at the moment the fire swallowed the rest.

That memory is the last log on the pile. After it, the fire eats dust.

* * *

Here is my fear, and I will say it plainly because a priestess who lies to herself tends a lie, not a fire: I do not know if we survive until tonight.

The fire has been dimming for years, slowly, the way a man starves. Two winters ago it stumbled — I felt it stumble, the way you feel a heartbeat skip — and for eleven minutes the shelter-thin veil over the Fisherman's Quarter went slack, and when it recovered, forty-three souls in that district had simply stopped. Not died. Stopped. They stood where they were, mid-errand, mid-sentence, and became the kind of absent that statues are. The erasure had gotten in through the gap and eaten them alive, quietly, while their neighbors watched and then, horribly, began to forget they'd ever been neighbors.

I walked those streets afterward. I found a table set for two meals where one person used to sit, the second bowl still warm, the second chair pushed back at the angle of someone who got up to answer a door that never knocked. I did not know the man's name. Neither did anyone else. The fire ate my grief about him too, eventually — grief is dense, it burns long — and now I remember only that I grieved, the way you remember having had a fever.

So yes. I am afraid. Not of dying. Dying is easy; dying is what happens to countries all the time. I'm afraid of the arithmetic. One memory left. Hours until the prophecy's date. And somewhere out past the sea-gate, if the fire showed me true, a boy with ink-stained fingers is walking toward a country that officially does not exist, and he had better walk quickly, because the thing keeping him — and me, and forty thousand lingering souls, and a hundred years of stubborn refusal — is down to its final meal.

I have thought, some nights, about whether I hate it. The fire. Whether kneeling here is devotion or just the longest hostage situation in history. And the honest answer is that I don't know, and that not-knowing is itself a kind of faith, and faith was easier when there were nine hundred of us to share the doubt.

But then some evenings the fire does this thing — small, gratuitous, unbudgeted. It flares gold and throws shapes on the shrine wall, and once, years ago, the shape was a woman dancing with a small child, and I sat beneath it with tears running down my face and no idea why, and the fire dimmed apologetically afterward, like a dog that has brought you a dead bird and realized you're crying.

It remembers her. It holds everything I've given it, and it holds it well, and sometimes it spends a little back on me, unasked. Whatever else the Emberhold is — furnace, vault, god, trap — it is also the only place left in the universe where my daughter exists at all.

You do not hate the ark you're drowning on.

* * *

Toward evening I climbed the shrine steps to the observation gallery, where the keepers of old used to watch the harbor, and I looked east across the drowned capital toward the sea-gate. The sun was going down the color of the fire's hunger. Salt-crusted towers stood against it like teeth. Somewhere beyond the horizon, past the veils and the veiled waters and the empire's tidy maps with their tidy blank space where we used to be, a millrace was carrying a book toward a young man, and a dead valley was about to shudder, and a door a century locked was about to hear a key turn.

The fire leaned east again. It had gone from crimson to something brighter — not hopeful exactly. Insistent.

"All right," I told it. "All right. Tonight, then."

And because I am a priestess, and because forty years of practice must count for something, I did the evening rite. I knelt. I cupped my hands. And I held out the last memory I owned — the rain, the window, the midwife's voice, the beginning of my child — and I held it there, trembling, at the edge of the flame, and I did not let go yet. I held it the way you hold a rope over a cliff, feeling its full weight for the first time in eleven years.

"Not yet," I said to the fire. "Wait for him. When he walks through that gate — when I see his face match the one you showed me — then I'll feed you the last of me, and you'll have strength enough to meet him properly. Until then, we starve together. Deal?"

The fire flickered — once, twice — and went still, and I choose to believe that was a yes, and I choose to believe that the warmth it pushed against my palms was not appetite but patience, and I knelt there in the gathering dark of a country that does not exist, an old woman bargaining with the last dragon-fire, holding my daughter over the flames.

Somewhere east, a boundary that cannot be crossed was about to be crossed.

And the fire, patient as a banked coal, waited for its boy.
