## 6. The Boy Who Was Told Never

The first thing you learn in the Vessian garrison is that Saltmark does not exist. They teach it before you learn to load a rifle. Before you learn to salute. Sergeant Brakke made us say it every morning for the first month, forty boys standing in the frost with our breath steaming, chanting it like a prayer: *There is no Saltmark. There was never a Saltmark. The coast is empty and always has been.*

I was good at it. I could say it in my sleep. Sometimes I think I did say it in my sleep, because Brakke had a habit of walking the barracks at night listening for wrongness, and I never got flagged.

Here is the second thing you learn, though nobody teaches it: if a place truly did not exist, you wouldn't need forty boys to stand in the frost every morning swearing it out loud.

I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me tell it properly, the way it happened, which is not the way it was supposed to happen.

* * *

The intruder came ashore on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Nothing important ever happens on a Tuesday.

I was on seawall watch, third shift, which is the shift they give you when you're fourteen and your job is mostly to be a pair of eyes that reports to real soldiers. The fog off the drowned quarter was doing its usual thing — lying thick over the salt-crusted towers like the whole district was asleep under a blanket of grey wool. Except the towers don't sleep. That's the thing about the towers. At night they sing.

Not metaphorically. I mean they sing. A low sound, like wind through bottles, but it comes in phrases. It has pauses where pauses shouldn't be. Corporal Denny says it's the tide in the hollow stone. Sergeant Brakke says there is no singing, and writes up anyone who reports it as hearing-things, which goes in your file, and files follow you in Vess the way debts follow gamblers.

So I'd learned not to report the singing.

Which is why, when I saw a man climbing down the salt-slick rocks below the seawall — a man with no lantern, moving like someone who'd been told all his life that falling is a problem for later — I hesitated about the singing more than about him. Because he had stopped halfway down, and his head was tilted, and he was listening to it the way you listen to a voice calling your name from another room.

Then he slipped, swore in a language I didn't know but somehow almost understood, and dropped the last six feet into the shallows with a splash that woke half the garrison's dogs.

I should have blown the whistle. That was the entire job. Whistle, shout, run, get corporals. Instead I stood there watching a stranger wring out his coat sleeve in the dark and look up at me through the fog like he already knew what I was going to do next, which was nothing, which was the wrong thing, and he looked sorry about it in advance.

"Evening," he said. His accent was wrong. Not foreign-wrong. Old-wrong. Like a word that used to be common and isn't anymore.

"You're trespassing," I said, because that's what the manual says, and the manual has never once been helpful.

"I'm sorry," he said, and he sounded like he meant it, which confused me more than anything else that night. "Where am I?"

"Vess," I said. "The coast road. You're not supposed to be here."

He looked past me, up at the towers, at their white crusted shoulders rising out of the fog, and something crossed his face that I've thought about every day since. It was grief. But it wasn't grief for himself. It was the kind you wear when you come home and find the house burned and everyone else pretending there was never a house.

"This is Saltmark," he said quietly. "It's still here."

"There's no such place," I said. Fast. Automatic. The morning chant coming out of me like vomit. "There is no Saltmark. There was never—"

"Who taught you that?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Because somebody had. Somebody sat down one day and decided that forty children needed to say those words every morning until the words wore grooves into them, and I had never once wondered who, or why, or what it cost. You don't wonder things like that in Vess. Wondering is a fileable offense.

"Nobody taught me anything," I said, which was the most honest lie I'd ever told, and he heard it, and he had the decency not to smile.

Boots were coming along the wall path — Corporal Denny, by the heavy flat tread. I had maybe thirty seconds to blow my whistle or not blow it.

I want to be clear that I did not decide anything. Deciding implies you know what the options are. What actually happened is that my hand went to the whistle and then went past it, to my lantern, and turned the shutter toward the sea instead of the shore, so the light fell wrong, so Denny would sweep his torch along the wall and see only fog and an empty beach and a boy doing his job badly.

By the time the light swung back, the man was gone into the drowned quarter, and the towers were singing, and I swear on whatever's left of my soul that this time they sang louder, like they'd seen what I did.

Denny clapped me on the shoulder. "Quiet night."

"Yes, Corporal," I said.

Both of us lied. Only one of us knew it yet.

* * *

Here's what I knew about the drowned quarter, which is to say, here's what I'd been allowed to know: nothing. Officially it was a geological formation. Unofficially — meaning, in the things soldiers say when the sergeant's out of earshot — it was ruins, old ones, older than Vess, and we didn't patrol it because maps showed nothing there, and you can't patrol nothing. That was the phrase Denny liked. *You can't patrol nothing, boy.* He said it like it settled something.

But here's what else I knew, the part that lived under my ribs where I kept everything I wasn't supposed to: my grandmother hummed songs in a language she claimed was just nonsense she'd picked up as a girl. She died when I was nine, and the last thing she ever said to me was *the water remembers*, and my mother laughed and said Grandma was soft in the head at the end, and I nodded, and I have never once believed her.

Because the songs my grandmother hummed were the songs the towers sing.

I figured that out years ago, alone in a watchtower at two in the morning, and I never told a living soul, and carrying it around was like carrying a coal in a closed fist. You get used to the burn. You even start to think the burn is yours.

The morning after the man came ashore, Brakke posted the notice. INTRUDER SIGHTED. SHOOT ON SIGHT IF ARMED. REPORT ALL CONTACTS. And beneath it, in the smaller print they use for things they're embarrassed by: REMINDER — THE RUINS ARE OF NO HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE.

Of no historical significance. Posted on a garrison wall, in a fort, built by someone, on a coast that someone named, guarding ruins that someone built. I read that line maybe forty times while pretending to read the rest, and each time it got less convincing, the way a word gets strange if you stare at it too long.

*No historical significance.* Then why remind us?

* * *

They gave me the arrest detail because I'd seen him first. That's how it works — the witness walks the beat, on the theory that a face seen once will be recognized twice. So there I was, fourteen years old with a rifle too big for me and a warrant too big for both of us, walking the drowned quarter's edge with orders to take one Gideon Zhou alive, unharmed, priority absolute, by order of the Inquisition.

The Inquisition being involved was its own bad sign. Garrisons handle smugglers and deserters. When the truth-eaters put their seal on a paper, it means the thing you're hunting knows something, and knowing things is the empire's rarest and most guarded commodity.

I found him in the third hour, sitting in the open, deliberately, on the steps of a tower that the charts called a rock formation. He had a fire going — a small one, hedge-sized, the kind of fire that looks like it's keeping itself lit out of politeness — and he was reading a book that looked like it had been fished out of the sea and resented it.

"You're the boy from the wall," he said without looking up.

"You're under arrest." My voice cracked on *arrest*, which was mortifying, and he had the grace again to pretend not to notice.

"All right," he said.

That stopped me. Warrants assume flight. Chasing, fighting, at minimum arguing. Nobody in the manual covers *all right*. I stood there with my rifle half-raised feeling like a dog that's caught a ball nobody threw.

"You're supposed to run," I said.

"Running hasn't worked out well for me lately." He closed the book. Up close he looked tired in a way that wasn't about sleep — tired like a debt, like something owed across a long time. "You let me go last night. Why?"

"I didn't. My light was—" I stopped, because lying to a man under arrest seemed suddenly stupid, and also because I genuinely didn't know why. "I don't know."

"Can I ask you something? Since you're arresting me anyway." He nudged the fire with a stick. "Do you hear them? The towers. Singing."

My fist closed around the coal in my chest. "There's no singing."

"Mm," he said, in the tone of a man filing something away. "That's what the book said people would do." He held it up. Torn cover, water-swollen pages, ink that moved if you looked too long. "Written a hundred years ago. It has tonight's date in it. It has my face in it. Do you understand how much I wish it didn't?"

"You expect me to believe—"

"No," he said. "I don't expect you to believe anything. Believing's not yours to give, is it? They took it when they were done teaching you." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "I just want to know one true thing before your sergeant gets here. When you were small — before the chanting, before the fort — did anybody sing to you? An old woman, maybe. Songs in no language you knew."

The coal went white-hot.

"My grandmother," I heard myself say, "was senile."

"Was she?" He turned the book toward me. There was a page of music in it — actual notation, faded, the notes swimming like fish in shallow water. "Hum something she sang. Just a few bars. For the record."

And God help me, I did. Fourteen years old, rifle in my hands, warrant in my pocket, and I hummed four bars of my dead grandmother's nonsense songs to a prisoner in a country that didn't exist, and the towers above us picked it up. Carried it. Sang it back down through the fog, note for note, with the pauses where pauses shouldn't be, and the fire on his little hearth flared green for exactly as long as the song lasted.

The book's pages rustled though there was no wind.

"That song's in here," Gideon said softly. "Under a title. It's a lullaby, Tam." He'd read my name off my collar tab; I hadn't given it to him. "It's called *Sleep, the Water Keeps You*. It's a Saltmark lullaby. Your grandmother was singing Saltmark to you the whole time."

"There is no Saltmark," I said. Third time in two days. And for the first time, saying it felt like biting tin.

"No," he agreed. "There isn't. That's rather the whole problem."

* * *

Sergeant Brakke's patrol was ten minutes out. I knew the schedule; I'd helped copy it. Ten minutes is either plenty of time or none, depending on what you are.

I pointed my rifle at the ground and told him the tunnel behind the cistern where the wall footing's collapsed — the gap the patrols call the Nothing, because officially nothing's there. I told him which streets flood and which just pretend to. I told him the truth-eaters were coming behind the patrol, three of them, and what truth-eaters do to people who know things, and I watched him go pale in the firelight and understood that he hadn't known that part, and that I had just chosen, without deciding, whose side the information belonged to.

"Why?" he asked. Same question as before. Still fair.

I looked up at the towers, singing low over our heads like a congregation holding a note.

"Because I'm tired of patrolling nothing," I said.

He studied me for a long moment, and then he did the thing that finished it — he doused his fire, tucked the terrible book inside his coat, and said, "Come with me, then."

"I can't. I have a post. I have—" A mother in a garrison town who'd stop getting my pay allotment. A bunk. A name in a file that was, as of four days ago, clean. Everything the chant had promised me if I just kept saying it. "I have to report that I lost you."

"Do you?" he said. "Or could you report that you never found me? One of those is a lie, Tam. Think hard about which."

Brakke's whistle sounded two streets over.

Gideon went, quick and quiet, down into the Nothing, and the fog folded shut behind him, and I stood alone on the steps of a tower that was not a rock formation, in a country that did not exist, holding a rifle I had not fired and a whistle I did not blow.

When the patrol reached me, I saluted and said, "Lost him in the flooded streets, Sergeant. He knew the ground better than us."

All true. Every word. I'm learning there's a craft to it.

Brakke wrote it up without looking at me. But the truth-eater standing behind him — grey coat, grey eyes, a face like paper that had been written on and scraped clean too many times — looked at me for one second too long, the way you look at a door you intend to come back to.

That night I didn't sleep in the barracks. I sat on the seawall with my grandmother's songs in my throat and the towers answering, and I made a list in my head of everything I'd been told, and beside each item I wrote, silently, the only word the last two days had left me.

*Liar. Liar. Liar.*

At dawn I deserted. Or I followed. Depending on which of us is writing the history — and I'm beginning to understand that's the entire war, right there, in that choice of word.

I went into the drowned quarter after him, toward the Door Out, and behind me I heard boots on the seawall taking up the chase, and ahead of me the towers sang my grandmother's lullaby like they'd been waiting a hundred years for someone to finally sing along.
