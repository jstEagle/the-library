## 7. Roads That Unmap Themselves

The road out of the drowned capital lasted exactly as long as Maren's confidence in it, which is to say: half a morning.

"West-southwest," she said, tapping her map with one finger the way you'd prod a suspicious animal. "The old salt road to Corrow Ford. It held when I mapped it six years ago."

It did not hold now. We watched it go.

I don't mean it collapsed — I mean it *left*. One moment there was a road under our feet, flagstones furred with salt-bloom and the ghostly impressions of cartwheel ruts; the next, the stretch behind us simply wasn't. Not rubble, not overgrowth. *Not.* The air where it had been had a texture like the inside of your own eyelid, a blankness so complete that my eyes kept sliding off it, refusing to land. Tam stood at the edge of the nothing and threw a pebble into it. The pebble didn't fall. It just stopped existing somewhere about knee-height, and Tam made a sound I'd only ever heard from cats discovering cucumbers.

"It eats from the edges inward," Maren said. Her voice was steady in the way of someone who has practiced steadiness at a thing until the practice holds even when the thing doesn't. "Cities first. Then roads. Then names." She rolled up her map with unnecessary violence. "We walk faster than it thinks."

"Does it think?" I asked.

"I don't know. I'd rather not find out by standing still."

So we walked faster than it thought.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about magic before this week: that it was a debt, and debts are shameful. Odile taught me charms the way other people teach children table manners — quietly, constantly, with an undertone of *don't let anyone see you doing this.* Every cantrip she put in my hands came with a warning attached. A warming charm could boil blood if you lost count. A finding-charm could find things that wanted not to be found. Hedge-magic, she called it, dirt-magic, small magic — as if smallness were a virtue and not a leash.

In the Valley of Ash, none of it worked anyway. That was the joke of my education: ten years of lessons for a talent that died the moment it crossed the valley line. I used to think Odile was teaching me for her own amusement, a caged bird taught to sing for an audience of one. Now I was starting to suspect the cage had been the point all along, but I wasn't ready to think about that yet. There was a country dissolving behind us. There was always something conveniently dissolving.

Here, though — here in Saltmark, which did not exist — here my hands itched.

I noticed it first at Corrow Ford, or where Corrow Ford should have been. The river was still there; water, apparently, is harder to erase than stone, maybe because nobody can agree on its shape long enough to delete it. But the ford was gone, and the crossing stones were gone, and on the far bank a cluster of souls stood watching us — Saltmark's remaining dead-alive people, gray around the edges like paper left in rain, their faces polite and hungry.

"They can't cross," Ilsa murmured. She'd been quiet most of the day, which frightened me more than the erasure did. Priestesses who talk constantly are performing calm. Priestesses who go silent are spending everything they have somewhere else. "The fire keeps them anchored where they lived. If they leave their ground—" She made a small gesture, opening her hand, letting something imaginary blow away.

"So we cross alone," Maren said.

"The current's wrong," Tam offered. He'd waded in to his knees and come back looking like he'd argued with something underwater and lost on points. "It pulls sideways. Toward the blank part."

I looked at the river, and the river looked back — not literally, I want to be clear, I'm not given to poetry about rivers — but there was a wrongness in it, a lean in the current like a held breath. And my hands itched. Not metaphorically. The palms of both hands prickled with heat, the exact sensation of holding Odile's charm-coals too long, except there were no coals. There was nothing in my hands at all.

"Move back," I said, before I could talk myself out of it.

"Why?" Maren asked, sharp. "What are you—"

I didn't answer because I didn't know. I knelt at the bank and put my palms flat against the water, and something went through me that I can only describe as *recognition*. The river recognized me. That sounds mad. It felt less mad than everything else that week.

Hedge-magic is small work. Finding, warming, mending. You ask politely and the world does you a courtesy. What I did at Corrow Ford was not asking. I took hold of the current the way you'd take hold of a rope, and I *turned it*, and the water bent itself into a humped ridge — a spine of river standing up out of itself, solid enough to walk on, bridging thirty feet of impossible current — and then I let go and sat down hard in the mud because my legs had stopped being interested in standing.

Nobody said anything. The souls on the far bank pressed their gray hands together, whether in thanks or prayer or hunger I couldn't tell. Ilsa looked at me with an expression I'd learn to read later, when there were fewer expressions left in her: recognition again, but grieving.

"That was dragon-work," she said softly.

"No," I said. "That was a finding-charm. Big."

"Gideon." Maren crouched next to me. She had mud on her boots and fear on her face and she was doing an admirable job of showing neither. "What did it feel like?"

"Warm," I said. Which was true, and insufficient, and we both knew it. What it felt like was coming home to a house I'd never lived in and finding my name already carved over the door. But you don't say things like that to people you met four days ago. "It felt warm. Can we cross the bridge before it remembers how to be a river?"

We crossed. Tam went last, and halfway across he stopped and said, "It's humming," and I said, "Don't stop on my bridge, Tam," and he moved, and the bridge held until the last boot left it, and then it sighed back into being ordinary water. Behind us, the near bank finished dissolving with a sound like a page turning.

* * *

That night we camped in the shell of a coaching inn whose sign still swung — *The Kindly Tide*, the letters said, though the paint was salt-eaten and the T had gone. Fires don't burn well in Saltmark unless they're fed properly; ordinary wood gives a sullen, apologetic flame, like it knows it isn't wanted. Ilsa built ours from memory instead. She'd take a stick, breathe on it, murmur something, and set it down glowing. Each log, she told me once, was a memory she no longer needed. I tried not to do the arithmetic of how many memories forty years of nights required, and failed, and lay awake doing it.

Tam slept like a garrison boy sleeps — instantly, totally, one hand curled near an imaginary weapon. Maren sat up redrawing the route by moonless light, muttering. And I sat by the fire and did the thing I'd been avoiding since the ford.

I held out my hand and asked the fire to jump.

It's a child's trick. Odile taught it to me when I was nine: cup your palm, invite the flame, catch it like a tame bird. In the valley it had never once worked, and I'd decided that was fine, that was good, small failures for small magics, no harm done. Here, in the light of Ilsa's memory-fire, I cupped my palm and invited.

The whole fire stood up.

Not jumped — *stood*, every tongue of it at attention, leaning toward me like wheat toward wind, and the warmth that poured off it wasn't warmth anymore but something with intent in it, something curious, and for one vertiginous moment I felt it *looking* at me, feeling along my edges the way a dog noses a stranger's pockets. Looking for something. Recognizing something.

I closed my fist. The fire sat back down, chastened, and I sat back too, and my heart was going like a drum-corps, and Tam — who was supposed to be asleep — said from the darkness:

"Do it again."

"No."

"You're scared of it."

"Yes."

He crawled closer, boy-shaped and earnest in the firelight. "My drill-master said fear's just information. Means the thing matters." He held his hands out toward the flames. "It mattered when I was scared of the dark. It matters now."

"That's the worst wisdom I've ever heard, and I was raised by a woman who communicated primarily in proverbs."

He grinned. Fourteen-year-olds grin like that when they've landed something. "Do it again."

And here is the thing I haven't admitted to anyone yet, the thing I'll probably deny under direct questioning: I wanted to. For ten years I had wanted nothing — wanting was dangerous, wanting got you found — and here, in a country that officially did not exist, being chased by an erasure that ate roads and names, sitting beside a priestess who paid for our safety with pieces of her own mind, next to a boy who'd been lied to by an empire since birth — here, wanting was suddenly the least dangerous thing available. My whole life I'd been taught that power was shame. Out here, shame was a luxury we couldn't afford.

I opened my hand. The fire rose. This time I didn't flinch, and this time, when it looked at me, I looked back, and I felt — faintly, far off, like a voice through several walls — something that was almost words. Almost a name. Almost mine.

Then Ilsa's hand closed over my wrist, gentle as snowfall, and the fire sank.

"Not tonight," she said. "It has so little to say with. Let it save its voice."

"What would it say?" I whispered.

She looked at me a long time. Forty years of spent memories were stacked up behind her eyes like rings in a tree, and whatever she saw in my face made her older.

"Your name," she said. "But you're not ready to hear it said properly."

* * *

Three more days west, three more roads eaten behind us. Maren navigated by stubbornness and landmarks that refused to die: a bell tower with no bell, a milestone reading CORROW 4 and VESS ∅ — the empire's mark, a circle with a slash, stamped over the old name like a censor's thumb. The erasure moved at night mostly, we learned, a tide-line of nothing advancing a few hundred yards per dark, patient as interest on a loan. You could stand at its edge and watch the country thin. Fields went first, then fences, then the idea of fields, and finally the horizon itself developed a smooth patch, like a dent in glass.

On the fourth morning we found the village that was mid-erasure.

I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is a kind of respect: the eastern half of it was gone, cleanly, completely, and the western half was still there, and the border between ran down the middle of the high street like a dropped stitch. On the living side, doors stood open. Meals sat half-eaten. A cradle rocked with no wind. The souls had gathered at the stitch-line, dozens of them, gray and flickering, pressed up against the nothing the way drowning men press against ice — and where they touched it, they frayed.

"We can't carry them all," Maren said. Flat. Practical. Hating herself for it.

"Some," Ilsa said.

"How do you choose?"

Ilsa didn't answer, and I understood, with a lurch, that she'd been choosing for forty years. Choosing what to feed the fire. Choosing what to forget. Choosing, each dawn, which piece of herself the country was worth.

Tam was crying without any noise at all, which is a garrison skill I hope he unlearns someday.

And my hands itched.

This time I knew better than to pretend otherwise. Whatever lived in me — hedge-magic grown monstrous, or dragon-fire wearing hedge-magic's clothes — it woke at the sight of that stitch-line the way a surgeon wakes at a wound. I walked to the edge of the nothing. Up close it wasn't black or white; it was *unwritten*, and it smelled like the moment before a sneeze, and my skin prickled with a knowledge I hadn't been taught and couldn't have been, because nobody alive had ever been taught it.

"Odile never showed me anything like this," I said aloud, mostly to myself. "She never showed me anything big at all."

"Maybe," Maren said carefully, "she was afraid of what you'd do with it."

"Maybe she was afraid of what I'd *learn* I was."

I put my hands out — not touching the erasure, I'm not suicidal, merely adjacent — and I pulled. There's no better word. I pulled on the village the way I'd pulled on the river, except a village is heavier than water, a village is *people-shaped*, and the thing inside me answered with a force that scared me worse than the nothing did: it rose up glad, eager, enormous, like a dog hearing its name after years, and the stitch-line *shuddered*. The nothing paused. Actually paused, the way a tide hesitates between waves. And into that hesitation I shoved — there is no dignity in the memory, I shoved like a farmer moving a stuck cart — a handful of the village: the high street, the doors, the cradle, the names of it, whatever names I could snatch from the air.

The western half held. The souls on that side steadied, gray going briefly silver, and turned toward me with their hungry polite faces, and I felt them *recognize* me, all of them, a whole streetful of recognition hitting at once, and I heard it again, clearer, that voice-through-walls, and this time it wasn't almost my name.

It was singing it.

I stumbled back. The effort cost me — nosebleed, ringing ears, legs like wet rope — but the village-half held, and the erasure, thwarted or bored, slid on northward around us, taking a barn and a well and leaving the rest.

Maren caught my elbow. "You stopped it."

"For about an hour. Maybe a day." I wiped my nose on my sleeve, which is not a heroic gesture no matter how you frame it. "Whatever I am, I'm apparently a very temporary wall."

"A wall that talks back," Tam said, awed.

"A wall that *sings*," Ilsa corrected, and her voice had gone strange, reverent and terrified in equal measure. She gripped my shoulder, and through her glove I felt how cold her hand had gotten, how much of herself she'd already burned. "Gideon. When the last dragon-fire gutters out, it doesn't vanish. It passes. Into a keeper. Into the one it recognizes." Her grip tightened. "It has been recognizing you since the ford."

Behind us, far to the east, the drowned capital's towers caught the morning light one last time — and then, district by district, began to go out like windows closing.

Ahead, on the road we still had, something else waited: hoofprints. Fresh. Round the prints, the dust lay in neat spirals that no horse makes and no wind draws — spirals I recognized from Maren's shuddering description two nights ago, drawn in spilled salt on an inn table while she wouldn't look at them.

Truth-eaters had entered Saltmark.

And the tracks led west. The same direction we were walking.
