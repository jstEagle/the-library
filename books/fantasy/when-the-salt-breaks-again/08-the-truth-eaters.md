## 8. The Truth-Eaters

There is a particular smell to a truth-eater's tent that you never forget once you've worked for the empire: ink and vinegar and something underneath like a cellar where bread has gone quietly, politely wrong. I had spent six years mapping coastlines the Survey wanted drawn and four years mapping ones it didn't, and in all that time I had learned exactly two rules about truth-eaters. Rule one: they are always hungrier than they let on. Rule two: never, under any circumstances, tell them something true while they're looking at your mouth instead of your eyes.

I broke neither rule that night. I'm rather proud of that, considering.

* * *

The camp sat on the ridge above the drowned causeway — white canvas, grey banners, and the Vessian double-sun stitched in thread so black it looked like a hole in the fabric rather than a decoration. Truth-eaters don't travel with armies; armies travel with them, the way a wound travels with a body. There were perhaps forty of them, which meant the empire believed whatever was happening in Saltmark was worth forty appetites.

I walked in through the front, because walking in through the back is what guilty people do, and I have been guilty of many things but never of being obvious about it.

"Maren Voss." The voice came from the largest tent, warm as a banked stove, and my stomach did the thing it always does when I hear that voice, which is to drop three inches and pretend it didn't. "Still carrying that ridiculous satchel. Come in before the wind takes you."

Corvelan Ashe. My old mentor. The man who taught me that a map is an argument, and that whoever wins the argument owns the ground. He sat behind a folding table covered in charts — *my* charts, I realized, with a small cold jolt. The ones the Survey had confiscated when they disgraced me. Someone had been reading them carefully. Someone had annotated them in a hand I knew better than my own.

"You look well," I lied.

"And you look tired," he said, which was not a lie at all, because Corvelan never bothered lying about anything he could get for free. "Sit. Drink. You're wet to the knee, which means you've been walking the salt flats, which means you've found him."

"I've been surveying," I said. "It's what I do. It's the only thing I'm still licensed to do, technically, since you people took everything else."

"We took nothing you hadn't already given away." He poured two cups of the vinegary tea truth-eaters drink because it keeps the palate sharp, or so they claim. I suspect they drink it because everyone else hates sharing a table with them. "You mapped ruins that were erased from every record in the empire. Do you know how that reads from where I sit, Maren? It doesn't read as treason. Treason is simple. It reads as *grief.* And grief, unlike treason, can be useful."

I drank the tea. It tasted like drinking an argument.

"There's a boy," Corvelan said, conversationally, turning one of my charts toward me. The Door Out was marked on it — I'd drawn the approach in my own cipher, the one only I could read, or so I'd believed for eleven smug years. "A hedge-witch's boy out of the Valley of Ash. A book named him. Books don't name boys unless someone wants them named, and we are very interested in who does the wanting. You've seen him. Your tracks are all over the western flats, and there are four sets of them now, not one."

"Surveyors travel in teams."

"Surveyors travel in teams of *surveyors.*" He smiled. It was the smile he used on first-year cartographers when they brought him a beautiful map full of beautiful errors. "One set of those tracks belongs to a garrison boy, Maren. Tam Ossler. Fourteen. His lantern went dark two nights ago and his captain is beside himself. The other set belongs to a priestess of a fire the empire extinguished eighty years ago, which should be impossible, which means it isn't, which means we have a problem shaped exactly like you."

Outside, the wind came off the salt flats and made the tents breathe. Somewhere below us, past the drowned causeway, the towers of Saltmark stood up out of their own reflection, singing that low note they sing at dusk. I watched Corvelan listen to it without listening. That was the horror of truth-eaters, the thing no briefing ever captured: they weren't monsters. They were patient men doing paperwork with souls.

"I'll make you a trade," I said.

* * *

Here is what I know about bargaining with Corvelan Ashe: he lets you finish. That's the trap. Most men interrupt you halfway through your offer so they can improve it in their favor. Corvelan waits until you've said everything, including the things you didn't mean to say, and then he prices the whole bundle.

So I said it plainly. "You want the boy's route. I'll give you the approach to the Door Out — the safe path, the one that doesn't cross the erasure-tide. In exchange, the boy walks into Vess custody alive, and the others walk away. Priestess, garrison deserter, all of it. No archiving. No consumption. You take your prize and you let the rest dissolve with the country."

"Generous," said Corvelan. "And incomplete. You're keeping something."

"I'm keeping my professional reputation, which is the only thing left of it."

He laughed. He had a good laugh, that was always the worst part. "Show me the path."

I unrolled the chart on his table and gave him the truth — most of it. The causeway route. The tide-tables of the erasure, when the country ate its own roads. The gate-warden's post at the salt gate, and the hour the wardens changed. I watched him annotate it in that hand I knew, adding details I hadn't offered, correcting errors I hadn't made, and I understood with a lurch that he had known the shape of this route already. He'd wanted confirmation. He'd wanted *me*, specifically, to be the one who handed it over, because a disgraced cartographer selling back her own map is a story the Bureau can file neatly.

"Good," he said, when I finished. "Now the part you kept."

"There is no part I kept."

"Maren." He folded his hands. Behind him, in the tent's shadowed corner, one of his junior eaters sat very still with an open ledger, and I understood that the junior wasn't there for decor. "I taught you. I know what you look like when you're holding a card face-down. Your left thumb moves. It's moving now."

My left thumb, traitor, went still.

"The Door has a lock condition," I said, after a long moment. "That's all. It doesn't open for keys. It opens for belonging. Someone who stands on both sides of the erasure at once. It's in the archive fragments. I told you the route because the route is yours by right — I mapped it for the Survey. But the lock is theology, not geography, and I don't sell theology."

"Belonging to both sides," Corvelan repeated softly. He wrote it down anyway, because of course he did. "Then we don't need to capture the boy at all, do we. We need him *willing*. How interesting that the prophecy agrees with the architecture." He looked up, and for one naked second I saw not the mentor but the appetite underneath, vast and tidy. "Thank you for the theology, Maren. You may go."

"That wasn't the trade—"

"The trade was safe passage for your companions, contingent on your continued cooperation. Your cooperation continues. Therefore—" he gestured pleasantly at the tent flap "—they're safe. For now. Do give the priestess my regards. She fed my predecessor a memory once, back in the pacification. He never recovered. He kept trying to eat his own hands."

I got out of the tent before he could see what my face did at that, which was, I am fairly sure, nothing, which was itself the giveaway.

* * *

They let me walk out of camp unmolested, which was the most frightening part. Two sentries nodded at me. One of them, a girl too young for her helmet, asked if I'd found what I was looking for on the flats.

"No," I said honestly. "Nobody ever does."

I made it two miles down the darkened causeway before I stopped and put my hands flat against a salt-crusted parapet and admitted the shape of what I'd done.

Because here is the arithmetic, laid out clean the way I lay out any coastline: I traded the Door's approach to save three people. Fine. Good, even. Except the Door's approach leads to the Door, and the Door opens for a boy who belongs to both sides, and I had just told the chief truth-eater of the empire of Vess precisely what kind of person can open it. Not the lock itself — I'd held that back, the mechanism, the cost, the detail that matters more than all the rest combined. But I'd shown him the shape of the keyhole, and Corvelan Ashe has spent forty years fitting shapes to locks.

Worst of all — and this is the part I keep circling, the reef my thoughts wreck on every time — some cold cartographer's corner of me had been *relieved* to trade it. Because handing over the route felt like paying a debt, and paying debts feels like cleanliness, and I have wanted to feel clean since the day the Survey burned my commission and called my maps of Saltmark a delusion. They weren't a delusion. I had seen this country. I had loved this country, briefly, the way you love a place you're measuring. And then I had helped erase it, because the alternative was becoming a footnote myself, and I chose the ink over the page.

Every choice since has been a variation on that choice.

"Talking to the parapet again?" said Tam.

He was sitting in the lee of a fallen pillar with his knees up, garrison coat buttoned wrong, looking fourteen in a way he'd managed not to look for the entire journey until now. Gideon was asleep against the pillar, worn hollow from the day's walking. Ilsa stood at the edge of the causeway facing the water, holding her hands cupped in front of her like a woman carrying embers, which is more or less what she was.

"How long have you been awake?"

"Since you started lying to the sentry girl." He shrugged. "You lie differently when you mean it. It's faster."

"Remind me never to play cards with you."

"Gideon says the same thing." He studied me with those eyes the garrison hadn't finished training out of him yet. "You went into their camp alone and came out with no new bruises and no new prisoners. So either you charmed them, or you bought something."

"Both," I said. "We have passage. Sort of. As long as I stay cooperative."

"Cooperative meaning?"

"Meaning I sold them a road."

He was quiet for a while. Down in the drowned city, the towers sang their low note, and I saw him tilt his head to it the way he always does, half-listening, half-ashamed of listening.

"My mother sang," he said suddenly. "Before the border fire. Nobody recorded it, obviously — unrecorded people don't get songs, that's how it works — but she sang, and it sounded like that. Like the towers. Low and sad and like it would keep going whether anyone heard or not." He looked at me. "Is that why you came back? After they erased it? Because you'd heard it too?"

I thought about lying. I thought about how fast I lie when I mean it.

"Yes," I said.

* * *

Ilsa joined us at midnight, when the tide of erasure pulled back far enough to expose the old pilgrim stairs. She moved like weather — slow, total, arriving whether you invited her or not.

"You met him," she said. Not a question.

"Corvelan. Yes."

"He ate my sister's wedding." Her voice was level, the way stone is level. "Forty years ago, during the pacification. Not the memory of attending — the event itself, out of the world. The guests woke up married to strangers. My sister spent six years wondering why a man she'd never seen slept beside her, and then the erasure took her too, tidily, like closing a ledger." She turned her head. Her eyes caught the starlight and gave none of it back. "What did you feed him tonight, Maren Voss?"

"The road."

"What else?"

"A piece of the lock."

Gideon stirred in his sleep and said something in a language I didn't know and, I suspected, didn't consciously know either. Ilsa glanced at him, then back at me, and I braced for judgment — hers, or the fire's speaking through her, which amounts to the same thing with worse acoustics.

Instead she said, "Good."

"...Good?"

"You kept the worst of it. The cost. What opening the Door truly does." She crouched beside the sleeping boy and adjusted his blanket with hands that had fed forty years of memories to a shrinking flame. "Truth is not a debt you pay all at once. It is a ration. You gave the empire enough rope to feel clever and not enough to hang us. That is not betrayal. That is cartography — you drew them a map with the cliff edges left blank." She rose. "But hear me, Maren. Blank spaces on maps are where sailors die. One day soon you will have to show this family the cliffs. All of them. And they will be angrier at the blanks than the empire ever was."

She walked back toward the fire-shrine's light, leaving me with that, the way she leaves everything: placed precisely, weighted correctly, impossible to pick up again.

* * *

I did not sleep. Instead I did what I always do with unbearable truths: I drew them.

By lantern-light, on the back of the chart I'd copied for Corvelan, I sketched the Door Out as I remembered it from the Survey fragments — the twin pillars, the salt-fused seal, the hairline crack running through both sides like a scar down the middle of a face. And beneath it I drew the thing I'd withheld from Corvelan, in cipher, small, ugly: the lock's true condition. Not merely *belonging to both sides.*

*Opening it fully lets the erasure through.* Backward. Upstream. Into the valley beyond, into the world beyond the valley, into every quiet borrowed life that dead air was built to protect. The Door doesn't just carry Saltmark's name home. It carries the hunger home with it.

I looked at the cipher a long time. Then I did what a coward does with a map of her own guilt.

I folded it small and hid it inside the lining of my satchel — the ridiculous satchel, Corvelan had said, and he was right, it is ridiculous, it has survived three confiscations and one drowning and it will survive this too, because it is the only honest thing about me: it carries everything and shows nothing.

Toward dawn, Gideon woke, rubbed his face, and looked straight at me across the cold ashes of our little fire with those valley-flat eyes that missed nothing and commented on less.

"You were at the truth-eater camp last night," he said.

"I was negotiating our survival."

"With what currency?"

"Geography," I said. "It's the only coin I have."

He held my gaze one beat longer than comfort allows, and then, mercifully, he looked away — toward the east, toward the drowned archive rising out of the morning mist on the far shore, its towers crusted white, its doors standing open like mouths.

"There's a building over there," he said slowly. "I've never seen it before. But I know which shelf my answer is on."

I should have asked him what he meant. I have replayed that moment a hundred times since, and every time I ask myself why I didn't ask, and every time the answer is the same: because I was busy hiding a map from the people I'd just saved, and a woman juggling knives cannot also catch falling questions.

We crossed at first light. And in the drowned archive, on a shelf that should have been underwater for a century, Gideon Zhou found a manuscript in handwriting I recognized instantly — because I had spent four years, once, transcribing its owner's field notes for the Survey.

I knew that slanted, impatient, brilliant script the way you know a scar in a mirror.

It belonged to Odile.
