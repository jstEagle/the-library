## 9. Odile's Handwriting

Maren called it an archive. What I saw when we came over the last ridge of drowned rooftops was a cathedral that had lost an argument with the sea.

It lay on its side in a basin of grey water, half-sunk, its spire poking up like a finger raised in permanent objection. Salt had grown down its walls in thick white curtains, so that the whole building looked shrouded — a mourner at its own funeral. The doors, tall as trees, stood open at an angle that suggested even they had given up.

"Records of the old kingdom," Maren said, consulting her map with the reverence other people reserved for scripture. "Land grants, census rolls, ship registries. Everything Vess burned in the capital, they missed here, because here was already underwater when the erasure started."

"Convenient."

"Nothing about this country is convenient, Zhou. It is merely thorough." She folded the map. "The erasure eats memory first, then names, then paper. But paper remembers differently than people do. Slower. Stubborner."

"Like you."

"I was going to say like you," she said, and started down the slope toward the doors without waiting for me to appreciate the compliment.

Tam brought up the rear, lantern held high, walking the way he always walked now — like a boy listening to two sets of orders and obeying neither. Three days since the salt gate. Three days since he'd turned his own lantern against his own captain, and his commander had seen him do it. He didn't talk about it. He talked about everything else instead, constantly, as if silence were something the garrison had installed in him and he was slowly unscrewing it.

"Do archives drown all the way through?" he asked. "Or just the bottom shelves?"

"Just the bottom shelves," Maren said. "Which are the interesting ones."

"That's what my sergeant said about the punishment cells."

Nobody laughed. Tam laughed anyway, alone, which I was beginning to understand was his way of carrying things from one place to another.

* * *

Inside, the archive smelled of cold stone and old salt and something faintly sweet, like honey left too long in a jar. Water stood shin-deep across the floor, perfectly still, perfectly clear — not seawater but something older, the way the light in Saltmark was older. Our footsteps made rings that spread outward and did not fade so much as settle, like the water was keeping them.

Shelves rose out of the flood in ranks. Books floated in bundles, tied down with cord, bobbing gently at chest height as if the room were breathing around them. Some were swollen to twice their size, furred with mineral bloom. Others looked freshly bound, spines crisp, as though they'd been waiting rather than drowning.

"The erasure can't decide about this place," Maren murmured. She'd gone quiet the way she went quiet near anything true. "It eats the records of Saltmark everywhere else. Here there are too many. It chokes."

Ilsa drifted between the shelves with her hands open at her sides, palms up, the way she carried herself near any fire, even a drowned one. She touched nothing. She only listened. Once she stopped before a shelf of ledgers and stood there for a long moment, and when she moved on her face was wet, and I decided — generously, cowardly — that it was spray.

We were looking for shipping manifests. That was the plan: Maren wanted the registry of vessels that had left Saltmark's harbor in the last year before the erasure closed, because somewhere in those pages would be the record of a boat, a woman, a baby, a heading west toward a valley that officially contained nothing. Proof. A trail. Something to hold against the truth-eaters when they came, and they were coming; Tam's misdirected signal had bought us hours, not weeks.

What I found instead was on the top shelf, which should have been impossible, because the top shelf was dry.

The book sat in a niche behind a bust of some king whose face the salt had eaten away, leaving a smooth grey egg of stone. The book was slim, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed string, and it was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way a kettle is warm ten minutes after the fire — the warmth of something recently alive.

I should have left it. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this: I knew, the moment my fingers touched the oilcloth, that I should have left it where it sat. There's a particular feeling hedge-witches get, the ones who were taught properly before they were taught to be afraid — a pressure behind the sternum, like a hand pressing flat against your ribs from the inside. Odile called it the knock. She said magic doesn't call out to you; it knocks, politely, once, and then it waits to see whether you're fool enough to answer.

The knock came. I answered. Story of my life, apparently, including the parts I hadn't lived yet.

I untied the string.

* * *

The manuscript inside was not the prophecy book. The prophecy book — the tattered thing that had washed up in my millrace with tonight's date printed beside my own face — was typeset. Printed. Mass-produced by someone, somewhere, for reasons I hadn't yet let myself think about.

This was handwritten. This was the original. And I knew the hand before I knew what it was saying, the way you know a voice through a wall before you make out the words.

Odile wrote the way she did everything else: fast, slanted, impatient with margins, letters that started confident and finished in a hurry because the next thought had already arrived. I'd copied out her recipes for years — feverfew tinctures, salt wards, the little charms she pretended were folk remedies and weren't. I knew the way she crossed her *t*s like small slashes, the way her *d*s leaned backward as if retreating.

This page leaned backward.

*The boy will be safe in the dead valley,* it said. *Dead air is the best air for hiding. Nothing follows him there. Nothing finds him. I have spent nine years building the quiet, and I will spend the rest of my life maintaining it.*

Nine years. The valley had been dead for ninety.

I turned the page with hands that did not feel like mine.

*The empire's truth-eaters hunt by appetite. They cannot eat what has no taste. So I have made the child tasteless — scrubbed him, ward by ward, year by year, until no divination can find a seam in him. He believes himself ordinary. Good. Ordinary is armor.*

My mouth had gone dry, which was a stupid thing for a mouth to do inside a flooded building, but bodies are loyal to their habits, not their circumstances.

*He will hate me for it. Let him. Hatred keeps a boy moving, and moving boys survive. Love would keep him looking back, and the erasure devours whatever lingers.*

There were diagrams. Ward-lattices drawn in her cramped sure hand, covering pages entire — and I recognized them. Not their purpose; their shape. The valley's boundary, the ring of dead air I'd lived inside for six years, was laid out on the page in ink, annotated, dated, *signed*. It wasn't a natural dead zone. It wasn't an accident of geology or a curse from some forgotten war.

It was a construction. An enclosure. She had built it the way you build a pen, or a vault, or a box you intend to sit on for a century.

And beneath the diagrams, smaller, pressed hard into the paper so the letters had nearly torn through:

*When the fire gutters — and it will gutter; I have seen the arithmetic — the vault must open on schedule. He must be drawn back. The prophecy will do the drawing. I have written it so that he cannot refuse: his date, his face, his vanity, his guilt. Forgive me the manipulation. I have never been able to make him do anything by asking.*

I sat down in the floodwater. It soaked through my trousers and I didn't care. Somewhere behind me, Tam was asking Maren something about cataloguing systems, and Maren was answering in her cartographer's voice, and both of them sounded very far away, like voices heard through the wall of a house I used to live in.

She hadn't predicted this. Prediction implies a future you watch arrive. Odile had *scheduled* it. The prophecy book with my face in it — the thing that had dragged me out of the only safety I had — was bait she had written a hundred years ago and cast, patiently, into time itself. The valley was a trap built around me, except the trap was lined with soft things: a cottage, a millrace, a quiet where nothing followed. Six years of peace, engineered. My flight from her, engineered — had she made the valley unbearable in just the right ways? Had she counted on me running, and run where?

Was I hidden, or was I stored?

The question arrived whole, cold, and stayed. Because hidden implies someone who loves you wants nothing from you. Stored implies a purpose, a shelf, a moment of retrieval. And the manuscript kept going, page after page, in handwriting that got more hurried as it approached its end, as if she could hear her century running out:

*The fire-keepers' line ended at the harbor. All but one. I carried the last of them out myself, through the Door, before Vess sealed it — a squalling infant with his mother's stubborn chin and no idea yet what he'd cost everyone. His name cannot be written here. Names are how the truth-eaters find things. But if you are reading this, boy, you already know your name. You simply don't know yet what it opens.*

*I am sorry. I am not sorry. Both are true and I refuse to choose, which you will recognize as the story of your entire education.*

That was so exactly her voice — that dry, deflecting, infuriating tenderness — that I laughed. One bark of it, ugly, echoing off the drowned shelves. Tam stopped talking mid-sentence. I waved him off without turning around.

The last page was different. Older ink, or newer — it was hard to tell in that light. Smaller writing, cramped against the bottom margin like it had been added after everything else, squeezed in the way you write a postscript when the letter is already sealed:

*One correction, made late. The Door does not lead out. I told them it led out because frightened people guard exits, and hopeful people guard entrances. It leads back. It has always led back. When he opens it — and he will; I have written him incapable of anything else — tell him I did love him. Tell him it was never a lie, only a strategy.*

*— O.*

I read it four times. The water rings from my sitting spread out and settled and spread again, and the floating books bobbed around me like patient animals, and the sweet cold smell of the archive filled my lungs, and I understood, finally, the shape of the thing that had been done to me.

Every choice I'd ever made that felt like mine — fleeing her, crossing into the valley, mending charms that couldn't work, staying, staying, staying — every one of them fit inside her handwriting like a bone fits inside a hand. I had never escaped Odile. I had followed her instructions with perfect obedience while believing, the whole time, that obedience was the one thing I'd refused her.

And the worst part — the part I'll deny under questioning — was that the manuscript was kind. Cruelly, carefully kind. She had known I'd find this. She had known I'd need the apology and hate needing it. She had left me a letter disguised as evidence, and I couldn't even be angry cleanly, because anger requires believing you weren't loved, and the letter wouldn't permit that.

"Gideon." Maren's voice, close now. "You've been sitting in the water for a quarter hour. What did you find?"

I held up the manuscript without turning around. She took it. I heard her breath change as she recognized the hand — she'd seen the prophecy book often enough these past days, and a cartographer knows handwriting the way priests know faces.

"This is a hundred years old," she said. "The paper, the ink, the binding — this is period. I've handled documents from this exact year in imperial collections. This isn't possible."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

"She wrote the prophecy."

"She wrote everything." I finally stood. My knees crackled. The water gave back its rings, one after another, keeping count. "The valley. The erasure's timing, maybe. Me. Maren — the Door. Does the phrase 'it leads back' mean anything to you?"

Her face did something complicated and quick, the way a lock does when the wrong key turns anyway. "Should it?"

"You tell me. You're the one who's known where we were going this whole time."

Tam had come up beside us, lantern raised, and Ilsa stood a little apart with her palms still open, and I realized all three of them were looking at me with the same expression — the look people give a door they've suddenly noticed is load-bearing.

"The fire-keepers," Ilsa said quietly. Her voice, when she chose to use it, had the weight of something carried a long way. "The last of that line was an infant, smuggled through the Door. The fire has shown me that much, and I never understood why it showed me a baby." Her eyes, faded as sea glass, found mine. "You know your name, boy. You've known it all along. You simply haven't said it aloud in this country yet."

I opened my mouth to say it — my name, plain, the only name I'd ever had —

And the water in the archive moved.

Not rippled. Moved — all of it at once, every flooded aisle draining backward toward the walls as if the building had inhaled. The floating books sank. The sweet cold smell sharpened into something burnt. And along the far wall, where the shadow between the shelves was deepest, the dark gathered itself the way a person gathers a coat around their shoulders, and stepped forward into Ilsa's fading lantern-light.

She looked exactly as I'd left her. Grey braid, ink-stained fingers, eyes that apologized and refused to, simultaneously. She looked exactly as I'd left her, and the manuscript in my hands said she was a hundred years older than that, and both things were true, and I refused, for one more breath, to choose.

"Hello, Gideon," said Odile. "You read faster than I expected. We have very little time, and I imagine you have a great many questions."

She smiled, tired and terrible and familiar.

"Ask me none of them until we're moving. The tide of erasure comes through this archive every night, and tonight, boy — tonight it's early."
