## 10. The Emberhold Dimming

I have kept the fire alive for forty years, and in all that time it has never once said thank you.

I want that noted somewhere, by someone, before I forget everything else: forty years of feeding a god-shaped flame with my own past, and not one courtesy. Not one small flicker of gratitude. Priests of other countries get incense and hymns and holidays named after them. I got a drafty shrine at the bottom of a drowned city and a fire that eats memories like a tax collector eats a budget.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and ahead of myself is a dangerous place to be these days, because "ahead" mostly means things I no longer remember well enough to dread properly.

* * *

The morning — if you can call it morning, under a sky the color of old dishwater — began with a stumble.

Not mine. The fire's.

You learn to hear it, after four decades. Dragon-fire doesn't burn like ordinary flame. Ordinary flame crackles; dragon-fire *hums*, low and constant, like a choir holding one note for a hundred years because nobody told them they were allowed to stop. When I was young — when I still had a *young* to refer to — I thought the hum was beautiful. Now I know it's the sound of ten thousand souls refusing to dissolve, and beauty looks different when you know what's paying for it.

That morning, the hum broke. Skipped. Like a heart tripping on a stair.

I was halfway up the shrine stairs with the morning's offering — two memories, minor ones, the taste of a plum from a market forty years gone, and the name of a street in the Weft district I'd walked as a girl — and I felt it go out through my feet before my ears caught up. The whole Emberhold shuddered. Salt dust sifted down from the vaulted ceiling. And out across the city came a silence so complete I could hear the sea chewing on the harbor walls three miles off.

When dragon-fire stumbles, souls go quiet.

I took the stairs two at a time, which at my age is less an act of devotion than an act of spite, and threw open the shrine doors.

The fire had guttered down to something the size of a cooking pot. It should stand the height of a man. It should fill the great bronze basin like poured light, humming its stubborn hundred-year note, and instead it crouched in the middle like a beggar ashamed of being seen. Its color was wrong, too — gold going gray at the edges, the way hair goes when a person has been frightened too long without relief.

"None of that," I told it. "None of that, now."

It flickered at me. Insolent thing. Even starving, it managed insolence, which I suppose is why we understood each other.

* * *

Here is what the empire of Vess never understood about dragon-fire, and what they were too proud to ask: it is not fuel. You cannot feed it wood or coal or oil, though the early keepers tried, and the scorch marks on the Emberhold's western wall are their monument. Dragon-fire burns memory. Every recollection fed into it becomes heat, becomes light, becomes the low humming note that holds Saltmark's remaining souls together against the erasure-tide.

A memory of a taste buys a few hours. A face buys days. A name — a true name, loved hard and long — buys a week, sometimes more, if the love was the kind that leaves scars.

And when it burns your memory, it takes the *why*. That's the part nobody warns you about. You don't just lose the plum; you lose knowing why plums mattered to you. You're left standing in your own life like a woman reading someone else's letters, recognizing the handwriting but not the grief.

Forty years of this. Do the arithmetic. I haven't tasted food in thirty years — I feed the fire every meal I would have eaten, because hunger was the cheapest thing I owned. I don't remember my mother's face, only that she had one and I resented her for dying. I don't remember my wedding day, if I had one; there's a gap where that should be, smooth as river stone, and sometimes I catch myself touching my bare ring finger and feeling like a liar.

I keep a ledger. Of course I keep a ledger. A priestess who forgets on purpose had better write things down, or she wakes one morning with nothing left but duty and no idea whose duty it is.

The ledger sits chained to my belt. In it, in my own hand, in ink I mix myself from salt and squid-black because proper ink stopped existing when Vess unmade the country that made it:

*Ilsa Vane, fifty-ninth keeper of the Emberhold. Born in Saltmark. Daughter of —*

And there the entry stops, because I burned my parents' names in the famine year of the fire, year eleven of my keeping, and I wrote the ledger line before I did it and never had the heart to cross it out. There's just a blank space where my family tree should branch, and every time I look at it I think: *well, that's honest, at least.*

* * *

By midday the district of Sallow Row went quiet.

I knew before anyone brought me word, because the quiet arrived like weather. Sallow Row held perhaps six hundred souls — fishers, ropemakers, a chandler I'd drunk with back when drinking was still something I remembered how to miss. They lived in the fire's glow the way plants live in sun. When the fire dimmed, they dimmed. And when it stumbled —

They didn't die. Dying would be simpler. They *went soft*, edges first, like figures in wet chalk. The erasure-tide doesn't kill; it subtracts. It takes the country out from under people until there isn't enough of them left to stand on.

A boy came running up through the salt-crusted streets to tell me — Tam, the garrison boy, the one who'd been letting his boots carry him toward the Emberhold more often than toward his barracks lately, which interested me greatly and worried me more. He skidded into the shrine, breathless, salt dust flying.

"Priestess. Priestess, Sallow Row — half the street's gone *thin*, you can see through them, the chandler's wife walked into a wall and kept walking—"

"I know."

"You *know?* Can't you—" He gestured wildly at the shrunken fire, all fourteen years of him certain that faith was a lever and priests stood at the handle. "Can't you make it burn?"

"I can feed it." I held up the ledger. "Question is what's left to feed it with."

He looked at the ledger the way boys look at anything a grown woman chains to herself — with the particular horror of the young confronting evidence that life takes payments. Then he looked at the fire, and his face did something complicated. These garrison children are taught that Saltmark never existed, that the singing towers are wind, that the ghosts are propaganda. But Tam hears the towers. I've watched him hearing them. And standing in front of a starving god, he forgot to perform his disbelief.

"What happens," he asked quietly, "if it goes out?"

"Everything goes quiet. Everywhere. All at once." I smiled, because it frightens children less when you smile, and also because wryness is the last luxury they'll pry from me. "So we don't let it."

* * *

The inventory was ugly.

Every priestess keeps a private ledger inside the public one — the list of what remains, ranked by cost. Mine read, by that evening:

*Remaining stores:*
*— The smell of rain on hot stone. (Cheap. Barely a mouthful. Buys hours.)*
*— My teacher Brannoc's laugh, which was like a door falling over. (Moderate. Days.)*
*— The reason I became a priestess. (Expensive. Unknown quantity. Possibly load-bearing.)*
*— Her name.*

Her name. My daughter's.

I won't tell it to you here, and not out of drama — out of arithmetic. If I write it on the page, some future Ilsa will read it back and think it belongs to a stranger, and that stranger will be me, and the name will have died anyway, just slower. Some things should live only where the forgetting can't reach them. So it lives in the ledger as *her name*, and in my chest as the single warm stone left in a cold house.

I bore her late, in the first years of the erasure, when the towers were already singing funeral hymns to themselves. She lived nine days. Nine days is nothing, and nine days is everything; the fire has eaten the details and left me the shape of the loss, like a footprint in ash. I know that I loved her with the whole stupid violence of a mother's heart. I know that I named her myself, against custom, because her father — whoever the fire tells me he was, some weeks I almost recall — never drew breath to do it. And I know that her name is the last true thing I own.

Everything else I've already spent. The fire has eaten my childhood street by street, my loves one by one, my griefs, my joys, my grudges (I mourn those least), and forty years of mornings. What's left is duty, wryness, and her name.

Duty and wryness are renewable. Her name is not.

* * *

The fire spoke to me that night.

It doesn't usually. It shows things — pictures in the flame, glimpses, prophecy if you're generous and weather-reading if you're honest. Words are rare. Words cost it strength it can't spare, which is why I pay attention when it spends them.

It showed me the boy first. Gideon — Maren's stray, the prophecy-child pulled through from the dead valley, all sharp cheekbones and flinches, looking at our drowned capital like a man reading a letter addressed to someone else. The flame curled around his image with something I can only call appetite, and I don't mean the eating kind. Recognition. The way a lock recognizes a key it's waited a century for.

Then it showed me the Door Out, far off in the salt hills, sealed and sleeping. Then darkness. Then — and this is the part I've turned over forty times since — it showed me *me*, standing aside, empty-handed, while others carried the fire forward.

"It wants me spent," I said aloud to the empty shrine. "That's the plan, is it? I'm the last log."

The flame guttered. Which could have meant yes, or could have meant nothing, or could have meant *stop asking questions you'll hate the answers to.* Forty years, and it has never once been articulate when it counted. Gods are like generals that way.

But here's the thing about being nearly empty: you get clear. Grief makes room. I sat with my ledger open to the last page and understood, with the terrible calm of a woman who has already sold everything else, exactly what the night required.

Sallow Row was thinning. Six hundred souls, unraveling into the tide, and the only currency that would buy them back a week — long enough for the prophecy-boy to find whatever he's supposed to find, long enough for Maren Voss to stop lying to everyone including herself, long enough for *something* — was the one thing I'd sworn to keep.

The mission or the mourning. That's the whole priesthood, boiled down. Nobody puts it on the recruitment poster.

* * *

I knelt at the basin. The fire raised itself as best it could, the size of a child now, gray-gold, trembling. Starving things always manage hope; it's the cruelest thing about them.

"I want it noted," I told it, "by someone, somewhere, that her name was beautiful. That it fit her. That when I said it, even to an empty room, the room was less empty. Whatever else gets forgotten — note that."

The fire flickered. Almost gently. Almost like a bow.

I put my hands into the light and gave it up.

I won't describe the mechanics. It doesn't hurt, exactly; it's more like being unstitched, thread by thread, from a garment you didn't know you were wearing. The name rose in me — warm, whole, hers — and I fed it out through my palms, and I heard it once, echoing in the flame, sung back in a voice like a bell dropped down a well, and then—

Then nothing, where the center of me used to be.

I knelt there a long while. The fire climbed. Up out of its begging-bowl crouch, up past the height of a man, gold flooding back into the gray, the hum rising note by note until the whole Emberhold rang with it and out across the drowned city the thinning souls of Sallow Row thickened, steadied, stood. I felt it happen the way you feel dawn through a window. Secondhand. Borrowed.

Somewhere below, six hundred people went on existing, and not one of them will ever know the price, and I am glad. Gratitude would just be another memory for the fire to eat.

* * *

The ledger. I made myself do the ledger. A priestess who forgets on purpose writes things down.

*Ilsa Vane, fifty-ninth keeper of the Emberhold. On this night gave the fire her final store: her daughter's name. Reason: six hundred souls in Sallow Row. Regret: none that she can locate.*

*Note to the sixtieth keeper, if there is one: there was a daughter. She lived nine days. She was loved beyond all measure and proportion. I cannot tell you her name. I am sorry. I am sorrier than I can prove.*

My hand shook writing that last part, and I couldn't have told you why, and that — that right there — is the cost they never put in the histories. Not the losing. The *not knowing what you lost.* I sat in my shrine surrounded by warmth I had purchased at the price of ever again understanding why warmth mattered, and I found I was crying, and I had to check the ledger to learn what for.

Wry to the end, though. Let the record show.

Near midnight, footsteps on the shrine stair — quick ones, young ones, and a second set, heavier, deliberate, with the drag of someone carrying maps and secrets in equal weight. Tam burst in first, eyes wild, and behind him came the cartographer Maren Voss with the prophecy-boy at her shoulder, salt-dusted, hollow-eyed, staring at the tall-burning fire like it had called him by name.

Which, I suspect, it had.

"Priestess," Maren began, in her careful exiled-cartographer voice, "we need to talk about the Door Out—"

I got to my feet. My knees complained. Somewhere in the blank space where her name used to live, something leaned toward the boy, the way a compass leans north, and I thought: *ah. So that's what I saved the week for.*

"Yes," I said. "We very much do."
