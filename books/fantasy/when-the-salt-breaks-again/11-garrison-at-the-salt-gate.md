## 11. Garrison at the Salt Gate

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my head because paper gets you hanged here — that I did not plan any of this.

Plans are for people whose lives still fit inside them. Mine had been leaking out through the cracks ever since the night I heard the salt towers singing and didn't report it. Every good Vessian soldier knows what to do with a crack: fill it, seal it, march over it. I'd been standing on mine for three weeks, listening to it sing.

Now here's how a boy with a lantern-signal wand ends up committing treason before breakfast.

* * *

The Salt Gate is called that because everything near it is salt. Not sea-salt, not the honest kind you shake onto potatoes — the other kind, the kind the erasure leaves behind when it finishes chewing a district. The buildings around the gate were once something. You can see their bones under the crust: an archway that was probably a temple, a wall that might have been a market, windows like closed eyes. The salt grows on them the way frost grows, slow and patient, and if you touch it too long your fingers go numb in a way that doesn't come back.

Captain Dreyle liked to hold inspections there. "Good acoustics," he said, which was a lie, because nothing echoes at the Salt Gate. The salt eats sound the way it eats everything else. He held them there anyway because it made the recruits jumpy, and jumpy recruits salute faster.

So when the order came down the garrison line — intruders sighted moving toward the third gate, all available soldiers — I knew exactly which stretch of crusted nothing we'd be marching to. And I knew, with the cold certainty of a boy who has spent fourteen years learning exactly one trade, who those intruders would be.

The hedge-witch with the tired eyes. The disgraced mapmaker. The priestess who walked like she was carrying something breakable inside her chest, which she was — the last fire in the country, more or less, though officially that sentence doesn't parse. And me, walking beside them since the night I didn't arrest Gideon Zhou, carrying my kit and my guilt and a growing suspicion that I'd never once in my life been on the right side of anything.

We'd stopped at the gate because Maren said we had to. "The Door approach runs through it," she said, unrolling a scrap of map so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. "There's no other way."

"There's always another way," Gideon said.

"Not in a country that's being erased," Maren said. "The roads behind us are already gone. You want another way, invent a country with more doors."

I loved her a little for that, the way you love anyone who says true bitter things like they're weather reports.

* * *

The truth-eaters came out of the salt fog the way truth-eaters always do: politely.

That's the worst part, if you're asking. Everyone expects monsters to come roaring. These came walking, unhurried, in grey coats buttoned to the throat, four of them, and the tall one in front smiled at us like a tax assessor. His face was pleasant the way a locked room is pleasant. Behind him the other three spread out without hurrying, cutting the angles, and I noticed — because I'm a soldier even when I'm trying not to be — that they'd positioned themselves between us and the garrison road. Not between us and escape.

Between us and witnesses.

"Good morning," the tall one said. "I'm Eater-Captain Sorrel. You'll forgive the hour." It wasn't early. He just wanted us to feel like we'd been caught doing something shameful before dawn, the way guilty people do.

Nobody answered. Ilsa stood very still with her hands folded, the way she does when the fire in her chest is talking to her. Maren had gone the color of old paper. Gideon — Gideon looked at Sorrel the way you look at a dog you've been told will bite, checking its teeth without meaning to.

"You're wondering why four inquisitors are this far from the archive ships," Sorrel went on pleasantly. "You're wondering what could possibly be worth the Empire's attention in a place that isn't anywhere." He tilted his head. "Don't worry. Wondering is harmless. It's *knowing* that gets eaten."

And then, because the world has a sense of humor and it's cruel, the horns sounded.

Garrison horns. Our horns. Captain Dreyle's company coming up the salt road at double time, boots crunching on crust, maybe sixty men with matchlocks and the particular blankness of soldiers who have been told today's enemy is nobody and are trying hard not to think about it.

Sorrel didn't even turn around. "Ah," he said. "Your captain. How convenient. We won't need to file the paperwork."

That's when I understood the shape of it. They hadn't cornered us despite the garrison. They'd waited *for* the garrison. Truth-eaters don't carry guns; they don't need to. They eat knowing, and a squad of soldiers who *see* a thing is a meal laid out on plates. Sixty men would watch us die or burn or simply stop, and then Sorrel would eat the watching out of them, and by supper the whole company would remember only that they'd inspected the Salt Gate and found nothing, again, same as always.

Sixty witnesses, pre-arranged. That's what we were standing in the middle of.

* * *

Dreyle came up the road with his chin forward, the way he does, and I fell into line beside the other boys because my body knows how to be a soldier even when the rest of me has resigned. Salute. Report. Stand ready. The liturgy of Vess, and I've known it longer than I've known my own name — which, given what happened to my family's name in some border fire the records say never happened, is saying something.

"Captain Dreyle." Sorrel inclined his head with the exact degree of respect a man gives someone he considers furniture. "You have your orders?"

"I have the general order, sir. Intruders at the third gate. Fire on command." Dreyle's voice did the thing it always does, flat as a ledger.

"And your men know what they're firing at?"

"They know their orders, sir."

"Orders are better than knowledge," Sorrel said warmly. "Knowledge can be wrong. Orders can't." He turned to us then, and his smile got wider by one tooth. "Although these four seem full of knowledge. Perhaps after — we'll sort out what stays and what goes."

Maren stepped half in front of Ilsa. Old habit from her, I'd learned — she put herself between danger and the priestess the way she'd once drawn lines on maps, precise and doomed.

"Whatever you think you're doing," Maren said, "the Door approach runs through this gate, and you can't eat a door."

"We don't need to eat the door," Sorrel said. "We need to eat the people who remember where it is. The door will forget itself soon enough. Everything here does. That's rather the point of the Empire."

I should tell you what happens inside a soldier's head at a moment like this, because nobody ever does. It's not glory. It's not even fear, not exactly. It's arithmetic. Sixty of us, four of them, four fugitives. My orders say fire. My eyes say the fugitives are a tired witch, a woman with a satchel of maps, an old priestess, and — me, technically, if anyone bothered counting, which no one had, because Tam of the border fire doesn't exist in any ledger worth consulting.

My orders say fire. And the person I'd most likely hit if I fired was myself, and somehow that was the funniest thought I'd ever had, and I had to bite the inside of my cheek not to laugh, because if I laughed I'd never stop.

"Garrison!" Dreyle raised his hand. "Ready!"

Sixty matchlocks came up. The sound of sixty safeties clicking off in the salt silence was very small and very enormous, like the whole sky clearing its throat.

Gideon looked at me across the space between us. He didn't mouth anything. He didn't beg. He just looked, tired and sorry and certain, like he'd already forgiven whatever I was about to do — whichever thing I was about to do — and that was worse than shouting. That was the worst kindness anyone had ever done me.

Because here's the arithmetic I hadn't done yet, the sum that changed everything:

My family died in an unrecorded fire. Unrecorded. I'd swallowed that word for six years, chewed it, told myself the clerks made mistakes, told myself grief makes things feel erased that aren't. But I'd walked through Saltmark now. I'd seen districts go quiet while we slept. I'd heard the towers singing names. And I knew — with the certainty I used to reserve for drill manuals — that somewhere in Vess's tidy archives there is a page about the fire that took my family, and on that page, in some clerk's careful hand, is the word *erasure*, and the reason I never found the page is that pages like that get eaten too.

They ate my family twice. Once in fire, once in paperwork.

Sorrel was going to do it a third time, to everyone standing on this salt, and call it mercy.

"FIRE ON MY COMMAND—" Dreyle began.

And my hands moved.

* * *

Every garrison boy carries a signal-lantern. It's drilled into us before we're allowed a weapon: colored shutters, code-flashes, the grammar of light. Green for advance. Red for hold. Blue for false alarm, stand down, nothing here, move along.

Blue means *nothing here*.

My hands knew the sequence the way they knew prayer, which is to say completely and without my heart's involvement. Shutter open, shutter closed, shutter open — blue, blue, blue — the stand-down flash, the all-clear, the oldest lie in the signal book. I aimed it past Dreyle's shoulder toward the rear companies, the ones too far back to see the gate clearly, the ones hanging on every flicker of light the way soldiers do when the fog is thick and the officers are quiet.

*Nothing here. False alarm. Move along.*

It took four seconds. I counted them. In those four seconds I was a soldier of the Vessian Empire, and then the shutter clicked shut on the fourth flash and I wasn't anymore, and the strange thing — the thing I'll tell my own recruits someday, if I live long enough to have recruits, if I live long enough to be allowed the word *someday* — the strange thing is how little it weighed. Treason. Four seconds of it. Lighter than my kit.

"Stand down!" bellowed Sergeant Halloway at the rear, because Halloway trusted lantern-code over his own mother, and sixty matchlocks wavered, and the wavering spread like water finding a level. "Signal says stand down! Captain — signal says—"

"I can *read*, Sergeant!" Dreyle roared. But he turned, because a commander has to, because discipline is a chain and he's a link in it same as me, and the four seconds stretched to eight, and in those eight seconds Maren moved — gods, she moved fast for a woman who complained about her knees — hauling Ilsa and Gideon back through the archway into the salt-crusted colonnade where matchlock lines couldn't form up.

Sorrel watched it happen with his head cocked, pleasant as Sunday. Then his eyes came to me.

Not to Dreyle. To me. Small, unimportant, ledger-absent me. And I saw him *taste* the air, the way they do, reading the knowing off a person like heat off a stove. I felt it like a finger drawn across the back of my neck.

"There," Sorrel said softly, almost fondly. "There's our leaky little bucket. Captain Dreyle. One of your soldiers just committed high treason with a lantern."

Dreyle turned all the way around then. Slowly. The way heavy things turn.

He looked at me. At my lantern. At my face, which I could not arrange into innocence fast enough, because apparently I'd spent my whole allotment of composure on the shutter-work. And Dreyle — flat-voiced, ledger-hearted, order-loving Dreyle, who once made a boy scrub the barracks floor with a toothbrush for humming a Saltmark lullaby — Dreyle looked at me for a long, terrible moment, and I swear on the ash of everything that his eyes went to the colonnade. To the fleeing shapes. And then back to me.

Something crossed his face that I had no word for then and have only barely found since. It wasn't sympathy. Sympathy would have been simpler. It was closer to a man recognizing, in a subordinate's face, an expression he'd worn himself once, long ago, in a mirror, and buried so deep even the truth-eaters couldn't find it.

"Treason," Dreyle said at last, loudly, for the record. "Corporal-level insubordination with signal equipment." His voice stayed flat as a ledger page. His hand did not reach for his sidearm. "You'll be dealt with, boy. After."

"After what, Captain?" Sorrel asked, silk over stone.

"After I determine whether my signal-man acted alone," Dreyle said, "or was misled." Which was nonsense, and we both knew it was nonsense, and it bought me thirty seconds I hadn't earned and couldn't account for. Maybe he wanted the traitor for himself. Maybe — and this is the thought I keep turning over like a coin that might be counterfeit — maybe there are more mirrors in the garrison than I knew.

"Captain," Sorrel said, and his warmth dropped away entirely, and underneath it his voice was the sound a glacier makes, "my warrant supersedes your rank. The boy comes with us. What he knows comes out of him. Standard procedure."

"He's garrison property," Dreyle said. "Standard procedure applies to my prisoners, not yours."

"Then take him into custody yourself," Sorrel said pleasantly, "and hand him over within the hour, or I'll eat the knowing of it out of your entire command and let you explain the gap to your wife."

Silence. Salt silence, total, the kind where you can hear your own blood.

And that's when the towers started singing.

All of them. Every salt-crusted spire around the gate at once, low and vast, a chord with no instrument behind it, the song I'd heard once at midnight and never reported — except now it wasn't faint, now it filled the gate like a tide coming in, and every soldier in the line heard it, sixty pairs of ears, no eating required, no forgetting possible, and I watched Sorrel's pleasant face crack down the middle like river ice.

"What," he said quietly, "is *that*."

I didn't answer. Nobody answered. Because down the colonnade, where Maren had pulled the others, Gideon Zhou had stopped running — I could see him between the pillars, backlit by something that wasn't sunlight — and he was holding out his hands, and the salt on the ancient walls was glowing, faint gold, like embers remembering being fire.

The towers sang louder.

Dreyle looked at the glow. Looked at Sorrel. Looked at me — one last time, longest of all.

"Lantern," he said to me, holding out his hand, palm up.

I gave it to him. My hands were steady. I don't know how.

Dreyle turned the lantern over, considering it, and then he flashed — once, crisp, textbook — a signal I have never seen in any manual. Three reds and a green. A sequence with no official meaning, sent toward no receiver, aimed at nobody, unless you count the towers, unless you count whatever else in this drowned country was watching and waiting for exactly that.

The towers answered. The chord shifted, brightened, and every matchlock in the line hummed against its owner's grip like a struck bell.

"Oh," said Sorrel, stepping backward for the first time, "this is *deeply* irregular—"

"Company!" Dreyle's voice cracked across the gate like a whip, and it was not the voice of a man suppressing treason, it was the voice of a man who has decided which side of the ledger he's standing on and intends to write it down in ink. "Fix bayonets. Not at the colonnade." He turned, slow and deliberate, until he was facing the truth-eaters, sixty soldiers turning with him like a key in a lock. "At the gentlemen in grey."

The towers sang. The salt glowed. Somewhere beyond the archway, Gideon Zhou — hedge-witch, prophecy-boy, the most wanted nobody in a country that officially wasn't — was learning what his hands could do, and the whole gate was learning it with him.

I stood in the ranks of the army I'd betrayed, next to the captain who'd covered for me, holding empty hands, grinning like an idiot at four inquisitors, and understood that there was no version of the world after this morning in which I was ever a soldier of Vess again.

There was only the question of what I was instead.

Behind the colonnade, the singing changed key — and Maren's voice rang out, ragged and urgent, carrying over all of it:

"The Door! It's opening from the other side — Gideon, *whatever you're doing, keep doing it!*"
