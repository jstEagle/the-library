## 12. What Gideon Was Never Taught

The erasure-tide came in at dusk.

I'd been told about it by Maren in the flat tone she used for things that should have terrified me: *the country eats itself on a schedule, like a tide table nobody wrote down.* Twice a day the nothing rolled through Saltmark's lower streets and took a little more — a stairway here, a memory of a stairway there, until even the salt forgot what shape it had been. You learned to read it coming. The air went thin. The towers stopped singing. And everything you were standing near suddenly felt like it was being held up out of politeness rather than physics.

We had climbed to the high quarter of the drowned capital to wait it out — Maren, Tam, Ilsa, and I — perched in what had once been somebody's very good house, if the surviving fireplace was anything to go by. It was carved with fish. Somebody had loved this room once. That seemed worth noting before the tide got to it.

That was when I saw her across the water.

She stood on a rooftop in the flooded district, where no one could stand, on a roof that had mostly already been erased, and the erasure-tide broke around her ankles without touching her, the way water breaks around a post. She was small. She had always been small, a woman made of elbows and disapproval, who could mend a broken charm and break your heart about your technique in the same breath.

Except her hair was white now. Bone-white, ash-white, valley-of-Ash white. And her face —

Her face hadn't changed at all.

"That's not possible," I said, which is what I say instead of praying.

Maren followed my gaze and went very still, the way she did around maps of places that shouldn't exist. "Who is that?"

"My mentor." My mouth had gone dry. "Odile."

"The witch." Tam said it carefully. He still wasn't sure how to hold the fact that I'd spent ten years hiding from an old woman, but he'd seen enough of my flinching to respect it.

"She's dead," I said. "Or she's ninety-something. Or — " I gestured helplessly at the impossible woman standing on the impossible roof. "*That* isn't either of those things."

Ilsa looked at Odile for a long moment with her half-empty eyes, and said, softly, the way she said everything now, like each word cost her something: "The fire knows her name. It's very old in her. Older than the fire's oldest log." She frowned faintly, as if listening to something. "It says: *the keeper of the vault.*"

"Of the what?"

But Odile raised one hand — the same impatient beckon she'd used when I'd tracked mud into her workshop at fourteen — and stepped off the roof onto the surface of the erasure-tide as if it were cobbles, and began walking toward us across the nothing.

* * *

I want to be honest about what I did next, because this whole story has been people lying to themselves for dramatic effect, and I refuse to be left out.

I ran down to meet her.

Not because I forgave her. Not because some prophecy page told me to. Because I was twenty-six years old and I had spent a decade telling myself that leaving home was a decision I'd made, freely, like an adult, and here was the woman I'd fled from walking across the end of the world toward me, and every part of me that was ever a boy in her kitchen stood up and shouted *she came, she finally came.*

Then I reached the edge of the tide and remembered myself, and stopped, arms crossed like a barricade.

"Ten years," I said. "You couldn't write?"

"I wrote a book," Odile said.

"You wrote ONE BOOK. A century ago!"

"Yes," she said. "Time management has never been my strength." She stopped three paces away, close enough that I could see the details that were wrong — the white hair untouched by any wind, the eyes that held the same flat gray as the Valley of Ash itself, the way the erasure slid off her like rain off wax. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and the particular dust of a workshop nobody had swept since I left. "Hello, Gideon."

"Don't 'hello' me." My voice cracked on the second word, which undermined the whole performance. "Do you know what I've been through? Do you have any idea? There are *truth-eaters,* Odile. There's a fire that eats memories, and a priestess who gave up her daughter's NAME to keep it lit, and a boy soldier who betrayed his entire garrison for us, and I found your handwriting in a drowned archive dated a HUNDRED YEARS AGO, and you're going to stand there and say hello—"

"Yes," she said again, gently, and it shut me up better than shouting would have, because it was the first kind thing she'd said to me in ten years and I didn't know what to do with it. "Sit down, Gideon. We have perhaps twenty minutes before the tide turns, and what I have to tell you will take all of them and most of your forgiveness besides."

"There is no furniture," I pointed out. "This street was eaten in 1841 according to Maren."

"So it was." She sat down anyway, on air, on the ghost of a step that had been erased so long ago even Saltmark's salt had forgotten it — and the step held her, briefly solidifying under her weight like a bruise pressed back into flesh. She patted it. "The erasure doesn't take things, exactly. It files them somewhere it can't reach them. Sit. Some things remember being useful if you ask nicely."

I sat on nothing. Nothing held. It was, I reflected grimly, the most comfortable I'd felt in Saltmark yet.

Tam, Maren, and Ilsa had come down after me and stood in a wary crescent behind my shoulder. I appreciated the backup. I resented needing witnesses.

"Start with the book," I said. "The prophecy. Your handwriting. Explain the century."

Odile folded her hands in her lap, and for a moment she looked every year of whatever she actually was — a hundred and forty, maybe, or a hundred and sixty, a life stretched thin over too many secrets. "You know what Vess does to countries it conquers?" she asked.

"Erases them. Maps, records, memories. Maren showed me."

"Maren showed you the *after.*" Odile glanced at Maren, who had the grace to look uncomfortable. "What your cartographer may not have shown you is the mechanism. Erasure requires an anchor — a person, born of the erased place, who carries its true name inside them. Vess finds the anchor. Vess unmakes them. The country loses its keystone and comes apart like wet paper. Every soul in it becomes a footnote, then a smudge, then a silence."

I felt the cold arrive before I understood why. "And Saltmark's anchor?"

"Was killed," Odile said. "In the first year of the erasure. Or so Vess believed. They were thorough people; they burned the fire-keepers' line down to the last recorded child. But the fire-keepers knew their own fate was coming — they'd read it in the flames for generations. So in the last winter, they did what keepers do." Her gray eyes came up to mine. "They kept something. They smuggled out the last heir of the line. An infant. Across the border, into the empire, into the arms of a hedge-witch with a reputation for discretion and a valley nobody bothered to map twice."

The nothing beneath me felt less solid all of a sudden.

"No," I said.

"The valley was already magic-dead," Odile went on, relentless, gentle, terrible. "A scar left by an old war, a place where power pooled wrong and died. No truth-eater could sense a spark there. No scrying could find a face. So I deepened it. I spent thirty years weaving the deadness tighter — not just a valley without magic, but a vault *built of* absence, sealed around one sleeping child, so that his own inheritance would stay dormant until—" She stopped. Started again. "Until tonight."

"Tonight is a date in a book," I said stupidly. "You wrote the book. You wrote tonight into the book."

"I wrote the night the seal thinned. Prophecy is just arithmetic done far enough in advance, Gideon. The fire-keepers' blood wakes on its own schedule. All I could choose was where you'd be standing when it did." She spread her hands. "So I chose the safest place in the world. And then, when you were nineteen and angry and ready to leave — which you were always going to be, I taught you too well for anything else — I let you go. Because a vault only protects what stays inside it, and you were never going to stay."

"You *let* me leave." The words came out of me like something coughed up. "I packed in secret. I waited until you were at market. I walked eleven miles before dawn so you wouldn't hear the door."

"I know. I watched you from the window. You took the wrong coat. I let you take the wrong coat." Something moved behind her face — grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes. "If I'd stopped you, you'd have fought me, and fighting me meant fighting the valley's deadness, and the strain might have cracked the seal early, with Vess still hunting. So I sat in my kitchen and watched my boy walk out of my life to keep him alive, and I have had ten years to decide whether that was love or cowardice, and I will tell you honestly: I still don't know."

The crescent behind me was very quiet. Tam was staring at me with his mouth slightly open, recalculating everything he thought he knew. Ilsa had closed her eyes. Maren wore the expression of a cartographer watching two coastlines fail to match.

"And the fire," I said, because my mouth needed a job while the rest of me came apart. "Ilsa's dragon-fire. What does that have to do with—"

"You are the last heir of the fire-keepers," Odile said simply. "The fire has been waiting for you since before you were born. It is not a shrine flame, Gideon. It is Saltmark's dead — every soul Vess erased, banked and burning, kept alive by feeding it memory. And it cannot pass to anyone else. It was never going to gutter out because of neglect. It was going to gutter out because it was waiting for *you*, and the waiting costs more fuel every year." Her voice roughened. "Ilsa has fed it beautifully. Braver than I ever was. But the arithmetic only ends one way, and it ends tonight, with you, at the Door Out, and that is the truth I hid from you for twenty-six years."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about learning your whole life was a lie: it doesn't arrive like a blow. It arrives like weather. It gets into everything at once.

I sat on a street that didn't exist, in a country that officially didn't exist, looking at a woman who shouldn't exist, and I tried to find the edges of the boy I'd been. The boy in the Valley of Ash. The one who mended charms that didn't work because the valley ate all magic, and told himself he was bad at craft, and believed it, and built a personality out of the belief — careful, unremarkable, harmless. Twenty-six years old and I had never once wondered why my hands itched around charms that worked fine everywhere but home. I had never wondered why Odile never let me practice anything above mending. Why she flinched — actually flinched — the one time I'd made a candle flame stand up on its own, at fifteen, laughing, proud, before snuffing it and saying *never again, Gideon, promise me.*

I had promised. I'd thought she was afraid of the empire finding a hedge-witch's apprentice.

She'd been afraid of me waking up.

"You stored me," I heard myself say. "Like a jar of preserves. Like — like a coin under a floorboard. You didn't hide a child. You *banked* an heir."

"Gideon—"

"Twenty-six years!" I was on my feet now, and the nothing under me hummed, and somewhere behind my sternum something that had been asleep my entire life turned over and stretched, and I hated it, I hated that it felt *good.* "Every birthday. Every time I asked about my parents you said *the road took them, don't pick at it.* Every time I asked why I couldn't learn real craft you said *the valley disagrees with you.* I thought I was broken! I thought there was something wrong with ME—"

"There is nothing wrong with you." For the first time, Odile's voice cracked. "There was never anything wrong with you. There was everything right with you, and that was the danger. Do you know what I did to build that valley's seal? I unmade my own gift, thread by thread, year by year, and poured it into the walls. I am a century old, Gideon, and unaged, because I paid my years into keeping you asleep and safe. And it worked. It worked for twenty-six years. Until a book washed up in your millrace, because the seal was thinning, and the country you came from reached through a hundred miles of dead air to hand you your own face, because Saltmark is DYING, and its last heir was mending broken charms in exile and calling it a life."

"I liked my life."

"No," she said, "you endured it. I watched you endure it. I wrote it down in my ledger every year: *he is safe, and he is not happy, and those are different words.*"

I wanted to hit her. I wanted, simultaneously, with the exact same force, to sit down on the nothing beside her and put my head on her shoulder the way I had at nine years old after nightmares, because she was the only mother-shaped thing I had ever had, and she had carried me out of a burning country as an infant, and spent a century paying for it, and lied to me every single day of it.

Rage and relief, tangled past any hope of separation. That was the honest shape of it. She had stolen my life and given me a life, and they were the same theft, and I could not return either one.

Behind me, Tam spoke up — hesitant, fourteen-going-on-forty: "Um. If he's the heir. And the fire's waiting for him." He swallowed. "Then when we open the Door Out..."

"The fire passes to him," Ilsa said quietly, eyes still closed. "All of it. Every soul. Every memory it holds. Into one body." She opened her eyes and looked at me with something between pity and awe. "That is what the fire showed me, the night you arrived. I thought the boy in the vision looked frightened because of the truth-eaters." A pause. "He wasn't frightened of them at all."

"Thank you, Ilsa," I said faintly. "Very comforting."

"Gideon." Odile rose too, slowly, and the erasure-tide shuddered around her like a dog recognizing its master. "I am not asking your forgiveness. I am asking for twenty minutes more of your trust, which I have not earned, at a door that decides whether a country lives or dies. Afterward, you may hate me properly, with the whole truth in hand. I'll even give you the wrong coat to throw at me. I kept it."

Against every rational instinct I possessed — and I want the record to show I have excellent instincts, I just never use them — I laughed. It came out ragged and wet, but it was a laugh.

"You kept the coat."

"It was a good coat. You just refused to wear a scarf."

Maren cleared her throat behind us, all business, saving me from whatever my face was doing. "The Door Out is half a day's march through the Unmapped Quarter," she said. "If we move now, we beat the tide and possibly the garrison. If we stand here having feelings, we beat neither."

"She's right," Odile said. Then, softer, only to me, as the others turned to go: "For what it's worth — I did not store you like a coin, whatever I said. Coins don't get read stories. Coins don't get taught to argue." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile, the first one in a decade aimed at me. "I stored you like a seed. And seeds, Gideon, are meant to wake up furious."

The erasure-tide groaned against the drowned district below us — and then, impossibly, it answered. Far off, beyond the salt towers, beyond the garrison fires, a sound rolled across the ruined country like a struck bell: the Door Out, sensing its heir approach, beginning to unlock itself.

And from the direction of the shore, faint but closing, came the drums of the Vessian fleet.

"Well," said Tam, shouldering his pack with the grim competence of a boy who had recently committed treason and was getting surprisingly good at it. "Everyone wants a word with you tonight."

I looked at the bell-sound, and the drum-sound, and the woman who had been my whole world and its prison both.

"Let's not keep them waiting," I said, and the fire in my chest — my fire, our fire, a country's worth of dead souls banking themselves behind my ribs — leaned toward the Door like a compass needle finally allowed to point north.
