## 14. Ash and Accusation

I have been lied to by professionals my whole life. The Vessian schoolhouse taught me that Saltmark never existed the way other children were taught their times tables — patiently, daily, until it settled into the bones. My captain taught me that orders are a kind of mercy, because they spare you from deciding anything yourself. And my mother — well, my mother only ever lied about small things, like how much sugar was left, so I can't even blame her properly.

But Maren Voss lying to us about the Door Out? That was a different category entirely. That was a lie with architecture. A lie she had built load-bearing walls into.

She'd told us at the fire in chapter thirteen of this disaster — I don't know how else to describe it — that opening the Door Out was simple. Turn the lock, walk through, done, country saved. What Gideon had worked out of her, in that awful quiet voice he gets when he's frightened, was that opening it fully would let the erasure flood backward through the threshold. Into the valley. Into the world. Into everything the Door had been built to protect.

"You knew," Gideon said. Not shouting. That was somehow worse. "You knew, and you let us march toward it."

Maren stood there in the ash-fall of the broken salt gate with her map-case hugged to her chest like a breastplate, and for once in her life she didn't have a clever answer ready. I watched her jaw work. I watched her decide, visibly, between three different versions of the truth, and I thought: *there it is. That's what a cartographer looks like when the map runs out.*

"I kept it from you," she said finally, "because if I told you, you would have stopped."

"Yes," said Gideon.

"And stopping meant Saltmark dies."

"Also yes."

"So which lie do you prefer?" Maren's voice cracked on it, and I hated her a little less for that. "The one where you die comfortable, or the one where—"

"That's not—" Gideon pressed both hands over his face. "That is *not* how you get to frame it."

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being fourteen and having just betrayed your entire garrison: your feelings don't schedule themselves conveniently. You'd think I'd be focused on the fact that Captain Rehl saw me turn my lantern-signal, that there is very likely a noose with my name penciled on it somewhere in a quartermaster's ledger, that I am now, officially, a boy of a country that officially does not exist.

Instead, standing there while the cartographer and the prophecy boy tore at each other, all I could feel was the fire.

Not dragon-fire. The other kind. The one from when I was nine.

They called it an unrecorded border incident. That's the phrase they used — *unrecorded*, like it was a clerical matter, like our house simply hadn't been important enough to make it into the paperwork. My father. My mother. My sister Nell, who was six and afraid of moths. All of them filed under nothing, because Vess does not record its mistakes, it records its corrections, and my family was apparently a correction.

I learned later — learned it here, in this dead country that sang at night — that there was no border fire at all. There was an erasure. An early one, a test run, a practice stroke before the empire painted out Saltmark entirely. My family didn't burn. They were *unwritten*. And the empire sent me a letter of condolence that misspelled my own name.

So when Maren stood there explaining, with her careful cartographer's cadence, that some truths must be managed for the greater good, something in me went very quiet and very cold, the way it used to go quiet and cold during drills, right before I did exactly what I was told.

Except this time I wasn't going to do what I was told.

"You're just like them," I heard myself say.

Everyone turned. Good. I'd been rehearsing.

"The truth-eaters," I said. "The archivists. Your old masters. They eat memories and call it mercy. They erase countries and call it peace. And you—" I pointed at her map-case, that smug leather box she loved more than any of us, probably, "you keep secrets and call it protection. You're the same animal. You've just got better manners."

Maren went pale in the particular way that people do when you've hit something they already suspected about themselves. "Tam—"

"No. Don't 'Tam' me." My voice was doing something strange, climbing. "My family is dead because people like you decided what we were allowed to know. My sister is *unrecorded*. There isn't even a grave. There's a gap in a ledger. And you want to manage information at the Door Out because you've decided what we can handle." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, nothing like a laugh. "You know what you can handle? Handle this: I followed you because you'd been thrown away by Vess too. I thought, here's someone who knows what the empire costs. Turns out you just wanted a smaller empire. One with four subjects. And you get to be the one who decides."

"Gideon," Maren said desperately, turning to him, "tell him—"

"Don't," Gideon said.

And the word landed in the middle of our little triangle like a dropped lantern.

* * *

Because here's the other thing. Here's the thing I'd been chewing on since the salt gate, since I turned my lantern and watched my whole old life swing away behind me like a door closing.

Gideon owed me. That's what I'd believed, anyway, in the warm stupid part of my heart. I'd disobeyed a direct order. I'd put myself between him and the garrison's crossbows. In Tam's private arithmetic — and I'm aware, now, how childish it was — that bought loyalty. Loyalty meant he picked my side. When I turned on Maren, he was supposed to stand next to me, because that's what a found family *does*, isn't it? It picks sides. It closes ranks. It makes you feel like belonging comes with a blade attached.

And instead Gideon looked at me with those tired hedge-witch eyes and said, "Don't."

"I won't choose," he said.

"You have to choose," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded. "That's how it works. Someone's right and someone's wrong and—"

"No." He said it gently, which made it worse. "Tam. Listen to me. If I choose between the person who's telling the truth and the people who carried me this far, then whoever I don't pick stops being family, and I will not do that. I spent eleven years in a valley learning that the safest thing to do with people is keep them at arm's length so they can't be taken from you. Odile taught me that without ever meaning to. I'm not going back to it. Not even for the satisfaction of being angry correctly."

"But she lied!"

"She lied." He nodded slowly. "And she also mapped a road through an unmapping country to get me to a door that might save everyone here. Both of those things are true at the same time, and I'm so tired of pretending the world sorts itself into clean columns. You want me to tell you Maren's a traitor so you can stop hurting. I understand. I want someone to tell me Odile was purely a villain or purely a saint so I can stop hurting too. Nobody's offering. Because it doesn't work like that."

"It should work like that," I muttered.

"Yeah," he said. "It really should."

And that — that stupid, gentle agreement — did more damage to my anger than any argument could have. Because he wasn't defending her. He wasn't even defending himself. He was just standing there in the ash admitting that everything hurt, and asking me to hurt alongside him instead of alone.

I sat down on a fallen salt-block, because my legs had opinions, and put my head in my hands, and said, to nobody, "Nell was scared of moths."

Nobody answered, because nobody knew what I meant yet. But I felt Maren sit down nearby, at a respectful distance, like a dog that knows it's been caught eating something forbidden. And I felt Gideon sit down on my other side, close enough that our shoulders touched, and stay there.

* * *

It was Ilsa who ended it. Of course it was.

You have to understand what Ilsa was by then. Forty years of feeding memories to the dragon-fire, log by log, and in the last weeks she'd given it nearly everything — her childhood, her ordination, her daughter's name, the shape of her daughter's face, the sound of her daughter laughing at moths, actually, which is why when I said it, her head lifted like a struck bell.

"Moths," she repeated softly.

"Nell was scared of them," I said, throat tight. "Why did I say that?"

"Because grief keeps what it can," Ilsa said. She crossed the ruined gateyard toward us with that terrible calm she carried, the calm of a woman who has forgotten almost everything except how to pay attention. "Anger forgets fastest. Anger burns hot and clean and leaves nothing to bury. But grief hoards little useless things. Moths. Sugar rations. The way someone coughed." She lowered herself down onto the salt-block across from me with a series of small joint-creaks, ancient at fifty, and looked at me with eyes that held almost nothing and therefore reflected everything. "Tell me her name again. You told me once. I gave mine away but I kept yours, all of yours. Yours, Gideon's, Maren's. Those I fed to the fire last, and those it gave back, because it likes to be fair."

"Tam," I said.

"Tam," she agreed. "And your sister?"

"Nell."

"And you are furious at Maren because she reminds you of the people who took Nell away. And you are furious at Gideon because he wouldn't take your side, and taking sides is how soldiers survive, and you were raised to survive." She said it without accusation, the way you'd read off a supply manifest. "Yes?"

"...Yes," I admitted.

"And Maren hid the cost of the Door because hiding things is how cartographers love. They draw the safe road and leave the cliffs off the map, because if they drew the cliffs, no one would travel, and then what was the map for?" Ilsa turned her empty, full gaze on Maren. "Yes?"

Maren's voice came out small. "Yes."

"You are not the same animal as the truth-eaters," Ilsa said. "The truth-eaters eat truth because they cannot bear a world that contains it. Maren hides truth because she cannot bear losing the people she tells it to. It is a cousin's mistake, not a twin's. But it is still a mistake, Maren, and you will not make it again."

"No," Maren whispered. "No more managing. I swear it. Everything, from here to the Door. Even the parts that gut us."

"Good." Ilsa folded her hands. "Now. The hard part."

"There's a harder part?" I asked, wry despite myself. Wryness is armor; I'd been wearing it since I was nine years old and a letter misspelled my name.

"The hard part is forgiveness before you feel like forgiving," Ilsa said. "If you wait until you feel it, you will wait forever, and the Door opens at dawn whether your hearts are tidy or not. So." She extended one weathered hand toward Maren, palm up. "Maren. Tell Tam one true thing about his family's fire. Something from your archives. You researched the early erasures — I remember that much, I kept that much. Give him back one page of his ledger."

The silence stretched so long I could hear the salt towers singing their low night-song, that mournful harmonic that had cracked my training open in the first place.

Then Maren opened her map-case with shaking fingers and drew out not a chart but a folded document, imperial vellum, sealed twice. "Border incident, eastern reach, year — the year you'd have been nine," she said quietly. "Officially unrecorded. Unofficially, there's a survey note in the margin of a military atlas. One line. The cartographer who wrote it was executed for it, eventually. Me, eight years later, for the same crime in the same region." Her hand trembled as she unfolded it. "It says: *Village of Hallowmere. Fourteen households. Not burned. Withdrawn.* That's all. Fourteen households. Not burned. Withdrawn. It's not enough. It's a crumb. But it's true, and true is what I owe you."

Hallowmere. My village had a name again. It had had a name all along; I'd just been carrying it around inside me where no one could check it against anything.

"Not burned," I repeated. Which meant — which meant they hadn't suffered. Which meant Nell hadn't—

I put my face in my hands again and this time what came out wasn't anger. It was the other thing, the wet ugly thing underneath, and Ilsa's hand settled on the back of my neck, light as moth wings, and stayed.

* * *

We didn't forgive each other cleanly, because that's not how people work. We forgave each other messily, in fragments, over the next hour, while we re-sorted the supplies by the light of Ilsa's dwindling ember-lantern. Maren spread every remaining map on the ground and walked us through the whole route to the Door Out, naming every danger, including three she'd previously "forgotten" to mention, and I watched her flinch each time she confessed one, and I decided — not felt, *decided* — to let the flinching count for something.

Gideon never apologized for not choosing. I didn't ask him to. Somewhere in the last hour I'd understood that his refusal was the most loyal thing anyone had ever done for me. A man who takes sides gives you half himself. A man who refuses to split the family gives you all of it, including the parts you're furious at.

"We move at first grey," Maren said finally, rolling up the maps. "The Door's approach is two days' hard walking through the Flats. After the salt gate, the truth-eaters will expect us to run east. So we run west, straight through the old tide-channel, and—"

She stopped.

We all stopped. Because the salt towers had stopped singing.

In all my weeks in Saltmark, the singing had never stopped. It was the country's heartbeat, its proof-of-life, the sound that had undone everything the garrison had drilled into me. Now the night held nothing but wind, and in that nothing, far off toward the Emberhold, I saw a glow on the horizon that was not dawn.

It flickered. Stumbled. Dimmed.

"Oh no," Ilsa breathed, rising, and her empty face was suddenly full of one thing, sharp and total. "The fire. The fire is failing — no, no, I fed it everything, it should have weeks—"

The glow guttered once, twice, like a candle in a doorway.

And then, faint across the flats, carried on air that had gone utterly silent, we heard the third sound. The one that wasn't wind and wasn't song.

Drums. Vessian drums. Many of them.

"They're not waiting for us to come to the Door," Maren said, and her voice had gone flat and professional, the voice of a woman reading her own doom off a chart. "The fleet's landed. The empire is marching on the Emberhold tonight."

Gideon stood, and in the dying glow I saw something kindle in his face that I'd only ever seen in the fire itself — something old, and patient, and absolutely unwilling.

"Then we don't have two days," he said. "We have one night. Get your maps, Maren. Tam — you know how soldiers march. Tell me how fast."

I looked at the dimming horizon, and at the family I'd chosen, and I gave them the worst good news of my life: "Faster than we can."
