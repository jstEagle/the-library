## 16. Feeding the Fire

There is a trick to feeding a fire that eats memories, and it is not the trick you'd think.

You'd assume you should hold the memory tight while you feed it, like a mother pushing a child's face into her breast so the child remembers her shape. You'd be wrong. The fire takes what you give it, and if you clutch, it takes the clutching too — the love along with the beloved — and then you've fed it a whole person and gotten nothing back but warmth. No. You open your hand. You let go on purpose. You say: *here, take this, I am giving, not losing.* A priestess who cannot tell the difference between giving and losing has no business tending dragon-fire.

I have been telling myself the difference for forty years.

I learned it from Mother Anselm, who tended the Emberhold before me, and she learned it from whoever came before, all the way back to the fire-keepers of the old dynasty, whose names are ash in my mouth because I fed them to the flame one winter when Saltmark was starving for light. That is the joke of my office, if you want it: I am the keeper of everything we no longer keep. I am a librarian made entirely of empty shelves.

* * *

The fire had been low for three days.

Low is a word with teeth in it. When the Emberhold fire is healthy, it burns blue-gold at its heart and stands tall as a man, and the whole district of Saltglass can feel it in their chests, a warmth like being remembered by someone who loved you well. When it is low, it crouches. It goes the color of old coals. And the souls nearest it begin to thin at the edges — not dying, nothing so honest as dying, but going translucent, the way a word goes when you say it too many times.

I had been feeding it small things to make it last. The smell of rain on the harbor road. The name of a dog I owned at nine. The tune my mother hummed while kneading bread — I kept the humming and gave away the melody, which is a theft only I will ever know about, and now not even me.

But small things are gruel, and the fire was asking for meat.

It asks in its own way. Not words — never words, until it did, and I'll get to that, and I'll hate that I got to that. It asks by leaning. The flame tilts toward you the way a hungry child tilts toward the table. Forty years and I still could not stand in front of it without my body wanting to answer.

That morning I stood in front of it with my whole life arranged behind my eyes like plates on a shelf, and I began taking them down.

* * *

You want to know what forty years of a woman looks like when she's about to spend it? Here is the inventory, and I'll do it quickly, the way you pull off a bandage.

The day I was consecrated, and the weight of the ember-crown, heavier than anyone warns you. My daughter's birth — I'll come back to that one, I'm saving it, a priestess is allowed one thing off the table and I had always said this was mine. Her first word. Her laugh at four, the gap-toothed one. The winter she died, no — not that, I never gave it that one, I fed the fire around it for thirty years like water around a stone, and if you're wondering whether that's why the stone wore smooth, wonder quietly.

Her name. Sefa. I gave her name eleven years ago when the fire stumbled during the second erasure-tide, and a district of two hundred souls went quiet in the space between one breath and the next, and I have been a woman whose daughter has no name ever since. Grief without a noun. Do you know what that's like? It's like reaching for a hand in the dark and finding the dark has hands too.

So no, I would not give Sefa again. There was nothing left of her to give except what I'd guarded. Everything else — take it.

* * *

Gideon came to the Emberhold that morning. I heard him before I saw him, because the salt towers sing at night and he walks like someone apologizing to the floor for standing on it. Hedge-trained. Odile's boy. He stood in the doorway of the shrine with his hands full of nothing, the way young men do when they want to help and know they can't.

"You shouldn't watch," I told him.

"Probably not," he said. He didn't leave. That's the thing about him nobody expects, because he looks like a man built out of exits. He stays. "What happens to you? After?"

"After what?"

"When it's — all gone."

I turned back to the fire. It had leaned so far toward me it was nearly horizontal, a flag in a wind that wasn't there. "Ask me tomorrow," I said, "if there's an after, and a me."

He sat down against the wall where I could hear him breathing, which I suspect was the entire point, though he'd never admit it. Boy was raised by a witch and a dead valley and he still worked out that the kindest thing you can do for someone walking into a fire is to be audible outside it.

I opened my hand. Metaphorically. Actually my hands were shaking like any other old woman's.

* * *

Here is how it works, since no one else alive knows and soon no one will at all:

You choose the memory. You hold it up — not in your mind, in your chest, there's a place under the sternum where remembered things live warm, everyone has it, the empire just calls it something clinical. You show it to the fire. And then you do the hardest thing, which is nothing. You don't push it out. You stop holding it in. The difference between those two actions is the whole of my religion, and I am the last person practicing it, and I was never very good.

The first thing I gave was the consecration. I felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave — a socket suddenly full of air. I knew afterward, from my own notes — I keep notes, ink doesn't forget even when I must — that I had been crowned at nineteen by Mother Anselm, that I had wept, that my father had been proud. The notes were in my handwriting. The pride of my father was a fact about a stranger.

Then faster, because the fire had tasted willingness and leaned harder: my ordination vows, the harbor road in rain, the dog, my mother's kitchen, my teacher's voice saying *give, Ilsa, don't lose*, my wedding day to a man I now understood I had married, though the loving of him was long since spent in some earlier famine year — the fire ate our anniversary every hard winter and left me a woman wed to a warm fact.

The fire rose as I fed it. Blue-gold climbing, taller than a man, taller than two. In the shrine's salt-crusted windows the light went strange and holy, and outside I could hear the district breathing easier, souls thickening at their edges like frost forming backward. Somewhere out there a woman who had been translucent picked up her child and felt the child's weight as real. That is what your memories buy, when you spend them here. Not light. Weight. Other people's reality.

I gave my teacher's face. I gave the sound of the sea before Vess drowned the harbor. I gave the taste of salt-plums, which meant I gave away the knowledge that anything had ever tasted like anything, and the world went flavorless as paper in my mouth, and I kept feeding, because outside that window there were people whose edges needed thickening, and I had promised a dead dynasty I would be the shelf they broke before the books did.

* * *

Somewhere past the middle — I couldn't tell you when, time goes soft when you're being emptied — I reached the stone in the water.

Sefa's death. Thirty years I had fed around it. The fire found it the way a tongue finds a broken tooth, and leaned, and waited, patient as hunger.

"No," I said. Out loud. To a fire. Gideon shifted against the wall.

It waited anyway. That's the horror of it — not greed. Patience. A beast would have taken. This *asked*, and asked, and asked, the way a child asks, and I understood in that moment that I had been confusing the two for forty years, patience and appetite, tenderness and need.

And here is what I did instead, and I want it written somewhere that I did it on purpose:

I gave it the emptiness around the stone. I gave it the thirty years of guarding. I gave it every dawn I woke and reached for her name and found the socket full of air. I gave it the grief without the noun, whole, unguarded, held open like a wound held open for cleaning.

And the fire stopped leaning.

It stood up straight, blue-white, and it did the thing it had never done in forty years or in any account of the fire-keepers back to the drowned dynasty.

It spoke.

Not words, exactly. Cadence. Rhythm. A rise and fall I knew the way you know a voice through a wall — not the sentence but the music of the sentence. A particular lilt that turned the ends of questions down instead of up. A way of laughing that arrived half a beat before the laugh.

Sefa's cadence.

I did not fall. Priestesses don't fall; we fold, slowly, decorously, onto things. I folded onto the altar step and the fire bent toward me, down from its great height, down and down, until its light lay across my knees like a blanket drawn up by someone who loves you.

*Ilsa,* said the cadence, in the tone she'd used at six years old when she wanted to tell me something important and didn't have the words yet. Just my name, carried in her rhythm, in her music, the way a stranger might carry your ring back to you.

"You're not her," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised us both, I think. "My girl is dead. I buried—" and I stopped, because I realized I couldn't remember burying her, only that the notes said I had, only that grief-without-a-noun had a shape and the shape was a small grave on a hill above Saltglass.

The cadence did the thing she did when I was being stubborn — a little downward turn, fondness and exasperation braided. And then, wordless still, but unmistakable, it said: *not her. Us.*

Us.

And the fire showed me. That's what it does, I understand now, that's all it has ever done — it isn't a beast that eats. It's a country that holds. Every memory I fed it didn't vanish into a stomach. It became part of the flame, the way a log becomes heat — transformed, given back. The warmth in Saltglass chests. The weight returning to thinning souls. The singing in the salt towers at night, which Tam hears and the garrison calls wind. All of it was the fed, giving back. My mother's kitchen was in the fire. The harbor road in rain was in the fire. Somewhere in that blue-gold heart my father's pride burned on, keeping a stranger's child warm.

Every soul Saltmark had lost to the erasure-tides — the districts gone quiet, the names unwritten — they hadn't dissolved. They'd been *carried*. The fire was not the last living thing in Saltmark. It was the last *remembering* thing, and it had been remembering us all along, and we had been starving it of ourselves and calling it hunger.

"Oh," I said. Which is what I said at her birth, too, according to a note in my own handwriting I no longer remember writing. "Oh, you great stupid beautiful country."

The cadence laughed, half a beat early.

* * *

I don't know how long I knelt there. Long enough that the fire, having spoken, seemed embarrassed by it — it drew back up to its proper height and resumed burning, dignified, the way she used to resume composure after crying, chin up, daring you to mention it.

Gideon touched my shoulder eventually. His hand was careful, the hand of a boy who has learned that people break. "Priestess. You're — " He searched for it. "You look lighter."

"I gave it everything," I told him. And it was true, and it wasn't, and I examined the place under my sternum where the stone had been, and found — not the memory of Sefa's death, no. But the grief was different now. Lighter, as he said. Carried. Somewhere in that fire my worst night was a log, and logs become warmth, and the warmth was holding up a district of souls, including, I suspected, mine.

"It spoke," I said.

He went very still. "With what voice?"

"With hers." I looked at him. He was white as the salt on the walls. "Boy, do you understand what that means? It's not a fire we're keeping lit. It's the dead, keeping *us* lit. It's everyone Vess erased, holding the door of themselves open." I gripped his wrist. Old woman's grip; it left marks. "When you open your Door Out — whatever price it asks — the price isn't paid to a beast."

"I know," he said quietly. "The prophecy says the fire passes into—"

"Not passes." I shook him, gently, once. "*Hands over*. There's a difference, and it's the difference between giving and losing, and I'm the last person alive who knows it, so listen: when it comes to you, don't clutch, and don't push. Open your hand. Say *here, take this, I am giving.*"

"And if I can't tell the difference?" Gideon asked. His voice cracked on it, young, terrified, exactly as old as he was.

I smiled at him with whatever face I had left. "Then you're ready. That's the whole secret. Nobody's ready who thinks they know the difference."

* * *

The fire stands tall tonight. Tallest in forty years. The whole of Saltmark can feel it, and the towers are singing louder than Tam has ever heard them, and the truth-eaters' fleet is coming, and I have almost nothing left to feed it.

Almost.

Because here is what the fire showed me, in her cadence, in the last hour, when Gideon had gone and the shrine was quiet: it showed me that it does not need what I guarded. It never needed Sefa's death. It needs what I spent thirty years refusing to give — not the grave, not the grief, but the *name*. Names are the wood this fire burns best. A name given freely is worth a hundred stolen smells of rain.

Sefa. Eleven years I lived without it. One syllable of her, sitting in my chest like a coal I mistook for a wound.

Tomorrow the fleet comes. Tomorrow Gideon goes to the Door, and the truth-eaters come for him, and the fire will gutter one final time, and everything will depend on what it has left inside it.

Tonight I stand before it with my hand open.

"Sefa," I say, giving, not losing, and the fire leans down to me like a daughter coming home, "her name was Sefa."
