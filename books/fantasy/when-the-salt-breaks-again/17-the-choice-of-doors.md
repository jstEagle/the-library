## 17. The Choice of Doors

The final page of the prophecy had been blank for a hundred years.

I know how that sounds. I'd spent three days in the drowned archive telling myself the same thing — that a blank page was a blank page, that Odile's handwriting simply ran out of ink or nerve or time, that the universe was not in the business of leaving me personalized notes. I had gotten very good at telling myself things. It was practically my only skill for eleven years in the Valley of Ash.

But the ink was coming.

I watched it come. That was the worst part — you could watch it. The letters surfaced slowly, like bodies rising in a flooded cellar, one stroke at a time, as though the page had been written in something that needed the right hour, the right fire, the right boy standing over it with salt on his boots and no good options left. By lantern-light the words were pale gray. By dawn they were black as a confession.

*The Door Out was never a door out.*

I read it four times before I let myself understand it, which is a personal record for self-deception and I'm not proud of it.

* * *

Maren found me at first light, because Maren always found me at first light; she claimed it was habit from her cartography days, surveying before the heat distorted the land, but I suspected she just didn't trust any of us to be alive unless she could see us breathing. She stood in the archive doorway with her maps rolled under one arm and her face doing that thing it did when she'd already done the arithmetic and hated the answer.

"You've been here all night," she said.

"The floor grew on me."

"Gideon." She came closer, and her eyes went to the page, and I watched two decades of imperial training fail to keep her expression neutral. "What does it say?"

"It says I've been misreading the assignment." I turned the book toward her. The new ink was still wet enough to smudge if you touched it, which felt like an appropriate metaphor for everything else in my life lately.

She read. Her lips moved slightly — she wasn't aware she did that, and I'd decided months ago never to tell her, because it was the only advantage I had over a woman who could navigate by dead reckoning through a country that actively tried to forget its own geography.

When she finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Who wrote this part?"

"Odile. A century ago." I tapped the margin, where a date sat in that same slanted, impatient hand I'd spent my childhood being scolded in. "It's dated tomorrow."

"That's not possible."

"Neither am I, apparently." I closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping. "The Door Out doesn't lead out, Maren. It leads *through*. Open it all the way and Saltmark's name goes back into the world — every map, every record, every head that's been scrubbed clean. The erasure reverses at the threshold. Everything Vess buried comes up singing."

"That's—" She stopped. I watched her want to say *wonderful* and *catastrophic* at the same time, and lose the argument with herself.

"Go on," I said. "Say the second half. Everyone eventually says the second half."

"The valley." Her voice went flat, professional, the voice she used for coordinates. "Your valley. If the erasure reverses everywhere, then the dead air dies too. The magic comes back. And the moment it does—" She didn't finish, but she didn't have to. We both knew what lived on the other side of that sentence. Truth-eaters. The empire's hounds, who ate forbidden memories and left people smiling and hollow. The moment the Door opened wide enough for Saltmark to be remembered, it would be open wide enough for them to find the boy the prophecies had been pointing at since before his grandmother was born.

"Every truth-eater in Vess comes for me," I said. "That's the other half of the page. Want to hear the third half? There isn't one. No instructions. No clever loophole. Just a door, a fire, and a footnote about how whoever opens it should probably get their affairs in order."

Maren sat down across from me on a drowned desk, salt-crusted, water-stained, a piece of furniture that had been waiting a century for someone to need it. "There's another option," she said carefully.

"No, there isn't."

"We take what we can carry. The souls nearest the Emberhold. We go back through the Door the way we came — narrow, quiet, the way you slipped in. We don't open it all the way. We save some of them."

"And the rest?" I kept my voice level, which cost me. "The districts past the salt gates. The ones already going quiet whenever the dragon-fire stumbles. Ilsa fed it her daughter's name, Maren. Her daughter's *name* — do you understand what that means? She gave away the last thing that made her Ilsa instead of a woman-shaped container for other people's hope. And you're proposing we save *some*?"

"I'm proposing we survive!"

"Congratulations. Surviving is what I did for eleven years. I have references."

The words came out sharper than I meant them, and I saw them land, and I didn't take them back, because they were true. That was the ugly thing about the Valley of Ash — it hadn't been a life. It had been a hiding place with a bed in it. I'd mended charms that couldn't work and told myself I was content the way you tell yourself a scar doesn't itch. Every day of it, I had been the answer to a question somebody else was asking, stored like a jar of preserves in a cellar built by a woman who loved me enough to erase the world around me.

And now the page said the cellar door worked both ways.

* * *

I took the book to Ilsa.

Not because I wanted to — Ilsa frightened me now, in the way that truly calm people frighten everyone who still has something left to lose. Since she'd given the fire her memories, she moved through Saltmark like weather. Nothing rattled her. Nothing *could*. When Tam shouted at her, she looked at him with mild interest, the way you'd look at rain.

She was at the Emberhold shrine when I arrived, sitting before the dragon-fire with her hands open on her knees. The fire was low. It had been low for days — a red so deep it was nearly brown, guttering in a way that made the whole city flinch. Around the shrine, the surviving souls of Saltmark stood in their patient half-lit way, watching the flame the way sailors watch a lighthouse they're not entirely sure is lit.

"Ilsa," I said. "The prophecy has a last page."

"Of course it does." She didn't turn. "Prophecies are like fires. They keep their worst news until the end, when you're too committed to walk away."

I told her. All of it — the reversal, the price, the truth-eaters, the valley's dead air burning off like morning fog. I told her because she was the only person I knew who would hear the whole thing without flinching, and because some small, cowardly part of me hoped she'd give me permission not to do it. That's the secret nobody tells you about seeking counsel: sometimes you're shopping for absolution to stay exactly where you are.

Ilsa listened with her hands still open on her knees. Then she laughed — a soft, cracked sound, like ice settling.

"You thought this was your choice," she said.

"What?"

"The door. You think it's yours to open or not open." She finally turned, and her eyes were the strangest thing about her now — empty of everything except attention, and somehow fuller than most people's. "Gideon. The fire showed me you coming before you were born. Not you specifically. A keeper. Someone who belonged to both sides of the threshold. Do you know why the fire-keepers died out?"

"Because Vess burned them."

"Because they *left*." She said it gently, which made it worse. "They carried the fire out through the Door, generation after generation, planting it in safe places — valleys, vaults, hidden countries where erasure couldn't reach. Waiting for one of those planted fires to grow a keeper strong enough to carry it home. Your mentor didn't build a hiding place, boy. She built a greenhouse. And the harvest is ripe."

The shrine was very quiet. The fire popped, a small sound, almost domestic.

"So it was never a choice," I said. My voice sounded far away to me.

"It's always a choice." Ilsa rose, and even her movements had that unhurried quality of someone who had traded away urgency along with everything else. "You can refuse. People refuse destinies constantly. It's one of humanity's finest traditions. The fire will gutters out in a season. Saltmark will dissolve politely, district by district, and the empire will have won so thoroughly that even winning will be forgotten. And you'll go back to your valley — except there won't be a valley, will there? Once the Door opens, once Saltmark returns to the world's memory, the erasure tide pulls back from everything it touched." She smiled, faintly. "Including the thing that was keeping you asleep."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It rarely is. But notice — you're angry because you *can't* hide anymore, not because you must choose. The choice was made for you a century ago by a woman who loved you. All that's left for you is to decide whether to do it bitterly." She patted my arm, once, like closing a ledger. "Bitter keeps poorly. Trust me. I've forgotten why."

* * *

Tam found me on the seawall at dusk.

He didn't say anything at first. He just climbed up next me and sat the way fourteen-year-olds sit — like gravity was a personal insult — and dangled his legs over the drop where the salt water gnawed at the foundations. He was still wearing pieces of his garrison uniform. He'd stopped wearing all of it weeks ago, one item at a time, like a man dismantling a former self and not wanting to waste the parts that fit.

"Maren says you're going to do it," he said finally. "Open it all the way."

"Maren says a lot of things."

"She also cried, but she'll deny it."

I looked at him. He was watching the horizon, where the erasure-fleet's lights had appeared two nights ago — a string of cold white points, patient as a sentence being composed. Somewhere behind those lights was the chief of the truth-eaters, and behind him, the full weight of an empire that had murdered a country and then murdered the memory of the murder.

"They'll come for you," Tam said. "All of them. The moment it opens."

"I know."

"They eat memories. What happens if they eat yours?"

I considered lying to him. He was fourteen; lying to children about death is one of civilization's oldest courtesies. But Tam had heard the salt towers sing at night in a country he'd been taught didn't exist, and he'd followed the singing anyway. Lying to him would have been like lying to the sea.

"I don't know," I said. "Maybe nothing. Maybe the fire protects it. Maybe I stop being anyone at all." I turned the prophecy book over in my hands. "Here's what I do know. Odile hid me for twenty-two years. Fed me, raised me, erased a valley's worth of magic to keep one boy invisible. And you know what I remember most from all those years? Being *careful*. Careful with charms. Careful with questions. Careful with the window at night, in case something followed the smell of me across the mountains." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I was never alive, Tam. I was *stored*. And the page says the storage unit's lease is up either way."

Tam was quiet for a while. Down below, the water slapped the stones in a rhythm that might have been a heartbeat or might have been the towers singing again, faintly, in a language the empire had failed to drown.

"When my family's village burned," he said at last, "they told us it was unrecorded. Like the fire happened somewhere maps weren't looking. I believed that for years — that places could just *slip*, that some fires fell between the cracks of the world." He picked at the salt crusting the stone beside him, flicking flakes into the dark. "It wasn't between the cracks. Somebody erased it. Somebody sat in a clean office and decided my mother's name was excess information." He flicked another flake. His voice stayed flat, which is how boys his age cry. "If opening the door means names come back — all of them, even the burned ones, even the unrecorded ones — then I don't care what eats you. I'll hold the door myself."

"You're fourteen."

"I'm fourteen and I've held worse doors." He stood, brushed salt off his trousers, and paused. "For what it's worth? You're bad at being scared. You do this thing where you make jokes faster when it's bad. Maren noticed. Even Ilsa noticed, and she's mostly weather now."

He walked off along the seawall toward the garrison watch-fire, whistling something tuneless, and I sat there feeling seen in ways I hadn't authorized.

* * *

Midnight. I went back to the shrine alone.

The fire was lower than it had ever been — barely a hand's height now, breathing in long, labored draws. The souls of Saltmark had gathered in the streets outside, silent, facing the Emberhold. They knew. Whatever they were now, they knew, the way animals know weather.

I knelt where Ilsa knelt, and I put the book down on the stones, open to the last page, and I spoke to the fire the way you speak to something old and dying that you love anyway.

"I know what you are," I said. "Ilsa figured it out. You're not a beast. You're the remembered dead. Every memory she fed you — you're made of them. You're the country, aren't you? The part of it that refused to be archived."

The flame leaned toward me. Not much. An inch of red light bending, like a listener.

"And I'm supposed to carry you home." My voice cracked, and I let it. Nobody was listening but the dead. "Through a door that opens onto everything. Every truth-eater in the empire, every erased name, every debt Odile spent a century arranging. And afterward I won't be — whatever I was. The careful man from the valley. He ends at the threshold. I keep reading that part hoping it says otherwise, and it keeps saying otherwise."

The fire guttered. For a horrible moment it shrank to a coal, and the whole shrine went gray at the edges, and somewhere out in the dark a district of souls made a sound like a held breath.

Then it steadied. And in the steadying, I heard it — not words, exactly. More like the shape of words, the way you hear your own name called from another room. It sounded, absurdly, tenderly, like Ilsa. Like her daughter, maybe, whose name none of us could recall anymore but whose cadence the fire had learned.

*We have been waiting a hundred years,* the silence said. *Do not make us wait out of politeness.*

I laughed. It came out wet and surprised, and it was the first honest laugh I'd had since the millrace handed me a book with my own face in it.

"All right," I said to the fire, to the dead, to Odile wherever she stood on the erasure-tide with her borrowed years running out, to the careful man I was about to stop being. "All right. Tomorrow. At the Door. Bring everyone."

The flame stood up straight, suddenly, impossibly — a finger's width taller, brightening from ember-red to something gold.

Outside, across the drowned capital, every salt tower began to sing at once.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I sat in the doorway of the ruin we'd been sheltering in, with the prophecy book on my knees, and I watched the fleet lights on the horizon multiply. More ships than two nights ago. They knew too, in their way. Truth-eaters can smell a decision being made; it's the rarest spice in the empire.

Toward dawn, Maren came and stood beside me, arms crossed against the cold, following my gaze out to sea.

"Changed your mind?" she asked.

"No."

"Good." She was quiet a moment. "Because I owe you a truth, and I'd rather you hear it before the fighting than during. The lock on the Door — the reason it needs someone who belongs to both sides? It isn't a keyhole, Gideon. It's a wound. Opening it fully means holding it open with your own blood and fire while the erasure tries to close it, and it will try, and it will be *hungry*, and—"

"Maren."

"—and I've known since the archive, and I didn't tell you, because I wanted—"

"*Maren.*" I touched the book on my knees. The last page had gone blank again overnight — spent, used up, its single job done. Except that as I watched, fresh ink was surfacing along the bottom margin, in Odile's slanted, impatient, beloved, infuriating hand. One line. A date.

Tomorrow's.

And beneath it, smaller, the first new words in a century:

*Bring salt. He will know why.*

I stood up, and the wind off the erasure-fleet smelled like cold iron and clean paper, and every tower in Saltmark was still singing.

"Well," I said. "I suppose I'd better find out why."
