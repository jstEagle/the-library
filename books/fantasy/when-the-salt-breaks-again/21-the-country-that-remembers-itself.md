## 21. The Country That Remembers Itself

I am told I used to have a daughter.

I am told this every morning, because I asked people to tell me, and then — because the woman who asked no longer remembers asking — they tell her anyway, out of habit, or kindness, or both, and she is always a little startled by it, the way you are startled by rain when you've forgotten the sky.

Her name was Wren. They say it to me over breakfast. Wren. It lands in me like a stone dropped into an empty well. I hear it fall and fall and never hear it strike bottom.

You would think that would be unbearable. It is not. It is worse than unbearable; it is *manageable*, which is a cruelty the gods invented later, after they'd run out of thunderbolts. I gave everything I had to the fire — forty years of memories, one log at a time, and at the end, when the flame guttered and Saltmark's dead began to go quiet, I fed it my daughter's name too. I remember deciding to do it. I remember the shape of the decision, its weight in my hands like a hot loaf. The decision is the last thing I own. Everything it purchased belongs to other people now.

They are paying me back in installments.

* * *

Saltmark did not rebuild its towers. I want that understood, because the maps — the new maps, the ones Maren Voss draws with hands that shake only when she thinks no one is watching — show cranes and scaffolds and honest smoke from honest chimneys, and people assume the country came back as buildings.

It didn't. Buildings were never the problem. Vess could erase a tower in an afternoon; they had whole fleets of ships for it, brass throats that drank the truth out of things. What they could not do — what they tried for a hundred years and failed to do — was stop people from *telling each other things*. So when the Door opened and the erasure reversed and every mind from here to the imperial capital woke up remembering a drowned country it had sworn never existed, Saltmark's survivors looked at their salt-crusted ruins and made an executive decision.

The towers could wait. The names could not.

That's how the Festival of Reading Aloud started. I am told I attended the first one. I have been told about it so many times I almost feel I remember it, which is either healing or fraud, and I've decided not to investigate which.

It works like this: once a season, on the salt flats where the Emberhold stands, everyone comes with paper. Not ledgers, not deeds, not petitions — just paper, cheap stuff, the kind that tears. And one by one they stand up and read names. Names of the dead. Names of the erased districts. Names of streets, of boats, of dogs, of a baker whose sourdough was famous along the Tidemark for sixty years until Vess decided he had never existed. You read until your voice gives out, and then someone else takes your page, and they read your names in your place, so nothing stops, so the reading goes on all night like a tide that has decided, finally, to come back in.

The fire loves it. Of course the fire loves it. We learned late — we learned too late, some of us — that the dragon-fire was never a beast at all. It was them. The remembered dead, burning together, and every memory I fed it for forty years wasn't fuel. It was *company*.

Now when the fire flares during the festival, it flares in patterns. Old priestesses — I am apparently still one of these, though my ordination is another thing I know only by report — used to say the fire danced when it was pleased. It doesn't dance. It *spells*. And on festival nights, if you stand close enough, you can watch it write names in light, one after another after another, faster than any reader can speak them, as if it has forty years of backlog and intends to clear it.

I sit near it now. People bring me there. I don't trust myself to find my own way, which sounds tragic and mostly isn't; Gideon Zhou says I have the only honest memory in Saltmark, because mine contains exactly what happened to it, no more and no less. He says this in his dry way, mending a charm that doesn't need mending while he says it, because Gideon cannot say a kind thing while looking directly at you. He'd short-circuit. We've all agreed not to force the issue.

* * *

Tam Vellik teaches school.

I want you to sit with that for a moment, because I knew him when he was a fourteen-year-old garrison boy with a lantern-signal in his hand and a captain shouting at him to burn intruders out of a salt gate, and he turned the lantern instead — turned it *wrong*, on purpose, misdirecting his own side with a code he'd been drilled in since he could walk — and in doing so became, in the space of one breath, not a soldier of Vess but a boy of Saltmark. A boy who had been taught Saltmark never was, and who heard the towers singing anyway, at night, through barracks walls, and had the courage to admit he heard it.

Now he stands in front of children — garrison children, mostly, the sons and daughters of the occupation forces who stayed on after the empire remembered its shame — and teaches them the country's real history.

He is terrible at it. He is magnificent at it. Both things are true and he manages them simultaneously, which may be the most Saltmarkish accomplishment of the age.

I visited his classroom once. I'm told I visit often; I don't recall the individual visits, so each one is fresh, which Tam says makes me his favorite student, since I laugh at the same jokes every time and never once sigh.

He had chalk dust to his elbows and a map on the wall that was wrong in seventeen deliberate ways. "All right," he said to the room, "who can find the lie?"

A girl of perhaps nine pointed at the coast. "The Tidemark's drawn inland."

"Good. Why?"

"Because the empire wanted the harbor to look smaller than it was. To make the trade records match."

"Match what?"

"The lie." She said it flatly, bored even, the way children say things that adults will spend decades failing to learn. "Records have to agree with each other or people notice. So they changed all of them. Except—" she squinted "—the tide tables. They forgot the tide tables."

"They forgot the tide tables," Tam agreed solemnly, "and a fisherman caught it, and that fisherman was my grandmother, and that is why none of you are allowed to trust a map until you've thrown it in the sea and see if it swims." Which is nonsense, obviously, and the children roared, and he let them roar, and then he said, quietly, in the voice he saves for the part that matters: "Your parents' orders were written by people who needed the lie to hold. Your grandparents' tide tables didn't care what anyone needed. When those two things disagree, believe the water."

Believe the water. I've decided to keep that. It's one of the few possessions I've acquired since I spent the old ones, and I carry it around like a coin I found on a road.

* * *

Here is how the giving-back works. This is the part I describe carefully, because people ask, and because I want the record — our record, the one we're writing ourselves now — to be accurate.

Each day, someone tells me something true that happened to me. Not a story about me; a memory, handed over whole, with its details intact. Maren does Tuesdays. She's precise, cartographically precise, and she describes rooms: where the doors were, which way the light fell, the exact crookedness of a picture frame. She gave me the Emberhold shrine, the forty years of it, in a single afternoon once, and I sat afterward with my hands flat on the table like a woman steadying herself on a boat, because I had been *there*, all that time, and now I knew it the way you know a coastline instead of the way you know your own hand.

Gideon does Thursdays. His are strange. He remembers my life better than I ever did, in one sense — because the fire showed him, in those last moments before it passed into him, everything it had ever eaten. He carries forty years of my devotions inside the same body that carries the last dragon-fire, and on Thursdays he sits across from me and gives them back one at a time, slowly, testing each one like a man handing back someone else's laundry.

"You kept a chipped blue cup," he said recently, "and you refused to replace it, and when Ilsa-the-younger broke it you were furious for three days, and then you mended it yourself with hedge-glue and it was uglier than before, and you liked it better."

"Do I still have it?"

"It's in your room. Third shelf. You look at it every morning and don't know why."

I went and looked. There it was. Ugly as sin, seam of glue like a scar across the blue. I held it and felt absolutely nothing, and the nothing was somehow full, the way an empty well is full of echoes.

"That's a horrible gift," I told him.

"I know." He almost smiled, which for Gideon constitutes a religious experience. "Want it anyway next week?"

"Yes."

* * *

But the ones I wait for — and I confess this, I, who supposedly have no self left to be vain with, wait for these the way the tide waits for the moon — the ones I wait for come from strangers.

Because here is the arithmetic nobody warned me about: I fed the fire forty years of memories, yes, but the fire wasn't just eating. All those years, the fire was also *keeping*. Every name read into the flames, every grief offered up at the shrine, every small prayer of the drowned city — the fire held them. And when the fire passed into Gideon, and Gideon opened the Door, and the erasure reversed — all of that came home. Scattered now, distributed, living in ten thousand heads across two countries, but alive. My devotions bore fruit I'll never taste directly. Instead it arrives by post.

Letters. That's the form it takes. People write to me — the priestess who forgot, the blank one, Saint Empty-Hands as the children irreverently call me — and they give me back pieces of what I fed away.

*A woman in the Tidemark district writes:* Your fire kept my grandfather's name for thirty years. He was Errol Fenn. He repaired nets. When the erasure took him, you took him in. Here is a memory of him, returned with interest: he taught me to tie a bowline when I was six, and his hands smelled of tar, and he hummed when he worked, always the same song, and I only learned the song's name last month because your Door opened and suddenly my mother could sing it again. The song is called "The Salt Breaks." It is yours now, if you want it. I hope you want it. You paid for it.

I want it. I want all of it. I am the richest pauper in the world, a beggar with a treasury, a woman who owns thousands of lives she can't remember owning. Some mornings I sit with a stack of letters taller than the teapot and I laugh until I wheeze, because it's *funny*, damn it all, it's genuinely funny — Vess built an entire empire on the principle that forgetting is permanent, that what's erased stays erased, and here I am, the most erased person in the country, being *remembered at* daily, against my will, by mail.

Healing as a communal act. Gideon said that once, or I'm told he did, and it's become the closest thing I have to a creed. Nobody heals alone. Not a country, not a priestess, not a boy who turned his lantern the wrong way. We heal each other, in shifts, like tending a fire — which is fitting, because that's the job I trained for, and it turns out I never left it. I just changed congregations. Now the congregation tends *me*.

* * *

Last week — I have this on good authority from three separate witnesses whose accounts disagree charmingly on the details — something happened at the festival that I want set down before it grows ornaments.

The reading had gone past midnight, as it does, and the fire was spelling names in the air over the flats, and the crowd had gone into that deep quiet that isn't silence but listening. And then the fire stopped spelling names.

It spelled a sentence.

Witnesses agree on the words, if not the order. The fire doesn't do grammar; it does emphasis. But the meaning came through clean, and the meaning was:

*SHE KEPT US. NOW WE KEEP HER.*

And then — this is the part that made grown fishermen weep into their beer — the flame bent low over the flats, toward the bench where they always seat me, and it wrote one more word. Small. Almost shy, if a century of accumulated dead can be shy.

*WREN.*

Not my daughter's name spoken *to* me. Her name given *back*. From inside the fire, where I sent it, carried by the remembered dead who had held it safe all this time the way I had held them — returned with interest, exactly like the letters say.

They tell me I stood up. They tell me I said, in a clear voice, "Well. About time somebody answered my letters."

And then I cried, allegedly, for a long while, surrounded by people who owed me everything and were paying it back in the oldest currency there is: presence. Just staying. Just witnessing. Nobody tried to fix me. Nobody told me not to cry. They simply made a ring around me, the way you make a windbreak around a young flame, and let the weather pass.

I don't remember any of it. I remember being told about it, many times, warmly, and I have noticed something strange: the retellings are starting to *settle*. The story of that night is developing texture in me, weight, the grain of lived things. Perhaps enough retellings become a memory. Perhaps that's all any memory ever was — a story told enough times, by enough voices, that the self agrees to carry it.

If so, then I am not empty. I am *crowded*. There is a difference, and the difference is everyone.

* * *

This morning, Wren's name came at breakfast, as it does. Maren said it — it was Thursday, so actually it was Gideon, hunched over his tea, delivering the daily installment in his flat careful voice — and then he added something new.

"There's a letter for you," he said. "Came with the dawn packet. It's not a memory-return." He slid it across the table, and I noticed his hands, and his hands were not steady, and Gideon's hands are always steady; they're the steadiest things in Saltmark. "It's from the valley. From Odile's valley. Someone there has begun to dream the fire's cadence — dreams in your voice, they say. Dreams about a door."

I took the letter. The seal was plain wax, valley-made, ash-grey.

"The door?" I said.

"The Door Out," Gideon said. "It's supposed to be resting. I keep it open a crack, just a crack, for the teaching." He looked out the window toward the salt flats, where the Emberhold glowed faintly even in daylight, like a coal that has opinions. "Something on the other side is knocking, Ilsa. Something that talks like the fire. Something that says it's been waiting a hundred years for us to finish the prophecy."

I broke the seal. Inside, in a child's careful hand, were four words:

*Come teach us to burn.*

I read it twice. Then I laughed — my own laugh, unprompted, unreported, rising up out of the empty well and startling us both — and Gideon stared at me the way he stares at anything that won't fit in a ledger.

"Well," I said, standing, well-full of echoes, crowded with ten thousand lives, richer than any priestess the Emberhold ever fed. "Fetch the others. It seems the fire has one more name to spell — and this time, I intend to be there when it writes it."
