## 22. The Door Stands Open

The Door Out has a habit of creaking.

Not the ominous kind of creak — I'd know the difference by now, believe me. This is the ordinary complaint of an ancient hinge that has decided, after a thousand years of standing shut and one year of standing open, that it is entitled to opinions about weather. On damp mornings it groans like an old man getting out of a chair. On windy ones it hums a low note that Maren swears is in no scale used by any living culture, which she says with the particular satisfaction of a woman who has personally catalogued every scale used by every living culture and several dead ones besides.

This morning it was humming.

I stood in the doorway with two cups of barley tea, one for me and one for the door, because you don't get to be the last heir of the fire-keepers without developing some eccentricities, and honestly the door had earned a cup. It had held against a fleet, an inquisitor-general, and Tam's attempt to hang a sign on it that read NO SOLICITING. The tea steamed out over the threshold, into the wind coming off the world, and I watched it scatter the way steam does when nothing is eating it.

That's how you know magic has come back gentle. In the old days — my days, the valley days — steam just sat there, sulking. Now it dances.

"Teacher." The word arrived before the boy did, the way it always did. Pell was twelve, gap-toothed, and possessed of the unshakable belief that arriving early made him my favorite, which was true, but not for that reason. He came up the salt road at a run, satchel bouncing, hair full of the fine white grit that Saltmark shed like dandruff from its towers. "Teacher, I did the exercise."

"You did the exercise wrong."

"I did it *enthusiastically* wrong," he said, skidding to a halt in front of me and holding up his hand, in the palm of which sat a small flame, blue-white at the heart, shaped — vaguely, generously speaking — like a bird. "It's supposed to be a wren."

"It's supposed to be *held away from your face*, is what it's supposed to be." I took his wrist and moved the fire out to arm's length, and he grinned at me with the total absence of fear that used to make Odile sigh and now makes me do it instead. Somewhere along this year I had inherited her sighs along with everything else. There are worse inheritances. She left me a whole valley of them.

"It flickered all night," Pell said. "In the jar. Like it was practicing."

Fire doesn't practice. Fire remembers. But I looked at the little wren-that-was-not-a-wren burning steady in a child's palm, in a country that had been erased so thoroughly that its own name had to be sung back into existence by a dragon's dying breath, and I thought: well. Maybe it does both.

"Again," I said, "from the top. And this time pretend the wren is shy."

* * *

We held lessons on the steps of what had been the customs house, back when Saltmark had customs and houses and a government to be disappointed in. The steps were broad and salt-bleached and faced the harbor, where the water had finally agreed to be water again instead of whatever ambiguous erasure-tide it had been during the bad century. Six students this season. Six. A year ago that number would have seemed like a miracle; now it felt like a beginning, which is a different and better kind of miracle, the kind you can build on.

Ilsa sat at the top step, as always, wrapped in gray wool despite the warming air, watching everything with the wide patient attention of a woman who had given away forty years of memories and was now collecting new ones the way other people collect shells. Someone brought her something every day. Yesterday it had been Tam, who told her about the garrison school he was teaching — actual history, actual maps, actual names — and she had listened and said, "Tell me again tomorrow," because she knew she wouldn't keep it, and wanted to hear it fresh anyway. That's the discipline of her. That's the holiness, if I'm honest, though she'd box my ears for saying holy.

Today it was Maren who climbed the steps at midmorning with a leather tube under her arm and the expression of someone delivering either good news or an invoice.

"The survey commission approved the coastal charts," she announced, to the group at large, in the tone of a woman refusing to be moved by her own triumph. "Saltmark appears on them. Name, borders, harbors, the lot. First official imperial cartography to include us since—" she paused, recalculated, "—ever, technically, since they never admitted we existed the first time."

"And?" Pell prompted, because everyone in Saltmark had learned within a month that Maren never delivered news without a second layer.

"And they've asked me to draw the interior." Her jaw worked. "All of it. Not the ruins. The *country*. They want a map of a place they erased, drawn by the woman they exiled for looking at it." She set the tube down on the step between her boots, carefully, the way you set down something that might still be listening. "I told them I'd need a year."

"A year?" one of the older students asked.

"To do it properly," Maren said, and there it was — the thing under the thing, the reason she'd really come. She glanced up at me, and for once the great disgraced cartographer of nothing looked almost shy. "And I'll need to start in the Valley of Ash. There are springs up there nobody's charted. Old boundary stones. If the valley's waking up, someone should map what it's waking into."

I looked at her for a long moment. She looked back with the flat defiance she used instead of asking permission.

"You're asking if you can borrow my childhood," I said.

"I'm asking if you want to come with me," she said. "It's your map too, Zhou. More yours than mine."

I didn't answer right away, because the honest answer was complicated and I've learned — slowly, painfully, at the cost of several small fires and one medium-sized one — that honesty goes down easier with tea. I handed her my cup instead. She drank. That was answer enough for both of us, for now.

* * *

Afternoons were for the hard lessons.

Anyone can teach a child to hold a flame. What I taught, or tried to, was the part Odile had skipped with me — not out of cruelty, I understood now, but out of terror. She'd hidden me from the truth-eaters by hiding my power from myself, and the hiding had cost her a century and cost me twenty years of believing I was less than I was. So I taught the part she couldn't: how to want things.

That sounds simple. It is not simple. Hedge-magic runs on wanting — real wanting, specific wanting, the kind that knows exactly what shape it's asking for and accepts exactly what it costs. The valley had starved me of it for so long that when I first came to Saltmark and my charms started working again, I'd flinched from every spark like it was an accusation. My students didn't have a dead valley behind them. But they had something else: a country that had been taught for a hundred years, in the deep grammar of its bones, that it wasn't allowed to exist. Wanting came hard to them too. We were learning together.

"Say what you want," I told them, perched on the customs house rail while six small fires guttered and steadied below me. "Out loud. The whole sentence. Not 'light.' Not 'burn.' Say the true sentence."

Pell went first, as always. "I want the wren to fly."

His flame leapt off his palm, wobbled, gained something almost like wings, crossed three feet of air, and burst into sparks over the harbor steps. The students cheered. Pell turned to me, incandescent with triumph, wearing the exact expression I imagined I'd worn at fourteen when a charm worked for the first time, before I ran from it, before I spent half a life mending broken charms in a town where nothing followed anyone home.

"That," I said, when I could speak evenly, "was the truest sentence anyone's said all day."

From the top step, Ilsa laughed — a sound like a bell wrapped in wool. She didn't remember why she was laughing. She remembered that laughing was worth doing anyway. Some days I think she understands more than any of us.

* * *

Evenings belonged to the book.

I kept it on a shelf above my hearth, in the cottage I'd built halfway between the Door and the sea — close enough to hear both, far enough from either to sleep. *When the Salt Breaks Again*, the tattered thing that had washed up in a millrace in a valley where nothing was supposed to follow anyone, and followed me anyway, across a boundary that couldn't be crossed, into a country that didn't exist, toward a page that hadn't been written yet.

For a year the last page had been blank. I checked it most nights, the way you check a wound that's healing — gently, expecting nothing, grateful for quiet. Blank meant unwritten. Unwritten meant free. Odile had filled that book a century ago with a future she engineered down to the date of my arrival, and the only mercy in the whole mad scheme was that she'd run out of ink somewhere past the breaking of the salt. Whatever came next, she'd left to me.

Most nights: blank.

Tonight I took it down after the students had gone home, after Maren had rolled up her charts and Tam had finished arguing with me about whether fourteen-year-olds should be allowed to learn wardwork (he said yes; I said ask me when they're fifteen; we'd been having this argument weekly and enjoying it), after the door had hummed itself hoarse at the evening wind and gone quiet.

I opened to the last page.

The lamp flickered. Not dramatically — I want to be clear about that, because dramatic flickers have meant terrible things in my life, and this was not one of them. Just the ordinary lean of a flame toward a draft, except there was no draft, except the flame leaned anyway, and on the blank page, in ink that surfaced like something rising through still water rather than being written by any hand—

A date.

Tomorrow's date.

I sat down on the hearthstone and looked at it for a long time. My heart was doing something complicated, part dread and part something I didn't have a name for yet, and then I looked closer and found the name for it, because beside the date, where a sketch of a frightened young man's face had appeared a year ago tonight — beside the date there was nothing. No face. No figure. No boy who was never taught to want anything.

Just the date, and beneath it, faint as breath on glass, a single line:

*The door stands open.*

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Somewhere, a century deep in whatever comes after centuries, I felt certain Odile heard it and was annoyed, and then — because I knew her better now, knew the terrified architect underneath the terrifying teacher — relieved.

She'd written my face on that page because she'd needed me found. Tomorrow's page had no face because tomorrow didn't need finding. It needed making. By whoever showed up. With whatever wanting we could carry.

I put the book back on its shelf, open, so the morning light could reach it.

Then I banked the fire — my fire, the last dragon-fire, sleeping coals-deep in the hearth and in my chest and, these days, faintly, in six pairs of student palms — and I stepped outside into the salt-sweet dark, where the towers sang their low night song and the Door Out stood open behind me and the whole unwritten country lay ahead, waiting for tomorrow, which had finally, at long last, no idea what was coming.

Neither did I.

I'd never been happier about anything in my life.
