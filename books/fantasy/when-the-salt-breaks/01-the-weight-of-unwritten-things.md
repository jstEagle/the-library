## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Things

The rain came to Corvane's Reach that evening the way all weather came to the free cities—late, apologetic, and half-hearted. Ivo Ashgrove heard it before she felt it: a low grumble rolling in off the eastern hills, dragging its thunder behind it like a reluctant dog. Her pen stopped mid-stroke. A bead of ink swelled at the nib and fell, blooming a small black flower across the ledger's margin.

She counted her breaths the way she'd taught herself to count them, nine years ago, on the road out of a country that no longer had a name. One for the sound of thunder. Two for the prickle along her forearms where the skin remembered lightning. Three for the taste of ozone rising at the back of her tongue like a word she refused to speak.

Four. Five. Six.

By ten, the storm-sense had settled into something bearable—a faint itch, no more. The thunder moved on westward, grumbling its complaints to the harbor, and Ivo blotted the ruined margin with a scrap of linen and returned to her figures.

*Seventy-two impossibilities received this quarter,* the ledger said, in her small, deliberately unremarkable hand. *Eleven sold. Four returned by dissatisfied buyers. Two expired.* She wrote the word *expired* with particular care. It was kinder than the alternative, which was *died*, and the Guild of Impossible Things was nothing if not kind in its paperwork.

She worked by the light of a single whale-oil lamp in the counting room's far corner, because the far corner was where the light didn't reach and therefore where nobody looked. This was not an accident. Ivo had spent nine years constructing herself into precisely the sort of person who occupied dark corners: a woman of middling height and forgettable face, hair bound in a knot so severe it seemed less a style than an apology, dressed in the grey-brown livery of a guild clerk with the guild crest unpicked from her collar because even that much distinction felt like exposure. She had practiced her walk until it announced nothing. She had sanded the Vhaleni vowels out of her accent one syllable at a time, though some nights, alone, she let them return to her mouth like birds coming home to roost, just to hear what she used to sound like.

Her name in the guild rolls was Ivo Ashgrove, junior ledger-keeper, third of four, unremarkable. It was a name like a locked door. Behind it lived other names she did not use.

"Ledger-keeper." The voice belonged to Master Quillon, the counting room's day-warden, a man whose belly preceded him into rooms by several seconds. He stood at her corner now, breathing through his nose in the manner of someone about to deliver bad news he had already decided was not his fault. "Master Corvane wants the deep archive audited. All of it. Before quarter's end."

Ivo kept her eyes on her columns. "The deep archive hasn't been audited in eleven years."

"Yes," Quillon said, with the satisfaction of a man who has located the exact center of a problem and intends to stand on it. "That would be why it needs auditing. There's talk of new charter inspections from the free cities' council. Someone upstairs wants everything in order. Everything accounted for." He dropped a brass key ring onto her desk with a clatter that made her flinch—only inwardly; outwardly her hand merely paused. "Yours now. Don't lose them. Some of those vaults haven't been opened since the old master's time."

*Some things down there have been waiting a long time to be found,* she thought, and did not let the thought reach her face. "When do you want the first report?"

"Yesterday," Quillon said, which was his way of saying *whenever*, and departed, his belly rounding the corner of the shelving well after the rest of him.

Ivo sat a while in the lamplight, listening to the counting room breathe. It was a vast room, the heart of the Guild of Impossible Things, though the guild's heart had always been more administrative than romantic. Shelves rose three stories in ranks, crammed with ledgers, each spine coded in the guild's cipher: blue for impossibilities held in trust, red for impossibilities sold, black for those whose ownership was disputed or unknowable. Somewhere above, clerks were cataloguing this week's acquisitions—a jar containing the last echo of a dead man's laughter, purchased from a widow in Sennet; a compass that pointed always toward whatever the holder had lost most recently; a key that fit no lock yet opened every door it was ashamed of. The free cities traded in such things the way other nations traded in grain. Impossibility, properly ledgered, was simply another commodity.

Ivo had learned, over nine years, to keep her hands steady around such objects. What she had never learned was how to stop listening to them.

She gathered her lamp, the key ring, and a fresh auditing folio, and descended.

* * *

The deep archive lay beneath the counting room, down stairs worn concave by a century of clerks' feet. The air changed as she went—cooler, saltier, tasting faintly of the sea that had once, according to the oldest maps, come much closer to Corvane's Reach than it did now. The lamps along the stair burned without flickering, fed by the guild's careful arrangements, and their light fell on shelf after shelf after shelf, receding into a darkness that was not empty but *full*—full of boxes, jars, sealed cases, and the patient weight of things waiting to be impossible somewhere again.

Ivo began at Vault One, as method demanded. She checked seals against seals, entries against entries, ticking her folio in her small hand. Most of it was mundane by guild standards: bottled dreams past their potency, letters written by people who did not yet exist, a taxidermied bird that sang only songs its owner had whistled badly. She recorded each with the flat precision that was her armor. Numbers did not remember. Columns did not ache. This was why she loved them.

It was in Vault Nine, near midnight, that the audit went wrong.

The vault was narrow, its shelves crowded, its air stale with the particular dust of paper that has not been touched in years. The ledger for Vault Nine lay chained to its post—the old system, predating even Quillon—and when Ivo unhooked the chain and opened the cover, the binding cracked like a knuckle. The handwriting inside shifted twice across the decades, three different clerks, and she followed their entries dutifully down the page:

*A mirror that shows the viewer honest. Sold, year 401, buyer withheld.*

*A debt forgiven so thoroughly it became a physical object. Held in trust.*

*A door that opens only from the other side. Status unknown.*

And then, in the middle clerk's hand—tighter, quicker, as if written fast and wished unwritten faster:

*One (1) country. Complete. Sold entire, year 397. Buyer: the Iron Charter Company, agents for His Radiant Majesty Aldric Vane. Consideration rendered. Provenance: contested.*

*Contents removed from archive. Location of contents: everywhere and nowhere. See attached.*

There was no attachment.

Ivo read the entry three times. The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the stair, and shadows swung across the page, and still the words did not change. *One country. Complete. Sold entire.*

Year 397. Thirty-one years ago. She knew the date—not from history books, which were vague on the matter, but from arithmetic. Year 397 was nine years before she was born. It was the year the maps of the eastern coast had gone quietly strange, cartographers leaving a blank space where their grandfathers had drawn harbors, and calling the blank space an error of the old school. It was the year, if you believed the maddest of the drowned god's defrocked priests, that a country had been bought the way one buys a warehouse: whole, with fixtures.

Vhalen.

The name arrived in her mind unbidden, the way thunder arrives—already overhead before you hear it. Ivo closed her eyes. The storm-sense stirred, faint and far away, though there was no weather underground. She pressed her thumbnail hard into the pad of her finger, a small pain to anchor against the larger one, and made herself think in columns instead.

*Item:* one country. *Status:* sold. *Buyer:* Aldric Vane, who styled himself His Radiant Majesty and had, within a decade of the purchase, issued charters declaring the territory east of the Greywater "unimproved wilderness, open to settlement." Unimproved. As if forty generations of orchards and bell-towers and salt-gardens were merely a failure of paperwork.

She had been told once, by the woman who smuggled her out, that forgetting was the usurper's true weapon—not fire, not armies, though he used both. Charters. Ledgers. Maps amended. Names struck from shipping manifests so that no ship carried goods *from* anywhere, because *anywhere* no longer existed. The free cities had signed the accords willingly enough; trade flowed better without complications. And so a country had been sold entire, consideration rendered, provenance contested, and the contest had been won by whoever controlled the records.

Ivo had built her life inside records. She should have known better than anyone that they could be rewritten.

But there was something else, something that made her hands cold despite the lamp's warmth. The entry was in the middle clerk's hand. And Ivo had spent eleven years in this guild reading every clerk's hand, studying them the way other people studied faces, because handwriting was the one signature no one thought to disguise. She recognized this one. It appeared elsewhere in the archive—in marginalia, in corrections, in a note pinned inside a box of letters written by people who did not yet exist. It was a distinctive hand, slanted leftward, the letters leaning like trees in a wind.

It was, mirrored—written right-to-left, reversed stroke for stroke—her own handwriting.

She had noticed it years ago, that odd kinship between her script and some long-gone clerk's, and had told herself it was coincidence, the way family resemblances are coincidence. She had told herself many things, nine years ago and since. That was the art she had mastered: not invisibility of the body, but of the story. She had made herself a woman with no east in her, no salt, no bells. A ledger-keeper. Third of four. Unremarkable.

Now the archive she tended had opened its mouth and spoken the one word she had spent a decade teaching it not to say.

Ivo stood in Vault Nine until the lamp burned low. Then she did what a good clerk does with a discrepancy: she recorded it. In her folio, in her small hand, she entered the item number, the date of sale, the buyer, and beneath it, in the space reserved for auditor's notes, she wrote: *Entry appears anomalous. Recommend further review.*

Then, after a moment, she added: *Provenance: contested.*

She closed the folio. She rehung the chain. And as she climbed the stairs toward the ordinary world—toward Quillon's complaints and cold tea and a narrow bed in the clerks' dormitory—she felt the ache arrive, the old ache, the one she had named *deliberate smallness* and tried to make peace with. It was the ache of living folded up, a letter never sent. She had chosen it. She reminded herself she had chosen it. Smallness was safety; safety was survival; survival was the whole of her ambition, and it had been enough, mostly, for nine years.

Mostly.

At the top of the stairs, the counting room lay silent under its shelves of impossible things. Through the high windows, the last of the storm was passing out over the harbor, and a final mutter of thunder rolled across the rooftops—and with it, faint as a bell heard underwater, Ivo felt the storm-sense turn in her chest like a key in a lock she'd hoped was rusted shut.

Below her feet, thirty-one years of dust settled back over Vault Nine. Above her head, the rain began again in earnest.

And on the desk in her far corner, where she had left her current ledger open to dry, the ink of her neat columns had run—just slightly, just enough—so that the numbers looked, in the lamplight, almost like writing in a language she had sworn she'd forgotten.

Three days later, a stranger came through the guild's front door bleeding, and asked for the keeper of the deep archive by a name Ivo had not heard spoken aloud in nine years.
