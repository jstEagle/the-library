## 3. Ledgers of the Erased

They took the map before they took her statement, and Ivo understood which one Corvane valued more.

The counting room had been cleared by noon. Warden's chalk still ghosted the floorboards where the stranger had fallen—a rough circle, the sigils of containment half-scuffed by boots—and someone had laid a linen sheet over what remained of him, though the salt smell would not be covered so easily. It hung in the air like a held breath. Salt and iron. Salt and old paper. Salt and something underneath both that Ivo refused to name.

She sat in the chair across from Corvane's desk with her hands folded in her lap because she had learned, nine years ago, that hands were the first thing to betray you. Her ledger lay closed beside her elbow. She had asked to bring it the way a drowning woman asks for a plank—not for use, but for the comfort of its weight.

Corvane read.

He was not a large man. He was the kind of large that happened around a person rather than inside them: broad robes, wide desk, a presence that filled a room the way water fills a hull—quietly, inevitably, from below. His fingers were stained the brownish gold of decades spent handling vellum and cheap ink, and he turned pages as if each one were a debt he intended personally to collect. The map lay open beneath his lamp, and he studied it with the patience of a man who had never once been surprised by anything worth being surprised about.

"You knew him," Corvane said, without looking up.

"No."

"You knew *of* him."

"I've never seen that man before in my life." This was true. It was also, Ivo suspected, the kind of truth that Corvane could smell the way sharks smelled blood—not the fact of it but the fear wrapped around it. She made her voice bored. "He came in bleeding. He put a thing on my desk. He died. That is the entire sum of my acquaintance."

"Mm." Corvane turned a page. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, thunder rolled over the free cities—far off, a summer storm worrying the mountains—and Ivo felt the prickle start at the base of her skull, the old itch, the old *listening*, and she crushed it down the way she crushed every morning the urge to look at the sky. Nine years of practice. She was very good at it. She was an artist of it.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," Corvane said, "trades in objects whose provenance cannot be established. That is our entire business model, Ivo. We are paid precisely because we do not ask where things come from." He looked up at last, and his eyes were pale and patient as river stones. "And yet every item we accept must be *catalogued*. Every impossible thing gets a number, an entry, a shelf. Do you know why?"

"Order," Ivo said. "You've told us. Order is the only miracle we can sell twice."

"Order," he agreed, "is the only miracle we can sell twice." He tapped the map once, lightly, with one ink-browned finger. "This object has no entry. No number. No shelf. It arrived in my guild through no registered hand, into the keeping of a ledger-keeper who has filed forty thousand entries without error, and within the hour there were men at my door wearing the colors of the Vhalen Inquest—which dissolved thirty years ago, Ivo, or did you know?—asking after it by *name*."

"I don't know any names."

"They said 'Vhalen.'"

The word went through her like a fishhook going through soft flesh. She kept her face still. She had been keeping her face still since she was eleven years old, when she'd learned that a storm-caller's daughter who flinched got noticed, and a girl who got noticed got questions, and questions in Vhalen—

*Don't.* Not here. Not now.

"I've heard it," she said. "In songs. Children's rhymes. There's a counting rhyme—*one for the salt and two for the sea*—"

"There is no such rhyme."

"There is. Pip—" She stopped herself. Too late; she heard the shape of it leave her mouth.

"Pip," Corvane repeated softly. "The child in your quarter. The one who sings songs nobody taught him." He folded his hands atop the map, and for a moment he simply looked at her, and Ivo had the distinct sensation of being audited—of being an entry in someone else's book, a line item under some column she couldn't see the heading of. "You understand my difficulty, Ivo Ashgrove. A man dies in my guild speaking a country's name that does not exist. An artifact appears that predates the erasure. And the inquisition arrives asking questions with the particular urgency of men who already know the answers. You will forgive me if I do not find your ignorance entirely convincing."

"Forgiveness is extra," Ivo said. "It's in the rate card."

Something moved at the corner of Corvane's mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been the beginning of a decision. With Corvane, she thought, those two things had always been difficult to tell apart.

* * *

They kept her three hours.

Not in a cell—the Guild of Impossible Things did not have cells; it had *storage*, and the distinction was apparently important to someone—but in a back room between a crate of bottled last words and a shelf of mirrors that showed rooms other than the ones they reflected. Ivo sat on a ledger-box and listened to the building settle and counted her breathing the way she'd been taught to count wave-crests, and she did not think about the map.

She thought about the map constantly.

She had seen it whole for perhaps four seconds before the wardens pried it out of the stranger's grip—from hers, really; she'd been holding it, she realized later, she had been *already holding it*, her fingers closing over it before her mind had given permission—and four seconds was enough. Enough to see the coastline drawn in a hand that shook with pain. Enough to see the margins.

The margins were what undid her.

Someone had written in them—small annotations, corrections, little arguments with the cartographer. *Not this ford. The bridge was nearer the millstone.* *Grandmother said the bells rang east in autumn, west in spring.* And the handwriting had been mirrored, reversed, written right-to-left across the page like text seen through wet glass, and Ivo had known it instantly, the way you know your own face in a reflection.

Because it was her handwriting.

Or it had been. Once. When she was small, before anyone told her that writing backward was strange, that a storm-caller's heir should write forward like everyone else, she had filled pages with mirrored script—a private alphabet, a secret language of one—and she had not written a single reversed letter in nine years, and yet there it was on a dead man's map, in her own childhood hand, annotating roads she could not possibly remember.

Unless she could.

Unless she did, and the remembering was buried so deep and so carefully that even she couldn't reach it—but someone else could. Someone had copied it. Someone had taken a child's marginalia—an archivist's copy of a child's notes—and pressed it into her hands while bleeding out on her counting-room floor, and whispered *the door only opens for the last one who remembers*, and died saying a name she had sworn, on salt and on stone, never to say again.

*I didn't choose this,* she told the dark of the storage room. *I chose smallness. I chose ledgers. I chose a life where the worst thing that can happen is a misfiled entry.*

The mirrors on the shelf across from her showed her a room with rain in it. She looked away.

* * *

Corvane came for her himself, near evening, carrying two cups of tea and the particular courtesy of a man about to be either very generous or very final.

"Archivist," he said, setting a cup before her. "That answers one question, anyway. The corpse. Our visitors left before we could question them further, but their colleagues were less discreet in Lowmarsh—there was a killing there yesterday, a woman who kept a paper shop. Also an archivist. Also Vhaleni, whatever that means these days." He sipped his own tea. "It seems the inquisition has developed a sudden appetite for people who keep records of a country no one is supposed to have records of."

Ivo held her tea and did not drink it and watched the steam rise in the lamplight. *A paper shop in Lowmarsh. A woman. Dead.* The guilt arrived quietly, the way floodwater arrives—no drama, just a slow rising against the shins, against the knees, cold and patient. Because the man had come to *her*. Of all the desks in all the free cities, he had crossed a bleeding room to press that map into her hands, and now others like him were dying in shops and doorways for holding lesser things, and the difference between them and her was nothing but luck and a warden's chalk circle drawn too late.

"Why me?" she said, before she could stop herself.

"Ah." Corvane set down his cup. "There it is. The first honest question you've asked me all day."

"That's not an answer."

"It isn't. But it tells me where to dig." He leaned forward, lamplight pooling in the hollows of his face. "I am going to offer you something, Ivo, and I want you to listen carefully, because I will only be this generous once. The men from the inquisition want the map. I have given them the map—or a map. The Guild of Impossible Things keeps excellent copies." His smile was thin as a ledger line. "What they also want is the person the map was meant for. They described her, actually. A woman who keeps books. Young. Careful. Who came to the free cities nine years ago with no history and bought her anonymity with perfect, flawless, *boring* competence." He spread his ink-stained hands. "I told them I had no such employee. I lied to an inquisitor's face, which is not something I do lightly, and which I will expect you to repay."

The room was very quiet. In the next chamber, the bottled last words murmured to themselves, dozens of voices like wind through reeds.

"Why?" Ivo said. "Why lie?"

"Because I run a guild of impossible things, Ivo Ashgrove, and a woman who is hunted by an empire for a country that officially never existed is the most valuable impossible thing to walk through my doors in twenty years. You are an *asset*. Assets are catalogued. Assets are protected." He picked up his tea again, delicately. "Assets are also expected to be forthcoming. So. One more time, and then I stop being generous: the man who died in your counting room. What did he give you?"

"Nothing," Ivo said. "He gave me nothing. He was dying. People say things when they're dying. Words. Nonsense words."

"What words?"

And here it was—the hinge, the narrow gate, the moment she could feel her whole hidden life balanced upon. Tell him the name. Let Corvane add her to his catalogue, file her under assets, trade her eventually the way the guild traded everything, because Corvane was Corvane and everything in the world was stock to him sooner or later. Or lie. Lie perfectly, flawlessly, boringly, the way she had lied for nine years, and go back to her ledgers, and wait for the inquisition to find another way through the guild's doors, and let the dead archivist's map walk away in someone else's hands toward a door she would never see.

She opened her mouth to lie.

"He said a name," she heard herself say instead. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else's voice heard through water. "A country's name. He said it like—" Like it was heavy. Like it was a stone he'd been carrying up a mountain. Like it was the last thing he owned and he wanted to spend it well. "He said *Vhalen*."

Corvane nodded slowly, unsurprised, and wrote something in a small black book, and Ivo sat with the taste of the name in her own mouth for the first time in nine years—salt and smoke and a mother's kitchen, rain on slate roofs, the sound of bells that were under the earth now or under the water or nowhere at all—and hated how much of her rose up to meet it. How much had been waiting. All this time, all this careful smallness, and some locked room inside her had simply been standing at a window, watching the road.

"You'll stay in the guild tonight," Corvane said, closing the little book. "Wardens at both doors. Tomorrow you'll return to your ledgers as if none of this occurred, because as far as the guild is concerned, none of it did. And Ivo—" He paused at the threshold, and for just a moment the merchant fell away and she saw something older and wearier underneath, a man who had spent a lifetime trading in other people's wonders and keeping accounts of what it cost them. "The inquisitor who came asking. Marrec. Sergeant Dain Marrec. I have dealt with his kind before. They are not cruel men, usually. They are *thorough* men, and thoroughness, given time and authority, arrives at cruelty the way a river arrives at the sea. Do not make yourself interesting to him."

He left. The lamps burned low. In the mirror-shelf, a room full of rain flickered and went dark.

Ivo sat alone among the impossible things with an empty cup going cold in her hands, and finally, finally allowed herself to say it—barely a breath, barely a sound, shaped more by memory than voice:

"Vhalen."

And somewhere below the floor, below the cellar, below the deep shelves where the guild stored the things too dangerous to sell—she would have sworn it, afterward, if anyone had asked—something answered. A hum. A bell-note, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, rising up through the foundations like a name called up from under water.

Outside, over the free cities, the distant summer storm rolled closer.

And Ivo Ashgrove, keeper of forty thousand flawless entries, reached for her ledger and found that her hands were shaking, and understood with terrible clarity that the small life she had built was already over—that it had ended four seconds ago in a counting room, in the space of a dying man's breath—and that the only choice remaining to her was whether to be dragged into what came next, or to walk.

She did not sleep. She sharpened a pencil. She opened a fresh page.

And began, in mirrored letters, right to left, to write down everything she could remember.
