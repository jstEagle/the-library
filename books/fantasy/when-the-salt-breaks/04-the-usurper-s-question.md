## 4. The Usurper's Question

The archives of Correnvale burned on a Tuesday, in the orderly way that all of Sergeant Dain Marrec's work proceeded: by appointment, with permits, and under the watchful eyes of men who signed their names to what they destroyed.

He had arrived at dawn with twelve riders and a warrant sealed in black wax—the usurper's own signet, a crown drawn over an empty circle. The archivist, a stooped woman named Ellery Vance whose fingers were stained forty years deep with ink, had read the warrant twice, then folded her hands atop the reading desk as though it were a funeral.

"You'll want the western stacks first," she said. "That's where we kept the genealogies."

Marrec appreciated this. In eleven months of service to the Ministry of Records Reconciliation, he had learned that the ones who fought him made the work long, and the ones who helped him made it unbearable.

"The genealogies," he said. "All of them?"

"All of them." She looked out the high window at the light coming up over the free cities' slate roofs, gilded now, ordinary, a morning like ten thousand other mornings in a town that did not yet know its history was ending. "There was a family here once that could call rain out of a clear sky. Farmers, mostly. Good people. You'd never know it from the ledgers—we weren't allowed to write it down even then."

"Then there is nothing to burn," Marrec said, "if you never wrote it down."

Ellery Vance smiled at him for the first time, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a woman who understood precisely what he was and had decided, somewhere in the night while he slept at the Wayfarer's Rest, how she meant to spend her last hours of employment. "Oh, Sergeant. We wrote everything down. That's what an archive *is*. We just wrote it slantwise. In the margins. In the misfilings. In books catalogued under names they don't belong to." She rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "You can burn every page in this building, and you will still be burning a country. You just won't be able to read which one."

* * *

He let her watch. This too was procedure—Ministry directive 114, *witnessing permitted, participation forbidden*—and Marrec had come to believe, in his careful and unexamined way, that the directive existed less as mercy than as instruction. The regime wanted witnesses. A fire no one saw was only heat; a fire seen and survived became a story, and stories, properly shaped, became silence. People who had watched an archive burn learned to stop asking questions near archives. Learned, eventually, to stop asking them anywhere.

So Ellery Vance stood on the cobbles with her stained hands clasped behind her back while his soldiers carried the western stacks into the street and fed them to a brazier built for the purpose, iron-bellied, chimneyed, designed to consume paper without scattering embers into the surrounding rooftops. Even his arson was tidy. He took a certain grim satisfaction in that. Whatever else the Ministry had made of him, it had not made him sloppy.

He stood beside her, cataloguing.

This was the part of the work no one spoke about but everyone knew: the sergeant's duty was not merely to burn but to *read*. Every document passed through his hands before it passed into flame, and he was expected to extract whatever the Ministry might want—names, dates, discrepancies, patterns. Eleven months ago this had seemed like simple diligence. Now it felt like being asked to memorize a face before drowning it.

A genealogy of the Correnvale tanners, six generations, each entry initialed by a parish priest long dead. A shipping manifest from the river towns, forty years old, listing cargo in a script he almost recognized—loops reversed, ascenders doubled—as though someone had taught children to write their letters backward so the words would keep secrets. A census fragment, water-damaged, in which an entire district's population had been recorded as *unaccounted* for three consecutive years, though the district itself appeared nowhere in the column headers.

And a single sheet, tucked inside a ledger of grain tariffs, bearing one word in faded violet ink:

*Vhalen.*

Not crossed out. Not corrected. Just written, once, as if the clerk had set it down to see whether it would stay.

Marrec held it over the brazier longer than he needed to. Long enough to feel the heat climb his wrist. The word sat in its small rectangle of surviving ink and refused to mean anything, because meaning required context and the Ministry had spent nine years systematically removing context from the world the way a surgeon removes shrapnel—one piece at a time, carefully, until the patient forgot there had ever been a wound.

"Sergeant." Corporal Hesk, at his shoulder, breath fogging in the cold. "The archivist's asking to go back inside. Says she left a kettle on."

"Let the kettle burn," Marrec said, and dropped the page.

It caught at the edges first, curled inward like a hand closing, and for half a heartbeat before the flames took the center he read the word again—or thought he did—and then it was ash, and ash was nothing, and nothing was the whole point.

*Vhalen.* The name surfaced in his mind the way a drowned thing surfaces: reluctantly, wrong-way-round, trailing weeds. He had heard it before. Everyone had heard it before, in the way that everyone has heard a sound in another room—not loud enough to identify, persistent enough to disturb sleep. It appeared in the Ministry's training materials exactly once, in a footnote to a footnote, described as *a cartographic error of the pre-Reform period, since rectified*. When recruits asked what it was an error *of*, they were told to review directive 12: *questions regarding rectified errors constitute insubordination.*

He had stopped asking at twenty-three. He was thirty-six now, and the question had gone quiet, the way questions do when you starve them long enough. But standing in the archive-ash with the smell of burnt vellum settling over Correnvale like snow, Dain Marrec felt the question shift in its sleep.

"Anything?" Hesk asked.

"Grain tariffs," Marrec said. "Tanners. River traffic." He turned away from the brazier. "Nothing relevant."

* * *

They came for the woman who keeps books four days later, or rather, they came for the idea of her, which was not yet a person and therefore easier to hunt.

The order had come down from the Ministry's regional office in a cipher Marrec alone among his company could read, which was itself a form of isolation the Ministry practiced deliberately—he had long ago stopped resenting it. The order contained no name, no description beyond the essentials, no crime. It said: *a woman who keeps books, formerly of the Guild of Impossible Things in the Free Cities, may hold documents material to the Reconciliation. Locate. Detain. Return materials intact. The woman may be returned damaged if necessary; the documents may not.*

*A woman who keeps books.* Somewhere in the upper floors of the Ministry, someone had typed those five words and felt satisfied with them, and Marrec understood why. It was elegant. It was deniable. A woman who keeps books could be any of ten thousand women across the free cities, and the search for her would require opening every ledger, questioning every clerk, disturbing every quiet corner where quiet people kept quiet accounts—which was, of course, the true objective. The woman was the pretext. The search was the weapon. You did not need to burn a guild from the outside if you could make it open its own doors room by room and show you everything, voluntarily, in the desperate hope of proving it had nothing to hide.

Marrec had watched this work in three cities already. In Osterhagen the guild masters had formed a committee to assist him, and the committee had spent two months informing on itself, member against member, until the guild dissolved from within and no soldier ever had to lift a torch. In Lowmarsh the clerks had simply fled ahead of him, abandoning ledgers by the hundredweight, and he had burned the ledgers in the town square while the town watched and learned. In Dunmark—

In Dunmark there had been a girl of perhaps nineteen, an apprentice bookbinder, who had swallowed a single loose page when his riders came through the shop door. They had cut her open on the binding table looking for it.

Marrec had signed the report afterward. His signature was steady. He remembered noticing that it was steady, and remembering being grateful, and remembering, distantly, that gratitude was the wrong thing to feel, and filing all of it away in the place where he filed things, which was very large and very organized and growing heavier every month, like a ledger of its own.

Now Correnvale, and after Correnvale, the Guild of Impossible Things itself—a day's ride east, in the city called Amberholt, where according to the Ministry's files the woman had been employed these nine years as a keeper of accounts. Nine years. The number snagged in him. Nine years ago was the Reform, the founding, the great unmaking-and-remaking of maps. Everything the Ministry hunted traced back to that year, the way all rivers trace to a watershed. Whatever this woman kept, she had been keeping it since the beginning.

"She'll run," Hesk said that evening, over the remains of a supper in the Wayfarer's Rest common room. The corporal was a solid man, unimaginative, loyal in the way of dogs and worse things. "Word travels faster than cavalry. By the time we reach Amberholt she'll be gone."

"Then we won't be reaching Amberholt," Marrec said. "I've sent a rider ahead. Plain clothes. He'll sit in the guild's counting house as a merchant seeking insurance against sea-loss, and he'll watch the ledgers being kept." He turned his cup slowly on the table, aligning its handle with the table's edge, because disorder in small things was a crack through which larger disorder entered, and he would not permit himself cracks. "People who run leave signs. People who intend to stay leave none. I want to know which she is before I knock on any door."

"And if she's neither? If she's just a woman keeping books?"

"Then the Ministry will have spent a rider's wages learning that," Marrec said, "and the guild will have opened its counting room to inspection, and every ledger in it will pass across a Ministry man's desk for a week. There are no losses in this business, Corporal. Only expenditures."

Hesk grunted and drank. Around them the common room filled with the evening traffic of a free city that did not yet know it was being measured: carters, fishwives, a pair of silk merchants arguing amiably about interest rates, a child chasing a dog between the tables. Ordinary life, dense and oblivious as a shoal of fish. Marrec watched it the way he watched everything now—with the detached thoroughness of a man taking inventory—and found, beneath the inventory, something he declined to name.

Because here was the truth he kept in the deepest drawer of his internal ledger, the entry he never balanced: he was good at this. Not competent—*good*, in the way that a key is good, in the way that a lockpick is good, shaped precisely for a purpose he had not chosen. He could find the hidden thing in any room. He could read a lie the way other men read weather. He could stand in a burning archive and feel, with terrible accuracy, exactly which book the archivist had moved that morning to safety, because her hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle but the stains on her cuffs were fresh and there was dust disturbed on one shelf and one shelf only, third from the window, eye level, the shelf a person saves last.

He had not searched that shelf. He had noted it. He had burned everything else and left it standing, and told no one, and did not examine why, and the not-examining had cost him more sleep than the cutting-open in Dunmark ever had.

* * *

On the sixth day, the rider came back from Amberholt.

He was windburned and hollow-eyed, having ridden through two nights, and he stood in Marrec's rented room dripping road-melt onto the floorboards and delivering his report in the flat voice the Ministry trained into its watchers: observations without interpretation, facts without weight.

*The Guild of Impossible Things occupies a converted granary on Tallow Street. Traffic consistent with a functioning counting house. Subject identified with high confidence: female, mid-thirties, employed as ledger-keeper, known to staff as Ashgrove. Habits regular. No indication of preparation for flight. She keeps to herself. She eats alone. She works late.*

*One anomaly.*

The watcher paused, and Marrec looked up from the page, and the pause itself was information—watchers did not pause unless the anomaly had gotten under their training and touched something human.

*On my fourth evening in the counting house,* the rider read, *there was thunder over the harbor. First storm of the season. The other clerks remarked on it, complained about their washing, sent a boy for the shutters. The woman did not move. She stood with her pen above the page and did not write for the duration of the thunder, and when it passed she resumed, and I observed that her hand shook, and I observed further that she pressed that hand flat to the ledger as if holding something down.*

*She looked toward the storm, Sergeant. Through the window. Like a woman listening for a voice in it.*

The rider lowered the page. Outside, Correnvale went about its evening, lamps being lit, carts rattling home, the smell of woodsmoke rising off ten thousand hearths in a town with one fewer archive than it had possessed last week.

Marrec sat with the report for a long moment. He should have felt satisfaction—the quarry located, the shape of the hunt resolved, the machinery engaging as designed. Instead he felt the question turn over again in its long sleep, deeper this time, troubled by something it could not name.

A woman who keeps books, who does not run, who shakes at thunder and presses her hand flat against the past as if it might escape.

"Ready the company," he said at last. "We ride for Amberholt at first light."

And that night, for the first time in eleven months of service, Sergeant Dain Marrec dreamed of a word he was not permitted to know, written in faded violet ink on a page that would not burn, and woke before dawn with the taste of salt in his mouth and no memory of why.
