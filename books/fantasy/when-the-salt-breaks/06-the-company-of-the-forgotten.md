## 6. The Company of the Forgotten

The road east out of the free cities was not a road so much as an argument between mud and memory, and Ivo Ashgrove walked it with her collar turned up against a drizzle that tasted faintly of salt.

She had left the Guild of Impossible Things three days ago with nothing but the map, two changes of plain clothes, and a ledger she had stolen from her own counting room—a slim black book whose pages were blank, which struck her as the only honest thing she had ever owned. Behind her, the free cities smoldered gently where the inquisitors' fire had caught the archive quarter. Ahead of her, the world dissolved into reed marsh and grey sky, the kind of country where a woman could become weather.

*Temporary*, she told herself, as she had told herself every morning since the squall had answered her call like a hound answering its master's whistle after nine years of silence. *You are temporary here. You owe nothing. You belong to no road.*

She had made invisibility an art, and arts did not survive company.

The first of them appeared at dusk on the third day, at a crossing where the road gave up and became river. A flat-bottomed ferry sat against the bank, its pole wedged in the mud, and standing in the stern of it was a broad-shouldered woman missing her left hand, the sleeve pinned and folded neatly at the wrist, her face brown and seamed as old rope.

"You'll want crossing," the woman said. It was not a question. Nothing about her suggested she asked questions she already knew answers to.

"I can walk the ford."

"The ford drowned three weavers last month." She said it without drama, the way one reports the price of bread. "Water's higher than it looks and colder than it is high. Two coppers, or your story if you're broke."

Ivo had coin. She paid it anyway—paid to keep the story—and climbed aboard.

The ferrywoman poled them across with the single hand doing work that should have taken two, her balance so practiced that the boat barely rocked. Halfway over, where the current ran deep and green, she said, "Name's Sefa. I ferry anyone who isn't carrying the usurper's seal, and I'd ferry his soldiers too if they paid double, but they never do. They think drowning people owe them."

Ivo kept her eyes on the water. "I'm no one."

"Mm," said Sefa, in the tone of a woman filing something away for later.

On the far bank, waiting in the lee of a leaning willow, sat two more figures: an old man in the rotted grey vestments of a faith that officially did not exist, his white beard stained with river silt; and a child, perhaps eight years old, sitting cross-legged with the absolute patience of someone who has been told to wait and has decided to interpret the instruction creatively.

"There's four coppers owed," Sefa said, jerking her chin at them. "They haven't got any. I ferried them anyway. Now you can pay for all of us, or you can walk on alone and let me feel good about my choices."

Ivo looked at the old man, who watched her with unsettling gentleness, and at the child, who was studying her the way children study locked boxes.

"Why would I pay for strangers?"

"Because you've been walking east for three days and east is where everyone with nothing goes," Sefa said. "And because the roads behind you are burning. And because—" she shrugged with her whole body, "—four coppers."

It was, Ivo reflected as she counted out the coins, remarkable how expensive other people's lives became when you agreed to carry even a small part of them.

* * *

The old man was called Brammel, and he had been, before the defrocking, a priest of Ulmaren, Vhalen's drowned god—the god of tides and thresholds, whose temples had stood in every coastal town of a country that, according to every map and record now extant, had never existed.

He did not say this aloud. He did not need to. Ivo recognized the vestments the moment she saw them, recognized the tide-knots braided into his beard, and felt the recognition move through her like cold water through a shoe.

"You shouldn't wear those openly," she told him the first night, when they camped in the shell of a burned tollhouse. "Priests of Vhalen are worth more to the inquisition dead than alive. Proof, they call it. Proof the country existed."

"Proof," Brammel repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. They came to my parish eight years ago and burned my books and told my congregation they had never worshipped anything at all. Half of them believed it by morning. Forgetting is a mercy, for some." He looked at her across the small fire. "For others it is a discipline. You have the look of a disciplined woman, ledger-keeper. Very tidy. Very closed."

"I'm a bookkeeper."

"You're a woman who counts things," he agreed mildly, "so that she does not have to be counted among them."

Ivo did not answer that. She checked the map instead, unrolling it by firelight, and frowned at what she found.

The route had changed.

Not much—a village marked on yesterday's version had vanished, replaced by a stretch of empty coast—but enough. Enough that her stomach tightened. Maps did not change. Maps were the opposite of people; that was why she loved them.

"Trouble?" Sefa asked, from where she was sharpening a boathook into something less polite.

"No," Ivo said, and rolled the map away before anyone else could see it.

* * *

The child was called Pip, though he admitted, on the fourth day, that he wasn't sure whether that was his name or one he'd picked up somewhere, the way you pick up burrs walking through long grass.

He remembered songs, he said. Songs nobody had taught him. He sang them while they walked, tuneless little things in a language Ivo understood without understanding—which is to say, the words bypassed her ears entirely and landed somewhere older, in the soft place behind her breastbone where Vhalen lived, folded small and guarded, like a letter never opened.

*"Down where the water wears the stair,*
*the bells remember, bells remember..."*

"Stop that," Ivo said, sharper than she meant.

Pip stopped. His face did something complicated and then settled into careful blankness, and Ivo recognized the expression because she had worn it herself for nine years. The face of someone learning, early, that certain parts of themselves make other people flinch.

"I didn't learn it anywhere," Pip said quietly. "It just comes."

"I know," Ivo said. And then, because silence seemed worse: "Sing it again. Slower. I want to hear the words."

He sang it slower, and Sefa, walking ahead with the boathook over her shoulder, did not turn around, but her stride changed—Ivo could not have said how, only that it did, the way a person's walk changes when they are listening with their whole spine.

That night Ivo lay awake under the leaning sky and hated herself a little for how easily it had happened. Three days. Three days and she had acquired a priest, a ferrywoman, and a child, the way a coat acquires burrs, simply by moving through the same country. She had spent nine years perfecting the art of being no one to anyone, and these three had simply—attached themselves. Had assumed. Had set a fifth notch in the fire circle without asking.

*Temporary*, she reminded herself, watching embers rise. *We go east until the map stops changing. Then we scatter. That's all any of this is.*

But she caught herself, near dawn, counting them. Four heartbeats around a dead fire. Four names in a ledger with no entries yet.

She turned over and pretended she hadn't.

* * *

The drowned villages began on the sixth day.

They came upon the first one by accident—or the map led them to it, which by then amounted to the same thing—a valley where the road sank into shallow grey water and kept going, visible as a darker seam beneath the flood. Rooftops broke the surface like sleeping animals: slate and thatch, some collapsed, some eerily whole. A church tower leaned at an angle that defied its own patience. Here and there a chimney stood above the waterline, and from one of them, absurdly, a single line of washing still hung, bleached by years to the color of bone.

"Flooded when Vhalen fell," Brammel said, voice low, in the register men use in churches. "When the usurper rewrote the coast, the water came in to take back what was erased. Ulmaren keeps honest accounts, whatever the guilds say."

Sefa poled them across on her ferry, all six of them now—they had gathered stragglers along the way, a widow, two brothers who didn't speak, a woman with a soldier's burn scar—because going around would cost four days and the map disapproved of detours. The water was glass-still, and looking down between the rooftops Ivo saw doorways, garden walls, a child's swing suspended in green light.

And names. Carved lintels, painted signs, doorstones—all of them legible beneath the water, all of them bearing words in the flowing Vhaleni script that no surviving document contained. She read them without wanting to. *Hallen-by-the-Weir. The Merrow's Rest. Ossa House, built by Ossa Anselin, 1189 of the Salt Reckoning.*

"That's not possible," she whispered. "The script was banned. The stones were defaced everywhere. I saw them do it."

"Everywhere above water," said Pip, hanging over the gunwale, delighted rather than frightened, because he was a child and children understand instinctively that drowned places are just houses holding their breath. "The water kept them. Like keeping letters."

Ivo looked down through the green glass at a name carved over a doorway—a common name, a baker's name—and felt something crack slightly in the wall she had built. Because she knew that street. Not this village, but that street shape, that lintel style, the little carved sun above the door meant to bless the bread. She knew it the way you know a lullaby you've forbidden yourself to hum.

Her hands ached suddenly with the memory of thunder.

"Land," she said roughly. "Get us to land."

* * *

They camped that night on a ridge above the flood, and the company—she had stopped calling them strangers, which was its own alarm—settled into a pattern she had not designed and could not stop. Sefa mended nets. Brammel untangled knots in his fishing line with enormous ceremony, as if the line were scripture. The widow taught Pip a clapping game. The brothers cooked, silently, competently, for seven.

Ivo sat apart, as was correct, as was safe, and studied the map by the last light.

The route had changed again. This time substantially: the coastal road she had planned to follow had been redrawn away entirely, the ink crawling inland toward the hills like a vein redirecting blood. In the margin, beside the new route, in handwriting she knew—*knew*, the mirrored ghost of her own childhood script, the left-slanting letters she had trained herself out of at fourteen—one word had appeared:

*Sooner.*

"Sooner than what?" she muttered.

"Than you'd like, probably," said Sefa, appearing beside her with two bowls of the brothers' stew, handing one over without asking. "Everything is. Sit closer to the fire. You count things better when your hands aren't frozen."

"I count fine here."

"You count fine anywhere, that's the problem." Sefa lowered herself to the ground with the economy of a woman who had learned to do everything one-handed and resented commentary on it. "Brammel says you flinch at the Vhaleni words. Pip says you hum in your sleep and it's the same tune as his song. I say you're running east on a map that moves, which means the map knows you better than you're admitting. Put those together and I get a story. I don't need to hear it. But I'd take better care of the teller if I did."

Ivo stared at her. "Why? You don't know me."

"Don't I?" Sefa ate her stew. "One-handed ferrywoman on the eastern marches, forty years old, no family listed anywhere. What do you think I'm running from, ledger-keeper? We're all from Vhalen out here. Some of us just forgot faster than others. Or worked harder at it." Her eyes, in the firelight, were very steady. "You worked hardest I've ever seen. That's how I knew."

Ivo looked down at the map, at the crawling ink, at the mirrored word in her own lost handwriting, and felt the terrible vertigo of a wall discovering it is a door.

"I'm not staying with you," she said. "Any of you. When this road ends, we separate. I need you to understand that now, so there's no—"

"—no unpleasantness," Sefa finished. "Of course. Temporary companions. We'll put it in the ledger." She stood, collected Ivo's emptied bowl, and paused. "For what it's worth, the widow doesn't talk, the brothers don't talk, Brammel talks too much, and Pip sings. Nobody here needs you to stay. They just need you to walk in the same direction for a while. Turns out that's most of what belonging is. Nobody tells you that."

She walked back to the fire.

Ivo stayed on the ridge a long time, the map across her knees, listening to the camp sounds behind her—the low voices, Pip's off-key singing, the click of Sefa's net-mending needle—and ahead of her, far across the flooded valley, a sound rising from beneath the water that she told herself she did not hear, that she would insist for many chapters of her life she had not heard:

bells.

Deep under the flood, slow and vast, ringing once each, in sequence, like names being called on the first morning of the world.

And in the map's margin, the ink moved again, and a second word appeared beneath the first, in that familiar mirrored hand:

*Hurry.*
