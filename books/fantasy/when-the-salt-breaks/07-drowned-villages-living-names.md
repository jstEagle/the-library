## 7. Drowned Villages, Living Names

The valley had been a country's throat once, Sefa's grandmother used to say, and now the sea had its hand around it. She thought of that saying as their little company came down off the blighted ridge and saw the water spread out below them—grey-green, patient, lying over what had been farmland and market roads and forty villages' worth of lives like a sheet drawn up to the chin.

"Is it deep?" Pip asked, hopping from stone to stone at the edge of the old causeway. The causeway ran straight out into the flood, its paving stones worn smooth by feet that had crossed here when crossing meant something else.

"Deep enough to drown you, shallow enough to mock you," Sefa said. "That's drowned country for you." She shifted her satchel higher on her shoulder—the one with her ferryman's hook strapped to the outside, useless here, carried anyway—and looked at Ivo Ashgrove walking behind Brammel with her ledger-keeper's stoop and her eyes fixed on the middle distance. "We go single file along the causeway to the first marker post, then we take the boats."

"What boats?" Pip said.

"The boats I told you about three times already, boy, and if you ask again I'll charge you double."

Brammel laughed, low in his chest. Ivo didn't laugh. She was looking at the water with an expression Sefa had been trying to read for two days and failing at—or succeeding at, which was worse, because every time she thought she'd caught it, it rearranged itself into ledger-keeper blankness again.

Sefa knew water. She'd spent thirty years poling ferries on the Merrow and the Vessa and the nameless brown channels between them, and she knew what water did to things. It took the sharp edges off grief. It made ruins into suggestions. A drowned house wasn't a house anymore; it was a rumor of a house, a shape your eye smoothed over because the truth of it hurt. That was the mercy of flooding. That was the lie of it too.

But this valley—this valley hadn't gotten the mercy right.

* * *

They put in an hour past noon, four of them crammed into a flat-bottomed skiff Sefa had traded for with the last of her honest coin and a length of good rope she'd have rather kept. Brammel took the stern because he was heaviest and steered best; Pip sat in the bow and trailed his fingers in the water like a child trailing fingers in water, which is exactly what he was, gods help them all. Ivo sat amidships with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at prayer who no longer believed in the god she was addressing.

The water was clear. That was the wrong thing about it. Floodwater should be murk, should be silt and shadow and things rotting down into the mud where nobody had to look at them. This water was clear as glass over a shop counter, and beneath the keel, the rooftops went by.

Sefa saw them first as shapes—dark rectangles in the green depths, regular as teeth. Then the light moved, a cloud letting go of the sun, and she saw what was written on them.

Names. Every roof bore its name, cut deep into the ridge-tiles or painted in colors the water hadn't managed to steal: *Harrowgate.* *Mellin's Rest.* *The House of Nine Daughters.* They glowed faintly, or seemed to—not with light exactly, but with legibility, the way a face you love stays visible in the dark to you alone.

"Brammel," Sefa said quietly. "Am I drunk? Don't answer fast."

The priest leaned over the gunwale. His face, when it came back up, was wet with more than spray. "Those are true names," he said. "Cut by the naming-masons. My order held the rites for—" He stopped, swallowed. "That's Cold Harbour. I buried my mother in Cold Harbour. There." He pointed at nothing anyone else could find. "Under that roof with the blue tiles."

"There aren't any blue tiles," Pip said, peering down. "It's all just... dark shapes."

"No," Brammel said softly. "There are blue tiles. You have to remember them to see them."

Pip frowned and looked harder, the way children look hard at things, whole-body, and then his mouth fell open. "Oh," he breathed. "Oh, there they are. There's a cat on the doorstep. There's a cat sitting on the—"

"Don't," Sefa said, sharper than she meant to. The boy looked at her, wounded, and she gentled. "Don't go naming everything you see down there, sprout. Some of those houses have been waiting a long time to be seen. Let them sleep."

But even as she said it, her own eyes were going where she'd sworn not to look. Down and left, past a drowned orchard whose trees still stood in rows like mourners at a graveside who couldn't remember why they'd come—to a rooftop with a crooked chimney and a name she could read without wanting to, without deciding to, the way you can't stop reading your own name on a debt notice.

*Vessamy's Ferry.*

Her grandmother's house. Her grandmother's ferry landing, before the ferry was Sefa's, before the ferry was anyone's, before the water came up the valley like a creditor and took payment in land.

She hadn't known it would be here. She'd told herself she didn't know—that the flooded valley they were crossing was any flooded valley, one more scar on a map full of them, because she had made a life out of not knowing things. But her hands knew. Her hands had gone cold on the pole the moment they'd put in, and now she understood why.

"You've gone quiet," Ivo said.

It was the first thing Ivo Ashgrove had said to her in an hour, and Sefa found she resented how well the woman had timed it. "I'm always quiet. Quiet's my best feature."

"That's not quiet. That's the other thing."

Sefa looked at her. Ivo Ashgrove, ex-ledger-keeper, ex-nothing, a thin woman with ink stains she never quite scrubbed out and a way of holding herself like a letter that hadn't been opened yet. And underneath all of it—

Underneath all of it, there was the accent.

* * *

Sefa heard accents the way other people heard music. Thirty years of ferries meant thirty years of passengers, and passengers came from everywhere and lied about most of it. She'd heard merchants from the free cities flatten their vowels into coins, heard border folk sand the edges off their words so they'd pass at checkpoints, heard people from places that weren't on any map anymore speak in careful, borrowed grammar, each sentence a small act of immigration.

Ivo Ashgrove spoke free-city trade-speak beautifully. That was the problem. Nobody spoke anything that beautifully unless they'd learned it. Learned speech sits on a person like a borrowed coat—you can see the shoulders it doesn't quite fill. And under the coat, when Ivo was tired, when she counted something under her breath, when she'd said *the other thing* just now with that particular softening of the second vowel—

Sefa's grandmother had that same softening. So had every soul in Vessamy's Ferry, so had the whole drowned length of this valley, the whole drowned country that wasn't supposed to exist anymore except that it was floating under their boat wearing its own name on every roof.

"You're from here," Sefa said.

It wasn't a question, but Ivo treated it like one. "I'm from nowhere in particular. It keeps the taxes low."

"Nobody's from nowhere. Everybody's from somewhere they left." Sefa shipped the pole and let the skiff drift, which was a ferrywoman's way of making a point: you don't move until you answer me. "Say 'the other thing' again."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Say it how you said it. Not how you're about to say it, how you said it."

For a long moment Ivo's face did something complicated—a lock turning, a door being weighed. Sefa watched her decide, watched the ledger-keeper win, watched the vowels come out flattened and filed smooth: "The. Other. Thing."

"There," Sefa said, and hated that her chest hurt. "You hear it? When you're not guarding it. That's Vhaleni. That's south-valley Vhaleni, if I'm any judge, and I am, I've poled every current from the delta to the—"

"Stop." Ivo's voice cracked like ice on a pond, sudden and thin. She looked over the side of the boat, at the drowned roofs, and Sefa saw her see them—truly see them, names and all—for the first time since they'd put in. Her face went grey. "How are you reading those?"

"Same as you, I'd guess."

"I can't—" Ivo pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "I looked at them an hour ago and they were shapes. Just shapes. Rooftops. Now I can read every one, and I don't know why, and I don't know what they say they are, only that I *do* know, somewhere, and knowing hurts."

"That's because you're home, you great fool," Sefa said, and then wished she could take it back, because tenderness was a dangerous cargo and it had slipped her hold entirely.

* * *

They drifted. The water carried them sideways, gentle as a hand on a shoulder, toward a stretch of rooftops where the names ran thick. Brammel had both hands pressed flat against the gunwale like a man steadying himself on a rail in high seas. Pip lay on his stomach in the bow, chin on his fists, reading the drowned village aloud in a whisper—*that one's called Anvil Row, that one's got a name like a bell, that one's mine, I think that one might be mine*—and none of them told him to stop, though Sefa caught Brammel's eye and saw the same fear in it she felt in her own ribs.

A child who remembered things no one taught him, drifting over a country that remembered things no one asked it to. Sefa didn't believe in omens. She believed in currents, weather, and the price of rope. But she knew a convergence when she stood in the middle of one.

"Here's what I want to understand," she said to Ivo, keeping her voice low, keeping her hands loose on the pole because a frightened grip tips boats. "You've been running with us five days. You flinch at thunder. You count things under your breath when you think no one hears. You speak Vhaleni when you forget to stop. And you looked at that map—the salt map, the one that bleeds—I looked over your shoulder at the inn at Marrow Cross, and I'll swear on my one good hand that the writing in its margins was your hand."

Ivo went very still. Boats feel stillness; the drift slowed somehow, or Sefa imagined it did.

"So here's what I want to know," Sefa finished. "And I want the truth, because I've got a child and a priest in this boat and I'll drown you myself if you're leading us into a trap. Who are you? What did you bury? And why does a woman who fled this country carry its map in her kit like a wound she won't stop picking at?"

The water lapped. Somewhere below them, a drowned bell in a drowned steeple shifted in the current and gave off a sound like a sigh through a closed door.

"I was born in Vhalen," Ivo said at last. Each word came out like a coin pried from a fist. "In a town called Greyford. I was storm-caller to the council there. And nine years ago I did something—" Her breath hitched. "I broke something. People died. The council sent me away instead of killing me, which was a mercy I never asked for, and I have spent nine years making myself small enough that the past couldn't find me." She looked at the rooftops. "It appears I failed."

"And the map?"

"A dying man gave it to me. He said it opens a door out of Vhalen. He said—" Ivo stopped, and started again, quieter. "He said the door only opens for the last one who remembers."

Sefa turned that over. She looked at Pip, whispering names to the dead houses. She looked at Brammel, mouthing prayers over his mother's blue tiles. She looked at the water, which was not water but a country, holding its breath.

"One last thing," she said. "Then I'll pole us on and we'll say no more about it today. Greyford." She let the word sit. "My grandmother ferried grain down from Greyford markets. She used to sing a work-song about the bridge there. *Stone by stone and oath by oath, the river pays what's owed it both.* Do you know it?"

Something crossed Ivo Ashgrove's face then that Sefa would spend a long time afterward trying to name. Grief, yes. Fear, certainly. But under both, unmistakable to a woman who'd spent thirty years reading faces the way she read water: guilt, fresh as a new-cut reed.

"I know it," Ivo said.

"Mm." Sefa took up the pole. "Well then."

She pushed off, and the skiff slid on over the sleeping names, and she told herself that suspicion and tenderness could share a boat the way they shared everything else in this broken world—but her hands stayed cold the rest of the crossing, and she kept her body between the child and the storm-caller the whole way, and she hated herself for it a little, and did it anyway.

* * *

They made the far shore at dusk, where the causeway climbed out of the flood onto salt-blighted ground that crunched underfoot like old bone. They camped in the lee of a fallen milestone. Brammel built a fire small enough to hide and cooked barley with the last of the onions. Pip fell asleep mid-sentence, still naming houses.

Sefa took the first watch, as she always insisted, because sleep was a thing other people deserved more than she did. She sat with her back to the milestone, hook across her knees, watching the flood turn copper and then black.

Ivo couldn't sleep either. Of course she couldn't. She came and sat a polite distance away, close enough for talk, far enough for flight, and for a while neither of them said anything. The drowned country glittered faintly below the dark, names glowing like fish-scale.

"My grandmother," Sefa said finally, "used to say the sea didn't drown this valley. It *banked* it. Like a fire. Kept it safe till someone came back to rake the coals." She glanced sideways. "I used to think it was just an old woman talking. Now there's a map in our kit that moves, and a child who knows songs nobody taught him, and rooftops down there lighting up when you look at them. So I'll ask plain, Ivo Ashgrove, and you can lie to me if you need to, but know that I'll know. Are we taking you home? Or are we taking you away from it?"

Ivo was quiet long enough that the fire settled twice. When she answered, her voice was barely above the lap of the water.

"I honestly don't know," she said. "The map redraws itself every night. Last night the route bent east. Toward the flats." She hugged her knees. "Toward the middle of it. Whatever the middle of it is."

Sefa nodded slowly. Home or away. Coming or going. With a moving map, it amounted to the same question asked different ways, and neither of them trusted the answerer.

"Then we watch it together tonight," she said. "Two pairs of eyes catch more lies than one."

But when they unrolled the map by the coals, the route hadn't bent east anymore.

It had doubled back.

The new line ran straight through the drowned valley behind them, through the flooded villages, through the glowing names, and ended—fresh-inked, still glistening, as if written moments ago—at a rooftop with a crooked chimney and a name Sefa had spent thirty years refusing to read.

*Vessamy's Ferry.*

Below it, in handwriting that was not the stranger's and not quite Ivo's, three words had been added:

*You forgot something.*
