## 8. The Map That Lies

The map lied to her on the fourth night.

Ivo discovered it by lamplight, the way she discovered most things—by numbers refusing to add up. She had taken to copying the stranger's map each evening into the back pages of her ledger, a habit that felt less like superstition than bookkeeping. If the thing was going to change, it would leave a record of changing. Everything left a record, if you watched it long enough.

She had copied it faithfully: the drowned road east out of the free cities, the fork at the broken millstone, the long climb through the ash-pines toward a pass marked only with a small salt-white circle. Four days' walk for a company as slow as theirs—Sefa's one hand and Pip's short legs saw to that. Four days to high ground before Marrec's riders finished sweeping the river valleys.

Tonight the fork was gone.

Not moved. *Gone.* The ink had pooled and dried in a different pattern entirely, the road bending south where no road could be, threading between two hills she did not know and descending toward water marked with a symbol she recognized from her childhood and had spent nine years trying not to recognize: a bell, drawn small and neat, half-drowned.

"Damn you," she whispered to it.

The map lay on her bedroll like a dead thing that refused to stop breathing. Salt crusted its edges—the stranger's blood had mixed with brine when he'd pressed it into her hands, and the stain had never quite dried. It was always faintly damp, always faintly cold, the way stones are cold near deep water.

Brammel stirred across the fire. "It's done it again, hasn't it."

"How would you know?"

"Because you have the voice of a woman arguing with an accountant." The defrocked priest sat up, gray beard silvered by moonlight. "And because it did the same to me the first week I carried it. Before the stranger took it back." He said this without bitterness, which was somehow worse. Brammel spoke about his own betrayal by the map the way other men spoke about weather.

"It moved the fork," Ivo said. "The whole road. There was a pass east of here. Now it wants us to go south, down to the—" she stopped herself before naming the symbol aloud.

"The bells," Brammel supplied gently. "Yes. It always wants to go to the bells."

* * *

In the morning she showed the others, because hiding it had become more exhausting than admitting it.

Sefa crouched over the map with her sleeve rolled above the stump of her right arm, tracing the false road with her remaining hand. Her fingers were rope-hard from thirty years of ferry lines. "South is wrong," she said flatly. "South is the Merrow valley. The water there doesn't freeze and doesn't drink. You cross it or you don't, but you don't camp beside it."

"I know."

"And yet your map says go."

"My map isn't mine." Ivo heard how bitter her own voice came out and made herself soften it. These people were not her responsibility. She reminded herself of that daily, the way a recovering drunkard reminds himself the tavern is closed. Temporary companions. Temporary. But Sefa had mended her boot with tarred thread three nights ago without being asked, and Pip had started leaving the best share of whatever they scavenged on her bedroll, and the reminders were losing their authority.

Pip, who had been hanging upside down off a fallen log the way children do when adults are being serious, swung upright. "Show me the bell part."

"You don't need to see the bell part."

"I remember bells." He said it simply, without the eerie weight it should have carried. He was seven, maybe eight; everything he remembered came out with the same casual certainty as his opinions about frogs. "Big ones. Under the ground. They sound like—" He screwed up his face. "Like when you drop a spoon in a well. But sad."

"We're not going to the bells," Ivo said.

"Why not?"

Because, she thought, the last time she followed something that sang, a bridge fell down. Because bells under the ground meant Vhalen, and Vhalen meant the storm-caller she had buried alive inside herself, and that grave was the only peace she had. Because the map wanted something from her and she refused to be wanted.

"Because the pass east gets us over the ridge faster," she said instead. "We go east."

The map, she was almost certain, shivered.

* * *

They went east. It took them two days through country that grew progressively less real—or rather, more real than it had any right to be, in ways that unsettled her ledger-keeper's soul. A village appeared on the second afternoon that had not been there before: twelve houses, a well, a shrine with its god's face worn blank by rain. Smoke rose from exactly one chimney. No one answered the door. In the garden, beans grew in perfect rows around a child's wooden horse, paint still bright, as though the family had stepped out minutes ago and might return any moment.

"They've been gone years," Sefa said quietly, touching the fence rail. "Feel it. The wood's rotting through. But the paint—"

"The paint's fresh."

"Everything here is both." Sefa looked at her sideways, and Ivo saw the old question in her face again—the question she'd been carrying since the flooded valley, since she'd heard Ivo say a place-name with the vowels softened the Vhaleni way. *You knew this country,* the look said. *You know it still.*

They camped that night in the empty village, because the alternative was walking in the dark, and Ivo copied the map again.

The fork had returned—but on the far side of where they now stood, and the pass east was marked differently. Smaller. Fainter ink, as if the map itself had grown tired of suggesting it. The route now bent northeast, toward a stretch of coastline she remembered from girlhood: white cliffs, a harbor town called Selvane, her mother's cousin's smokehouse where the herring hung in golden rows—

She stopped writing.

She had not thought of the smokehouse in nine years. She had certainly never told the map about it. And yet there it was rendered in loving detail, every curve of the little bay, the breakwater, even the crooked chimney of the smokehouse itself, drawn with the affectionate precision of someone who had stood on that shore a thousand times.

"That's not on the road to anywhere," Brammel said behind her. He had come to stand at her shoulder without her hearing, which priests apparently learned in seminary along with the rites of the drowned god. "Selvane's a fishing village. Nothing strategic. Nothing the usurper wants."

"Then why does the map want it?"

"Does it?" Brammel lowered himself painfully onto a stool, joints cracking like green wood in frost. "Or do you?"

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat by the banked fire and interrogated the map the way Corvane had once interrogated her, politely and without mercy, looking for the lie in the numbers.

Here was what she knew. The stranger had been an archivist—a keeper of records, like her, except his records had been a country. He had carried this map out of the burning archives of the capital and west across the mountains for months, bleeding slowly, hunting for what he called *the last one who remembers*. He had found her in a counting room full of traded impossibilities and died saying the name of a nation that officially did not exist, had never existed, according to every charter, census, and chronicle in the usurper's empire.

And the map changed. Nightly. Not randomly—she'd charted eleven nights of movement now, plotted them against each other like tide tables, and the pattern refused to resolve until she asked the question backwards. Not: where does the map go? But: what does the map follow?

It followed belief.

When she had first taken it, raw with grief and terror, convinced the whole enterprise was madness, the route had been short and brutal—a straight line east, almost suicidal in its directness, as if the map agreed with her that none of this mattered. As her resolve had firmed, the line had grown careful, cunning, threading safe passages. When Sefa held it, the roads ran along water—ferries, fords, drowned causeways, the routes of a woman whose whole life was crossings. When Pip held it, giggling, the margins filled with drawings: little fish, a dog, a house with a red door. For one terrible hour it had shown a road straight to the free cities, back the way they'd come, and Ivo had snatched it from him with her heart hammering, because that was the road home, and home was the one direction forbidden.

The map wasn't lying. The map was *mirroring*. It drew the country as the bearer believed it—and her belief was a ruin. Half memory, half invention, all nostalgia. She remembered Vhalen the way exiles remember: too beautiful, too lost, stitched together from a child's impressions and an adult's guilt. Of course it led them astray. She was astray. The terrain was honest; she was not, not even to herself.

"Oh," she said softly, to the fire. "You're not a guide. You're a mirror."

"Most holy things are," said Brammel, who had not gone to sleep either, and who apparently considered eavesdropping a sacrament. "My order taught that the drowned god doesn't answer prayers. He answers the prayer you actually prayed, beneath the words. People hated that teaching. It's why they defrocked me, among other charges." He fed a stick to the fire. "You think the stranger gave you a map to find the door. I think he gave you a mirror to find yourself. The door won't open for a guidebook, Ashgrove. It opens for the last one who remembers. So the map keeps asking: do you? Do you really? Or do you just remember wanting to?"

"Then it's useless."

"No. It's the truest document in Vhalen." He said it with the mild infuriating certainty of a man quoting scripture. "Every other record of this country has been burned or rewritten by men who wanted it forgotten. This one rewrites itself based on whether you remember honestly. It can't flatter you. That makes it the only map of Vhalen that cannot be forged."

Ivo looked down at the little bay, the crooked chimney, the golden rows of herring she could almost smell. Her chest ached with a homesickness so specific it had a street address.

Except.

She frowned. Leaned closer. Held the lamp up.

The smokehouse chimney was wrong. She turned the page sideways, then back. In her memory—in the warm amber version of Selvane that lived behind her eyes—the smokehouse stood at the harbor's north end, hard against the cliff. On the map it sat at the south end, past the breakwater, on a spit of shingle that had been underwater at high tide. She was sure. She was nearly sure. She had run that beach a hundred summers, had hidden from her mother among the herring racks—

Hadn't she?

A coldness moved through her that had nothing to do with the night air. She reached for the memory the way you reach for a stair in the dark and found it spongy, uncertain, the details rearranging even as she grasped them. North end. South end. Had the breakwater been stone or timber? Had there been two chimneys? Was the cousin's name Aldous or Aldren, or had she invented him entire, a supporting character in the sentimental drama of her own lost childhood?

"Which end?" she whispered.

"Ashgrove?" Brammel again.

"Nothing." She pressed her palms against her eyes until sparks bloomed. "Nothing. Go to sleep, priest."

He went. She sat alone with the fire dying and the map breathing its faint salt breath, and understood, finally, the shape of her predicament, and it was worse than pursuit, worse than magic. Somewhere east of here stood a door carved with the full name of her country, and it would open only for someone who remembered that country whole—every street, every name, the way the archivist must have known it before the burning. And she, the last one, the chosen one, the woman the stranger bled out to find, possessed a Vhalen built of soft-focus summers and guessed-at details, a country assembled from the wreckage of guilt, its geography shifting nightly because her own heart couldn't decide what had been true.

Memory, she thought, was supposed to be the ground you stood on. Hers was marshland. Every step sank.

* * *

Near dawn, Pip screamed.

Ivo was moving before consciousness fully arrived, storm-sense crackling down her arms unbidden—nine years of suppression dissolving in a heartbeat, the old hunger rising like floodwater—before she registered the source of the cry. The boy stood at the village well, barefoot, pointing down into the dark.

"There's a bell down there," he said. His voice had none of its usual frog-and-nonsense lightness. "It's ringing. It's been ringing all night. Didn't anyone hear it?" He looked around at their faces, at Sefa's grim dawn expression, at Brammel emerging gray-faced from his blankets, and his lower lip trembled with the particular fury of a child telling the truth to adults. "It knows my name. It keeps singing my name, and I don't—I don't know how it knows my name, because nobody ever told me my name. I picked it myself. Off a signpost. So how does the bell—"

"Pip." Ivo knelt. Took his thin shoulders. Over his head, Sefa met her eyes, and in the ferrywoman's face was the question that had been growing since the drowned valley, patient and terrible: *What are you, Ivo Ashgrove? What did you bury?*

The boy was shaking. She gathered him against her chest and felt, through his ribs, a faint answering vibration, as if the hum of the buried bells had gotten into his bones and set up residence.

Behind them, on her bedroll, the map rustled—though there was no wind—and when Ivo looked, the route had redrawn itself one final time for the night: no longer east, no longer south, no longer toward any coast or pass she recognized. The new road ran straight as a struck bell-line, down into the earth itself, ending at a mark she had never seen it make before.

Not a bell symbol. Not a door.

A name, written in mirrored handwriting she knew better than her own—her own childhood script, reversed, the way writing looks from the inside of a memory.

And beneath it, in smaller letters, freshly inked, wet enough to shine:

*You are remembering me wrong. Come and be corrected.*
