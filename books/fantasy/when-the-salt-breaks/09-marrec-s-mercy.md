## 9. Marrec's Mercy

The rain came down on Ferrow's Crossing like a verdict, thin and gray and without warmth, and Sergeant Dain Marrec stood in the doorway of the ferry house watching it stitch itself into the river. He had been in the free cities eleven days now, and in that time he had burned four archives, questioned thirty-one persons of interest, and learned precisely nothing about a woman who keeps books—except that she had been here, recently enough that the air still held her.

He did not think of himself as a cruel man. That was important to him, though he would never have said so aloud. Cruelty was inefficient. It produced noise, and noise produced errors, and errors produced letters from the Office of Questions in Vhalen's Rest asking why his quotas of confession ran high and his convictions ran thin. No. Marrec worked clean. He arrived with warrants instead of soldiers when he could. He asked his questions twice, once gently and once not, and he wrote everything down afterward in a hand so neat it had been complimented by two different undersecretaries. The regime required men who could do terrible things tidily, and Marrec had discovered early that he was one of them, and that this discovery, made at twenty-three and confirmed every year since, was easier to live with if he never once looked directly at it.

So he watched the rain and did not look directly at anything.

Behind him, in the ferry house's back room, two of his men sat with the priest. Brammel, the records said. Defrocked, formerly of the Order of the Drowned God, currently of no fixed address and no fixed faith. He had been taken at dawn on the east road, walking alone with a child's mitten in his pocket—which was odd, but priests were odd—and had offered no resistance whatsoever. He had thanked the soldiers for their trouble. That, too, was odd.

"Water?" Marrec asked, entering.

The priest sat with his wrists bound loosely in front of him—he had not struggled when they were tied, which struck Marrec as either wisdom or surrender—and he looked up with eyes the color of river mud, calm and bottomless and faintly amused. "Bless you, sergeant. Yes."

Marrec poured it himself. He always poured the water himself. It was a small ritual, a way of telling his own body: *this is procedure, this is not yet the other thing.* He had learned it from an old interrogator named Hess, dead now, who used to say that mercy was a tool like any other, and dulled if you didn't keep it sharp through use.

"Your companions," Marrec began, settling onto the stool across from him. "Four travelers, moving east along the salt road. A woman, tall, dark-haired, walks like a soldier but isn't one. A ferrywoman missing her left hand. A boy of perhaps eight years." He paused. "And yourself, until this morning."

"Three travelers now," Brammel agreed pleasantly.

"You'll tell me where they've gone."

"I expect I won't."

There it was. Marrec had heard refusal a hundred ways—screamed, wept, spat, whispered—but never this, never *cheerful*, and something about it set a hairline itch between his shoulder blades. He took out his notebook. He opened it to a fresh page. The paper was good stock, cream-colored, stamped with the double crown of the usurper's chancery, and writing on it felt, as it always did, like laying the first stone of a wall.

"Let me describe your options," he said. "Not as a threat. As arithmetic. Option one: you give me the road they've taken, the names they use, and anything you know of the woman called Ashgrove. In exchange, you walk out of here free. Not watched, not followed—at least, not closely. You go back to whatever god will have you."

"And option two?"

"You already know option two."

"I do," said the priest. "But I find I'd like to hear you say it. There's a difference, you know, between a thing spoken and a thing written. You're a ledger-man, aren't you? I can tell. You have ink on your fingers in the shape of numbers." He smiled, and it was not unkind, and that somehow made it worse. "Say what you do, sergeant. Out loud. I think it costs you more than it costs me."

Marrec did not say it. He turned instead to the window, where the rain had thickened into sheets, drowning the sound of the river. He was aware of his two soldiers in the corner—Voss, young and eager, picking at the binding of his crossbow; Kellem, older, staring at the priest with the expression of a man watching weather he didn't trust.

"The woman," Marrec said. "Ashgrove. What is she?"

"A keeper of ledgers," said Brammel.

"She was a storm-caller."

The word dropped into the room and sat there. Marrec watched it land. Nine years since the last verified storm-caller in Vhalen; nine years since Greyford, since the bridge, since the council's shame and the exile that followed; nine years during which the usurper's chancery had rewritten the event four separate times, each version cleaner than the last, until the official record stated that the bridge had collapsed due to structural neglect and the forty-one dead had died of nothing in particular. Marrec had copied those revisions himself. He remembered noticing, the third time through, that the number of the dead kept changing. Forty-one. Thirty-eight. Twenty-six. As though the chancery believed that grief, properly edited, would simply shrink.

"She was," Brammel said at last. "Yes."

"And you're protecting her."

"I'm protecting a memory, sergeant. She happens to be carrying it."

Marrec wrote: *Subject refuses. Motive unclear. Possibly religious.* His pen moved steadily. It always moved steadily. That was the thing no one understood about his work—the steadiness was the whole discipline, the entire art. Anyone could be brutal. Almost anyone could be brave. But to sit in a wet ferry house in a drowned province and write in a straight line while a bound man watched you with pity—that was craft.

"Here is what I don't understand," Marrec said, capping his pen. "You are defrocked. Your own order cast you out. Whatever god you served drowned or abandoned you—I've read the histories; I know what happened to the Drowned Order after the annexation. So why die for a stranger's secret?"

Brammel considered this with the seriousness of a man being asked a genuinely interesting question. "Because she remembers my country," he said finally. "All of it. Every street of it. And I—" For the first time something moved behind the mud-colored eyes, some sediment stirred up from the bottom. "I remember almost none of it anymore, sergeant. That's what forgetting does. It doesn't take the country away all at once. It takes the *edges* first. Then the middle. You wake up one morning and you can't picture your mother's door, and you think, well, that's natural, doors are common things. And then you can't picture her face, and then her voice, and then one day you're standing in a market in a foreign city and someone says a word—a *name*, just a name, a girl's name from three streets over from where you grew up—and you realize you are the only place left in the world where that name has ever lived. And you feel it going out of you like heat from an open door in winter. And you understand that when it's gone, she will never have existed. Not died. *Never existed.* Those are different things, sergeant. Death is a wound. This is a subtraction."

The room was quiet. Outside, the rain went on stitching its gray seam through the world.

Kellem shifted his weight. Voss had stopped fidgeting. Marrec found that his pen was uncapped again, though he had no memory of uncapping it, and that he had written nothing, because there was nothing in the chancery's vocabulary for what the old man had just said. *Subtraction* was not a category. The Office of Questions dealt in treason, sedition, heresy, concealment. Nowhere in its manuals was there a column for a mother's face.

He should press harder now. He knew the schedule of these things. Gentle question, then firm question, then the instruments, then the confession, then the tidy report. Eleven days he had been doing exactly this, and the machinery had run smooth as a mill wheel, and he had gone to sleep each night in whatever lodging the road provided and slept *well*, because the machinery ran smooth and smooth things make no noise.

"Where are they going?" he asked quietly. It was not the firm question. He noticed that it wasn't. He filed the notice away, the way he filed everything, to be examined at a later date that never quite arrived.

"They're going home," said Brammel.

"Vhalen doesn't exist."

"No," the priest agreed. "It exists beautifully. On paper. In the absence of paper. The usurper has made it the finest country in the world, sergeant—no borders, no people, no history, no debts. Nothing left to govern. You could not ask for a more perfect nation." He leaned forward as far as his bonds allowed, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "You burn our books because you're afraid of them. Do you know why you're afraid? Because a book is a door, and behind every door is a room, and behind every room is a country, and your master built his throne in the space where ours used to stand. One true memory, spoken aloud in the right place, and the whole architecture comes down. That's why it must be me today, and her tomorrow, and the boy after. Not because you're wicked. Because you're *load-bearing*."

"Enough," said Marrec.

He hadn't meant to say it sharply. It came out sharply anyway, and both soldiers looked up, and the priest settled back with an air of mild satisfaction, as though a point had been scored in a game whose rules only he knew.

*Load-bearing.* Marrec turned the word over. He thought of the chancery vaults beneath Vhalen's Rest, the shelves of confiscated genealogies, parish rolls, shipping manifests, songbooks—forty years of erasure, stacked and catalogued and *kept*, because even the usurper's ministers could not bring themselves to destroy everything, and what they kept they kept the way a man keeps a weapon. He thought of his own notebooks, twenty-two volumes of them, filled with the confessions and refusals of hundreds of people, most of whom he could no longer picture. He had subtracted them too, in his way. Written them down precisely so he would never have to hold them in his head.

"Voss," he said. "Outside."

"But sir—"

"*Outside.*"

The young soldier went. Kellem lingered, and Marrec looked at him, and Kellem—who had served with him six years, who had seen him do everything—gave a small slow nod, the nod of a man recognizing weather, and went out into the rain as well.

That left the two of them, the interrogator and the bound priest, in a room that smelled of wet rope and river stone.

"I'm going to ask you once more," Marrec said. "Not the firm question. Just this: is there any argument you can make that I will accept? Anything. Give me something and I'll work with it. I'm—"

He stopped. He had nearly said, *I'm reasonable,* and the word had snagged on something coming up his throat, and for a moment—a long moment, three full heartbeats—he sat very still and let himself look, directly and without flinching, at what he was. A tidy man. A load-bearing man. A man who poured water for prisoners and burned archives and slept well, whose mercy was a tool kept sharp through use, and who was at this instant searching for a *reason* to destroy an old man because destroying him was the schedule and the schedule was smooth and smooth things make no noise.

"Father," he said, and the title came out before he decided to use it. "Why did you thank us? When we took you. On the road. Why did you thank the soldiers?"

Brammel's smile returned, small and tired. "Because you'd found me before you found her. And because I prayed, nine days ago, that if it came to this, it would come to a man like you."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I've met the other kind." The priest's eyes held his. "I mean, sergeant, that I saw your hands pour the water, and I thought: here is a man still keeping accounts with himself. Here is a man who hasn't yet finished his own subtraction. And I thought—maybe the ledger isn't closed. Maybe there's a line in it still unwritten." He shrugged, ropes shifting. "Or maybe I'm an old fool who talks to gods that drowned. You'll know better than I."

Marrec stood. His legs felt strange, distant, as though he were reading about them in a report rather than inhabiting them. He crossed to the brazier in the corner where Kellem had left a fire burning against the damp, and beside it, in a canvas satchel, lay the contents of the priest's pockets and pack, inventoried that morning: a tin cup, a broken comb, a wooden fish on a cord, a psalter with water-swollen pages, and a bundle of loose papers wrapped in oilcloth—the priest's own records, Marrec realized. Notes. Fragments. He untied the oilcloth and saw lists of names in a shaking old hand. *Mara Tenlow, weaver, third bell street. Ossen Bright, boatman. The twins, Ada and Sefa, ferry-house row.* Names, dozens of them, each with a line or two after it—a face described, a song half-remembered, a door. A private archive. A single man's stubborn ledger against the subtraction.

He knew what the schedule demanded. The papers to the fire, the priest to the Office's longer questions, the report filed by nightfall, the machinery running smooth.

He looked at the fire. He looked at the old man, who watched him without fear and without hope, which was somehow the most terrible combination Marrec had ever seen on a human face, because it meant the man had already done all his arithmetic and accepted the sum.

"Sergeant," Brammel said softly, "whatever you decide—it's yours. Not theirs. Not the schedule's. Yours."

Marrec fed the papers into the brazier.

He did it slowly, one handful at a time, and he watched the flames take them—the names curling brown, then black, then rising as sparks—and he told himself, in the steady voice he used for reports, that this was procedure. That a defrocked priest's private notes had no evidentiary value. That their destruction simplified the file. That he would write it up cleanly tonight, in his neat hand, and sleep well, because smooth things make no noise.

The lies arranged themselves obediently, as lies always did for him, and he noted each one, and filed it, and knew he would examine them later at a date that would never arrive.

When the last page was ash, he cut the priest's bonds with his belt knife.

"East road," Marrec said, not looking at him. "There's a ford below the mill weir, two miles down. The soldiers watch the bridge. Go quickly, Father. My men rotate at dusk."

Brammel rubbed his wrists, studying him. "Sergeant. This line you've just written—"

"Don't." Marrec's voice came out rougher than intended. "Don't bless it. Don't interpret it. Just go, and forget my face, because in three days' time I may be the man hunting you again, and I would prefer—we would both prefer—not to have to remember this."

The priest rose, stiff and slow, and gathered his tin cup and his broken comb and his wooden fish. At the door he paused, rain-light behind him, and said: "The bells under the salt, sergeant. When you hear them—and you will hear them—listen for a name. Yours, or someone's you've lost. Everyone's in that song somewhere. That's what the usurper can't bear. Not that the bells are magic. That they're *true*."

Then he was gone into the rain, a gray figure swallowed by gray weather, and Marrec stood alone in the ferry house listening to the river rise.

He sat afterward for a long time with his notebook open. In the end he wrote the report the way the schedule demanded: subject questioned, materials destroyed, no intelligence obtained, prisoner released through administrative error pending recapture. He signed it. He dated it. His hand was perfectly steady, and looking at that steadiness—at the straight lines marching across the cream-colored paper, the double crown stamped at the top, the whole tidy weight of the machine he had served for nineteen years—Dain Marrec understood, with the clarity of a man stepping off a ledge, that the fracture was not in the file at all.

It was in him. Hairline, yes. Barely visible. But load-bearing walls, he knew, did not forgive cracks.

He capped his pen, whistled his men in from the rain, and gave the order to ride east—not toward Vhalen's Rest and the Office of Questions, but toward the salt flats, toward the bells, toward whatever waited under the salt singing names.

Toward the woman who keeps books, and the company of the forgotten, and the country that everyone had agreed, on paper, to stop remembering.

"Dusk rotation," he told Voss. "We move within the hour."

The rain was easing. Somewhere far to the east, beneath miles of blighted white ground, Marrec could almost imagine he heard them beginning to ring.
