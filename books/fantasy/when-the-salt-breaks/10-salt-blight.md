## 10. Salt-Blight

Pip had never seen a place so hungry.

He hadn't known that was possible until they came over the last brown ridge and the world below them turned white—not cloud-white, not milk-white, but a white like bone left out too long in the sun, stretching away farther than his eyes could follow. The salt flats. Sefa said the word the way you'd say the name of someone dead.

"Three days' crossing," she told them, squinting against the glare. "Maybe four. There's no water on the flats themselves, so we carry it. No shade either. We walk at dawn and dusk and we sleep through the worst of the day."

"And the night?" Brammel asked.

Sefa's mouth did something complicated. "The night is its own matter," she said, which was what she always said when she didn't want to answer, and Pip had learned to count how many times she used it, because the more she used it, the worse the thing she wasn't saying was.

Pip walked at the back of the line because he liked walking at the back of the line. From there he could watch everyone: Sefa with her one hand steady on her shoulder-slung water skins, her empty sleeve pinned and folded neat as a hymnal; Brammel murmuring under his breath the way he did when he thought no one could hear, though Pip always heard; and Ivo at the front, small and straight-backed, carrying her ledgers wrapped in oilcloth like they were babies.

Ivo was the one Pip watched most. Not because she was the most interesting—Ivo was mostly interesting the way a locked box is interesting—but because sometimes she looked at him like she was trying to work something out, and Pip wanted very much to know what it was. He'd caught her twice now watching his hands while he talked, as if the words were coming from somewhere other than his mouth.

The salt crunched under their boots like old snow. By midmorning it had worked into everything—into the seams of their clothes, into the corners of their eyes, into the bread, which tasted like tears by the time anyone thought to eat. The air itself tasted salted. When Pip licked his lips he could feel it gritting between them.

"Does anything live out here?" he asked, because the silence was getting into his teeth.

"Saltwort, near the edges," Sefa said. "Beetles. Nothing else wants it." She paused. "It wasn't always like this."

"It was fields once," Brammel said softly. He crouched and pressed his palm flat against the ground, the way he did before praying, except Pip noticed he didn't pray anymore, not properly, not since the river. His hand just rested there, like he was listening for something. "Wheat. Barley. Orchards along the lowlands. This was the breadbasket of—" He stopped himself. Everyone always stopped themselves. Nobody ever said the name.

Pip said it inside his head where nobody could stop him. *Vhalen.* It felt warm to say, even silently. Like a stone kept in a pocket.

They walked until the sun stood straight overhead, and then they made camp in the open, because there was nothing to make camp behind. Sefa rigged a scrap of canvas between two bundles of gear, and the shade it made was thin and grudging, but it was shade. Pip lay under it with his cheek against his pack and watched heat shimmer off the white in slow waves, like the land was breathing.

That was when he first heard the humming.

At first he thought it was the shimmer itself, some trick of the heat getting into his ears. But the shimmer made a sound like a fly trapped under a bowl, high and wavering, and this was different. This came from underneath. From down. A low note, round as a bell's tongue, rising up through the salt and through the ground and through the bones of his heels when he shifted his weight.

"Sefa," he whispered.

She was dozing with her hat over her face. She lifted it an inch. "What?"

"Do you hear that?"

She went still in a way Pip had learned meant *yes*. Then she said, "It's the wind moving under the crust. The flats do that. Hollow places sing."

"Is that all?"

"That's all." She pulled the hat down again.

But Brammel's head had come up too, and he was looking at Pip across the little camp with an expression Pip couldn't read, and after that nobody slept much, least of all Pip, who lay with his ear against the earth and listened to the hollow places sing.

* * *

The second day, the singing got words.

Pip woke before dawn with the taste of salt thick on his tongue and the sound already in his ears, except now it wasn't one note anymore. It was many notes, layered, braided, the way a crowd sounds from far away—and threaded through the hum, faint as breath on a window, were voices. Not talking. Singing. Long slow notes that rose and fell and rose again, and under the notes, names.

He knew they were names the way you know your own name: without having to think about it. They came up through the ground and into his teeth and sat there ringing. *Maren. Maren of the low orchard. Maren who planted willows by the sluice-gate.* And then another voice, another name. *Osk. Osk the ferryman's son. Osk who was afraid of eels.* And another, and another, dozens, hundreds, more than Pip could hold at once, all of them pouring up out of the salt like water finding a crack.

He sat up so fast the canvas shade sagged.

Ivo was already awake. She was sitting cross-legged at the edge of camp with her ledger open on her knees, not writing, just looking at the blank page, and when Pip scrambled over to her she closed it quick, like he'd caught her doing something.

"Pip. You should be sleeping."

"I can't. Listen." He grabbed her sleeve before he remembered you weren't supposed to grab Ivo, and let go fast, but she didn't pull away. "Listen! They're singing. Underneath. Can't you hear?"

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice. Pip saw it because he was watching for it. It wasn't surprise. That was the strange part—it wasn't surprise at all.

"The wind," Ivo said. "Hollow ground. Sefa told you."

"But they're *singing names*, Ivo. Maren of the low orchard. Osk the ferryman's son. There's hundreds of them. Thousands." His own voice was shaking and he didn't know why, only that the names were still coming up through his knees where he knelt on the salt, patient and endless, like the ground had been holding them a long, long time and was very glad someone had finally come to listen. "Why would hollow wind know anybody's *name?*"

Ivo looked at him for a long moment. The sky behind her was going grey at the edges, the first light coming up over the ridge they'd left, and her face was half dark and half lit, and Pip thought she looked like two people standing in the same place.

"You hear names," she said quietly. Not a question. Not quite.

"I told you. The songs. Nobody taught me them but I know them." Pip hugged his knees. The bells—or whatever they were—hummed on beneath him, sweet and sad and bottomless. "These are new ones, though. These are different songs. These are—" He stopped, because he didn't have the word, and then found it, and the word scared him a little: "*theirs.* These are theirs. The ground's."

Ivo closed her eyes. For a moment her lips moved, shaping something without any sound coming out, and Pip had the wild feeling that if she'd let the sound out it would have been a name, maybe the same kind of name, maybe the biggest one of all. But she swallowed it. He watched her swallow it, actually watched her throat move.

"I don't hear anything," Ivo said.

She opened her eyes and they were dry and level and completely, perfectly calm, and Pip understood, in the way children understand things adults think they're hiding, that she was lying. Not mean-lying. Scared-lying. The same way he lied about being brave before crossing the flooded valley, chin up, voice steady, knees knocking.

"Okay," Pip said.

"We should wake the others. Long walk today."

She folded her ledger away and stood, and she didn't look down at the ground once the whole time she packed camp, keeping her eyes on the horizon like the salt might catch her out. But when she thought Pip wasn't looking—which he was—she crouched, just for a moment, and laid her bare palm flat against the crust the exact way Brammel did when he was pretending not to pray.

And Pip, ear cocked, heard the singing change.

All those hundreds of voices, patient as rain—they hushed. Not stopped. Hushed. The way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in. And one note rose above the rest, clear and aching, and though it wasn't a word yet, it was shaped like one. It was shaped like the beginning of a name, held just under the surface, waiting to be spoken.

Ivo snatched her hand back like the ground had bitten her.

"Time to walk," she said, too loud, and marched off toward the white horizon, and Pip scrambled up to follow, and behind them, soft as breath, the buried bells took up their song again.

* * *

By the third day the salt had gotten inside everything, and Pip had stopped asking questions, because the answers were all the same shape.

The crops were dead here—had been dead so long they weren't even dead things anymore, just pale ghosts of stalks pushing up through the crust in rows, row after row after row, marching to the horizon like a congregation. Some of the rows followed the curve of old field-bunds. Once they passed what Sefa said was a wellhead, a low ring of stones with a tree growing out of the middle of it, black and leafless and salted white, its branches spread like it was still reaching for rain. Pip touched the trunk as he passed and the humming climbed right up through his fingertips and into his skull, and for a moment the names came so fast and so many—*Nella, Nella who drew this water, Nella whose daughter married the miller's boy*—that he had to sit down in the salt and breathe.

Brammel sat with him.

"They were people," Pip said. "Underneath. Weren't they. The voices."

Brammel was quiet a long time. Out on the white, Sefa and Ivo had stopped and were arguing about something—about the map, Pip thought, because Ivo had it out and was staring at it like it had insulted her.

"The drowned god's teaching is this," Brammel said finally. "Nothing that was loved is ever fully lost. It only goes somewhere deeper." He smiled, and it was a tired smile. "I thought that was a story for comforting widows. I'm starting to think my whole order was a bell-rope and none of us knew it."

"What's a bell-rope?"

"The thing you pull to make the bell speak." He rapped his knuckles gently against the salt crust. "Somebody built these flats to be quiet, Pip. Somebody poured the salt and burned the records and renamed every road. But you can't silence a country by covering it. It just goes underground. And underground things carry." He looked at Pip sideways. "You've been hearing them since the valley. Don't think we haven't noticed."

"Is that bad?"

"No, lad. I think—" Brammel broke off, because Sefa was calling them to walk, and he stood and offered Pip his whole hand, the real one, and Pip took it, and they walked on.

But Pip thought about it all day, walking the white rows with names ringing up through his boot-soles. *Bad.* Was it bad? The names weren't scary. That was the thing nobody seemed to get. The names were *sad*, but sad like a lullaby is sad, sad like somebody waiting at a window. They didn't want anything from him. They just wanted saying. Every one of them, when it rang up through the ground, sounded relieved. Like: *oh, good, someone heard. Someone's still counting us.*

Counting. Pip stopped mid-stride, and Sefa nearly trod on him.

That was it. That was what the bells were doing. They were doing what Ivo did with her ledgers—every impossible thing traded, every debt owed, entered neat and true so nothing got lost. The ground was keeping accounts. The ground had been keeping accounts for nine years, all alone, with nobody to audit it, and now here came four ragged travelers walking its columns like figures on a page, and the ground was *ringing itself hoarse* trying to give them its books.

That evening, at dusk-camp, Pip dug a little hole in the crust with his fingers. Just a small one, shallow, the size of a cup. He put his mouth close to it and sang one of his songs—the ones nobody taught him—the one about the miller's wheel and the girl who waited by the sluice-gate, and as he sang he felt the humming rise to meet him, felt the ground lean in under his knees like a dog hearing its name.

When he finished, the salt around his little hole had gone dark with damp.

Sefa saw it. He saw her see it. She didn't say anything, but that night she moved her bedroll closer to his, between him and the open white, and stayed awake long past dark, facing outward, one hand resting on the salt like she was keeping watch over something sleeping.

* * *

On the fourth morning—the morning they should have reached the far edge—Pip woke to find Ivo standing at the edge of camp with the map open in both hands, and her face was the color of the salt.

"The route's changed," she said, when she saw them stirring. Her voice was flat and careful, like someone reading numbers aloud. "It's happened before. Small shifts. But this is—" She turned the map around so they could see.

Pip didn't understand maps properly, but even he could see that the inked line no longer ran straight across the white to the far ridge. It bent. It bent sharply, swinging wide in a great loop, and at the deepest point of the loop, in the heart of the flats where nothing was marked at all, the ink had gathered into a small dense knot, like a bruise.

And beneath the knot, in handwriting that shimmered faintly, letters had surfaced overnight in the margins, fine as frost:

*You are close. It hears you. Do not answer.*

The humming underfoot swelled, gentle and vast, and for the first time Pip could pick out a single voice rising above all the others—one bell among thousands, ringing clear and deliberate, over and over, patient as tide:

a name. Her name.

*Ivo. Ivo. Ivo.*

The ground had learned hers, and it was calling her home.
