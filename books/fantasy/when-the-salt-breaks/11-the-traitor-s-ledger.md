## 11. The Traitor's Ledger

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers the way other institutions kept their dead: carefully, privately, and with a certain embarrassment about how many there were.

Ivo had counted them once, in her first year—forty-one volumes in the counting room alone, not counting the deep archive below the salt cellar where the truly delicate entries lived. She had been a different woman then, or at least a woman trying on a different skin: the quiet one, the precise one, the ledger-keeper whose hands never shook and whose eyes never strayed to the window when thunder rolled over the free cities. Nine years of practice had made the skin fit so well she sometimes forgot it was a disguise. Almost.

She was not thinking of that now. She was thinking of Corvane's inkwell.

It sat on his desk the way it had sat there for as long as she'd worked beneath him—a squat thing of black glass, chipped at the lip, filled always with ink the color of drowned men's lips. She had refilled it a thousand times. She had catalogued it, in the guild's inventory of curiosities, as *inkwell, black glass, unremarkable*. And that, she realized now, standing in the doorway of his office with the rain hammering the shutters behind her, was exactly what it was meant to be. Unremarkable. A thing too small and dull to look at twice.

Corvane looked up from his desk with the expression of a man who had been expecting her for days and was mildly disappointed she'd taken so long.

"Ivo." He set down his pen with care, squaring it against the desk's edge. "You've come back."

"You knew I would."

"I hoped." He folded his hands. He was a narrow man, Corvane, dry as August paper, with a face like something left out in weather until all its softness had bleached away. Only his eyes were quick. They had always been quick. She had mistaken that quickness for intelligence, back when she was young enough to confuse the two. "Sit down. There's no need for whatever you're planning to do with your jaw. I haven't betrayed you."

"No," Ivo said. She did not sit. "You've sold me. It's different. Selling implies I got something."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt; she doubted the machinery of his conscience still turned after all these years—but calculation, the flicker of a man revising an estimate.

"Ah," he said softly. "You've seen them. The inquisitor's records."

"I've seen *your* records." She came forward and laid the ledger on his desk between the inkwell and a cold cup of tea. She had taken it from the deep archive three nights ago, by lantern light, while Sefa waited outside with Pip asleep against her shoulder. It was volume thirty-eight, bound in grey calf, its spine cracked from handling. "Entry four hundred and eleven. Shall I read it aloud? I have it memorized. I find that memorizing things is safer than keeping them in buildings you own."

Corvane said nothing. Rain filled the silence.

"*Sold to Sergeant Dain Marrec, Inquisition of the True Charters*"—she read evenly, though her voice wanted to shake—"*one woman, description enclosed: storm-touched, exiled, employed as under-ledger-keeper. Price: continued guild exemption from charter audits, ten years' term.*" She turned the page. "*Note: subject believed unaware of her market value. Recommend patience.*"

"Patience," she repeated. "You were patient. I'll give you that. Nine years I balanced your books. Nine years I made myself small enough to fit between the lines, and you were pricing me the whole time."

"I priced everything." Corvane's voice was level, almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty. It was the voice he used for difficult clients. "That is the guild's business, Ivo. Impossible things. Do you imagine we trade only in bottled ghosts and doors to nowhere? People are impossible too. People are the most impossible things there are. Every soul in this city is carrying something that cannot be true—their griefs, their hopes, their memories of countries that no longer exist. We simply find the buyers."

"The buyer is Marrec." She leaned forward, palms flat on his desk, and let herself look at him properly for the first time in nine years. "He burns archives, Corvane. I've seen the ash on the road east. Whole libraries. And the people who kept them—he doesn't burn those quickly. You know what the inquisition does with archivists."

"I know what empires do," Corvane said. "I have watched several of them rise and fall through that window. The charters change. The seals change. The guild remains." He rose, went to the shuttered window, and stood with his hands clasped behind him, a silhouette against the grey light. "You think I'm a monster because I sold your name. Consider what I didn't sell. Consider what a man in my position could have sold, and didn't."

"What could you possibly—"

"The map." He did not turn around. "The dying archivist's map. The one you took from the counting room the night Wrenna bled out on my floor. I told Marrec's men it had been lost in the scuffle. That the stranger destroyed it before she died. Do you know what that lie cost me? Marrec does not forgive inaccurate inventories. I am carrying that debt still."

Ivo's hands curled slowly against the wood. "Why?"

"Because a door out of Vhalen is worth more closed than open." Now he turned, and for a moment the dry paper face showed her something underneath—not warmth, nothing so human as warmth, but a kind of terrible professional pride. "While Vhalen stays erased, I hold the only inventory of it in the world. The moment it returns—its name, its memory, its *existence*—every entry in my archive becomes worthless. A country that exists can be audited by anyone. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I have spent thirty years building the one ledger the usurper cannot read, and you are proposing to hand him the key."

"You're protecting your monopoly."

"I am protecting *order*." His voice sharpened, just slightly, the first crack in the clerk's calm. "There are forty thousand refugees east of the salt marshes who are alive because the usurper believes there is nothing east of the salt marshes worth remembering. You want to give him a country to conquer. I want to keep the emptiness empty. Call it corruption if you like. I call it the lesser arithmetic."

For a moment—Ivo hated herself for it—for a moment the argument almost landed. She had done her own arithmetic on the road east: Pip's fevered sleep, Sefa's ruined hand, Brammel gone ahead of them into Marrec's custody, the miles of blighted ground between here and the door in the water. Every choice had cost. She knew about lesser arithmetics. She had buried a bridge full of people under one.

But that was precisely why she recognized the shape of Corvane's reasoning. It was the same shape her own shame had worn for nine years: *the numbers excuse me, the numbers absolve me, look at the numbers and stop looking at me.*

"No," she said. "That's not what this is. I've read the rest of volume thirty-eight, Corvane. Entry four hundred and twelve. Entry four hundred and nineteen. The ferryman who ferried Vhaleni families across the Miren—you sold his route to the inquisition and they drowned him in his own boat. The bell-ringer of Lowmarsh. The schoolmistress at Cander's Ford who taught the old songs." She pulled the second ledger from inside her coat—a thinner book, privately kept, in her own careful mirrored hand—and set it beside the first. "I copied your entries. All of them. Names, dates, prices, buyers. Two hundred and six transactions over eleven years. Do you know what the guild's charter says happens to a master who trades in persons? Expulsion. Stripping of seals. And the free cities' magistrates—" she smiled, and felt the smile arrive crooked and cold on her own face, "—the magistrates still hang traffickers. Even ones with exemptions."

Corvane looked at the thin ledger for a long time. When he spoke again, the clerk's gentleness was gone entirely, and what remained was smaller and older and, Ivo thought, more honest.

"You'd burn thirty years of the guild to save four people."

"To save everyone your arithmetic decided didn't count." She picked up the thin book and held it against her chest. "Here is my offer. One offer. My companions travel east through the guild's river gate—your barges, your pilots, your seals on the manifests. Safe passage to the drowned heart, no questions, no reports. In exchange, I don't send copies of this to the magistrate, the temple, and the *Herald*, which has been hungry for a guild scandal since the spring. And I don't leave it where Marrec will find it—which, incidentally, would be very bad for you, because the inquisition pays its informants in trust, and trusts, unlike people, can be revoked."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow morning every copy shop in the free cities has my handwriting in it."

The rain thickened against the shutters. Somewhere below, in the counting room, a clerk laughed at something, the ordinary sound of ordinary work, and Ivo thought absurdly of the thousands of hours she had spent in that room perfecting her invisibility, learning to be a woman whom no one looked at twice, and how it had all been leading here—to standing in the light, demanding to be seen, threatening a man with the full weight of her own attention. She had made an art of being unwritten. Now she was holding a pen over someone else.

Corvane sat down slowly. He drew the black-glass inkwell toward him, touched its chipped lip with one finger, and seemed to take some private counsel from it.

"You sound like a trader," he said finally. "Threats, terms, leverage. It suits you. I want you to know that it suits you, because I suspect no one has ever told you, and because I think it will hurt you to hear it." He looked up. "Yes. You'll have your passage. The river gate opens at dawn. I'll seal the manifests myself." He paused. "And Ivo—the door you're walking toward. Wrenna died to bring you that map. Ask yourself what she thought you'd find on the other side of it. Not a country. Countries don't wait behind doors. Ask yourself what she thought *you* were."

"Don't," Ivo said quietly. "Don't do the thing where you make it philosophical so you don't have to feel like what you are."

"What am I?"

"A man who sells people and calls it stewardship."

"Yes," Corvane agreed, without flinching. "And you're a woman who just learned how well threats suit her, going off to save a nation, and you'll be calling *that* stewardship. We are more alike than your friends would find comforting." He extended his hand across the desk, palm up, waiting. "The manifests. Give me the names."

Ivo stared at his hand. Then she tore a page from the back of her thin ledger, wrote four names in her careful mirrored script—*Sefa of the Miren Ferry. Brammel, priest of the Drowned God. Pip, called Sparrow. Ivo Ashgrove*—and set it down without taking his hand.

"My name goes last," she said. "On everything. From now on."

She was at the door when he spoke again, and she did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," Corvane said, "Marrec knows you're east. I couldn't prevent that part. The manifest will get you past the river patrols. It won't get you past him."

* * *

The river gate opened at dawn into fog, and the fog smelled of salt.

Sefa was already at the rail when Ivo came down the wet stairs, her single hand wrapped in the mooring rope, her missing sleeve pinned and snapping in the wind. Behind her, in the barge's shallow hold, Pip slept in a nest of sacking with Brammel's stolen prayer-book under his cheek, and six refugee families huddled around what little they carried—pots, portraits, a hen in a basket, a child's shoes grown too small but kept anyway.

"He sealed them himself?" Sefa asked, nodding at the wax tags on the cargo crates. Each bore the guild's sigil: a key, a coin, and a closed eye.

"He sealed them himself."

"And what did it cost us?"

Ivo watched the fog peel back from the water. Ahead lay the drowned valley, and beyond it the flats, and beyond the flats a door that would not open, and somewhere behind them, patient as arithmetic, a sergeant of the inquisition who already knew her name.

"Less than it should have," she said. "More than I want to say out loud."

Sefa studied her a moment with those flat grey river-eyes that missed nothing, then held out her hand—not for an explanation, Ivo understood, but simply to hold. Ivo took it. The rope creaked. The barge slid out into the current, eastward, toward the place where Vhalen's name slept under the brine.

Behind them, on the quay, a figure in grey stood watching them go, and did not move even when the fog closed over the water like a ledger shutting.

Ivo did not see him. But Pip, waking in the hold, did—and pressed his small palm flat against the hull, and whispered a word he swore later no one had ever taught him.
