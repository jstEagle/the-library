## 12. What Ivo Buried

Nine years before the map, nine years before the salt, there was a bridge at Greyford that Ivo Ashgrove loved more than any person alive.

She would not have said so out loud. Vhaleni children were taught early that love was a thing you kept in the cellar of yourself, dark and quiet and fed on scraps, because everything you loved could be taken as evidence against you. But she had loved the bridge. It had stood over the river Sere for six hundred years, its seven arches worn smooth as knuckles, its stones carved with the names of every mason who had ever set a hand to it — four hundred and eleven names in the old script, spiraling down the piers like watermarks. She had learned to read by tracing them with her finger, her grandmother's voice behind her saying each one aloud: *Oren Vael, mason of the second arch. Ilse Marrow, mason of the third.* The bridge remembered its makers. That was what Vhalen had believed, once, about all made things — that craft was a form of keeping, and keeping was a form of prayer.

Ivo was seventeen the autumn she unmade it.

* * *

The storm had been coming for three days before anyone named it. The sky over the Sere valley went the color of pewter, then the color of a bruise going bad, and the air took on the flat mineral taste that Ivo knew the way other people knew the smell of bread baking. Pressure. Gathering. Her skin prickled along the old lines her grandmother had traced when she was small — the storm-channels, the paths lightning liked to take through a body built for it — and she had done what she always did, what she had sworn to do since her first bleeding: nothing. She pressed the feeling down. She stacked it under ledgers and chores and the endless arithmetic of the ferry-house where she worked, and she told herself the storm would pass over Greyford the way storms passed over everywhere, indifferent, needing no one's help.

But the river was high. The rains up-country had been wicked all season, and the Sere ran brown and swollen under the bridge, chewing at its piers, and on the third evening the ferrymaster came into the counting room white-faced and said the middle pier had shifted. Said the masons were already talking of bracing it. Said, looking sideways at her, that the council had sent word asking after her.

Asking after her. Not summoning her — asking. As if she were a tool hung on a wall, gathering dust, that someone might remember in an emergency. As if nine years of her grandmother's discipline — *you do not touch it, Ivo, not for rain on crops, not for drought, not even for war; the last time our family touched a sky, a village stopped existing* — meant nothing against one cracked pier.

She went anyway. That was the first sin, if you wanted to count sins, and Ivo had spent nine years counting them, so she knew the order by heart. The first sin was vanity. She walked out to the bridge in the failing light because part of her — the part she would later bury deepest — wanted to be asked. Wanted to stand in front of the masons and the councilmen and the whole wet crowd of Greyford and have someone say, finally, *we need you.*

They did say it. Councilman Verel, a dry stick of a man who had never once spoken to her without checking first that no one important was watching, took her aside under the toll-gate while the wind worried at their clothes and said, "If the storm hits the river at flood, the pier goes. If the pier goes, the bridge goes, and half the town is on the far bank — market day, girl, there are two hundred people over there tonight." He wiped his mouth. "Your grandmother turned the harvest-storm away from Corren Field in '41. They still sing about it."

"They don't sing about what it cost her," Ivo said.

"No," Verel agreed. "They don't."

And here was the second sin, the one that mattered: she believed she could hold it. Not turn the storm — turning was the great work, the work that had burned her grandmother's channels gray and put her in the ground at fifty-one — but hold it. Ease it. Take a fistful of the storm's fury and bleed it off into the empty hills north of the valley, just enough to spare the pier, just enough to be the girl who saved Greyford. A small working. A measured one. She had read every book in the ferry-house attic. She had practiced with static and small winds for years, little thefts from the sky, nothing that left a mark. She was seventeen and she had spent her whole life being careful and she believed — this was the poison of it, this was the sentence she would rewrite ten thousand times in the dark of the following decade — *she believed she was ready.*

The storm broke at moonrise.

* * *

Afterward, they told her what it had looked like from the ground, because she could not remember it herself. That was the cruelty of storm-calling: the caller stood inside the eye of her own working and saw only light. Witnesses said the sky split open above the bridge like a struck drumhead. Witnesses said the lightning did not fall but *stood*, a column of white fire planted on the middle pier, thick as a temple pillar, humming a note that burst eardrums at forty paces. Witnesses said the river itself flinched, leapt its banks in a single convulsive wave, and that when the light went out the bridge's second and third arches went with it, folding down into the flood like a man kneeling.

Four hundred and eleven names went into the water. Oren Vael, mason of the second arch. Ilse Marrow, mason of the third.

Thirty-one people died. She learned the number the way she would learn her own heartbeat: permanently, uselessly, in a room full of candle smoke while a clerk read names off a list and someone's mother screamed in the courtyard below and the sound came up through the floorboards of the council hall like water rising. Two of the dead were masons who had run toward the collapse to pull others clear. One was a child of six who had been on the far bank for market day and whose name Ivo would later write, in a ledger no one ever audited, in a hand that shook too badly to be legible: *Tamsin Vale, age six, liked river-glass.*

She remembered fragments. That was all the working left her — shards, like the bridge itself. Standing on the toll-gate steps with her arms raised and the taste of copper flooding her mouth, thinking *yes, yes, come* — and hearing, underneath the thunder, her own voice saying it, greedy as a beggar at a feast. Remembering the storm pouring into her and it being *good*, that was the unforgivable part, it felt like being filled after nine years of deliberate emptiness, like a door opening in a house she'd thought had only walls. Remembering trying to close her hands and the storm not caring, the way a river does not care about a cupped palm. Remembering — this fragment she kept locked in the innermost box, and opened perhaps once a year, on the worst nights, as penance — remembering the moment she understood, with perfect clarity, mid-working, that she could not stop it, and feeling not horror first but *disappointment*. Disappointment, in herself, the way you are disappointed in a horse that throws you. As if the storm were the failure and not her.

The horror came later. The horror had never entirely left since.

* * *

The council met for three days. Ivo spent them in a cell beneath the toll-house that had been built, she realized on the second night, for exactly this purpose — for storm-callers — though no one had needed it in a generation. Its walls were lined with lead sheeting gone green with age. Someone long dead had scratched words into the stone at sitting-height, low, where a prisoner curled on the pallet would see them: *it answers because it knows you. grieve, but do not hate it.* An older hand. Another caller, another century, another ruined bridge or burned field or nameless village that had stopped existing. Ivo sat with her fingers on those words for most of three days, the way she had once traced masons' names on the bridge, and understood that she was reading her own future written by someone who had survived it.

On the fourth day they brought her before the council.

There were eleven of them, seated in a crescent in the hall of records, with the archive-shelves rising behind them — the whole memory of Vhalen bound in leather and vellum, ten thousand volumes, and every spine suddenly looking to Ivo like a door that could shut. Verel was among them. He could not look at her. Councilor Ashe sat at the center, a broad woman with a mason's forearms, and it was Ashe who spoke.

"You know what the law requires."

"I know what the law requires," Ivo said. Her voice came out flat. She had rehearsed flatness for three days and it held, mostly.

"Death, for an unsanctioned working that takes life." Ashe's hands lay open on the table, palms up, the gesture of a woman showing she held no weapon. "Thirty-one dead, Ivo. The law is not cruel. But the law is not flexible either, and I will not insult you by pretending otherwise."

"Then why am I standing here?"

Ashe looked at her for a long moment. Around the crescent, Ivo watched the councilors exchange the particular glances of people who had argued for days and arrived somewhere uncomfortable. "Because you are nineteen—" someone murmured correction; "—seventeen," Ashe amended without breaking stride, "—and the last storm-caller born in Vhalen. Because your grandmother's line is ended with you, and what ends with you cannot be executed twice." She paused. "Because half this council remembers what we were before the border lords came, and knows that a country that kills its own rarest gift is already halfway to being the country they want us to be. And because—" here her voice changed, roughened, became less a councilor's voice and more a mason's, the voice of a woman who had personally rebuilt things after floods, "—because executing you would be easy, child, and everything easy we have done these last twenty years has cost us double."

"So what happens instead?"

"Exile." The word dropped into the hall like a stone into a well. "You leave Vhalen. Tonight, before the grief in Greyford finds its voice. You go beyond the borders, past the free cities if you must, anywhere the name Ashgrove isn't known. And you do not call. Not a squall, not a breeze off a lake, nothing. You live as though the talent were buried, because for the sake of everyone who remembers Greyford, it has to be." Ashe leaned forward. "Understand me. This is not mercy. Mercy would be a quick death and everyone's grief given a shape. This is a sentence. You will carry thirty-one names across every border you cross for the rest of your life, and you will carry them alone, because we cannot let you belong to us again. Do you accept?"

Ivo had expected — she would realize this much later, in a guild counting room, balancing ledgers of traded impossibilities — she had expected to feel relief. Instead what she felt was the precise sensation of the bridge falling: something structural giving way, quietly, internally, with no lightning at all.

"I accept," she said.

"There is one more thing." Ashe nodded to the clerk, who brought forward a slim volume — a register, Ivo saw, the master registry of Vhaleni citizens, the book that made a person real. "The exile is recorded. Your entry will be closed. In time—" Ashe's jaw worked, and for the first time the councilor looked her age, looked tired, looked like a woman signing something she'd spend her remaining years rereading. "In time the borders will tighten. The border lords want Vhalen forgotten, Ivo, name by name, ledger by ledger, and we are not strong enough to stop them — only slow them. So we slow them where we can, and one of the ways is this: the ones who leave are struck from the rolls. Not erased in malice. Erased as armor. What the border lords cannot find, they cannot burn." She closed the book. "You will be unwritten. It is the kindest violence left to us."

That night a ferryman — not the ferrymaster, a younger man who owed Ivo's grandmother a debt he never explained — rowed her down the swollen Sere in the dark, past the ruin of the bridge, where lanterns still bobbed among the wreckage and searchers called names into the reeds. She made herself look. She made herself say each name she knew under her breath as the current carried her by: the masons, the market-day strangers, Tamsin Vale, age six, liked river-glass. She said them like a spell, like a debt, like a prayer to a god she no longer believed was listening. The river said nothing back. Rivers never do.

At the border post they gave her papers with a false name — *Ivo Renner, ledger-keeper's daughter, bound for the free cities* — and the clerk who stamped them did not meet her eyes, and then Vhalen was behind her, and she did not cry until the road bent and she could no longer see the valley's shape against the stars.

* * *

What she buried, she buried carefully.

Not the memory of the storm — that she could not have buried if she'd tried; it surfaced nightly for years, the column of light, the kneeling arches, the sound of the mother in the courtyard. No. What Ivo Ashgrove buried, deliberately, methodically, with a craftsman's attention, over nine years of small surrenders, was the *why*.

She buried the fact that she had gone out onto that bridge wanting to be needed. She buried the greed of the working, the *yes, yes, come*, the animal joy of being filled. She buried the disappointment. She buried the knowledge — which the council's kindness had handed her, wrapped and bowed, and which she had refused to unwrap for nearly a decade — that her exile had been a sentence and not an accident, a judgment and not a fate, and that every morning she woke in the Guild of Impossible Things and chose smallness, chose dust, chose the safe gray arithmetic of other people's wonders, she was not being prudent. She was serving out her term.

It was easier to believe she had fled. Cowards could hide without shame compounding; a coward's hiding was simply what cowards did. But a woman who had been sentenced — a woman who had *accepted* — owed something different. Owed vigilance. Owed the daily, grinding labor of staying buried, because the alternative was admitting that the burial itself was the second crime: that in erasing herself she had finished the border lords' work for them, one ledger at a time, striking her own name from the rolls of the living.

So she buried it deeper each year. She buried it under audits and inventory counts. She buried it under Corvane's cold approval and the guild's warm anonymity. She buried it so well that on most days she genuinely could not feel its weight — and on the days she could, she told herself the ache was only weather coming on, an old ache in old channels, meaning nothing, meaning nothing, meaning nothing.

Nine years. She almost managed it.

And then a stranger bled out on her counting-room floor, whispering a country's name, pressing a salt-crusted map into her hands — and Ivo looked down at the margins, at the mirrored handwriting she had last seen in a girl's practice-books in a ferry-house attic, and the grave she had dug gave a shudder, deep underground, like something turning over in its sleep.

Like bells, buried, beginning to ring.
