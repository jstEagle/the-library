## 13. The Door in the Water

Sefa had rowed through water her whole life, but she had never rowed through a memory.

The valley opened before them like a held breath. On either side, the hills rose green and ordinary, sheep-dotted, indifferent—but between them lay a shallow sea that should not have been there, stretching to a horizon blurred with salt-haze, and beneath it, unmistakable even from the ridge where they stood, the shapes of rooftops.

"There," Brammel said quietly, and his voice did something she had never heard it do: it broke.

Sefa said nothing. She was learning, on this road, that silence was sometimes the only honest answer. She shifted Pip's weight on her hip—the boy had walked himself lame two days past and refused, with the particular stubbornness of children, to admit which foot hurt—and looked out over the drowned country with the eye of a ferrywoman, which is to say the eye of a woman who measures everything in terms of crossing.

The water was knee-deep at the shore and darkened slowly toward the center, where it might reach the chest, perhaps the chin. A mile, maybe more, to whatever waited in the middle. The surface lay flat as hammered tin, without a single ripple, though there was wind enough up here to pull at their cloaks. That was wrong. Water moved; that was its nature and its lesson. This water did not move.

"It's waiting," Pip said, from her hip. He said it matter-of-factly, the way he announced that bread was bread or that Sefa smelled of fish, and somehow it chilled her more than if he had wept.

"Water doesn't wait," she told him.

"This does."

* * *

They came down to the shore as the sun leaned westward, and the shallows took them one by one.

Ivo went first, of course—she always went first, though she pretended otherwise, pretending to scout while everyone understood she was simply too proud to let anyone else step into danger before her. The ledger-keeper had shed most of her disguise along the road east; the ink-stained clerk who had joined their fire three nights ago was gone, and what remained moved differently. Straighter. Watchful. Like a woman listening to something under the world.

Sefa followed with Pip on her back, one hand working the strap of the crude sling she'd made for him, her stump aching inside its wrapping the way it always ached when weather came or when old griefs stirred. The water slid cold around her knees, then her thighs, and it tasted of salt and of something older than salt—stone, iron, and underneath all of it, faintly, sweetly, like the ghost of honey.

And the names began.

She felt them before she saw them: a pressure against her mind like a word remembered on the tip of the tongue. Then, as they waded deeper and the light slanted low across the water, she looked down and saw the first rooftop beneath her feet.

It was only a rooftop—a few courses of pale stone furred with weed, a chimney stub leaning like an old man against his wall. But carved into the lintel below the waterline, in letters worn but legible, ran a name: **KELVARETH**. Not the name of a family. The name of a house, the way Vhalen named houses—not after those who built them but after what the builders hoped for. *Kelvareth.* Sefa turned the word over in her mind and it opened like a flower: *the-warmth-that-stays-after-the-fire-dies.*

She stopped wading.

"You see it," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. The storm-caller had turned back and stood waist-deep, watching her face with an expression Sefa couldn't read—hope and dread fighting for the same ground.

"I see a roof."

"Sefa."

"And a name." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "I see a name, and I know what it means, and I have never been to Vhalen in my life."

That was the thing she had not said aloud, not to any of them, not once in all the miles since the flooded valley had begun. She was not Vhaleni. Her people were river-folk, ferry-born, three generations of poling travelers between banks that belonged to no one. She had no business knowing that *Kelvareth* meant warmth-after-fire. No business feeling, when she looked down into that drowned street, a homesickness so sharp and specific it was nearly nausea—for a home she had never had.

But she knew. The way she knew the pull of a current by the sound of it. The names were down there in the water, and the water was offering them to anyone willing to remember, and remembering, apparently, asked no permission of birth or blood.

"The country's spilling out of itself," Brammel murmured. He had waded close, and his defrocked hands—one whole, one scarred from some old penance Sefa had never asked about—trailed in the brine. "It was erased from every record, every mind, and now the erasure is failing, and everything it held is leaking out through the cracks." He laughed, a strange wet sound. "I spent thirty years serving a god whose name I wasn't allowed to say. And here He is, saying Himself."

"Keep moving," Ivo said. But gently. Gently, Sefa noticed, the way you spoke near a sleeping animal—or a wound.

* * *

The deeper they went, the more the city rose around them, or rather sank around them, for they were walking through the upper stories of a town whose streets lay far below. Doorways passed at thigh-height, windows at ankle-depth, and every lintel bore its name. *Ossuin. Maravelle. Threnody-of-Bridges.* Sefa read them as she passed because she could not stop herself, and each one settled into her like a stone dropped into a well, and each stone made the well deeper.

Pip had gone silent on her back, which frightened her more than anything else that day. The boy talked constantly—songs half-remembered, questions without end, commentary on everything from Brammel's beard to the quality of the clouds. Silence from Pip was like silence from the bells of a harbor town: it meant something had changed.

"What is it, little fish?" she asked.

"They're humming," he whispered against her neck. "The houses. They're humming the songs. The ones I know." A pause, and then, smaller: "I thought I made them up."

Sefa reached back and squeezed his ankle, because there was nothing else to do with a truth that size. "Maybe you didn't make them up. Maybe you were keeping them safe."

"For who?"

"For whoever comes to collect."

Ivo had stopped, ahead of them, in water to her ribs. She stood very still, and Sefa followed her gaze to the horizon of the shallow sea—and saw it.

At first she took it for a sea-stack, one of those pillars the tide carves and abandons. But it was too regular, too deliberate, and it stood at the exact center of the drowned valley, where the deepest heart of old Vhalen must lie. As they waded closer—hours, it seemed, though the sun barely moved, another wrongness she chose not to examine—she made out its shape: two great standing stones, taller than any man, leaning toward each other but not touching, with a third laid across their tops. A gate. A doorway standing free in the middle of the water, framing nothing but sky and the far green hills.

The brine shallowed again as they approached, as if the water itself were making way, until they stood in water no deeper than their shins on a plain of pale stone that might once have been a plaza. Salt crusted everything in white rime, glittering in the last light like frost. And the gate—

The gate was covered in writing.

Every inch of it, from buried base to crossbeam, was carved with characters dense and interlocking, flowing into one another like waterweed, like currents, like script and wave-form become the same thing. Sefa had seen a hundred scripts in her years at the crossings—guild-marks, river-cant, the high formal hands of charters—and none of them looked like this, and yet she could *read* it, or almost read it, the words rising in her unbidden the way the house-names had risen.

And she understood, with a certainty that arrived whole like a fish pulled complete from a net, what the writing was.

"That's the name," she heard herself say. "The whole name. Of the country. All of it at once."

Ivo stood before the gate with her hand raised but not touching, and her face—Sefa studied faces for a living; a ferrywoman learns to read passengers the way she reads water—her face was doing several things at once, none of them simple. Longing. Terror. Recognition. And underneath them all, something that looked terribly like guilt.

"It won't open," Ivo said.

"How would you know? You haven't touched it."

"I can feel it from here." Her hand closed into a fist and lowered. "Like knocking on a door and hearing someone on the other side, breathing, and not answering."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the western pillar, on the salt-white plaza, because there was nowhere else to go and because leaving felt unthinkable now that they had arrived. Brammel built a small fire from driftwood and dried gorse carried from the hills, and the flames burned strangely upright, as if the air here also declined to move without permission.

They ate. Hard bread, smoked eel from Sefa's stores, the last of the hill-plums Pip had insisted on carrying though they'd bruised to pulp. Around the fire, the company sat in the loose circle that had become their shape on the road—Ivo and Brammel facing each other, Sefa with Pip curled against her side, the salt plain gleaming beyond them, the drowned city humming its low note beneath everything.

Because it did hum. Sefa could feel it through the soles of her boots now, a vibration too deep to be sound and too patterned to be chance. The whole valley resonated like a struck bell that had been ringing for nine years and could not stop.

"The archivist died to bring us here," Brammel said at last, into the fire. "He bled out pressing that map into your hands, Ivo. He believed it would open."

"The map brought us here." Ivo stared at the gate. In the firelight the carvings seemed to shift, currents within currents. "Here isn't enough."

"Then what is?"

Ivo didn't answer. Sefa watched her, and watched the not-answering, and felt the old ferrywoman's suspicion stir in her chest alongside the tenderness that had grown there despite her best efforts. There was something the woman knew and wasn't saying. There always was, with Ivo Ashgrove—every kindness wrapped in concealment, every truth rationed out like grain in a bad winter.

Before Sefa could decide whether to press, Pip stood up.

He did it quietly, without ceremony, the way he did everything important. He walked out of the firelight toward the gate, small bare feet slapping on the wet stone—he'd lost his boots days ago and Sefa had failed, yet again, to make him care—and he stopped before the door in the water and looked up at it the way a child looks up at a parent.

"Pip—" Ivo started.

He sang.

It was the song he'd been singing fragments of since the night they met him, the song he claimed no one taught him, the one about the girl who kept the rain in her cupped hands until the fields grew greedy and the sky grew jealous. He'd sung it badly and cheerfully around a hundred fires. Now he sang it slow, and whole, and clear, and Sefa realized with a lurch that she had never actually listened—that all this time she'd heard the melody and missed that the words were not a nonsense rhyme at all, but names. Dozens of names. Streets and houses and rivers and people, braided together in a child's clear voice, a census set to music.

The hum beneath the plaza changed.

It deepened. It gathered. Sefa felt it climb through her bones, through her missing hand, through the ache she'd carried since the day the tax-men's boat took her fingers and her livelihood in the same stroke—and the gate *shivered.*

Not shook. Shivered. The way a horse shivers off a fly. The way skin shivers at a voice from a room you thought was empty. The salt crust on the stones crackled and fell away in fine white powder, and for one heartbeat—one impossible heartbeat—the carvings *lit*, faint as foxfire, gold-green, alive, and Sefa could have sworn she heard, from somewhere impossibly far away and impossibly close, the sound of a bolt drawing back—

Then it stopped.

Pip's voice trailed off. The glow faded. The hum sank back to its long grief-note, and the gate stood exactly as it had stood, shut, silent, patient as stone.

The boy turned around. His eyes were enormous in the firelight, and wet.

"Why won't it open?" he asked. "I knowed—I *knew*—all the names. All of them. Why won't it open?"

No one answered him. Sefa got up and gathered him in, stump and all, and held him against her chest, and over the top of his head she looked at Ivo.

The storm-caller had not moved. She stood with both hands pressed flat against her own thighs, hard, as if holding herself down, and her face had gone the color of the salt, and she was looking at the gate with an expression that Sefa finally, finally recognized, because she had seen it before on the faces of passengers stepping onto her ferry: the look of a person about to pay a fare they cannot afford.

"It knows what it wants," Ivo said quietly. "And it's waiting to see if we're willing."

"Willing to do what?" Brammel asked.

And Ivo Ashgrove, who rationed truth like grain in winter, opened her mouth—and closed it again—and looked away, out across the darkening water, where the drowned lights of Kelvareth and Ossuin and Threnody-of-Bridges glimmered up through the brine like a city of stars lying on its back beneath the world.

Behind them, far to the west, above the black line of the hills they had crossed, the sky flickered. Once. Twice. Not lightning.

Something burning.

Brammel saw it too. "Those aren't storms," he said.

"No," Sefa said, pulling Pip closer. "Fires don't flicker in time."
