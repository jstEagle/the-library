## 14. Armies on the Horizon

The vanguard came to the free cities the way rot comes to a wall: from the inside first, then all at once.

Sergeant Dain Marrec watched it arrive from the tower of the Saltmarket customs house, where he had spent three days cataloguing the papers of a shipping guild that had, by every account he could find, never existed. The column appeared at dawn on the coast road—two thousand men, four storm-engines lashed to wagons like captive animals, and above them the usurper's banner, grey as dirty ice. He had seen such columns before. In Corvane's ledgers they were called *corrections*. On the road they were just dust and boots and the particular silence of soldiers who had been told what they were about to do and had decided not to think about it.

Marrec had decided not to think about it too, once. He was beginning to suspect that decision had cost him more than he'd known.

"You're expected below," said the courier, a boy of maybe sixteen with the pale eyes of a man who had already read Marrec's file and knew exactly how much it weighed. "Lord-Inquisitor Vess herself."

*Vess.* Not Vess's clerks, not Vess's proxies. The woman who had signed the order burning the archive at Tallowmere, all forty thousand volumes, and who had stood in the ash afterward with her sleeves rolled up as though she'd done the work personally. Marrec descended the stairs slowly, buying himself the length of each step.

She received him in the customs house's counting room, which the vanguard had already stripped of its furniture and replaced with a field table and a single chair. She did not offer him the chair. She stood instead, small and neat in her grey coat, sorting papers into two piles with quick, precise hands—one pile growing, one pile shrinking—and did not look up until he had stood before her long enough for the silence to become an answer of its own.

"Sergeant Marrec." Her voice was pleasant. That was always the worst part. "You've been in the free cities for eleven weeks."

"Twelve."

"Eleven weeks and some days." She set a paper down on the growing pile. "In twelve weeks you have burned two archives, seized six private collections, and taken into custody—" she glanced at a card in her hand, not because she didn't know the number but because she wanted him to watch her know it— "nine persons. Nine. And yet your reports speak constantly of a woman who keeps books, a fugitive archivist of unusual importance, a thread you are *close to pulling*. Close, Sergeant. For eleven weeks."

"I have a method, Lord-Inquisitor."

"You have a *delay*." She finally looked up, and her eyes were the color of dishwater, entirely without malice. "Do you know what I admire about you, Marrec? You were honest when we found you. A border sergeant who reported his own commander's falsified muster rolls. Most men would have kept their mouths shut and shared the profit. You wrote to the capital instead. You believed the ledger should be true even when truth was expensive. That is a rare quality." She turned another page. "It is also why I am going to say this to you plainly, because plainness is the only courtesy you have ever wanted."

She pushed the last paper across the table toward him. It bore the seal of the Office of Continuity, and beneath the seal, in the cramped hand of a junior clerk, a single instruction.

*The free cities are to be corrected. All archives, all guild records, all registries of debt and trade and birth. Fire is sufficient. Begin with Saltmarket.*

"Begin with Saltmarket," Marrec said. His mouth had gone dry. "That's eleven thousand people."

"That is eleven thousand *records*, Sergeant. People are only records that walk." Vess gathered her shrinking pile of papers and squared their edges against the tabletop, a small compulsive gesture he had watched her perform in three different cities. "The woman you've been hunting—the bookkeeper—I no longer care whether you find her. She was a means. The door out of Vhalen is a rumor with a body attached, and bodies can be replaced. What matters is that nothing remain here that remembers anything worth keeping. You will fire the archives tomorrow night. The engines will handle the rest."

"And if I decline?"

Vess smiled. It reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Then you'll be relieved, and someone else will do it, and you will spend the remainder of your career auditing grain shipments in a garrison town so far inland that the sea will be a rumor to you. Or—" and now she picked up a second document, one she had evidently been keeping beneath the others, waiting for this exact moment the way a surgeon waits with a blade already warm— "you'll be arrested. There is a warrant. It has existed since Tallowmere. Your hesitation there was noted, Sergeant. Burning a priest's papers instead of the priest. Letting his congregation scatter. I let it pass because you were useful. Useful has an expiration date."

Marrec looked at the warrant. His own name on it, spelled correctly, which felt strangely intimate—as though someone had been holding it against the light all these months, studying him through the paper.

"The free cities fed us during the war," he said. He heard his own voice and disliked how thin it sounded. "They opened their granaries when the capital wouldn't. There are children alive in Saltmarket because those granaries opened."

"There are records alive in Saltmarket," Vess said, "and records are how countries refuse to die. Burn them."

He took the order. He didn't remember deciding to take it; his hand simply moved, the way a hand moves when a man has stood too long in one posture and the body chooses obedience over collapse. Vess nodded once, satisfied, and returned to her papers, dismissing him without a word because dismissal, in her grammar, required none.

In the corridor outside, Marrec leaned against the wall and pressed the back of his hand to his mouth and thought, with terrible clarity: *I am the man who burns things now. When did I become the man who burns things?*

He could not find the moment. That was the horror of it. There had been no single choice, no midnight bargain, no day he'd woken and said *yes, I am this now*. There had only been orders, each one slightly worse than the last, each one survivable, and somewhere in the accumulation he had become a man whose name fit comfortably on a warrant.

* * *

He did not sleep. He walked the city instead, past midnight, with the order folded in his coat like a coal he couldn't put down.

Saltmarket at night was a country of small lights and smaller sounds. Fishermen mending nets by lantern-light, arguing prices in voices worn soft by repetition. A baker's boy hauling water. An old woman on a stoop singing something tuneless to herself—not a song, really, more a habit of sound, the way old women everywhere fill silence with half-remembered melody. Marrec stopped in the shadow of an awning and listened, and the old woman sang a name. Just a name, folded into the nonsense syllables, a name he did not recognize and yet which landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*Selenn.*

He knew, distantly, that names meant something here. That was the whole disease of the free cities, according to the Office of Continuity—they remembered things, they traded in remembrance, they kept ledgers of impossible goods and impossible debts. But standing in the dark listening to a stranger sing a stranger's name, Marrec felt the machinery of his own conviction grind against something it could not process. The Continuity held that memory was a vector of sedition, that forgetting was hygiene, that a name sung aloud was a door left unlocked. And yet the old woman's voice carried nothing seditious at all. It carried only tenderness. Whoever Selenn had been—mother, sister, daughter lost to the salt or the sea—the singing of her name was not rebellion. It was grief, wearing its oldest clothes.

*People are only records that walk.*

Vess's words came back to him and would not lie still. He had repeated them to subordinates a dozen times, approvingly, because they were efficient and because efficiency had always been his shelter. Now he heard them properly for the first time, heard what they actually said, and understood that he had built his entire life inside a sentence that meant *nothing here is real unless we say it is*.

Near the harbor he passed the burned shell of the ferry-house, one of his own fires from weeks ago, and stopped. Someone had chalked words on the scorched lintel. Small letters, hurried, in a child's uneven hand:

*we were here*

Three words. The Continuity would call them graffiti; the correction teams would scrub them before noon. But Marrec stood before them for a long time, feeling the warrant in his coat and the order beside it, two documents that together made him the instrument of erasing exactly this—that exact stubborn little sentence, scrawled in the ashes of something his own hands had helped destroy.

*We were here.*

He thought of Brammel, the fat defrocked priest with ink-stained fingers and a spine of actual iron, who had looked at Marrec across an interrogation table and said, mildly, *You keep asking me what I believe. Perhaps ask what you believe. I already know my answer; it costs me nothing now.* And Marrec had burned the man's records instead of the man, and had told himself it was pragmatism, and had known even then, in some sealed room of himself, that it was the first entry in a ledger he did not want to read.

The bells, someone had said. In the salt flats east of here, the ground hums with buried bells, and they sing names. He had filed the report as superstition. Superstition was also a record, technically. Everything was a record. Even the wind over the flats.

Marrec turned east and began to walk toward the harbor road, and only after several streets realized he was walking away from the customs house, away from his quarters, away from the column and the order and the fire scheduled for tomorrow night, and that his feet had made a decision his mind was still drafting its objections to.

* * *

He went back once, because he was still, in whatever remained of his nature, an honest man—a man who closed his accounts properly.

His quarters held little: uniforms, boots, a writing case, eleven years of reports filed in duplicate. He sat at the desk and, by the light of one lamp, did the thing that had brought him comfort his whole adult life. He made a list. Two columns, the way his father had taught him: what is owed, and what is owed to you.

In the left column he wrote: *the granaries of Saltmarket, which fed us*. *The archive at Tallowmere, forty thousand volumes.* *Brammel's library.* *A name sung by an old woman: Selenn.* *We were here.*

In the right column he wrote nothing for a long time. Then: *eleven years of salary. A rank. A warrant with my name spelled correctly.*

The left column filled a page. The right column filled three lines. He looked at the arithmetic of his life laid out in his own careful hand and saw, with the clarity that had gotten him promoted twice and condemned once, that he had been insolvent for years. He had been spending a currency he did not have—belief—and borrowing against a treasury that had gone empty sometime around Tallowmere, and the interest had compounded silently until tonight, when the collection notice had arrived bearing his own name.

He folded the list into his coat beside the warrant. If he was going to defect, he would do it as himself: a man who kept accounts.

Then he did the one thing left that mattered. He went down into the customs house cellar, where the vanguard's clerks had stacked the seized records of the shipping guild-that-never-existed, and he struck a lamp, and he set fire to them himself.

It felt, absurdly, like prayer.

Let Vess find ash in the morning and conclude the fugitive bookkeeper had struck first. Let the correction begin with a lie, for once—let the great machine of forgetting devour one false entry and choke on it. It was a small sabotage, a clerk's revenge, unworthy of the word *resistance*, and Marrec stood in the smoke watching eleven years of his own reports curl and blacken, and understood that he was not burning documents at all. He was burning the version of himself who had written them.

By the time the alarm bells rang—real bells, the city's own, crying fire—he was over the harbor wall and down among the pilings, moving east along the tideline with a stolen oilskin over his uniform, a deserter's bundle on his shoulder, and the salt flats somewhere ahead of him in the dark, humming, they said, with buried bells.

Behind him, Saltmarket's fire crews fought the blaze in the cellar while the archives slept untouched, and Vess stood at a window with her dishwater eyes narrowed at the flames, doing arithmetic of her own.

Ahead of him lay the flats, and the company of the forgotten, and a woman who kept books whom he had hunted across half a country and now intended to find again—for reasons he could not yet have written in any ledger, except perhaps this one: *a debt is a debt, and mine has come due*.

He walked east into the dark, a hunter becoming a fugitive, carrying his warrant like a confession, and behind him the horizon began, very faintly, to glow with the kindling of war-engines—and ahead, beneath his boots, so faint he might have imagined it, the ground hummed.
