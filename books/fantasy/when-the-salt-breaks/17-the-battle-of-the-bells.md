## 17. The Battle of the Bells

Pip had learned to count the way the ground counted.

Not in numbers—Sefa had tried teaching him numbers on the road, and he could manage up to twenty before they went slippery—but in the way salt hummed under his bare feet when he pressed them flat against it. One hum meant morning coming. Two hums meant the bells were restless. Three hums meant someone was remembering something hard enough that the land felt it.

That morning there were so many hums he stopped counting and just stood there with his toes curled in the white crust, listening.

The army came out of the west like a bruise spreading across a pale arm.

Pip had never seen an army before, except in the stories Brammel used to tell before the inquisitor took him away, and those armies had been made mostly of words. This one was made of dust and metal and the smell of men who hadn't bathed since the free cities. They came over the low rise beyond the flats in long grey lines, banners hanging limp because even the wind didn't want to touch them, and behind them rumbled the war-engines—the stolen storm-craft, iron cradles on wheels, each one holding a knot of captured weather that glowed sickly green between its bars like fireflies kept in a jar.

"They're smaller than I thought," Pip said.

Sefa, crouched beside him at the lip of the trench they'd spent three days digging, snorted. "Everything's smaller from far off, little fish. Wait till they're close enough to count their teeth."

"I can count to twenty."

"Then you'll run out."

But her hand found his shoulder and stayed there, and Pip knew what that hand meant even if her mouth said jokes. Her one hand. She'd lost the other to the river years ago, she'd told him once, back when telling him things had still been a thing she did reluctantly, like paying a toll. Now her hand stayed on his shoulder the whole time the army arranged itself against the sky, and Pip pretended to watch the soldiers instead of watching how her knuckles had gone white around the haft of her ferry-pole.

* * *

Ivo Ashgrove stood at the door.

That was where she always stood now, ever since Marrec had come running out of the west two days ago with the usurper's execution order still folded in his coat and a look on his face like a man who has read his own ledger and found it short. The door stood half-sunk in the shallow brine at the heart of the flats, its carved name worn smooth by water and forgetting, and Ivo stood before it every hour that wasn't sleep, mouthing syllables, holding pieces of Vhalen up to her mind's eye like a jeweler checking stones for cracks.

Pip watched her do it while the army formed its lines.

She looked small out there. Smaller than the storm-caller in the songs Pip remembered without being taught. Her hair was coming loose from its braid and the wind off the flats pushed it across her face, and she kept talking to the stone gate in a voice too low for him to hear, and sometimes she would stop and press both palms flat against the carved letters the way you press a hand to a fevered forehead.

"She won't finish it," Sefa said quietly. "Not in time."

"Maybe she doesn't have to," Pip said.

Sefa looked down at him. In the flat white light her face was all angles and salt-dust, and her eyes were doing the thing they did when she was deciding whether to believe him or feed him another lie to keep him quiet. He hated that thing. He was nine years old, nearly ten, and he had decided some months ago on the drowned road that he was done being managed.

"The bells are already ringing," he said. "Can't you hear them?"

Sefa went still. Then, slowly, she turned her head toward the east, toward the deep flats where nothing stood but salt and the memory of streets, and Pip watched her listen the way you watch someone try to remember a language they spoke as a child.

"No," she said. But her voice wavered on it, like a rope taking weight.

* * *

The first engines fired at midmorning.

Pip heard it before he saw it—a sound like the sky tearing along a seam—and then a column of green-black cloud unspooled from the nearest engine and crawled across the flats toward the trenches, trailing lightning the way a rotten log trails ants. It wasn't real weather. He knew that the way he knew the difference between bread and the picture of bread. Real weather breathed. This stuff twitched.

"Hold!" someone shouted down the line—an old soldier named Vess who had come over with Marrec and still wore half his usurper's uniform because the other half had burned. "Hold until they're in the salt! Let the ground do the work!"

The conscripts came on. That was the word Marrec used for them, later, when Pip asked: not soldiers, conscripts. Men pulled off farms and out of fishing towns in the usurper's territories, handed spears, told that Vhalen had never existed and that anyone who said otherwise would be unmade along with it. They came on in their grey lines, boots crunching the salt crust, and the false storm rolled ahead of them, and Pip pressed his hands over his ears because the wrongness of it made his teeth ache.

And beneath everything, getting louder, the bells began to sing.

* * *

He couldn't tell anyone exactly when he'd first heard them. It had been somewhere on the flats, the night they crossed, when the ground hummed three times and he woke up and the humming had words in it. Not words the way people talked. Words the way water talks, if you lie under a boat long enough—meaning soaked through sound, arriving whole in your chest before your head gets any say about it.

Names. The bells sang names.

They sang them now, as the conscripts came on. The salt trembled. Deep under the crust, deeper than roots go, deeper than the drowned rooftops with their true names still painted on the ridgelines, bronze throats that had waited nine years in the dark opened themselves and rang, and the sound didn't come up through the air at all. It came up through the ground. Through boots. Through bones.

Pip stood on the trench lip and let it pour into him.

*Maren. Maren Collet. Maren Collet who baked on Tallow Street. Maren Collet whose granddaughter is standing in the fourth line with a spear she doesn't know how to hold.*

He heard it plain as porridge.

*Osk. Osk Vey. Osk Vey who mended nets. Osk Vey who taught his grandson the ferry-song, and the grandson forgot, but the bells didn't.*

He looked down the trench at the defenders—the refugees, the fishermen, the defected soldiers with their torn-off badges—and saw that some of them had gone pale. Old Vess had taken off his helmet. A woman named Dalla from the flooded valley had her mouth moving, shaping a word she didn't know she knew.

And out past the trenches, in the grey lines, the conscripts were starting to stop.

* * *

It began like anything begins: one man.

He was young, maybe seventeen, and he was walking in the second wave with his spear held wrong, and when the bells sang the name *Maren Collet* he stumbled as though the ground had reached up and grabbed his ankle. Pip watched him through the shimmer of heat and false storm. Watched him stand very still while the men around him shuffled past. Watched his lips move. And then the boy dropped his spear—it fell into the salt with a sound like a spoon dropped in an empty house—and sat down right there in the open, in the path of the storm-craft, and put his face in his hands.

Nobody shot him. Nobody dared, somehow. The bells were singing too loudly for courage.

It spread after that, the way fire spreads through dry grass, the way a yawn spreads through a room of tired people. A woman in the fifth line tore her helmet off and screamed a name—not *Maren*, another one, one the bells had given her—and started walking backward, away from the flats, away from the war, and two men followed her, then five. An officer on a grey horse rode up and down the lines shouting orders, and the orders dissolved in the ringing like sugar in rain, because you cannot shout louder than your own grandmother's name sung out of the bones of a country everyone swore had never existed.

*You are Vhalen,* sang the bells, in the voice of buried bronze and brine. *You were told otherwise. You were lied to in ledgers and charters and burned books. But we kept the roll. We kept the roll of every name, and here is yours, and here is yours, and here—*

Pip was crying. He noticed this with mild surprise, the way you notice rain. He wasn't sad. That was the strange part. Something was pouring through him that was bigger than sad and bigger than glad, something with no name yet that he supposed he'd grow into, and it came out of his eyes because there wasn't anywhere else for it to go.

"Pip." Sefa's hand on his shoulder again, hauling him down behind the trench lip as the false storm finally reached the lines. Lightning cracked overhead, green and sour, and the smell of scorched salt filled the world. "Get down, little fish, get—"

"It's working," he said. "Sefa, it's working, they're hearing them, they're hearing their grandmothers—"

"I hear it too." And there it was, the crack in her voice, the rope taking full weight. "God help me, I hear it. My mother's name. It's singing my mother's name and I haven't said it aloud in nine years because saying it felt like digging her grave twice." She laughed, wet and ragged. "Anwen. Her name was Anwen. The bells remember my mother, Pip."

Down the line, the defense was barely a defense anymore. The conscripts weren't attacking; they were kneeling. Whole ranks of them, spears abandoned, faces lifted toward the empty flats as though the sky had started reading aloud from a book they'd been told was blank. Some of the usurper's regulars fought on—the ones from too far away, maybe, or the ones with nothing buried in them—but they fought in little clots now, islands in a flood of kneeling men, and old Vess was striding along the trench with his helmet held high on his sword-point shouting, "Throw down! Throw down and listen! You're being counted! You're being *counted!*"

* * *

The engines were the problem.

Pip saw it happen the way you see a rockslide start—one pebble, then all of it. The engine-crews were not conscripts. They were the usurper's own, hooded men who tended the captured storms like priests tending a captive god, and the bells did not reach them, or they had stopped their ears with wax, or whatever lived inside them had been scooped hollow years ago. As their infantry melted around them, the crews worked faster, cranks spinning, and the green knots inside the iron cradles swelled and brightened, and the false storms crawling across the flats grew fat and hungry.

One of them turned toward the door.

Toward Ivo.

She was still standing there, small and stubborn, her hands on the carved name, and Pip watched the green wall of borrowed lightning rear up over her like a wave over a swimmer, and something in his chest tore straight down the middle.

"No," he said.

He was running before Sefa could catch him. He heard her shout—he heard lots of things, all jumbled together, the battle and the bells and Sefa's voice and the shriek of the engines—and none of it mattered, because the bells were pouring through him now like he was a channel dug on purpose, like all those nights of counting hums had been somebody carving a pipe through a little boy and waiting for the water. His feet hit the brine. His feet hit the shallows. The green light fell across him, cold as pond ice, and Ivo turned at last from the door with terror all over her face, reaching for him, reaching for a storm she'd sworn off—

And Pip opened his mouth and sang.

* * *

He didn't know the song. That was the truth he'd carry afterward, when people asked: he didn't know it, he only delivered it. It rose out of the ground through his bare feet and out through his throat, a child's round, the kind of song children sing in Vhalen to call the tide pools in, four notes going round and round, and the moment it left him the buried bells caught it and carried it, and every bell under the salt took up the round and passed it on, and the sound went up through the green storm like sunlight going up through water.

The stolen weather shuddered.

Lightning stuttered mid-fall. The great green wall over Ivo flickered like a lamp in wind, and inside its glow Pip could see the shape of it—not clouds, but *grief*, grief packed into iron jars by men who thought a country could be erased if you burned enough paper—and the grief heard the round, heard the bells, heard the name of itself, and it wanted to go home.

The nearest engine burst.

Not exploded—*burst*, like a seedpod, like a fist opening. Its captured storm tore free of the iron cradle and rose into the sky unwinding, green fading to grey, grey to silver, and then it broke apart into ordinary rain, sweet and clean, falling on the salt flats for the first time in nine years. The second engine burst after it. Then the third. All along the usurper's line the iron cradles popped open one by one like buds forced early, and the stolen storms climbed free and wept themselves out over the battlefield, and conscripts threw back their heads and let the rain hit their faces and some of them wept harder than the sky did.

Through it all, Pip stood in the shallows and sang, and the bells rang, and the rain came down.

* * *

Afterward—after the last engine was a broken shell and the last loyal officer had fled east with a handful of men and the flats lay steaming under honest rain—Pip found himself sitting on the trench lip wrapped in Sefa's cloak, shivering, hoarse, happier than he had ever been in a life that contained almost no happiness until a year ago.

Dalla brought him broth. Vess brought him a helmet to sit on, which was backwards, but kind. And Ivo Ashgrove knelt down in front of him in the wet salt, mud to her knees, rain still dripping off her chin, and looked at him for a long time without saying anything.

"You heard them," she said finally. "Before any of us. You've been hearing them since the crossing."

Pip nodded, then thought better of modesty. "Since before that. On the boat. I never said because—" He shrugged. "Because grown-ups take things away when they find out you have them."

Something moved across Ivo's face, quick and painful, like a bird hitting a window. She reached out and pushed his wet hair back from his forehead, the way nobody had done since Brammel was taken.

"What else do they tell you?" she asked. "The bells. What else is in the song?"

Pip listened. Down in the dark, under the rain-wet salt, the bells were still ringing, softer now, patient, the way a mother sings after the crying stops. And underneath the names—under Maren and Osk and Anwen—there was something else gathering, something vast and slow turning over in its sleep, like the biggest bell of all beginning to swing.

"They're saving the last verse," Pip said. "For her." He nodded toward the door, where Ivo had stood all morning. "For whoever opens it. It's the country's name, I think. The whole one." He frowned, trying to hold the shape of it, feeling it slip. "It's too big for me. It's too big for anybody."

Ivo went very still.

Beyond her, out on the flats, the rain was stopping, and the eastern horizon had begun to glow—not dawn, it was hours from dawn—with a light that had nothing to do with weather. Dust, miles of it, rising off the earth in a slow brown curtain. The vanguard had been broken here. But behind the vanguard, the way a body follows a hand, came everything else.

"They'll be here by tomorrow," Sefa said quietly, at Ivo's shoulder. "All of them."

Ivo looked at the door. Then at Pip. Then out at the dust of the coming army, and Pip saw her jaw set in the particular way it set when she was about to do something brave and stupid and necessary, all at once.

"Then I'd best learn the last verse," she said.
