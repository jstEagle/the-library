## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The first thing every apprentice taster learns is that the tongue is a liar.

It lies about honey, telling you it is sweet when half the honeys of the world carry enough gray-vein poppy to stop an old man's heart between bites. It lies about wine, which can be coaxed into holding arsenic the way a riverbed holds silt — invisibly, patiently, until someone drinks. And it lies most of all about almonds, because bitter almond and sweet almond are twins, and one of them loves you and one of you will die.

Wendell Nystrom had learned this at nine years old, kneeling on a cold kitchen floor in Saltmere, his mouth burning from a single drop of decoction his mother had held under his nose like a scolding grandmother.

"Tell me what you taste," she'd said.

"Bitter," he'd said, eyes watering. "Bitter, and — and green somehow. Like chewing a leaf off the wrong tree."

"Good." She'd taken the vial back before he could be tempted to touch it again. "That's hemlock's little cousin. Now drink your milk."

He had drunk his milk. He had drunk a great deal of milk over the years since, and broth, and bread, and wine, and soup, and once — memorably — an entire roasted pheasant prepared by a cook who bore a grudge against the magistrate's second cousin, and who had seasoned it with something Wendell still refused to name aloud because naming it gave it power, the way saying a bog's true name might make the bog notice you.

Now he was thirty-four, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who haul things for a living, narrow through the waist in the way of men whose stomachs have been professionally inconvenienced for two decades, and he was standing in the kitchens of Thistlewake Manor doing what he did every morning: lying to his tongue on purpose.

"Almond," he announced, after a careful sip of the marzipan tart meant for Lady Vessimore's breakfast table. "Sweet almond, ground fine, with rosewater. A whisper of salt." He paused, tilted the fork, considered. "And too much salt. Tell Halfrida if she puts that much salt in again I'll start tasting *her* cooking instead, and we'll see how long her pride survives."

Behind him, the kitchen girl — Pim, fourteen, all elbows and freckles and an unshakeable conviction that Wendell was the most interesting man alive — snorted into her apron.

"You're not going to taste Halfrida," she said. "She'd poison you within the week."

"She'd try," Wendell agreed mildly, moving on to the porridge. "I'd give her three days. She has a temper but no patience, and patience is ninety percent of a good poisoning. The other ten percent is cleanup."

"That's horrible."

"That's the trade, Pim." He set down the spoon, rolled a sip of porridge across his tongue the way other men rolled wine, and waited. One count. Two. Three. The bitterness he was hunting never came; only oats, cream, a thread of honey, and yes, entirely too much salt. "Everything in this house is oversalted today. Did someone kick the salt cellar?"

"Halfrida says salt keeps the valley safe."

Wendell's spoon paused, just briefly, above the bowl.

Saltmere. Named for the white crust that rimmed the valley floor like frost that never melted, the wide flats where nothing grew and nothing rotted and, so the old stories went, nothing *escaped*. Every child in the valley grew up hearing the same rhymes. *Salt on the sill and salt on the stone, what sleeps below must sleep alone.* Farmers salted their doorsteps. Mothers salted their children's baths. Halfrida, like every good cook in Saltmere, salted everything twice — once for flavor, and once, quietly, at the window, for the thing underneath.

Wendell had stopped believing in the thing underneath when he was twelve, mostly because his mother — who had believed in it fiercely — had died of a perfectly ordinary fever, undeterred by forty years of salting her own doorstep. Grief had made an atheist of him in more ways than one.

But he still tasted carefully. That habit had nothing to do with gods or ward-stones. It had to do with the simple fact that people poisoned each other, everywhere, always, and somebody had to stand between them and the dinner plate.

"Porridge is clean," he said. "Oversalted, but clean. Send it up."

* * *

Thistlewake Manor was not, strictly speaking, a manor. It had been built by a wool merchant two generations back who had decided that wealth without pretension was like a feast without a table, and so had raised a gray stone pile with crenellations decorative enough to be charming and impractical enough to be honest. The current master, Lord Aldous Vessimore, was the merchant's grandson and had inherited both the house and the family talent for being rich without ever quite becoming important — a state of affairs that suited Wendell perfectly. Important lords attracted assassins. Unimportant lords merely attracted tax collectors, and tax collectors could be handled with a locked gate and a rude letter.

Wendell's official title was Keeper of the Table, which sounded grander than it was. His actual duties were these: taste everything before the household ate it, keep the household's antidote cabinet stocked and alphabetized, maintain a professional relationship with every cook, peddler, and traveling merchant within twenty miles, and write, once a month, a report to the Magistrate's Office in Lowmark detailing anything unusual he had eaten.

In nineteen years, the "anything unusual" line of his reports had contained, variously: a suspiciously good cheese (turned out to be a new goat), a suspiciously bad cheese (turned out to be revenge, resolved amicably), and a pie containing a human finger (turned out to be pig knuckle, mislabeled by a butcher with poor lighting and worse eyes). He had written up the finger incident with great care. It remained the highlight of his career.

Today's tasting finished, he took his mid-morning ritual onto the back step: bread, hard cheese, a cup of small beer, and the sun. Pim joined him uninvited, as she always did, settling onto the step beside him with the ease of a cat that had decided a lap was now communal property.

"Tell me about the worst thing you ever tasted," she said. This was her favorite game. She asked it perhaps four times a week and never remembered the answers, or pretended not to.

"The worst?" Wendell chewed thoughtfully. "Taste-wise, or consequence-wise?"

"Taste-wise."

"There's a root that grows up past the high pastures. Wolfsbane's uglier brother. Cooks itself, it smells so strong, and it tastes like — imagine a candle, but angry. I had two drops of its extract in my tea when I was seventeen. An old woman in Lowmark was testing whether our household's guards were worth their pay." He shrugged. "My tongue swelled up like a wet boot. I couldn't speak for six days. My mother said it was the quietest week of her life and she treasured it."

Pim laughed. "Consequence-wise, then?"

"Consequence-wise." He turned his cup slowly. "A man came through Saltmere when I was twenty-two. Peddler. Friendly fellow, good teeth, sold ribbons and needles and a lovely line in flattery. He dined at three houses on his way through the valley. At the third house, he put something in the stew." Wendell drank. "Not to kill anyone. Just to see. That's what he told the magistrate afterward, calm as Sunday. He wanted to know if the valley's tasters were real or ceremonial. He'd heard Saltmere trained its own."

"What happened to him?"

"He spent a year breaking rocks on the Lowmark road. And the family whose stew it was spent a very bad night, and the mother of that family never cooked mutton again." Wendell looked out over the kitchen garden, where the bean rows stood in neat green ranks and the air smelled of thyme and turned earth. "That's the part nobody tells apprentices. The poison isn't the worst of it. The worst is the morning after, when everyone who ate from your table looks at you and wonders how close they came, and you wonder it too, and nobody says anything, and the porridge gets cold."

Pim was quiet for a moment, which for Pim was nearly unprecedented. Then: "Is that why you taste everything twice?"

"It's why I taste everything twice," Wendell agreed. "Once for the household, and once for me. If I'm going to ask a family to trust my tongue, my tongue ought to have some skin in the game."

* * *

He spent the afternoon, as he spent most afternoons, in the antidote room — a small stone chamber off the pantry that smelled of vinegar, dried herbs, and the particular mineral sharpness of ground salts. Shelves lined the walls in tidy ranks: emetics and adsorbents, tinctures of ipecac and activated charcoal, honey jars labeled in his mother's fading hand, and one locked box, bottom shelf, containing things he hoped never to open. He inventoried it all by memory and by ledger, checking quantities against the neat columns in the Warden's Ledger — his own private book, bound in oiled leather, in which nineteen years of tastings were recorded in a cramped, disciplined script.

Entry 4,112. Marzipan tart, porridge, small beer, spring water, brown bread, hard cheese. All clean. All oversalted. Note: speak to Halfrida re: salt.

He was halfway through the inventory when the light changed.

It was a subtle thing — the kind of change most people would have missed, would have blamed on a cloud. But the antidote room had one small window, east-facing, and at half past three in the afternoon the sun came through it at an angle that laid a bright bar across the third shelf, right across the jar of powdered chalk, every day, in every season, because Saltmere's weather was as reliable as its salt. Wendell had noticed the bar of light on his second day in this room, seventeen years ago, and had used it as a clock ever since, because a man who trusted his tongue learned to distrust clocks.

At half past three, the bar of light should have been there.

It wasn't.

Wendell set down his pen. He looked at the window. The sky outside was the flat, pale, cloudless blue of a Saltmere autumn afternoon — no cloud anywhere near the sun, nothing to explain the dimness except that the dimness was coming from inside the glass itself, a faint gray shimmer across the pane, like breath fogging from the wrong side.

Then it passed. The bar of light snapped back across the chalk jar, sharp and ordinary, and the room was just a room.

Wendell sat very still for a while.

There were explanations. There were always explanations. Dust on the glass. A trick of his eyes — and his eyes, like the rest of him, had been thoroughly educated in the difference between *strange* and *dangerous*, and knew that most strange things were only tiredness wearing a costume. He was thirty-four. He worked long hours. He had eaten a suspicious amount of cheese that morning.

Still, he picked up the Warden's Ledger, and after a moment's hesitation, he wrote:

*Entry 4,113. Antidote room window, approx. 3:30. Light dimmed, duration some seconds. Glass appeared frosted from within. No cause identified. Probably dust.*

He looked at the word *probably* for a long time. Then he left it. A taster's ledger recorded what happened, not what he wished had happened. His mother had drilled that into him along with the hemlock: *write the truth, Wendell. The day you start editing your observations is the day you miss the poison that kills someone.*

His mother had also, he reflected, salted her doorstep every night for forty years against a monster under the valley, and he had loved her anyway. People contained contradictions. That was rather the point of people.

He finished the inventory. He banked the fire in the stillroom. And as the light went long and gold across the kitchen garden, he stood on the back step with his cup of beer and let himself feel, briefly and privately, the contentment that was the true reward of his strange trade: a day in which nobody had been poisoned, including himself. Nineteen years of days like that. You couldn't measure a career in triumphs. You measured it in uneventful evenings, in families that slept soundly because a broad-shouldered man with a liar's tongue had sipped everything first.

Tomorrow he would ride to Lowmark with the monthly report, as he did on the first market-day of every month. Tonight he would eat supper — Halfrida's mutton stew, oversalted, almost certainly clean — and sleep in his small room above the stable, and the world would remain exactly as sensible as it had always been.

Which was, of course, when the dog started barking.

Not the way dogs bark at cats, or at the rag-and-bone man, or at their own reflections in the rain barrel. Biscuit was an old hound with a philosopher's temperament who had barked maybe a dozen times in the five years Wendell had known her, and every one of those times had been justified. What he heard now was low and continuous, rising from the yard near the garden gate — not alarm exactly, though there was alarm in it, but something older than alarm. The sound a dog makes at a door it knows should not be opened.

Wendell set down his cup.

The evening had gone violet at the edges, and the salt flats beyond the valley's eastern rim caught the last light and threw it back white as bone. Between the kitchen garden and the flats ran the low stone wall that marked the boundary of Thistlewake's land, and beyond the wall the ground fell away in a long slope of scrub and silverweed toward the flats themselves.

Something was lying at the wall's foot.

Wendell crossed the garden quickly, gravel crunching, Biscuit pressing against his knee like a furry conscience. As he drew closer the shape resolved: small, slight, wrapped in what might once have been cloth and was now mostly mud and river-reed. A child. A boy, by the look of him, seven years old perhaps, eight — thin as a winter branch, dark hair plastered to his forehead, lips faintly blue.

From the flats. From *across* the flats. Nothing crossed the flats. The flats were the whole reason the valley existed, the reason for the rhymes and the doorsteps and the ward-stones that dotted the rim like gray teeth. Nothing crossed the flats, and certainly nothing that weighed less than the wind.

Wendell knelt. The boy's skin was cold — properly, dangerously cold, the cold of deep water — but there was a pulse, quick and fluttering, and when Wendell pressed two fingers beneath the boy's jaw the child's eyes opened.

They were gray. Not blue-gray, not storm-gray. Gray the way the flats were gray, the color of old salt, and they fixed on Wendell with a focus no half-drowned child should have possessed.

The boy's mouth moved. No sound came out — not at first, not for several tries, his throat working around words like a man learning to swallow. Then, in a voice cracked dry as the flats themselves, in a tongue that had been dead for six hundred years, in words that Wendell did not understand but that his bones, inexplicably, recognized —

— the child spoke.

Behind him, at the edge of the garden, Biscuit stopped barking all at once, and every ward-stone on the valley's rim began, very softly, to sing.
