## 2. Saltmere Rules

There were eleven rules in Saltmere, and Wendell Nystrom could recite all of them in the time it took to swallow a mouthful of suspect soup—which was, coincidentally, the only way he ever got anyone to listen.

"Rule one," he said, holding up a finger while the kitchen of the Gilded Boar held its collective breath around him. "Nothing crosses the threshold that wasn't born here or carried here by honest feet."

He chewed. He swallowed. He waited the traditional count of ten, during which the cook, a mountain of a woman named Bess Halloway, gripped her ladle like a weapon she was legally permitted to use.

"Rule two," Wendell continued, "no strangers stay past sundown unless vouched for by three households." He paused, smacked his lips thoughtfully. "Bess, is there tansy in this?"

"A whisper," Bess said. "For the flavor."

"There's more than a whisper. There's a full argument." He took another spoonful anyway, because rule seven of Wendell's own private rules—unwritten, unshared—was that a taster who flinched at tansy had no business calling himself a taster at all. The bitterness bloomed across his tongue like a small, rude flower, and he let it sit there until it wilted on its own. Twenty years of deliberate poisoning had given his body opinions most bodies didn't have. His liver, if livers could be said to hold grudges, held several.

"It's fine," he announced, and the Gilded Boar's common room exhaled, and the evening's first dozen diners were served.

This was the whole of Wendell's profession: eat first, live or don't, and make it look boring either way. In the capital they'd called it an honor. Here in Saltmere they called it what it was—a job nobody wanted, done by a man who'd been built for it the way a mule is built for carrying things.

* * *

Saltmere sat in a valley folded between two gray ridges, and everything about it was designed to be overlooked. The houses were low and stone-roofed. The roads came in and went out again without ambition. Even the river seemed apologetic, sliding along the valley floor like a guest trying not to wake the host.

But the salt was real, and so was the warding.

Every child in Saltmere learned the rules before they learned their letters, because the rules were older than letters, older than the kingdom, possibly older than the concept of learning. Rule one, about thresholds. Rule three: no iron left out under the open sky overnight. Rule four: if the mist comes down off the ridge before dawn, stay indoors until the bell rings twice. Rule five—the big one, the one carved into the lintel above the meetinghouse door in characters nobody could read anymore—**what sleeps beneath must not be fed**.

Nobody knew exactly what slept beneath. That was sort of the point of rule five. You didn't feed what you couldn't name, the same way you didn't poke a sleeping dog whose breed you'd never identified. The old texts in the warden's office spoke of a war, of sorcery bound and buried, of a valley salted—not with the mineral in the flats, but with intention—and of wards laid so deep that the founders had hoped the land itself would forget them.

The land hadn't forgotten. But it had, mostly, kept quiet.

Wendell had grown up thinking all valleys were like this. It wasn't until he was fourteen and traveled to Lowmarket with his father to sell wool that he learned other towns didn't ring bells for mist, didn't check travelers' hands for marks, didn't have a Warden's Ledger with a page for every soul born inside the boundary stones. He'd come home with that particular hollow feeling you get when you realize your family is strange and no one told you.

"You're back early," his mother had said, which was how she said *I missed you*, and also *stop looking at me like that*.

"The people out there are wrong," twelve-year-old Wendell had reported.

"They're not wrong," his mother said. "They're just asleep. There's a difference, and someday you'll wish you were asleep too."

She'd been right, in the way mothers are irritatingly right. Wendell was thirty-four now, and he wished it most nights.

* * *

The Gilded Boar emptied out by nine, as it always did, because rule eight was that honest folk were abed by dark, and Saltmere's folk were nothing if not honest. Wendell stayed to help Bess bank the hearth, partly from habit and partly because Bess paid him in leftovers and gossip, the two currencies he valued most.

"Ilsette came by this afternoon," Bess said, in the tone of someone dropping a lit coal onto a dry floor.

"Did she."

"Wanted to know when you'd be in. Wanted to know if you'd been eating well." Bess snorted. "Asking a poison-eater if he eats well. That girl has a sense of humor like a wet sock."

"She's not a girl anymore. She's the Warden's apprentice."

"She's a girl who used to put frogs in your boots, and she'll be a girl doing it at sixty. Some things don't age." Bess wiped down the bar with the ferocity of a woman punishing the wood for existing. "You should talk to her, Wendell. She looked—" Bess hunted for the word, found it, disliked it, and used it anyway. "*Worried.*"

Ilsette Vane worried the way rivers ran downhill: constantly, quietly, and toward something you didn't want to think about. She was the closest thing Wendell had to a friend who wasn't paid in stew, which meant she was the person most likely to tell him true things, which meant he generally avoided her on principle.

"I'll see her tomorrow," he said.

"You'll see her tonight," said a voice from the doorway, "because I've been standing out in the cold listening to Bess slander my sense of humor, and I'm coming in whether you offer me cider or not."

Ilsette stepped into the firelight. She was twenty-eight, sharp-faced, with ink stains on her fingers that never fully washed out and a braid that suggested she'd done it while walking and reading simultaneously. Under one arm she carried the thing every Saltmere native both respected and dreaded: the Warden's Ledger, thick as a brick, bound in leather gone soft as cloth with handling.

"Cider," Wendell said. "And whatever Bess poisoned tonight, minus the tansy."

"Tansy builds character," Bess said, but she poured it.

* * *

They took the corner table, the one with the bench worn smooth by four generations of elbows. Ilsette set the Ledger down with the reverence of a priest laying a relic and then immediately ruined it by drumming her fingers on the cover.

"Before you say anything," Wendell began.

"Then I'll go first. Good. Faster." She leaned in. "When did you last walk the boundary stones?"

"Autumn. With Old Corvin, before his knees quit."

"The eastern stretch?"

"The eastern stretch," Wendell confirmed slowly, "where the third stone has always leaned a little, yes."

"It doesn't lean anymore." Ilsette's voice dropped. "It stands straight. Perfectly straight. And the moss that's been on it since my grandmother's grandmother died off it in a single night. And the carving underneath—the old script—it's *deeper*. As if somebody re-cut it from below."

Wendell turned his cider mug a quarter turn on the table. A habit. Something for his hands to do while his head caught up.

"Stones settle," he offered.

"Stones settle *downward*, Wendell. They don't stand up straighter and grow sharper. I checked the Ledger entries going back a hundred years. Every inspection notes that stone leaning east. Every one. Until mine." She pulled a folded paper from her coat—a rubbing, charcoal on cloth, of the carved characters. They looked like lightning that had decided to become writing. "I showed Master Corvin. He looked at it for a long time and then he told me to fetch him his good boots."

"His good boots?"

"Corvin hasn't worn his good boots since the last time the wards were checked twice in one year. Which, according to the Ledger, was forty-one years ago." She met his eyes. "Something is pushing on the boundary from the inside, Wendell. Something is *testing* it. And rule five says we don't feed what sleeps beneath—but nothing in the rules says anything about what happens when it starts waking up hungry on its own."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, the inn's cat knocked something over with the casual destructiveness of cats everywhere.

"What do you want from me?" Wendell asked. "I taste soup."

"You taste everything. That's the point of you." Ilsette tapped the table. "You've got a body that notices things other bodies shrug off. So notice this: for the past week, have your meals tasted different? Off? Metallic? Like the food itself was nervous?"

He wanted to laugh. He started to laugh. It died somewhere in his chest, because now that she'd said it—

"Bread," he said. "Tuesday's bread. From Marta's oven, same recipe she's used since I had milk teeth. It tasted like pennies. I assumed her well water had turned."

"And did you check her well?"

"Marta would sooner let me inspect her teeth than her well."

"So we'll go together. Tonight." Ilsette stood, hauling the Ledger up under her arm. "Because there's one more thing, and I saved it for in person, because if I wrote it down even the Ledger might get ideas."

"Bess can hear us, you know."

"Bess already knows everything," Bess called from behind the bar, not looking up. "Go on."

Ilsette lowered her voice anyway, bending close, and Wendell smelled ink and cold air and, faintly, the mineral tang of the salt flats.

"This morning," she said, "Corvin and I walked the western boundary. Down where the stream leaves the valley. The wards there aren't just thinning, Wendell. There's a place where they're *worn through*. A gap, maybe a hand wide, in a ward that's supposed to be seamless. And the ground outside it is churned up. Mud, tracks—small tracks, a child's size, barefoot—and they lead *in*. Through the gap. Toward the village."

The warmth drained out of the room, or perhaps just out of Wendell. Barefoot, in this season, across the flats. Nobody's child walked the flats barefoot. Nobody's child walked the flats at all. The flats were where the salt killed anything green, and the ward-wards—the outer ring—discouraged anything with sense from crossing them in either direction.

"A lost kid from over the ridge?" he said, grasping. "A shepherd's brat?"

"In winter. Barefoot. Alone. Over the flats, past the outer stones, through a gap in a ward older than the kingdom." Ilsette straightened. "Or else something wearing the shape of a child. Either way, Corvin wants every household warned by dawn, and he wants you tasting everything that comes into the village, even the well water, even the grain, starting now."

"That's not how any of this works—"

"Rules change when the stones do." For just a moment, under the ink-stained apprentice, Wendell saw the girl who'd once put a frog in his boot and then, when he yelled, put another one in the other boot so the situation would be symmetrical. She was scared. She was very good at not showing it, and she was showing it anyway, because it was him.

"One more thing," she said at the door, hand on the latch. "The tracks led in, like I said. But Corvin followed them to the millpond, and there they stopped. Dead stop, at the water's edge. No tracks out." She swallowed. "And this evening, old Tam Whitlock swears he saw a light out on the pond. Green, he says. Sitting on the water like a lantern with nobody holding it."

Outside, the wind came down off the ridges and moved through the village streets, rattling shutters, testing doors, patient as something that had waited a very long time and was beginning to wonder if its patience was finally going to be rewarded.

Wendell looked at his half-finished cider, at the warm and ordinary room, at everything he'd spent thirty-four years assuming was permanent.

"Get your coat," Ilsette said. "We're checking the pond before the bell rings first watch. And Wendell—if anything speaks to you tonight, in a language you don't know—"

"Don't answer it," he recited. "Rule nine."

"Rule nine," she agreed grimly, and opened the door onto the cold.
