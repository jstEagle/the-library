## 3. The Child at the Threshold

The storm came up the valley on a Tuesday, which Wendell Nystrom felt was typical of storms in general — they never had the courtesy to arrive on a rest day when a man might properly prepare for them. Instead they chose market day, when half of Saltmere was out on the flats trading mutton for flour and the other half was indoors pretending not to watch the sky through their windows.

Wendell was in the kitchen of Warden Ilsette's house, doing what he did best and least glamorously: tasting things. Not for poison this time — that duty belonged to the royal table, two hundred miles east and mercifully distant — but for salt. The ward-stones that ringed Saltmere were fed with brine twice a season, and it was Wendell who tested each batch, because a ward fed with bad salt was worse than no ward at all. Bad salt made the stones *hungry*.

"This batch tastes like regret," he announced to the empty kitchen.

"Regret has a flavor?" said Maren from the doorway. She was the warden's niece, seventeen, and possessed of the unshakable belief that Wendell's entire profession was an elaborate excuse to be dramatic about soup.

"Bitter on the back of the tongue, sour at the finish, with a long aftertaste of things you should have said to your father." He held out the spoon. "This salt was cut from the eastern pan too late. It pulled up mud with it."

Maren tasted, made a face worthy of her uncle's ledger books, and spat into the slop pail. "So we throw it out?"

"We throw it out, and we tell Ilsette the eastern pans are done for the season, and she'll sigh the way she sighs, and then she'll write three letters about it." Wendell tipped the crock of tainted salt out the window onto the midden heap, where it would do no more harm than usual. "That's governance. Everything important is just sighing and correspondence."

Outside, the first fat drops of rain hit the window like fingers testing a lock.

It was on his way home through that rain — hood up, collar up, dignity abandoned somewhere around the tannery lane — that Wendell saw the light.

Saltmere had rules about lights. That was the first thing any child learned: if you see a light where no light should be, you go home, you bar the door, and you tell the warden. Lights near the ward-line meant one of three things. Wanderers, which happened; ward-thieves, which also happened; or cracks, which everyone pretended didn't.

The light was near the ward-line. Of course it was. It was a small light, low to the ground, guttering orange against the grey curtain of rain — someone crouched at the threshold stones with a lantern they had no business owning, because lanterns near the line burned wrong, green-edged, hungry.

Wendell stood in the downpour and conducted a brief internal argument between the sensible part of him (go home, bar the door) and the part of him that had spent twenty years putting strange substances in his mouth for a living (well, how bad could it be).

The second part won, as it usually did. This, his mother had often said, was why he'd survived childhood by luck alone.

He approached the light the way he approached everything unfamiliar: slowly, hands visible, mouth already composing apologies. "Hello! Wardens' business! I'm unarmed except for opinions!"

The light flinched. Then it steadied, and he saw what crouched beneath it.

A child. Small — six years old, maybe seven, though it was hard to tell with the soaked bundle of blankets wrapped around him like a chrysalis. Dark hair plastered flat. Skin gone blue-grey with cold. And the eyes — Wendell stopped three paces away because of the eyes. Not their color, which was ordinary brown, but their *focus*. Children looked at your face, or your hands, or the interesting mud. This child looked at the space just over Wendell's left shoulder as though something enormous and patient were standing there.

Which, Wendell noted with professional calm, was exactly where the ward-line ran.

"You're outside the stones," Wendell said gently. "Do you understand me? You can't come past them."

The child turned his head. Looked at the nearest threshold stone — a knee-high pillar of grey rock, streaked white with old brine. Then he looked back at Wendell, and his mouth moved.

No sound came out. But his lips shaped something, deliberate and slow, and Wendell — who had spent his life training his tongue to catch the faintest bitterness in a broth, who could taste hemlock in honey and arsenic in wine — felt every hair on his arms stand up under his wet sleeves.

Because he knew those shapes. Everyone in Saltmere knew those shapes, the way everyone knew a coin with the dead king's face on it: the old tongue, the pre-kingdom script, the letters carved into the inner faces of the ward-stones where no one was permitted to read them. The priests called it the Tongue of Chains. Mothers used it to frighten children: *the sorcerers spoke it before the crowns, and look what speaking bought them.*

The child's lips said, clearly, silently: *open.*

"No," Wendell said, before his brain could file a better response. Then, because manners were drilled into him deeper than fear: "I mean — hello. No. Are you lost? Where is your —"

The child swayed. The lantern tipped. Wendell lunged without thinking, caught the boy around the middle as he folded like wet laundry, and got a lapful of freezing, shaking, silent child for his trouble.

That was when he noticed the boy's hands. Clenched in his blanket, knuckles white — and on both palms, in the same dead script, marks like brands, or scars, or writing written into the skin itself. They weren't bleeding. They were *faintly warm*. Wendell could feel it through his gloves, a heat like a stone kept too near a fire, and the heat pulsed once — twice —

— and every threshold stone from the mill road to the north gate sang.

Not loudly. A hum, a glass-rim whine, felt in the teeth more than heard. Wendell had lived beside the wards all his life and had never once heard them make a sound. Around him, the rain seemed to bend, drops curving away from the child in his arms as though the water itself wanted no part of whatever was sleeping in those small branded palms.

Then it stopped. The rain fell straight again. The boy shivered violently and buried his face against Wendell's chest, and became, abruptly, entirely an ordinary half-drowned child.

Wendell stood at the edge of the only home he'd ever known, holding a mystery, and thought: *Ilsette is going to write so many letters.*

* * *

Getting the boy past the ward-line was, technically, illegal.

Wendell knew this the way he knew most laws: vaguely, and with a strong personal sense that they didn't apply to situations involving hypothermia. The rule was absolute — nothing crossed the threshold stones that hadn't been born inside them. Saltmere's whole survival hung on that rule the way a lamp hangs on its chain, and every soul in the valley could recite it.

But the rule assumed the thing trying to cross was trying to cross. This child wasn't pushing at the boundary. If anything, the boundary seemed to have stepped aside for him. When Wendell carried him between the two gate-stones, the hum rose again — a single low note, almost welcoming, almost *relieved* — and died away, and the boy passed through dry-shod while Wendell got soaked to the skin, which felt like a pointed editorial comment from the universe.

The walk to the warden's house took eleven minutes normally. It took forty with a boy in his arms who weighed approximately nothing and gripped approximately everything. Twice the child went rigid and stared off into the rain at nothing, and both times the lanterns along the lane guttered in perfect unison, like a congregation bowing its heads. Both times, Wendell talked to him — nonsense, mostly, descriptions of the houses they passed, warnings about which dogs bit — and both times the boy settled, as though the sound of a voice were rope thrown to a drowning man.

At Ilsette's door, Wendell kicked instead of knocking, because his hands were full of child and emergency.

Ilsette opened the door wearing her nightcap and the expression of a woman who had been expecting trouble since roughly 4 o'clock. She was sixty, built like a well-used fence post, and had been warden of Saltmere for thirty years, which meant she had seen floods, feuds, one memorable cattle uprising, and precisely zero events that had surprised her. She looked at Wendell. She looked at the boy. Her eyebrows climbed a quarter inch, which from Ilsette was the equivalent of another woman screaming.

"Well," she said. "You've found something."

"He found us," Wendell said. "More or less. He was at the line. Ilsette, he — the stones sang."

Something changed in her face then. Not surprise. Recognition, old and unwelcome, like finding a debt notice behind a picture frame. She stepped back and held the door wide.

"In. Fire's low but it'll do. Maren! Hot water and the good blankets, and don't ask questions until I've asked mine."

Maren appeared already halfway down the stairs with a blanket, having apparently developed her aunt's talent for knowing when things had gone wrong. She took one look at the boy and went pale. "Is he — where did he come from? Nobody comes from outside."

"Nobody does," Ilsette agreed. She crouched by the fire and built it up with brisk violence, sparks flying. "Set him down here, Wendell. Hands where I can see them."

"It's his hands," Wendell said quietly. "There's writing on them."

Ilsette went still, poker in midair. For a moment the fire was the loudest thing in the room. Then she set the poker down with exaggerated care, the way a woman sets down a thing when throwing it would be satisfying but expensive.

"Show me."

They peeled back the boy's fists together. In the firelight, the script on his palms glowed faintly, letters fine as stitching, and Wendell watched Ilsette's face do something he had never seen it do in all his years: lose an argument with itself. She looked old, suddenly. Older than the house. Older, somehow, than the stones.

"That's not possible," she said, in the tone of a woman reading a letter from a man she had personally buried.

"Aunt Ilsette?" Maren hovered with the kettle. "What is it? What does it say?"

"It doesn't *say*, girl. Writing says. That—" Ilsette pressed her lips together. "That is a ledger entry. That is the warden's mark, the kind that goes on the inside of the stones, and it hasn't been written by living hands since before my grandmother's grandmother. It means *account open*. It means the valley owes a debt, and the debt has just walked up to our door and knocked."

The boy, wrapped now in two blankets and propped against Wendell's side like a small hot coal, lifted his head. He looked at Ilsette with those unsettling, over-the-shoulder eyes, and then — deliberately, gravely — he turned his palms upward and showed her. An offering. Or a bill presented for payment.

And then his mouth moved again, and this time, faintly, a sound came with it — a whisper worn thin as old cloth, but a sound:

*"Nystrom,"* the boy breathed.

Every head turned toward Wendell.

"I haven't done anything," Wendell said immediately, with the reflexes of a man who had spent his career being blamed for poisonings.

"You're the only Nystrom in the valley," Maren pointed out.

"I'm aware of my surname, thank you, Maren."

But the boy was looking at him — really looking, for the first time, past his shoulder, past whatever patient enormous thing stood behind him — and his cracked lips curved into something that was unmistakably, impossibly, a smile. Relief, plain and naked, the smile of someone who has walked a very long way in the rain toward one particular door.

Ilsette sank into her chair. She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger, sighed the sigh Wendell had predicted earlier — the deep one, the one that preceded three letters — and then some deeper sigh beneath it, geological, ancestral.

"Wendell," she said. "Your family didn't always keep bees and taste soup. You know that much, don't you?"

"I know my grandfather claimed we were 'custodial,' which I took to mean tidy."

"Custodial." She laughed, short and bitter. "Boy, the Nystroms held keys before there were locks. And I would wager my warden's seal that this child did not wash up at our line by accident. He came *to you*. Which means whatever woke tonight knows your name, your blood, and probably your breakfast habits." She leaned forward, firelight carving deep lines in her face. "So before dawn, before anyone else sees those hands — you're going to sit down with this boy, and you, Wendell Nystrom, are going to find out exactly what it is your family has been keeping."

Outside, the rain thickened. And far off along the ward-line, faint as a heartbeat, the stones began — very softly — to sing again.
