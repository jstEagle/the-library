## 4. Words Older than Crowns

The boy slept in Wendell's bed, and Wendell sat in his one chair and did not sleep at all.

It was a narrow bed, built for a narrow man, and the child fit in it as if it had been made for him—small feet drawn up under the blanket, hands curled near his chin like a pair of sleeping birds. He had not let go of the bread until he'd actually fallen asleep, at which point Wendell had pried it gently from his fingers and set it on the sill for morning. The boy had eaten three more pieces since then, slowly, watching Wendell the whole time with those gray eyes that seemed to take notes.

Wendell took notes too. He was good at taking notes; a taster's life depended on observation. *Pale but not starved. No lice, no sores. Boots worn through at the heel but well-made once. Speaks a dead tongue and eats like a prince afraid the kitchen will close.* None of it added up to anything except trouble, and trouble of a particular flavor: the kind that came with riders behind it.

He'd hidden the boy's wet clothes by the stove and given him a spare shirt that hung off him like a sail. He'd put out the lamp twice and lit it again both times, because every time the room went dark, the boy made a small sound—not a word, not quite a whimper—that Wendell found he could not bear.

"You're safe," Wendell told him now, in Saltmere common, knowing it wouldn't land. "Whatever you are, whoever sent you, you're safe tonight."

The boy opened one eye, considered him, and closed it again. It was the most skeptical thing Wendell had seen all day, and he'd spent the afternoon being glared at by Brannic Hodge's goose.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of rain that wouldn't commit. Wendell rose before dawn out of twenty years' habit and made porridge, because porridge was what he knew how to make when his mind was elsewhere. He tested it first, naturally—the habit ran deeper than thought. One spoonful, held on the tongue, counted against the roof of the mouth. Bitter? No. Metallic? No. Nothing but oats, salt butter, and a drop of honey he allowed himself on principle.

The boy ate two bowls and then, without warning, pointed at the pot and said something long and liquid and utterly foreign. The words had a shape Wendell almost recognized, the way you almost recognize a face across a market crowd—a cousin of words, a grandparent of them. Old Salterian had a few bones of it left: place names mostly, and the blessing the millers still muttered over the sluice gates every spring, meaning lost but rhythm preserved.

"Again," Wendell said. "Slower."

The boy frowned, then repeated it, syllable by syllable, tapping the pot each time he said the word for it—or what Wendell guessed was the word for it—and by the fifth repetition Wendell understood that the child was teaching him. Not begging. Not pleading. Teaching. There was a patience in it, a schoolmaster's patience, wildly at odds with the small face delivering it, and Wendell laughed before he could stop himself.

"All right, all right." He tapped the pot. "*Porridge.*"

The boy listened, then produced something that was either "porridge" or an elaborate insult involving porridge. They went back and forth until the window showed full daylight and the pot was empty, and somewhere in the middle of it Wendell realized two things at once.

First: the boy was clever, quick, and hungry for names the way other children were hungry for bread.

Second: Wendell could understand perhaps one word in ten, and that tenth word kept being *salt*.

Every language borrows from its neighbors, and dead tongues leave their fingerprints on living ones. But this wasn't borrowing. This was bedrock. The boy's speech wasn't related to Old Salterian—it *was* Old Salterian, or its mother, or its mother's mother, the tongue that had been old when Saltmere's founders carved the first ward-stones, the tongue the valley had been warded *against*. Wendell had grown up on the stories the way everyone in Saltmere grew up on them: the sorcerer-kings, the war that ended in ash, the great working that sealed something in the mountains and salted the valley floor so nothing planted in memory could grow there again. Every child learned the blessing-rhyme. Every adult pretended not to believe it.

And here was a mute-voiced, bright-eyed scrap of a boy speaking the war itself over breakfast.

"You," Wendell said quietly, "are going to be the death of me. Or possibly the life of me. I haven't decided."

The boy pointed at him, then at himself, then raised his eyebrows: *your name, my name, go.*

"Wendell," Wendell said, tapping his own chest. "*Wen-dell.*"

The boy tapped his own chest and said a word like water going over stones. Wendell tried it three times and got it wrong three times, and on the third failure the boy laughed—a real laugh, sudden and startling, the first sound of joy Wendell had heard from him—and corrected him with the air of a patient tutor dealing with a hopeless student.

"Ilsette is going to love this," Wendell muttered.

* * *

Ilsette Marrow taught the village letters, numbers, and enough history to keep the crown's census-takers satisfied, which meant she taught exactly none of the true history, and she did it in a drafty room behind the meeting hall that smelled of chalk and dried yarrow. She was sixty, sharp as a paring knife, and had buried a husband, two children, and any number of illusions, and it had only sharpened her.

She looked up when Wendell ducked through the door with the boy half-hidden behind his coat, and her eyebrows performed a brief, eloquent conversation with each other.

"Before you say anything," Wendell began.

"I wasn't going to say anything. I was going to enjoy the silence while it lasted." She set down her chalk. "Wendell Nystrom. You have brought a child into my schoolroom. A child. You, who once described children as 'small drunk people with excellent hearing.'"

"That was a joke."

"It was a Tuesday." She beckoned. "Bring him here where the light's better."

The boy came out from behind Wendell's coat with the caution of someone who had learned that open rooms contained open hands, but Ilsette crouched to his level and simply waited, hands visible, expression neutral, until something in him decided. Then she held up the slate and wrote on it in careful strokes: *Can you write?*

The boy stared at the marks. His brow furrowed. Slowly he reached out and traced the shape of the last letter with one finger, then shook his head—but it was a specific shake, Wendell thought, a *this is wrong but I know it should mean something* shake.

"He speaks," Wendell said. "That's why I'm here. That's why—" He stopped, aware of the open windows, the neighbor's cat on the sill, the whole listening valley. "May we speak privately?"

Ilsette dismissed her single pupil—a drowsy farm lad named Tam who left with the gratitude of a man reprieved—and dropped the latch herself.

What followed took the better part of two hours, a pot of yarrow tea, and every scrap of Wendell's patience. The boy talked. Wendell scribbled phonetics. Ilsette, who in her girlhood had studied at the archive-house in Corvane before deciding that cities were "a rumor with walls," pulled down three books and cross-referenced until her fingers were gray with ink.

"It's pre-dynastic," she said at last, and her voice had gone quiet in a way Wendell had never heard from her. "Not dialect. Not corruption. The root. The tongue they spoke before the First King unified anything, before writing came south, before—" She stopped herself, the way people in Saltmere always stopped themselves at that particular cliff edge. "Before the war."

"The wards," Wendell said.

"The wards were sung in this language." She looked at the boy, who was watching them both with his chin on his knees, following the tone if not the words. "Or against it. The histories disagree on whether the binding-tongue and the sorcerers' tongue were the same. The founders may have used it to lock the door—or the door may have been locked with its own voice." She rubbed her eyes. "Either way, Wendell, nobody has spoken it aloud in four hundred years. Nobody *could*. It died with the kingdom that fell. And this child carries it in his mouth like it's milk."

The stove ticked. Outside, someone's cart rattled past, ordinary as bread.

"Why mute?" Wendell asked. "You said he was mute when he washed up."

Ilsette shook her head. "I said no such thing. You said it. And I think you were wrong." She crouched again before the boy. "Child. Can you make another sound? Any sound?"

The boy opened his mouth—and Wendell saw it happen, saw the effort gather in his throat like a swallow going down wrong, saw his face tighten and redden and finally close. He shook his head. But his eyes had gone, briefly, somewhere else. Somewhere far away and very loud.

"He can't," Wendell said softly. "Not won't. *Can't.* Something took his voice and left him everything else."

"Something," Ilsette repeated. "Or someone." She sat back on her heels and looked at Wendell with the flat, level gaze she usually reserved for boys caught copying sums. "You know what this means. You've known since you fished him out. The wards are failing, and this boy is either the proof or the reason."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Then hear an old woman's arithmetic. If the crown learns a child speaking the old tongue washed up in Saltmere, they will send inquisitors. Not soldiers—inquisitors, with their white hoods and their polite questions and their instruments. They will take him to Corvane, and whatever they do to him there will be done slowly, and it will be done in the name of the king, and no one in this valley will be able to stop it. And if they don't come—if it's not the crown that finds him first—then it will be the others. The quiet ones. The ones who've been sniffing along the ward-lines for decades hoping the door would crack." She jabbed a finger at him. "So which is it, Wendell? Who do we fear more this season?"

Wendell looked at the boy. The boy looked back, and then did something that undid Wendell entirely: he picked up the slate, and with agonizing care, copying letter-shapes he clearly didn't understand but had watched Ilsette make, he drew three wobbly symbols and pushed the slate toward Wendell.

They weren't letters. They were older than letters—loops and a bar and a stroke, arranged with a confidence that had no business living in a six-year-old's wrist. Ilsette made a sound like she'd been struck.

"That's a ward-sign," she whispered. "That's the founder's mark. From the standing stone. He's never seen it. He *can't* have seen it."

But even as she said it, the boy shook his head and tapped the drawing, then touched his own throat, then pointed east—toward the mountains, toward the sealed slopes, toward the thing the whole valley existed to forget.

And Wendell, who had spent his whole life learning to taste what wasn't there yet—danger in a broth, hemlock in honeyed wine, ruin in a lord's smile—felt the taste of the future arrive on his tongue, bitter and unmistakable.

Because the boy hadn't drawn the ward-sign to show them what it was.

He'd drawn it to tell them what was coming for it. And beneath the loops and the bar, in the dead tongue, in a voice he couldn't use but a hand that refused to be silent, he had written—small, urgent, scratched twice over—

*They are already awake.*

* * *

They were still staring at the slate when the knock came.

Three raps. Even, unhurried, official. Wendell's body knew that knock the way it knew poison—before thought, in the marrow. In Saltmere, friends knocked twice and family just walked in. Three knocks meant authority, and authority in Saltmere wore one of two faces.

Through the window he saw the horse first: a tall gray with a crown-brand on its flank, lathered despite the cool morning. Then the rider's boots, dismounting. Then the rider himself, turning toward the schoolhouse door, and Wendell felt the floor tilt under him.

The man wore traveling leathers and a surveyor's badge, and his smile was warm as fresh bread, and none of it mattered, because Wendell recognized the way he moved. He'd served enough years at court to know a Warden's Ledger man when he saw one—the crown's quiet counters, the ones who tallied wards and tolls and tongues, who arrived in valleys asking gentle questions about census and grain and never about the thing they'd actually come for.

"Wendell," Ilsette breathed. "The slate."

He swept it behind his back. Too late, too obvious—he knew it even as he did it—and the boy, bless him, slid off the bench and folded himself into the shadow behind the flour barrels with the ease of long practice, as though hiding from crown men was a skill he'd been born carrying.

The knock came again. Polite. Patient.

Wendell crossed the room, unlatched the door, and put on the best empty smile of his career—the one that had gotten him through nineteen years of tasting for paranoid nobles, the one that said *nothing here but porridge and honest labor*.

The man on the step was younger than his voice, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of a ledger line. He bowed, precisely the correct depth, and said:

"Good morning. Master Nystrom, isn't it? The royal food-taster, retired to his home valley. What a coincidence." His smile deepened by exactly one degree. "I'm here on behalf of the Warden's Ledger, doing a routine count of valley residents. Routine." He produced a scroll, and his gaze drifted, light as a fly, past Wendell's shoulder into the room. "Though I confess my superiors did mention... unusual weather on the eastern ridge lately. Strange lights. And rumors of a flood-born stranger."

Behind Wendell, in the dark beside the flour barrels, a small hand found the hem of his coat and held on.

"We keep no strangers here," Wendell said pleasantly. "Only neighbors. Will you come in for tea?"

The ledger-man's eyes came back to his, and for one long moment neither of them smiled at all.

"Oh," the man said, "I'd love to."
