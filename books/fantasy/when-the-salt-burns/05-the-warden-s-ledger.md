## 5. The Warden's Ledger

The archive beneath the Saltmere meeting hall smelled, in Wendell's professional opinion, like old cabbage and older secrets.

He had been inside exactly twice in his life—once when he was seven and had dared Bram Fenwick to steal a pen nib from the warden's desk, and once now, carrying a mute child by the light of a lantern that sputtered more than it shone. The second visit was going considerably worse than the first.

"Touch nothing," Maren had said, which was rich, coming from her, since she'd spent the last ten minutes touching everything: shelves, ledgers, a jar of something that might have been pickled eel or might have been a warning. She moved through the dark like a woman revisiting a house she'd been thrown out of.

Wendell set the boy down on a stool near the lantern. The child sat the way children sit when they have decided sitting is a temporary condition—a coiled stillness, eyes tracking every shadow. He hadn't spoken since the creek. He hadn't needed to. The word he'd said—the one that had made Wendell's teeth ache and the salt pans hum like struck glass—was still hanging in the air between them, or so it felt.

"You knew about this place," Wendell said quietly. "The archive. The ledger."

"I knew about a ledger." Maren pulled a cloth off a lectern with the flourish of someone unveiling a monument to herself. "I did not know it would be quite this dusty. Sit down, Wendell. You look like a man who has recently discovered the world is larger than his kitchen."

"My kitchen has never tried to kill me."

"Give it time. You poison yourself for a living."

"That *is* my kitchen trying to kill me. I just argue back."

The boy made a sound. It took Wendell a moment to recognize it as a laugh—a small, startled bark, quickly smothered, as if laughter were another language he'd been told to forget. But it was there. Wendell found himself absurdly pleased, the way he'd been pleased the first time a bitter tonic stopped tasting of anything at all.

Maren opened the ledger.

It was enormous—bound in hide gone stiff and gray, its corners capped in tarnished silver worked into shapes Wendell didn't want to look at too long. Spirals. Coiling lines. Something that might have been a serpent swallowing its own beginning. The first page was written in a hand so precise it looked printed:

*Let it be known what was buried here, and why, and at what cost, that no warden after us may plead ignorance.*

Below that, names. Columns and columns of names, each with a date and a single word beside it. Wendell leaned closer.

"They all say the same thing," he murmured. "*Served.* Every one of them. Three hundred years of wardens, and the only epitaph anyone gets is 'served.'"

"It's an honest word," Maren said. "Shorter than most lives deserve."

The boy slid off his stool. Wendell tensed—ready to catch him, ready to grab his wrist if he reached for the pages—but the child only stood before the lectern, very straight, and looked at the ledger the way a man looks at his own name carved somewhere unexpected.

Then he reached out and touched the silver corner, gently, two fingers, and the lantern flame stood up straight as a soldier.

"Maren," Wendell said.

"I see it."

"Do you see it?"

"I am choosing," Maren said, in the voice of a woman who had seen considerably worse than a well-behaved candle, "to see it calmly."

* * *

They read for hours.

That was the thing about Saltmere nobody told you until you needed telling: the valley kept accounts. Not of grain or sheep or births, though those were recorded too, in the ordinary books upstairs. Down here, in the cold and the cabbage-smell, the ledger kept accounts of the *other* thing—the reason the valley existed at all, the reason the salt flats shimmered wrong at dusk, the reason every child learned the boundary stones' names before they learned their prayers.

Wendell read aloud, because Maren's eyes were failing in the bad light and because, he suspected, she liked hearing someone else's voice carry the weight for a while. He read in his kitchen voice, low and even, the one he used when describing a dish to a nervous steward. Poisoners learn early that tone matters more than content. You can say *this soup may be compromised* in a way that starts a riot or a yawn.

"*Year of the Founding,*" he read. "*The war is ended, which is to say it is hidden. The sorcerer-kings are not dead, whatever the ballads claim. Dead men do not require eleven wards to stay quiet. We have bound them beneath the valley and set the salt to watch, and we have agreed, all of us who remain, that our children will be taught nothing, so that nothing can be pried out of them. Ignorance is the deepest wall we have. Let it hold.*"

The boy had gone very still at *sorcerer-kings*. Not frightened, Wendell noted. Attentive. Like a hound hearing its own name in a stranger's mouth.

"*Year Forty-One. The eastern stone sings in its sleep. We fed it a season's harvest of salt and it quieted.*"

"*Year One Hundred and Twelve. A girl child born in the lower village speaks words she was never taught. The council debated three nights. Her family was given land in the hill country and a story to tell. The girl herself—*" Wendell paused. "*—the girl herself was drowned in the cistern, gently, with wine, by her own mother, who asked this office to record that she did not hesitate, and to record also that she lies.*"

Nobody said anything for a while after that.

Maren broke the silence first, because Maren always broke silences first; it was one of the many ways she'd failed as a warden and succeeded as a person. "Well," she said. "Your ancestors were thorough, I'll grant them."

"They murdered a little girl."

"They murdered a little girl *three hundred years ago*, and they were wrong to do it, and they knew they were wrong, and they wrote it down anyway, because they thought someone ought to know. That's not nothing, Wendell. Shame on paper is still shame. It means they wanted to be judged."

"Comforting. I'll carve it on the cistern."

The boy tugged Wendell's sleeve. When he had his attention, the child pointed at the ledger, then at himself, then made a shape with his hand—flat, level, moving forward slowly across invisible pages.

"He's saying he's in there," Maren said softly. "Or ones like him. Look."

She turned ahead through the years, faster now, past entries that thinned from paragraphs to lines to a terse annual formula—*wards stable, nothing spoken, nothing heard*—until, near the end, the handwriting changed. Newer ink. A different, cramping script that Wendell recognized with a small cold shock.

"This is your father's hand," he said.

"Yes," said Maren. "Read it."

"*Year Two Hundred Ninety-Eight. The wards hold, but thinly. The salt burns shorter each winter. I believe the binding was designed to fail—not broken, not damaged: designed. A lock with a slow leak, so that whoever built it could return to find the door already open. We were not wardens. We were caretakers of someone else's hourglass. I have written to the capital requesting guidance. I request it again. I request it annually now, and receive annually a receipt.*"

"And below that?" Wendell asked, though he could see.

"*Year Three Hundred One. My daughter has read this ledger against my instruction, which is fitting, since I read it against hers. If you are reading this, Ilsette, I am sorry, and you were right, and I hope wherever you are, you are insufferable about it.*"

Maren closed the ledger with unnecessary care, the way one closes a door on a sleeping person.

"He knew," she said. "About the design. About the failure. He knew, and he wrote letters to men in tall hats for six years, and when he died they sent a wreath and a form." She smiled, and it was one of her real smiles, the crooked dangerous kind. "So I became insufferable about it. You're welcome."

* * *

Wendell took the boy home through the wet dark, one hand hooked in the child's collar not to drag him but because it was easier than holding hands and being refused. The boy allowed it. He had begun allowing things from Wendell specifically—a development that delighted and terrified him in equal measure. Children, like poisons, grew tolerant of small doses.

At his cottage door, the boy stopped and pointed up.

The ridge line against the stars. Wendell looked, expecting wind, expecting the familiar jagged teeth of Greyfell Crags.

Instead he saw lights. Three of them, maybe four, spaced along the ridge path where no honest traveler walked at night—moving in the slow, deliberate way of people checking their footing, or checking the ground for tracks.

Inquisitors rode with lanterns. Everyone knew that. It was in all the stories: the crown's men carried light into dark places so that everyone could see them coming and understand that hiding was pointless.

But these lights were shuttered. Nearly dark. Only a thin blade of glow each, hooded and careful, the kind of light you used when you wanted to see without being seen.

And that, Wendell thought, taking the boy firmly by the collar again and drawing him inside, was worse.

He barred the door, banked the fire, put the boy in his bed and himself in the chair by the hearth with a kitchen knife and a lifetime of practice at staying awake through worse.

Somewhere on the ridge, the little hooded lights kept walking down.
