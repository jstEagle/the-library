## 6. Riders on the Ridge

Wendell Nystrom had eaten things that would have killed a mule twice over, and he had done it all with a pleasant expression and clean boots. He liked to think that nothing in Saltmere could surprise him anymore.

The knock at his door before dawn proved otherwise.

"Wendell." Maren's voice, low and urgent through the wood. "Wake up. There's smoke on the ridge."

He was out of bed with his boots half-laced before his mind caught up with his feet. Smoke on the ridge meant travelers, and travelers meant the pass was open, and the pass being open in the third week of Thaw-Month — weeks too early — meant something had changed in the mountains.

Or someone had come through it very quickly.

He found Maren standing in her doorway across the lane, wrapped in her husband's old coat against the cold, her gray hair unbound. She was seventy-one years old and had buried two husbands and one king's tax collector, and she did not frighten easily.

"How many?" he asked.

"Hard to say. Lantern-light, moving single file along the crest. Six, maybe seven." She paused. "They're not coming down yet. They're watching."

"Watching what?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment, and then past him, to where a small shape stood in Wendell's doorway — barefoot, silent, clutching the ragged blanket Wendell had given him three nights ago. The boy had not spoken a word since the river gave him up. But his eyes were fixed on the ridge as though he could see through the dark itself.

"He hasn't slept," Maren said quietly. "Has he?"

"No."

"And you have?"

"Some."

"Liar." She said it without heat, the way she said most true things. "Get the kettle on. If riders are coming down into my village, they'll do it after breakfast like civilized people, or I'll know the reason why."

* * *

By full light, the whole of Saltmere had found an excuse to be outdoors.

Old Tammswey swept a stretch of lane that had no need of sweeping. The Cooper boys climbed the roof of their father's shop under the pretense of fixing a shingle that had been loose since the reign of the last king's grandfather. And at the well, a dozen villagers drew water they did not need, all of them angling their buckets toward the ridge.

There were seven figures up there now, clearly visible in the morning sun. They wore no livery that anyone could make out at this distance, but their horses were wrong for travelers — too matched, too fine, standing in that disciplined line that horses only learn in a king's stable.

"Inquisitors," said Old Tammswey, with the satisfaction of a man who has finally been proven right about something after forty years of being wrong about nearly everything else. "I said so at midwinter. Marked wagons on the high road, I said. Nobody listens to me."

"We listen," said the cooper's wife wearily. "That's rather the problem."

Wendell stood apart from them, the boy's hand in his own. The child had not taken his eyes off the ridge. His grip was tight enough to hurt, and Wendell did not pull away.

"Do you know them?" Wendell asked softly, for perhaps the tenth time.

The boy shook his head. Then, after a moment, he lifted his other hand and pointed — not at the riders, but beyond them, eastward, toward the far end of the valley where the salt flats began their long white burn toward the mountains.

"The flats," Wendell said. "You want to go to the flats?"

Another shake of the head. The boy pressed his palm flat against his own chest, then pointed again, harder this time, his finger stabbing the air as if the direction itself were a word he could not speak.

"You're *from* there?"

A pause. Then the boy made a sound Wendell had never heard him make — a short, frustrated exhale through the nose, almost a laugh, the sound of someone trying to explain water to a fish. He tugged Wendell's hand and pointed once more, and this time Wendell understood.

Not *from*. Not *to*. Something was *there*. Something the riders wanted, or something the riders feared, and the boy knew which.

"Well," Wendell said, mostly to himself. "That's inconvenient."

* * *

The riders came down at midday, as Maren had predicted, because even inquisitors, apparently, respected a good breakfast hour.

There were six of them, not seven — the seventh figure on the ridge, Wendell realized with a cold settling in his stomach, had been a lantern left burning on a stone. A signal. Someone was still up there, watching, and whoever it was had let the six ride down alone.

Their leader reined up at the edge of the square and dismounted with the careful precision of a man whose body was worth more than the horse beneath him. He was younger than Wendell expected — thirty at most — with a long, thoughtful face and eyes the pale gray of winter sky. He wore no black robes, no insignia. Just good wool traveling clothes and a sword worn like an afterthought.

But Wendell had spent fifteen years reading faces across banquet tables, learning which smile hid which poison, and he read this one easily. This man was courteous the way deep water is calm.

"Good day," the man said, to the square in general. "I'm looking for the warden of this valley. Or whoever keeps the ledger."

Every eye in Saltmere turned to Wendell, which was unfair, because the warden was Ilsette Vane, and Ilsette had gone up to the ward-stones at dawn and not come back, and Wendell had been trying very hard not to think about that.

"I keep the household accounts," Wendell said pleasantly. "If you've come about the tax assessment, I'm afraid you've missed the collector by two years, and honestly, the less said about that visit the better."

Someone snorted. The gray-eyed man smiled without warmth.

"Not taxes. Records. My name is Corvane. I serve the Crown's Office of Inquiry." He produced a folded paper, sealed with the double-crown wax that Wendell had seen perhaps four times in his life, each time on documents that preceded bad news. "We're searching for a missing child. Fled the royal nursery at Corvane's Crossing — forgive me, my own name is a coincidence of geography — some months ago. Last traced toward the northern valleys."

The square went very quiet. Wendell felt the boy's hand tighten in his.

"A missing child," Wendell repeated. "How old?"

"Six, perhaps seven. Dark-haired. He doesn't speak — or won't." Corvane's gaze moved across the gathered villagers, unhurried, cataloguing. "We understand such a child may have passed this way. A traveler's report. Fishermen on the Vane water."

"Fisherfolk," said Old Tammswey loudly, "will report anything for a hot meal."

"They will indeed," Corvane agreed mildly. "Which is why we verify everything personally." His eyes came back to Wendell and settled there, and Wendell understood, with the particular clarity of a man who has tasted enough poisons to recognize the flavor of attention, that he had just become the most interesting thing in the valley. "And you are?"

"Wendell Nystrom. Taster. By appointment to the crown, actually, so we're practically colleagues."

"Taster." Corvane turned the word over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "In a farming valley."

"We grow excellent parsnips. Some of them try to kill you."

The corner of Corvane's mouth twitched — genuine, Wendell thought, or very nearly. "Then you'll understand our work. We also test what people put in their mouths. We simply call it questioning."

Behind Wendell, so quietly that only he could have heard it, the boy made a sound. Not the mute frustration Wendell had learned to expect. Something smaller. A breath shaped around a syllable that never quite arrived, like a word drowning.

Corvane heard nothing. But the horses shifted, all six of them at once, stamping and tossing their heads as though a hornet had gone through the square, and the gray-eyed man's smile faded into stillness.

"Your animals seem nervous," Wendell offered.

"They do." Corvane looked at the horses. Looked back at the crowd. Somewhere in that long face, behind the courtesy, a door closed. "We'll want lodging for the night. And I'd take it kindly if your warden would present herself. The Office does prefer to deal with the responsible authority."

"She's inspecting the boundary stones," Maren said, stepping forward with the immovable calm of a woman who had raised four sons and outlived three bailiffs. "Boundary stones being our chief industry here, besides parsnips. She may be some hours. Days, if the frost-heave was bad."

"Days," Corvane repeated.

"It's a large boundary," said Maren, daring him to argue.

* * *

They lodged the riders in the tithe barn, because Maren refused on principle to let inquisitors sleep under any roof she owned, and because the tithe barn had the advantage of a floor Wendell had personally inspected for loose boards and listening ears.

It didn't matter. That evening, Wendell sat in his kitchen with the boy, a pot of barley stew between them, and listened to the boy breathe, and tried not to count how many ways the next two days could go wrong.

Ilsette hadn't returned. That was the largest of the wrongs. Ilsette Vane, warden of Saltmere, keeper of the ledger, keeper of every secret the valley had ever bothered to keep, had walked out to the ward-stones before dawn and left Wendell a note that said only: *If riders come, tell them nothing. Feed them. Be boring.*

Be boring. As if that weren't his life's profession.

The boy ate slowly, the way he always did, testing each spoonful — a habit that had startled Wendell the first time he noticed it, because it was *his* habit, learned in banquet halls and poison gardens, and no six-year-old should eat like a man who expects the world to kill him.

"You know they're looking for you," Wendell said. It wasn't really a question anymore.

The boy looked up. His dark eyes held that terrible steadiness again, the one that made Wendell feel like the child in the room.

"If I give you up," Wendell went on, and the words came out rougher than he intended, "they stop asking questions. They leave. Everyone here sleeps easy. That's the arithmetic. Simple sums." He stirred the stew he wasn't eating. "I'm very good at simple sums. It's the complicated ones that ruin a man."

The boy reached across the table and put his small hand flat on Wendell's wrist — the same gesture, Wendell realized, as on the ridge that morning. Pointing. Saying something.

"What?" Wendell asked. "What is it? East again? The flats?"

The boy shook his head slowly. He tapped Wendell's wrist once, twice, and then drew his finger up along his own forearm, tracing a line from wrist to elbow, and his face did something complicated — fear and apology tangled together — and Wendell felt the skin under the boy's fingertip go strangely warm.

Warm. Where the boy touched. Warm like a fever, like a coal wrapped in cloth, like—

Like the first time Wendell had ever tasted something truly deadly, and felt it bloom.

"No," Wendell said, and his voice came out flat and certain, a taster's verdict. "Whatever you think you're doing — whatever you think you can put into me — no."

The boy withdrew his hand. And then he did the thing he had never done in all the nights since the river.

He opened his mouth, and spoke.

One word. Barely voiced, cracked from disuse, older than any language Wendell knew — and yet Wendell understood it perfectly, the way he understood bitterness on his tongue, instantly and below thought:

*"Soon."*

Outside, on the dark ridge above the valley, the seventh lantern flared once — bright white, the color of ward-fire, the color of the stones themselves — and went out.

And down in the tithe barn, Wendell heard the inquisitors' horses begin to scream.
