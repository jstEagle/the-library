## 7. What the Boy Remembers

Wendell had tasted a great many terrible things in his life, and he liked to think he'd developed a professional appreciation for them. There was the artistry of a well-made poison, the craftsmanship of a bad one, and then there was Ilsette's barley porridge, which occupied its own category entirely: not poison, merely punishment.

"It's good for you," she said, setting the bowl down with the particular firmness of a woman who has decided that nutrition is not a democracy.

"Everything is good for me," Wendell said. "That's rather my whole profession."

"Then eat it twice as fast."

The boy — they had taken to calling him Ash, because he'd arrived gray with river-silt and smelling faintly of smoke, and because naming him anything grander felt like tempting fate — sat cross-legged on the floor by the hearth, watching them argue about breakfast with an expression of profound scholarly interest. He was perhaps eight years old, small for it, with dark hair that refused to lie flat and eyes that were, Wendell had finally admitted to himself on the third sleepless night, the exact color of old bronze. The color of coins buried so long the earth had begun to claim them back.

He still hadn't spoken. But he had begun, in the last two days, to *write*.

* * *

It had started with salt.

Wendell had been grinding it coarse at the table — Saltmere salt, from the flats below the village, white and clean and utterly without memory, or so everyone had believed for three hundred years — when Ash reached out and stopped his hand. Not grabbed. Stopped. His fingers closed around Wendell's wrist with a grip far stronger than his size suggested, and he stared at the little pile of crystals as though they had personally insulted his ancestors.

Then he took a pinch of it, spilled it onto the tabletop, and began dragging one finger through it, tracing shapes with the intense concentration of a child doing sums.

Lines first. A triangle, but wrong — the angles bent inward, the way geometry bends in dreams. Then a circle inside it, then marks around the rim of the circle that weren't letters in any alphabet Wendell knew, though something in his hindbrain stirred uneasily at them, the way a taster's tongue stirs at a flavor he can't name but knows is *wrong*.

"That's not writing," Wendell said. "That's ward-script." And then he stopped, because he had no idea how he knew that word, except that every child in Saltmere grew up hearing half-stories about the ward-stones on the ridge, and somewhere in those half-stories someone had once said *the old script*, and it had lodged in him like a fishbone.

Ash looked up at him. Tapped the salt-circle. Looked at Wendell again, expectant, the way a scholar looks at a colleague who is being disappointingly slow.

"I don't know what it means," Wendell said honestly.

Ash made a sound — the first sound Wendell had heard him make, a short exhalation through the nose that was unmistakably, universally, *children everywhere* — the sound meaning *obviously*. Then he pointed out the window, toward the ridge, toward the standing stones half-swallowed by gorse, and pointed back at the salt, and pressed his palm flat over his own chest.

"You remember it," Wendell said slowly. "The stones. You remember them being built."

Ash nodded once, gravely, and then ruined the gravity by stealing a heel of bread off the table and retreating behind Ilsette's chair with it, which was where he did all his best thinking.

* * *

By the third day, the kitchen table looked like the aftermath of a very small, very determined invasion.

Salt, of course. Also flour, when the salt ran low. Ashes from the grate, which Ilsette objected to on grounds of tidiness and lost on grounds of fascination. Ash — the boy — worked hunched over the wood like a scribe with a deadline, drawing diagrams Wendell couldn't read, maps Wendell could, and one long, looping sequence that appeared again and again, always drawn first, always largest, always finished with a tap of the finger that said *this is important, pay attention*.

"It's a name," Ilsette said.

They were alone for the moment; Ash was asleep, face-down on the bench, one hand still curled around a pinch of flour like a miser with coin. Outside, the afternoon light lay thick and golden over the valley, and everything was so ordinary that Wendell kept having to remind himself that anything extraordinary was happening at all.

"What makes you say that?"

"The way he does it." Ilsette wiped down the counter even though it was already clean, which was what she did instead of wringing her hands. "My mother used to talk about my grandmother the same way. Over and over, like a prayer. You don't repeat a thing that much unless it's a name."

"A name of what?"

Ilsette didn't answer, which was answer enough. Everyone in Saltmere knew the shape of the story, if not the details: the valley had been warded before there were kings, warded against something that had needed imprisoning, and the wards had been paid for in ways nobody discussed above a whisper. The founders' names had been struck from every record deliberately. You couldn't curse a name that no one spoke.

Except, apparently, this child could.

"He remembers too much," Wendell said quietly. "Ilsette, I've been thinking about it. He can't be older than eight. Even if someone taught him — who would know? Who's left to teach?"

"Maybe the same someone who sent a mute child floating down a river into the most heavily warded valley in the kingdom," Ilsette said. "Maybe somebody wanted him found here. Or maybe—" She stopped wiping the counter. "Or maybe nobody sent him anywhere, and he came because the wards are thin enough now to let him through, and that's worse."

It was worse. Wendell agreed privately. The wards were meant to keep things *out*, mostly, but a cage that weakens lets things out too, and the boy remembered the thing in the cage the way a man remembers a fire he was burned in.

* * *

The breakthrough, such as it was, came via soup.

Wendell was testing a delivery from the market cart — routine, nothing royal in it, just habit — and had a spoonful halfway to his mouth when Ash slapped it away. The spoon clattered into the bowl. Broth went everywhere. Wendell sat there blinking while the boy seized his sleeve, dragged him to the salt-covered corner of the table, and wrote, fast and furious, a single symbol Wendell had seen a dozen times in the last three days: the bent triangle, the circle within.

Then he pointed at the soup.

"No," Wendell said. "No, this is fine, I've tasted worse than this a hundred times—"

Ash stamped his foot. Then, with visible effort, with the frustration of a mind stuffed full of things his mouth couldn't carry, he tried something new. He picked up a dried bean from the jar, held it up, dropped it into the empty cup beside him. Pointed at the bean. Pointed at the soup. Drew the triangle-and-circle in spilled broth on the table.

And Wendell, who had spent twenty years learning to read danger in flavors, in the faint bitter ghost under honey, in the almond note that meant cyanide and the garlic sweetness that wasn't garlic — Wendell finally understood what the boy was trying to tell him.

"It's in the food," he breathed. "Something's in the food. In the water?" He thought of the stream, snowmelt off the ridge, passing down past the ward-stones before it ever reached a well. "The wards are leaching into the water. That's why it tastes—" He stopped. Because it did taste. It had tasted faintly wrong for weeks, months maybe, a mineral edge he'd filed away as the season, as his own tired tongue, and he had swallowed it every day like everyone else in Saltmere, and his trained body had simply metabolized whatever it was without telling him, the way it metabolized everything—

Ash was nodding, urgent, both hands pressing Wendell's arm.

"You've been drinking your own prison bars," Wendell told the boy, half-laughing, half-horrified. "We all have. Every cup of tea in this valley. No wonder the inquisitors' maps put us here. We're not hidden behind the wards, we're *steeped* in them."

Which raised an interesting question, and Wendell did not like the question, and asked it anyway:

"If the water carries the ward outward — does it also carry the ward *inward*? To the thing underneath?"

Ash went very still.

Then, slowly, he drew the long looping sequence. The name. And beneath it, for the first time, he added something new: a line, and another line crossing it, and a third, until the name was crossed out — no, not crossed out. *Bound.* Bound with lines the way a parcel is bound with cord. And then he drew the lines again, thinner, fainter, and rubbed one away with his thumb, leaving only two.

Two lines left. Out of however many there had once been.

Wendell looked at the smudge where the third line had been, and felt the temperature of the room change, though the fire hadn't moved.

"How many were there originally?" he whispered.

Ash held up his whole hand, then folded down three fingers.

Seven bindings. Two remaining. And somewhere beneath the valley, something patient had spent three hundred years learning to count along with them.

* * *

That evening, Wendell walked up to the ridge alone.

He told himself it was to check the stones, which was even true. But mostly he needed to stand near the thing the boy remembered and see if standing near it changed anything — whether proximity woke something in him the way the salt-diagrams had. Whether some part of the old knowledge was already seeping into him along with the water.

The five visible ward-stones rose out of the gorse like bad teeth. Weathered, lichen-spotted, unremarkable unless you knew to look — and now Wendell knew to look. The bent-triangle script was carved down their flanks, worn nearly smooth, but there. He'd walked past these stones ten thousand times. He'd carved his initials into one at fourteen and received the beating of his life from his father for it, and had never once wondered what else was written there.

He laid his palm against the nearest stone. Cold. Ordinary cold. And yet —

There it was. Under the stone-smell and the lichen, faint as a memory of a taste: that wrongness from the water, concentrated. The stone was *leaking* in the other direction. Feeding outward. Holding something down and bleeding itself dry to do it.

"Two left," Wendell said aloud, to the stone, to the valley, to nobody. "Whoever you are down there, you're patient, and I respect that, but I'm a food-taster, and patience is the one thing I've never had any use for."

The stone, naturally, said nothing. Stones in Saltmere were famously tight-lipped.

But when he turned to go, his boot caught on something half-buried in the turf — a sixth stone, fallen flat, sunk almost entirely into the hillside. And on its exposed face, freshly cut, edges sharp enough to sting a fingertip: the bent triangle. New work. Days old, at most.

Someone had been here recently. Someone who knew the script. Someone who was either repairing the wards—

—or counting them.

Wendell stood in the failing light a long moment, tasting the air out of pure habit, and found in it, very faintly, woodsmoke that wasn't his own, and horse, and under the horse, the particular bitter tang he'd been warned about in his first year of training and had smelled only once since: a compound the inquisitors used to steady their nerves before difficult work.

Riders. On the ridge. Recently.

He walked home faster than dignity allowed.

* * *

Ash was awake when he got back, sitting up by the banked fire, and he took one look at Wendell's face and reached for the flour.

Before he could draw anything, Wendell knelt down to his level. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to try to answer with more than drawings. Can you do that? Try to make a sound. Any sound."

Ash's mouth worked. His jaw trembled with effort, and Wendell watched him fight for it the way a man fights a current — and understood suddenly that this wasn't muteness of the throat. This was muteness of permission. Something in the boy knew what his voice carried, and kept it locked.

What came out was barely a breath, shaped and sibilant, in a language that predated the kingdom, predates the roads, predates the names of the mountains themselves. Two words. Maybe three.

But Wendell had grown up on the half-stories, and one of them had come from Old Marta, who claimed her grandmother's grandmother had been a warden's daughter, and who used to croon a nonsense rhyme that everyone laughed at — and the middle syllables of that rhyme, the ones everyone assumed were babble, matched exactly.

"*Vael issun koreth,*" Wendell repeated, clumsily, and Ash's eyes went wide and wet.

He grabbed Wendell's hands and pressed them against his own temples, hard, as if to say: *yes, yes, it's in here, all of it, take it.*

"I can't take it," Wendell said gently. "Whatever it is. I'm not built for carrying sorcery. I'm built for carrying arsenic."

Ash shook his head fiercely. And then, with the grim practicality of a child who has run out of languages that work, he crawled to the shelf, dragged down the jar of powdered willow-bark Wendell used for fevers, dipped one finger in it, touched the powder to his own tongue — and did not flinch. Did not cough. Looked at Wendell with those bronze eyes and waited.

Wendell's stomach dropped through the floorboards.

Willow-bark in that quantity should have soured the boy's gut for a day at minimum. Ash was watching him with perfect calm, and Wendell recognized the look, because he saw it in his own mirror every morning. It was the look of a body that had learned, somehow, to treat poison as food.

"Oh," Wendell said softly. "Oh, you clever, terrible child. You weren't just given the memories. They hardened you to carry them. Someone made sure you'd survive the trip."

Ash nodded. Then he pointed at Wendell — at Wendell's chest, specifically, where twenty years of graduated doses lived in flesh and bone — and pointed at himself, and then drew, in spilled salt on the floor, the simplest diagram Wendell had ever seen and the most frightening: two figures, and an arrow between them.

The hook of it settled into him at once, patient and cold: the boy had been made a vessel, and Wendell had been made a vessel, and somewhere on the ridge riders with inquisitor-nerves were deciding which of them to collect first.

Outside, far off across the darkening valley, a dog began to bark — and did not stop.
