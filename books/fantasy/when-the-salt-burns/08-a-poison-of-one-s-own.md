## 8. A Poison of One's Own

The morning after the hearth-confession, Wendell woke with the taste of copper in his mouth and a thought so obvious he felt foolish for not having it sooner.

He had spent thirty years teaching his body to be a locked door. Belladonna, arsenic, widow's milk, the gray powders that came north with spice caravans — he had swallowed them all in careful, measured doses until his blood learned to argue back. The royal physicians called it conditioning. His old master, Grett, had called it something less flattering: *teaching your gut to hold a grudge.*

But a door, once proven against intruders, could hold other things.

He sat up in his narrow bed above Maren's kitchen and watched dust drift through the light. Downstairs, the boy was already awake. Wendell could tell by the particular quality of silence a quiet child makes when he is trying very hard to be quiet — a silence with intention behind it.

He dressed, went down, and found the boy sitting at the scrubbed table with one of Maren's honey buns clutched in both hands, watching Wendell with those too-old eyes.

"Good morning," Wendell said. "I've had an idea. It may be a stupid idea. Most of my ideas are, but they're usually survivable ones."

The boy chewed.

"I'm going to teach you how to poison me properly," Wendell said. "And then I'm going to eat it."

* * *

Maren put her foot down about the kitchen.

"Absolutely not," she said, arms folded, flour to her elbows. "You are not brewing deathcap tea over my good kettle. The last thing you brewed over my kettle was a love charm for Tilda Brannock, and even *that* stained the pot."

"That was chamomile and wishful thinking."

"It stained the pot, Wendell."

In the end they compromised on the old smokehouse at the edge of her garden, where the brick floor had seen forty years of salt-cured hams and could hardly be scandalized by anything else. Wendell swept it out, set a stool in the corner for the boy, and laid out his traveling kit on a plank table: the small brass scales, the mortar, the row of stoppered jars whose labels were written in a cipher only he and three dead men had ever known.

"Now," he said to the boy, "lesson one."

The boy sat forward on his stool, honey bun abandoned, attention total. He learned like dry ground drinks rain. It frightened Wendell a little, how fast he learned.

"The first rule of poison is that poison is honest." Wendell held up a pinch of dried foxglove between finger and thumb. "A knife lies. A sword lies. A man with a cudgel tells you exactly what he means to do and you can run from it. But this—" he let the foxglove crumble, "—this never raises its voice. It just waits for you to trust it. Everything poisonous in this room is patient. That's what makes it dangerous, and it's also what makes it *manageable*. You understand?"

The boy tilted his head. Then he reached out, took the chalk Wendell kept for marking jar counts, and drew on the smokehouse floor: a long, coiling line, like a river seen from very high up. At intervals along the line he made small marks.

Wendell looked at it for a long moment. "Yes. Patience has a shape. I suppose it would."

* * *

They worked through the week, and if anyone in Saltmere noticed that the royal taster had taken to shuttling jars out to Maren's smokehouse with a mute foundling on his heels, they had the grace to say nothing. Small towns survive on the principle that most curiosities cure themselves if left unwatered.

The plan itself was simple in outline and terrifying in practice. The boy carried something inside him — a memory, a weight, an inheritance of the old sorcery that the valley's wards had been built to bury. Ilsette wanted it. The crown's inquisitors wanted it. And every day, Wendell could feel the wards straining thinner beneath their feet, like ice on a spring pond, because the thing the boy carried pulled at them the way a lodestone pulls at iron.

So Wendell reasoned: he had spent a lifetime making his body into a vessel that could take in harm and not break. Perhaps the same trick worked at scale. Not all at once — never all at once, that was lesson one applied to himself — but drop by drop, the way he'd built tolerance to arsenic as a young man, spoonful by spoonful over years.

He began with the edges of it. When the boy slept badly, thrashing, the air in the room going strange and thick, Wendell would sit beside him and press his bare hand to the boy's brow and *pull*, gently, the way you draw a splinter. What came across was not pain exactly. It was older than pain. It tasted — and everything, for a taster, eventually came down to taste — it tasted like standing in a doorway before a storm that has been waiting since before there were doors.

The first time, he was sick for two days. The second time, one. By the fifth, he only sweated through his shirt and dreamed in a language he didn't know, and woke with the boy watching him anxiously, chalk in hand.

"You're worried about me," Wendell said, hoarse. "Don't be. This is what I'm *for*. Thirty years of swallowing terrible things so other people didn't have to. Turns out I was practicing."

The boy drew on the floor: a stick figure. Then another stick figure beside it. Then he crossed out the second one and drew arrows from the first figure into the space where it had been, filling it up.

"Yes," Wendell said quietly. "That's the idea. You pour yourself into me, and I hold it. A poison of one's own." He laughed, which turned into coughing. "Grett would have called it the stupidest thing she ever heard. She'd also have helped."

* * *

On the fourth evening, Maren came out to the smokehouse with supper and stood in the doorway holding the tray like a shield.

"Show me," she said.

"Maren—"

"You've been coming out of here gray as dishwater for four days. Show me or I fetch Doc Ellery, and you know what he'll say."

Wendell sighed and stuck out his tongue. She peered at it in the lamplight, frowned, prodded his jaw with one floured thumb, and looked into his eyes.

"Hm," she said.

"'Hm'?"

"Your pupils are doing something odd. And your breath smells like a thunderstorm, which is new." She set down the tray and, without asking, took his hand and turned it palm-up. The calluses there — the taster's calluses, built from decades of holding spoons steady while dying men's dinners tried to kill him — had changed. They'd gone faintly silver at the edges, like frost forming on glass.

"Wendell Nystrom," Maren said, "what have you been eating?"

"Nothing I haven't eaten before. Just... more of it than there's ever been." He flexed his fingers. The silver didn't wipe off. "It's working, though. The boy sleeps now. Actually sleeps. Two nights running without screaming."

Maren looked at the boy, who was drawing again — the coiling river-line, but longer now, and with little houses along its banks. Then she looked back at Wendell, and her expression did the complicated thing it always did when love and fury arrived at the same moment.

"If it starts killing you," she said, "you will tell me. Swear it."

"I swear it."

"Swear it properly. On the salt."

He rolled his eyes — everyone in Saltmere swore on the salt, it was practically municipal law — but he said it, hand flat on the brick: "On the salt, Mare. If it starts killing me, you'll know before I do."

* * *

The breakthrough came on the seventh night, and it came sideways, the way breakthroughs do.

The boy had been pulling memories out of himself deliberately now — guided extractions, Wendell calling them, mostly to feel organized. Each session ended with the boy exhausted and Wendell sweating, and each time a little less of the strangeness remained coiled in the child's sleep. But there was a core of it, deep down, that wouldn't move. The boy would reach for it and flinch back, shaking his head hard, drawing the same image over and over: a circle with a crack in it, and something behind the crack drawn as a scribble so violent the chalk tore.

"It won't come out gently," Wendell said. "Fine. Nothing important ever does."

That night, instead of pressing his hand to the boy's brow, he did something different. He took the boy's hands in his own — the silvered calluses against the small warm fingers — and instead of pulling, he *opened*. He did what he had done ten thousand times at a thousand banquet tables: relaxed the throat, steadied the heart, and said to his own body, *come in. I am a door, and I decide who passes.*

The core came across like a flood through a keyhole.

He was on the floor before he knew he'd fallen. The smokehouse swam around him. For a moment — a long, vast moment — he was not Wendell Nystrom of Saltmere at all. He was a valley full of screaming, he was a war with no banners, he was a word spoken so loudly it had killed the people who spoke it, he was the reason the first warden had dragged stones up the ridge and taught them to sing—

Then it settled, the way arsenic settles, the way belladonna settles, somewhere deep in him where his body kept its grudges, and he was himself again, lying on cold brick, laughing weakly at the ceiling.

The boy knelt beside him, terrified, patting his cheeks with both hands.

"I'm fine," Wendell rasped. "Better than fine. Do you know what happened? It fit. All of it — the whole rotten inheritance — it *fit*. Because I've been building the shelf for it my whole life and nobody told me." He sat up, swaying, grinning like a madman. The silver had spread past his palms now, tracing his forearms in faint filigree, visible even by lamplight. "I'm the ward now, boy. Not the stones on the ridge. Me. The wards outside can rest. They can finally, actually rest."

The boy stared at him. Then, slowly, he picked up the chalk and wrote on the floor, in the dead tongue first and then, carefully, letter by letter, in trade-common he'd been picking up from Maren's kitchen:

*does it hurt*

Wendell considered the question honestly, because the boy deserved honesty.

"Yes," he said. "But I've been hurt worse by oysters."

* * *

It was nearly midnight when Maren shook him awake, and the fact that she was shaking him awake at all meant something was wrong, because Maren treated midnight as a personal insult.

"Wendell. Get up. There's a light on the ridge."

He was downstairs in under a minute, heart hammering, the new weight inside him shifting uneasily like ballast in rough water. Through the kitchen window he could see it: a thin orange point moving along the crest of the western ridge, where the old ward-stones stood in their broken procession.

Not a lantern. Lanterns swung. This light moved steadily, deliberately, pausing at intervals — pausing, Wendell realized with a chill, at each stone.

"Someone's inspecting them," Maren whispered. "Checking the wards."

"No," Wendell said. "Someone's checking whether the wards are still *needed*." Because he could feel it now, from inside — he was part of the lattice whether he liked it or not, and along the whole length of the valley he sensed the stones relaxing, drowsing, leaning their ancient weight gratefully onto the new door they'd found. Anyone reading the ridge tonight would find the wards going quiet. Going dark. Like a house whose master had died.

The boy appeared at his elbow, silent, and pressed something into his hand. The chalk. On the kitchen flagstones, quick and urgent, he'd drawn the figure Wendell had come to dread: the circle, the crack, and beside it now — new — a rider on a horse.

"They've found us," Wendell said quietly. "Or they're about to. Either way, we're done being patient."

He went upstairs and started packing.
