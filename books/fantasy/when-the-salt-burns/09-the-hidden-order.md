## 9. The Hidden Order

The miller's road out of Saltmere ran along the old salt canal for three miles before it remembered it was supposed to climb, and then it climbed all at once, as though embarrassed by its own gentleness. Wendell had walked it a thousand times carrying baskets of flour and once, memorably, a live goose that had opinions about his hat. He had never walked it at dusk with a silent boy's hand in his and the taste of ward-stone dust still bitter at the back of his throat.

The boy—Wendell had stopped calling him *the child* somewhere around the second mile and started calling him *Ash*, because of the grey in his eyes and because naming things made them easier to carry—had not spoken since the ridge. Not in the dead tongue, not at all. He walked with his thumb hooked into Wendell's belt loop the way a boat hooks a mooring, and every hundred paces or so he would look back over his shoulder at the valley below, where the lamps of Saltmere were coming up one by one like slow yellow stars.

"They'll have noticed by now," Wendell said. "Maren will have noticed. Maren notices things before they happen; it's her most irritating quality." He said it lightly, but his chest did something complicated around her name. Maren Ostrow, warden's daughter, keeper of the ledger, the only person in Saltmere who had believed him about the wards without requiring him to say anything embarrassing first. She had pressed a satchel of bread and dried plums into his hands at the gate and told him, in front of witnesses, that if he came back he owed her a proper accounting, and if he didn't come back she would enter him in the ledger under *fools, departed*.

Ash tugged his belt loop twice. That was their code. One tug meant *stop*. Two meant *I hear you*. Three meant something Ash had never yet needed to say, and Wendell hoped sincerely never to learn.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen waymarker, an old pillar carved with the salt-crown sigil of the first kings, its face worn to a suggestion. Wendell built a small fire because small fires were safer than no fires, and roasted the plums, which was a habit from childhood and made Ash wrinkle his nose in a way that was nearly, blessedly, ordinary.

"You know," Wendell said, turning the fruit with a stick, "when I trained as a taster, they taught me that fear has a flavor. Did I ever tell you that?" Ash shook his head. "Bitter, but not like herbs. Like copper gone wrong. Every poison has its own signature, and fear is the only one that comes from inside the dish. So when I tasted for the magistrate, I wasn't tasting the food. I was tasting the cook." He offered a plum on the flat of his knife, an offering, not a trick. "You're afraid of something besides the riders. You've been afraid of it since the water. And someday you'll tell me what. No rush. I've got a famously patient tongue."

Ash took the plum. Then, very quietly, in a voice like a door opening in an empty house, he said one word in the dead language—and pointed east, past the waymarker, into the dark hills.

Wendell felt the hair on his arms stand up, which was interesting, because his body had been chemically bullied into not reacting to much of anything for twenty years. Whatever the word meant, some older part of him understood it perfectly and disapproved.

* * *

They were found at dawn.

Not by riders. By a woman selling apples.

She was simply *there* on the road ahead of them, sitting on a folding stool beside a basket, as though dawn markets were common on mountain tracks where there were neither towns nor customers. She wore travelling grey, a shawl the color of wet slate, and an expression of tremendous patience, the kind usually reserved for people who fish.

"Good morning," she said. "You look like a man who hasn't slept, a boy who hasn't spoken, and two people who haven't eaten properly. The apples are free."

Wendell stepped in front of Ash, which was absurd—he was a food-taster, poison was practically his native cuisine—but the body insists on its little dramas. "How long have you been sitting there?"

"Since before you decided to leave Saltmere," the woman said pleasantly. "Though I confess we only narrowed down the *where* yesterday. Roads are honest that way. There are so few of them."

"*We*," Wendell repeated. His mouth had gone dry, and he noted it professionally. Dry mouth, copper-fear, no smell of poison on the apples—though of course the best poisons had no smell at all, and the best poisoners no smell of themselves either.

"Oh yes. *We*." She smiled. "You may call me Sister Vess. Or Vess. The sisterhood of it is optional and mostly traditional."

"There is no sisterhood," said a voice behind them, "there is a committee, and Vess missed the last three meetings."

A man came around the waymarker, leading a mule loaded with nothing visible, which Wendell thought was either very poor packing or extremely good packing. He was broad, bearded, and dressed like a failing farmer, and he moved like none of those things. "Brother Anselm," he said, with a small bow. "And he's right, it's a committee. We vote on everything. It's agony."

"We want," Vess said, "to offer you breakfast, an escort, and an explanation—in ascending order of length."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we vote on what to do about it," Anselm sighed, "and you'd be amazed how long that takes."

Ash tugged Wendell's belt loop twice, then, after a moment, once more—a third tug, the unneeded one—and pointed at the apples.

He wanted one.

Wendell looked at the boy, then at the strangers, then took an apple himself and ate half of it slowly, watching his own pulse the way a sailor watches weather. Nothing. Clean, sweet, faintly tart, grown somewhere with honest soil. He handed the rest back to the basket, took another, and gave it to Ash.

"Well now," Vess said softly. "That was either very brave or very foolish."

"It was professional," said Wendell. "Now somebody explain why a committee of apple-sellers has been waiting on this road for me."

"Not for you," said Anselm gently, and both of them looked—not rudely, but unmistakably—at Ash.

* * *

Their camp, when the committee led them to it, was hidden in a fold of the hills behind a curtain of woven brush, and it was less an army than a household. Six people, a mule, two tents, a cook-fire, and a great deal of laundry. A lean young man was mending a net. A heavyset woman was arguing with a map. An elderly man sat sharpening a knife that already looked sharper than any tool ought legally to be.

"The Hidden Order," Vess announced, with the air of someone introducing a disappointing relative, "sworn since the founding of the wards to watch them, record them, and—if it ever became necessary—" she paused delicately, "—do something about them."

"You knew," Wendell said. It wasn't a question. "About Saltmere. About what's under it."

"We knew there was a seal," said the woman with the map, who turned out to be called Odile and spoke as though maps could hear her. "We knew it was old, and we knew it was quiet. Quiet seals are the good ones. Yours stopped being quiet about a month ago. We felt it here, forty leagues off, like a tooth starting to ache."

"So the wards are cracking, the crown sends inquisitors, and your committee sends..." Wendell counted, "...apples."

"The inquisitors send soldiers," Vess said. "Soldiers arrive loudly, take what they find, and break what they take. We arrived quietly, with breakfast. You'll forgive us for choosing our method based on results."

Anselm crouched by the fire and stirred it with a stick, unhurried. "Taster. May I ask you something? In your years at the magistrate's table—did anyone ever taste for *you*? Did anyone ever check what went into your mouth?"

Wendell blinked. "That's not—I *am* the check."

"Mm." Anselm fed the fire another branch. "Twenty years of swallowing what might kill you, so others can eat safely. And who tastes the taster?" He looked up, and his eyes were kind and terribly awake. "We've read the old records, Wendell Nystrom. The founders didn't just build walls around the sorcery they buried. They built *doors* through themselves. Wardens who carried a thread of the binding in their own bodies, so the seal had anchors living and breathing. Generations of them, thinned by time and marriage and forgetting, until Saltmere kept the rules but lost the reason. Your family was one of the door-lines. Didn't you ever wonder why poison slid off you when it should have laid you out? Why you can hold things in your blood that would fell an ox?"

The fire popped. Somewhere behind Wendell's ribs, an old ache—the one he'd always blamed on bad wine—turned over in its sleep.

"I wondered," he admitted. "I assumed I was just disagreeable."

Odile laughed despite herself. Vess did not laugh. She had taken Ash's hand, carefully, asking permission with her eyes first, and was looking at the fine pale scars on his palms—the ones he'd come out of the water with.

"He carries the whole memory," she murmured. "Every word of the dead tongue. The sorcery they drowned under your valley wrote itself into him before it let him go. He isn't a vessel, taster. He's a *library*. And libraries burn."

"That," said Wendell, "is exactly the sort of thing I wanted you not to say."

"Then here is the other thing." Vess released the boy's hand and sat back on her heels. "The crown wants him because inquisitors destroy what they cannot control. Corvane especially—you'll meet him, gods forbid, and when you do, remember that he genuinely believes he is saving the world by amputating it. But there are others who want him. Older than the crown. Things that have waited under mountains and marshes since the founding, listening for the seal to thin. To them, the boy is not a library." Her jaw tightened. "He is a *key*, and keys get used."

The camp had gone quiet. Even the mule seemed to be listening.

"So what does your committee propose?" Wendell asked. His voice was steady. He was rather proud of that. "Because if the answer involves taking him somewhere for study, or safekeeping, or any word ending in *-keeping*, I should tell you now that I've heard 'for his own good' from better liars than you."

"No one takes him anywhere," Anselm said. "He goes where he chooses, with you, or not at all. That was the vote. Eleven to two, and the two were being difficult."

"Eleven to two? There are six of you."

"There are nine more of us scattered between here and the high passes, and voting by messenger-bird is its own special torment." Anselm smiled, then let it fade. "But there is a place, taster. A sanctum in the mountains, where the old war was studied instead of buried. If his memory must be understood—*if he chooses* to understand it—that is where it can happen without crowns or worse things prying. We can take you as far as the foothills. After that, the road decides."

Wendell looked at Ash. Ash looked at the fire, at the scars on his own palms, at the eastern dark where he had pointed the night before and said the word that had made Wendell's skin crawl.

Then Ash reached out and took Wendell's hand, and turned it over, and tapped one finger against Wendell's wrist—right where, all his life, an unexplained ache had lived.

Three tugs. The unneeded signal.

"What does that mean, then?" Wendell asked, his mouth suddenly dry again. "You've never used it."

Ash couldn't answer in words. Instead he pressed Wendell's palm flat against his own small chest, then drew it away and held it up between them, open, as if to say: *what's in me can go into you.*

The fire cracked. Odile's map rustled. And far off down the valley road, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Wendell had been dreading since the ridge—hoofbeats, many, moving fast, and a horn blown once, clean and cold, like a blade being shown to the light before use.

Vess stood, all patience gone from her face. "Inquisitors," she said. "They've crossed the ford early. Taster—how fast can you walk?"

"Faster than I can run," Wendell said grimly, hauling Ash up. "It's a specialty of mine."
