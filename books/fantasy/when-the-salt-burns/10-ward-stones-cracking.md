## 10. Ward-Stones Cracking

The first stone cracked on a Tuesday, which Wendell found insulting. Catastrophe, in his experience, ought to have the decency to arrive on a day with some gravity to it—a Sunday, perhaps, or at the very least a Friday. Tuesdays were for market carts and mended fences and the baker's burnt loaves sold cheap at the door. They were not for the end of the world.

He was crossing the mill bridge with Fen perched on his shoulders when he heard it: a sound like a knuckle cracking inside a mountain, low and wrong, and then the bridge stones hummed underfoot the way a wineglass hums when someone sings its note three streets away.

Fen's hands tightened on Wendell's hair.

"I felt it too," Wendell said. "No, don't do that thing where you point dramatically at the ridge. People are watching. Mrs. Halloway is watching, and if she sees you pointing at things that aren't there she'll be at my door by supper with her theories and her seed cake, and I can only survive one of those."

Fen pointed anyway, with the serene stubbornness of a child who knew perfectly well that pointing was the entire job.

The ridge above Saltmere wore its ward-stones like an old dog wears its ticks—half-buried, long ignored, mostly forgotten. There were eleven of them, or had been once; Wendell could remember his grandfather counting nine and grumbling about two that had "wandered off," as though standing stones were sheep. From the bridge they looked like nothing. Gray lumps. Sheep-adjacent rocks. The kind of geology you'd walk past your whole life without a second glance.

Except now, the nearest one—the one above the millrace, squat and mossy as a sleeping troll—had a crack down its face as clean and bright as if someone had split it with a chisel of light.

And through the crack, faint as breath on winter glass, something glowed.

* * *

Maren did not say "I told you so." She said it so thoroughly with her face, however, that words would have been redundant.

"You're enjoying this," Wendell said.

"I'm cataloguing this." She stood at the base of the cracked stone with her ledger open, sketching the fissure in her quick, precise hand. Maren had been the valley's record-keeper since before Wendell could read, a wiry woman with ink-stained cuffs and the unshakeable conviction that anything worth happening was worth writing down twice. "There's a difference. Enjoyment is for people whose lives are quiet."

"Your life is quiet."

"My life has a boy who speaks a dead language living in my back room, an inquisitor's letter on my desk, and now a glowing rock. Quiet left. Quiet packed its bags." She glanced up. "Touch it."

"No."

"You're the one who can survive anything. That's rather the point of you."

"The point of me is surviving poisons, Maren. Poisoned food. Poisoned wine. Nobody ever slipped arsenic into a boulder." But he put his palm against the stone anyway, because she was right, which was the most irritating quality a person could have.

The stone was cold. Colder than a rock had any right to be on a morning climbing toward summer, cold the way the bottom of a well is cold, and beneath the cold, faintly, like a pulse taken through too many blankets, something beat. Once. Twice. Then stopped, as though embarrassed to be caught.

"It knows you're touching it," Maren said quietly.

"Rocks don't know things."

"This one does. Look at Fen."

Wendell looked. The boy had gone still in the particular way he went still around things that mattered—knees bent slightly, weight forward, the whole small body become an instrument of listening. His lips moved. No sound came out, but Wendell had spent enough weeks with him now to read the shapes: the old tongue, the pre-kingdom words, the ones that made the back of Wendell's neck prickle like a storm coming.

"What's he saying?"

Maren consulted her notes, then frowned. "If I've got the translation right—and Ilsette checked it twice—he's saying 'the shepherd is wounded.'"

"The what?"

"That's how the old texts describe the wards. Shepherds. Stones that watch the flock." She snapped the ledger shut. "And this shepherd just took a wound, Wendell, and nobody in this valley has tended a ward-stone in four hundred years. Do you see the problem?"

Wendell looked at the crack again. The light behind it had dimmed—or shifted—or perhaps it had simply noticed them noticing, and decided to play dead.

"Yes," he said. "The problem is that I liked my life. It was small, but it fit."

* * *

Ilsette arrived at dusk, which was typical of her; she had never once in her life been early for anything except other people's disasters.

She came up the hill road on a mule named Duchess who hated everyone equally, and she dismounted at Wendell's gate with the groan of a woman twenty years older than she was and the eyes of one considerably younger. Her satchel was full of paper. It was always full of paper. Wendell suspected she slept on a mattress of it.

"You felt the crack," she said. Not asked.

"The whole valley felt it. Old Cobb fell off his ladder. He says the end times are here. He also said that about the new tax assessment, so I'm reserving judgment."

"Wendell." Ilsette's voice did something rare: it dropped all its performance. Underneath the theatrics she was simply tired, and frightened, and old in the way that mattered. "Show me the stone."

They went up in the last gray light, the three of them, Maren trailing with her ledger and a lantern she didn't light because, as she muttered, "if the rock glows we won't need it, and if it doesn't glow I don't want to see what else does."

At the stone, Ilsette knelt. She pressed her ear against the crack the way a physician listens to a chest, and Wendell watched her face change—watched the color leave it in stages, like a tide going out.

"How bad?" he asked.

"When the wards were raised, they tell me there were songs. Whole choirs of them, woven into the stones, each one holding up the next. The valley wasn't warded, Wendell. It was sung shut." She sat back on her heels. "This song has been fraying for centuries. Everyone assumed the valley's silence meant safety. Silence means the singers have nearly finished dying."

"And when the last one dies?"

Ilsette looked at him, and then—this was the worst part—at Fen, who stood with his hand resting flat against the cracked stone as though comforting it, and whose lips were moving again in those silent, ancient syllables.

"Then whatever the valley was built to imprison," she said, "remembers where it lives."

* * *

That night Wendell couldn't sleep, which was unusual; his body was a professional sleeper, trained by years of odd hours and poisoned dinners to rest whenever opportunity offered. Instead he sat at his hearth with a cup of chicory pretending to be coffee, and listened.

Not to the house. To the ground.

It had started an hour ago—a vibration below hearing, more felt than heard, the way you feel a cart pass a mile off through the soles of your boots. Fen sat across from him doing the same thing, chin on his fists, eyes enormous. Between them, on the table, Maren's latest letter lay open: word from the capital, forwarded through three hands, that Inquisitor Corvane had left the city with twelve riders and a writ of full authority, and that his route passed within two days' ride of Saltmere.

"He's coming for the boy," Wendell said to the fire.

Fen made no sound, but his expression answered clearly enough: obviously.

"And the order wants you too. Ilsette's friends in their gray cloaks." He rubbed his eyes. "And the valley itself is coming apart underneath us. And I'm sitting here drinking pretend coffee like a man deciding between two kinds of bread."

Fen reached across the table and patted his hand. It was, Wendell reflected, exactly the gesture he himself used on grieving widows after tastings went poorly, and being consoled by an eight-year-old was a new indignity even in a year that had been nothing but indignities.

The floor shuddered.

Not a tremor this time—a proper shudder, enough to rattle the cup in its saucer, enough to make the hearthstones tick. Outside, a dog began to bark, then another, then half the dogs in Saltmere in a ragged chorus, all of them howling at nothing anyone could see.

Fen was already on his feet, pulling at Wendell's sleeve, pointing—not at the ridge this time, but at the floor. At the ground. His mouth shaped the old words fast and urgent, over and over, and Wendell caught the shape of them well enough now to know they were the same every time:

*The shepherd is wounded. The shepherd is falling.*

By the time they reached the square, half the village was out in their nightclothes with lanterns, and everyone was looking up at the ridge, where the second ward-stone—that one above the old quarry, visible even by starlight—was shedding a thin thread of light from a new crack like smoke rising from a chimney of glass.

Old Cobb grabbed Wendell's arm. "Boy! What in the salt and stone is happening?"

Wendell opened his mouth to say *nothing*, to say *settling earth*, to say any of the comfortable lies a taster learned to serve up like courses—and found he couldn't. The lies stuck in his throat alongside everything else that had ever tried to poison him.

"A crack in the wall," he said instead. "An old one. Go home. Bar your doors. If anything stranger happens than a humming rock, you'll hear it from me before you hear it from anyone."

Cobb squinted at him. "You've gone strange, Nystrom."

"Sir, I've been strange for months. You're only now paying attention."

* * *

Maren met him at dawn at the foot of the ridge with her ledger, her lantern finally lit, and an expression he'd seen on her perhaps three times in thirty years of friendship: the expression of a woman about to propose something sensible and terrible.

"There are eleven stones," she said. "Two lost, nine accounted for. One cracked six days ago. Two cracked last night. Ilsette thinks the failures will accelerate—each one that falls puts more weight on its neighbors, like arches, like teeth."

"Teeth," Wendell said. "Wonderful. So what's the terrible sensible thing?"

"We can't mend the songs. Those are gone; Ilsette's certain, and she's forgotten more about the old ways than the order ever wrote down. But the stones were also built to draw strength from something. A reservoir. A keeper." Maren turned the page, and Wendell saw the sketch she'd made—his own face, done without flattery, frowning at a ward-stone. "When you touched it, it pulsed. When Fen touches it, it quiets. The stones recognize both of you. And I keep thinking about what you are, Wendell. What you made yourself into. All those years of taking in poison, bit by bit, until your body learned to hold what should kill it."

"I don't follow."

"Don't you? A ward is a body that holds what should kill it." She tapped the sketch. "You've been rehearsing for this your whole life without knowing it. The question isn't whether you can carry it."

"Then what's the question?"

Maren closed the ledger gently, the way she closed it before delivering figures nobody wanted to hear.

"The question is whether carrying it leaves enough of you behind to bury."

Above them, on the ridge, the third stone sang—one clear, high, dying note, like a wineglass at the end of its song—and cracked from crown to root.

And down in the valley, at the edge of the salt flats where the road came in, dust rose in a long straight line that no cart makes and no wind holds: riders, many riders, moving fast toward Saltmere, and at their head a black banner Wendell had hoped never to see closer than a woodcut.

Fen took his hand. His grip was small and fierce.

*The shepherd is falling,* his fingers seemed to say against Wendell's palm. *Catch him.*
