## 11. Confession at the Hearth

The storm arrived an hour after sundown, the way storms did in Saltmere — all at once and without apology, rattling the shutters of Ilsette's cottage like a debt collector.

Wendell had known Ilsette Voss for most of his life, which meant he knew better than to knock. He let himself into her kitchen with the boy's hand swallowed in his own and was immediately struck by the smell of onions, browned butter, and the particular medicinal bitterness of willowbark tea that Ilsette brewed whether anyone was ill or not, on the principle that prevention was cheaper than grief.

"You're dripping on my floor," Ilsette said without turning from the stove. She was a small woman built like a fence post, silver-haired, with forearms that had delivered half the babies in the valley and slapped sense into most of them afterward.

"It's raining."

"I can see that. I have eyes and a window." She turned then, wiping her hands on her apron, and her gaze went straight past Wendell to the boy. It always did. Everyone's did, these days. "Tam. Come here, love. You're soaked through."

The boy crossed the kitchen to her without hesitation. This still astonished Wendell, though it shouldn't have. Children were practical creatures; they went where the warmth was. Tam — the name they'd given him, because he wouldn't say his own, couldn't, and *the boy* made everyone feel like they were discussing livestock — climbed onto the stool by the hearth as if he'd been doing it for years instead of weeks.

Ilsette pressed the back of her hand to his forehead, then his neck, then held his fingers a moment longer than strictly necessary. Her eyes flicked up to Wendell over the boy's bowed head.

"He's cold," she said.

"He's always cold lately."

"And you're always noticing." She said it lightly, but Wendell heard the weight under it, the same way he could taste arsenic in almond cake — faintly, unmistakably, and only because he'd trained himself his whole life to notice what others missed. "Sit down before you drip yourself into nothing. There's stew."

"I've eaten."

"Wendell Nystrom, you have never once in your life refused food, including that time at Brannic's wedding when the bride's mother served something you described later as 'a crime against pigs.' Sit."

He sat. It seemed wise. It usually did, where Ilsette was concerned.

* * *

They ate mostly in silence, broken by the rain hammering the roof and Tam's small, careful sounds of eating — the boy ate like someone who remembered hunger the way other people remembered a fever, vividly and with respect. He was seven or eight, nobody knew for certain. His hair was dark river-mud brown, his eyes a grey so pale they looked like weather. And when the firelight caught him at certain angles, when he was tired or frightened or dreaming, faint lines moved beneath his skin — not scars, not veins, but something older, like writing seen through parchment.

Ward-script. The warden's ledger had confirmed what Wendell already feared: markings that matched, glyph for glyph, the oldest stones at the valley's edge. Stones that had been cracking since the boy washed ashore.

"You're staring," Ilsette said.

"I'm thinking."

"Your thinking face and your staring face are identical, and both are rude." But there was no sting in it. She refilled his bowl without asking, which was its own kind of tenderness, and set a second honey biscuit beside Tam's plate, which the boy received with the solemn nod of a king accepting tribute.

It was Tam who broke the quiet — not with words, he had none, but with sound. A low hum, three notes, rising and falling. He did this sometimes in the evenings, and Wendell had learned, with a chill he kept to himself, that the melody matched the resonance of the great ward-stone at Salt Gate exactly. The boy wasn't humming a tune. He was humming a frequency. He was *tuning himself*, the way a taster learns the note a poison strikes in the blood.

Ilsette set down her spoon. She watched the boy hum, and her face did something complicated — grief and wonder fighting for the same ground.

"That's the old song," she said quietly. "My grandmother used to hum it. Said her grandmother sang it during the Burning Years, when they raised the wards. She called it the lullaby of the valley." She laughed, short and dry. "We all thought it was nonsense. An old woman's fancy."

"Maybe it was," Wendell said. "And maybe the nonsense is the only true thing left in Saltmere."

Ilsette looked at him for a long moment across the steam rising off the stew. Then she stood, banked the fire, poured three cups of willowbark tea — one of which she immediately replaced with honeyed milk, because even she had limits — and said, "Tam, love. Go lie down in the back room. There's a blanket with sheep on it and no wolves anywhere near, whatever the wind says otherwise."

The boy went. At the doorway he paused and looked back at Wendell — that long, ancient, weighing look that no seven-year-old should be able to produce — and then he was gone, and the sheep-blanket room door clicked shut.

Ilsette sat down across from Wendell, folded her hands, and waited.

She could outwait a mountain. Wendell had seen her do it, once, against the tax assessor.

"I need to tell you something," he said finally.

"I know. That's why I sent the boy away. Out with it."

He turned his cup in his hands. Outside, the storm threw itself against the valley walls as though testing them, the way a prisoner tests bars.

"The night the riders came through — Corvane's men, the ones in grey — I told you I found their camp by accident, while gathering saltwort."

"I remember."

"That was a lie." He said it flatly, because he'd rehearsed it flatly, and flatness was the only armor he owned that worked. "I went looking for them. I'd known they were on the ridge for two days before they came down. I wanted to see what we were dealing with."

Ilsette's expression didn't change, which was worse than anger. "Go on."

"They're not just inquisitors. They're not here for a stray child, whatever their writ says. Corvane has maps. Old ones. Maps of Saltmere that show the ward-line as it was *before* the kingdom redrew the borders — before anyone alive should have known where it ran." He paused. "And they have a list of names. People in this valley who've been... noted. Marked. Families whose grandmothers hummed the old song. Midwives. Stone-cutters. Your name is on it, Ilsette. Third from the top."

For the first time in forty years of knowing her, Wendell saw Ilsette Voss go still in a way that wasn't calm.

"Third," she repeated.

"Corvane himself is first. Or rather—" Wendell exhaled. "There's a name above his. Someone he answers to. And next to that name, there's a word in the dead tongue. I copied it down." He slid the scrap of paper across the table. "I can't read it. But Tam can. I showed him, and he touched it, and the marks under his skin lit up like coals, and he looked at me like — like I'd handed him a knife and asked him to remember whose throat it had been at, last time."

Ilsette didn't touch the paper. She looked at it the way one looks at a snake in the woodpile: respectfully, and from a distance.

"Why are you telling me this now?" she asked. "Why not the night you came back soaked and lying about saltwort?"

"Because that night I hadn't decided anything yet. And because—" His voice caught on something, and he pushed through it the way he'd once pushed through hemlock, deliberately, knowing the cost. "Because I'm going to take him to the mountains, Ils. The sanctuary Maren spoke of. And taking him means leading Corvane to him, and leading Corvane to him means every person on that list becomes leverage. Including you. Including—" he gestured vaguely at the village beyond the walls, the whole warm, stubborn, salt-crusted world of it, "—everyone."

"So you came to confess before you run."

"I came to ask if you hate me for it."

Ilsette was quiet for so long that the fire popped twice and the kettle began, softly, to complain. Then she reached across the table and cuffed him on the ear, gently, the way she'd done when he was nine and had eaten a beetle on a dare.

"You stupid man," she said. "You brave, stupid, self-important man. You think this is *your* confession? You think you're the one carrying secrets in my kitchen?"

"Ilsette—"

"My name is on a crown's list, Wendell. Third from the top. You don't get on a list like that by accident. You get on it because somebody, somewhere, wrote down that the Voss women sing songs they shouldn't know." She pulled open the collar of her dress — briskly, unromantically, the way she did everything — and showed him the skin above her collarbone. In the firelight, faint as watermarks, Wendell saw the ghost of script. Ward-script. Faded almost to nothing, but there.

"I was marked at birth," she said. "Every woman in my line for six generations, back to the Burning Years. We thought it was a birthmark. My mother thought it was a birthmark. My grandmother knew better and died smiling about it." She fixed her collar. "I've been part of this war since before I could walk, and I never knew it. So no, Wendell. I don't hate you. I'm furious with you, which is different, and I'll be billing you separately for the tea. But hating you would require me to forget that you're the first person in fifty years to tell me the truth in this valley."

"The truth gets people burned."

"The lie got us here." She stood, gathered the dishes, and stopped with her back to him at the basin. When she spoke again, her voice had gone soft, and soft was the rarest thing Ilsette owned. "Take him to the mountains. Take my name off your conscience while you're at it — I'll manage Corvane's list better than you'll manage your guilt. But there's something you need to understand first, and I'd rather you heard it from me than from the stones themselves."

"Which is?"

She turned around. Behind her, through the kitchen window, lightning lit the ridge — and for one white instant Wendell saw the peaks above the tree line, and the thin thread of smoke rising from a place where no shepherd would camp in this weather.

"The wards aren't just failing," Ilsette said. "They're being *called*. Whatever sleeps behind them — whatever the valley was built to keep — it knows the boy is here. And it knows he's leaving." She dried her hands slowly. "Maren's order thinks the danger is the crown. The crown thinks the danger is the boy. But my grandmother's song has a last verse nobody ever sings aloud, and I'll tell you why, Wendell—"

The lights went out.

Not dimmed — extinguished, every lamp and candle in the cottage at once, and the fire in the hearth dropped to embers blue as frostbite. In the sudden dark, Wendell heard Tam cry out from the back room, a small sound of pure alarm, and felt the hairs rise along his arms the way they did when a poison entered his blood — some instinct older than training screaming *wrong, wrong, wrong.*

Then, from the direction of Salt Gate, carried impossibly clear through the roar of the storm, came a sound like a bell the size of a mountain being struck once.

The great ward-stone. Singing.

Ilsette found his hand in the dark and gripped it hard enough to grind bone.

"Now you know why," she finished, "we never sang the last verse."

Outside, on the ridge, the smoke had gone out too — snuffed like a candle — and where it had been, Wendell could just make out, in the lightning's stuttering light, a line of figures standing motionless along the crest. Not riders.

Watchers.

And they were all facing the cottage.
