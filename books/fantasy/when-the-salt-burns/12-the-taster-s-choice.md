## 12. The Taster's Choice

Wendell had faced down a great many terrible things in his twenty-nine years. He had swallowed monkshood steeped in wine and kept his face pleasant for a visiting duke. He had eaten a suspected pie while the cook who made it wept into her apron three feet away. He had once been asked to taste for a bishop whose enemies numbered, by Wendell's own count, at least forty — and he had done it smiling, because that was the job: smile, swallow, wait, live.

But he had never before had to choose.

He sat on the floor of Ilsette's back room with his spine against the grain barrels, a cup of barley tea gone cold in his hands, and tried to think the problem through the way he thought about poison. That was the trick of it, really. Every problem was a poison if you looked at it long enough. It had a dose, an onset, a course of symptoms, and — if you were lucky — an antidote.

The boy was asleep on the pallet, curled small under Ilsette's good quilt, breathing the deep even breaths of someone who had finally stopped running. His name — or the name they'd given him, since he couldn't tell them his own — was Perrin. He was perhaps seven years old. And sleeping there in the lamplight, with his thumb halfway to his mouth and one sock missing, he looked like absolutely nothing worth burning down a valley over.

Which was, of course, the most dangerous thing about him.

"You're doing the face," Ilsette said from the doorway.

"What face?"

"The one where you pretend you're weighing herbs." She came in carrying another lamp and set it on the shelf, and the room went gold at both ends. "You do it when you're frightened. You've done it since you were nine years old and ate a bad mussel to prove a point."

"I proved the point," Wendell said. "The mussel was bad."

"You were sick for two days."

"And yet vindicated." He turned the cold cup in his hands. Outside, Saltmere was settling into its evening noises — shutters latching, someone's dog complaining about something, the mill wheel turning slow and patient as it had for two hundred years. Somewhere out past all of that, on the ridge road, riders were making camp. He could feel them the way he felt weather coming in a bad knee.

Ilsette lowered herself onto the stool across from him, which took some doing, and studied him over her spectacles. "So," she said. "Tell me the doses."

He blinked at her. "What?"

"You've got your thinking face on, so treat it like your work. Dose, onset, course, antidote. Go on. I'm listening." She folded her hands in her lap like a schoolmistress, which, for forty years, she had also been.

Wendell almost smiled. Almost. Instead he set the cup down and did what she said, because Ilsette Amberdale had taught half of Saltmere to read and every one of them to mind their manners, and he was not going to be the exception.

"Dose," he said quietly, glancing at the sleeping boy. "One child. Carrying — I don't even know what to call it. A memory. The old sorcery. The thing the valley's wards were built to keep locked up, walking around in a pair of mismatched socks."

"Onset?"

"Slow, so far. The wards are failing whether we want them to or not — the salt stones are cracking, you saw the one by the mill. But the crown's inquisitors arrived ahead of schedule. That's Corvane's doing. Which means onset is now measured in days."

"Course of symptoms?"

"That's the ugly part." He rubbed his eyes. "If I give the boy up, Corvane takes him to the capital. The inquisitors will try to pull whatever he's carrying out of him — they'll want it as a weapon, or they'll want it destroyed, and either way the pulling kills him. I've seen what their kind does with things they don't understand. Everyone has. That's why the valley exists."

"And if you don't give him up?"

"If we run — north, into the mountains, toward the hidden order Maren keeps hinting at — then Corvane burns Saltmere looking for us. The wards fail anyway. The valley dies either way. The only question is whether it dies *for* something."

The lamp flames stood very still. Down the hall, the shop clock ticked with the sound of a patient heart.

"There's your error," Ilsette said.

Wendell looked up. "Sorry?"

"You've laid it out like there are two dishes on the table and you must eat one. Surrender the boy, or flee with the boy." She tapped the arm of the stool. "But you're the taster, Wendell Nystrom. Since when have you ever eaten what was put in front of you?"

He stared at her. "I taste everything put in front of me. That is literally my profession."

"You *test* it first," she said, with the mild triumph of a woman landing a point she'd been sharpening all evening. "You find out what's in it. So before you go choosing between surrender and flight, ask yourself what's actually being served. Who benefits? What's the true ingredient?" Her eyes flicked to the boy, then back. "Corvane wants the child because of what's inside him. The hidden order wants the child because of what's inside him. Nobody — nobody — has asked what the child wants. Or what the thing inside him wants. And until you know that, any choice you make is just you swallowing blind."

Wendell sat with that for a long moment.

It was, annoyingly, exactly right.

* * *

Maren arrived after dark, the way she always did, which was to say without knocking and slightly out of breath. She was soaked to the knees — she'd come down through the millstream shallows rather than use the bridge, because bridges had eyes on them now — and she stood dripping on Ilsette's clean floor with the total lack of apology that Wendell had come to regard as her defining feature.

"They've moved," she reported. "Two riders came down to the village green at dusk. Not inquisitor gray — plain cloaks, but the horses are capital-bred, you can tell by the hocks. They bought bread at the baker's and asked questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"'Have you seen a child.' 'Has anyone new come to stay.' Very polite." Maren wrung out her braid over the slop bucket. "Old Tammett told them he hadn't seen anything but his own shadow in thirty years, which is technically true, because Tammett is mostly shadow these days. Then they asked about you specifically, Wendell."

A cold thread slid down his spine. "Me?"

"By name. 'The taster, Nystrom.'" She looked at him steadily. "They know you're involved. Corvane must have gotten word from the ridge — someone talked."

Wendell thought of the warden's ledger, of the neat columns in his own handwriting, of the confession he'd written and sealed and never sent. *Someone* hadn't talked. *He* had, in ink, in advance, like a fool preparing his own trial. He'd left the ledger in the warden's keeping precisely so that the truth would survive him — but Corvane would read it as evidence, and evidence pointed at the boy, and the boy slept ten feet away.

"He'll make an example," Wendell said. "That's what Corvane does. He doesn't need to catch us. He needs the valley to hand us over, and the fastest way to get a valley to hand over a child is to convince them the child is the reason their world is ending." He paused. "And the worst part is — he wouldn't even be lying. The wards *are* failing. The salt *is* burning. If the old power wakes, everyone here suffers for real. Corvane can stand on the green and tell the truth, and the truth will do his work for him."

"So we leave tonight," Maren said. "Now. Before morning. There's a shepherd's track over the northern shoulder — hard going, but no rider takes it in the dark, and by the time they realize we're gone we'll be two valleys away."

"And the wards?" Ilsette asked sharply. "The moment you cross the ward-line with that child, whatever's left of the valley's protection frays further. You know that. The stones are already singing wrong."

"I know," Wendell said.

"And the order in the mountains — the ones you keep not telling me about—" Ilsette's voice had the particular steel it developed only when discussing her son's habit of secrets, "—you trust them?"

"No," Wendell said honestly. "Not yet. I trust Maren. The rest is a door we haven't opened."

"Then open it carefully," Ilsette said. "Doors are how trouble gets in."

Maren started to answer, and stopped — because the boy was awake.

None of them had heard him stir, but there he was, sitting up on the pallet with the quilt pooled around him, watching them with those too-old eyes. In the lamplight, his mouth moved. No sound came out; there rarely did. But his hands spoke instead, quick little shapes in the air, and Wendell felt the hair rise on his arms, because he knew those shapes now. Three nights of sitting with the boy, of Ilsette sounding out syllables from her oldest books, had taught him the beginnings of it.

*Choose*, the boy signed. Then he pointed at Wendell. *Your* choose. And then, with terrible simplicity, he pressed both hands flat against his own chest — *me* — and drew one finger slowly across his throat.

The room went silent.

"He understands," Maren whispered. "Wendell. He understands every word."

"Of course he does," Wendell said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt. "He remembers the language the wards were written in. Why wouldn't he remember ours too?" He got up off the floor, crossed to the pallet, and knelt so that his eyes were level with the boy's. Up close, Perrin smelled of woodsmoke and river water and something faintly mineral underneath, like the inside of a cave. Like salt.

"Perrin," he said softly. "Can you show me what happens if they take you? Can you show me what's inside you?"

The boy considered him for a long moment. Then he held up one small hand, palm out, fingers spread — and closed it slowly into a fist, and as it closed, the lamp nearest him guttered. Both lamps guttered. The shadows leaned in like a crowd. For one heartbeat Wendell felt the taste of the air change — copper and lightning and something old, old, old — and then the boy opened his fist again and the flames stood up straight and the room was only a room.

But Wendell had tasted it. And a taster knows: it wasn't malicious. It wasn't cruel. It was simply vast, and barely contained, and *frightened*. The same combination, he realized with a strange lurch, as a seven-year-old child who had washed down a river alone.

"It doesn't want to hurt anyone," he said aloud, wonderingly. "It wants to be *held* properly. That's all the wards ever were, isn't it — someone holding it, generation after generation. And the holders died off or forgot, and now it's loose in the smallest possible vessel, and everyone with power wants to grab it by the throat."

Maren crouched beside him. "Then we take it to people who remember how to hold it. The sanctum. My teachers." Her jaw set. "It's a long road and Corvane is faster, but we don't need to outrun him forever. We need to outrun him to the gate."

"And Saltmere?" Ilsette asked. Quietly. She didn't look at Wendell when she asked it, and that was how he knew how much the question cost her — this woman who had buried a husband under the eastward oaks and delivered half the valley's children onto her own kitchen table.

Wendell rose. He walked to the window and looked out at the village: the crooked chimneys, the mill wheel turning, the ward-stone on the corner glowing its faint steady amber like a nightlight over a cradle. Two hundred years of ordinary life. Bread baking. Dogs arguing. Children learning their letters from Ilsette Amberdale.

Every dish on the table was poisoned. That was the truth of it. Surrender the boy and Saltmere lived a few more comfortable years with a weapon growing in the capital's cellars. Flee with the boy and Saltmere burned for harboring him. Stay and do nothing and the failing wards killed them all slowly.

Unless.

The thought arrived the way dangerous thoughts always arrived for Wendell — quietly, in the shape of his own body. Twenty-nine years of swallowing what should have killed him. Monkshood and mercury, rot-ergot and widow's venom, every poison the kitchens of three duchies had feared. His blood carried the memory of all of it. His flesh had learned, dose by dose, the art of taking harm into itself and remaining standing.

*A living ward,* the hidden order's letter had called such people. *Rare. Precious. Perhaps unique.*

Perhaps, said the quiet thought, that was the antidote nobody had bothered to taste-test.

"Wendell?" Maren said. "You've got the face."

"I know," he said. He was still looking out the window. "Ilsette. When we go north — how much of the old lore did you keep? The ward-craft, from before the valley sealed itself off. You told me once your grandmother's grandmother wrote protections in the margins of her recipe book."

Ilsette's eyebrows climbed. "I told you that in confidence, and also it's a recipe book, not a grimoire."

"A recipe book with ward-lines drawn around the preserves," Wendell said, "so nothing spoils. I've seen it. I've *tasted* the difference in her pickles." He turned around, and the lamplight caught him, and both women went still at whatever expression he wore. "Blood and salt and intent. That's all a ward is. That's all it's ever been. And I have spent my whole life becoming a body that can hold what would kill anyone else."

"No," Ilsette said.

"You don't even know what I'm—"

"*No,*" she said again, louder, and then, with the discipline of four decades in a schoolroom, she mastered herself. "Whatever you're about to propose, you will eat supper first, sleep, and propose it in daylight like a sensible person."

"Mother—"

"I am not your mother," Ilsette said, which was a lie so old and so load-bearing that neither of them remarked on it anymore, "but I am the woman who raised you, and I say: supper, sleep, daylight. The boy needs rest. Maren needs dry boots. And you, Wendell Nystrom, need to stop treating yourself like an ingredient."

Outside, far off on the ridge, a horse whinnied. None of them moved for a moment. Then the night sounds resumed — wind, wheel, dog — and Wendell let out a breath.

"Supper," he agreed. "Sleep. Daylight." He looked down at Perrin, who was watching him with an expression of grave, unblinking attention, the way one might watch a cook decide what to do with a difficult cut of meat. "But tomorrow, we choose. All of us. And Perrin—" he knelt one last time, and signed clumsily, *your choose too*, "—you get the biggest vote."

The boy's mouth twitched. It was the first thing resembling a smile any of them had seen from him, and it was, Wendell thought, exactly like watching dawn come up somewhere that had forgotten the sun existed.

It was Maren who found it, an hour later, while checking the seals on the shutters. She came back into the main room holding a scrap of paper that had been slipped under the door — no wax, no mark, no footprints in the fresh mud outside, which was itself impossible and therefore a message all its own.

The paper bore a single line, in handwriting so fine it might have been etched by a needle:

*The Order knows you hold the vessel. The Order knows what you are. Come north alone, taster, or we will come south for both of you.*

Below it, drawn with the same needle-fine precision, was a symbol Wendell recognized from the cracked ward-stone by the mill — and from the letter he had burned weeks ago.

Ilsette looked at it. Maren looked at it. Wendell looked at it longest of all.

"Well," he said at last, into the silence. "Daylight may have to come early."
