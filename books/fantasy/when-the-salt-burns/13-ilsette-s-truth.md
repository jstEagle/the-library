## 13. Ilsette's Truth

They walked through the night, and by the time the grey light came seeping over the salt flats, Wendell had learned three things about Ilsette Marsh.

The first was that she could keep a pace that would shame a mule. The second was that she fed the boy dried plums from a pocket in her coat as though she had been carrying them for him specifically, which was a thought Wendell filed away and did not like the shape of. And the third was that she had not once asked where they were going.

That last one bothered him most.

"You've been quiet," he said, when they stopped to rest beneath a leaning shelf of rock that stank of old brine. "Quieter than usual, even, and you're usually quiet the way a stone is quiet."

"I'm resting," Ilsette said.

"You're thinking."

"I'm allowed."

"Of course you're allowed." He lowered himself against the rock and winced at the ache in his knees. "I only notice that we are fleeing across the flats with an inquisitor's writ on our heads, and you haven't asked me our destination. Most people ask. Corvane's people asked. You'd think it was the first question anyone learns."

The boy sat between them, working a plum stone between his fingers with great seriousness. At the sound of their voices he looked up, then back down, content. Since the night at Maren's hearth — since the words he had spoken in the old tongue, the ones that had made the ward-stone sing and crack — he had been calmer. As if saying what he carried out loud had loosened something.

Ilsette pulled her collar up against the flat's cold wind. "Perhaps I already know where we're going."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. The wind moved her hair; she did not move at all.

"Do you," he said.

"The mountains," she said. "Northwest. Past the last shepherd's hut there's a valley the maps leave blank because the mapmakers' ink wouldn't take. There's an order there that most people think is a story told to frighten young clerks. And you've known about them longer than you've let on, Wendell Nystrom, because you have the look of a man who has been carrying two secrets for years and only recently discovered room for a third."

He laughed before he could help it — a short, tired bark of a laugh. "You know, most people find my face unreadable. It's considered one of my better features. Years of tasting poison taught me not to flinch at anything."

"Yes," Ilsette said. "And years of watching people eat taught me to read every flinch they didn't know they made. We're rather well matched, you and I. Unfortunately for us both."

* * *

They made camp that evening in a hollow where the salt gave way to hardpan and a thin spring trickled out from under a shelf of stone, tasting faintly of iron. Wendell built a small fire — small enough to hide behind his own body, cupped like a secret — and boiled barley with the dried meat Ilsette produced from yet another inexhaustible pocket.

The boy ate three bowls. Then he fell asleep sitting up, mid-chew, the way children do when their bodies simply close the shop without warning, and Ilsette eased him down onto her rolled cloak.

"He trusts you," Wendell said quietly.

"He trusts you more. He watches your hands when you cook. He watched mine all day and never once took my arm when we crossed the scree." She said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Children remember kindnesses. Even ones given before they could name them."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Ilsette said, drawing her knees up toward the fire, "that I owe you a truth, and I have been putting off paying that debt since Saltmere burned. So. While the boy sleeps, and while you still have the strength to be angry."

"Ilsette—"

"Wendell." She said his name gently, the way she used to say *swallow this* before handing him something bitter. "Let me finish, or I'll lose my nerve, and I'm too old and too tired to say it twice."

He closed his mouth.

"I was not born in Saltmere," she said. "I came to it thirty-one years ago, in autumn, with a cart of medicines and a story about plague work in the southern provinces. Both were true enough. What I didn't tell anyone — what I have never told anyone — is why I stopped in Saltmere at all. Of all the warded valleys in the world, I chose yours. Because I was looking for it."

The fire popped. In the hollow beyond, the wind combed the hardpan with a sound like someone slowly sweeping.

"The order you've been walking toward," Ilsette continued, "the one in the blank place on the maps — we call ourselves the Wardens' Remnant. We were the ones who built the wards. Not the crown, whatever the crown's histories claim. The sorcerers themselves. Those who refused the war. When our brethren sealed the old powers beneath the valleys, some of us chose exile inside the prisons we helped make, to stand watch from within. To keep the wards fed. To notice, if ever they began to fail."

Wendell sat very still. His whole life, the valley had been explained to him in crown terms: royal decree, royal surveyors, royal wards laid by royal mages long dead. The king's peace, the king's salt. And here was Ilsette, who had bandaged his childhood scrapes and dosed his fevers, telling him the salt had been hers.

"You fed the wards," he said. "All those years."

"Some of us did. There were others in Saltmere, over the decades — you'd know none of their names now, they passed as ordinary folk and left no ordinary traces. A cooper. A schoolmistress. An old man who fished the mere and talked to it, and everyone assumed he was mad, and he was, a little, but that's neither here nor there." Her mouth curved briefly. "We kept the stones fed with attention, with ritual, with memory. That is what a ward truly is, Wendell. Not salt. Not stone. Memory. The valley forgets nothing so long as someone in it remembers everything."

"And the boy?"

Ilsette looked down at the sleeping child. The firelight moved on his face.

"We don't know where he came from. Not precisely. But we know what he is. When the wards began to weaken — and they have been weakening for twenty years, faster every season — the thing imprisoned beneath Saltmere needed a vessel. A way to preserve itself outside its cage. So it reached outward, the way a drowning man reaches, and found... a child. Unborn, or newly born, I can't say. It poured what it could spare into him. Not the whole of it — thank every mercy — but a seed. A living memory of the old sorcery, carried in a body small enough to pass beneath the wards' notice, because the wards watch for power, and no one watches for a baby."

"That night at Maren's hearth," Wendell said slowly. "When he spoke. The stone cracked."

"The seed woke. Hearing the old tongue spoken aloud — hearing *itself* spoken aloud — called it toward the surface. Which means the inquisitors felt it too, wherever their instruments are. Which means, I am sorry to say, that the child is a beacon now, and will be until either the seed is removed or—" she paused, choosing, "—or it is taken into someone willing to hold it."

The silence stretched.

"Someone," Wendell said. "Willing."

"Someone whose body has spent a lifetime learning to survive what should kill it." Ilsette met his eyes across the fire. "Yes, Wendell. I know what you are planning. I've known since the night you stood in my doorway with the boy behind you and asked me what poisons do to memory. You thought you were being subtle. You were being about as subtle as a dropped anvil."

Heat crawled up his neck. "It doesn't matter how I feel about it. If the wards are failing anyway—"

"They are failing because they are starving, not because they are broken." Ilsette leaned forward, and for the first time all journey her voice sharpened into the tone she'd used on patients who tried to leave their beds too soon. "A ward fed with memory holds. A ward held by a dying man's poisoned body — Wendell, listen to me — that isn't a ward. That's a fuse. You'd be carrying the seed the way a torch carries flame. One bad fever, one wound, one moment of forgetting yourself, and everything the old ones imprisoned comes pouring out through the hole where you used to be."

"Then teach me not to forget myself."

"With what? You want to spend ten years in the sanctum learning disciplines that took our founders lifetimes?" She shook her head. "No. There is another way. The reason I came to Saltmere all those years ago wasn't only to keep the ward. It was to prepare for exactly this. The Remnant has a rite — an old one, dangerous, but tested. It can draw the seed out of the child and bind it into the ward-stones themselves. Renew the valley's protection for another generation. Properly. Safely. Without feeding a man to it like kindling."

Wendell stared at her. "And you're only mentioning this now? We've walked two days across the flats!"

"I'm mentioning it now because I wanted to see whether you'd choose the mountains for the boy's sake or for your own plan's sake, and I'm pleased to report—" she gestured at him, "—you sulked beautifully either way."

"I do not sulk."

"You absolutely sulk. You've been sulking with tremendous dignity since the village burned."

Despite himself — despite everything — Wendell laughed. It came out ragged, and it turned, partway through, into something else, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until it passed. Ilsette waited, the way she always had, as if grief were simply another medicine that needed time to work.

"The rite," he managed finally. "What does it need?"

"A ward-stone that hasn't cracked. The child. And a keeper of the old memory — which is me, and which is why Corvane wants me alive, Wendell, and why we cannot be caught. I'm not baggage on this journey. I'm half the cargo."

The boy stirred in his sleep, murmured something in the dead tongue — soft, wordless-sounding, like water moving under ice — and went still again. Neither of them spoke until he settled.

"There's something else," Ilsette said. "Isn't there. You've got your taster's face on. The one where your mouth says 'hmm, lovely' while your stomach says 'we die tonight.'"

Wendell almost smiled. Then he reached into his shirt and drew out the shard — the sliver of white ward-stone he had pried loose from the cracked stone at Maren's hearth, wrapped in linen. It had been warm ever since. Not hot. Warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm.

"When the boy spoke," he said, "this sang. I felt it through my glove. And it hasn't stopped. Listen."

He unwrapped it and held it out. Ilsette took it with both hands, careful as a priestess, and bent her head close.

For a moment, nothing. Then her breath caught.

"It's not singing," she whispered. "It's answering. Something is calling to it, Wendell. From the northwest." Her eyes lifted to his, and in the firelight he saw, for the first time in thirty years of knowing her, genuine fear. "There's nothing in the northwest except the sanctum."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn. The boy rode on Wendell's shoulders, heavy as a sack of grain and twice as warm, pointing at things — a hawk, a strange salt formation like a petrified wave — with silent insistence, as though Wendell might otherwise miss them.

Ilsette walked beside them, and after a mile she spoke without turning her head.

"The rite needs three days of preparation once we reach the sanctum. During those days, the child stays with the Remnant's teachers. You'll hate that. You'll hover. Try to hover politely."

"I don't hover."

"You hovered over the soup kettle in Maren's kitchen. You hovered over the soup kettle, Wendell."

Behind them, far to the south, the horizon had changed overnight. Where the smoke of Saltmere had risen for two days, there was now only a pale smear, and beneath it — visible only when the light struck right, the way ink refuses a mapmaker's page — a line of figures on the ridge. Too distant to count. Not distant enough to ignore.

The boy saw them first. He went rigid on Wendell's shoulders, both fists closing in his hair, and made a sound Wendell had never heard from him: not a cry, but a low note, sustained, in the old tongue. The shard in Wendell's shirt grew suddenly, unmistakably hot.

Ilsette turned. Squinted. And swore with a fluency that would have shocked the schoolmistresses of Saltmere.

"That's not an inquisitor patrol," she said. "Inquisitors travel in twos and threes and complain about their horses. That's a column." She was already moving, quick and low, waving them toward a fold in the hardpan. "Down. Down, both of you. They've found the trail, and they're not following it — they're ahead of it. They know where we're going."

"How could they possibly—"

"Because Corvane has a keeper of his own," Ilsette said grimly, pulling the boy down into cover with hands that were not quite steady. "Every order has its traitor, Wendell. Ours had one. And hers was me — until I ran. Whatever she told them before I escaped, they've had thirty years to dig it out of her."

The low note from the boy's throat faded. In the silence after it, from the ridge, faint as a memory, came an answering note — the same tongue, the same pitch, sung by a voice that had no business reaching that distance.

The shard burned against Wendell's chest like a second heart.

"They're calling to him," Ilsette breathed. "Wendell — they have a speaker. Move *now*."
