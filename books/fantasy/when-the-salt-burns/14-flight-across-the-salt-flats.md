## 14. Flight Across the Salt Flats

The salt flats began where the road gave up.

Wendell felt it in his boots first—the packed clay of the valley road softening, whitening, until each step crunched like biting into old bread. Behind them, three days' hard riding back, Saltmere lay hidden under its wards, and somewhere behind *that*, Corvane's riders were regrouping after Maren's stand at the ford. Ahead lay nothing but white. Miles and miles of nothing but white, glittering under a sky so wide it seemed to be trying to escape itself.

"We're going to die out here," Ilsette announced cheerfully, swinging down from her mare to check the animal's hooves. "Of thirst, probably. Or boredom. Boredom first, I think."

"You're in a fine mood," Wendell said.

"I'm always in a fine mood when I'm certain of disaster. It saves time." She pried a crust of salt from her horse's frog and flicked it away. "How's our scholar this morning?"

The boy sat on the packhorse with his knees drawn up, watching the horizon with that unnerving stillness he had. He'd been named Oren by the woman who'd pulled him from the river, though whether it was truly his name no one knew. He was seven, perhaps eight. He had not spoken a word since Saltmere—not aloud. But he wrote, now, in letters older than any kingdom Wendell had sworn to serve, and sometimes at night the ground near him hummed faintly, like a struck bell deciding whether to ring.

Oren looked at Ilsette, then at Wendell, then held up one finger and pointed east.

"One day?" Wendell guessed.

Oren shook his head. Pointed at the sun, traced an arc with his finger, then another arc, smaller.

"A half-day to the far side," Wendell translated. "You've been reading the ward-stone maps again."

Oren permitted himself the ghost of a smile. It was the closest thing to speech he offered, and Wendell had learned to treasure it the way other men treasured gold.

"Well," said Ilsette, remounting, "if the child who carries the last living memory of a sorcery that ate kingdoms says we can cross by sundown, I feel *much* better."

* * *

They crossed the flats the way you cross a frozen lake: quickly, quietly, and without discussing what was underneath.

By midday the heat came up off the salt in shimmering sheets, and the world shrank to three colors—white below, blue above, and the small sweating space between where two adults and a child and three tired horses moved like insects across a dinner plate. There was no shade. There was no water except what they carried in skins that grew lighter by the hour. There was no cover whatsoever, which meant that if Corvane's scouts reached the ridge behind them, they would see everything.

Wendell tried not to think about that. He thought about it constantly.

Instead he made himself useful the only way he knew how. He doled out water in careful measures—he'd spent twenty years measuring things against his own tongue, drops and grains and half-swallows, and his hands did not know how to be careless. He chewed a strip of dried mutton and handed Oren a piece, watching the boy eat with the same professional attention he'd once given the king's soup.

"You're doing it again," Ilsette observed.

"Doing what?"

"Tasting the air around him. You get a look. Like a man listening for a wrong note in a song."

Wendell swallowed. "Habit."

"It's more than habit. You've been tasting him since the valley." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "What do you taste?"

He considered lying. He'd never been good at it; a taster's whole craft was truth extracted through the body, and his body had opinions it shared freely.

"Bitter," he admitted. "Not poison-bitter. More like... the rind of something. Underneath everything, all the time now, there's a bitterness coming off him that wasn't there in Saltmere. It's getting stronger the farther we go from the wards." He turned his canteen in his hands. "I think the wards were holding *him* together as much as holding the world away from him. And I think whatever's inside him knows it's running out of cage."

Ilsette was quiet for a long moment. The salt crackled under the horses' shifting hooves.

"And when the cage finishes running out?"

"Then we find out why they built a valley to bury it."

Ahead of them, Oren had stopped the packhorse and dismounted. He stood staring at the salt, and as they rode up, Wendell saw why.

There were lines in the salt. Old ones, half-dissolved by rain and centuries, but unmistakable once seen—a pattern radiating out from beneath their feet in great sweeping curves, like the rings of a felled tree. And at the center, where the boy stood, the salt had grown in a low pale ridge, almost like a wall, almost like—

"A ward-stone," Wendell said slowly. "There's a ward-stone under the flats."

Oren crouched and pressed both palms flat against the crust. His eyes went distant. When he looked up, he pointed east again, then made a shape in the air with his hands—a mountain, unmistakably, two peaks and a notch between.

"The sanctum," Ilsette breathed. "He's saying the old chain runs all the way to the mountains. They built the flats *on* the wards." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "All this time everyone thought the salt country was empty because it was worthless. It's empty because it's *armed*."

Oren nodded once, gravely, and then—this was the part Wendell would remember later, in the bad hours—he knelt and drew one deliberate line in the salt with his fingertip. A straight line, pointing back the way they'd come. Then he crossed it out.

Behind us, the line said. Not anymore.

"He's telling us the chain's broken behind us," Wendell said quietly. "Maren's stand at the ford. When she burned the relay stone—" His throat tightened. Maren, who had stayed behind with a crossbow and a stubbornness the size of a cathedral, buying them their head start. "She didn't just slow them down. She cut the valley off from the outer wards. Which means these flats are the last living link before the mountains."

"Which means," Ilsette said, "that if Corvane reaches the flats while the boy's still on them..."

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Wendell finished it in the privacy of his own skull, where he kept all his worst knowledge, professionally organized: *then the last living ward in the world becomes a battlefield.*

"Ride," he said.

* * *

They rode.

The afternoon went long and strange. The salt played tricks—showing them lakes that weren't there, riders who dissolved into heat-shimmer, a second horizon floating above the real one like a promise the world kept forgetting to keep. Oren rode with his eyes half-closed, and twice Wendell saw his lips move, shaping words in the dead tongue, silent as prayer. Each time, the bitterness Wendell tasted grew a shade stronger, and each time, the horses grew a shade harder to handle. Animals knew. Animals always knew.

It was near sundown, with the sky going the color of a fresh bruise, that Ilsette's mare threw a shoe.

Of course she did. Of course it was then. Wendell had survived forty-one assassination attempts by proxy—every meal a battlefield, every banquet a war—and he had learned that catastrophe does not arrive dramatically. It arrives at the worst possible moment, wearing the most boring possible face. A thrown shoe. A loose stone. A dropped cup.

They stopped in the lee of a salt ridge barely tall enough to be called a ridge. Wendell knelt and examined the hoof while Ilsette scanned the backtrail and Oren stood watch over the water skins, counting them with his eyes the way he counted everything.

"Nailed it loose this morning," Wendell muttered. "I *saw* it nailed loose. I thought we'd make the far side."

"You thought wrong. It happens even to you." Ilsette crouched beside him, keeping her body between him and the open flats, an old soldier's instinct she probably didn't know she had. "Can you fix it?"

"With what? I'm a food taster. My kit contains tweezers, a silver spoon, and an alarming quantity of antidotes."

"Give me the tweezers."

Between the two of them—her hands, his instructions, the boy holding the mare's head and murmuring to her in a language without sound—they got the shoe cinched tight enough to hold. Probably. Wendell tested it, then tested it again, then caught himself testing it a third time and made himself stop.

"You're doing the thing," Ilsette said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you check the door three times because you checked it twice already. Your master trained you well and ruined you thoroughly."

"My master," Wendell said, tightening the last strap, "taught me that the difference between paranoia and preparation is whether you're still alive to argue about it. He also taught me eleven ways to detect nightshade and none of them help with horseshoes, so perhaps we should both admit my education has gaps."

Oren tugged his sleeve. He was pointing north—not toward the mountains, but along the flats' edge, where the white gave way to a smudge of darker land. Wendell followed his finger and saw it: a thread of woodsmoke, thin and gray against the darkening sky.

A village. Or what had been a village.

"That's Harrow's End," Ilsette said, and her voice had changed. All the humor had gone out of it, drained away like water into salt. "Or it was. Last year it was a wool town, four hundred souls, a decent inn. The crown emptied it when the inquisitors decided the old tongue might still be spoken there." She squinted. "That smoke's too small for a burning. That's a chimney smoke. Somebody's *living* there."

Oren was shaking his head. Slowly, firmly. He pointed at the smoke, then at himself, then pressed his hand flat against his chest, over his heart, and his face did something Wendell had never seen it do—it crumpled. Just for a moment. A seven-year-old's grief, naked and enormous, before he mastered it again the way he mastered everything, silently, terribly young.

"He knows someone there," Wendell said softly.

"He knows *everyone* everywhere," Ilsette said. "That's the whole cursed problem. He's the memory of a dead age walking around in a child's legs. Every ruin is his house. Every grave is his family." She exhaled. "Question is whether we go around or through."

Wendell looked at the smoke. He looked at the boy. He tasted the air—habit, comfort, the one sense that had never lied to him—and found the bitterness sharper than it had been at noon, and underneath it something else now, something new.

Salt. Not the flats' salt. An older salt, mineral-deep, the taste of the ward-stones themselves. It was coming from the direction of the village.

"Through," he heard himself say. "Something's there. Something of the old chain. If the boy can touch it, maybe he can steady himself before the mountains. And if Corvane's riders are watching the flats' far edge, we'll never cross open ground unseen anyway. We need walls, even broken ones."

"Broken walls full of ghosts," Ilsette said.

"I've dined with worse company."

She snorted despite herself. "That's the spirit. Terrible, doomed spirit."

They moved out as the last light failed, three small shadows crossing the white, toward the smoke and the salt-taste and whatever waited in the bones of Harrow's End. Behind them the flats lay silent and vast, hiding their armed emptiness under the stars. Ahead, the darkness smelled of woodsmoke and old grief.

And once—just once, as they crested the last low ridge—Oren turned in the saddle and looked back the way they had come, at the miles of moonlit salt, and raised one small hand as if in farewell.

Far off, at the very edge of seeing, torchlight flickered. Many torches. Moving fast.

"They've found the flats," Wendell said.

"No," Ilsette said grimly, gathering the reins. "They've found where the flats *end*. Ride, Wendell. Ride now."

The horses plunged downslope toward the dark ruins of Harrow's End, and behind them, patient as poison, the torches began to spread across the white like fire across paper.
