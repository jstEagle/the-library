## 15. The Village That Burned

They smelled it before they saw it.

Wendell had been walking half-asleep for hours, one hand on Illik's shoulder out of habit rather than necessity—the boy steered himself fine, picking his way over the salt crust with the surefootedness of a goat who had never once doubted his own hooves—but the smell woke him fully. Woodsmoke, yes, that was ordinary enough, but beneath it something sweeter and wronger, like bread left in an oven three days too long.

"That's not a hearth fire," Ilsette said quietly. She had stopped at the crest of a low rise and stood very still, her hood pushed back, her gray hair lifting in the flat wind.

Maren came up beside her, squinting against the pale glare of morning on the flats. "Village," she said. "Half a mile, maybe."

"How can you tell?"

"Smoke doesn't lie about its size. A chimney makes a thread. That—" she nodded at the smudge on the horizon, thick as a bruised thumb, "—is a whole street's worth of chimneys at once, or was." She paused. "Or is."

Nobody said *or was burning*. Nobody needed to. The wind shifted and brought them the sweetness again, and Illik made a small sound in his throat—not a word, he hadn't spoken since the flats began, but a noise like a dog hearing thunder two valleys away—and pressed closer to Wendell's side.

"We could go around," Wendell said. He heard how it sounded even as he said it. Cowardly. Practical. Both things were true, and he'd learned long ago that most truths were.

Ilsette looked at him with those patient gray eyes that always made him feel like a boy caught stealing honey. "Around where, Wendell? The flats run forty miles in either direction. There's no water between here and the mountain passes except in villages." She softened it with a small, tired smile. "Besides. If someone burned it, I'd rather know who, before they burn us."

So they went forward, because forward was all there was.

* * *

The village was called Bracken Hollow—at least, it had been called that according to the crooked sign at the crossroads, which now hung from a single nail and pointed cheerfully toward nothing. It had perhaps thirty houses built of fieldstone and turf, a well with a mossy bucket-chain, a shrine to some harvest saint whose painted face had bubbled and peeled in the heat. Half of it still smoked. The other half had burned down to black ribs and was already growing a skin of ash-gray snowfall, sifting gently over everything like the valley itself trying to apologize.

There were no bodies.

That was the thing Wendell noticed first, and the thing that would not stop bothering him. He had expected—well, he had tried not to expect anything, but expectations happen anyway, unbidden, in the gut. Bodies. Crows. The terrible arithmetic of a massacre. Instead there was only absence: doors standing open, a cradle tipped in a doorway, a pot of porridge scorched dry on a hearth, a single shoe by the well. As if everyone had simply stood up mid-meal and walked into the hills.

"People got out," Maren said. She crouched by the shoe, turning it over—a child's boot, leather cracked with wear. "Look. No drag marks, no blood, no struggle. They left ahead of it or during it."

"Or were taken ahead of it," Ilsette murmured. She had drifted toward the shrine and was studying the ground around it with an intensity that made Wendell nervous. "Inquisitors don't usually leave villages intact, you know. When Corvane's kind decide a place is tainted, they salt the earth and post a notice. This is different. This is—" she frowned, pressing two fingers to a scorch mark on the shrine's stone base, then pulling them back quickly with a small hiss. "This is old sorcery. Pre-kingdom work. The same grammar as the ward-stones."

Wendell felt the bottom drop out of his stomach. "The wards are this far south?"

"The wards are everywhere, dear. That was rather the point of them." She wiped her fingers on her cloak. "But this isn't the wards. This is what the wards were built to keep in. Or keep out. Scholars argued about it for centuries, which tells you everything about scholars."

Illik tugged Wendell's sleeve.

The boy was staring at the largest house in the village—a manor of sorts, or what passed for one, with a slate roof that had mostly survived and a door of good black oak hanging open on darkness. His lips moved. No sound came out, but Wendell had spent enough weeks with him now to read the shape of words in his mouth, and the shape was unmistakable, because it was the same word he'd mouthed at every ruin, every old stone, every crack in the earth since Saltmere:

*Home.*

"No," Wendell said, before he could think better of it. Then, gentler, crouching to the boy's level: "I don't know what this place is to you. But we're not going inside that house just because your feet remember it. We get water, we get food if there's any food, and we go."

Illik looked at him with an expression Wendell had come to dread, because it wasn't defiance—it was patience. The patience of someone who knew something you didn't and was being terribly polite about it.

"He's right, though," Maren said, rising. "We should check the manor. If anyone stayed behind—if anyone's hiding—we'd want to find them before nightfall."

"And if nobody's hiding?" Wendell asked.

Maren shrugged. "Then we sleep under a roof instead of the stars, and I'll forgive the roof for being smoky."

It was, Wendell reflected as they crossed the ash-sifted square, impossible to argue with Maren when she decided to be reasonable, because she did it so rarely and so thoroughly.

* * *

Inside, the manor smelled of cold smoke and old paper. The great room's furniture had been shoved against the walls—deliberately, it seemed, cleared for something—and in the center of the floor, drawn in white chalk or salt or both, was a circle. Within the circle, more writing, dense and looping, in a script that made Wendell's eyes slide off it the way a cart wheel slides off ice.

He'd seen it before. In Saltmere, in the warden's ledger, in the margins of documents no one was supposed to read. The dead tongue. The boy's tongue.

"Illik," he started, "don't—"

But the boy was already stepping over the circle's edge, careful as a cat avoiding a puddle, and walking to the far wall where a tapestry hung, scorched at one corner. He pulled it aside with both hands, revealing a stone door set into the wall, low and narrow, with no handle.

Above the door, carved deep and old, ran a line of the dead script. Illik touched each character in turn, and though he made no sound, Wendell found—to his considerable alarm—that he could almost *hear* them anyway, a whisper at the edge of sense, like a song remembered from childhood. The boy turned back to him and pointed at the characters, then at Wendell, then made a gesture Wendell had learned meant *you say it.*

"I don't speak it," Wendell said. "I've told you. I'm a food-taster. My entire education is knowing which mushrooms kill you and which ones merely ruin your week."

"He wants you to try," Ilsette said from the doorway. Her voice had gone strange and soft. "Wendell. The poison training. Your tongue. You've tasted things no living person should be able to taste and survived. Do you understand? The old sorcery was a language of the body before it was a language of the mouth. That's why your order existed. That's why the tasters were created in the first place." She smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "You're not illiterate, dear. You're just reading with the wrong organ."

"That is the worst sentence anyone has ever said to me," Wendell said, "and I once dined with a duke who described his gout in detail through four courses."

But he stepped forward. Because of course he did. Because the circle on the floor was humming faintly against the soles of his boots, the way a tuning fork hums, and because the boy was watching him with that awful patient hope, and because somewhere in the last weeks Wendell Nystrom had stopped being the man who avoided trouble and become the man who walked into burned villages, which was a transformation he intended to file a complaint about at the earliest opportunity.

He touched the carved letters. Closed his eyes. Did the thing he did when tasting—an odd interior attention, a listening with the flesh.

The letters tasted of iron and rain and something older, something like the smell of a cave that has never known sunlight. And underneath, faint as a fingerprint in wax:

*A door opens to a keeper. A keeper is one who has swallowed the word.*

"Well," he said aloud, his mouth dry. "It says something about keepers. About swallowing." He opened his eyes. Everyone was staring at him. "What?"

"You read it," Ilsette breathed. "You actually—you're certain? Swallowed?"

"As certain as I am of anything, which, historically, has gotten me poisoned twice."

Maren had gone very still, her hand resting on the knife at her belt in a way that was probably unconscious and probably shouldn't be. "The hidden order," she said slowly. "Corvane mentioned them. The ones who want the boy. What if this village—what if these people weren't fleeing the fire?"

"What do you mean?"

"What if they were the order? What if they lived here, guarding whatever's behind that door, and something flushed them out?" She looked around the emptied room. "Thirty people don't abandon their homes without a fight unless they're running from something they can't fight. Or unless they're leaving *on purpose*, to lead the pursuer away from—"

The stone door clicked.

Everyone froze. Illik stood with his palm flat against it, looking up at Wendell with an expression of profound apology, the universal expression of someone who has just done the thing they were explicitly asked not to do approximately eleven seconds ago.

The door swung inward on a breath of cold air that smelled of stone and deep water and, very faintly, of salt.

* * *

The stair went down farther than any cellar had a right to. Maren lit a lantern—she carried oil the way other soldiers carried lucky charms—and the light reached maybe ten feet into a darkness that swallowed it without gratitude. Illik led. Of course he led; arguing with him about it would have required speech, and he had the advantage of apparently knowing the way in his bones.

The passage opened into a round chamber, and the chamber was full of books.

Not shelves of them—though there were shelves, sagging under the weight—but stacks, towers, drifts. Books piled on tables, on chairs, on the floor in drifts knee-deep. Some were bound in leather gone soft with age. Some were clay tablets. One enormous volume lay chained to a lectern like a dog that had bitten someone. Candles stood in niches along the walls, and when Maren touched flame to the nearest, the whole ring caught alight at once, wicks igniting in sequence around the circle with a sound like a held breath released.

"Thirty people," Ilsette whispered, turning in place. "Thirty keepers. This is what they kept." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Wendell realized, with a lurch, that she was crying—silently, without changing expression, tears simply falling down her face as if her eyes had finally given up pretending to be dry.

"You knew this place," he said. Not a question.

"I suspected. I hoped." She wiped her face briskly, annoyed at herself. "My order—the little splinter order, the one that found me in Saltmere—they spoke of a library that predated the kingdom. Every copy of every record of the old war. Everything the crown burned, someone saved. I thought it was a story we told recruits." She laughed, wet and short. "I thought most of it was stories."

Maren had drifted to the chained book and was frowning at it. "If the villagers fled, why leave all this? Why not carry it out?"

"Because you cannot carry a memory," Ilsette said. "That's the point. These aren't just books, Maren. Look at the bindings. Look at the ink." She lifted a slim volume and showed them the pages: the text shimmered faintly, moving like insects under frost. "These are vessels. Written memories. The old sorcerers poured what they knew into books the way we pour wine into skins. Whoever holds the book can drink the knowledge." She set it down carefully. "And whoever drinks too much stops being whoever they were."

Wendell thought of the circle upstairs. *A keeper is one who has swallowed the word.* He thought of his own ruined tongue, scarred and hardened by twenty years of poisons, and wondered—not for the first time, but for the first time with real fear—exactly what kind of vessel *he* might be.

Illik had stopped at the center of the room, where the candlelight gathered thickest. On a low stone plinth lay a single object: a small clay tablet, no bigger than a hand, blank on its face.

Except it wasn't blank. As Wendell drew closer he saw the surface was covered in script so fine it might have been etched by a needle held by a praying mantis. And as he watched, the script moved. Slowly. Deliberately. Rearranging itself.

"It's writing," he said stupidly.

"No," said a voice from the doorway behind them. "It's finishing."

Wendell spun. Maren's knife was out before he finished turning.

A woman stood in the stair-passage, lean and gray-faced, dressed in the scorched remains of a scholar's robe. Ash streaked her cheeks like war paint. In her arms she carried a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and in her free hand she held a torch that had burned down nearly to her fingers. She looked at Illik, and her stern expression broke apart like a dam.

"There you are," she said. "Oh, thank every salt-blessed stone, there you *are*."

The boy made a sound—his first in two days, thin and rusty as an unoiled hinge—and ran to her, and she dropped the torch in a puddle and caught him, and for a long moment the only sounds in the library of the dead tongue were a mute child's broken breathing and a woman's voice saying *hush, hush, I have you, hush.*

Wendell looked at Maren. Maren looked at Wendell. Ilsette, wisely, said nothing at all.

Finally the woman raised her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely ferocious.

"Which of you is the taster?" she demanded.

Wendell raised his hand halfway, feeling like a student in a class he hadn't studied for. "That would be me. Wendell Nystrom. Formerly of Saltmere. Currently of nowhere, increasingly."

"Good." She stood, keeping one arm locked around Illik. "Because the tablet is nearly finished rewriting itself, and when it finishes, everything it contains pours into the nearest open mind within a hundred paces. I've been holding it shut for six days with the last working binding-spell in this library, and I am *very tired*." Her gaze fixed on him with terrible hope. "And the binding will only transfer to someone whose body has already survived worse than anything a memory can do. So. Taster. How attached are you to remembering your own name?"

Behind him, Wendell heard Maren exhale slowly, and Ilsette murmur, "Ah," in the voice of a scholar watching a theory become catastrophically true.

The tablet's fine script crawled faster.

"Soon, then," the woman said. "Choose quickly, taster."
