## 16. Corvane's Offer

The inn at Harrow's Bend had once been called The Gilded Goose, though the goose had been gilded so long ago that only the memory of gold remained—a ghost of shine on a weathered sign that creaked in the wind like an old man clearing his throat before saying something he'd rather not say.

Wendell stood outside it in the gray morning light and did not want to go in.

Behind him, the salt flats stretched away toward the smoke still rising from what had been Millbrook. Behind *that*, somewhere beyond the horizon of his dread, rode the inquisitors. And ahead, through the crooked door of the Gilded Goose, waited a man who had sent three messengers and a gift of candied plums to say he wished only to talk.

Nobody wished only to talk. In Wendell's experience—and his experience was considerable, having spent fifteen years putting things in his mouth that other people wanted dead—nobody who said *only* meant it. Only was a word that arrived dressed in its Sunday clothes while carrying knives under its coat.

"He'll have food," said Maren beside him. She had her hood up and her hand near her knife, which was how she looked even when handing out bread to orphans. "Food is good. You're the one always telling me food is good."

"Food I've tasted first," Wendell said. "That was rather the entire arrangement of my life until recently."

The boy tugged at Wendell's sleeve. Tam—that was the name they'd given him, because the first word he'd ever spoken to Wendell had been a word from the dead tongue that sounded, to uneducated ears, remarkably like *tam*, and because naming a child *the inheritance of an extinct civilization* seemed a heavy thing to put on a boy who couldn't yet grow a beard. He pointed at the sign, then at the goose, then made a flapping motion with his arms and a face of such profound skepticism that Wendell laughed despite everything.

"Yes, it's a very ugly bird," Wendell agreed. "You have a critic's eye. That will serve you well or ruin you completely."

Tam signed something else—quick, fluid, half the old tongue and half the improvised language the two of them had built between them out of necessity, hunger, and long nights on the flats. *Danger inside.* Then he touched his own chest, twice. *But I am not afraid.*

"Liar," Wendell said fondly, and touched the boy's chest back, and then his own. *A little afraid. Both of us. That's allowed.*

Maren watched this exchange with the expression she wore whenever the two of them spoke without words—which was to say, the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "He says there's danger," she translated, unnecessarily, because she'd learned to read the signs faster than she admitted. "And you're going in anyway."

"I'm going in anyway."

"Why?"

"Because if Corvane wanted us dead, we'd be dead already, and because a man who sends candied plums wants something, and I would very much like to know what." Wendell paused. "Also because I'm hungry. Fear curdles the appetite but does not eliminate it. Ask any condemned man."

They went in.

* * *

The common room of the Gilded Goose smelled of woodsmoke, spilled ale, and—Wendell noted immediately, the old reflexes never dying—roasted parsnips that had been cooked with rosemary by someone who did not understand that rosemary is a tyrant among herbs and must be governed firmly. There were perhaps a dozen patrons, all of whom found sudden and absorbing interest in their breakfasts when the three of them entered, which meant they had all been paid or warned, which meant everyone in the room knew exactly who was walking through that door.

Corvane sat at the table by the hearth as though he had grown there, like a particularly well-dressed mushroom.

Wendell had heard descriptions, of course. Everyone in the hidden order had opinions about Corvane, most of them delivered in the hushed tones people used for dangerous weather. But the descriptions had failed to capture the essential strangeness of the man: he looked *ordinary*. Middle years, brown hair going gray at the temples, a face like a respected provincial magistrate—the kind of man you'd trust to hold your coat. It was only the eyes. They were patient in a way no ordinary eyes were patient. They were the eyes of someone who had decided, decades ago, exactly how long he was willing to wait, and had budgeted accordingly.

"Wendell Nystrom," Corvane said, rising. He did not offer his hand, which Wendell appreciated. Men who offered hands wanted to see if yours trembled. "Please sit. I've taken the liberty of ordering breakfast, which I promise has been tasted—by me, personally, an hour ago, and again just now by my associate, who has suffered greatly for the cause." He gestured to a thin woman at the next table who gave Wendell a weary thumbs-up. "I'm told you have professional standards on the matter."

"You've done your research," Wendell said, sitting. Tam slid into the seat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. Maren remained standing, which was her way of sitting.

"I make a study of useful people." Corvane settled back down. For a moment he simply looked at Tam, and Wendell felt the boy go still against his side—not frightened, exactly, but *attentive*, the way a deer attends to a wolf that has not yet decided to hunt. "Hello," Corvane said to the boy, gently. "My name is Aldous Corvane. I've been looking for you for a very long time. Longer than you've been alive, in fact."

Tam said nothing, in the way of boys everywhere confronted with strange adults, which is to say he stared with the frank hostility of a cat being shown a new piece of furniture.

"He doesn't speak," Wendell said. "Not our tongue."

"So I've heard. Though I'm told he speaks otherwise." Corvane turned those patient eyes back to Wendell. "You've heard it yourself, haven't you? The old speech. Words older than any crown on any head in this kingdom. Words that were old when Saltmere was a marsh." He leaned forward slightly. "Tell me what it sounded like, Wendell. I've read every account, every scrap of the wardens' ledgers, every fragment the order has salvaged in four hundred years of searching. But reading about a thing and hearing it—" he spread his hands, and for one unguarded moment there was something almost human in his face, something like hunger that had nothing to do with breakfast— "hearing it must be like hearing a song you've known only from sheet music."

"It sounded like words," Wendell said flatly. "In a language. Spoken by a child. I'm a food-taster, Master Corvane, not a scholar. My area of expertise is whether or not a soup intends you harm."

Something flickered across Corvane's face—amusement, genuine and quickly mastered. "Yes. Ilsette warned me you'd be like this."

"Ilsette talks too much."

"Ilsette talks precisely as much as she chooses, which is a discipline I envy." Corvane poured tea into three cups—three, deliberately, leaving the fourth empty, a small courtesy of theater—and pushed one toward Wendell. "Willowbark and honey. Good for the nerves. And you, Master Nystrom, are holding your nerves together with thread and spite, which I respect, but which cannot continue indefinitely."

"Everyone keeps telling me how I feel lately," Wendell said. "The inquisitors told me I was confused. The order tells me I'm exhausted. Perhaps I should start charging for the service."

"What do *you* think you feel?"

The question caught Wendell off guard, mostly because it was a good question, and he resented good questions from men he'd decided to dislike. He thought about lying. His whole life had trained him to lie—to smile over poisoned dishes, to praise sauces that could kill, to let a king believe himself beloved. But Corvane's patience made lies feel like throwing pebbles at a mountain.

"I feel," Wendell said slowly, "like a man who has discovered the floor of his house has been hollow all along. Everything I trusted—the wards, the rules, the whole careful architecture of Saltmere—it was all standing on top of something buried, and now the something is awake, and it has chosen my kitchen boy to carry it." He wrapped his hands around the tea cup, not drinking. "And I feel angry. At the founders who built the prison instead of solving the problem. At the kings who let the wardens rot. At myself, for spending fifteen years learning to survive poison and not one day learning what to do when the poison is a *person*."

Tam's hand found Wendell's under the table. Small fingers. Warm. Steadying.

Corvane listened with his head tilted, and when Wendell finished, he nodded slowly, as if Wendell had passed some examination whose subject had never been announced. "Good," he said. "Angry is honest. The inquisitors want your fear; the fanatics in my own order want your faith. I want neither. I want your anger, because anger is the one thing in this affair that points *toward* the truth instead of away from it."

"And what truth is that?"

"That the choice you think you're facing isn't the real one." Corvane set down his cup. Outside, the wind worried the sign of the Gilded Goose, and somewhere upstairs a bed creaked as some traveler rolled over and went back to sleep, oblivious. "Let me tell you what happens if you keep running. You know the inquisitors have hounds now. You know Warden-Superintendent Vess has authority to burn any village that shelters you—we saw how that went in Millbrook, and I am sorry for it, truly, whatever you believe of me. You have maybe six days of lead. The flats will slow them, but the mountains won't; Vess grew up in mountains and hunts like a woman who holds grudges against geography itself."

"We know all this," Maren said from her post by the wall. Her voice was flat as slate.

"You know the shape of it, not the weight." Corvane didn't look at her; he kept his gaze courteously on Wendell, which Wendell suspected was itself a tactic, making Maren furniture. "Here is the weight. The thing sleeping under Saltmere—the thing your valley's wards were built to cage—is not merely powerful. It is *patient*. Four hundred years it has pressed against those stones, wearing them the way water wears rock, and the cracks you've seen, the ward-stones splitting, the child arriving at the threshold carrying its memory in his head—those aren't accidents. Those are symptoms. The cage is failing because the thing inside has finally learned the shape of the lock. And when it gets out—and it will get out, running or not running, surrendered or not surrendered—every warded place in the world fails with it, because they were all built from the same design, the same craft, the same *flaw*."

The tea had gone cold in Wendell's hands. He hadn't noticed drinking any of it. "Ilsette said something like this. In her way. Which is to say she said it sideways, in riddles, while mending a sock."

"Ilsette believes the answer is to strengthen the wards. Rebuild the old network, restore what the founders made. She's brilliant and she's wrong, because the design itself is the flaw. You cannot patch a cage whose prisoner has memorized the bars." Corvane's voice dropped, and the room seemed to lean in with him, the fire popping softly in the hearth. "But there is another path. Not the founders' path. A *third* thing. We have fragments of it—the order has spent four centuries gathering them, and I have spent thirty years assembling the pieces into a working whole. A working *ward*, Wendell. Not stone and salt and prayer, but living. A person, trained, prepared, hardened—" his eyes moved, deliberate and slow, over Wendell's face, his hands, the whole poison-scarred architecture of him— "who can take the inheritance into themselves and hold it. Contain it. Not a cage around the power. A cage that *is* the power. Nothing pressing to get out, because there is no outside anymore. No crack to find. No lock to learn."

The silence lasted four heartbeats. Wendell counted them; it was a habit.

"You're describing a person swallowing a god," he said.

"I'm describing a vessel," Corvane said. "And yes. I am aware of what I'm asking. I have spent thirty years asking it of myself, in fact—I began the preparation when I was younger than you are now. My body rejected it." He pulled off his left glove, casually, as though showing a scar from a childhood fall, and the hand beneath was wrong—smooth, faintly translucent at the fingertips, like wax held too near a flame and pulled back just in time. "It takes a particular constitution. One tempered against poison for years. One that has already taught itself to treat the lethal as the ordinary." He drew the glove back on. "There may be a dozen people alive suited to it. There may be three. There is exactly one who has already begun the work without knowing it, who already carries a fraction of the inheritance in his blood from months of proximity to the boy, whose body has been quietly negotiating terms since the night you took him in."

Wendell became aware that his mouth was dry, which was absurd, given the tea. "The headaches," he said quietly. "The dreams. The way the ward-stones hum when I walk past."

"The symptoms of a lock recognizing its key," Corvane said. "Or the beginning of a door becoming its own wall. The texts disagree on the poetry. The mechanics, however, are quite clear."

Beside him, Tam made a sound. Small. Wendell knew all his sounds by now—the hunger ones, the fear ones, the stubborn ones, the one that meant *bad dream, don't ask, just stay*. This one was none of these. This one was low and rough, and when Wendell turned, the boy was signing, fast, his hands shaking:

*No. No. No.*

"No," Wendell agreed aloud, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. He turned back to Corvane, and fifteen years of smiling over poisoned soup came up from somewhere deep and arranged itself on his face like armor. "Master Corvane. Let me make sure I understand your generous offer. You want me to eat, in one sitting, the entire banquet. Every dish the old war ever cooked up. All of it, forever, on the strength of your research, your fragments, and a handshake."

"I want you to *choose* it," Corvane said. "Which is the entire point. The founders caged the thing with force, and force wears out. What I propose requires a willing keeper—one who understands the cost and walks in anyway. Willingness is the missing ingredient in every attempt for four hundred years. It cannot be compelled." He smiled, small and terrible. "Believe me. I have tried compelling it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you run for the mountains with a child who carries the last memory of a dying age, pursued by a woman who burns villages, and we all discover together which of us guessed correctly about the ending." Corvane spread his hands. "I won't stop you. I can't. That's the truth of it, Wendell—I'm not offering you a bargain from strength. I'm a beggar at your table. I've simply brought better silverware than the others."

From the wall, Maren said, "And what does he get? Your willing vessel. Besides the honor of being a jar."

"A generation," Corvane said simply. "Perhaps more. Time for the world to forget the old war well enough to never repeat it. Time for the boy to grow up as a boy, and not a weapon or a relic. Time—" and here his voice did something complicated, and Wendell, connoisseur of voices, heard under it thirty years of failure and hope worn smooth as river stone— "time for one stubborn man's sacrifice to mean what sacrifices are supposed to mean, which is that somebody else gets to live."

Tam's grip on Wendell's hand had gone white-knuckled. Wendell covered it with his other hand and looked at the cold tea, the ugly sign creaking outside, the fire, the thin woman with the thumbs-up, the mushroom-shaped monster in the magistrate's face across the table.

He opened his mouth to answer—

And the door of the Gilded Goose banged open, letting in wind and rain and a rider in travel-stained gray, who crossed the room without a glance at anyone, bent to Corvane's ear, and whispered.

Corvane's face did not change. That was the frightening part. Wendell had spent his career watching faces for the flicker of arsenic behind the smile, and Corvane's face gave him nothing at all—until, at the end, the patient eyes came up and found Wendell's, and there was something in them that Wendell had never seen there before.

Urgency.

"Well," Corvane said quietly. "It seems, Master Nystrom, that the question of what you'll choose has just become somewhat more immediate. Vess has reached the flats. She isn't following your trail."

He rose, buttoning his coat.

"She's following mine."
