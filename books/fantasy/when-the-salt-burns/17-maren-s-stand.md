## 17. Maren's Stand

The storm came down off the mountains an hour before dawn, and with it came Maren Doss, hammering on the door of the shepherd's hut like the world was ending and she'd been asked to hold it shut personally.

"Wendell Nystrom, you open this door or I will open it for you, and I promise you my way is less polite."

Wendell was already awake. He had been awake since the boy sat bolt upright in the dark three hours ago and spoken a single word in that old tongue — a word that had made the salt-crusted windowpane buzz like a trapped fly. He opened the door with the particular weariness of a man who has been poisoned professionally for twenty years and has learned that everything else in life is negotiable.

"You look terrible," he said.

Maren pushed past him into the hut, snow melting off her shoulders, her gray braid coming loose from its coil. She was sixty-one years old and built like the door she'd just threatened, and she carried her father's old warding-axe the way other women of Saltmere carried market baskets.

"You look worse," she said. "And I've been walking all night, so one of us has an excuse." Her eyes went to the boy, curled small under the blanket by the dead hearth, watching her with those too-old eyes. "Hello, little ghost."

The boy didn't smile. He never smiled at strangers. But he tilted his head, the way he did when he was listening to something underneath the words.

"Talk fast," Wendell said. "What's happened?"

"What hasn't." Maren pulled off her gloves finger by finger, which meant she had news and wanted him to suffer through the suspense. It was a game they'd played since they were children, and even now, with riders presumably on the road behind her, she couldn't resist winning it. "Corvane's inquisitors reached the valley last night. Six of them, plus Corvane himself. They're quartered at the Warden's house. Old Ilsette let them in without so much as a cup of tea."

"Ilsette would let the plague in if it signed the right forms."

"That's not the worst of it." Maren's humor dropped away, and Wendell felt his stomach do the thing it did when he tasted something bitter that wasn't supposed to be there. "They've taken Brannic."

* * *

Brannic Hale was nineteen years old, the son of the woman who sold Wendell his bread, and the only person in Saltmere besides Maren who had known where Wendell was hiding and hadn't turned pale about it. He was also, Wendell realized with a cold clarity, exactly the sort of boy who would lie badly under questioning and then apologize for lying.

"They took him this morning," Maren said. "Dragged him out of his mother's kitchen in front of everyone. Corvane stood in the square and said—" she closed her eyes, reciting, because she had a memory like a ledger and knew Wendell would want every word exact—"'The man who shelters the vessel of the old sorcery is guilty of treason against crown and covenant alike. Any who aid him share his guilt. Bring him to me by nightfall, and no one else need burn.'"

"Burn," Wendell repeated. "He said burn."

"In a valley warded against fire-magic, standing next to a fire he lit himself. The man has a sense of theater, I'll give him that."

The boy had gone very still. Wendell crossed the room and crouched beside him, and the child looked up with an expression Wendell had learned to read over these hard weeks: not fear exactly, but recognition. The boy remembered burning. Whatever lived in his memory — the memory of a whole dead age of sorcery — remembered what it was to watch things burn.

"We're not giving you to him," Wendell told him quietly. "I want that understood. Nobody here is giving anybody to anybody."

"He doesn't understand your words half the time," Maren said.

"He understands more than we think." Wendell touched the boy's shoulder. "Don't tell him that, though. He'll get ideas."

The boy made a sound that might, in a child with a lighter inheritance, have been a laugh.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been forming in Wendell's mind for days, and it had all the structural integrity of a wet paper sack. They would go east, over the flats, toward the mountains where the hidden order claimed a sanctum existed — the people with the silver knot sigil who had left messages in dead trees and paid for trust in old coin. The order wanted the boy alive. Corvane wanted him contained. Between the two, Wendell preferred the ones who had, so far, only been mysterious rather than menacing.

But the flats were two days' walk, and the inquisitors had horses, and the boy was seven years old with legs to match.

"So we don't take the flats," Maren said, spreading a scrap of hide on the floor and drawing on it with a stub of charcoal. "Not yet. We go north first, along the ridge, through the old quarry. There's a path the wardens used to use, before my grandmother's time. It comes out above the flats where nobody watches."

"The quarry path collapsed forty years ago."

"Partially collapsed. I walked it in autumn."

"When? Why were you walking it?"

Maren gave him a look. "Because Ilsette asked me to check whether anyone could come at the valley from the north, and I said yes before I thought about how much I hate climbing. The point is, it holds. Barely. Which means horses can't follow, but neither can we move fast, and the boy—"

"I can carry him when the footing's bad," Wendell said. "I've got the shoulders for it. Finally, a professional application."

"And your body?"

It was a fair question and they both knew it. Since the night at the threshold, since the boy's inheritance had begun leaking into the world around him like ink through cloth, Wendell's trained flesh had been doing something no taster's training had ever covered: drinking it in. Every time the boy's power spiked — fear, nightmares, the word he'd spoken at the window — the sickness passed through Wendell instead of into the valley. His hands shook now in the mornings. His food tasted of copper and old smoke. He was, he suspected, dying in a slow and interesting new way, and he had decided not to examine that too closely until after they were safe.

"My body," Wendell said, "is the finest instrument of its kind in the kingdom, and it will perform."

"You're a fool."

"I'm a professional."

"You're a fool with excellent shoulders." Maren rolled up the hide. "Fine. North, through the quarry. But someone has to slow them down at the village, Wendell. If Corvane finds the hut empty by midmorning, he'll have riders on every path before we're halfway up the ridge. Someone has to make him believe we went south across the flats."

"Someone," Wendell repeated carefully.

"I'm sixty-one years old," Maren said, in the tone of a woman announcing her own coronation. "I've buried a husband, outlived three head wardens, and once talked a tax assessor out of levying the chapel. You think I can't stall six inquisitors and their peacock of a leader?"

"It's not the stalling I'm worried about. It's what happens when the stalling stops working."

"Then it's fortunate," said Maren Doss, "that I stopped caring what happens to me somewhere around the second head warden."

* * *

They argued for another hour, which is what family does, though neither of them would have used the word. In the end they compromised the way they always had: Wendell lost.

He packed while Maren watched the road through the gap in the shutter. Bread, salt-pork, the boy's one possession — a smooth gray stone he clutched in sleep, which hummed faintly if you held it to your ear, like a seashell full of weather. Wendell's own kit was smaller: a knife, a phial of broadleaf antidote, and a folded page torn from Ilsette's ledger, given to him weeks ago, covered in her cramped hand. Directions, she'd said. And an apology, which he still hadn't read past the first line.

"Maren." He paused at the door. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does. Corvane can't prove anything against you. You're a widow with a bad hip and a reputation for opinions. Play the fool. You'll find it restful."

Maren took his face in both her rough hands and pressed her forehead to his, an old gesture from a childhood spent daring each other to swim in the cold river. "You come back," she said. "That's the whole speech. Come back, and bring the little ghost, and we'll all grow old and tiresome together in a valley that finally knows what it owes you."

Then she kissed his brow, shoved him out the door into the snow, and went to fight her war.

* * *

The ridge path was everything Wendell remembered and worse. Ice glazed the stone. The wind came off the peaks with teeth. Twice the boy stumbled and twice Wendell caught him, and by the third mile he simply carried him, the child riding his back with his arms around Wendell's neck and his humming stone pressed between them like a shared heartbeat.

Somewhere below and behind, muffled by distance, Wendell heard a sound that did not belong to any storm: a bell. The chapel bell, rung in alarm — or rung, perhaps, by a woman who wanted six inquisitors and their peacock looking the wrong way.

"She's doing it right now," he murmured. "She's standing in the square telling them some magnificent lie."

The boy stirred against his shoulder. Then, softly, in a voice like dust settling, he spoke — not the dead language this time, but Saltmere words, plain and small, words Wendell had taught him himself over weeks of patient pointing and naming.

"Old. Lady. Brave."

Wendell laughed, and it cracked something loose in his chest. "Yes," he said. "The oldest, bravest lady in the valley. Don't tell her I said so. She'll be insufferable."

They climbed.

* * *

Below them, in the square of Saltmere, Maren Doss stood in the falling snow with her arms crossed and gave the performance of her life.

Corvane was taller than she'd expected, and younger, with the polished patience of a man who had never once been interrupted successfully. He listened to her account of the southerly footprints — invented, detailed, supported by her own aching feet — with his head slightly tilted, like a bird considering whether a worm was worth the effort.

"You saw them yourself, mistress...?"

"Doss. Widow Doss, if we're being formal, which you clearly enjoy. Yes. Before dawn, crossing the flats, heading south. A big man carrying a bundle. I assumed smugglers. We get smugglers. It's the excitement of our year."

"And you did not think to report this earlier."

"I'm reporting it now. I walked four hours in the snow to report it, which my hip would like noted in whatever ledger you people keep."

One of the inquisitors leaned close to Corvane and murmured. Corvane's gaze drifted, unhurried, to the axe on Maren's back.

"A warding-axe," he said. "Old pattern. Warden's issue."

"My father's. He kept the boundary stones. It's decorative now. Like most things in this valley." She smiled with all the warmth of a frozen pond. "Would you like to see my late husband's fishing rod as well? Also decorative."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvane's mouth. Almost respect. Almost.

Behind him, held between two soldiers, Brannic lifted his head. His lip was split and his eyes were red-rimmed, and when they found Maren's face he started to speak — started to say her name, or say sorry, or say something that would undo everything—

Maren looked at him the way she had once looked at Wendell across a freezing river, sixty years and one lifetime ago: *be brave, be quiet, I have this.* Brannic closed his mouth.

"Well," Maren said briskly, before the silence could turn dangerous. "Is there anything else? Only there's bread rising at home, and unlike some people, I respect a schedule."

Corvane studied her a moment longer. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he did not brush it off, as if weather itself had agreed to wait for him.

"No," he said at last. "Thank you, Widow Doss. You've been most helpful." He turned to his men. "South. To the flats. Ride ahead to the ferry crossings and send word back."

As the soldiers moved, he paused beside Maren, and said, quietly, for her alone:

"The boy's stone hums, they tell me. When it's near its owner. An interesting detail, from a thorough man." He smiled, thin and courteous as a blade laid flat. "Your bread, Widow Doss. Do see it doesn't burn."

He walked away. Maren stood in the square with her heart going like a drum and her face set in mild, opinionated boredom, and waited until he was out of sight before she allowed herself one long breath.

Then she went home, took her father's axe down from the wall, and began, with great care, to plan how a sixty-one-year-old widow could reach the north ridge before nightfall.

* * *

High on the quarry path, as the light went long and gold, the boy suddenly stiffened against Wendell's back and gripped his collar.

Wendell felt it too. Not heard — felt. A wrongness in the air, a pressure behind the ears, the taste of hot metal at the back of his throat. The same taste that preceded every surge of the boy's inheritance.

Except the boy wasn't afraid. The boy was staring north, up-slope, toward the rocks above the path, where the evening shadow lay deepest.

From the shadow, a figure stepped out. Hooded, robed in travel-gray, and on the breast of the robe, catching the last light: a small silver knot.

The figure raised one hand — palm out, empty, peaceful — and spoke in a voice worn smooth by distance and wind.

"Taster. We've been watching you climb for an hour. You're slower than we hoped, and braver than we feared." A pause, and then, gently: "We should talk before the others following you arrive. Because they are following you. And they aren't ours."
