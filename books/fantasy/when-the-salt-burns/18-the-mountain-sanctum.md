## 18. The Mountain Sanctum

The boy slept through the last three miles of the climb, which Wendell considered a small miracle and a large mercy. His own legs had stopped negotiating with him somewhere below the snow line and had simply begun obeying orders out of fear. Each step was a bargain his knees struck without his consent: *one more, and then we'll consider mutiny.*

Ilsette walked ahead of him on the narrow path, sure-footed as a goat and half as sympathetic. She had not spoken much since Maren's stand at the river ford — since Maren had turned to face the inquisitors' riders with a quarterstaff and an expression Wendell would carry for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be.

"She bought us the afternoon," Ilsette said now, as if reading his thoughts, which he suspected she did regularly and without apology. "Maren doesn't lose fights. She just decides how long they last."

"And if she decided wrong?"

"Then we'll know by the sound." She gestured ahead with her chin.

Wendell listened. Wind. Rock shifting under ice, somewhere far off. And beneath it — so faint he might have invented it — a low hum, like a wet finger circling the rim of a very large glass.

"What is that?"

"Home," Ilsette said. "Or the nearest thing I've had to one in thirty years."

* * *

The sanctum did not announce itself. That was the first thing Wendell noticed, and the thing he would remember longest. After Saltmere — where every building stood in plain view of every other building, where privacy was a rumor and a locked door was considered a personality flaw — the hidden order's sanctuary was an act of deliberate forgetting. The path ended at a wall of stone that looked exactly like every other wall of stone, except that Ilsette pressed her palm into a hollow worn smooth by generations of hands and the wall sighed open like an old man settling into a chair.

Inside, the passage sloped downward and then up, lit by veins of something pale blue running through the rock itself. Not torches. Not lamps. The stone glowed the way deep water glows, patiently, from nowhere in particular.

"The founders built it into a dead ward-line," Ilsette explained, her voice dropping into the hushed register people use in churches and libraries and other places where the walls are listening. "When the old sorcery was broken and buried, the mountains kept a residue. Harmless, mostly. Enough to light a corridor."

"Harmless, mostly," Wendell repeated. "You people say that a lot."

"We're optimists with experience."

The passage opened, all at once, into a cavern so vast that Wendell's breath actually left him — a real, physical event, an evacuation of the lungs. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some impossible organ. Terraces had been cut into the rock in spirals, and on them grew gardens: pale ferns, silver-leafed herbs he recognized from his taster's training and dozens he didn't, mushrooms cultivated in glowing racks, a whole orchard of small hard fruit trees growing sideways out of terraces, their branches trained along wires. Water fell somewhere unseen, a sound like quiet applause.

And people. Perhaps two hundred of them, moving among the terraces, hauling water, sorting herbs, teaching children — actual children, a dozen or more, chasing each other between garden beds until an older woman caught two of them by the collars without looking up from her work.

The boy woke in Wendell's arms.

He went rigid the way he always did around strangers, pressing his face into Wendell's shoulder — but then he lifted his head, and his eyes went wide, and he made a sound. Not a word. Not yet. A sound like a bell struck once, low and wondering, aimed at the glowing stone.

"He knows this place," Wendell said.

"No," Ilsette said quietly. "This place knows him. There's a difference, and it matters."

* * *

They were given a room — a real room, with a door that closed, which nearly moved Wendell to tears — carved into the third terrace. Two cots, a shelf, a basin fed by a pipe of mountain water so cold it hurt pleasantly. A window, if you could call it that: an opening onto the cavern, shuttered with a woven screen.

The boy inspected everything twice, touched nothing until Wendell nodded, then climbed onto the cot and sat cross-legged, watching the door as though expecting the world to apologize its way back in.

"You can sleep," Wendell told him. "That's generally what rooms are for."

The boy shook his head once, firmly, the way he rejected overcooked fish. Then he pointed at Wendell. Pointed at the floor beside the cot.

"I'm sleeping there anyway," Wendell said, and spread his cloak.

It was true. He had no intention of sleeping anywhere the boy wasn't. This had started as duty — the child was, functionally, the most dangerous object Wendell had ever been asked to guard, more dangerous than the night the ambassador's soup had been laced with greyvein extract and Wendell had drunk it anyway because refusing would have meant questions, and questions meant heads rolling, possibly literally. But somewhere across the salt flats and the burned valley and the long cold climb, duty had quietly changed professions. It wasn't guarding anymore. He couldn't have said when. These things never announce themselves; they arrive the way salt arrives in bread, invisibly, and you only notice when you taste it.

He tasted it now, watching the boy finally lie down, watching his eyes fight sleep and lose in slow increments.

*Footsteps.* Wendell was upright before he'd decided to be, which was the poison-taster's reflex, honed by years of meals that might be his last.

"It's only me." Ilsette stood in the doorway holding a tray: bread still warm, cheese, dried fruit, and a clay cup steaming with something that smelled like pine and honey. "Food first. Questions later. That's house law, and yes, before you ask, the food is tested. Everyone here has been poisoned at least once. It builds character and also immunity, and we've found character is easier to acquire chemically."

Wendell took the cup and drank. It was good. It was, in fact, the best thing he'd tasted in weeks, and he said so, and Ilsette looked absurdly pleased, the way cooks do.

"There's someone who wants to meet you," she said. "Not tonight. Tomorrow, when you've slept. But I'll warn you now because you strike me as a man who hates surprises: her name is Mother Vess, and she is going to look at the boy the way a jeweler looks at a stone she's been searching forty years for. Don't let it frighten you. Or do. Fear's honest. It's hope I don't trust."

"That's a terrible philosophy for someone with a sanctuary full of children."

"My philosophy has kept me alive. My heart does what it likes regardless." She set down the tray. "Sleep, taster. You're safe here. Mostly."

"*Mostly,*" Wendell said again, but she was already gone, and he found, to his genuine surprise, that he slept.

* * *

Mother Vess was smaller than Wendell expected, and older, and moved with the particular economy of someone who has stopped wasting motion on anything that isn't essential. Her hair was white as the peaks above them, cropped short, and her hands were a working gardener's hands, earth in the creases no matter how recently she'd washed. She received them in a round chamber at the heart of the cavern, beneath the tallest stalactite, where the hum Wendell had heard on the path was loudest — close enough now that he could feel it faintly in his teeth.

She did not look at Wendell at all. She knelt, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, in front of the boy.

"Little warden," she said.

The word came out in the dead tongue. The old language. The language the boy spoke when he spoke at all, which was rarely, and which had made scholars in Saltmere go pale and make signs against evil.

The boy stared at her. Then — carefully, the way he did everything, as if words were fragile dishes he'd been trusted to carry — he answered.

Wendell understood none of it. But he watched the exchange the way he watched everything about the boy: closely, hungrily, filing away each gesture. The boy spoke four syllables. Mother Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet.

"Yes," she said in the common tongue. "Yes, little one. We remember too. That is rather the entire point of us."

She rose, with difficulty, and finally turned her attention to Wendell, looking him up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman buying a horse.

"So. The taster." She smiled, and it transformed her face from formidable to grandmotherly in a single stroke, and Wendell distrusted the transformation immediately and completely. "Ilsette tells me you drank your way through the crown's kitchens for nine years. Is it true you once identified hemlock in a honey cake by smell alone?"

"It was marzipan," Wendell said. "Hemlock smells like mouse nests. Marzipan was covering it badly. Any decent taster would have caught it."

"Modesty. How refreshing. Everyone else in this mountain brags." She gestured for them to sit on benches worn smooth by decades of sitting. "You want to know why the order exists. Ask."

"I—" Wendell had prepared, overnight, a list of questions, ordered by priority. All of them evaporated. "Why does everyone keep calling him *warden*? He's five. Six. He's a child who nearly drowned and lost his voice doing it, and everyone keeps treating him like—"

"Like what he is?" Mother Vess's gentleness didn't waver, but something underneath it did, something vast shifting its weight. "Child, do you know what the wards of Saltmere actually are? Not the stones. Not the rites your village performs every equinox. Do you know what they truly are?"

"A prison," Wendell said. "For the old sorcery."

"A prison needs a jailer. The founders could not watch forever; they were mortal, and the sorcery was not. So they did what desperate clever people do." She folded her hands. "They made the prison watch itself. The wards of Saltmere are not walls, taster. They are a memory. A living memory, seeded into the land, that remembers what was bound and holds the binding closed. And memories, unlike sorcerers, cannot defend themselves against a man with a chisel."

"Corvane."

"Corvane." She said the name without heat, which frightened Wendell more than shouting would have. "The Inquisitor-General believes the old power is a weapon the kingdom failed to collect. He is wrong, but he is wrong the way an avalanche is wrong — massively, and with momentum. He has spent eleven years finding the ward-stones and cracking them. Your valley's protection is nearly spent. And when it fails entirely, the binding fails with it, because the memory will have nowhere to live."

The hum filled the pause. The boy had climbed onto the bench beside Wendell and was watching Mother Vess with an intensity that made the hair rise on Wendell's neck.

"Except here," Wendell said slowly. Understanding arrived like cold water. "Except in the boy."

"The last seed of that memory," Mother Vess agreed. "Born when the valley's wards first cracked — born *of* the crack, if the oldest texts are right. The land shed its memory the way a tree sheds a limb, to save the trunk. And it landed in a child, as such things always do. The order has waited two hundred years for a warden to come home." She reached out, and the boy — unbidden, unasked — put his small hand in hers. "We can teach him to carry it safely. To hold it without being held. It will take years, and he may never speak your tongue, and there will be days you will curse every stone in this mountain. But he will live. And so will everything the world forgot on purpose."

Wendell's chest was doing something complicated. "And the cost?"

"Ah." Mother Vess's eyes sharpened. "There it is. The taster's question. What's the poison in the meal?" She laughed softly. "The cost is time, taster. Years of it. And Corvane is eleven years ahead of you, and riding."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day being absorbed into the life of the mountain, which happened with the gentle inevitability of sinking into warm water. Someone assigned Wendell to the kitchen — "you'll be useless at everything else for a week, so start where you're useful" — and he discovered, chopping pale mountain onions beside a cheerful one-armed former soldier named Brakka, that his hands remembered how to work even when his mind was elsewhere.

The boy was taken to the nursery terrace, and went willingly — *willingly*, which stung more than Wendell expected it to — drawn by the other children and by the strange gravity of the place. Wendell peeked in on him twice. Both times the boy was sitting in the middle of a circle of children, all of them silent, all of them listening to the humming stone, and both times Wendell's throat tightened for reasons he chose not to examine.

At evening meal — hundreds of people eating together at long tables, loud, laughing, passing dishes hand over hand, a chaos that would have given any royal steward an apoplexy — Ilsette slid onto the bench beside him.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"You've been composing objections all day. You get a line between your eyebrows, right *there*, when you're arguing with someone in your head. You've had it since breakfast."

"I don't have lines. I'm twenty-nine." He put down his spoon. "It's too easy, Ilsette. That's my objection. Nine years of the crown's kitchens taught me that nothing offered freely is free. They're kind, they're good, they feed you well, and underneath it all they need the boy the same way Corvane needs him. Just with better manners."

"Manners matter," Ilsette said. "Ask anyone who's been tortured." She stole a piece of his bread, which seemed to be allowed here. "But you're not wrong. Vess will tell you herself, eventually — the order has dreamed of a warden for two centuries. Dreams make people grabby. Watch her hands when she talks about him. Gardeners' hands. They plant things where they want them."

"And yours?"

Ilsette looked out at the hall, at the noise and the steam and the children running between tables. "Mine are tired," she said. "I just want the boy to grow up somewhere the ground isn't trying to eat him. Whether that's here or somewhere else is between him and whatever's in his head."

From the children's table, a clear bell-tone rang out. The boy was standing on his bench, pointing — not at Wendell, not at Ilsette, but up, past the stalactites, toward the mountain's crown, where no one had any business being.

The hall quieted section by section, the silence spreading outward from the boy like rings in water, until two hundred people sat listening to nothing.

Then Wendell heard it too. Under the hum of the dead ward-line, faint and rhythmic and wrong: hoofbeats. Many horses. High on the ridge, where the snow lived, where no honest traveler rode at night.

Mother Vess rose from the high table. Around the hall, two hundred people began, calmly and without panic, to put out the lights.

"Taster," she said, finding him in the darkening crowd. "How attached are you to your sleep?"

Wendell looked at the boy, who had found his way to Wendell's side without seeming to cross the distance, whose small hand had closed around two of Wendell's fingers with the grip of someone who had learned, recently and permanently, that the world takes things away.

"Not very," Wendell said.
