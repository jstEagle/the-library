## 19. Corvane at the Gate

The sanctum smelled of beeswax and old paper, which Wendell decided was the most reassuring thing he'd encountered in weeks. Poison, he understood. Ink, he understood. A fortress full of quiet people who filed things alphabetically — that, he could live inside of.

The boy, whose name they had finally coaxed out of him in fragments — *Ash*, or something so close to *Ash* that Wendell had stopped correcting it — sat cross-legged on a bench by the eastern window, watching the clouds come down the mountain like slow white sheep. He had not spoken since they'd arrived three days ago. Not in his old tongue, not in anything. But he had eaten everything set before him, which Wendell took as a good sign. In his experience, an appetite was the soul's way of saying it intended to stay.

"You're staring at him again," said Maren.

"I'm supervising," Wendell said. "There's a difference."

"He's eating bread."

"Someone has to make sure it's not poisoned."

"It's my bread. I baked it this morning."

"Exactly the sort of person a clever poisoner would impersonate."

Maren threw a dish towel at his head. He caught it without looking up, which was the single most impressive thing Wendell Nystrom had done all week, and he had crossed the salt flats carrying a child while being shot at.

* * *

Ilsette found them in the scriptorium an hour later. She moved through the sanctum's halls the way she moved everywhere now — quickly, quietly, with her hood down and her chin up, as if daring the stones to remember what she used to be. The wardens had accepted her back with the wary tenderness one shows a dog that has run away twice. They fed her. They did not entirely trust her. Wendell thought that was fair. He wasn't sure he trusted her either, but he had learned on the flats that distrust and love are not mutually exclusive; sometimes they share a bed and complain about the cold.

"The sentries signal," she said. "Riders. Nine of them, coming up the switchback road. Slowly."

"Slowly is good," Maren said. "Slowly means they want to talk."

"Slowly also means," Ilsette said, "that they're confident enough not to hurry. Which is worse."

Wendell set down the ledger he'd been pretending to read. "Is it him?"

"There's a black banner with a silver key." Ilsette's jaw tightened. "It's him."

The boy looked up from the window. He couldn't have understood the words, but he understood tone the way animals do, and his small body went still in the particular way of someone deciding where to hide. Wendell crossed the room and sat down beside him without ceremony.

"Nobody's taking you anywhere," he said. "I've tasted worse things than Corvane, and I'm still here. That's practically a professional guarantee."

The boy leaned, very slightly, against his shoulder. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

* * *

They met Corvane at the gate because there was nowhere else sensible to meet him. The sanctum's front approach was a courtyard of pale stone ringed by arches, and beyond the wall the mountain dropped away in green steps toward the valley they had all, in one way or another, given up. The nine riders dismounted at the gatehouse and only one man walked through.

Inquisitor Corvane had always reminded Wendell of a well-made knife: nothing decorative about him, everything purposeful, and the sort of balance you only appreciated when he was pointed at you. He wore grey travel clothes instead of his inquisitorial blacks, which was its own kind of message. *I am not here officially,* the clothes said. *Officially, I do not exist.*

"Warden-Commander Vess," he said, inclining his head to the tall woman who had come out to meet them. Then, to Maren: "Sergeant. You look well. Desertion suits you."

"I didn't desert," Maren said. "I transferred."

"To an order that technically doesn't exist."

"Everything technical doesn't exist until it wins," Maren said. "Then it's called history."

Corvane's mouth did something that on a warmer face might have been a smile. His gaze moved past them all and settled — Wendell felt it settle, the way weather settles — on the boy half-hidden behind Wendell's leg.

"So it's true," Corvane said softly. "He speaks the First Tongue."

"He doesn't speak much of anything lately," Wendell said. "You've frightened him. Congratulations. That's generally considered a professional accomplishment among your people."

"And you must be the taster." Corvane looked at him with genuine, unhurried interest, the way a man looks at a strange coin. "Nystrom. The one who drank from the cracked ward-cistern and lived. The one who walked out of Saltmere with a mute child and no horse and somehow arrived here ahead of every rider we sent. I've read your file, Master Nystrom. It's very short. Mostly it says *nothing remarkable*. I find that file increasingly funny."

"I've never found it funny," Wendell said. "It got me free meals at court for eleven years, though. 'Nothing remarkable' is an excellent disguise. Nobody poisons the unremarkable man first."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — the briefest acknowledgment of a fellow professional — and then it was gone, filed away, alphabetized.

"I haven't come for the boy," Corvane said.

Everyone looked at him.

"That's a lie," said Ilsette.

"It's a negotiation position," Corvane corrected. "There's a difference. Ask any merchant." He folded his hands behind his back and addressed the courtyard at large, the arches, the mountains, the sky. "You believe I want to cut the memory out of him. Burn it, bottle it, put it in a vault under the palace with the other things the crown pretends it doesn't have. That was true once. It may still be true of the Council. It is no longer true of me."

"Why should we believe that?" Maren asked.

"Because three days ago, inquisitors burned a village called Harrow's Bend looking for you. All of it. Every roof, every granary, every person who gave you so much as a cup of water." Corvane said it flatly, the way a surgeon describes a wound. "I signed none of those orders. I could not stop them. And I have spent those three days doing arithmetic." He turned back, and for just a moment the knife of him showed something underneath — weariness, maybe, or fear, which in a man like Corvane was more frightening than either blade or fire. "Do you know what the wards of Saltmere were, Master Nystrom? Really?"

"A prison," Wendell said. "For sorcery. For the old war's leftovers."

"A dam," Corvane said. "And dams are built by people who expect someone else's grandchildren to maintain them. The valley's wards were keyed to a bloodline of wardens. That bloodline thinned. It married out, died out, forgot itself. Your village elders kept the rites the way people keep a grandmother's recipe — faithfully, and without understanding a word of it. When the last true warden died, forty years ago, the wards began to fail. Slowly. The way a tooth rots." He paused. "You've seen the salt crack. You know I'm right."

No one answered. The wind came over the wall and stirred the banners of nobody.

"The child is not a prisoner's memory," Corvane went on, quieter now. "He's the last living key. Whatever sleeps beneath Saltmere — whatever the old war buried and the valley was built on top of — it is waking, because the lock above it is rusting open. The Council wants to seize the key. I want—" he stopped himself, visibly recalibrated, and Wendell watched a man decide, in real time, to tell the truth for perhaps the first time in twenty years, "—I want to understand what the door opens before someone kicks it in."

"And if understanding fails?" Ilsette asked. Her voice was very steady. "If your arithmetic comes out wrong? What does the crown do with a key it can't use?"

"Then the crown does what crowns do," Corvane said simply. "It breaks the key and hopes the door stays shut out of spite." He looked at each of them in turn, and last and longest at the boy. "I am offering you the chance to make sure it never comes to that. Come down with me. Bring him. I will give you standing, protection, access to the archives, everything the hidden order hoards behind these walls—"

"We're not hiding," Maren said hotly. "We're keeping."

"You're keeping *paper*, Sergeant. Paper burns beautifully. I've burned a great deal of it myself." Corvane spread his hands. "I offer you the world, such as it is. Ugly, heavy, worth saving. Or keep your sanctuary and your beeswax, and wait for the Council's next nine riders to be ninety."

The silence afterward had weight. Somewhere below, a hawk rode the thermals off the crags, patient as arithmetic.

Wendell crouched down beside the boy. "Well?" he asked gently, in the old tongue — the few words of it he'd laboriously stolen from Ilsette's lessons, pronounced like a man walking on river ice. "*What do you hear?*"

The boy studied Corvane with those too-old eyes. Then he opened his mouth and spoke — his first words in days, and the whole courtyard flinched at the sound of them, because the First Tongue did not sound like speech so much as like the memory of speech, like wind through a house where everyone had died.

"*He is afraid,*" the boy said. "*Like us.*"

Corvane went very still. Whatever he had prepared for — refusal, defiance, a knife — it had not been sympathy from a six-year-old in a dead language.

"He says you're afraid," Wendell translated, straightening up. "Like us." He let that sit for a moment, then added, with the mildness he'd once used to inform a king his soup was compromised: "In my profession, sir, when two dishes smell wrong in the same kitchen, you don't blame the spices. You check the cellar."

Corvane looked at him for a long moment. "Meaning?"

"Meaning somebody told the Council where we were. Somebody who knew about Harrow's Bend before the ash was cold. Meaning there's a third player at this table, and I don't think it's wearing your colors or ours." Wendell nodded toward the road below, where the nine riders waited with the stillness of men under orders. "So before anyone decides anything — who else knew the child existed, Inquisitor? Who else is doing arithmetic?"

For the first time since he'd ridden through the gate, Corvane looked genuinely unsettled. And far down the switchback, faint on the wind, came a sound that made the boy grab Wendell's hand and say, in the tongue older than crowns:

"*They are already climbing.*"

Not the riders. Something else. Off the road, up the cliff-face itself, where no horses could go.
