## 20. When the Salt Burns

The ward-stones were singing.

Wendell had never heard them before—nobody in living memory had—but there was no mistaking it. A high, thin keening rose from the circle of grey monoliths that ringed the valley like the teeth of some enormous, patient jaw. It came up through the soles of his boots and settled behind his eyes, and it sounded, he thought absurdly, like a kettle left too long on the fire.

"They're frightened," the boy said. Not in words, exactly. The boy hadn't spoken in words since the night at the threshold, but his meaning arrived in Wendell's head anyway, clear as a bell struck in an empty room. *The stones are frightened.*

"Stones can't be frightened," Wendell said, mostly because saying foolish things out loud helped him think.

*These can.* The boy's hand was wrapped in Wendell's, small and cold and trembling. Behind them, up the switchback trail, Maren stood at the mouth of the mountain sanctum with her arms crossed, wearing the particular expression of a woman who has argued every argument she has and lost all of them on purpose.

"You don't have to do it this way," she called down. "There's still the third option."

"The third option being everyone dies," Wendell said.

"The third option being we *try*, you stubborn, self-marinating man."

"I appreciate the concern. Truly. It's the nicest thing anyone's said to me since Ilsette told me I smelled of arsenic and good intentions." He looked back at her, and for a moment he let himself memorize her—the wind pulling at her braid, the old scar on her jaw, the way she stood like a door nobody could close. "But the wards are cracking, Maren. You said it yourself. Whatever's imprisoned under this valley is waking up, and Corvane is standing at the gate with a writ and forty soldiers, and the boy—" he squeezed the small cold hand— "the boy is carrying more than any child should carry through one lifetime. Somebody has to take it off him. And I am, as previously established, extremely difficult to poison."

Maren's jaw worked. "You'll lose yourself. You understand that. Everything the valley is, everything it remembers—it'll pour through you, and you'll pour back into it."

"Yes," Wendell said. "That's rather the point."

*You'll forget,* the boy said, and there was grief in it, old grief, the kind no child should own. *You'll forget your name. Your mother's kitchen. The taste of honey.*

"The taste of honey," Wendell repeated, and smiled despite himself. "You know what my whole life has been? Tasting things so other people don't die from them. Bitterness, rot, nightshade, worse. I have spent thirty years making sure the flavor never reached anyone else." He turned back toward the valley, toward the thin ribbon of the river, the rooftops of Saltmere huddled under their chimney smoke, the ward-stones keening their frightened song. "One last dish. Let me serve it properly."

He knelt so he was eye level with the boy. Up close, the child's eyes were wrong again—that deep amber, old as peat, old as the war the histories pretended had ended. But beneath the ancient gold there was still a boy in there. A boy who liked bread crusts and hated boots and had once, memorably, tried to feed a beetle to a chicken just to see what would happen.

"Here's how it goes," Wendell said. "I'm going to hold out my hands. You're going to give me what you've been carrying. All of it. Every memory, every word of the dead tongue, every scrap of sorcery they locked under our valley before there were kings. And I'm going to swallow it, because swallowing terrible things is the one thing I do better than anyone alive."

*It will hurt.*

"Oh, almost certainly."

*You might not survive.*

"Well." He scratched his chin. "Inquisitors are at the gate, an elder god is under the floorboards, and the woman I love is about to watch me do something unforgivably dramatic. Honestly, the odds of surviving the day weren't great regardless. At least this way I choose the manner of my ruin. A taster's privilege."

The boy made a sound then—not language, not quite—a small cracked noise, and threw himself against Wendell's chest, and held on with both arms as though he could anchor him to the world by stubbornness alone. Wendell held him back and did not say that it wasn't working, and did not say goodbye, because goodbyes were for people who had time.

"Maren!" he shouted over the boy's shoulder. "When the stones go quiet—if they go quiet—get everyone down into the sanctum and seal it. Corvane won't matter anymore. Nothing he wants will be here."

"And you?"

"I'll be everywhere," Wendell said. "Or nowhere. Historically those have been the same thing in this valley."

She laughed, wetly, and saluted him with two fingers, which was the most affection Maren had ever displayed in public, and Wendell decided he could die content on that alone.

He stood. He walked to the edge of the overlook where the first ward-stone thrust up out of the earth, its surface crawling with hairline cracks that glowed faintly, the way a dying coal glows. He set the boy down behind him, gently, like setting down something breakable.

"All right," he said quietly, to the stone, to the valley, to whatever was listening underneath it. "Kitchen's open."

He spread his arms. And the boy, understanding at last what was being asked—not taken, *asked*—pressed both small palms against Wendell's back, between the shoulder blades, right over the heart.

It began, as all the worst things in Wendell's life had begun, with a taste.

* * *

Salt first. That was fitting. It flooded his tongue in a wave so dense it was nearly solid, the taste of the flats, of tears, of preservation—of everything the valley had ever been built to keep. Then beneath it, older: iron, ash, something green and growing and *hungry*, the taste of the sorcery itself, the living memory that predated crowns, that remembered when the mountains were young and men had learned to bargain with things that lived under the roots of the world.

It poured into him and Wendell poured back, and for a long moment—for an hour, for a heartbeat; time went strange—he was not a man standing on a hillside. He was the valley.

He felt the wards as his own skin: ten thousand years of vigilance braided into stone and salt and blood-oath. He felt the thing below, vast and patient and *wounded*, turning over in its sleep like a giant disturbed by a splinter, and he understood suddenly why the wards were failing—not because they were weak, but because the prisoner had finally learned patience to match theirs.

And he understood what he had to do, the way you understand a recipe halfway through tasting the broth.

A ward was a promise. A promise needed a promiser. For generations the stones had kept the oath alone, mindless, faithful, fraying. But a living body—a body trained across thirty years to hold what would kill anything else, a body that had metabolized enough poison to stock an assassin's guild—could take the promise into himself. Could become the ward. Flesh instead of stone. Memory instead of magic.

The cost, of course, was memory. The valley would need somewhere to put itself while it worked, and he was the only vessel on offer.

*Take it,* he thought, or perhaps said aloud, or perhaps the valley simply heard him the way the valley now heard everything inside him. *All of it. My name, my kitchen, my mother's voice, the smell of rain on the flats, the color of Maren's eyes—I'm not using any of it where I'm going. Take the boy's burden too. Take the old tongue. Take the hunger under the hill and hold it, hold it, HOLD IT—*

The ward-stones shattered.

Not violently—they crumbled, all around the valley rim at once, gently, like sandcastles surrendering to the tide—and the light that had lived inside them for uncounted centuries came home to him. Wendell felt it arrive in his chest like a swallowed sun, and he heard himself make a sound that was not a scream, though it wanted very much to be, and he held on.

Below, at the valley gate, Corvane's soldiers saw the monoliths fall and cheered, believing the wards broken, believing their writ had triumphed. They did not see that nothing escaped. They did not see that the barrier remained—thinner now, human now, woven out of one stubborn taster's bones and bitterness and love—but remained.

They did not see the boy collapse weeping into Maren's arms, empty at last, only a child, only hungry, only asking for bread.

They did not see Wendell Nystrom stand on the ridge with his arms wide and his eyes gone the amber of deep old peat, smiling, already forgetting why he was smiling.

* * *

The fire started at dusk.

Later—much later, in the histories written by people who hadn't been there—it would be called a tragedy. A spark from Corvane's siege fires, the chroniclers would say, catching the dry grass of the flats, and the poor village of Saltmere consumed in a single terrible night. There would be a memorial. There might even be a plaque.

Maren, watching from the high trail with a sleeping child on her hip, knew better. She had seen Wendell's last coherent hour. She had watched him turn away from the ridge, walk down into the valley he was becoming, and pause once outside Ilsette's old cottage—the cottage where he'd grown up, where a kind apothecary widow had fed a poisoned orphan spoonfuls of tolerance one careful dose at a time—and she had seen him touch the doorframe the way a man touches a grave.

Then he had looked at the dry summer grass, and at the smoke rising from Corvane's camp, and something behind his fading eyes had arranged itself with a cook's practicality.

*A valley that holds a god shouldn't hold people,* he'd said—or the valley had said it through him, it was getting hard to tell. *Best the maps call it cursed. Best nobody comes looking. Fire writes the cleanest page.*

So he had walked the perimeter, and where he walked, the grass had caught, obediently, terribly, and by full dark the valley of Saltmere was a bowl of flame, and the wardens of the hidden order guided three hundred villagers up secret trails to the mountain sanctum while the crown's soldiers fled the blaze and reported, later, that nothing survived.

Nothing survived. That was the official finding, and Maren let them keep it.

She stood until the fires burned down to embers, until the shape of the village was only a shape in blackness, until the child on her hip stirred and murmured a word in the dead tongue—but a *small* word now, a child's word, a word for *warm* or maybe for *home*—and the weight of the old sorcery did not stir beneath the earth, because it was held. Somewhere in the ash, woven into salt and stone and the stubborn residue of one ordinary extraordinary man, it was held.

"Wendell," she said, to the embers, because someone should say it. "You absolute idiot. You beautiful, impossible idiot."

The embers did not answer. But far down in the burning heart of the valley, in the place where the deepest ward-root anchored into bedrock, something that used to be a man tasted the smoke and found it familiar—found, without knowing why, that he knew exactly how long it would burn, and exactly when the rains would come, and exactly how much salt the ground would need to sleep safe for another hundred years.

He did not remember his name. He did not remember honey.

But he kept the watch. One dish at a time.

And far to the north, in a capital that believed its war was over, a rider in inquisitor's black dismounted before the palace gates carrying a sealed report and a fragment of shattered ward-stone that, though every expert swore it was inert, was still—faintly, impossibly—warm.
