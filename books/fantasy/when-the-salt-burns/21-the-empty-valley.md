## 21. The Empty Valley

The first thing Wendell Nystrom learned about having no memory was that the body remembered everything.

His hands knew how to test water for bitterness before his mouth ever touched it. His feet knew which flagstones in Saltmere's market square wobbled in the rain, and stepped around them without being asked. His tongue—oh, his tongue remained a scholar even when the rest of him had been emptied out like a purse turned upside down on a table. It catalogued flavors with the same patient precision it always had: the iron tang of the well at the valley's north end, the faint sweetness that meant the baker had used honey instead of sugar again and hoped nobody would notice.

The trouble was that knowing a thing and remembering a thing were two different animals entirely. He could stand in front of his own cottage and feel absolutely nothing, the way one feels nothing in front of a stranger's door. He could look at the scar on his left forearm—a long white seam from wrist to elbow—and have no story for it at all.

"You're staring at your own house," Maren said beside him. "Again."

"Am I?"

"You've been staring at it since we came down off the ridge. The shutters are green. There's a cat in your window. It's yours. Its name is Turnip."

Wendell considered this. "Why would I name a cat Turnip?"

"Because you thought it was funny." Maren shifted Ilsette higher on her hip—the boy was nearly too big to be carried now, all knees and elbows, but he still went limp and trusting against her shoulder when the world got to be too much, which lately was often. "You told me once you'd never met an animal less suited to grandeur than a turnip, and you wanted to give something in this world a name it couldn't possibly live up to."

"That sounds like me."

"It is exactly you. That's rather the point." She set the boy down, and he stood there in the lane, looking up at the cottage with those old, old eyes that held nothing of childhood except the fact of being small. "Go on, then. Open the door. It won't bite. Probably."

The latch knew his hand. That was the strangest part—not the cat winding between his ankles, not the cold hearth, not the dust—but the latch, which gave under his palm at precisely the angle it wanted to be pressed, as if the house itself had decided to remember him so he didn't have to.

Inside, everything waited for him like a room of polite strangers. A chair by the window. A shelf of jars, each labeled in a hand he recognized without recognizing: *foxglove, prepared spring*. A kettle hung over the dead fire, and his body reached for it before his mind had finished asking why.

Turnip jumped onto the table and knocked over a cup, which shattered, because cats keep their promises.

"Well," Maren said from the doorway, "at least some things survive any catastrophe."

* * *

They had come back down into the valley three days ago, down the goat track from the high passes where the snow still lay thick enough to swallow a man to the thigh. Maren had argued against it. Ilsette had said nothing—he said nothing always—but he had taken Wendell's sleeve in one small fist and pulled, gently, toward the trailhead, and that had settled the matter more thoroughly than any argument could have.

Because the boy remembered what Wendell could not. Someone had to hold the memories now, and it seemed only fair that they should trade them back and forth like neighbors borrowing salt.

"You gave away eleven years," Maren reminded him that evening, while the three of them sat around a fire Wendell's hands built without instruction. "Maybe twelve. Everything from the day you took the taster's oath. You poured it into yourself along with the boy's inheritance, and you used it to burn the wards, and the wards burned it right out of you. That was the price. Your own words."

"I don't regret it."

"I know you don't. That's what worries me. You say it the way you'd say *I don't regret the weather*." She poked the fire. Sparks rose and died among the rafters. "There are people in this valley who loved you, Wendell. The cooper's widow. Old Tam who ran the ferry. Half the village owes you their lives—you tasted poison for the crown for twenty years so the rest of us could sleep soundly, and then you did it one last time for all of us together. And now you look at them like strangers."

"Do they mind?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. "Tam cried. The cooper's widow brought you a pie and then couldn't stay in the same room with you, because you thanked her politely and it was like being thanked by a ghost wearing your face." She sighed. "But they understand. Most of them. They know what you bought with what you paid. They just—" she gestured vaguely with the poker, "—they miss you. And you can't miss yourself, which seems unfair to everyone."

Outside, the valley lay silent under the early stars. That was the other thing. Saltmere had always hummed—or rather, it had always *not*-hummed, a silence with a texture to it, the wards holding magic out the way a dam holds water, and everyone who lived beneath that pressureless quiet had stopped noticing it years ago. Now the dam was gone. The silence was ordinary silence, river-silence, wind-in-the-grass silence, and every soul in the valley felt the difference the way you feel a tooth that's stopped aching: grateful, and strangely bereft.

Nothing had come in through the gap. Not yet. Whatever Ilsette had carried, whatever inheritance of the old sorcery Wendell had swallowed and smothered in his poisoned, stubborn flesh, it had gone with the wards when they burned—scattered, spent, or sealed somewhere deeper than any ledger recorded. The mountains to the east kept their counsel. The crown's riders, when they finally crested the southern ridge a week later, found nothing but ash-stones and a valley full of people who had, by unspoken agreement, decided to remember very little and say less.

But that came later. That first night, there was only the fire, and the pie the cooper's widow had left cooling on the sill, and Ilsette asleep against Maren's side with his thumb in his mouth like any child of six.

"He laughed today," Maren said softly. "Did you see? When Turnip fell in the rain barrel. First time since the burning."

"I saw."

"He's lighter, Wendell. Whatever you took from him—it worked. He's just a boy now. Mostly." Her eyes found Wendell's across the fire. "And you're just a man. Mostly. We'll manage. We'll fill the empty parts in, one dish at a time, one story at a time. That's how memory works anyway. It was never really yours alone to begin with."

Wendell looked into the flames and felt—nothing dramatic. No grief, no terror. Just the pleasant warmth of a fire and the taste of woodsmoke and honey on the air, catalogued neatly by a tongue that never slept. It occurred to him that this might be the most peaceful he had ever been, precisely because he had no past to measure it against.

Then someone knocked at the door.

Three knocks. Even, unhurried, and wrong—wrong because the whole village knew better than to knock at the taster's cottage after dark, and wrong because the knock had a rhythm to it, deliberate as a signature. Maren's hand went to the knife at her belt. Ilsette woke without crying, which was somehow worse, and stared at the door with eyes gone ancient again.

Wendell rose. His body knew the drill: weight on the balls of the feet, one hand loose, the other ready to catch a thrown cup or a thrown blade. Some things survived any catastrophe indeed.

He opened the door.

A woman stood in the lane, travel-worn, gray at the temples, with the bearing of someone accustomed to doors opening faster. Behind her, half-hidden in the shadows of the hedge, stood two more figures—and though Wendell had no memory to tell him so, the sight of their gray cloaks made his skin prickle with a recognition older than thought, the way a tongue remembers a poison it has never tasted.

"Wendell Nystrom," the woman said. "Or what remains of him." Her gaze flicked past his shoulder—to Maren, to the boy—and something in her face shifted, softened, broke. When she spoke again her voice was barely above the wind.

"My name doesn't matter anymore. But thirty years ago, in this valley, I had another one. And I believe your friend Maren has been waiting a long time to hear it."

Behind him, he heard Maren's breath catch—as if, for the first time in years, she had heard something she'd stopped letting herself hope for.
