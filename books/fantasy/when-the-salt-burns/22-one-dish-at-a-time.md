## 22. One Dish at a Time

The morning the peach seller arrived in Lowmark, Wendell Nystrom was arguing with a chicken.

"Not that basket," he told her, for the third time. "The *other* basket. The one without the—yes. Yes. Thank you."

The hen, whose name was Duchess and who had no respect for authority whatsoever, clucked once, with what he could only describe as smugness, and hopped off the table she'd been surveying her kingdom from. She'd laid an egg in the flour bin again. He found it by feel, warm as a coal, and held it up to the light like a jeweler appraising a stone.

"You," he said, "are a menace to pastry."

Duchess preened.

He set the egg on the windowsill where it would wait its turn beside three others, and stood for a moment in the doorway of his shop, letting the morning come to him. Lowmark smelled of woodsmoke and river water and bread from the ovens two streets over, and none of it meant anything except that it was morning in Lowmark, which was, as far as he knew or cared to know, the whole of the world.

He had been here eleven months. He knew this because Tam the cooper had told him so last week, counting back to the flood season when a thin, quiet man had walked into town with nothing but a knife roll and a way of looking at food like it might be lying to him. The man had asked for work. Old Sefa at the hearth-house had fed him first and hired him second, because—as she put it—"anyone who tastes before they trust is worth feeding twice."

That was all anyone knew about him, and it was enough. Lowmark was not a town that pried. It was a town of coopers and tanners and one extremely ambitious peach farmer, and it had decided within a month that Wendell Nystrom belonged to it, the way a stone belongs to a wall: without asking how it got there.

His own past was a house with most of the lamps blown out. He knew how to read—three languages, though only one of them usefully. He knew the exact weight of every poison that had ever been named in the herbals, and several that hadn't, and this knowledge sat in him like a cellar full of locked jars. He knew that his body was a fortress with old battle-scars inside it, that certain foods made other people ill and left him untouched, that he could smell rot in milk from across a room. And he knew—though he never said it aloud, even to himself—that somewhere behind the fog there had been fire, and salt, and a boy's voice saying words in a language older than any crown, and a choice he had made with his eyes open.

When he reached for those memories, they were not gone exactly. They were *paid for*. Spent, the way a purse is spent, and what remained was the shape of the spending: a certainty that whatever he'd given up, it had been worth it. He woke some mornings with his heart hammering and the taste of burnt salt on his tongue, and he would lie there until dawn and think: *someone is safe because of this.* He didn't know who. That was all right. A man doesn't need to know the name of everyone he's ever pulled from a river.

*One dish at a time,* Sefa had told him, back when his hands shook too badly to hold a ladle. *You don't cook a feast, Wendell. You cook a soup. Then you cook another soup.*

It had become, without either of them noticing, his entire philosophy.

* * *

The peach seller's name was Brannic, and he arrived every autumn with a cartload of fruit so ripe it was practically gossiping.

"Wendell! My friend! My brother! I have brought you the finest—"

"I can smell them from here."

"—the finest peaches in the valley, sun-kissed, honey-hearted, each one hand-picked by my own—"

"Brannic." Wendell lifted one from the crate, turned it, pressed his thumb gently against the flesh just below the stem. He breathed in. Closed his eyes. "Three days past peak. You've been watering them heavy this week—the skin's split at the shoulder. And this one—" he tapped another, "—was picked wet. Rain on the branch. It'll go soft as sorrow by tomorrow."

Brannic stared at him with the wounded dignity of a large man being told small truths. "How," he demanded, "do you *do* that?"

"Practice." Wendell weighed four of the best in his palm, counted out coins. "I'll take these. And if you bring me the ugly ones next time—the bruised ones, the ones nobody wants—I'll pay for those too."

"The ugly—Wendell. My friend. Nobody pays for the ugly ones."

"Somebody should. They've had a harder life. They're usually sweeter for it." He paused, hearing himself, and something flickered behind his eyes—a corridor with a door at the end, a child's small hand, someone laughing at a joke about bitter herbs. Then it was gone, the way fish vanish under dark water, leaving only the ripple.

"You all right?" Brannic asked.

"Peaches," Wendell said. "Preserves. Busy day. Go sell your handsome ones to people with less sense than me."

By midmorning the kitchen smelled like the inside of summer. He worked with the door open because Duchess liked to sit on the step and supervise, and because Lowmark wandered past his window all day long, and half the town considered it their civic duty to lean in and ask what was cooking.

"Can't you just make jam like a normal person?" asked Ilse from the chandlery, watching him skim the foam with a slotted spoon of silver so old the handle had been mended with wire.

"This *is* normal jam. You're thinking of your mother's, which was a war crime involving raisins."

"It won a ribbon at the harvest fair."

"So did treason, historically. Doesn't make it right."

She laughed and stole a slice of peach on her way out, which was the tax everyone paid, and he let them, all of them, because a kitchen with people stealing from it was a kitchen doing its job.

He had learned the town the way he'd apparently once learned everything else: by tasting. Not literally—not anymore. But he watched what people ate when they were sad (Ilse wanted butter and salt; the ferryman wanted anything his late wife used to make; Tam wanted to eat standing up, fast, before the feeling caught him). He kept a mental ledger of it, a warden's ledger, though he didn't know to call it that. Feed a grieving man the food of his grief, he'd written once in the margin of his recipe book, and then feed him something new, and watch which one he comes back for.

The answer, always, was both. People came back for both. That was the whole secret of cooking and, he suspected, of living: you carried the old flavors and you made room for the new ones, and you did it one dish at a time until the carrying stopped hurting.

* * *

In the afternoon, the child came.

Not *a* child—the child, though Wendell had no way of knowing that, and wouldn't have believed it if he did. She was perhaps nine years old, dark-haired, mud to the knees, and she appeared in his doorway the way cats appear: as though she had always lived here and was only now informing him.

"I'm looking for work," she announced. "Sefa says you need help and Sefa is never wrong about anything except her stew, which has too much thyme."

"Sefa's stew is a monument."

"Sefa's stew is a hedge. She puts in so much thyme you can't tell what died." The girl planted her hands on her hips. "My name is Wren. I can scrub, I can carry, I can count faster than you, and I don't steal. Much. Anymore."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Something in his chest did an odd, quiet thing, like a key turning in a lock that hadn't been opened in years—and then didn't turn all the way, and settled, and waited.

"Where are your parents?" he asked.

"Where's yours?" she shot back.

"Fair." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Rules of my kitchen. One: you taste everything before you serve it. Everything. If you wouldn't feed it to yourself, it doesn't leave this room. Two: we don't lie about food. Burnt is burnt. Three—" he hesitated, because rule three had been forming in him for eleven months without his permission, rising out of the fog like a stone out of a receding tide, "—if you find a word you don't know, and it feels old in your mouth, heavier than it should be, you tell me. Don't say it twice. Just tell me."

Wren frowned. "What kind of word?"

"The kind that remembers things." He heard himself say it and blinked, surprised, as though someone else had spoken through him. Then he shrugged and handed her an apron that would fit her approximately never. "Peaches don't peel themselves. Probably. We'll find out together."

She worked like a small storm, all motion and commentary, narrating her own failures with delight ("that one's now a *decoration*") and asking questions with the tireless greed of her age. Where did he learn to cook. Why did he smell everything twice. Why did he keep his knives sharper than the barber's. Why did he flinch, just slightly, every time someone mentioned the mountains north of the flats.

"I don't flinch," he said.

"You did. Just now. Like a horse with a bee."

"Horses are sensible." He kept his eyes on the preserving pan. "Mountains are cold and far away and full of rocks. There. Opinion delivered. Peel."

But after she'd gone home at dusk—he'd fed her first, of course, eggs and the heel of yesterday's bread fried in butter, and watched her eat with an attention that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with an old, old habit of making sure people were *safe to feed*—after she'd gone, he sat by his window with a cup of chamomile he wasn't drinking, and looked north.

The mountains were black against the last light. Somewhere beyond them, he understood without knowing how, there was a valley that had once been guarded by salt and wards and the stubborn love of ordinary people, and it was empty now, and it was *peaceful*, the way a battlefield is peaceful twenty years on—sheep grazing on it, wildflowers coming up through the cracks. Whatever had slept beneath it slept elsewhere now, or slept no more, and the world above it went on selling peaches and raising chickens and complaining about thyme.

He raised his cup to the mountains, a little drunkenly on chamomile, and said, "You're welcome," to no one, meaning everyone, remembering nothing.

Then he went and wrote in the margin of his recipe book, under the note about grieving men:

*A boy came to me once. I don't remember his face. I remember that I would do it again. I remember that it cost everything and bought more. Note for future self: when the fear comes at night, and it will, get up and cook. Fear keeps hours; kitchens keep better ones.*

* * *

Autumn deepened. Wren stayed—first "just till the preserves are done," then "till fair season," then indefinitely, in the manner of children and cats. Duchess accepted her as a rival and then, grudgingly, as staff. Sefa declared her "less useless than most apprentices," which in Sefa's currency was a knighthood.

And on a bright cold morning in late autumn, when frost feathered the windows and the whole town smelled of woodsmoke and the last of Brannic's apples, Wendell stood in his kitchen teaching Wren to temper chocolate—which he knew how to do, though no one in Lowmark had ever asked him why a valley cook possessed such knowledge, and he had simply smiled and said "a man picks things up"—when the knock came at the door.

Two travelers. A woman with grey in her braid and a sword worn like a tool rather than a weapon, weathered and steady-eyed. And a young man beside her, broad-shouldered, with a burn scar along one jaw and the particular stillness of someone who had once been very afraid for a very long time and had made peace with fear the way you make peace with a limp.

"We heard," the woman said, "there's a cook in Lowmark who can taste rain in a peach."

"There is," Wendell said carefully. His hands had gone still on the counter. Something in him was ringing, low and far off, like a bell heard through water. "Who's asking?"

"Maren," the woman said. "This is Corvane. We've come a long way. We've come—" she glanced at Wren, at the peaches-in-winter gleaming in their jars on the shelf, at the egg Duchess had smuggled onto the windowsill overnight, sitting there warm and impossible and defiant among the frost, "—we've come to thank someone. If he'll let us."

Wendell looked at them for a long time. The bell was ringing louder now. Behind the fog, doors he had spent eleven months not opening began, very softly, to glow around their edges.

"Sit down," he said finally, and reached for the pan, because that was what he did, that was all he had ever known to do, that was the whole of his wisdom and the whole of his salvation: *feed them first.* "Both of you. It'll take an hour. Good food takes an hour, and bad news does too, and I have a feeling we've got a lot of both to get through."

Maren smiled like sunrise over salt flats.

"And afterward," Wendell added, cracking eggs one-handed while Wren watched him with wide eyes and Corvane leaned forward like a man recognizing a voice in a dream, "you can tell me why your friend there looks at me like I'm a ghost. And why—" he paused, thumb resting on an eggshell, feeling the faint warmth of it, the small stubborn life inside, "—why I already know I'm going to believe you."

Outside, Duchess crowed—badly, belatedly, entirely on her own schedule.

Inside, the kettle began to sing.
