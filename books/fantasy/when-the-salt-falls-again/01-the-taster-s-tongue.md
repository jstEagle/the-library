## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The palace kitchens woke before the sun, and Ansel Zhou woke before the kitchens.

They had learned, in twenty years of service, that the tongue was a ledger and the body a book written against its will. Every poison they had ever swallowed lived somewhere in them still — the hemlock in their left knee, which ached before rain; the arsenic ghosting faintly through their hairline; the nightshade that had cost them the hearing in one ear and left them with a permanent, low ringing like a struck bell underwater. The royal physician called it resilience. Wren, had she still been anything but memory, would have called it what it was: a debt the body keeps collecting.

Ansel stood at the tasting bench while the ovens breathed heat into the stone room. Before them: the morning's service for Her Majesty Queen Isolde — spiced barley porridge, honeyed figs, small loaves still steaming from their tins, and a pitcher of goat's milk so fresh it was warm as a sleeping animal.

Routine first. Routine kept a person alive.

They broke a corner from the smallest loaf. Chewed. Waited the count of ten heartbeats, then twenty. Bitterness? No. Metal? No. The bread was bread — good bread, Marda's bread, with the faint char she always pretended wasn't there. They took the porridge next, a spoonful held on the tongue until it cooled, then swallowed, then waited. The figs. The milk, last, because milk lied most easily; sweet rot hid in sweetness like a knife in silk.

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

"Clean," Ansel said, and the word went down the kitchen like water finding its channel. Cooks relaxed by degrees. The steward's boy chalked the mark on the serving board — a small circle, open at the top, the sign for *safe* — and the trays began their journey up to the queen's table.

Ansel drank water laced with powdered willow bark, their daily medicine against nothing in particular, and felt the familiar hollow settle into place behind their ribs. Twenty years of mornings like this one. Twenty years of swallowing death by inches so that a woman they'd never spoken to could eat breakfast without fear.

*You are a fork,* Wren used to say, back when she'd been alive enough to say cruel things. *A fork is useful. A fork is not loved.*

She'd been wrong about the second part, mostly. But only mostly.

* * *

The night market opened an hour after dark, in the crooked streets below the Salt Gate where the lamplighters' rounds ended and the honest city gave way to the other one.

By day, Ansel Zhou was the queen's taster, gray-robed and unremarkable, a servant whose office everyone knew and no one saw. By night they were something the law had no clean name for. The Edict of Ashes — signed the year Ansel turned fourteen, the year the purges came — forbade the working of magic within the realm's borders. It did not, strictly speaking, forbid the selling of charms, because charms were difficult to prove. A knotted red thread was just thread until someone's fever broke. A pinch of salt in a doorframe was just salt until the nightmares stopped coming.

Ansel set up their stall between a candle-seller who never sold candles and a man who claimed to repair broken promises. Their stock was humble: threads, sachets, vials of river water blessed in ways Ansel refused to call blessings. Small things. Deniable things. Magic worn down to the size of a secret.

The customers came slowly, as they always did, hooded and quiet.

A fishwife with cracked hands wanted something for her son's night terrors. Ansel tied three knots in a blue thread and told her to hang it above his bed and speak the boy's name into each knot at dusk. She pressed two coppers into their palm and looked at them with the particular gratitude of people who have decided not to ask questions.

A young soldier, barely grown into his uniform, bought a sachet of dried lavender and iron filings "for steadiness." He meant courage. Ansel let him mean courage. Courage was cheaper than truth, and it worked nearly as often.

An old woman Ansel half-recognized from years of markets brought her usual request: a charm against forgetting. Her husband had been dead nine years and she could feel his voice slipping out of her memory like sand through fingers. Ansel mixed her a locket of rosemary, grave-dirt from her own garden, and a sliver of beeswax from a candle burned at his funeral, if she had such a candle. She did. She always did.

"Forgetting is the slowest poison," Ansel told her, sealing the locket. "And the only one I can't taste."

The old woman laughed, wet-eyed, and paid too much, and left.

And then the market emptied, as it did between the supper hour and the midnight hour, and Ansel sat alone among their small deniable magics and felt the loneliness arrive on schedule. It was a punctual thing. It came every night after the last customer, sat down across the stall like an old friend who never bought anything, and stayed until sleep drove it off.

Twenty years ago there had been four of them. Four fugitives from the purge, running the salt roads together with the wards humming overhead like a held breath: Odile with her ward-smith's hands and her terrible laugh, Marisol with the tide still audible in her voice, Wren already dying and pretending otherwise, and Ansel, youngest, tasting everything the road offered and keeping them alive by the ledger of their tongue.

Then the crown had caught up, or the world had pulled apart, or both. Odile taken to the workhouse. Marisol gone to sea or gone to ground, depending on whom you asked. Wren dead, though dead was a generous word for whatever she'd become. And Ansel returned to the palace that had outlawed everything they were, hired back through channels best not examined, surviving by becoming furniture.

*I could find them,* Ansel thought some nights, arranging their charms in rows. *I could look.*

But wanting things was dangerous in a city that punished wanting. So they folded the wanting up small, the way they folded their charms, and put it away in the dark place behind the sternum where they kept everything that mattered.

* * *

It happened at the end of the evening service, when the stars should have been the only witness.

Ansel had heard the commotion earlier — servants clustering at the courtyard windows, pointing up, whispering. The stars were gone. Not clouded-over gone; the sky had been clear as washed glass all day. Simply gone, every one of them, as if the whole black ceiling of the world had been wiped clean. The older servants made signs against ill fortune. The younger ones laughed nervously and said the astronomers would explain it by morning.

Ansel hadn't looked. Looking felt like an invitation.

Now they stood at the tasting bench for the final course — a chilled soup of almond and white grape, pale as moonlight, served in bowls of glazed porcelain. The kitchen was nearly empty. Even Marda had gone to gawk at the sky.

Ansel dipped the spoon. Tasted.

Almond. Grape. A whisper of orange blossom. Clean.

They lowered the spoon — and stopped.

Because underneath, so faint it might have been imagination, might have been fatigue, might have been the ringing in their ruined ear playing tricks, there was something else. Something they had tasted exactly once before, twenty years ago, on the night the four of them had camped beneath the border wards and watched the salt-folk rise shining from the drowned coast to renew the old covenant.

Starlight.

Not the *idea* of starlight. The taste itself — cold and bright and faintly mineral, like snowmelt over silver, like the air after lightning, like the smell of a struck flint translated somehow onto the tongue. It had been in the covenant-wine the tide-priests poured that night. Wren had let Ansel taste it and said, *Remember this, little fork. Most people live their whole lives and never taste what holds the sky up.*

Ansel set the spoon down very carefully, as though the bowl might notice.

There were no stars tonight. There had been no stars for hours. Whatever made that flavor had nowhere in the world to come from — unless the world was leaking. Unless the thing that had been holding the stars was letting go, or breaking, or bleeding.

Their hands, of their own accord, curled into the old shapes. Thumb to forefinger, the gesture for a sensing-charm, the first magic anyone had ever taught them. They didn't complete it. The Edict of Ashes carried penalties that began with the loss of a hand and ended worse. Instead they stood in the empty kitchen, breathing oven-warmth and almond steam, and felt the longing rise up out of the dark place behind their sternum where they kept everything that mattered.

They wanted it to be magic. That was the terrible, childish truth of it. After twenty years of knots and sachets and magic whittled down to the size of a secret, they wanted the old vastness back — the hum of the wards overhead, Wren's laugh, the salt-folk rising silver from the sea. They wanted to belong to a world that had room for what they were.

Ansel picked up the spoon again and tasted once more, methodical, professional, cataloguing.

Still there. Fainter than before — fading even as they tasted it, like a scent leaving a room. But unmistakable now that they knew what to listen for. Starlight, dissolving out of the queen's soup like sugar in water.

And beneath it, threaded through it, something else. Something their tongue had never tasted and yet recognized the way you recognize your own name shouted from a great distance.

Salt. Old salt. Sea-bottom salt. The drowned coast, calling through the food itself, through the water in it, through whatever thin membrane still connected this kitchen to the edge of the world.

The bowl trembled. Ansel realized it was their own hand shaking.

"Clean," they said aloud to the empty kitchen, because saying it aloud was the ritual, and rituals were how you kept the world from noticing you. But their voice came out wrong — too loud, too raw — and the word fell flat on the flagstones like a dropped coin.

Somewhere far away, past the walls and the wards and the sleeping honest city, Ansel felt — not heard, not saw, but felt, deep in the ledger of their poisoned body, the way one feels a tooth crack — something break.

The ringing in their deaf ear surged, sharp and sudden, and for one vertiginous moment it resolved into words. Wren's voice. Dry as paper, close as breath:

*Little fork. It's time. Count the moon's turns, and run.*
