## 2. Thirteen Turns

Midnight came to Vhalen's Rest like a held breath finally let go.

Ansel felt it before they heard it — a wrongness in the teeth, deep in the roots, the way a taster feels a poison a half-second before the tongue can name it. They were climbing the servants' stair with a candle stub and a ledger of tomorrow's menus when the world broke.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. The wards had stood for two hundred years, a lattice of salt and oath strung along the border cliffs, humming so constantly that no one alive remembered silence. Every child in the city learned its note the way they learned their mother's heartbeat. And now it simply stopped, the way a tooth stops being a tooth: one moment anchored, the next a hollow ache where something used to live.

Ansel gripped the banister. The candle flame leaned sideways though there was no draft.

Across the city, in attics and cellar-shrines and the back rooms of dyers' shops, every outlawed mage in Vhalen's Rest must have felt it too — Ansel imagined them all freezing at once, a hundred small magics flaring like startled birds. The city's hidden covenant-keepers, the hedge-charmers, the tide-whisperers who paid the palace guard in charms to look away. All of them feeling the same thing: the great ward above them going dark, and their own little flames guttering in sympathy.

Ansel's own magic — the small, unsanctioned gift that let them taste truth in any dish, read intention in any broth — clenched like a fist inside their chest and then, terrifyingly, went quiet.

*No*, Ansel thought. *Not now. Not after twenty years of hiding you.*

But it wasn't gone. It was listening. The way a dog goes still when it hears a wolf on the far ridge.

Then the bells began. Not the midnight bells — those had already rung, soft and punctual. These were the alarm bells of the Citadel, and beneath them a sound Ansel had never heard but knew instantly from Wren's stories: the sea-horn of the drowned coast, blown by no human throat, carrying forty leagues overland because there was nothing left to stop it.

The wards were down. The border was open. And something on the other side was announcing itself.

* * *

Ansel did not go to bed. Old habit: when the palace convulsed, the food-taster made themselves useful or made themselves invisible, and useful was safer. By the second bell past midnight they were in the lower kitchens, lighting ovens for a garrison that would need feeding by dawn, when half the household guard would be marched to the walls and the other half would be too frightened to sleep.

The kitchen filled slowly. Cooks arrived with sleep-rumpled faces and rumors arriving faster than they did — the eastern beacons were lit; the Cliffs of Oath were dark, which meant either the watch had abandoned them or the watch was dead; a shepherd had come down from the high pasture raving about stars falling into the sea.

The stars. Ansel had almost forgotten. Last night the sky had been naked — not clouded, *empty*, every star gone as if plucked, only the moon remaining, swollen and alone. The astrologers had called it an eclipse out of season. The priests had called it a test. Ansel, tasting the evening's wine while the sky gaped overhead, had tasted nothing amiss and said nothing, which was its own kind of lie.

Now, kneading dough that didn't need kneading, Ansel let themselves remember what the starless sky had actually tasted like on the tongue's secret level, the level no one else could reach. Cold. Vast. Familiar in a way they couldn't place — like a word in a language they'd spoken as a child.

"Zhou."

Ansel looked up. Mistress Havel, the under-kitchen-mother, gray-faced in the oven light. "They're saying the wards fell. Actually fell." She wiped her hands on her apron again and again, wringing cloth that was already dry. "My grandmother used to say the wards weren't built. They were *fed*. Is that true? You'd know, wouldn't you? You know everything that goes in anything."

"I taste," Ansel said carefully. "I don't know."

Havel stared at them a moment too long — long enough that Ansel wondered, with a cold slide of dread, whether she suspected, whether anyone had ever suspected — and then turned away to shout at a boy who'd let the butter sit too near the fire.

Ansel exhaled. Around them the kitchen roared back to life, and underneath its noise Ansel heard it: the absence. A silence where a hum should be. Twenty years they had lived beneath that hum without hearing it, the way you don't hear your own blood — and now its silence rang through the whole city like a cracked bell.

And in that ringing, faint as breath on the back of the neck, a voice:

*"Ansel."*

* * *

No one else heard it. Ansel was sure of that — the boy with the butter hadn't flinched, Havel hadn't turned. But Ansel knew the voice the way they knew their own pulse, and it pulled them out the scullery door into the herb garden behind the kitchens, where the night lay starless and close.

Wren stood among the rosemary.

She looked exactly as she had the day she died — small, sharp-faced, her taster's graying hair pinned with two silver pins, her hands clasped behind her back in the posture she'd drilled into Ansel until it lived in their spine. She was translucent at the edges, like steam off a cooling bowl, and the rosemary showed through her skirts.

"You're real," Ansel said, and hated how young their voice sounded.

"I'm persistent. There's a difference." Wren's eyes — quick, dark, missing nothing, the eyes that had taught Ansel to read a courtier's soup — swept the garden, the lit windows, the dark sky. "Twenty years I've been too thin to speak to you. Whatever broke tonight knocked loose enough of me to manage it, so listen fast, because I don't know how much I've got."

"The wards—"

"Were fed by the covenant. Yes." Wren's jaw tightened. "You felt it break. Everyone with a drop of old blood felt it. What you don't understand yet — what *nobody* understands, because the crown buried it so deep even I only found the edge of it before they purged us — is that the wards were never the point. They were the *receipt*. Proof that the bargain was being kept."

Ansel stepped closer. Rosemary scent moved through Wren without stirring her. "Bargain with whom?"

"The salt-folk. The drowned coast. The people your histories call monsters and my grandmother called neighbors." Wren's voice dropped, though no living soul could have heard her. "Long ago the realms swore a covenant at a stone beneath the tideline. Sacrifice freely given, both directions, every turn of the moon. In exchange: the wards, the peace, the stars in their courses. Your queen's regent stopped the payments. Started feeding the wards lies instead — pretty words written down as if ink could bleed. Tonight the stone called the debt due, and there was nothing in the account."

The words landed in Ansel's chest like stones dropped down a well, each one striking deeper than the last. "The stars," Ansel whispered. "That's why the stars—"

"Held. As collateral. The stone swallowed them the way a lender seizes a house." Wren's translucent hands unclasped, and for the first time Ansel saw that they were shaking. "There is a window. One moon-cycle. When the moon completes its thirteenth turn, the covenant closes forever — the realms seal apart, the sea takes back the coast, and everything we were to each other becomes a story with no ending. Thirteen turns, Ansel. Count them. We are already one night poorer."

"And how do we—" Ansel stopped. Because they already understood, in the way you understand a fall after your foot has left the ledge. "It needs sacrifice. Freely given. Like before."

"It needs more than that. It needs the old terms *and* new ones, witnessed and written, at the stone itself. It needs someone the stone will recognize." Wren looked at them then — really looked, with the full weight of the gaze Ansel had spent a childhood earning — and Ansel felt seen down to the ledger of poisons in their bones. "You carry both realms in your blood. You always did. That's why I chose you, all those years ago. Not your tongue. Your *descent*."

The garden seemed very far away suddenly, the oven-glow behind them, the city's panic muffled, just the two of them and the empty sky.

"There were four of us," Ansel said quietly. It wasn't a question. It was the wound they'd carried longest.

Something crossed Wren's face like weather. "Odile Ferran is in the Crown workhouse — they blamed her for the wards' design when the wards failed. Marisol I can't find; the tide took her somewhere, but tide runs to coast, and coast is where you're going. And me—" she spread her insubstantial hands, and smiled, wry and terrible, "—me you've already got."

"I can't free Odile from a crown workhouse. I'm a food-taster. I taste things."

"You are the only person in this kingdom who can survive the regent's table," Wren said. "Think about what that's worth in a palace. Think about what you could make a king drink." Her edges were fraying now, the rosemary showing brighter through her, and urgency roughened her voice. "Thirteen turns. The moon doesn't wait, and neither does the sea. Go east. Find the smugglers running the fallen coast — whatever family Marisol kept, she kept it there. And Ansel—"

She was nearly gone, a shape of silver and dark, only her eyes still solid.

"—stop hiding. You were never good at it anyway. Your charms sold out every market night."

Then the garden was empty, and the hum was still absent from the world, and Ansel stood alone under a sky with no stars, holding a purpose the size of a knife.

* * *

Dawn found Ansel in their narrow room above the laundry, packing.

Not much: a change of clothes, a whetstone-wrapped bundle of tasting knives, the flat tin of salt they had never once been without in twenty years. On the shelf, the small stock of charms they sold in the night market — knots of thread, wax seals, paper fish — and Ansel looked at them a long moment before sweeping every one into their coat. Not to sell. To give. Something in Wren's voice had rearranged the arithmetic of hiding.

Below, the palace woke into crisis: hooves in the courtyard, the regent's banners going up over the gatehouse, criers announcing a curfew and a reward for information regarding "unlicensed practitioners." Ansel listened with a taster's ear and heard what the proclamation actually said: *the crown is afraid.*

Good. Fear opened doors that loyalty sealed.

At the window, the moon hung low over the eastern wall, fat and pale and alone in its terrible vacancy, already visibly begun on its thirteenth journey. Ansel counted forward without meaning to. Twelve turns. Eleven after that. A purse of nights, spending itself.

Somewhere east, a woman ran salt past broken wards. Somewhere underground, a smith with ruined hands knew how the first ones were made. Somewhere between life and memory, a ghost spent herself like a last candle to deliver one warning.

Ansel buttoned their coat, took up the tin of salt, and went down through the kitchen — past Havel, past the ovens, past the life they had built out of small safe services — and out the postern gate into a city holding its breath, walking toward the sea it had been taught to fear.

The tide, Wren had said. Tide runs to coast.

Ansel only hoped they could run faster than the moon.
