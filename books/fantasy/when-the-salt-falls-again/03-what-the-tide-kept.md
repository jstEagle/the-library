## 3. What the Tide Kept

The night the wards fell, the sea forgot how to be quiet.

Marisol Vane heard it from the wheelhouse of the *Kestrel's Debt*, a groan rolling up through the hull like something enormous turning over in its sleep. She'd been running salt out of Grayharbor for six years, long enough to know every voice the water owned — the slap of chop against a lazy hull, the hiss of a squall coming in off the shallows, the low conversation of current and stone beneath the channel. This was none of those. This was the sound of a held breath finally let go.

She cut the lanterns first. Old instinct. Whatever was coming, whatever had just broken, you didn't want to be lit up like an offering when it arrived.

Then she went to the rail and watched the horizon where the border wards had stood for three hundred years.

There was nothing to see. That was the horror of it. The wards had never been visible, not exactly — but everyone who lived near them knew they were there the way you knew a wall at your back in a dark room. Fishermen swore the air tasted different on the far side. Birds turned at the line as if it were drawn in salt across the sky itself. Tonight the birds were gone entirely, and the air tasted like the inside of an empty mouth, and the stars—

Marisol looked up before she could stop herself.

The stars were going out. Not clouded over, not swallowed by fog — *going out*, one by one, in no order she could read, like candles snuffed by a hand moving through a house she couldn't see. She counted eleven before her throat closed and she made herself stop counting.

"Captain." Tam, at the hold ladder, young enough that his fear still showed plain on his face. "Cargo's shifting. Down below. It's — I don't know what it's doing down there."

"Cargo doesn't do things." But even as she said it, she was moving, taking the ladder two rungs at a time, because the sea had just forgotten how to be quiet and the cargo was doing things and the stars were dying overhead, and if this was the night everything ended then Marisol Vane intended to meet it standing on her own deck with her own knife in her own hand.

The hold smelled of brine and lamp oil and, underneath both, something older — a smell she hadn't encountered since childhood, since her mother's tide-shrine, since the days when priests still came to the shore to sing the water calm. Salt-iron. The smell of a bargain being kept, or broken.

Her lantern found the source of the noise.

A person lay half-buried under the tarpaulins over her salt barrels, soaked through, coughing seawater into the bilge like their lungs had been full for hours. Alive. Barely. Dressed in kitchen whites gone gray with wet, sleeves rolled to the elbow, and around one wrist — Marisol's breath caught — a cord of knotted red thread, tied in the old way, the way charms used to be tied before tying them got you hanged.

"Tam," she called up the ladder, not taking her eyes off the stranger. "Get me the boathook. And don't come down yet."

The stranger coughed again, rolled onto their side, and looked up at her with eyes that were too clear for someone nearly drowned. Sharp eyes. Taster's eyes, she thought absurdly, though tasters were palace creatures and palace creatures did not wash up in smugglers' holds.

"Your cargo," the stranger rasped. "It's wrong. The salt's been cut with ash. Someone's feeding it lies."

Marisol went cold all the way down to her boots.

* * *

She didn't untie the red thread. That was the whole argument, really, and she knew it even while she pretended otherwise. She sat across from the stranger in her cramped cabin with a pot of barley tea between them and her knife flat on the table where they could both see it, and she told herself she hadn't cut the thread because she wanted answers first. Not because she remembered.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. "Night market, Grayharbor. Little stall behind the cooper's, no sign, no name. You sold my brother a knot-charm for his lungs when he was ten."

The stranger wrapped both hands around the tea. Up close, in decent light, Marisol could read the years on them — twenty years of tasting, maybe more, written in the careful way they held their hands, the way they tested the steam before drinking. Ansel Zhou. She'd heard the name once or twice in whispers, the way you hear about a saint or a criminal, whichever side of the law your sympathies fell on.

"He'd have died without it," Marisol said. "The fever-menders gave up on him. My mother wouldn't take him to the tide-priests because — well." Because her mother had stopped believing in anything the sea might answer, after the purge. After the year the crown outlawed small magic and the tide-priesthood knelt and signed and kept singing hymns to a god they'd agreed to pretend was dead. "So yes. I know what you are. What I want to know is what you're doing in my hold."

"Falling." Ansel drank. Winced. Drank again. "The wards came down at midnight. Did you feel it?"

"I felt it."

"Then you felt the rest too. Everything the wards were holding back is coming through now. Everything they were holding *in*. I was on the harbor steps when the break hit the leylines and the backlash threw me off the pier. I swam for the nearest hull. Yours happened to be nearest."

"Lucky you."

"I've been luckier." A ghost of a smile, quickly gone. "I'll pay for passage. I'll work it, if coin's no good to you. I need to reach the drowned coast."

Marisol laughed — one short, ugly bark. "The drowned coast. Of course you do. Everyone wants the drowned coast tonight. Do you know what's happening out there?" She jerked her chin toward the porthole, where the sky sat blacker than any honest night. "Stars are going out like lamps in a rich man's hall. The sea's pulled back half a mile past the old markers. My crew's saying the world's ending, and the ones who aren't saying it are thinking it louder. And now a half-drowned charm-seller crawls out of my bilge wanting passage to the one stretch of coast even smugglers won't touch."

"It's where the covenant-stone is."

The words landed in the cabin like a dropped anchor. Marisol stared. Her mother had said those words exactly once in Marisol's life, drunk on shrine-wine, weeping: *the stone under the drowned coast, child, the stone that ate our names.* Marisol had been seven. She'd spent twenty years deciding she'd imagined it.

"Say that again," she said quietly.

Ansel set down the cup. "The border wards weren't crown-work. They were a covenant — human realm and salt-folk, bound together at a stone on the drowned coast. Somebody broke it. Tonight. Deliberately. The stars are part of the bargain, and the bargain's failing, and when the moon finishes its thirteenth turn—" they paused, and Marisol saw them decide to say the rest plainly, the way you tell a patient the taste of the medicine, "—the realms seal apart forever. Everything on this side stays here. Everything on the other side stays there. Including the sea's memory, and the coast's, and ours."

"And you know this how?"

"A ghost told me." Ansel's mouth twisted. "Wren. She trained me. She's dead, and she's back, and she says the stone can teach us how to mend what was broken — if we can reach it. If we can find the people who know how to listen to it."

Marisol looked at her knife. At the stranger. At the porthole where the starless dark pressed like a face against glass.

"My mother was a tide-priest," she heard herself say. "Real one. Before the signing. She used to sing the tide in at Grayharbor, and it came." Twenty years of not saying this, and now it poured out of her like the sea through a cracked dike. "When the crown outlawed the old ways, she signed the renunciation with the rest of them. Kept the shrine. Kept the songs. Just sang them quieter, and then not at all, and then she died and I took her boat and started hauling salt, which is the least holy cargo there is, and I told myself the sea didn't remember me." She stood, abruptly, needing to move. "And now the sea's pulled back half a mile and the stars are dying, and you're sitting in my cabin telling me it's because somebody fed the wards lies instead of sacrifice. So don't ask me for passage, taster. Tell me why I shouldn't put you back in the water."

"Because your brother's alive," Ansel said simply. "Because the salt in your hold has ash in it, which means someone above us is already working the breaking, and every barrel we carry honestly is one less lie in the world. And because—" they hesitated, and Marisol watched them choose the last truth with visible care, "—because I can't get there alone. None of us can. Wren said *we*. There's a ward-smith in a prison workhouse who built the things we're trying to rebuild. There's a priest's daughter who knows the drowned coast better than any chart." Their clear eyes held hers. "That was always you, wasn't it. Even before I knew your name. The daughter who kept the songs."

"The daughter who *forgot* the songs," Marisol corrected. Her voice came out rough. "There's a difference."

"There's a difference between forgetting and burying," Ansel said. "Buried things come back. Ask any grave-digger. Ask any tide."

* * *

They sailed on the pre-dawn ebb, south along the deadening coast, and Marisol stood her own watch because she didn't trust the sky enough to sleep and didn't trust herself enough to try.

Below, Ansel slept in the crew hammock, red thread still knotted at their wrist, and Tam reported that the ash-cut barrels had gone quiet, and Marisol chose not to examine what that meant.

At gray light she took the wheel and steered past the first of the abandoned villages — houses with doors standing open, cooking pots still on cold hearths, nameboards over the thresholds blank as fresh snow. The sea had withdrawn so far the fishing racks stood dry over cracked mud, and the smell of the exposed seabed rose up faint and strange, like a room sealed for a hundred years.

Her mother's songs rose with it. Unbidden, wordless, just the melody, humming itself in Marisol's chest like a second pulse.

She gripped the wheel tighter and did not sing.

Behind them, far to the north, a smudge of smoke rose from the direction of the prison workhouses at Corven's Reach — and Ansel, waking, climbed to the rail beside her and looked at it a long time.

"That'll be Odile Ferran," they said. "If the crown blamed her for the wards failing, that's where she'll be. We'll need her hands."

"We'll need a miracle," Marisol said. "But fine. Corven's Reach first." She turned the wheel, and the *Kestrel's Debt* heeled toward the smoke, toward the ruined coast, toward everything her family had buried. "But you steer us wrong, taster, and I feed you to the tide myself."

"Fair terms," said Ansel Zhou, and smiled like someone who had already paid worse.
