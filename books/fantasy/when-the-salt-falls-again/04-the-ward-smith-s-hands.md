## 4. The Ward-Smith's Hands

The prison workhouse at Corren's Hollow smelled of lye and iron and the particular despair of people who had been counted so long they had forgotten they were persons. Ansel stood in the queue of visitors—merchants mostly, delivering bread and taking back debts—and kept their eyes down and their hands still. Marisol's smuggler's coat itched at their shoulders. She'd given them a forged pass stamped with a salt-merchant's sigil that didn't exist anymore, which was either very clever or very sad, depending on how you looked at the coast these days.

"Name," said the gate-warden.

"Ferran," Ansel said. "Here for my sister."

The warden consulted a ledger thick enough to stop a door. "Ferran. The ward-smith." He looked up with something between contempt and fear. Ward-smiths were not popular in a city whose border wards had fallen like a dropped plate. "Third gallery, loom-line. You get half an hour."

* * *

They found her at the looms.

Ansel knew her by silhouette before anything else—the breadth of shoulder, the way she sat slightly forward as if leaning into weather that wasn't there. Odile Ferran had built the border wards when she was twenty-three years old, weaving starlight into stone with hands the ballads called blessed. Now she sat at a loom weaving rough wool into rougher cloth, and her hands—

Ansel's stomach turned.

The workhouse punished its troublemakers by the hands; everyone knew this. But no one had told Ansel what it looked like done to a smith's hands specifically, hands that had been instruments of impossible precision for twenty years. The fingers were swollen at every joint, the knuckles misshapen, two of the nails black and lifting. Scars crossed her palms in patterns too regular to be accidents. Someone had taught those hands to be clumsy on purpose.

She felt Ansel watching—smiths always knew where weight fell—and did not look up from the shuttle.

"If you've come for an apology," she said, voice like gravel sifted through water, "the crown took mine already. It's in a vault somewhere, I expect. Everything I had is in a vault somewhere."

"I've come for your help."

That got her head up. Her face was thinner than the wanted-broadsheets had made it, cheekbones like shipwreck timber, but the eyes were the same: gray-green, tide-colored, always measuring. They measured Ansel now, top to bottom, and stopped.

"You're the taster." Not a question. "Zhou. The one who sold charms under the fishmonger's awning on Tenth-Night market. My brother bought a fever-charm from you once. Paid in buttons because he'd lost his purse to dice." Something almost moved across her face and was gone. "It worked, incidentally. He swore by you until they outlawed you."

"They outlawed the magic, not me. I'm still perfectly legal."

"You're standing in a prison wearing a dead man's merchant seal asking favors of a traitor. Legal is a word that doesn't fit you anymore." She set the shuttle down. Around them, the loom-line clattered on, forty women and three men pretending not to listen. "What do you want, taster?"

"The wards are down. You know that."

"I built them. Of course I know that. Do you know what it's like to feel your own work die?" Her ruined hand flexed on her knee, involuntary, like a bird remembering flight. "Like losing teeth. All of them at once."

"Wren says the covenant's broken. Says we have until the moon finishes its thirteenth turn before the realms seal apart forever. Says the regent fed the wards lies instead of sacrifice." Ansel crouched beside her chair, low, keeping their voice inside the loom-noise. "I need to know if that's true, and I need to know how to fix it, and there is no one alive who understands the ward-work better than—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a snapped thread. Odile's eyes had gone bright and hard. "Don't say *no one alive understands better*. You know what they gave me for understanding better? They gave me this." She held up her hands, palms out, the scars catching the gray window-light. "Twenty years I fed that border. Twenty years of calibration, of walking the line-stones in winter, of arguing with accountants about the cost of starlight. And when it failed—not when it *was made* to fail, mind, when it *failed*—they needed someone whose guilt would look good on a gallows notice. So they took my warrant and my name and then, when I wouldn't confess to a lie, they took my hands."

"I know."

"You don't know. You hid your little charms in soup and sold them for coppers and stayed free. I stood on the wall and told the truth and—" She stopped. Breathed. The looms clattered. Somewhere a supervisor's keys jingled. When she spoke again her voice was lower, scraped nearly clean. "Why do you think I wouldn't sign their confession, Zhou?"

Ansel thought about it honestly, and found they didn't know. Everyone signed. Signing cost nothing but the truth, which in Corren's Hollow was the cheapest thing on any table.

"Because the confession was wrong," Odile said, answering herself, "and because if I signed it, then everything—the twenty years, the line-walking, the starlight woven into stone with these hands—it all became a failure. A crime. Mine. And I couldn't do that to the work. Whatever they did to me after, I could live with, because the work itself was true. The covenant was real. The bargain held for six hundred years because people kept it honestly, and it broke because one man decided honest sacrifice was inefficient." She laughed, short and terrible. "So yes. I know Wren is right. I knew it the night the stars went out. I felt the wards swallow the sky like a debt collector swallowing collateral, and I sat here in this chair and screamed until they beat me for disturbing the line."

Ansel's throat was tight. "Then come with us."

"To do what?" She spread her hands again, deliberately this time, cruelly. "Look at them, taster. I can't hold a stylus. I can't tie the simplest binding-knot. The fine work, the weave-work, the *actual smithing*—that's finished. What's left of me is a woman who remembers everything and can touch nothing."

"Then remember at me," Ansel said. "Teach me. Teach all of us. Marisol can call the tide to stand witness. Wren—Wren will spend what she has left. And I—" Ansel hesitated. They had not yet said it aloud to anyone, and it came out smaller than intended. "My blood carries both realms. Wren thinks that might matter. To the stone."

Odile went very still. Even the loom-clatter seemed to bend around her silence.

"The covenant-stone," she said at last. "You mean to go to the drowned coast. To the original."

"It's the only thing Wren trusts."

"And she's right." Odile's gaze drifted toward the high barred windows, where the light was the color of old pewter. Beyond it, somewhere east, lay the sea that had walked away from its own villages. "Listen to me, taster, because I'll say this once and then never again. The border wards I built—they were copies. Good copies, the best anyone ever made, but copies. The original covenant-stone is not a tool. It isn't even a monument. It's a *teacher*. It learned the bargain from the people who first struck it, and it keeps that knowledge the way bone keeps the shape of a wound. If anyone alive can learn to rebuild what I built, it won't be from my memory. My memory is secondhand." Her ruined fingers curled slowly shut. "It will be from the stone itself. And the stone does not teach gently."

"How do you know?"

"Because I found it once. When I was young. Before I ever built a ward, before the crown knew my name." Her eyes came back to Ansel's, and there was something in them Ansel hadn't seen before—not hope exactly, hope was too clean—but its scorched and stubborn cousin. "I went looking for it because my grandmother's grandmother had been salt-folk, far enough back that the blood barely showed. And the stone tasted that blood and it *spoke*, and what it showed me was the first night of the bargain. Two peoples on a cold shore, starving and afraid, deciding to belong to each other. Not a treaty. Not a transaction." Her voice frayed. "*A household.* That's what the covenant is, Zhou. That's what the regent broke. Not a contract—a home. And you don't rebuild a home from blueprints. You rebuild it by living in it."

The supervisor's keys jingled closer. Half an hour, the warden had said, and the sand was running thin.

"So teach me," Ansel said again, quieter. "Come with us. Or tell me everything you remember and stay behind and let me carry it."

Odile looked at her hands for a long moment. Then, with the deliberate care of a woman handling something breakable, she reached out and took Ansel's wrist—grip weak, scarred palm dry as old paper—and pressed her thumb against Ansel's pulse. Against the ledger of poisons that lived in Ansel's veins. Her breath caught.

"You really do carry both realms," she murmured. "Salt and starlight, both in the same blood. No wonder Wren chose you." She released the wrist and sat back, and Ansel watched a decision move through her the way a swell moves under ice—slowly, unstoppably, cracking things open on its way. "There's a laundry-cart leaves the north yard at dusk. The driver owes me. He doesn't know he owes me, but he does—I built the ward that saved his village from the raiders in the Year of Ash, and men like him pay their debts whether or not they remember incurring them."

"That's it? You'll come?"

"Don't mistake me." Her jaw set. "I'm not coming because I forgive myself. I don't, and I'm not sure I ever will, and I am certainly not coming because you forgive me—you don't even know what I did yet, what I *built*, what the wards actually ate every winter to keep the border sealed. You'll learn. On the coast, you'll learn, and then we'll see whose forgiveness survives the lesson." She picked the shuttle back up, and her maimed fingers closed around it clumsily, painfully, holding on. "But the moon is turning, and the stone is waiting, and I have spent five years being punished for a crime I didn't commit while the crime itself walks around in a regent's robes eating off gold plate. So yes, taster. I'm coming. Dusk. North yard. Wear something that smells like laundry."

Ansel rose. Behind them the loom-line rattled on, forty lives measured in cloth, and Odile Ferran bent over her work like a woman leaning into a wind only she could feel.

At the gallery door Ansel glanced back once. Odile wasn't watching them go. She was looking at her own ruined hands, turning them over in the gray light—studying them, Ansel realized later, the way a smith studies a blade before the forging. Not mourning them.

Counting what was left to work with.

Outside, the afternoon had gone strange and dim, though the sun was hours from setting, and far to the east, beyond the walls and the workhouse and the whole wounded length of the realm, Ansel could feel the sea pulling—patient, enormous, keeping count of the turns left on the moon.

Three remained.

Dusk came fast after that.
