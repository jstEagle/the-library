## 5. A Kitchen of Ghosts

The dead do not sleep, but we forget. That is the mercy the living never understand — that a ghost's existence is mostly absence, a long gray drifting between the cracks of the world, and only rarely does something pull us sharp and whole back into ourselves.

Tonight, the thing that pulls me is cumin.

Ansel is working the late shift in the border kitchen where Marisol's crew has sheltered, toasting spice over a low flame because none of us can bear to sleep with the sea groaning beyond the walls. The smell rises off the pan and into the air, and I rise with it, helpless as smoke drawn up a flue. For a moment I am nothing but appetite — the ghost of a woman who spent forty years tasting for kings and died still hungry.

Then Ansel's senses open like a door, the way they always do when their magic reaches for mine, and I step through.

It is not possession. Ansel would never call it that, and neither would I. It is closer to what happens when two cooks share one kitchen: you learn the rhythm of each other's hands. When they let me in, I feel the heat of the pan on skin I no longer have, taste the bitterness of char on a tongue that remembers every poison it ever swallowed. I feel the small bright knot of Ansel's magic sitting under their breastbone, banked low like a coal they've spent twenty years pretending isn't there.

*You're staring at the cumin,* I say.

*I'm staring at the past,* Ansel answers. Their hands keep moving, steady, automatic. *This is the blend from the purge night. The one you made us eat before we ran. Don't tell me you forgot.*

I had not forgotten. I had only buried it deeper than most graves go.

*Sit down, child,* I say. *If we're going to remember it, let's remember it properly.*

* * *

Twenty years ago there was a kitchen beneath the east wing of the palace, and it belonged to me.

Not by deed or title — the crown would never have granted a taster ownership of anything, not even her own tongue. But I had run that kitchen for thirty years, and the copper pots knew my hands, and the apprentices knew the sound of my cane on the flagstones, and when I said *taste this*, they tasted it, and when I said *spit*, they spat, and four times in those thirty years the spitting saved a life. You can own a room that way. Quietly. Without anyone writing it down.

Odile Ferran was there that night because she was always there — the ward-smith who came down from the towers smelling of ozone and chalk dust, complaining that palace food was an insult and eating three plates of it anyway. She was twenty-six and already the finest ward-smith the realm had produced in a century, with burn scars laddering both forearms from the covenant-stone's edge, though in those days she laughed about them. Called them her wages. She'd built the border wards herself, three years prior, singing while she worked, or so she claimed; I never saw it, but I believed it, because Odile did nothing without song in it somewhere.

Marisol was seventeen and furious about everything. Her mother had sent her inland as a tide-priest's offering — a diplomatic gesture, a girl delivered to court like a jar of preserved kelp, proof of the drowned coast's loyalty. She wore her mother's coral beads and hated them. She came to my kitchen the first week and asked, point-blank, whether the palace food was poisoned, and when I said *constantly*, she pulled up a stool and never really left.

And Ansel. Ansel was twenty-two, newly bound as royal taster, so thin the apron strings wrapped twice around them. They had come out of the river provinces with a forged name and a small forbidden magic folded up inside them like a moth in cupped hands. On their first day they tasted a consommé meant for the queen mother and went white as salt and said, in a voice that shook only slightly, *there is henbane in this.* There was. There should have been no way to know. I took them aside afterward and said, very quietly, *I know what you are. So does the crown, if you're careless. So we will not be careless.* And Ansel looked at me the way the drowning look at rope.

That was our kitchen. A disgraced-in-advance ward-smith, an exiled priest's daughter, a taster with illegal blood, and me — old Wren, who had outlived three monarchs by being too useful to kill.

The purge came for us on a Tuesday.

* * *

Here is what the histories will tell you, if the histories are ever allowed to be written: that on the night the stars first guttered, a cabal of unlicensed mages attempted to sever the border covenant and were justly purged by crown forces. That is the story they nailed to every gate in the capital. I read it by candlelight in a safehouse window while soldiers marched below, and I laughed until Ansel thought I'd finally gone mad, which may yet turn out to be true.

Here is what actually happened.

The border wards were not merely Odile's craft. Craft raises the walls; the covenant feeds them. Every season, at the turning of the moon, the drowned coast sent up its tithe — salt and memory, freely given, the tide-priests' oldest office — and the crown sent down its half: a sacrifice of royal magic, drops of the sovereign's own power poured into the covenant-stone. Blood for salt. Song for tide. That was the bargain, older than the palace, older than the road, and the stars themselves stood surety for it. That was the part everyone forgot until it mattered: the stars were not decoration. They were witnesses. Collateral. The sky itself had cosigned the oath.

Three years before the collapse, the old king died. His heir was nine years old. And a regent took the throne-room chair — a careful man, a patient man, Lord Veyran Ashgrave, who smiled like a locked drawer.

The first seasonal tithe under his regency, he did not send a sacrifice to the stone. He sent a substitute: a binding worked in ash and assertion, a spell that said *the sacrifice has been made* in words loud enough for the stone to hear. A lie, dressed in liturgy. The second turning, he sent a bigger lie. The third, he stopped bothering with ceremony at all and simply ordered the covenant-office closed, its records burned, its priests pensioned into silence. Why pay a debt, his reasoning ran, if the creditor lives underwater and cannot walk up to collect?

He did not understand what he was cheating. That is the thing I hold onto, some nights, to keep from hating him cleanly. He thought the wards were Odile's machine, and machines run on fuel or they don't, and if the fuel ledger could be falsified then the machine would run forever on paper. He thought the stars were lights.

But Odile felt it. Every lie fed to the stone, she felt — a wrongness in the ward-lines like a sour note held too long. She came to my kitchen three nights before the end, gray-faced, and laid her ruined-by-then hands flat on my table and said, *Wren, someone is feeding the borders forgery. The stone is swallowing it because it must, but it's keeping count. Stones keep count, Wren. Everything fed to a covenant is written down, and someday there's a reckoning, and I don't want to be standing in this kingdom when the ledger's read aloud.*

So we did what careful people do. We prepared to run, slowly, over months, in ways that looked like nothing. Odile transferred her tower records to untraceable forms. Marisol wrote to her mother and got back, for the first time in two years, a letter in tide-code: *COME HOME. THE SEA IS ANGRY. COME HOME NOW.* And I — I taught Ansel everything remaining, all my poisons and antidotes, the taste-signatures of forty lethal substances, and also the other thing, the thing I had sworn I'd never pass on: how to feed a charm with your own small magic without letting it show in your face. Because I had known about Ansel's gift since their first week, and I had decided, in the private parliament of my old heart, that some outlawries are worth protecting.

We might have slipped away clean, all four of us, if the reckoning hadn't come early.

On the last night, the stone called in a fraction of its debt — a warning, a tremor, the magical equivalent of a fist banging once on a door before breaking it down. The stars flickered. Everyone in the capital with a drop of talent felt it, and the regent understood, in that instant, exactly what was happening and whose fault it would look like. He moved within the hour. Not against himself, of course. Against us. Against the ward-smith whose work had failed, the tide-priest's daughter whose people's covenant it was, the unregistered mage he'd tolerated in his kitchens, and the old taster who had known too much for too long and said nothing to the right ears.

They came at midnight with white hoods and official seals, which in this country have always been the same garment.

* * *

I remember the running better than I remember anything else now. Memory is all a ghost owns, so we hoard the strange currency: I keep the sound of Odile's boots on wet cobbles, the coral beads snapping under Marisol's own hand — *no chains, even pretty ones* — the way Ansel gripped my arm, my actual solid arm, because that night my magic and their terror together held me to my body like nails in a plank. We went out through the kitchen's ash-chute, down into the cellars, out through the old tithe-tunnel that ran toward the river — built, in the first days of the covenant, so the drowned coast's emissaries could enter the palace without breathing surface air. The regent's men sealed it behind us. They thought they were cutting off our retreat. They were sealing us safely away from the swords.

At the river fork we broke apart, because staying together meant dying together. Odile south, to the works, swearing she could fix it from the outside if they'd only let her near the anchor-points — they let her, for two more years, right up until they needed someone to blame. Marisol east, home, carrying her mother's code-letter like a live coal. Ansel and I north, because a taster who vanishes is missed and hunted, but a taster who reappears in another great house, humble and useful, is forgotten.

Before we split, Odile grabbed each of us by the collar, the way she used to grab apprentices, and said: *The stone keeps count. Whatever happens, whatever they blame on us — the stone keeps count. Find the ledger when it opens. Read it out loud.*

Those were almost her last words to us for twenty years. Mine were to Ansel, later, at the mouth of an alley where I could feel myself thinning, my borrowed solidity bleeding away as the panic left my body: *Live dull, child. Live so quietly that the world forgets to check whether you're alive.* And then the night took me, and I drifted, and twenty years went by like water through open fingers.

* * *

Now the fire has burned down and Ansel is sitting with the cold pan between their hands, and I am sitting with them, insofar as I sit anywhere, wearing their warmth like a coat I'm not entitled to.

*The regent,* Ansel says at last. *Veyran Ashgrave. He's still regent. The girl-queen is twenty-four now and still hasn't been crowned, and he's still feeding lies to whatever's left of the wards.*

*Yes.*

*And when the ledger opens — when we get to the stone —*

*We read it out loud,* I say. *Like Odile told us. Every lie he fed it, every tithe he stole, every name he purged to cover it. The stone keeps count, Ansel. All we have to do is make the kingdom listen.*

Ansel is quiet for a long moment. Through their eyes I watch the embers settle, and I feel what they feel — not fear exactly, though fear is there, but something underneath it, older and steadier. Purpose settling into place like a bone set straight.

*Marisol knows these waters. Odile built the walls. And you—* Ansel's mouth twitches, that dry almost-smile I have watched for twenty years from the far side of everything. *You know how to taste a lie.*

*I invented it,* I say.

But before they can answer, the door of the kitchen bangs open, and Marisol stands in it with spray frozen in her hair and her face the color of the deep-water coral she used to hate wearing.

*Lights on the headland,* she says. *Crown lanterns, moving in formation. They found the smugglers' beach, and they're asking the villagers for a taster, a tide-daughter, and a smith by name.* She swallows. *By our names. Someone told the regent exactly where we're going.*

And I feel Ansel's heart, the heart I am borrowing tonight, begin to beat faster — and beneath the fear, cold and clear as the stone keeping its count, the understanding:

The regent is not trying to stop us from reaching the covenant.

He is trying to reach it first.
