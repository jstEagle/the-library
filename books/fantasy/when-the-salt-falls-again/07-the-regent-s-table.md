## 7. The Regent's Table

They took Ansel's knives first. Then the copper charm sewn into the collar of their coat, then the one braided into their hair, then the little tin of salt they had carried so long the tin wore a thumb-shaped shine in its patina. The queen's soldiers were thorough and silent, the way soldiers are when they have been told their prisoner is dangerous, and Ansel let them take everything with the calm of a person who has been searched before and knows the truth: the real charms were not in the pockets. They were in the hands. In the tongue. In twenty years of small, outlawed, deliberate practice.

Still, they played the part. They stumbled when shoved, wept a little — not performance, exactly; fear was honest even when it was useful — and kept their eyes down as they were marched through corridors that smelled of beeswax and old ambition, up stairs Ansel had cleaned fish beneath as an apprentice, past kitchens where no one looked at them because no one ever looked at a taster.

The regent dined in the Hall of Glass Meridians, where the light of a hundred candles fractured through panes of sea-colored glass and fell across the long table like drowned sunlight. Twenty years ago this hall had hung with banners from every coastal province. Now the walls were bare except for one thing: a frame of black iron, and inside it, pinned like a moth, a sheet of pale parchment that hummed faintly against the air.

Ansel knew what it was before they let themselves know it. Covenant-ash pressed into vellum. The broken treaty itself, rendered into an object the crown could own — could *edit*.

At the head of the table sat Lord Regent Cassian Vael, and he had not aged the way powerful men were supposed to age. He had grown thinner, sharper, more like the glass around him — as if the years had polished him down to his intentions.

"Taster Zhou." He did not look up from his cup. "Sit. Eat. You've had a long journey, I'm told, though you made it very inconvenient for my riders."

"The marsh road," Ansel said, taking the chair indicated at the regent's right hand. "It's flooded this time of year."

"It was flooded on purpose. By us. To slow you." A thin smile. "You'll forgive the discourtesy. We wanted you rested for dinner."

The first course arrived carried by servants who would not meet Ansel's eyes: a cold soup of green things, a breast of quail glazed dark, bread still warm from an oven Ansel could smell from here — rosemary, and under it something wrong. Something mineral and vast and grieving, like licking a cliff face in a storm.

Covenant-ash. Baked into the bread, stirred through the glaze, dusted over everything on the table like flour. Not enough to kill a body. Enough, if the stories were true, to kill a *choice* — to bind anyone who ate it to the crown's blood-oath, so that when Vael signed himself as sole inheritor of the covenant, every fed mouth in the realm would nod along like puppets.

And Ansel's job — the oldest job they had — was to taste it first.

"Your Majesty," Ansel said carefully — *my lord regent*, correcting silently; Vael liked being called majesty, which was answer enough about him — "I should warn you. My trade has left me with certain immunities. Poisons pass through me like rain through sand. If your cooks have prepared something meant to be tested, I may taste it accurately without suffering it."

"I am counting on it." Vael lifted his wine. "That is precisely why you're alive, why your friends in the workhouse are alive, why the smuggler girl's boat wasn't burned at anchor. I need a taster whose tongue can tell me whether the binding took. Eat, Ansel. Everything. And then tell me if it worked."

So that was the shape of the trap. Not the banquet — the *question after*. Eat the ash, survive it by the grace of two decades of swallowed poison, and then be asked to report on their own enslavement.

Ansel picked up the spoon.

*You are going to lie to him,* said Wren's voice inside their skull, faint as breath on a winter window. She had been riding quiet since the prison break, conserving herself, but now she gathered at the back of Ansel's mind like a listener leaning close. *You cannot. He'll see it.*

*I'm not going to lie,* Ansel answered, and took the first mouthful of soup.

The ash tasted exactly as they remembered from the wards' collapse — starless night given texture, a void you could chew. But underneath it ran the second flavor, the one only a taster would catch: a binding-spell's signature, sweetish and metallic, threaded through every dish with a cook's patience. It was good work. It was, Ansel thought with professional sorrow, better than Odile's early ward-work, which meant someone had taught the palace cooks from Odile's stolen notes.

Ansel ate. All of it. Soup, quail, bread, a honeyed pear, a small cup of broth that tasted of nothing at all, which frightened them more than anything else on the table. Around them, the court ate too — lords and ladies and officers, all of them chewing down their own futures without knowing, laughing at jokes that would stop being funny the moment the oath sealed.

Vael watched Ansel the whole time. When the last plate was cleared he set down his napkin, folded his hands, and asked, in the gentle voice of a man asking after a child's fever:

"Tell me, taster. Do you feel bound?"

*Now,* Wren whispered.

Ansel reached for the wine.

* * *

There is a kind of magic that never appears in any ledger because it is made of nothing but repetition. Twenty years of tasting meant twenty years of knowing — of holding a flavor in the mind's exact grip, of separating one thread from ten thousand. Every charm Ansel had ever sold in the night market had begun the same way: with a taste remembered perfectly and offered freely. That was the secret the purges never understood. Small magic wasn't stored in tin charms or sewn hems. It was stored in *attention*. And attention, unlike knives and salt-tins, passes entirely unnoticed through the hands of a servant pouring wine.

Ansel poured for the regent with the steady wrist of twenty years' service, and into the pour — into the falling line of dark red between bottle and cup — they spoke the only spell they had ever made that mattered. Not a curse. Not a ward. A *revelation*: every palate in the hall would taste, for one swallow, the absolute truth of what they had eaten. Not the food. The intent.

Wren spent nearly everything she had left to hold the moment steady. Ansel felt her flicker, felt her grip on the hall's hundred senses like fingers tightening on a rope, and heard her say, almost laughing, *worth it,* and then—

Vael drank.

Around the table, sixty throats swallowed in near-unison, and sixty faces changed.

It began with the youngest lord, a boy of perhaps nineteen, who went grey and put both hands flat on the table as if the room had tilted. Then the lady beside him made a sound like a hiccup and covered her mouth. Then the officers — soldiers know the taste of betrayal faster than courtiers do; they've been fed it before. One by one, the Hall of Glass Meridians filled with people tasting covenant-ash and binding-sweetness on their own tongues, remembering their own swallowing, understanding in a single vertiginous instant what had been done to them while they complimented the cook.

"What—" Vael stood. His own cup was still in his hand. Whatever he tasted in it, it was not what his guests tasted; Ansel had written the charm narrowly. For the regent, the wine showed only itself: honest, ordinary, blameless wine.

Which was, of course, the point.

"The soup," said the boy-lord, hoarsely. "There's something in the— there's *ash*, there's oath-ash in the food, I can taste the *treaty*, I can taste the *coast*, gods, I can taste the whole drowned shore—"

"You've been poisoned," someone else said, and then louder, standing: "We've ALL been poisoned. This is a binding. This is a BLOOD BINDING—"

"Lies," Vael said, but his voice cracked on the word like thin ice, and Ansel watched the hall watch it crack. "Taster. Tell them. You tasted everything. Was there anything in the meal?"

And here was the beautiful, terrible arithmetic of it: Ansel had indeed tasted everything. Ansel's immunity was real, earned in sweat and terror across two decades, and it meant that when they opened their mouth to answer, their tongue carried no ash, no sweetness, no binding at all. Nothing but the plain, unimpeachable truth.

"No, my lord," Ansel said clearly, into a silence you could have bottled and sold. "Nothing passed my lips that bound or harmed me. Which is strange, isn't it? Since everyone else at this table just tasted a covenant-binding in their wine."

The hall erupted. Not into panic — into *certainty*. Sixty palates could not be wrong; one taster's clean tongue proved the meal was poisoned for everyone but the one person whose testimony the regent demanded; therefore the poison was meant for them, and the taster was proof, planted or spared, and either way the regent had questions to answer that he could not survive.

Vael looked at Ansel then — really looked, the way he must have looked at ledger entries revealing embezzlement, at maps showing enemy movements — and Ansel met his eyes and understood that he knew. Not everything. But enough. Enough to know the servant at his right hand had turned the whole apparatus of his power against him, using nothing but a bottle, a wrist, and twenty years of paying attention.

"You," he said softly.

"A taster's whole trade," Ansel said, rising, "is noticing what powerful people put in other people's food." They set their napkin beside their untouched-by-choice place setting, a courtesy so mundane it was nearly an insult. "My lord regent, I'd recommend against having me killed tonight. In front of sixty witnesses who believe I'm the only innocent at this table."

Guards were moving. Courtiers were shouting. Somewhere a glass shattered and the sound ran up the meridian panes like fire. Through it all, Wren's presence in Ansel's mind was fading fast, guttering like a candle in a doorway, and her last clear thought before she drew out of reach was not triumph but urgency, sharp as a fishhook:

*He kept the covenant-stone's map in the vault under this floor, Ansel. Under our feet. Right now. And if his guards burn the archive before we—*

The great doors of the hall boomed open behind them, and Ansel turned, expecting more soldiers.

It was Marisol, soaked to the thighs, a smuggler's cutlass in one hand and Odile Ferran — ruined hands wrapped in bandages, eyes blazing — standing in the doorway behind her, silhouetted against torchlight and, beyond it, through the high windows, the impossible sight of stars returning one by one to the sky above the capital.

"We got the map," Marisol called over the din, grinning like sin. "Now run."
