## 1. Charms for Ordinary Weather

The bell above the door of Blackwood Charms had a crack in it, and Elodie had never fixed it because the cracked note warned him who was coming before he looked up. Fishermen came in with a jangle and a salt-smell and no apology for their boots. The tax collector came in silent as a debt. And on this particular morning in the gray throat of autumn, the bell gave its broken half-ring, and Elodie knew without turning that it was Old Bramm Halloway, who had been buying luck-knots from his mother before Elodie was tall enough to see over the counter.

"Morning, charm-man." Bramm filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley. He was seventy if he was a day, hands like knotted rope, and he smelled of tar and herring and the particular despair of a man whose nets had come up empty three tides running.

"Bramm." Elodie set down the spool of red cord he'd been winding. "You look like a man who's lost an argument with the sea."

"The sea don't argue. It just takes." Bramm leaned on the counter hard enough to make the glass cases rattle. Their shelves were crowded with the harmless inventory of Elodie's trade: dried sea-lavender bundles for keeping moths from wool, painted pebbles for children's fevers, knotted cords in every color a superstitious heart could want. "I need a luck-knot. A strong one."

"They're all strong."

"Don't lie to me, boy. I've known you since you wet your breeches. Your mother used to sort them—strong ones for the desperate, gentle ones for the hopeful. You do the same. I can tell by how long you wind the cord."

Elodie smiled the smile he kept for exactly this kind of customer, the ones who watched too closely, and reached beneath the counter for the good cord—the deep crimson imported coil that cost him more than he'd ever admit. "Three winds or five?"

"Five. I'm not hopeful, I'm desperate."

"Five it is." And while Bramm talked about his grandson's apprenticeship and the price of lamp oil, Elodie's fingers did the work they'd done ten thousand times: loop, cross, draw tight, whisper. The whisper was nothing anyone could prove. A breath shaped into something older than words, a small asking directed at whatever patient power lived in the space between his ribs. *Hold. Keep. Turn misfortune aside.* The knot drank it the way dry rope drinks water, and when he tied off the fifth loop, the cord was warm in a way that cord should not be, and only Elodie would ever know.

He'd hidden the working inside the ritual of sale so thoroughly that even he sometimes forgot where the craft ended and the magic began. That was the point. In Saltmere, magic was not merely outlawed; it was *unfashionable*, which was worse, because fashion could be survived but unfashionable things got reported to the Magistrate's office by neighbors who wanted to be seen being virtuous. So Elodie sold charms the way a baker sold bread—openly, cheerfully, at fair prices—and every charm he sold was exactly what it appeared to be, except for the part that wasn't.

"That'll be two coppers," he said, sliding the knot across. "Wear it under your shirt. Don't thank me out loud—it embarrasses the luck."

Bramm grunted, paid, and tucked the knot away with the tenderness men like him reserved for living things. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back with eyes gone pale and watery as old pearls. "Your mother would've liked how you run this place. She'd have hated how quiet you keep it, though. She laughed loud, your mother. Whole street heard her."

"I remember," Elodie said, which was true, and which was also the only thing about her he remembered clearly anymore, and he did not examine why.

The bell gave its cracked note behind Bramm, and the shop settled back into its morning stillness.

* * *

Saltmere woke around him in stages, the way it always did. First the gulls, arguing over the fish-market scraps. Then the harbor bells marking the tide. Then the carts, the cries of vendors, the slap of water against pilings—a city built on stilts of commerce over a foundation nobody thought about, least of all Elodie, who thought about foundations more than any man alive and had made an art of never letting it show on his face.

He opened the shutters and let the light in. Morning in Saltmere was the color of pewter nine days out of ten, and today was no exception: low clouds dragging their bellies along the rooftops of Charm Row, the narrow street below already busy with the morning's small economies. The chandler across the way was sweeping her step. Two boys were racing paper boats down the gutter runoff. Somewhere a fiddle practiced the same eight bars over and over, badly, with the stubbornness of someone who intended to get them right if it killed them.

Elodie stood in his doorway with his cup of chicory and performed the exercise he performed every morning: he looked at his street until it looked ordinary back at him.

This was the discipline. Not the magic—that was easy, had always been easy, came to him like breathing through skin. The discipline was invisibility. Twenty years of being precisely unremarkable. The shop was cluttered but not strange. His prices were fair but not generous. He attended the Tide-Feast services at the Harbor Chapel and put exactly one copper in the offering, same as everyone. He was friendly to the constables and forgettable to the Magistrate's clerks. He had cultivated a personality the way other men cultivated beards: trimmed, tidy, impossible to describe to a stranger afterward.

*What's the charm-seller like? Oh—pleasant enough. Quiet. Sells a decent luck-knot.*

That was the whole architecture of his life, and most mornings he could live inside it without hearing the walls.

Most mornings.

He went back inside and began the day's real work, the work no customer ever saw. On the workbench at the back, behind a curtain of hanging lavender, lay the pieces of a dozen commissions: a birthing-charm for the midwife on Tanner's Lane, disguised as a decorative hair comb; a fever-breaker for the orphanage, worked into a painted spinning top because children couldn't be trusted to wear anything they weren't allowed to play with; a storm-turning for the pilot guild, sewn into the lining of a coat that would hang in their office looking like any other coat. Each one required hours. Each one required him to reach down into that quiet place and pull the working up through himself, and each time he did, he felt the faintest resistance, like drawing water from a well that was lower than it used to be.

He noticed that. He'd been noticing it for months. He told himself it was age, or tiredness, or the season.

He was very good at telling himself things.

* * *

Midmorning brought the fishermen, as it always did, a tide of them, big weathered men and sharp-eyed boys wanting knots for luck, knots for safe return, knots to keep the wife sweet until payday. Elodie sold them all, winding and whispering, and the cracked bell rang so often he stopped hearing it.

A girl of maybe twelve came in alone, which meant trouble, because girls of twelve didn't buy charms; they delivered messages or stole stock. This one bought nothing and stole nothing. She stood at the counter turning a painted fever-pebble over in her grimy hands and said, "My da says you're a fraud."

"Does he." Elodie kept winding cord. "And what does your da sell?"

"He doesn't say. He says your charms are just string and paint and people pay because they want to believe." She set the pebble down with great care, aligning it with the counter's edge. "But my brother had the fever last winter and Mam bought your top, and he played with it all night and by morning the fever broke, and Da says that would've happened anyway." She looked up. Her eyes were the flat gray of the harbor in November. "Did it happen anyway?"

Elodie held her gaze. Here was the question he'd answered a thousand ways in twenty years, to priests and skeptics and dying women, and here was the answer he always gave, because it was the only honest one:

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe. But your mam slept that night instead of sitting up terrified, and sleep is medicine too. If the string did nothing else, it did that."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and left without buying anything, and Elodie realized his hands had gone still on the cord and his heart was doing something complicated in his chest. Children asked the questions adults had learned to stop asking. *Is it real? Are you? What are you for?*

He wound the cord. He whispered. He did not answer himself.

* * *

The afternoon brought rain, thin and persistent, drumming on the shingles, and with it a lull that let Elodie do the accounting. Not the money accounting—he did that at night, quickly, because numbers bored him—but the other ledger, the one he kept nowhere and everywhere.

Two hundred and eleven charms sold this quarter. Fourteen workings strong enough to matter. One birth-charm that might have saved a child, or might not have. Three storm-turnings that had, he was fairly certain, bent weather off the coast, though proving such a thing was impossible and he preferred it that way. Against that: zero friends who knew him. Zero letters from anyone. Zero evenings in the past month spent anywhere but this shop, above this shop, or below—

His quill paused.

Below.

There it was again, the thought he steered around the way a cart driver steers around a pothole he's hit too many times. *Below.* The cellar. The inspection was due—three days overdue now, if he was honest, and he was honest with himself exactly once a month whether he wanted to be or not. The monthly walk down the stairs with the lantern and the iron key, the counting of seams, the listening at the door. Twenty years of monthly walks. His mother had done it before him, in the years he'd thought she was simply afraid of cellars, before she'd grown too ill to manage the stairs and had pressed the key into his hand with both of hers and said, in a voice scraped down to wire:

*"Once a month, Elodie. No matter what you hear. Especially no matter what you hear."*

He hadn't understood then. He understood now, mostly, and understanding had not made the stairs shorter.

The rain thickened. The fiddle across the street finally got its eight bars right and immediately ruined them by attempting a ninth. Elodie closed the ledger, and that was when the bell rang its cracked note and a woman came in out of the wet whom he didn't know, and Elodie Blackwood, who prided himself on knowing every face on Charm Row, felt the small cold finger of attention touch the back of his neck.

She was perhaps fifty, dressed well but travel-worn, hood dark with rain. She moved through the shop slowly, reading labels, touching nothing, and there was a quality to her attention that Elodie recognized the way prey recognizes a certain stillness in the grass. He'd seen it before, twice in twenty years. Once from a Magistrate's clerk who'd turned out to be nothing. Once from a man who'd turned out to be something, and who had left Saltmere very suddenly thereafter, and Elodie had never learned the whole story of that and had never wanted to.

"Good afternoon," he said pleasantly. "Looking for anything particular?"

"Curiosity, mostly." Her voice was low and unhurried. She lifted a bundle of sea-lavender, sniffed it, set it down. "I'm new to the city. I'm told Charm Row is where one comes for... protection."

"Protection against moths, fevers, bad luck, and mothers-in-law," Elodie said. "Though for the last one I can only offer sympathy."

She smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "And against the crown?"

The rain filled the pause. Elodie kept his hands loose on the counter and his face arranged in mild, shopkeeperly incomprehension. "Against the crown, madam, I'd recommend paying your taxes promptly."

"Mm." She drifted toward the door, then stopped, half-turned. "They say the old families of Saltmere had gifts. Before the bans. Sea-gifts, some of them. Weather-sense. Do you find that people still come in asking for those? Inherited things?"

"People come in asking for luck," Elodie said. "Luck is inherited enough for anyone."

For a moment she studied him with an expression he couldn't catalogue—something between appetite and grief—and then she thanked him for his time in a voice like a closing ledger and stepped out into the rain. Through the window he watched her go, unhurried, hood up, turning not down toward the harbor but up, toward the Magistrate's square.

He stood a long time in the empty shop.

Then he did what he always did after moments like that: he checked the cracks in his composure, found none, and got back to work. There was a birthing-comb to finish. There was cord to wind. There was a life of careful, invisible, airless quiet to maintain, and he maintained it, and the ache of it sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed so long ago he'd mistaken it for his own heart.

* * *

He closed at dusk, as the lamps were lit. He ate standing up, bread and cheese and pickled herring, and then he took the lantern and the iron key from the box beneath the floorboards of his bedroom, and he stood at the top of the cellar stairs for a long moment, listening.

The house made its night sounds. Settling wood. Rain easing off. The fiddle player had given up at last, and the silence where the music had been seemed louder than the music.

*No matter what you hear.*

He went down. The stairs were stone, worn hollow in the middle by his feet and his mother's feet and, he suspected, feet far older than either of theirs. The cellar at the bottom was not a normal cellar, though he'd furnished it to look like one: barrels of brine, crates of dried kelp for the charm-work, a rack of empty bottles. And at the far end, behind the barrels, a wall that was not a wall.

The door was iron, green with age, banded in bronze that no smith in living memory could have worked, and it fit its frame so perfectly that not even a knife blade could have found the seam. It had no handle on this side. It had no keyhole on this side. The key his mother had given him didn't unlock it; it did something else, something he performed once a month with his eyes closed and his breath held, pressing the cold iron into a depression worn smooth as a coin, and speaking—not words, exactly, but the shape of words, the warding his mother had taught him syllable by syllable in the last year of her life, her hands over his hands, her voice failing, her eyes fierce as a young woman's right up to the end.

He counted the seams. Dry. He checked the floor. Dry. He pressed the key into its depression and spoke the shape of the words, and felt the answering hum rise through the iron and up through his arm and settle somewhere behind his sternum, familiar as his own pulse.

Twenty years. Dry seams, steady hum, once a month, no matter what.

He turned to go.

And that was when he heard it—faint, muffled, coming through iron that had been silent for twenty years, through iron that had never once in his keeping made a sound:

Three slow taps. Like knuckles on a door. Like a greeting.

Like something patient on the other side, saying: *I know you're there. I've always known you're there.*

Elodie stood in the dark with the lantern shaking in his hand, and his first feeling was dread, cold and total, flooding him like seawater through a breached hull.

His second feeling arrived a heartbeat later, unwelcome as a stranger's smile, and it was recognition—and somewhere above him, in the waking world, the crown's criers were sharpening their bells for a proclamation that would burn his careful invisible life to ash before the week was out.
