## 2. The Proclamation

The bell began at dawn, and Elodie Blackwood knew before he opened his eyes that something had broken.

Not a church bell. Not the harbor bell that warned of fog. This was the King's Bell, the one mounted above the customs house, and it rang only for two things: war, and proclamations. Saltmere had not heard it in eleven years—not since the old king died and the new one was crowned with such promise of mildness. Elodie lay beneath his blanket and counted the strokes, because counting was what keepers did. Nine strokes. Then silence. Then nine again.

War would have been twelve.

He dressed in the dark without lighting a lamp—habit older than memory—and stood a moment at his window over Charm Row, watching the gray light come up on wet cobbles. The street below was already filling. People came out of their doors with shawls thrown over nightclothes, fishermen with their boots unlaced, the baker's boy still flour-white to the elbows. They moved the way water moves when it finds a crack in a wall: slowly at first, then all at once, pouring toward the customs house square where a platform had been erected sometime in the night.

Elodie watched them go and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear of a thing but from recognition of it. He had known this day would come. Every keeper knew. His mother had told him once, in one of her rare unguarded moments, stirring tea she never drank: *The crown forgives magic the way the sea forgives a seawall, love. It doesn't. It just waits.*

He closed his shutters, turned the sign on his door to CLOSED—a small mercy to himself; he would open by noon whether he wanted to or not, because a shop that stayed shut on proclamation day was a shop people remembered—and went down to make tea he also would not drink.

* * *

By the time he allowed himself into the square, the crowd had gone quiet in the way crowds do when they are afraid and don't want to admit it. The crier stood on the platform in royal blue, a narrow man with a voice trained to carry over storms. Beside him, two soldiers in the king's colors held a banner upright: black saltire on white, the mark of the newly chartered Office of Inquiry.

Elodie positioned himself where he always positioned himself—in the second rank of onlookers, close enough to seem concerned, far enough back to be no one in particular. Twenty years of practice lived in his feet. They found the right place without being asked.

The crier unfurled a scroll with a snap like a breaking bone.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Second, rightful King of Saltmere and the Salt Coast," he read, "and by counsel of the Lord Inquisitor Corvane, let it be known throughout the city and all lands under the crown: that the practice of the Gift—that is, witchcraft, charm-craft, hedge-magic, tide-work, and all arts of unnatural power—is hereby declared treason against God and King."

The words went out over the crowd and Elodie felt them land on people physically, the way cold rain lands. Somewhere behind him a woman made a small sound and stopped it.

"Any person found practicing, harboring, concealing, or profiting from the Gift shall be taken to trial before the Office of Inquiry. Those convicted shall suffer death by fire, publicly, as example." A pause, professionally measured. "Informants presenting verified reports shall receive compensation of ten crowns per conviction."

Ten crowns. Elodie heard the number move through the crowd like a current through still water—the almost imperceptible shift, the recalculation happening behind a hundred pairs of eyes. Ten crowns was half a year's wage for a fisherman. Ten crowns was a winter's coal. Ten crowns was a neighbor.

"The hunt begins in three days' time," the crier concluded. "In the name of the King, cleanse your streets. In the name of God, cleanse your hearts."

The banner snapped in the wind. Nobody cheered. That was the worst part, Elodie thought later—nobody cheered, and nobody left either. They stood there in the gray morning, several hundred ordinary people who owed each other money and lent each other tools, and you could feel the thing being born among them: the new arithmetic, the terrible new ledger in which every face had acquired a price.

Then the crowd broke apart, quietly, the way ice breaks—no single crack you could point to, just a general weakening, everyone drifting home along different lines than they'd come.

Elodie stayed a moment longer. On the platform's edge sat a man he hadn't noticed before: plain-dressed, soft-featured, with ink-stained fingers and the patient posture of someone prepared to wait years for something. He wasn't watching the crowd. He was writing in a small ledger, and every few moments he looked up, marked something, looked down again.

Their eyes met for exactly one second.

The man smiled—politely, briefly, the smile of a clerk acknowledging a customer—and returned to his book.

Elodie walked home with his heart beating very evenly, because he had trained it to.

* * *

Charm Row opened late that day, and wrong.

Old Sella from the rope-walk, who had bought a luck-knot from him every spring for fifteen years, came in and stood at the counter without quite meeting his eyes. She asked for a knot against rheumatism. Ordinary request. Harmless. But her hands were careful around the counter's edge, and when Elodie reached up for the twine she flinched—actually flinched—at the small flicker of light his fingers always carried when they worked, faint as heat-shimmer, invisible unless you were looking for it.

She was looking for it now. Everyone would be looking for it now.

"Three coppers," he said gently, tying the knot with exaggerated slowness, letting her see that it was only cord and patience. "And Sella—it's good luck, is all. Nothing more. You know me."

"I know you, Elodie." She paid, took the knot, and paused at the door with her back to him. For a moment he thought she might say something real—something from the woman who had once wept on this counter when her son drowned and Elodie had sat with her until dark, doing nothing magical at all, just sitting. But whatever it was, she folded it away. "You've always been fair with me," she said, and left, and did not look back.

That was the shape of it, then. That was the sentence his neighbors would serve him first: not betrayal, but distance. The slow withdrawal of the familiar.

Through the afternoon others came, and he catalogued their changes the way a doctor reads symptoms. The cooper Halvard, who'd bought a charm for his wife's breathing every winter since Elodie opened the shop—he kept his gloves on indoors and mentioned, twice, unprompted, how much he trusted the church's guidance in these matters. Young Pimm the errand-boy, who used to run messages for Elodie across the whole city, stood in the doorway a long time and finally said, too loudly, "Just checking if you needed anything, master Blackwood. From the market. Which I could get. Being as I'm going anyway." And then flushed and fled, and Elodie understood that Pimm had come to see if the shop was worth denouncing, or worth defending, and hadn't yet decided which side of ten crowns his loyalty weighed.

By dusk the street itself had changed. Lights burned later than usual in windows along the Row—people awake, talking low, deciding things. Twice Elodie saw neighbors stop in the street to speak to each other and fall silent when a third approached. Old certainties were being unwound and rewound into new shapes, and everyone could hear the loom working even through the walls.

He closed the shop and stood a long time in the dimness, surrounded by his inventory of harmless lies: knots and coins and painted stones, dried lavender, knucklebones, ribbons blessed by nobody. Two decades of craft built on a simple principle—if they never suspect you, they can never find you. And it had worked. It had worked so well that he had nearly forgotten it was a strategy and not a life.

His safety had never been his own. He saw that clearly now, standing among his little trinkets in the failing light. It had been *lent* to him—issued year by year out of his neighbors' trust, renewed by every honest transaction, every kindness done without expectation. And trust, it turned out, had a price on its head now. Ten crowns, payable on conviction.

The knock, when it came, nearly stopped his heart.

Three raps. Measured. Unhurried.

He composed his face into mild merchant puzzlement and opened the door on an evening gone violet-dark, and there in the street stood the soft-featured man from the platform, ledger under his arm, holding up a single coin between ink-stained fingers.

"Master Blackwood," the man said pleasantly. "I'm told you sell charms for ordinary weather. I wonder—" and here he tilted his head, courteous as a knife laid flat on a table, "—if you have anything for a long journey ahead."

Behind him, down the length of Charm Row, window after window went dark, one by one, like eyes closing.
