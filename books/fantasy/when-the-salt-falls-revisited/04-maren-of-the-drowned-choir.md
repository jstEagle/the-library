## 4. Maren of the Drowned Choir

The fish cart smelled of brine and old money, and Maren rode into Saltmere buried under three layers of silver herring, praying to gods the city had already forgotten.

She had been folded beneath the catch since before dawn, knees drawn to her chest in the dark of the cart bed while the driver—a broad-shouldered woman with forearms like mooring rope and no interest in conversation—cracked the whip over two tired mules. Every jolt drove a fin or a frozen tail into Maren's ribs. She didn't mind. Pain was honest. Pain meant she was still moving, still southward, still away from the burning.

*They came at low tide,* she kept thinking, though she had sworn to stop thinking it. *They came at low tide, and that was the insult of it.*

Nine days ago, the Drowned Choir's sanctuary on the Greysand coast had still been standing. Nine days ago, she had slept beneath the whalebone arch with eleven other priestesses, their voices braided through evening prayer, singing the tide up and singing it down. Then the crown's riders arrived with pitch and royal writ, and Mother Ostrya had pressed a wet palm against Maren's cheek and said, in the voice she used for naming things, "You're the youngest. The sea wastes nothing. Go."

Maren had gone. She had not looked back at the fire, because looking back would have meant seeing it, and seeing it would have meant carrying the exact shape of the flames for the rest of her life, and she needed what room remained in her for other things.

So now: Saltmere. The city rose out of the morning fog like something the sea had dreamed and then abandoned—grey stone, black timber, rooftops stacked crookedly up hillsides as if flung there by an indifferent hand. Gulls wheeled screaming over the harbor. Somewhere beyond the fish market, bells rang the hour, and the sound was wrong somehow, flattened, like a bell underwater. Maren noted that and filed it away. Everything here would need noting. Everything here might kill her.

"End of the line, girl," the driver said, without turning around. "Gutters' row is past the third arcade. Tell Old Sefa that Bren sent you and that I want my two coppers' worth of work out of you by noon."

Maren crawled out from under the herring, stiff and stinking, and dropped into the mud of the cart yard. Her legs barely held her. Above her, gulls screamed. Ahead of her, the city waited.

She touched the small bone charm at her throat—an old habit, half prayer, half lie—and walked into Saltmere.

* * *

Old Sefa was seventy if she was a day, missing three fingers on her left hand, and possessed of the flattest gaze Maren had ever seen on a living person. She circled Maren twice in the reeking gutting shed behind the fish market, prodded her shoulders, looked at her hands.

"Bren says you can work," Sefa said. It wasn't a question, exactly, but it wanted an answer.

"I can work."

"Says you keep your mouth shut."

"I keep my mouth shut."

Sefa grunted, which Maren would come to learn was the woman's entire emotional register, and thrust a short-bladed knife and a leather apron at her. "Silver herring first. Split from the spine, roe saved, heads to the left barrel. You cut yourself, you bleed down the drain and don't say nothing about it. You see anything strange in this city—and you will—you gut your fish and look at your knife. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Then work."

And Maren worked. She worked through the morning with her sleeves pushed to the elbow and her fingers going numb in the cold slurry, splitting silver herring until the motion became automatic and her mind could drift where it wanted to go, which was everywhere at once. Around her, a dozen other women and girls bent over their boards, and the shed filled with the rhythmic *shhk, shhk* of blades and the low river of gossip that ran through every workplace in every city in the world.

She listened. Listening was the first discipline of the Choir. You could not sing the tide if you could not hear it.

"—burned the hedge-witch at Millbrook, they say, right in her own garden—"

"—the criers read it in Copper Square yesterday. Gift-Hunt, they're calling it. Anyone found with the gift, tried and burned, no exceptions—"

"—my cousin's boy had the sight, could find lost things, sweet child, and now Marta swears he's never once said so aloud, but who believes a mother—"

"—Vane's coming north, that's what I heard. The Inquisitor himself. Quiet man, they say. Quiet like ice is quiet."

*Shhk, shhk.* Maren split another herring and let the fear move through her the way the Choir had taught her to let everything move through: acknowledged, honored, released. She had known about the purge, of course. The purge was why she existed in this disguise at all, why her hair was cropped short and rubbed with ash, why the bone charm at her throat was hidden beneath a gutter-girl's rough shirt. But knowing the hunt existed and feeling its shadow fall across a whole city's talk were different things. Here, magic wasn't just hunted. It was being unspoken. Erased from gossip, from memory, from the future.

Her people had sung to the tide for nine hundred years. Now the tide-priests were smoke on the Greysand wind, and the crown was hunting anyone who could do so much as warm water with a word.

*Mother Ostrya, what am I even doing here?*

But she knew. Of course she knew. She had known since the night the sanctuary burned, when the oldest of them, blind Sister Halle, had gripped Maren's wrist hard enough to bruise and whispered words she'd been holding onto for sixty years:

*There is a door in Saltmere, child. There has always been a door. And it is opening.*

* * *

It happened in the early afternoon, on her way to dump the offal barrels.

Later, Maren would try to describe it and fail, because there was no proper word for what it felt like—not hearing, not seeing, none of the senses she had names for. It was closer to the moment you step off a boat after weeks at sea and the land seems to sway beneath you, except inverted, inside-out: the ground itself had a rhythm, and the rhythm was wrong.

She stopped in the middle of the drain-lane, barrels slopping grey water over her boots, and listened with the listening that wasn't listening.

Under the noise of the city—the carts, the gulls, the hawkers crying oysters and eel-pies, the flat wrong bells—there was something else. Something deep. A pulse, slow and immense, like a heart beating through miles of stone. And threaded through that pulse, faint as a hairline crack in glass, came a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat.

Singing. Or the ghost of singing. Or the memory of what singing was, worn thin by centuries of pressure, leaking upward through the foundations of Saltmere like blood through a bandage.

Maren stood very still. A porter shouldered past her, cursing. She didn't hear him.

Because she knew that song. Not the words—the words were gone, taken, that was the whole wound of her people—but the *melody*. Every Tidewrought-touched child learned it in the cradle without ever being taught: the Lament of the Returned, the song the exiled dead were said to sing forever on the far side of the world's oldest door. The priests called it the Drowned Choir's founding grief. They taught that the door had been sealed since the last war, sealed by human keepers, sealed shut and silent.

Silent. But this wasn't silence. This was leakage. This was a seam giving way somewhere under her feet, and the song coming through it like light through a keyhole.

*Oh,* she thought, and her eyes stung suddenly, absurdly, in front of the whole fish market. *Oh, it's true. It's true, and it's dying.*

Because that was the thing, the terrible thing, the thing that turned her tears to iron almost as fast as they came: the song was weak. Fainter than any echo had a right to be after only twenty years of sealing. Whatever lay behind that door was fading—thinning out, dissolving, the way the dead dissolve when no one sings their names.

Her people were already nearly gone. The purge on the coast was killing the singers. And the sung-for, the ones beyond the door, were fading in the dark with no one left alive who remembered how to call them home.

Except.

Except there was a door under this city, and it was leaking, and leaks could be widened.

Maren set down the barrels. She tipped her face up to the pale slice of sky between the rooftops, breathed the cold fish-smelling air, and laughed—one short, cracked sound that made a passing urchin stare. Hope hit her like a wave breaking over a seawall, sudden and total, and she let it take her for exactly three breaths before she made herself be practical again, because hope without a plan was just drowning with your arms open.

Find the door. Find whoever kept it. Do it before the crown did.

* * *

She spent four days learning the shape of Saltmere from the inside, and the city taught her in the way cities do—through her feet, her shoulders, the ache of stairs climbed and alleys navigated.

By day she gutted fish for Old Sefa and said nothing to anyone. By evening she walked. She walked the harbor ring and counted the moorings. She walked the terraces of Charm Row, where shopfronts displayed luck-knots and weather-charms and painted signs promised nothing more dangerous than good fortune, and she noted—with the grim attention of someone memorizing a battlefield—that half those trinkets carried real working inside them. She could feel it, faint heat behind cheap glass, like candles hidden in cupboards. A whole district of small magics, all of them pretending, all of them now one proclamation away from kindling.

But always, underneath everything, the pulse. The leak. Each day she found it slightly stronger, the seam widening, the ghost-song a shade less ghostly. And each day she mapped its strength against the streets above, walking slowly with her senses open, letting the depth of the pulse guide her like a divining rod.

The pattern emerged on the fourth night, and it frightened her worse than the leak itself.

The strongest readings did not sit under some ruin or forgotten quarter. They sat beneath the prosperous streets. Beneath the counting-houses. Beneath, if her feet weren't lying to her, the very foundations of the city's oldest district—the part of Saltmere the tour-guides called the Old Bones, where merchant families had lived for generations in houses with brass knockers and clean-swept steps. The door wasn't hidden under the city. The city was built *on top of it*, deliberately, densely, the way a body is laid over a grave.

Someone had known. Generations of someones had known. And instead of marking the place, warning it, warding it—they had built a city over it like a cork in a bottle, and then, apparently, forgotten why.

"No," Maren murmured to the empty street, to the cobblestones humming faintly under her boots. "Not forgotten. *Made* to forget." Because that was a working too; she could feel its edges, old and smooth with use, a forgetting woven so deeply into the stones that even the weavers' descendants had unraveled into ignorance.

She stood in the dark lane with the fog rolling in off the harbor, and she pressed her palm flat against a wall, and she asked the city the only question that mattered.

*Where.*

The pulse answered—not in words, never in words, but in weight. South and down. Toward a crooked lane where shop-signs hung close together and one of them, she'd noticed days ago, sold charms.

* * *

On the fifth morning, she saw him.

She was hauling her offal barrel down the drain-lane at midday when a man came out of the narrow shop at the lane's end—a tall, careful man with dark skin and grey threading his temples, carrying a crate of trinkets as though it were made of eggshell. He moved like a man conscious of being watched, which in Saltmere now meant he moved like everyone. But it wasn't his manner that stopped Maren's breath.

It was the pulse.

When the shop door opened, the leak-song surged—just for a heartbeat, just a swell, the way a candle flares when a draft finds it—and Maren felt the melody bend *around him*, recognize him, lean toward him the way grass leans toward an open gate.

He was the keeper. Or he was the keeper's key. Either way, the man selling luck-knots out of the crooked little shop at the end of the drain-lane was the hinge on which every life she loved would swing or break.

Maren gripped her barrel handle and forced her face into the dull blankness of a gutter-girl with nowhere to think about. She watched him set the crate down, watch him glance up the lane, then down it, then go back inside and pull the door shut with a soft, precise click.

*He doesn't know it's failing,* she realized. *He checks it, I'd wager—he's the checking sort—but he doesn't know how fast it's failing now, or why, or what's bleeding through.* And why would he? The keepers had been made to forget too, hadn't they? Layer under layer of forgetting, seals over seals, a prison disguised as a city guarded by a man disguised as a shopkeeper, and all of it—every lock, every lie, every carefully harmless charm—beginning, right now, to rot.

She should approach him. Tonight, tomorrow, soon. She should walk into that shop and say the words Sister Halle had given her, the old words of challenge and greeting that no keeper alive had heard spoken in twenty years, and see what woke in his face.

But Vane was coming north. The marked coin, the arrests, the burnings—those were already beginning; she'd heard the women whispering about it in the shed that very morning. Time was a tide going out, and she knew better than anyone what got stranded when the water withdrew.

Maren lifted the barrel onto her shoulder and walked back toward the shed, unhurried, invisible, her heart hammering out a rhythm that matched, beat for beat, the slow pulse under the stones.

Tonight, she told the city silently, the way she'd once prayed. Tonight I find the door. Tomorrow I find the keeper. And someday soon—soon, please, whatever drowned gods are left to ask—I'm going to open it wide enough to sing you all home.

Behind her, deep beneath five layers of stone and a hundred years of lies, something heard her.

Three slow taps echoed up through the drains, soft as a knuckle on wood.

Maren froze in the middle of the lane. The offal water slopped over her boots. She turned her head slowly, staring down at the grate at her feet, and the singing below seemed to hold its breath, waiting.

It knew she was here.

Somehow, impossibly, it knew.
