## 5. Inquisitor Vane Pays a Call

The bell above the door had a voice Elodie knew the way a man knows his own heartbeat. The dull clank of a fishwife's elbow. The bright double-tap of children sneaking in to gawk at the luck-knots. The hesitant half-ring of someone who wanted magic but was afraid to ask for it.

At a quarter past noon on a grey Tuesday, the bell rang in a way he had never heard before: once, softly, deliberately, as though the person entering wished to announce himself without disturbing anything.

Elodie looked up from his workbench, where he was threading a hair-fine copper wire through a dried sea-lavender bloom, and saw a tall man standing just inside the doorway, letting the shop arrange itself around him.

He wore no uniform. That was the first thing Elodie noticed, and the second thing, immediately after, was that the absence of a uniform was itself a kind of costume. Grey wool coat, well-cut but not fine. Boots polished to a modest shine. A face that would not be remembered in a crowd—middling years, middling coloring, a jaw neither strong nor weak—and eyes that were doing all the work of the man. They moved across the shop with the unhurried thoroughness of an appraiser at an estate sale, touching nothing, missing nothing.

*Inquisitor,* Elodie thought, with the strange calm that twenty years of concealment had taught him. *They've finally come.*

"Good day," the man said. His voice was soft. Not whispering-soft—the softness of a man who had learned that quiet made people lean toward him, and leaning people told him things. "I'm told this is the finest charm-shop in Saltmere."

"I'm told that too," Elodie said, smiling, setting down his wire. "Though I suspect the teller owed me money."

A small laugh from the man, warm and easy, and Elodie hated how good it was. How practiced. He knew practiced when he heard it; he'd been practicing his own laugh for two decades.

"I'm looking for something for my sister," the man said, drifting along the shelf of weather-charms. "She's anxious by nature. Sleeps badly. Wakes at every sound." He picked up a knotted cord—a genuine trinket, no more than braided flax and a river stone, the kind of harmless thing Elodie sold by the dozen. "Would this help?"

"It helps some people," Elodie said. "It's a settling-knot. Braided on a calm day, which—" he glanced out the window at the flat grey light, "—wasn't today, so I'll be honest with you: it's a fair knot, not a blessed one. For sleeplessness I'd add this." He reached under the counter and produced a small sachet of dried chamomile and salt-meadow grass. "Steep it. If she believes the knot works, it will work better. Belief is most of any charm, and I say that as a man whose livelihood depends on the other part."

"The other part being?"

"Craft," Elodie said pleasantly. "Knots tied tight hold better than knots tied loose. Same as promises."

The man turned the cord over in his long fingers. His nails were clean and trimmed short. His hands were still. Elodie had spent twenty years reading hands—fishermen's hands, widow's hands, the trembling hands of people who came to him desperate and the steady hands of people who came to him dangerous—and these were the steadiest he had ever seen.

"My name is Vane," the man said. "Inquisitor Vane." He said it the way another man might have said *I'm your new neighbor*, mild as milk. "I should mention that, since we're likely to speak again, and I dislike the appearance of deception."

"And I'm Elodie Blackwood. Proprietor, knot-tier, and occasional dispenser of honest disappointment." He wrapped the sachet and the cord together in brown paper. "Four coppers."

Vane paid. He counted the coins out one at a time onto the counter, each placed rather than dropped, and Elodie found himself watching the man's face instead of the money, because watching the money would have been too obvious, and because Vane's face while paying was the face of a man memorizing a room.

"There," Vane said. "Now that business is concluded, perhaps you'll allow me to impose on your hospitality a moment longer. I find shops like yours are the best places to understand a city. Everyone passes through a charm-seller's door eventually. Grief, hope, spite—it all comes through, doesn't it?"

"Some of it," Elodie said. "Mostly they come for luck with fish and luck with love, in roughly equal measure, and both are disappointed at similar rates."

Another warm laugh. Vane settled onto the stool by the window uninvited, arranging himself as though he'd been offered the seat, and Elodie understood the move for what it was: a demonstration. *I take what space I wish. You will not object.*

He objected internally, at length, and poured the man tea.

* * *

"You've been here a long time," Vane said, cradling the cup. "Twenty years, the tax rolls say. That's a long time for one trade in one city."

"Saltmere suits me. Damp air's good for the sinuses and bad for ambition."

"And before Saltmere?"

"Before Saltmere there was a village so small it doesn't appear on maps, which suited it fine." True, as far as it went. The village had been called Brackhollow, and it did not appear on maps because it had been drowned on purpose, along with everyone in it who could work the tide-gift, including a woman named Sera Blackwood who had walked into the sea carrying a secret the size of a door. But Vane didn't need the true part. The true parts were like the real charms: hidden inside harmless ones.

"A mapmaker's answer," Vane observed mildly. "You think in borders."

"I think in customers. Borders don't buy luck-knots."

Vane smiled and sipped his tea. For a while he simply looked around the shop, and Elodie let him look, keeping his own breathing slow and his heart-rate down through an old discipline his mother had taught him before he was old enough to know it was a discipline: *when the hunter comes, be furniture. Furniture has nothing to hide.*

But Vane was not a man one could be furniture around. His gaze snagged on things. On the iron key hanging on its nail behind the counter—mother's key, disguised among a dozen decorative keys bought for exactly this purpose. On the cellar door, visible through the back archway. On the faint tideline stain along the baseboard that Elodie had scrubbed twice since the leaks began and which had come back both times, patient as a confession.

"Old families," Vane said suddenly, conversationally. "That's what I'm in the city to ask about. Old families of Saltmere. There's a pattern in the Gift-Hunt records, you see. The gift runs in lines, like a bloodline or a curse. It pools. Generation to generation." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "The criers talk about catching practitioners in the act. Fire and spectacle. But the real work is genealogy. Quiet work. Ledgers and parish rolls and grandmothers' gossip." He looked up, and his eyes were perfectly friendly and perfectly empty. "Do you know anything about old families, Mr. Blackwood?"

"I know the Harrow family still owes me for three storm-charms they never collected," Elodie said. "And the Vancelets, before their name got mangled into yours, kept a fishing boat called the Lucky Widow that sank twice, which suggests they overpaid for whatever they bought." He shrugged. "Old families mostly owe me money, Inquisitor. Is that the pattern you're hunting?"

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes—not anger. Interest. The interest of a man who has set out a hundred traps and finally felt one twitch.

"You lie well," Vane said.

The words landed in the shop like a dropped glass. Elodie kept his face pleasant, his hands loose on the counter, and gave the silence exactly as long as it deserved—one breath, no more—before answering.

"That's the second-worst thing anyone's said to me this week. What was the first, you're wondering? A woman told me my storm-charms were 'adequate.' I nearly closed the shop."

Vane laughed again, and this time Elodie could hear the machinery in it, gears and patience, and he understood that the accusation had been deliberate—not a trap but a test of recovery time. He had recovered fast. He noted, with cold professional respect, that Vane would have logged that too.

"I meant it as a compliment," Vane said. "Most people lie poorly. They over-explain. They fill silences." He rose, unhurried, and drifted toward the shelf of luck-knots again. "You fill silences with jokes, which is different. Jokes are load-bearing." He touched a knot, straightened a jar of sea-glass, and paused at the display of protective wards—horse-shoe charms, threshold-braids, the little iron bells Elodie sold to nervous households. "These are popular lately."

"Fear is good for business. I'd rather it weren't."

"Would you?" Vane asked, without heat, as though asking about the weather. "Fear keeps a man like you fed. And yet—" he lifted one of the threshold-braids, examined its weave with fingers that knew knots, knew them far better than a customer should, "—some of your work is very fine, Mr. Blackwood. Finer than it needs to be. This braid has nine strands. Nine is a tide-number. Old work. Coastal order work, the priests used to tie nine-strand braids before the crown discouraged the practice." He set it down gently, aligning it with the others. "Where did you learn that?"

"My mother," Elodie said. "She fished. Fishermen's knots run deep—you learn a hundred before you're ten and forget none of them. Nine-strand braid holds a net-closure. Nothing mystical about it."

"No," Vane agreed softly. "Nothing mystical about it at all."

He wandered once more along the shelves, and Elodie watched him the way a man watches a slow tide come up a beach, noting where each wave reaches, knowing the water is not going to stop until it has covered everything. Vane looked at the ceiling beams. At the floorboards. At the back archway and the cellar door beyond it, and stayed looking at the cellar door perhaps four seconds longer than curiosity required, and then turned back smiling.

"Well. I've taken enough of your morning." He shook Elodie's hand—dry, firm, brief. "Thank you for the charm. My sister will be pleased, whether or not it works. She believes in craftsmanship." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and glanced back with an expression of almost gentle apology. "One last question, purely for my ledger. Your family—Blackwood, you said. Any connection to the Blackwoods of the old harbor district? The charts call it the Foundry Quarter now, but it had another name once."

"I couldn't say. We were inland folk. Water makes me nervous."

"Mm," said Vane. "It does that to people who know too much about it."

The bell rang once, softly, as he left.

* * *

Elodie stood motionless for a full minute after the door closed. Then he moved, quickly and quietly, doing the things that twenty years had drilled into his bones: he checked the street through the gap in the shutters—Vane walking away at an even pace, pausing to speak to the pie-seller, laughing at something, buying nothing. He checked the tea cup—untouched past the first sip, or perhaps untouched entirely; he hadn't watched closely enough, and hated himself for it. He checked the coins on the counter.

Three coppers and one silver piece.

He had charged four coppers.

The silver sat among them innocently, slightly larger, slightly brighter, minted with the crowned profile of the young king. Elodie did not touch it at first. He leaned close instead, the way his mother had taught him to lean over water before trusting it, and listened.

Nothing. No hum. No whisper. Just a coin.

But he had heard the stories from the coastal towns ahead of the purge—stories the tide-priests used to tell, back when there were tide-priests left to tell them. The Inquisition minted them in the south. Blessed silver, or cursed, depending on which side of the fire you stood. Coins that slept quietly in a pocket until magic woke nearby—and then they sang. A coin left behind in a charm-shop was not payment. It was a listening ear. It was a hook left baited in dark water.

*We are likely to speak again,* Vane had said. *I dislike the appearance of deception.*

Elodie fetched a pair of wooden tongs from the workbench, took the coin to the iron strongbox beneath the floorboards—buried in ash and old iron filings that had muted worse things than silver—and locked it away. Then he sat back on his stool and made himself think, because panic was a luxury and he had never been able to afford it.

Vane knew about nine-strand braids. Vane knew the old name of the harbor district. Vane had looked at the cellar door and counted seconds. None of that was proof. All of it was thread, and men like Vane did not need proof—they needed only enough thread, patiently gathered, to weave a rope.

And somewhere beneath Elodie's feet, behind a door sealed with his mother's memories, saltwater was seeping through seams that had held for twenty years. Every leak was a signature. Every failed ward was a sentence in a language the Inquisition had spent decades learning to read. If Vane stood in this shop on the wrong night—if the gate knocked loudly enough, if the wells ran salty enough, if the marked coin began to sing—

Elodie looked at the strongbox, then at the cellar door, then at the brown paper parcel Vane had left behind on the counter in his distraction—or had he left it in calculation? Inside lay the settling-knot and the sachet, paid for honestly, evidence of nothing.

Except that Vane had a sister who slept badly, and Inquisitors, in Elodie's experience, did not have sisters. Inquisitors had ledgers.

He untied the parcel with careful fingers and found, tucked between the knot and the sachet, a slip of paper in a small, precise hand:

*Thank you for the tea. The nine-strand braid was lovely. — V.*

Below it, a single line more:

*I'll return on Thursday. Do give some thought to old families in the meantime.*

Elodie read it twice. Then he opened the shop's front door, stepped out into the grey afternoon, and looked down the length of Charm Row—at the candle-dipper who charmed her wicks to burn steadier, at the locksmith whose keys remembered their doors, at old Nettie the herb-wife who had buried three husbands and knew more working cantrips than any priest—and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water rising, that he had until Thursday to decide which of his neighbors to warn.

And that every warning he gave would bring the rope one weave closer to his own neck.
