## 6. The Cartographer's Error

The map was wrong, and Tobias Wrenn had known it for eleven days before he let himself admit it.

He knew it the way a man knows a room has changed since childhood—not by any single detail, but by an accumulation of small wrongnesses that finally grew too heavy to ignore. He had been copying the Grand Survey of Saltmere for the third time in his career, a commission from the Office of Works, when his pen stopped above the harbor district and refused to move.

There it was again. The gap.

Officially, the charts called it the Old Salterns—a stretch of low ground east of Charm Row where the city's first salt-works had stood before the great fires, now given over to tumbledown warehouses and, according to every document he could find, nothing else. On the Grand Survey it was rendered in the palest wash of gray, as if the surveyor's hand had grown tired, or frightened. No streets named. No structures marked. Not even the little cartographic fictions mapmakers used to fill dead space: a clump of stylized trees, a decorative compass rose. Just gray, and at its center, so faint he'd needed a magnifier to confirm it wasn't an artifact of age, a small circle with no label at all.

Tobias had spent twenty-three years making maps for the crown. He knew what empty space looked like, and this wasn't it. Empty space had texture. Empty space got annotated—*marshy here*, *unstable footing*, *disused*. This space had been *emptied*. Someone had scraped the district off the parchment the way you'd scrape a mistake from vellum, carefully, thoroughly, and then washed the whole wound in gray so no one would think to look twice.

Someone had wanted old Saltmere forgotten.

He sat back in his chair in the rented room above the chandlery on Gull Street—the room he'd lived in for four years now, ever since the disgrace—and looked at the evidence spread across his worktable like a confession. The Grand Survey itself, three generations old, borrowed from the archive under a legitimate commission and due back within the fortnight. His own working copies, six of them, spanning two decades of royal cartography. And beneath those, weighted flat under a chipped inkstone, the thing that had started all of it: a scrap of pre-Survey charting he'd bought for two coppers from a rag-and-bone man who'd found it wadded inside a dead man's boot.

On that fragment, drawn in a hand older than the kingdom's current dynasty, the Old Salterns were not gray. They were *dense*. Streets upon streets, a grid as fine as lacework, a district larger than the Merchant Quarter. A church—or what the old symbol for one, a circle within a circle, suggested was a church. And at the center, where the modern charts held their blank gray circle, the ancient cartographer had written something Tobias could not read: a script that predated the common alphabet, looping and tidal, more like water than writing.

Eleven days. That was how long he'd told himself it was a curiosity. A footnote. The kind of discrepancy that filled the margins of every old document, because maps were made by men and men made mistakes.

But Tobias Wrenn did not make mistakes. That was the bitter joke of his life. He had been the most precise hand in the Royal Cartographic Office, the man they brought difficult commissions to, the man whose coastal charts had saved ships, whose boundary surveys had ended disputes without a sword drawn. Precision was the only thing he'd ever been praised for, and then it had become the only thing he'd been punished for, when he'd refused to sign his name to a boundary chart he knew had been falsified to favor a duke's land claim. *The map is wrong,* he'd told the Surveyor-General, standing in front of the whole office with his hat in his hands like a fool. *And I won't put my name on a lie.*

They had taken his post, his pension, and his access to the archives, and they had let him keep his skill the way you leave a man his hands after cutting out his tongue. Four years of copying other people's charts for private clients, work beneath his talent but honest, and through all of it one consolation had kept him upright: whatever else the crown did, whatever lies dukes told, the maps themselves remained true. Somewhere in the great vaults of the Office of Works lay charts that would outlast everyone alive, and someday, someone honest would read them and know where the lines truly ran.

Now he was looking at a hole in the truth, and the hole had been made deliberately, and it sat directly beneath the city where he slept.

* * *

He went back to the archives on a Tuesday, using the last favor he had left—a junior clerk named Pell who still owed him for teaching the boy to draw a proper coastline. Pell signed him in as a contracted copyist, which was even true, in its way; Tobias was copying, just not anything anyone had asked him to copy.

The archive annex smelled of dust and tallow and the particular cold of stone that never quite sees sun. Tobias moved through the stacks with the ease of long habit, pulling folios by feel, and began the slow, methodical work of tracing the gap backward through time.

It took him three days, working from memory of the catalog rather than the catalog itself, since his name had been struck from the readers' rolls. What he found was this:

The erasure was not recent. It was not even old. It was *original*. The very first Grand Survey, commissioned ninety years ago, already showed the Old Salterns as blank. He found the surveyor's field notes from that expedition—thin, nervous pages—and read them twice to be sure of what he saw. The surveyor, one Aldous Merrick, had recorded precise measurements for every district of Saltmere, down to the width of doorsteps. For the eastern salterns, his notes read, in full:

*Ground here refuses the chain. Instruments turn. Men will not remain past dusk. Recommend the district be omitted per prior instruction. Payment received in full.*

*Per prior instruction.* Omitted on purpose, then, before the ink of the kingdom's first great map had dried. And paid for. Tobias turned the page looking for what instruction, whose payment, and found the rest of the notebook torn away—razored cleanly at the spine, decades ago, by hands that had known exactly which pages mattered.

He sat in the cold aisle between the folio cases for a long while after that, listening to the tick of his own heart, and understood that he had crossed a line that could not be uncrossed. Curiosity was a private sin. This was something else. He now possessed knowledge that people had gone to trouble—expensive, careful, generational trouble—to suppress, and knowledge like that was never neutral. It was a stone lifted from a wall, and things lived behind walls for reasons, and some of those reasons had teeth.

But underneath the fear, quieter and more stubborn, sat the thing that had gotten him ruined once already: the plain, unglamorous conviction that a map owed the truth to whoever read it. The Old Salterns existed. People had built them, street by street, and something had made the crown afraid of them, and fear was not a legitimate reason to falsify a record. He had lost everything once for refusing a small lie. He found he could not bring himself to accept a large one, not even by silence.

* * *

He should have taken it to Maren—he didn't know her name yet, wouldn't for another week—but the thought of allies hadn't come to him yet. What came to him instead, that same evening, was simpler and far worse: he took it to the crown.

Not out of loyalty. Loyalty had left him at the boundary hearing, four years past. He went because it was procedure, and procedure was the last religion he practiced. Discrepancies in official cartography were reported to the Office of Works; the Office determined whether correction was warranted. That was the rule. He wrote his report himself, three fair-copy pages in his best hand, dry and factual as a tide table. He noted the erased district, the torn notebook, the phrase *per prior instruction.* He requested access to the restricted holdings to complete his investigation. He signed it *Tobias Wrenn, formerly Senior Cartographer, Royal Cartographic Office,* because hiding his name felt like one more falsification, and he was done with those.

Then he walked it to the Office of Works himself, through streets gone strange with new paper—proclamations nailed up everywhere, black letters on white, the Gift-Hunt edict repeated in every square. *Any person found to possess or practice the forbidden gift shall be tried and burned.* Crowds stood before the notices the way crowds stand before executions, which, he supposed, was what they were. He passed a charm-seller's shop on his route, one of the cluttered little places on the edge of Charm Row, and saw the owner—a thin man with careful eyes—taking down a display of knotted luck-charms with unhurried hands, as though he were merely rearranging. Something about the man's stillness stayed with Tobias longer than the proclamations did.

At the Office of Works, he handed his report to a reception clerk with a wax-stained doublet, who glanced at the signature and smiled the smile you give a beggar asking for alms in a language he doesn't speak.

"Formerly Senior Cartographer," the clerk read aloud, tasting the word *formerly* like gristle. "We'll have it reviewed."

"I'd appreciate acknowledgment of receipt," Tobias said. "In writing."

"Certainly." The clerk set the report facedown on a pile of others, where it immediately began to look like litter.

Tobias waited three days. On the fourth, instead of an acknowledgment, two men came to Gull Street.

They were not soldiers. Soldiers announced themselves. These two wore the plain dark clothes of clerks or undertakers, and they were polite in the way that made his skin crawl, and the elder of them asked, mildly, whether Master Wrenn might accompany them to discuss his report with a gentleman from the Inquisition who had expressed interest in matters of historical geography.

"Inquisition," Tobias repeated. "This is a matter for the Office of Works."

"The Office has referred it upward." The man's mildness never flickered. "Given the present climate, certain records are considered sensitive. You'll understand."

He understood. He understood with a clarity that arrived like cold water: he had carried his stone to the wall's builders, and they had not come to thank him. Every instinct said refuse, run, burn the copies in his grate and go back to copying harbor charts until his hands gave out. But the younger man was already drifting toward his worktable with unconcealable interest, eyes on the weighted papers there, and Tobias knew that refusing meant they simply took everything anyway, and him with it, and the truth went into a drawer next to the razored pages of Aldous Merrick's notebook.

So he went. He took his coat, and he locked his door behind him with hands that were almost steady, and he walked between the two mild men through the afternoon streets toward the Inquisition's chapter house, and the whole way he performed the arithmetic that would define the rest of his life: whatever happened next, he had done it. Whatever they built from his report—the searches, the seizures, whatever the erased district had to do with witches and burning—he had fetched the stone and handed it over with both hands, politely, in triplicate.

In a paneled room smelling of lamp oil and clean linen, a soft-spoken man with patient eyes received him, introduced himself as Inquisitor Vane, thanked him sincerely for his diligence, and began to ask questions about old Saltmere.

And Tobias Wrenn, good man, precise man, answered every one.
