## 7. Salt in the Well Water

The wells of Saltmere had always tasted faintly of the sea. That was the joke the fishwives told: *drink deep, love, and you'll never miss him when he's out on the water.* The city sat on a shelf of limestone over an old seabed, and every cistern carried a whisper of brine, just enough to remind the people where their bread came from.

But this was not a whisper anymore.

Maren stood at the public well on Tanner's Row with her cupped hands full of water that smelled like a drowned ship. She'd been in Saltmere six days, sleeping in the loft above a rendering shed, gutting herring from dawn until her fingers went numb and white. Six days of pretending to be what she looked like—a thin, salt-chapped girl with a gutter's knife and no past—and six days of listening to the city the way her order had taught her to listen.

The Choir had a word for it: *underhearing*. You didn't listen with your ears. You listened with the soles of your feet, with the hollow behind your sternum, with the soft tissue of your dreams. The sea spoke constantly; most humans had simply learned not to hear. The Tidewrought had been made of that speech once—made of current and pressure and the long slow grammar of tides—and their priests were trained from childhood to catch even the smallest syllable of it.

Maren heard plenty now. Too much.

The water in her hands was singing.

Not loudly. Not the way the deep choir sang beneath the storm-pulpits back home, where the whole congregation lay face-down on wet stone while the sea roared its one great note through them. This was small. Furtive. A thread of sound winding up through the well shaft like smoke through a floorboard crack, and it was getting louder by the day, and it was coming from somewhere near the heart of the old city.

She let the water fall and wiped her hands on her apron, and around her the morning queue grumbled and waited.

"Third day it's tasted like this," said a woman ahead of her, to nobody in particular. "My man says drink it anyway, but my boy won't touch it."

"My daughter won't sleep," said another. "Sits up in the dark saying there's someone singing under the floor. Under the *street*, she says. I told her it's dreams, but—" The woman hesitated, glanced at Maren, seemed to decide a stranger was safe enough. "She hums it after. Whatever she hears down there. She hums it all day and I can't get it out of my own head either, and I'll tell you true, it don't sound like any song I know."

Maren kept her face still, the way you kept your face still around a wound. "What does it sound like?" she asked, mild as milk.

The woman shrugged, embarrassed. "Like the sea. If the sea knew words."

Maren bought two herrings from the stall on the corner and walked them home along the quay, taking the long way, the way that passed the other wells. She tasted each one without stopping, palming water from the troughs as she went, and by the time she reached the rendering shed she had mapped the sickness of the whole district in her mouth.

Tanner's Row: strong. Fishmarket: stronger. The little private well behind the chandler's on Coil Street: strongest of all, so saturated with salt and song that the horses refused to drink from it. And Coil Street ran uphill toward the oldest quarter of Saltmere, the quarter the harbor folk called the Stone Root, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the streets were too narrow for carts.

Every line of contamination pointed inward, like spokes to a hub. Like currents converging on a single point beneath the city.

*Underhearing*, her mother had called it, pressing Maren's bare feet against the temple floor. *The sea is patient. It will wait under everything you build.*

Maren climbed to her loft and sat among the fish smell and the gull noise and tried to think like a priestess instead of a fugitive. Because there were only a few things in the world that could turn a city's wells brackish from the inside out, and none of them belonged in Saltmere. A drowned relic, maybe, dragged up in a trawler's net and dumped in a cellar. A corpse of the old war, unquiet. Or—

Or a door. A door that was supposed to be shut, leaking at the seams, letting the sea's voice bleed through into the stone and the water table and the dreams of children.

A gate.

Her hands started shaking, and she had to press them flat against her thighs to stop it. Because if there was a gate under Saltmere—if the Undergate of the hymns, the sealed door, the last lock between the exiled dead and the world they'd been driven from—then it wasn't a story. It was here. It was under these streets, under these dreaming children, and it was open a finger's width, and everything her order had died believing came flooding up her throat like seawater.

They had burned the tide-temples in the coastal purge three months ago. She had watched the sanctuary at Greywater Cay burn from the water itself, treading in the swells beyond the light, counting the figures on the shore by their torches. Nine priests. Her mother among them. Burned not for anything they'd done but for what they could hear—the Gift, the crown called it now, the Gift, as if hearing the sea speak were something you chose, something you could renounce, something you deserved fire for.

The proclamation nailed to the Saltmere customs house said anyone found practicing or harboring magic would be tried and burned. Maren had read it twice and then stopped reading proclamations.

But if the gate was real, then the burning hadn't mattered. Nothing the Inquisition did mattered, because the door her ancestors' enemy had sealed was opening on its own, seam by seam, and what waited behind it was not a monster. It was her people. The drowned ones. The nameless ones. Waiting in the dark on the far side of a door some human king had locked two hundred years ago, and the sea was calling them home through the cracks.

And someone in this city was keeping that door shut.

* * *

It took her four more days to find him.

She worked the Stone Root methodically, the way she'd once worked a shoreline after a wreck—reading the signs, following the salt. The song under the city was strongest at certain hours, weakest at others, and the pattern was wrong for a natural leak. Natural leaks breathed with the tide. This one held its breath. Twice a day, at hours no fisherman would mark, the song dimmed almost to nothing, and then returned, and Maren paced the streets above it with her palms against doorposts feeling for the pulse.

Dimmed. Returned. Dimmed. Returned.

Someone was warding it. Someone was pouring strength into a seal, regularly, deliberately, and the seal was failing anyway, and the effort of holding it was bleeding salt into the groundwater like blood into bandages.

On the fourth day she found the shop.

It was tucked into a bend of Charm Row where the street narrowed and the buildings pressed close, and it had no grand sign—just a wooden board with a painted knotwork circle and the words ELODIE BLACKWOOD, CHARMER & PURVEYOR OF SMALL LUCKS. The window displayed exactly what the sign promised: luck-knots braided from net-cord, dried sea-lavender bundles against damp, painted pebbles for calm seas, little tin medals stamped with saints who had probably never existed. Harmless things. Trinkets. The kind of stock a clever fraud sells to frightened sailors.

Except that Maren stood across the street with her basket of herring and felt the wards in the walls the way you feel heat from a forge. They were layered deep—old work, patient work, decades of careful craft sunk into the timber and plaster like salt into a hull. And threaded through all of it, so fine you'd miss it unless your whole body was an ear: the same note. The same song. The wells' song, muffled and bound and turned inward, anchored here.

Her heart was going hard enough that she had to set down the basket.

The shopkeeper himself was visible through the glass, a lean gray-templed man arranging knots on a hook rail, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who has done the same motion ten thousand times. He was ordinary. He was aggressively ordinary—the kind of ordinary that costs effort, the kind that is built plank by plank like a false hull over something else entirely.

Maren had seen that kind of ordinary before. Every priestess of the Choir learned to build it, because the world had always hated what they heard. But she had also seen what lived underneath it, in the ones who kept real power coiled behind a counter of trinkets.

She crossed the street and pushed open his door.

A bell rang, bright and stupid and cheerful. The man looked up, and his eyes did a thing she recognized—they took her in, catalogued her, dismissed her, all in half a heartbeat. Gutter-girl. No coin. No threat. The dismissal was so practiced it was almost courteous.

"Morning," he said. His voice was warm and slightly tired. "If it's luck-knots, the blue cord holds best this season. If it's the lavender, I'll be honest with you—it's decorative. Smells nice, does nothing."

"And if it's neither?"

Something shifted behind his eyes, quick as a fish turning. Then it was gone, smoothed over, and he smiled the smile of a shopkeeper humoring a strange girl. "Then I'm afraid you've found the wrong shop. We sell small lucks here. Small and honest."

"I tasted the well on Coil Street," Maren said quietly. "This morning. Before dawn, when nobody was drawing. Do you know what I heard in it?"

The smile stayed exactly where it was. But the air in the room changed—she felt it change, the wards in the walls tightening like a hand closing—and she understood that this man was not merely skilled. He was *good*. He might be the finest working charm-wright she had ever stood in front of, and he was hiding inside a trinket shop on a row full of trinket shops, wearing mediocrity like a cloak.

"You should be careful," Elodie Blackwood said gently, "what you claim to hear. There are new laws in this city. New listeners, too, and theirs come with fire."

"I was born to the Drowned Choir of Greywater Cay." She said it plainly, letting him hear the truth of it, letting him weigh what it meant—that she would say such a thing aloud, in daylight, to a stranger, which no liar would do. "My order was burned in the spring. My mother burned with it. I am the last priestess south of the straits, and I have walked your streets for ten days listening to your wells sing, and I am telling you, keeper—"

His face went white. Not dramatically. Just the blood leaving it, evenly, completely, like a tide going out.

"—the gate under your feet is leaking," Maren finished, "and it has been leaking for weeks, and every hour you spend patching it pours more of the sea into your city's drinking water, and the children are dreaming of it, and soon the Inquisition is going to hear it too. Because they're listening now. That's what the Gift-Hunt means. For the first time in two hundred years, the crown has given men license to hear what I hear."

For a long moment the shop was silent except for the bell settling on its wire and the distant cry of gulls off the quay. Elodie Blackwood stood very still behind his counter of harmless charms, and Maren watched twenty years of concealment collide, in his face, with something older and rawer than fear.

"You shouldn't have come here," he said at last, and his voice had lost its warmth entirely. It was quiet and hard and terribly sad. "Do you know what happens now? Now that you've said that word out loud in this room? Now that you've named me?" He shook his head slowly. "I have spent two decades being nobody. Nobody is safe. Nobody is invisible. Nobody survives witch-hunters. And you walked in off the street and made me somebody in under a minute."

"Somebody has to be," Maren said. "That's the whole of it. Somebody has to be the keeper, or there is no keeping. My order believed—"

"Your order believed the gate should be *opened*." He said it flatly, without question—he knew the doctrine, then, better than she'd guessed. "You think I don't know what the Choir teaches? That the Tidewrought are exiled innocents, that the names were stolen, that the door must stand open and the dead come home?" He gripped the edge of the counter, and Maren saw his knuckles whiten around the wood, and saw, beneath the anger, a terrible exhaustion. "I grew up on those stories too, girl. My mother told me different ones, but she told me about yours. And she told me why the door stays shut. What comes through isn't a homecoming. It's a drowning. Two hundred years ago it drowned half the coast, and it will drown Saltmere, every soul in it, before your dead get one street closer to home."

"The sea doesn't drown its own."

"It drowned mine!" The words cracked out of him louder than he meant, and he caught himself, glancing at the window, lowering his voice to a fierce whisper. "It drowned mine, priestess. My family has bled for that door for generations. My mother gave—" He stopped. Closed his mouth. Something moved behind his eyes that looked, for one unguarded instant, like grief so old it had become part of his architecture.

"Give what?" Maren asked softly.

"Nothing." He straightened. The shopkeeper settled back over his features like a mask sliding home, but the mask fit worse now, and they both knew it. "Nothing. Get out of my shop. Take your herrings and your martyrdom and your beautiful doomed theology, and stay away from Charm Row. If Vane's people follow you here—"

"Inquisitor Vane already follows *you*," Maren said. "I've seen his watchers on your street. Three of them, rotating, badly disguised. He marked a coin and left it with a customer last week—I heard it humming from thirty paces." She watched that land, watched it strike deeper than any argument she could have made. "You're not invisible, keeper. You haven't been since the proclamation. The hunt is coming for this shop whether I walk through your door or not. The only question left is what you'll be doing when it arrives—patching a dying seal with both hands, or finally asking yourself what your mother really died for."

Elodie Blackwood said nothing. He stood among his luck-knots and his useless lavender and his painted pebbles, in his fortress of harmless things, and looked at her with an expression she couldn't fully read—anger braided through fear braided through something that might, in a kinder light, have been hope.

"Coil Street well," he said finally. "You say the horses won't drink."

"They won't."

"Then it's worse than I calculated." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and several he hadn't earned yet. "Monthly, it was supposed to hold for a month between workings. I've been down twice this week. Twice." He looked up sharply. "This doesn't mean I'll help you. Understand that. The gate stays sealed. Whatever your order promised you, whatever you heard burning with your temples—the answer is not opening that door."

"The answer was never the door," Maren said. "The answer is what was taken through it. But we can argue theology when your city isn't filling up with sea from the inside."

He stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—one short, bitter, genuine note, surprised out of him.

"You're going to be a problem," he said.

"I've been called worse by drowned men."

He came out from behind the counter at last, and Maren saw him properly for the first time—not the shopkeeper, not the mask, but the man underneath, tired and sharp-eyed and carrying something enormous on his back with the posture of someone who has forgotten it can be put down. He crossed to the door, checked the street through the glass with a fluid, wary glance, then turned the little brass lock with a click that sounded louder than it should have.

"Cellar," he said. "Come see what your singing looks like from the other side. And priestess—if you scream down there, or pray, or do anything your Choir trained into you, I swear on my mother's grave I will leave you standing in the dark."

Maren shouldered her basket of herring and followed him toward the back of the shop, past a curtain of drying herbs, toward a stair leading down into the earth—down toward the sound she had crossed the straits to find, growing louder with every step, patient as the tide.

Three taps echoed up through the floorboards.

Slow. Deliberate. Almost friendly.

Elodie stopped on the stairs, and Maren saw his shoulders go rigid.

"That's new," he whispered. "It never knocks."

From below, through the stone, through the dark, through twenty years of seals, something on the far side of the door tapped again—

—and this time, unmistakably, it tapped in rhythm with Maren's own heartbeat, as though it had been waiting all along for someone whose blood it recognized.
