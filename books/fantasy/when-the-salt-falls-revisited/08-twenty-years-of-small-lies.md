## 8. Twenty Years of Small Lies

The marked coin sat on his workbench like a sleeping wasp.

Elodie had put it in an iron dish, the kind he used for cooling hot wax, and then—because that felt too much like leaving a snake in the kitchen—he had wrapped it in three layers of linen and buried it in the salt barrel by the window. Salt drowned most small magics. It did not drown Inquisitors, but it would have to do for coins.

He could not sleep. Every time he closed his eyes he saw Vane's face, mild and patient as a man waiting for bread to rise, and heard the soft questions: *Old families in Saltmere, Master Blackwood? Families with... inherited gifts?*

So instead of sleeping, he went down into the cellar, because it was the only place in the world where lying had never been required of him, and because the gate had knocked three days ago and he had not yet answered, and some stubborn, superstitious part of him believed that if he kept postponing the conversation, it would keep postponing itself.

The stairs were narrow and smelled of tallow and cold stone. He lit no lamp. He had never needed one down here; the seams of the Undergate gave off a faint grey shimmer, like moonlight trapped in mortar, and twenty years of walking these steps in the dark had taught his feet every worn place in every stone.

The water was worse.

It had come up past the third seam now, pooling in the shallow depression where he knelt each month to check the wards. Not brackish—the wells across the city were turning brackish, Maren had told him, Maren who had named him *keeper* in front of his own counter as though it were a thing anyone could simply say—but salt-clean and cold, tasting of deep places. Of the other side.

He crouched at the edge of the pool and did not touch it.

"You knocked," he said to the door.

The Undergate was not impressive to look at, which had always struck him as its cruelest joke. A door of black iron set into raw stone at the back of a cellar beneath a charm shop, half its surface furred with old salt-bloom. No hinges. No handle. His mother's iron key hung against his chest on a cord gone soft with sweat and years, and he had never once used it for anything but this monthly walk downstairs, this monthly conversation with something that did not answer in words.

Three taps came through the door. Slow. Deliberate. The same rhythm as before—not urgent, not angry. Almost polite.

*Like a greeting,* he had thought the first time, and hated himself for thinking it.

"I can't let you out," he said. "You know I can't. You must know what's happening up there. They're burning people for less than what you are."

Silence. Then, fainter than sound, more like pressure than voice, something moved against the far side of the door—and spoke his childhood name.

Not *Elodie*. The other one. The small one, the secret one, the one his mother had used only when she tucked him in, only when the lamps were out, only when they were alone: a name made of two syllables and the sound of water pulling back from sand.

Elodie sat down hard on the wet stone.

Because his mother had never told anyone that name. He had carried it alone for twenty years, a pebble in his boot, a private grief. And whatever stood beyond the door knew it—which meant it knew her, or had been her, or had eaten her, or had loved her, and he could not tell which possibility frightened him most.

*Tell me about her,* the silence seemed to say. And so, because there was no one else left to tell, he did.

* * *

She came to Saltmere in the autumn after the war, carrying nothing but him.

That was the story he'd been given, and for twenty years he had believed it whole: a widow fleeing the burned coast, a baby under her cloak, a city that took refugees because it needed hands for its herring fleet. She had found work mending nets, then selling charms—harmless ones, luck-knots and weather-wax, the kind of magic that was technically superstition and therefore legal. She had been good at it. People trusted her face.

What he remembered himself began later, in fragments, the way childhood always keeps its records: in scraps of lamplight and smell.

He remembered her hands. Always working, always making—knots, braids, little wax figures, charms by the hundred. He remembered thinking, at six, that his mother's hands were like spiders, quick and many-fingered and tireless. He remembered that sometimes, mid-knot, she would stop. Her fingers would hover over the rope, trembling, and her face would go somewhere else entirely—a blankness, a reaching, like a woman standing at the edge of a cliff in fog, trying to remember whether she had ever climbed it.

Then she would shake herself and smile at him and keep knotting.

"Where do you go?" he asked her once. He was seven. He asked it the way children ask terrible things, plainly, at supper.

"Nowhere, love." She said it without hesitation, which should have told him everything. People who are telling the truth hesitate; they search. His mother never searched. The lie was already built, load-bearing, older than his memory. "I've never been anywhere but here."

"But you talk in your sleep. About water."

The spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. For one moment—one long, salt-flavored moment—her eyes were not his mother's eyes. They were looking at something behind him, or below him, or very far away, and they were full of a grief so enormous it seemed impossible a body that small could hold it.

Then it closed, like a hand closing over a coin.

"Dreams," she said. "Everyone dreams of water here, love. We live beside it."

But she checked the cellar door that night. He heard her go down, heard the low murmur of her voice speaking to someone—or something—in the dark, and when she came back up her hands were shaking and she made him cocoa at midnight and told him stories about the herring festival until he slept.

He understood now, sitting on the cold stone with the gate humming faintly against his spine, why she had checked the door. Why she had spoken to it. Why her sleep-talk sounded like prayer.

She hadn't been guarding it. She had been apologizing.

* * *

The truth arrived, as truth does, sideways.

He was nineteen, and she was dying—not quickly, not dramatically, just going out the way a lamp goes out, lower and lower, until one morning she could not get out of bed and looked at him with an expression he had spent years afterward trying to name. It was not fear. It was not peace. It was *apology*, again, the same expression from behind the cellar door, and this time she could not close her hand over it.

"There's a key," she said. "On the cord. Never take it off."

"Mama—"

"Listen." Her grip on his wrist was shocking, a fishwife's grip, a working woman's grip, though she had not worked in weeks. "There is a door under the shop. There has always been a door. Your grandmother kept it, and her mother, and back and back past all the names we have, which—" and here her voice cracked, actually cracked, like ice on a pond— "which is not many names, Elodie. Do you know how few names we have?"

He didn't understand. He nodded anyway.

"The war," she said. "They'll teach you it ended. Wars don't end. They're sealed, like wounds, like doors, and someone has to be the scar." Her eyes went to the ceiling—to the floor beneath the floor—and he watched her gather herself the way she gathered thread, pulling something loose from somewhere deep. "When I took the keeping, the door wanted payment. It always wants payment. Memory is the coin it takes. I paid. I paid and I paid and I paid, and what's left of me is what you see, love, what's left of me is the part that could still learn to love you, because loving you happened *after*, do you understand? Loving you is mine. Everything before is in the sea."

He had held her hand and said *I don't understand* and she had smiled, that blank-reaching smile, the cliff-in-the-fog smile, and said:

"No. But you will. That's why I chose to forget everything except how to teach you to lie well. Because one day, my darling, the door will knock, and you will have to decide what kind of keeper you are. And I couldn't—I couldn't make that choice for you. It was the one payment I refused to make in advance."

She died three days later with the tide going out, and he buried her above the tideline because he could not bear to put her in ground that might flood, and he went back down the cellar stairs that same night, and he knelt, and he took up the keeping, and he began the life that was a lie shaped exactly like a man.

* * *

Twenty years of small lies.

He counted them sometimes, the way other men counted money. The lie to Old Sef, the net-mender, that his weather-charms were "just good wax." The lie to the harbor tax-man, that his shop's cellar was "damp, useless, nothing down there but rats." The lie to the girl from Candle Lane who had shown him, once, trembling, the small green light that lived between her palms—*you're safe, no one knows, I won't tell*—and then watched from his doorway two winters later as the old purges took her away in a cart, saying nothing, doing nothing, because doing something meant the door, and the door meant everything.

The lie to himself, longest of all: that he was keeping the world safe from the sea, rather than keeping the sea prisoner for the world's convenience.

Because that was what it was, wasn't it? He had dressed it up in duty for two decades—*warden, sentinel, last bulwark*—but kneeling here tonight with the gate leaking and the knocking patient and his mother's name-syllables still ringing in the dark like dropped coins, he finally saw the shape of the thing underneath.

His family had stolen something. Long ago, generations back, his people had taken something from whatever lived beyond this door—had taken it and built a city on top of it, and called the taking victory, and called the prison peace, and handed the keys down mother to child with a payment plan written in memories.

And his mother—his careful, knotting, sleep-talking, apologetic mother—had known. Had known and chosen not to pass the guilt down as doctrine. She had paid the gate everything except her love for him and her instruction to him, and those two things together amounted to this: *Look with your own eyes. Decide with your own heart. I'm sorry I can't tell you what I'd want, because I sold the knowing.*

He pressed his forehead against the cold iron of the door.

"You knew her," he whispered. "Whatever you are—you knew her voice. You know my name. Tell me what she was to you. Tell me what we took."

The answering silence had texture now, weight, the silence of a crowd leaning in. And beneath it, faint as breath on a winter window, he heard singing—many voices, wordless, rising and falling with the rhythm of a tide against a shore that no longer existed anywhere but here.

Maren's theology, delivered in his shop like an accusation: *they stole our names.* He had dismissed it as priest-craft, tide-lore, the pretty mythology of a drowning order. But his mother had said *we have so few names*, and the gate had said his childhood name in her voice, and the wells of Saltmere were running salt, and somewhere in the palace archive, if Tobias Wrenn was right, the crown kept records going back to the founding—

Records. Founding stones. Names.

Elodie lifted his head. His heart was going hard and strange, grief and purpose braided so tightly he could no longer tell them apart.

"If the names are in the vault," he said to the door, to the singers, to his mother's ghost in the mortar, "then maybe they can come back. Maybe that's what she was buying time for."

The gate was quiet for a long moment.

Then, softly—three taps. But different now. Not a greeting.

A knock. The kind you give when you have been invited in.

Above him, through the floorboards, through the shuttered windows of the shop that had been his whole disguise for twenty years, Elodie heard boots in the street—many boots, moving in formation—and the thin, clear note of an Inquisition whistle splitting the night.

Charm Row was burning early tonight.
