## 9. The Marked Coin

The coin sat on Elodie's workbench for two days before it began to sing.

He had told himself it was nothing—a royal minting, a bit of silver with Vane's face stamped into its obverse like a man who wanted to be remembered even in small change. He had left it where the Inquisitor's gloved fingers had placed it, on the corner of the bench beside the tray of unfinished luck-knots, because moving it would mean admitting what he feared it was. Two days of telling himself stories. That was a keeper's oldest craft, older than warding: the story that the creak in the stair was only the house settling, the story that the salt smell rising from the cellar was the harbor breathing through old stone, the story that his mother had died simply and quietly instead of empty.

On the third morning, as he threaded a knot of harbor-grass through a fisherman's charm—harmless twine, harmless knots, nothing in it but patience—the coin began to hum.

It was not loud. It was the hum of a wet finger circling a glass rim, of a fly trapped behind a shutter, thin and insistent and *wrong*. Elodie's hands went still over the half-tied knot. The sound rose and fell, and when it fell, it fell in time with the faint warmth gathering in his own fingertips, the warmth of magic being worked somewhere near.

He crossed the shop slowly, the way one crosses a room where a snake has been seen but not located, and picked up the coin.

The humming sharpened. Beneath it, almost too deep to hear, was a second tone—a low pulse, rhythmic as a heart, and Elodie knew that rhythm. Every hidden practitioner in Saltmere knew it, though none of them would ever say so aloud: the thrum of a working spell, felt in the teeth, felt in the breastbone, felt in the water standing in a bucket. The coin was not humming at him. The coin was *listening*.

Vane had left it deliberately. Of course he had. Not payment—an ear. A little silver snitch of royal make, tuned to the frequency of gift-work, sitting on Elodie's bench among the charms like a spy among friends.

Elodie carried it to the hearth, meaning to drop it in the coals, and stopped with his arm outstretched.

Because if it was listening here, in his shop, then it had been listening for two days. And if Vane had planted one coin, how many others were scattered across Saltmere? Tucked into change at the fish market. Pressed into a palm by a beggar who wasn't a beggar. Left on altars, dropped in wells, paid out by soldiers buying bread. The city was suddenly full of open mouths, and every charm-worker in Saltmere who whispered an enchantment over a birthing-cord or a sickroom candle was singing into them.

He did not burn the coin. Burning it might tell Vane exactly where it had died. Instead he wrapped it in three layers of oiled silk, carried it down the stairs past the shop's false cellar floor, and set it on the lowest step above the Undergate—where the leaking sea-damp would muffle its hearing, where twenty years of his mother's wards still clung to the stone like barnacles. Then he climbed back up and stood in the middle of his shop, breathing, doing arithmetic with people's lives.

*Half of Saltmere*, he thought. *At least half.*

* * *

He made a list, because lists were how he kept from drowning. Not written—written lists could be found, read, used—but held in the skull, in the order of the city's streets, which he had walked so many thousands of times that the map lived behind his eyes brighter than any cartographer's ink.

Old Sefa, who charmed the fever out of children with a word breathed into their soup. The Corvane sisters, who sold "sweet-smelling" candles down on Lamp Row that burned away nightmares and nothing else, nothing else, they swore. Bram Tulloch the netmender, whose knots held against storms no honest rope should survive. The widow Anselm, who could quiet a crying infant with a lullaby that wasn't entirely a lullaby. Tam Wicker, barely nineteen, who thought nobody knew he could call gulls to his window, who thought it was a game.

Fourteen names. Fourteen names he could hold with certainty, and beyond them the gray shapes of maybe a dozen more—people he suspected, people who had never quite met his eye across a market stall, people who bought too much of certain herbs. Half of Saltmere's hidden ones. Perhaps more. Perhaps all of them now, with coins listening from every pocket in the city.

And he could warn perhaps three before dark.

That was the arithmetic that broke something in him, standing there in the dusty gold light of his own shop. Not that he couldn't save them all—he had known since the Proclamation that saving them all was a child's dream—but that choosing meant *ranking* them. That some would burn because he judged their danger smaller, or their distance longer, or simply because he had run out of hours. He saw Tam Wicker's face, young and sunburnt and laughing at his gulls, and tried to imagine explaining to the boy's mother why the warning had come too late, and found he could not imagine it at all, which meant only that his mind refused the picture, not that the picture would refuse to happen.

He took down his coat.

* * *

Sefa first, because she was closest and oldest and her magic was the loudest—fever-breaking was strong work, bright work, the kind that rang like a struck bell in the ears of anything listening. He went as a customer, bought dried cod he did not want, and while she wrapped it he leaned close and said, in the flat voice of a man discussing weather:

"There are coins going round that sing when the gift is used. Stop working. Burn nothing, bury nothing—just stop. If a stranger pays you, bite the coin. The marked ones have a seam."

She went white to the lips. She was seventy if she was a day, and she had survived three purges in other cities before coming to Saltmere, and she understood within a single breath. She pressed the cod on him without charge and said, "You're a good boy, Elodie Blackwood," using his name like a knife laid gently on the table between them, reminding him what it cost him to be seen.

"You never knew me," he said, and left.

The Corvane sisters next, both together at their candle shop, and there his warning took longer because Petra Corvane wept, and her sister Ilse gripped his sleeve and asked the question he had been dreading: "How do you know? How do you know about these coins, Elodie?"

Because one is on my workbench. Because the Inquisitor himself sat in my shop and smiled at me and left it there like a cat leaving a dead bird. Because I am more guilty than any of you and less likely to hang, because my magic wears the costume of commerce.

"I pay attention," he said, which was true, and pulled free, and went on.

Bram Tulloch was third, and by the time he had climbed the netmender's outside stair the light had gone long and amber over the rooftops, and the bells of the Harbor Temple rang the hour of evening prayer, and somewhere below in the streets a crowd was gathering around something with the particular silence of a crowd watching power at work.

Elodie froze on the stair, one hand on the rail.

Then came the screaming—not many voices, one voice, a woman's voice, and under it the tramp of boots, and he knew, the way you know a thing in your body before your mind consents to it, that the night had already begun. That Vane had not waited for the coins to accumulate evidence over weeks. That the Inquisitor had gathered what the coins had sung in two days and moved tonight, tonight, while Saltmere slept unprepared.

He warned Bram in eleven words, at the top of the stair, with the woman's screams still threading up through the boards: *"Marked coins. Stop all workings. Go to ground now."* Bram's face did something terrible and obedient, and Elodie was already descending, already running, because the screams had resolved into a name and the name was Anselm.

* * *

Widow Anselm's door stood open. That was the first obscenity—doors in Saltmere were never left open after dark; the city taught you that before it taught you letters. Inside, her little front room was overturned with the tidy violence of soldiers doing a job: chairs stacked, floorboards pried, her herb jars lined up on the table in rows like specimens. Two witch-hunters in the crown's grey-and-white held her between them, and she was not struggling. She was an old woman, small and soft as a loaf, and she had gone limp the way prey goes limp, and her eyes were fixed on a third figure standing by the cold hearth.

Inquisitor Vane did not look up when Elodie filled the doorway. That was the worst of it—the lack of surprise, the lack of theater. He examined the widow's lullaby-books, turning pages with one finger, unhurried, a man reading correspondence.

"The marked coin," Vane said mildly, to the room rather than to Elodie, "was found sewn into the hem of her coat. She paid a tinker with it last week. Curious thing, marked coin—it sings when gift-work is done nearby. And this house sang for two nights running." Now he looked up, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without hatred, which somehow made them worse than hatred. "Mistress Anselm says she only sings to babies."

"She does," Elodie heard himself say. His voice came out level. Twenty years of practice, holding. "Everyone knows her lullabies. Half the street learned them from her."

"Half the street," Vane repeated, tasting it. "Yes." He closed the book. "You were seen at her door yesterday, Master Blackwood. Buying honey, I'm told. You buy a great deal of things from a great many people. You are," and here he smiled, small and genuine, the smile of a man finding a pattern finally resolve, "a remarkably social merchant."

"I sell charms," Elodie said. "Charms are social."

"Charms are *commerce*," Vane agreed softly. "Knotted string. Dried herbs. Superstition, priced fairly." He stepped closer, and the hunters shifted, and Widow Anselm's eyes found Elodie's over their shoulders, and in them was not a plea but an instruction—old Sefa's same instruction, given silently: *you never knew me.* She was telling him to let go. She was spending herself to keep him safe, and she didn't even know what he was; she only knew he was young and that youth should be spent on the living.

"Take her," Vane said, turning away. "Trial at dawn. Master Blackwood—" he paused at the door, and did not look back, "—I will come and see your shop again soon. There is a question I did not ask, the other day. About old families. About what gets passed down in them." The lantern-light caught the edge of his jaw. "I find I am very interested in inheritance lately. Things flow down family lines like water finds low ground. Names. Duties. *Guilt.*"

They took her out into the dark. Elodie stood in her ruined kitchen until the boots faded, and then he did the thing he had not permitted himself on the stair at Bram's: he put his fist through the wall, once, hard enough to split skin, because if he did not break something small and stupid he would break instead, entirely.

* * *

He walked home the long way, counting arrests by the lights. A bonfire on Lamp Row—the Corvane sisters' shop, candles burning, all of them, the whole stock, a pillar of sweet smoke visible from the harbor. Grey-and-white at the mouth of Netmenders' Lane. A wagon already loaded, going toward the Inquisition house, faces pale behind the slats. By midnight, by his count, nine of his fourteen names had been taken, and he had saved five, and five felt like nothing, felt like a handful of water carried out of a burning house.

But it was not nothing. That was the lesson, and it arrived not as comfort but as iron.

He stood in his dark shop, hand wrapped in a strip of linen, and made himself look at the truth without flinching from it: he had spent twenty years building a life whose entire architecture was concealment, and concealment had a cost, and the cost had come due tonight in nine names. Hiding had not protected anyone. Hiding had only delayed the moment when protection became impossible—and worse, hiding had *disarmed* them. Fourteen gifted people scattered and silent, each believing themselves alone, each waiting for someone else to move first, each ready to be taken one at a time because no one had ever said the true thing aloud: *we are many, and they are few, and fear is the only army they have.*

His mother had hidden too. Had hidden so thoroughly she'd buried her own past to keep the secret, and died with the gate beneath her son's feet and the truth locked behind her emptied memory. And where had hiding gotten her? Where had it gotten Saltmere? The gate leaked. The hunt burned. The city sang with marked coins.

Elodie unwrapped his hand and looked at the split knuckles in the moonlight from the window, and felt the survivor's guilt in his chest change shape—not cooling, not passing, but *hardening*, the way sea-foam hardens into salt crust, into something you could stand on.

Five saved was not nothing. Five saved was a beginning. And tomorrow he would not go alone, knocking softly door to door like a beggar of warnings. Tomorrow he would gather them—the five, and whoever else could still be reached—and he would say aloud what the city had forbidden for a generation. He did not yet know what they would do when gathered. Resist, flee, hide deeper—he didn't care. Anything was better than dying in ones and twos, politely, in front of neighbors trained not to look.

Somewhere across the roofs, a new sound began. Faint, at first, easy to mistake for wind in the rigging of the harbor ships. But Elodie knew it instantly, and his blood went cold, and he crossed to the window with his heart hammering.

It was singing. From below the streets—from everywhere below the streets, rising up through cellars and well-shafts and storm drains—a vast, layered, mournful chorus, dozens of voices braided into one, the drowned-dead song Maren had described, the Choir of the exiled. And it was answering the screams of the arrested, verse for verse, grief answering grief, as if something beneath Saltmere had heard the night's burning and begun, at last, to sing back.

And beneath the singing—three slow taps, unmistakable, patient, coming up through his own floorboards.

The gate knew. The gate had heard everything. And whatever waited behind it had just counted the night's losses alongside him, and was waiting, with something horribly like sympathy, to see what he would dare do next.
