## 10. Names Are Currency

Maren had learned to measure cities by the sound of their water.

The drowned choir had taught her that in the years before the purge—before the fires on the headland, before she ran with salt crusted in her hair and nothing but a stolen knife and a prayer she wasn't sure was still permitted. Every city sat atop water the way a singer sits atop a held breath. Saltmere's breath was wrong. She'd known it within an hour of passing the harbor gate, gutting fish for coin with her sleeves rolled and her marks hidden under leather bracers, listening with the part of her that never stopped listening.

The water under Saltmere was singing.

Not loudly. Not yet. A thread of it, deep below the cobblestones, rising through cellar walls and well shafts and the roots of old pilings—a thin sustained note like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass. To anyone else it would have been nothing. To Maren it was the most beautiful and most terrible thing she had heard since the night the choir burned.

Because that note meant a gate was open somewhere. And gates did not open by accident.

* * *

She found the charm shop on the fourth day, though she could have found it on the first if she'd let herself follow the song instead of circling it like a gull over a drowning man. The shop sat at the crooked elbow of Charm Row, wedged between a chandler and a dyer's, its sign hand-painted and deliberately unremarkable: **BLACKWOOD CHARMS — LUCK, WEATHER, SMALL BLESSINGS**. The window displayed exactly what a frightened city wanted to buy: knotted cords, dried sea-lavender, iron nails bent into circles, little wax figures of fish meant to be nailed above doors. Nothing that would interest an Inquisitor. Nothing that would interest anyone, really.

Which was, of course, the point. Maren recognized craftsmanship when it was trying not to be seen.

She watched the shopkeeper through the window for a long time. He was older than her by fifteen years or more, lean and gray-streaked, with careful hands and a face arranged into pleasant blankness the way a curtain is arranged across a window. He sold a luck-knot to a fisherman's wife and touched the woman's wrist as he handed it over—two seconds, no more—and Maren felt the small working pass between them like warmth off a hearthstone. Real magic, folded so flat inside a harmless trinket that even she nearly missed it.

*He's been doing this a long time,* she thought. *Hiding in plain sight. Building a whole life out of almost-nothing.*

Then he looked up, directly through the glass, directly at her, and his face didn't change at all—which was how she knew he'd felt her feeling him. Two listeners recognizing each other across a street of people who heard nothing.

Maren bought a weather-charm she didn't need and waited three days before coming back after dark.

* * *

He let her in without argument, which told her he'd spent those three days deciding, and that what he'd decided frightened him.

The shop at night was a different country. Lamplight turned the hanging bundles of lavender into small brown ghosts. The air was thick with beeswax and salt and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a cave that had once held the sea. Maren stood just inside the door and let it wash over her, and the thread of song beneath the floorboards rose half a tone, as if the water down there had noticed her too.

"You're the one who's been standing outside," the shopkeeper said. "Elodie Blackwood. I own the shop."

"Maren." She gave no other name. Names were currency; you learned that young in the choir. "I gutter fish on the quay."

"No, you don't." He said it without heat, wiping his hands on his apron as though she were simply another mess to be cleaned up. "Your forearms are marked. You wear the bracers even to work. And for four days there has been a tide-priestess standing outside my window listening to my cellar."

"The cellar," Maren said. Her heart was going hard against her ribs. "What's in the cellar?"

"That depends on why you're asking."

"Everything depends on why anyone asks anything now." She pulled the left bracer off. The tide-marks ran from her wrist to her elbow—seven silver lines, each one a year of the drowned choir, each one proof enough to burn her. Elodie went very still. "They burned the sanctuary at Greywater Head eleven weeks ago. Nine priests and priestesses. I was out gathering at the tideline. That's the only reason I'm alive, and I've hated myself for it every day since. So don't ask me why I'm asking, keeper. Ask me why it took me this long to knock."

The word *keeper* landed on him like a stone dropped into still water. She watched the ripples move through him—the flinch, the denial, the exhaustion underneath both. Twenty years of hiding, she guessed, looking at his face. Twenty years of being the only wall between two worlds, and telling no one, because keepers were always alone. That was how the old litany went. *One door, one key, one life given to the hinge.*

"I don't know what you think I am," Elodie said quietly.

"I think you're the last of your line in this city, and I think something under your floor is leaking." She stepped closer, and the song beneath them swelled again, and this time she saw him hear it too—saw the way his jaw tightened, the way his eyes flicked down toward the boards as if toward a sick relative. "It started recently. Weeks, maybe a season. It's getting stronger. And it's not just leaking water, Blackwood. It's leaking *want*. Can't you feel it? The whole city is dreaming of singing."

Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, and grief, and fear braided together. "The wells," he said. "The children."

"The wells. The children. The dogs won't walk certain streets at night. Old women are leaving bowls of milk on their doorsteps again, the way they did before the wars, because some part of them remembers what's under us and wants to feed it." Maren pulled her sleeve back fully, letting the lamplight touch the marks. "I came here to find the Undergate. Everyone in the scattered choir believed there was one left, somewhere on this coast, still sealed, still holding. I believe it's under this shop. And I believe you're the reason it hasn't already torn open and drowned everyone you've ever sold a luck-knot to."

For a long moment the only sound was the lamp's small hiss and, far below, that patient rising note.

"Come downstairs," Elodie said at last. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking out onto ice. "And then we'll see whether you still want answers."

* * *

The cellar stairs were narrow and the dark at the bottom was the honest kind—not empty but full, crowded with the presence of held-back water. Maren felt it press against her skin like the deep does a diver. Her marks prickled. The lamp in Elodie's hand shook very slightly, and she understood that his calm upstairs had cost him everything he had.

The gate stood in the far wall.

It was not grand. That was the first shock—that something so world-shaping should be so plain: an iron-banded door set into ancient stone, taller than a man, its timbers black with age and salt-bleached gray in streaks, as if it couldn't decide which world it belonged to. Chains crossed it, old ones, and newer ones, and seals worked into the wood in patterns Maren knew from forbidden pages—the interlocking knots of the keeping-craft, each knot a memory, each loop a price paid. Along the door's lower seam, water seeped in a thin bright line and pooled on the flagstones, spreading slowly toward their feet.

The pool reflected the lamp. And in the reflection, for just a heartbeat, Maren saw faces.

She knelt. She couldn't help it. The choir trained you to kneel at open water, and this was open water, however thin, however caged. She put her palm flat on the flooded stone and listened with everything she had, and the song resolved into voices—many voices, layered like tide on tide, murmuring in a language that predated every human tongue on the coast. Not threatening. Not yet. *Asking.* That was the quality of it, the thing that raised the hair on her arms. All those voices were asking the same question, over and over, patient as erosion:

*Do you remember us? Do you remember our names?*

"They knock," Elodie said behind her. His voice was barely there. "Three taps, sometimes. Slow. Like—" He stopped.

"Like a greeting," Maren finished. She rose and turned to face him, and the lamplight caught the tears she hadn't decided to shed. "You've been holding this door for twenty years and they've been knocking politely the whole time. Do you understand what that means? They could tear it down. Whatever's down there, it isn't strength they lack. It's *permission.*"

"It's hunger," Elodie said. "My mother called it hunger."

"Your mother called it what she needed to call it to keep sealing." Maren took a breath. This was the part she had rehearsed on the road for eleven weeks, walking through marsh and gorse and burning country, saying it aloud to gulls because there was no one left alive to say it to. Once spoken, it could not be unsaid. She spoke it anyway.

"Let me tell you what the Tidewrought are, Blackwood. Not monsters. Not demons from the deep. They were *here first.* Before the first harbor, before the first keel, they tended these waters the way priests tend altars. They had names the way the sea has waves—true names, given names, names that were their works and their wonders. A Tidewrought's name was its craft and its self and its place in the choir, all one thing." She gestured at the door. "And our ancestors stole them."

Elodie had gone white. "Stolen. How do you steal a name?"

"How do you steal anything? You take it when its owner trusts you. There was trade, in the oldest days—there was friendship, the songs say, real friendship, humans and Tidewrought building the first harbors together. And the humans learned that a name, freely spoken in trust, could be *held*. Written. Bound into stone and gold and ledger-book. A held name is power—you can command what you can name. Our ancestors bound the Tidewrought's names into their treasures and their foundations, and a creature without its name cannot hold its shape, cannot hold its home, cannot hold itself together. The exiling wasn't a war won. It was a theft completed. We pushed them through doors like this one and locked them out of the world their names built." Her voice cracked and she let it. "Every city on this coast sits on stolen goods, Blackwood. Saltmere most of all. Your foundation stones aren't just stone. I'd stake my life they hold names."

The water at the door's seam had crept to the toe of Elodie's boot. He didn't step back. Maren gave him that.

"And the hunger," he said slowly, "is the wanting back."

"It's worse than wanting. A nameless thing doesn't end—it *erodes*. What's beyond your door is the exiled dead and the exiled living all tangled together, centuries deep, forgetting themselves one grain at a time. That knocking? That isn't an attack. That's the last of their courtesy. They're asking to be remembered before there's nothing left to remember." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, done pretending they weren't there. "There's one way to end it. Return the names. Freely, willingly, spoken back to the tide by someone with the right to speak them. Not any thief's descendant—the keeping-line. The family entrusted with the door. The debt runs through the blood that holds the lock."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, faintly, a cart rattled past on Charm Row, a normal sound in a normal night, and it seemed obscene down here beside the listening water.

"And the price," Elodie said.

Maren had promised herself she wouldn't look away for this part. She kept the promise.

"A returned name must be replaced. That's the law of it—the balance the keeping-craft was built on. Every name you give back to the tide costs the giver one of his own. Not his true name, perhaps, at the start—memories go first, the small ones, then the large ones, then the load-bearing ones, the ones a person stands on. And at the end—" she made herself say it plainly, gently, the way the choir said the hardest verses, "—at the end, if the debt is great enough, and it is, Blackwood, it is centuries deep—a keeper gives his own name entire. And a thing without a name doesn't die. It simply... isn't, anymore, in any way that matters. Unmade. A gap where a man used to stand."

Elodie looked down at the thin bright line of water, and the faces in it, and said nothing for a long time.

"My mother," he finally said, "paid memories. Didn't she. When she sealed it. That's why she—" He stopped, and Maren watched twenty years of confusion reorganize itself into grief. "She never talked about before. She'd lose words. Whole years. She used to look at me sometimes like she was trying to place me."

"Yes," Maren said softly. "That's the payment schedule. That's what your family has been doing, generation after generation—topping up the seal with pieces of yourselves, buying the world time, one memory at a time." She stepped closer, over the cold water, close enough to see the lamplight tremble in his eyes. "But the seal is failing anyway, and you know it, and I know it, and the things beyond your door know it better than either of us. Time was never going to be enough. It was only ever going to be *enough time.* Enough for someone of your blood to grow up brave enough to stop paying rent on a prison and end the debt outright."

"You're asking me to unmake myself."

"I'm asking you to consider it. I'm asking because nine of my people burned on a headland while the crown calls the gift a plague, because children in this city are dreaming of a choir that only wants its names back, and because you are the only soul on this coast who can choose, and choosing is the whole point. It only works *willingly.* That's the mercy and the horror of the law. No one can force the return. Not Vane with his fire, not the crown with its vaults. Only you, freely." She held out her hand, palm up, the way the choir offered blessing. "I won't pretend I'm not asking for my people. I am. But I'll tell you something else, keeper, and it's true: I watched the water listen to you just now. It knows you. Whatever's down there has been knocking on your door for twenty years, and it has never once tried to break it. That's not hunger alone. That's hope. They think you might be the one who says yes."

Elodie did not take her hand. But he didn't turn away, and down in the pool the reflected faces had gone quiet, all of them listening, waiting on his answer the way the whole drowned coast had waited for two hundred years.

"There's something you should know before I answer anything," he said at last, and his voice had changed—steadier now, or perhaps just more afraid in a useful way. "Vane was in my shop. Ten days ago. He bought a charm and he left a coin behind, and the coin hums when the gifted use their art nearby. Half of Charm Row is already arrested or running. If I'm going to make a choice like this, I have days at most, and I have to make it with the Inquisition closing around us." He looked at her, and beneath the fear Maren saw something kindle that she recognized from mirrors, from the survivors of Greywater Head: the moment grief becomes resolve. "So before I give anyone an answer—mine, or yours—we're going to need to find where my ancestors hid the stolen names. And I think I know someone who can get us into the royal archives."

Above them, muffled through floorboards and streets and stone, a church bell began to toll the hour—and beneath its bronze voice, patient as ever, the water under Saltmere sang on.
