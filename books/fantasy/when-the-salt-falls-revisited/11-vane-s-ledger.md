## 11. Vane's Ledger

The Inquisition's offices in Saltmere occupied the top floor of the old customs house, and Inquisitor Corvus Vane liked it precisely because of what you could see from there. From his window, on a clear evening, you could watch the whole city light its lamps one district at a time—Charm Row first, always first, because charm-sellers worked late into their little theatrical hours—and he would sit with his ledger open and count them. Not lamps. People.

He had been counting for twenty years.

Tonight the ledger lay open to its newest pages, and Vane read through them again the way other men read scripture: slowly, with his lips slightly parted, as though the words might change if he blinked.

*Blackwood, E. Charm-seller, Salt Row. Licensed. No irregularities in eleven years of licensing records. Sells luck-knots, weather-charms, birthing charms. Customers report "they work." Customers always say they work. Note: no customer has ever reported that one failed.*

He underlined that last line for the third time. Ink bloomed under his nib like a bruise.

The candle guttered. Vane did not move to trim it. He had learned long ago to read by dying light—it kept the eyes honest, kept the mind from drifting toward comfort. Comfort was the enemy's first weapon. Not fire, not hexes. Comfort. A warm shop, a familiar face, a charm that seemed to work because you wanted it to work. Twenty years ago he had believed in all of those things with his whole heart, and it had cost him everything.

It had cost him Liss.

* * *

Her name was Alissa Vane before she married, and after she married she was still Liss to him, because her husband was a soft man who called her *my dear* in a voice like porridge and never once said her name the way their mother used to, sharp and fond at once, *Alissa Vane, if you've charmed the milk again I'll skin you.*

She had. She did. She'd charm the milk so it stayed sweet three days past any reason, charm the bread so it didn't stale, charm their father's bad knee so he could climb the cliff stairs to the nets without weeping. Small things. Ordinary things. Their mother had done the same, and her mother before her, down some unbroken thread of coastal blood that nobody in their village had ever thought to call a curse. It was just what women in their family did, the way some families made rope or salted fish. Nobody burned anyone for sweet milk.

Then came the winter of the last Tidewrought war's tail-end, when fear came up the coast like fog off cold water, and a royal assize arrived in the village with a list and a scale.

They weighed her against a church bell, in those days. That was the test. If the accused weighed less than the bell—if the gift gave her body the lightness of things not wholly bound to earth—she was guilty. Liss had laughed when they told her. He remembered the laugh. He could still hear it sometimes, in the hour before sleep or the hour after waking, that bright disbelieving laugh, as though the whole thing were a joke they'd all share later over sweet milk that had gone off at last.

She weighed less than the bell.

Vane had stood in the crowd—he was seventeen, he was her brother, he was nobody, he had no standing to object—and watched them burn her on the shingle below the tide line, because even then the law required witch-fires be lit where the sea could witness. The smoke went out over the water in a long grey plume, and the tide came in that evening exactly on schedule, indifferent as arithmetic, and something in him broke along a seam so deep he didn't find it again for years.

Here was the thing he had learned in the ash and the years after, the thing that made him different from every zealot who'd ever worn the white cord of the Gift-Hunt:

Liss had been innocent of malice. She had never cursed a soul. And she had still been guilty—guilty of carrying something that the world could not safely hold, something that turned sweet milk into kindling the moment men grew afraid. The gift itself was the crime, regardless of use. His sister had died not because she was wicked but because the thing inside her was a lit fuse walking around in a beloved face, and no one—not she, not their mother, not the soft-porridge husband—had ever once considered putting it out.

That was the doctrine Vane built his life on, alone, in ledgers, in lamplight: *the gift is a wound that blesses its bearer right up until it kills everyone nearby.* And every year the evidence mounted. Every year some village drowned in a flood that followed a hedge-witch's weather-working. Every year some child dreamed true and woke screaming about things beneath the streets. Every year the hidden practitioners of Saltmere grew bolder, selling their little protections, weaving themselves into the city's trust like rot into a beam.

And every year, somewhere in the back of the ledger, in small cramped script he never showed anyone, Vane wrote the same sentence over and over, the way penitents write their sins:

*If someone had put out her fuse twenty years sooner, she would have lived.*

He did not hate witches. God knew, God knew, he did not hate them. He pitied them the way you pity a woman carrying plague, and he hunted them the way you quarantine a harbor—grieving, thorough, and without apology.

* * *

The knock came at midnight. Vane closed the ledger before the second knock landed; that reflex was older than his beard.

"Enter."

Sergeant Hallow, a broad grey man with ink-stained cuffs and the soul of a clerk, came in holding a satchel the way a midwife holds a newborn—carefully, and a little frightened of it.

"From the marked coin, sir." Hallow set the satchel on the desk. "The one you left at Blackwood's shop. It's been singing since sundown."

Vane felt something tighten behind his ribs that he refused to call hope. Hope was a luxury for men whose sisters hadn't burned. "Singing how?"

"Like a wet finger on glass. Steady, then—" Hallow swallowed. "Then it climbed. Sir, half the coin-bells in the evidence room rang with it. Whatever happened tonight, it happened big, and it happened near Salt Row."

Near Salt Row. Near the charm-seller with eleven spotless years and customers who never reported failure. Vane opened the satchel. Inside, wrapped in wool, the copper coin sat dull and ordinary—but when he laid his palm above it, flat, not touching, he felt the hum crawl up through his bones like a hymn sung by something drowning.

The coin-work was old law-magic, licensed, sanctioned, blessed by three separate ordinaries: a coin steeped in counter-song, which lay quiet in honest rooms and woke in the presence of working craft. Vane had left it on Blackwood's counter among the luck-knots, paid for honestly, tucked into the merchant's till with the rest of the night's copper. A seed. You planted seeds. You did not dig them up to check on them; you waited for the green.

But this wasn't green. This was a harvest overnight.

"Show me the map," Vane said.

Hallow unrolled it across the desk: Saltmere in surveyor's ink, and across it, small red pins where the coin-bells had sympathized. Vane studied the constellation of red with a scholar's patience. Not scattered. Clustered. Two clusters, in fact—one sprawling across the tenements south of Charm Row, where the poor bought love-charms and fever-knots from women who worked out of kitchens. And one—

One pin, alone, sitting directly on Salt Row. On Blackwood's shop.

"It rang hardest here," Hallow said quietly. "Hardest I've ever heard one ring. Like it wasn't echoing magic nearby, sir. Like it was answering something underneath."

Underneath. Vane's finger rested on the pin a long moment. Underneath Charm Row was cellar ground, cisterns, old foundations. Underneath most of old Saltmere was nothing worth naming—or so the charts claimed.

"Who drew this survey?" he asked suddenly.

Hallow blinked. "Royal cartography. Why?"

"The district lines." Vane traced them. Something about the boundaries bothered him the way a wrong note bothers a musician—the shapes were too neat, too round, curving around Salt Row's oldest quarter as though the mapmaker had drawn the city and then decided, politely, to leave out a tooth. "Get me the original plates. Every edition, twenty years back."

"Sir? It's midnight."

"Hallow." Vane looked up, and the sergeant—who had served under softer men and harder ones and preferred Vane to both, though he'd have cut out his tongue before saying so—went pale in the way of men who have heard the quiet voice before. "My sister is dead because everyone in our village decided the strange thing next door was fine, actually fine, nothing to look at, look away, be comfortable. I do not look away anymore. The plates. Tonight."

* * *

He worked until the third hour, and the plates confirmed what the map's silence suggested: in the oldest surveys, before the crown standardized its cartography, there had been a district marked where now there was only blank stone hatching. A name had been scraped from the plate—not faded, *scraped*, deliberately, with a burnisher, and the parchment beneath re-sized to hide the scarring. Someone in the crown's own service, decades ago, had erased a piece of Saltmere.

Vane sat back. Around him the office ticked and settled, the great customs house groaning on its piles the way old buildings do over water. Over water. The whole city sat on pilings driven into tidal mud, and everyone spoke of it as engineering, as triumph, as *we built where the sea said no.*

His sister had grown up on a coast full of stories about what the sea said. Tide-priests in their grey-blue rags, singing at dawn to keep the water polite. Mothers marking doorposts with salt. Old women who wouldn't let children whistle after dark, *lest something whistle back.* He'd dismissed it all as superstition at fifteen, at seventeen, at twenty-five—at every age except the age he was now, when he had spent two decades cataloguing the strange and learned this one hard truth: *superstition is simply knowledge that outran its witnesses.*

The Tidewrought had been real. That much the archives admitted in careful, hedged language—the last war, the exile, the sealing. The church called it heresy to say the exiled still pressed at the doors of the world. The crown called it sedition. Vane called it data, and data had a way of refusing to stay buried.

Something was underneath Charm Row. Something had rung a law-coin like a struck bell. And the man who owned the shop above it sold charms that never failed, and kept eleven years of immaculate records, and met an Inquisitor's eye with the smooth calm of a man who has practiced being invisible for so long that invisibility has become a discipline, a craft, almost a devotion.

Vane understood that kind of devotion. He wore it himself every day, in the soft-spoken patience that made hardened smugglers confess and made magistrates think him harmless. You could hear devotion in another man's silences, if your own silences had been carved from the same stone.

He opened the ledger again, to the Blackwood page, and beneath the underlined line about charms that never fail, he wrote in his small precise hand:

*Coin sang at moonrise, 14th. Strength suggests working of scale far beyond retail charm-craft. Source possibly subterranean. Cross-reference: scraped survey districts; sealed-site registry; keeper's-office records (defunct).*

Then he stopped, nib lifted, and looked at the page for a long time.

Because here was the confession he kept even from this book, the one he spoke aloud only once a year, on the shingle where the fire had been, to the indifferent tide: he was tired. Not of the work—never of the work—but of the arithmetic. Twenty years of quarantines, and the gift kept surfacing like oil in a well. For every practitioner he took, three more lit candles in cellars. Burn them all and you burned a city's grandmothers, its midwives, its lucky fishermen, and still the well ran sweet-tainted. Erasure was a task for gods, and the gods had not answered his letters.

Unless.

Unless the erasure could be total. Unless the source itself could be found—the spring, not the streams. Vane had read the fragmentary accounts of the sealing, the keeper's office, the gate. If there was truly a door beneath Saltmere, and if the door was leaking, then the leak was why the gift grew bold, why hedge-witches' workings rang louder every year, why the city's dreams had gone strange. Cut the leak, seal the source absolutely, and maybe—maybe—the gift would starve. Maybe some girl in some coastal village with sweet milk and a bad knee would grow old unburned, because the thing that lit her fuse had finally been locked away beyond reach.

That was the future he wanted. Not pyres. Silence. A world so thoroughly ordinary that his sister's gift would have nowhere to catch.

He wanted it so badly his hands shook writing the word *subterranean.*

Which was, he knew with the cold clarity of twenty years' practice, exactly how they got you. Zealots wanted fire; he wanted silence; both wanted it too much. The ledger had taught him that. Every fanatic's file he'd ever compiled began with grief and ended with justification. He had read a hundred men into that pattern and sworn he'd know it in himself.

So he did what he always did with the shaking. He wrote it down. Made it data. Gave the trembling a column and a number, and numbers held still when hands would not.

Below the entry, he added one final line, the operational order, plain as a shopping list:

*Return to Blackwood's within the week. Second purchase. Watch the hands, not the face. Hands forget to lie.*

* * *

Dawn found him on the window side of the office, ledger closed, watching Saltmere wake. Smoke rose thin from a thousand chimneys. On Charm Row, the shops were shuttering open one by one—luck-knots turning on their hooks, weather-charms swaying, the whole gentle machinery of concealed craft beginning its day. Somewhere down there a man named Elodie Blackwood was sweeping his step, probably, humming, arranging his trinkets, being loved by his neighbors.

Vane did not hate him. That was the strangest part, the part no one would ever believe if they took his ledger and read it cover to burning cover. He felt toward Blackwood something closer to dread tenderness—the feeling of a man watching someone walk unknowing along a frozen pond, knowing the ice, knowing the weight of secrets, knowing the sound a crack makes.

*Liss had smiled too,* he thought. *Right up to the scale.*

He gathered his coat, his hat, the marked coin's twin from the evidence drawer—a second seed, for a second planting—and paused at the door with his hand on the latch.

Behind him, on the desk, the ledger lay shut. But under the desk, locked in the bottom drawer where no clerk would ever file it, was a smaller book, bound in his sister's own embroidered linen, containing a single pressed sprig of sea-lavender and one line in a young man's hand, written the night of the fire and never rewritten since:

*I will make it so no one ever has to burn for what they carry — either by taking it from them, or by making the world safe enough to carry it. Forgive me whichever way I choose.*

Vane turned the latch, descended into the waking city, and went to buy a second charm from Elodie Blackwood.

And three streets from Salt Row, passing the fish-market, he stopped—because beneath the cries of gulls and the auction bells, faint as memory, the air itself seemed to be humming. A wet finger on glass. A hymn sung by something drowning.

The coin in his pocket had begun to sing before he ever reached the shop.
