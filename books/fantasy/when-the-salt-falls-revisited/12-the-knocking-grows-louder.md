## 12. The Knocking Grows Louder

The rain came in off the harbor like it had a grudge, and by the second hour of it, Elodie knew the cellar was lost.

He heard it before he saw it—the wrongness of the sound under his feet as he closed the shop for the night. Water should not have a voice. But the floorboards above the cellar stairs were singing a thin, high note, and when he knelt and pressed his palm flat to the wood, he felt the tremor of movement beneath. Not dripping. Not seeping. Moving.

He lit no lamp. Twenty years of habit kept him from lighting lamps in that cellar, though the reason for the habit had eroded until only the shape of it remained—his mother's voice, *never light where you can listen*—and he obeyed the shape because obedience was all he had left of her.

The stairs were slick. At the bottom, cold water lapped at his ankles, black and gleaming in the grey light that came through the high grate. The Undergate stood at the far wall where it had always stood: an iron door set into living stone, older than Saltmere, older than the crown that claimed Saltmere, its surface worked over with sigils so worn they were less writing than weather. His mother's iron key hung against his chest on a cord, warm from his skin even now, even here.

The water had risen past the first step of the dais. It had never touched the dais. In twenty years of monthly inspections he had watched the seams weep salt and dry again, had patched them with wax and prayer and the particular stubbornness of a man who has nothing else to offer. Now the whole floor was a shallow sea, and the gate stood in it like a woman wading, patient, unhurried, certain.

"Three taps," Elodie said aloud, to no one. "You've been tapping for weeks."

And the water shivered.

* * *

He had not told Maren how bad it was. That was cowardice dressed as caution, and he knew the difference even if he refused to look at it straight on. She wanted the gate open. She had said so with the naked hunger of a girl whose people had been drowning in exile for generations—*it might save them, don't you see, it might save all of them*—and every day the leaks worsened, and every day he lied to her a little more smoothly, and every day Vane's marked coin hummed in its lead box in the back room like a wasp in a jar, reminding him that time was not merely short but already spent.

So instead he told Tobias, because Tobias could be relied upon to be frightened in a useful way.

"It shouldn't be possible," the cartographer said, crouched at the edge of the flooded dais with his boots off and his stockings rolled, prodding the water like a man testing a bath he suspected of harboring eels. "Stone doesn't flood upward. The water table in this district sits forty feet below—" He stopped. He had stopped saying things like that lately, ever since his map had shown him the district that official charts pretended did not exist. Ever since he understood what his beautiful, meticulous work had nearly handed to the Inquisition. "I keep forgetting," he said quietly, "that I'm standing inside something that was built to be forgotten."

"You're standing inside my shop's cellar."

"I'm standing inside a prison door, Elodie. Let's not be delicate about it." Tobias wrung out his stocking with the air of a man performing normalcy as a discipline. "The question isn't whether it leaks. The question is what changed. Gates like this—I've read the fragments, the ones your order didn't burn—they don't weaken from the outside. They weaken when something on the other side stops being willing to wait."

Elodie looked at the gate. The sigils across its face had darkened, filled with brine, and in the lamplight they seemed almost wet-eyed, almost weeping.

"Then it's done waiting," he said.

That night the knocking began in earnest.

* * *

It had always been three taps. Slow, spaced, courteous—the knock of a neighbor asking rather than demanding. He'd heard it first a month ago, during the inspection after the proclamation, when fear had already begun to curdle in the streets above and he'd come down half-hoping the gate would simply explain itself. Three taps. He'd pressed his ear to the iron and heard, beneath the taps, something worse: a vast soft rushing, like breath drawn through drowned lungs, like a sea remembering it used to be a choir.

Now the knocking came every night, and it was not three taps anymore.

He sat on the top stair with his back against the wall and his knees drawn up like a boy, listening to it work. Tap-tap-tap-TAP. A rhythm. A phrase. Then silence, long enough to make him hope, then again: tap-tap-tap-TAP. Patient as tide. It was trying, he understood dimly, to speak in the only language it had left—the language of pressure, of repetition, of things that wear stone down by refusing to stop.

His mother had taught him the counting rhyme keeper-children learned, the one that was really a ward disguised as nonsense. *One for the door, two for the lock, three for the name the water forgot.* He had sung it to himself for years without letting himself think about what it meant. Now, sitting in the dark above a rising sea, he sang it under his breath, and the knocking paused.

Paused. As if listening.

"Don't," he whispered to whatever waited beyond the iron. "Don't do that. Don't act like you know me."

Tap-tap-tap-TAP. But gentler now. Almost tender.

* * *

On the fourth night, the water reached the third step, and the voice came.

Not through the gate. Through the water itself. That was the horror of it and the wonder of it braided together—the flooding wasn't damage, it was speech. The Tidewrought had no mouths down there, or if they did, the sea spoke for them, and every inch the water climbed was another word assembled, another syllable pushed up through stone the way roots push up through pavement, patient, inevitable, breaking things without malice.

He was checking the sigils when it happened, his fingers tracing the worn grooves, feeling the magic in them guttering like candle flames in wind. The water around his ankles went suddenly, perfectly still—a held breath—and then it spoke, and the voice was his mother's.

*"Lio."*

The nickname landed in his chest like a dropped coin.

No one had called him Lio in twenty years. No one alive. His mother had died—or gone, or been spent; the distinction mattered less each year—when he was nineteen, leaving him the key and the cellar and a life built entirely of concealment, and she had called him Lio the way she called him nothing else: softly, from the kitchen doorway, when the morning was good. When the charms sold well and the fishmonger laughed at her jokes and for one hour the world let them forget what slept beneath their floorboards.

"Lio," the water said again, and it was her voice exactly—not remembered, not imitated, but *hers*, the slight roughness at the bottom of it, the way she softened the vowel like she was afraid of waking someone. "Oh, my love. You grew tall."

He did not remember sitting down. He was kneeling in the floodwater, which had climbed to his knees without his noticing, and his hands were pressed flat against the iron of the gate as if he could reach through it, and he was crying in great ugly silent gulps, and somewhere very far away part of him was screaming that this was the trap, this was precisely the trap, that a gate hungry to open would use the oldest hunger it could find—

"Stop it." His voice cracked. "Stop it. You're not her. She paid everything. She gave her memories to seal you behind that door, and she wouldn't—she wouldn't ask me to—"

*"I'm not asking you to do anything, sweetheart."* The voice was gentle in a way the sea had no right to be. *"I'm just glad you can hear me. We weren't sure you'd be able to hear us yet."*

"Us." The word snagged like a nail. "Who is us?"

Silence. Then, from deeper in the water, fainter voices rose—not words yet, more than murmurs. A chorus. Dozens of voices. Hundreds. The drowned dead of the Tidewrought, exiled beyond the gate since the last war, pressing close to the iron the way children press close to a window in winter, watching a world they were stolen from.

And among them, unmistakable, woven through the rest like a silver thread: his mother. Singing. The counting rhyme. *One for the door, two for the lock, three for the name the water forgot.*

"She sealed you in," Elodie said, and his voice came out small and strange. "You were on the wrong side when she shut it. Weren't you? You weren't the enemy. You were just—left."

*"Some of us,"* his mother's voice said, sad and warm. *"And some of us chose it. There were keepers who went through on purpose, Lio, to stand witness. To remind the exiled that someone remembered them. That's why the knocking started gentle. That's why it's never broken the door. They won't break it. They're waiting for it to be opened rightly, by a hand that knows the cost."*

"The cost." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Maren told me. Names are currency. Memories are payment. I know what it costs. I watched what it took from you—you couldn't remember my birthday, Mother. You couldn't remember Father's face. You'd sit by the window some mornings looking like a woman who'd woken in a stranger's house, and I thought—God help me, I thought you were disappointed in me."

*"Never,"* the water said, fierce now, and the whole cellar trembled with the word, and the sigils flared dull red and guttered. *"Never you. What was taken from me was worth losing twice over, because it bought you time. Time to grow. Time to become someone strong enough to choose better than I could."*

"Choose what?"

But he already knew. He had known since Maren stood in his shop and named the theft that started the war—that humans had stolen the Tidewrought's true names, that the exile was built on robbery, that the gate hungered because a debt unpaid will hollow out any vessel holding it. He had known since he found the foundation stones in the archive records, since Tobias's maps showed him that Saltmere was not a city with a secret underneath but a lock with a city grown over it.

The choice was opening the door. The choice was returning the names. The choice was giving the tide whatever it asked, memory by memory, until there was nothing of Elodie Blackwood left to stand keeper.

And duty said no. Duty said: you are the last of your line, the door is yours to hold, hold it until your hands fail and pass the burden on and pray the next fool is stronger. Duty was the shape his whole life had been cut from. Duty was the cluttered shop and the harmless trinkets and the careful invisibility, twenty years of small lies stacked into a fortress with one occupant.

But longing—longing said *Lio*, said it in his mother's voice, said *we weren't sure you'd hear us*, and longing sounded so much like home that his hands shook against the iron.

"I can't think," he said finally. "I need to think."

*"Take all the time you need, sweetheart,"* his mother said. *"We've waited two hundred years. We can wait while our boy decides."*

*Our boy.* He climbed the stairs on legs that didn't feel like his own, and behind him the water did not rise further, did not push, simply waited, holding its new level like a held breath, like courtesy, like love with nowhere to put itself.

* * *

Upstairs, the shop was cold. He lit the lamp over the workbench and sat among the luck-knots and the fair-weather charms, the little brass bells and the bundles of dried gull-feather, the harmless trinkets that hid the real workings the way his smile hid the cellar. Everything in the room was a lie he had made with his hands, and he loved every piece of it, and tonight every piece of it looked like a coffin.

Vane would come soon. The marked coin hummed louder each week; half the practitioners on Charm Row had already vanished into the Inquisition's wagons, and Elodie's warnings had saved perhaps four of them and cost him sleep and maybe, eventually, his neck. The hunt was tightening. And beneath his feet the sea was rising through the floor of the world, polite and grieving and full of the dead, calling him by the name only his mother had ever used.

There was a third thing in him now, besides duty and longing. It had arrived quietly sometime during the fourth night, while he knelt in the floodwater, and it did not shout. It simply observed that his mother had not failed. That sealing the gate had not been surrender—it had been strategy. *Time to grow,* she'd said. *Someone strong enough to choose better.*

She hadn't built a prison. She'd built a delay. And a delay, unlike a prison, is meant to end.

The lamp guttered. Below the floorboards, faint and rhythmic and unbearably kind, the knocking resumed—but different now. Slower. Softer. And this time, between the taps, he could hear the whole drowned choir breathing his nickname like a lullaby passed hand to hand through two centuries of dark:

*Lio. Lio. Lio.*

He put his head in his hands, and above the workbench, hanging from its nail, his mother's spare charm-kit swayed gently—as if the house itself had turned to look at him, waiting to see what he would do.

Outside, in the street, boots. Many boots. And a voice he knew too well, soft as a knife going into bread, calling the district to wake.

Inquisitor Vane had found Charm Row.
