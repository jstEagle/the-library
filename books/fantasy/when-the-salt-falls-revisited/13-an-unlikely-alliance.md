## 13. An Unlikely Alliance

The map hung in Tobias Wrenn's memory the way a hanging body hangs from a gibbet: still, official, and impossible to unsee.

He had drawn it himself—no, that was the coward's version. He had *corrected* it. Three weeks ago, a clerk from the Office of Works had brought him the old survey charts of Saltmere's lower districts and asked him to reconcile them with the new harbor plans. Tobias, who had spent eleven years as the crown's finest cartographer before one honest error about a river delta cost him his post, had done what he was always praised for doing. He had noticed things.

The old charts showed a district called Brackish Row. Forty-one structures, a chapel, a well, a street that curved like a question mark down toward the waterline. The new plans showed nothing there at all—not empty ground, not demolished lots. Nothing. The district had been erased so cleanly that even the ink of the newer surveys seemed to flinch around it, leaving a pale rectangle where a neighborhood should have been.

Tobias had asked the clerk about it. The clerk had smiled the way men smile when they are being paid not to know things. *"Old Saltmere shifts, Master Wrenn. The sea takes what it likes."*

But the sea did not take things off vellum. Vellum remembered. And someone, somewhere in the long chain of royal surveyors, had decided that forty-one buildings and everyone who lived in them should simply stop having existed—and every mapmaker since had copied the omission without question, because that was what loyal mapmakers did.

Tobias had reported it to the Office of Works. He had stood in a cold room with cold men and explained, with his old precision, that the foundations of half of Saltmere's merchant quarter rested on structures no chart acknowledged, and that if the crown intended to drive pilings for the new harbor works, they would be driving them into spaces the official record claimed were void.

One of the cold men had thanked him. Another had written something down. And three days later, Inquisitor Vane's people had come to his lodging—not to arrest him, worse, to *thank* him—with a purse of silver and a request that he prepare a full archival summary of the omitted district for the Inquisition's review.

They knew. Whoever had erased Brackish Row from the maps, the Inquisition wanted to know what was under it, and Tobias's careful little correction had handed them the spade.

He had taken the silver. That was the part that sat in his chest like swallowed stone. He had told himself a man with debts and no position could hardly refuse the crown's coin, and then he had drunk two bottles of wine he couldn't taste and lain awake listening to the harbor bells count hours he wasn't sleeping through.

On the fourth night, he stopped lying to himself, dressed in his least memorable coat, and went to find the charm-seller on Lantern Row.

* * *

Elodie Blackwood's shop smelled of cedar oil, dried kelp, and something older underneath—brine and iron, like a beach at low tide after a storm. The shelves were chaos arranged into the appearance of chaos: luck-knots tangled in baskets, jars of gray salt labeled in a hand too neat for a shopkeeper, wind-chimes made of fish bones that chimed without wind. Tobias had passed this window a hundred times in his years in Saltmere and never once come inside. Charm-sellers were a copper a dozen in this city, and most of them sold exactly nothing, which was the point of them.

He waited until the last customer—a fisherman buying a knot against squalls—had gone, until Elodie had turned the sign, until the lamp in the neighboring chandler's window went dark. Then he knocked.

"I'm closed," came the voice through the door, pleasant and flat as pondwater.

"My name is Tobias Wrenn." He kept his voice low. "I used to make maps for the crown. Three weeks ago I gave the Office of Works a report about a district your city seems determined to forget, and I think the Inquisitor is going to use it to dig down to whatever you're keeping under there."

Silence. Long enough that Tobias counted his own heartbeats and lost track at thirty.

Then the bolt drew back, and Elodie Blackwood looked out at him—not opening the door, just looking, the way a man looks down a well. He was younger than Tobias expected and older than his face, with the particular stillness of someone who has spent twenty years being nobody on purpose.

"Brackish Row," Elodie said quietly.

"You know the name."

"I know a great many names I'd rather not." The door opened another few inches. "You'd better come in, Master Wrenn, before someone sees you standing where honest men don't stand."

Inside, Elodie lit a single lamp and did not offer wine or a chair, which Tobias respected; it meant the man was taking the situation seriously. Up close, the shopkeeper had hands that moved constantly, small adjustments—the kind of hands, Tobias thought, that belonged to a man who spent his life touching objects to make sure they were still real.

"Tell me exactly what you told them," Elodie said.

Tobias told him. The omitted district. The pale rectangle on the surveys. The pilings. The purse of silver—he set it on the counter between them, all of it, because some part of him needed it gone from his pocket tonight or he would never sleep again. Elodie looked at the coins the way other men looked at snakes.

"They'll bring surveyors," Tobias said. "Within the month, probably within the fortnight. Vane's people don't move fast, but they move *completely*. They'll chart every cellar on Lantern Row, and yours sits closest to—" He stopped. "I don't actually know what's under there. Do I?"

"No."

"Should I know?"

"No." Elodie almost smiled, and it was a tired, terrible smile. "That's rather the whole art of my profession, Master Wrenn. Knowing things is how people die in Saltmere. I sell ignorance by the jar." He turned a luck-knot over in his fingers. "Why are you here? Not for absolution—I don't sell that either. You could have taken the silver and left the country. Men do."

"Because I've spent eleven years drawing coastlines for men who burn people," Tobias said, and heard his own voice crack on the word *burn*, "and I told myself it was just ink. Lines on paper don't hurt anyone. But lines decide where soldiers go. Lines decide which villages get taxed and which get forgotten and which get erased. I gave Vane a line, and now there will be soldiers at the end of it." He made himself meet the shopkeeper's eyes. "I can't unmake the report. But I know the archives better than any living man except perhaps the archivists themselves, and I know which of them can be bribed, which can be fooled, and which simply need to be told a better story than the truth. If there's something in the vaults worth finding before Vane finds it—I can find it faster."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. The fish-bone chimes stirred, though there was no draft.

"There's a woman," he said finally, "who will want to hear this. She'll want to hear it less kindly than I have. Are you prepared for that?"

"I'm prepared for considerably worse."

"No," Elodie said, dousing the lamp, "you're really not. But come anyway. Tonight. Through the yard, not the alley."

* * *

The woman was waiting in the back room behind a wall of hanging herbs, and she looked at Tobias the way gulls look at a dropped fish—as a thing that might be useful and might be trouble and deserved neither courtesy nor trust. Maren, Elodie called her, and said nothing else about her, though everything else about her said *the drowned choir*: the salt-scars on her forearms in patterns too deliberate for accident, the way she stood with her weight forward like a swimmer, the smell of deep water that clung to her more strongly than to the shop itself.

"Tobias Wrenn," she repeated, when Elodie finished the account. "The cartographer." She laughed once, without humor. "You drew the maps that keep my people's graves off every chart in the kingdom, and now you've brought the grave-robbers a shovel."

"Yes," Tobias said. It would have been easy to explain—to talk about clerks and omissions and how the erasure predated him by generations—but he had learned, in one long wineless night of honesty, that explanations were often just guilt wearing a tidy coat. "Yes. And I'm trying to take the shovel back."

Maren studied him for a long time. She had eyes the gray-green of water over sand, and they did not blink as often as most people's.

"The names," she said to Elodie, ignoring him entirely. "If the crown keeps records of the founding—and crowns always keep records of their thefts, they call them deeds and charters and histories—then the proof of what was taken is in the vault. And if it's in the vault, Vane will find it eventually. He's not clever the way scholars are clever. He's clever the way hounds are clever. He doesn't understand what he's looking for, but he cannot be thrown off the scent."

"And you think I can beat a hound to a bone?" Tobias asked.

"I think you're the only one who knows which drawers the bone is hidden in." Maren turned to him at last, fully, and the force of her attention was physical, like stepping into cold surf. "So here is what happens now, mapmaker. You tell us everything. Every archive, every catalog, every clerk with loose morals and every door with a lazy guard. Everything you know about how the Office of Works stores its founding surveys. And then we decide whether you're an ally or a liability, and I promise you the second option is shorter."

"Maren." Elodie's voice was quiet but it landed in the room like a dropped anchor. "He came here. That counts for something."

"It counts for a beginning. Beginnings drown people all the time."

Tobias sat down on a crate, because his legs had opinions of their own, and began to talk. It was strange how easily it came, once started—eleven years of professional knowledge pouring out of him like bilge from a holed hull. The Royal Archive beneath the palace keep, three levels down, where the oldest holdings were kept in sealed lead cases against damp and fire. The Office of Works' duplicate registry, sloppier but more complete, because surveyors were vain and always kept personal copies. The state funeral scheduled for the old Lord Admiral—everyone knew the date; the whole city knew it, because the whole city would be forced to line the processional route. On days of grand ceremony, the archive staff was halved. Half the lamps went unlit. Half the doors went unwalked.

"A funeral," Maren said slowly. "You're proposing we walk into the heart of the palace during a funeral."

"I'm proposing we walk into the heart of the palace while every eye in Saltmere is pointed at a coffin," Tobias corrected. "It's the best cover either of us will ever be offered. Grief makes guards bored, and boredom makes gaps."

Elodie had gone very still. Tobias had learned enough in one evening to read that stillness correctly: it was not hesitation. It was a man feeling a trap close gently around his own future and deciding, with visible effort, to step further in.

"If the founding records exist," Elodie said at last, "they won't say *names*. No one writes down what they steal when they intend to keep it. They'll write boundaries. Deeds. Compensation ledgers. A war has paperwork, Master Wrenn—it's the one universal truth of wars. Somewhere in those lead cases there's a document that says what the first families of Saltmere took from the sea, and what was promised in return, and why the district under our feet was built the way it was built." He looked at Maren. "And if we find it, we find the shape of the lock. Maybe the key too."

"And if Vane's people reach it first?" Tobias asked.

Neither of them answered, which was answer enough.

They worked through the small hours. Maren knew the tides of the palace cisterns—there were maintenance ways, old ones, that ran with seawater, and she spoke of them with the ease of a woman describing her own veins. Tobias sketched from memory: the archive floor plan rendered in charcoal on the back of a shelf-board, each corridor and stair and sealed case placed with the obsessive accuracy that had once been his pride and was now, at last, pointed at something worth the pointing. Elodie mostly listened, and occasionally asked questions that made both of them pause—the kind of questions that revealed he understood far more about the founding of Saltmere than a charm-seller had any business understanding, and that he would die before explaining how.

Near dawn, Maren stood, stretched like a cat deciding whether to stay, and looked down at Tobias.

"You understand what you are now," she said. It was not quite a question. "You've seen what we might be carrying. You've met him—" a tilt of her head toward Elodie, "—and you know he isn't merely a shopkeeper, and you know that knowing is the dangerous part. There's no walking away clean after this. Vane already has your name in his ledger once. If we fail, you'll hang beside us. If we succeed—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes, something vast and sad, "—if we succeed, the world changes shape, and there's no place on any map for what we were."

"I spent my career believing maps held the world still," Tobias said. "I've since learned they hold it *down*. I'd like to spend whatever's left of my life letting something up."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she extended her hand—salt-scarred, strong—and he took it, and her grip was cold as deep water and did not let go quickly.

"An alliance, then," she said. "Fragile. Like all the honest ones."

* * *

Tobias walked home through streets going gray with dawn, past the harbor where the fishing boats were putting out, past the criers setting up their stands for the morning proclamations. At the corner of Lantern Row he stopped, because a figure stood beneath the eave of the shuttered chandlery, reading a notice with the unhurried attention of a man committing it to memory.

Soft-spoken. Neat. Gray gloves, despite the mildness of the season. Inquisitor Vane did not look dangerous, which was the most dangerous thing about him.

Tobias kept walking, matching his pace to the other early risers, a tired middle-aged man going home from wherever tired middle-aged men went. But as he passed, he saw what the notice was—the funeral proclamation, the Lord Admiral's procession, the route, the hour. And he saw that along the margins of the posting, in pencil fine as spider silk, someone had already marked the archive entrance, the cistern grate, the servants' stair.

Vane had reached the same conclusion they had. Perhaps days ago.

Tobias did not run. Running drew the eye. He walked, one measured step at a time, all the way to Elodie's door, and knocked three times, fast, the way he'd been told to knock if ever he saw anything wrong.

When the bolt drew back, he said only: "He's ahead of us. He has the funeral route marked."

Behind Elodie, in the dim herb-hung room, Maren rose to her feet, and the water-smell of her filled the shop like a tide coming in.

"Then we go tomorrow," she said, "during the rehearsal drills, when they think no one would dare. Get your charcoal, cartographer. We draw our own map—and this time, we decide what it leaves out."
