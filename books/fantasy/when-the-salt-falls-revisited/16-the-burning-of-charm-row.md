## 16. The Burning of Charm Row

### I. Vane

Inquisitor Aldous Vane woke before the bells and dressed in the dark, as he had every morning for twenty years, because the dark was honest. It did not pretend to be anything other than what it was.

Tonight it would end.

He stood at the window of his rooms above the Inquisition house and watched Charm Row gutter and glow in the lamplight below—the crooked street of painted signs and hanging luck-knots, the shopfronts with their cheerful little wards, the whole gaudy artery of Saltmere's quiet lawbreaking. He had walked that street a hundred times. He had bought a charm there once, from a soft-spoken man with careful hands, and left behind a coin that hummed like a trapped wasp whenever the gift stirred nearby.

The coin had sung for three weeks. It had named forty-one doors.

Vane had spent those three weeks doing what he always did: reading. Ledgers, tax rolls, baptismal records, letters intercepted at the harbor. He built his lists the way a mason builds a wall—stone by stone, no gaps, no sentiment. Forty-one doors. Behind thirty-eight of them he had found what he expected: hedge-witches, weather-singers, a midwife who eased labor with a whispered word, a boy who could make candles burn blue.

Three doors had surprised him. One was empty now—the fish-gutter girl with the sea-grey eyes who had vanished from her rented room two nights ago, leaving behind a mattress that still smelled of brine. One belonged to the cartographer Wrenn, whose confession had come so easily Vane almost pitied him. Almost.

And one belonged to Elodie Blackwood, charm-seller, whose cellar floor, according to the surveyor's report Vane had read four times, did not appear on any map older than twenty years.

*There*, Vane thought, buckling his belt. *That's where you've been hiding it.*

His sister had hidden too. In a root cellar outside Corrow Fen, under sacks of turnips, while the village she'd grown up in stood around the hatch and waited. Vane had been fourteen. He had stood there too. He had heard what came up out of that cellar when they finally opened it—not screaming, not begging, but singing, low and strange and wet, like a hymn learned underwater—and he had watched his mother's face as the smoke rose, and he had understood, in the way children understand terrible things completely and wrongly, that magic was a door and nothing good ever came through a door.

He touched the iron ring at his collar. *Tonight we close them all.*

He went down to his soldiers in the yard, forty of them with lanterns unlit and torches ready, and he did not give a speech. Speeches were for men who needed to believe in themselves.

"Charm Row," he said. "Every shop. Every cellar. No fire until the people are out." A pause. "Then fire."

They moved into the streets like water finding its level, silent, certain, and Vane walked at the center of them with his ledger under his arm, feeling—for the first time in twenty years—not dread, but something dangerously close to relief.

### II. Maren

Maren felt it coming the way she felt storms coming, in her teeth and in the scar tissue over her ribs where the tide-priest's brand had been cut away.

She was gutting herring on the dock at moonset when the gulls went up all at once, a white ragged sheet of them wheeling over the rooftops toward the city's heart, and beneath her knees the boards of the pier began to tremble—not with footsteps, not with carts, but with a deep rhythmic shudder, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

"No," she said, to no one, to the fish, to the sea. "No, not yet."

Because she knew that trembling. She had been raised on it. It was the pulse of the Undergate, the heartbeat old Maren-the-elder had pressed her palm against when Maren was seven years old, saying, *feel that, girl? That is your people breathing on the far side of a locked door.* And someone was about to put an axe through the wall beside that breathing heart.

She ran.

She ran through streets waking to shouts, past windows flaring with candlelight, past a crier with a cracked voice bawling *"By order of the Crown, all practitioners of the forbidden gift—"*. She ran the way water runs, downhill, along the gutters, following the salt-smell that only she could taste in the air, and it led her—of course it led her—to Charm Row, where the first torchlight was already staining the sky the color of an open wound.

She stopped at the mouth of the alley across from Blackwood's shop and pressed herself into the shadow of a rain barrel.

They were dragging people out.

An old woman Maren had sold eels to, barefoot in the street, hands bound with cord, her lips moving in what might have been prayer or might have been the small cantrip that had gotten her listed. The candle-boy from the chandler's, fifteen at most, sobbing without sound. The midwife. The cooper's wife. Out of thirty-eight doors, thirty-eight families' worth of ordinary courage, and the soldiers took them gently, almost courteously, which was somehow worse than cruelty—cruelty you could hate, but courtesy you had to *watch*.

And above it all, calm as a man inspecting a warehouse, walked Inquisitor Vane with his ledger open, ticking names.

Maren's hands found the drain grate beside her without her deciding it. Cold iron, rusted at the bolts. Below it, if the city's old veins ran the way Tobias Wrenn's stolen maps said they ran, a service channel running straight toward the district the maps forgot—the district over the gate.

*You have one job*, she told herself. *Get to him. Warn him. Get him out.* Not the old woman. Not the boy. Not the thirty-eight doors. One door, and one keeper, because if the keeper died tonight the names died with him and everything—her order, her exile, the drowned choir, all of it—would have been for nothing.

It was the most cowardly arithmetic of her life, and she hated it, and she did it anyway.

She worked the grate loose with a guttering knife and dropped into the dark, into water to her calves that tasted of the deep sea, of the far side of the world, and somewhere ahead of her, faint through stone, she could hear it: three slow taps, patient as tide.

### III. Elodie

Elodie smelled the smoke before he heard the bells, and for one absurd moment he thought: *the chimney's blocked again,* because twenty years of habit dies hard, because a man who has made a life of small lies will reach for the smallest lie even with the truth standing in his doorway with a torch.

Then came the shouting, and the splintering of the cooper's door three shops down, and Elodie stood in the middle of his cluttered, beloved shop—luck-knots swaying on their strings, jars of river-stones, the counter worn smooth by two decades of palms—and understood that it was ending.

He did not panic. Panic was for people with somewhere to run. He simply began, with hands that shook only slightly, to do what he had rehearsed in his head a thousand times without ever believing he'd need it.

The false panel behind the shelf of weather-charms swung open. Down the stairs, lamp in hand, into the cellar that was the truest room of his life.

The Undergate filled the far wall like a wound in the world: black stone veined with silver, older than Saltmere, older than the crown, seeping seawater in slow bright threads that had been seeping for months now and pooling around his boots. His mother's iron key hung at his throat, warm against his sternum, warmer than it should have been.

"Three taps," he whispered to it, the way he'd begun whispering to it every night lately, like a madman greeting a ghost. "I know. I hear you. Not yet."

Above him, the front door of his shop broke inward with a sound like a rib cracking.

Boots on the floorboards. A soldier's voice, bored and professional: "Clear. Cellar hatch here, sir." Then Vane's voice, softer than it had any right to be, drifting down the stairwell like smoke itself: *"Careful on the stairs. Bring a light."*

Elodie looked around the cellar—at the gate, at the rising water, at the single narrow drain-grate near the floor's lowest corner that carried overflow out toward the harbor culverts. He had never once considered it an escape route. It was barely wider than his shoulders. It was choked with twenty years of silt.

It was also, he realized with a strange floating calm, the only way out that did not lead through Vane.

The hatch at the top of the stairs opened. Lantern light spilled down.

"Inquisitor," Elodie called up, keeping his voice pleasant, shopkeeper-pleasant, the voice of a man with nothing under his floor but wine casks. "Can I offer you something? I've a decent plum brandy—"

"Master Blackwood." Vane descended slowly, one hand on the rail, and his face in the lantern light was not cruel. That was the horror of it. He looked tired. He looked like a man arriving at last at the end of a very long errand. "I'm going to ask you to step away from that wall."

"What wall? This is just—" But Elodie followed the man's gaze and saw what the lantern showed, what the seeping months had done: the silver veins in the black stone had widened, brightened, and the water running down them caught the light and threw it back in patterns no wine cellar had ever made. There was no hiding it anymore. There hadn't been, perhaps, since the night it knocked.

Vane studied him for a long moment. "Twenty years," Vane said quietly. "My records say this building changed hands twenty years ago. My records say a great many things stopped being written down twenty years ago. Do you know how long I've been looking for a hole in the paper?" He stepped closer. "Who sealed it?"

"My mother," Elodie said, because lying now was like arguing with the tide.

Something flickered across Vane's face—not triumph. Recognition, almost. Grief's poor cousin. "Then you know what's behind it."

"I know what my mother paid," Elodie said. "Do you?"

For a heartbeat—one heartbeat, no more—Vane's hand drifted to his own collar, to the iron ring there, and his eyes went somewhere else entirely. Somewhere with turnip sacks and singing.

Then boots thundered overhead, and a soldier shouted *"Fire's set in the chandler's, sir!"*, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"Seal the hatch," Vane ordered. "Mason's team, up here, now. Nothing comes out of that door. Nothing goes in." He turned back to Elodie, and his voice gentled into something almost kind. "You'll come quietly. There's been enough burning tonight."

*Has there*, Elodie thought wildly. Above him the floor was already beginning to breathe smoke; the heat of it pressed down through the boards like a hand.

And then, through the gate itself—through the black stone, through the silver seams, through twenty years of his mother's silence—he felt it: not knocking now but *pushing*, a pressure like the whole drowned world leaning its shoulder against the door, and the key at his throat burned hot enough to sting.

The soldiers came down the stairs. Elodie backed away, hands half-raised, and his heel found the edge of the drain grate.

"I'm coming," he said—to Vane, to the soldiers, to no one. And under his breath, so softly that only the gate could hear: "*Hold on. I'm coming back.*"

Then he dropped, let himself fall sideways into the dark water, and pulled the grate shut behind him as the mason's hammer began to ring against the cellar door like a funeral bell.

### IV. Vane

They sealed it in ninety minutes. Vane supervised every stroke of the mortar, every course of brick, though the masons were the best in the city and needed no supervision. He had the hatch bricked over too, and the stairwell filled with rubble, and when it was done he stood in the smoke-hazed cellar with water lapping at his boots and pressed his palm flat against the new wall.

Cold. Ordinary cold. Mortar-cold.

Behind it, faintly, he thought he heard singing—but he had thought he heard singing for twenty years, in cellars and chapels and his own dreams, and he had learned to distrust that particular sense the way a man learns to distrust an eye that has lied to him.

"The charm-seller's gone, sir," the sergeant reported, dripping, apologetic. "Drain grate. He must have gone into the culverts. We can send men—"

"No." Vane looked at the wall. Whatever was behind it had wanted the man gone—or the man had wanted to go, which amounted to the same thing, which meant the door had won a round after all these centuries, and the knowledge sat in Vane's chest like a swallowed coal. "Let the sea have the culverts. We'll find him when he surfaces." He climbed the stairs out of the drowning cellar, out of the shop that was already roaring above him, out onto Charm Row where the flames had jumped the eaves and were walking shopfront to shopfront like a landlord collecting rent.

Forty-one doors. Thirty-eight opened. Three empty.

He wrote *Blackwood — fled via culvert* in the ledger with a steady hand, and then, because the night demanded it, because twenty years demanded it, he stood in the street with the heat on his face and watched the row burn, and waited to feel finished.

He did not feel finished. He felt, distinctly, like a man who has nailed a door shut and heard, on the other side, the handle begin to turn.

### V. Maren

She found him a quarter mile out, where the culvert mouth spilled into the harbor shallows, hauling himself through the black water on his elbows with an iron key clenched in his teeth like a pirate in a child's story.

She waded out and caught his arm, and he came up swinging, and then he saw her face and stopped.

"They took them," he said. His voice was wrecked, smoke-scraped. "The midwife. Old Sela. The boy—Maren, the *boy*, he was fifteen, he only made candles burn blue, it wasn't even—" He was shaking now, all the calm burned off him, twenty years of invisibility gone to ash along with his shop and his neighbors and his name on no list but Vane's. "I hid. Twenty years I hid and it didn't save a single one of them, and the gate is *bricked over*, they've walled it up like a grave—"

"Elodie." She held his face in both her salt-cold hands, the way she'd hold a drowning man, because that was exactly what he was. "Look at me. They sealed the wrong side."

He blinked through the water streaming down his face. "What?"

"You escaped through the drains. So did I. So did—" she pulled his hand, the one holding the key, and pressed it flat against the culvert wall behind her, against the stone slick with seawater that had no business being that far inland, seawater that was rising, inch by inch, hour by hour, up through every vein and crack and forgotten channel beneath the city. "Walls keep things in or keep things out. But the tide doesn't care about walls, keeper. The tide goes *under* them. And whatever your mother started—whatever she paid for—it isn't finished. It's just begun."

Far off across the burning water, Charm Row glowed like a second sunset. Somewhere beneath it, behind fresh mortar, three slow taps were falling on a bricked-over door, patient as the sea.

Elodie looked at the key in his fist, and then at the rising water, and Maren watched something move through his face—rage and grief and, underneath them both, terrible and unmistakable, resolve.

"The royal vault," he said hoarsely. "Tobias. The names. If the gate's sealed, then we do this the other way—we take the names back to the source." He stood, swaying, soaked, a man with nothing left except the oldest inheritance in Saltmere. "We go tomorrow. Tonight we watch it burn."

And behind them, unnoticed, the harbor water crept another inch up the stones—toward the city, not away from it, gentle and inevitable as a hand reaching for a door.
