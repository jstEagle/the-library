## 18. The Tide Rises in Saltmere

Maren felt the gate break the way a diver feels a net part: a sudden loosening all along her skin, a pressure she had carried since childhood lifting and then reversing itself into something vast and moving.

She was on the Harbormaster's steps when it happened, watching the dawn come up gray over the masts, and the first thing that changed was the sound. Saltmere had always been a city that muttered—the groan of hulls, the clatter of the fish market waking, gulls arguing over scraps. But beneath all of it there had been a silence, a held breath, the sound of a door kept shut for twenty years. Maren had lived her whole life inside that held breath without knowing what it was. Now the breath went out.

The sea stood up.

Not a wave—she would have understood a wave, could have named its height and read its danger in the old ways her mothers taught her before the purges came. This was not weather. The harbor simply rose, all at once, calm as a cup filled to the brim and then past it, and the water came up Ramp Street in a slow silver sheet that did not rush or roar but *walked*, climbing stair by stair as if it remembered every step from some former life. Fishing boats lifted gently off their moorings and drifted sideways through the streets like leaves on a stream that ran uphill.

The people around her on the steps were screaming. Maren found she could not. Her throat had closed around something older than fear.

*They're coming home.*

She had spent four days in this city, disguised in a gutting apron that still smelled of brine and blood, telling herself she had time—weeks, maybe, to find the keeper, to learn whether he was a tyrant or a coward or a man worth persuading. Four days, and now the choice had been taken out of everyone's hands. She should have been terrified. Part of her was. But underneath the terror ran a current of joy so fierce it shamed her, because she knew what was rising out of that water, and the people screaming around her did not.

They came with the tide.

The first of them walked out of the flooded mouth of Cooper's Lane at midmorning, and the crowd on the Harbormaster's steps went quiet in the way animals go quiet. He was tall, taller than any man Maren had seen, and his skin had the gray-green shimmer of deep water, and where his hair should have been there flowed something like kelp, like light caught in current. His eyes were the color of the sea over sand at low tide, and they were full of grief—not menace, nothing like menace, but the particular sorrow of someone arriving at a house that has been rebuilt over their grave.

He did not walk so much as *arrive*, one moment at the mouth of the lane, the next standing among the overturned carts, water streaming from him and running back downhill to join the flood as though reluctant to leave him.

A fishwife nearest him raised an oar in shaking hands.

"Peace," the Tidewrought said, and his voice was the sound the sea makes inside a shell, and Maren wept to hear it because it was the voice of her order's oldest hymn, sung by something living—or something past living, which amounted to the same faith. "We have not come to take. We have only come to ask."

"Ask for what?" the fishwife said. "What do you want?"

And the Tidewrought looked at her with those low-tide eyes and said, gently, terribly:

"Your name. It is mine. I only want what is mine."

* * *

By noon there were hundreds of them.

They walked the flooded avenues in ones and twos and sometimes in processions of thirty, moving through water waist-deep on the humans and shin-deep on themselves, and everywhere they went they asked the same question in the same gentle voices. They stopped at doorways and spoke to the families huddled behind them. They knelt beside the barricades the militia had thrown up—useless barricades, walls against a river—and asked the soldiers their names. Not their given names; Maren understood quickly that they meant something else, something deeper, the true names that the theology of the drowned choir said had been stolen a thousand years ago and bound into stone. But the citizens heard only the question, and the question sounded like theft, like a demand, like the opening move of an invasion.

"They want our souls," a woman screamed from a window on Rope Walk. "That's how they take you—they ask your name and you give it and then you're theirs!"

"It isn't like that!" Maren shouted back, wading toward the building with her arms out. "Listen to them—they aren't threatening anyone—"

The woman slammed the shutters. Down the street, a militiaman put a crossbow bolt through the chest of a Tidewrought woman who had reached toward his arm—not grabbing, Maren saw it clearly, only reaching the way you reach toward a fire in winter—and the bolt passed through her as if she were made of morning fog, and she looked down at it with sorrow and said, "Oh. You are still so afraid," and walked on.

That was the thing the city could not bear, Maren realized. Not violence. The Tidewrought offered no violence at all. What they offered was worse: patience. Grief. A thousand years of exile worn lightly, worn courteously, worn like a wet cloak they had long since stopped noticing. Every gentle word they spoke was an accusation no human could answer. They asked for their names back the way a beggar asks for bread, and every citizen who heard it heard instead the sound of everything they owned being called into question—their houses, their history, the ground under Saltmere itself, which Maren now knew was not ground at all but a lid.

She tried to make herself useful. That was the shape of her failure, later: she had run toward the disaster with open arms while it curdled around her.

At the shrine of Saint Brine on Chandler's Court she found a knot of refugees from the low streets, forty people crowded into a chapel built above the flood line, and she climbed onto the font and told them what her mothers' order had preserved in secret songs for twenty generations. "They are not invaders," she said. "They are the exiled dead. Your city is built on their prison. They only want to go home, and home is here, and the only thing keeping them out has been a theft so old no one alive remembers committing it."

"Theft," spat a harbor official with a chain of office, his fine boots soaked to the knee. "You speak like one of them. Girl, look outside. The sea is in my counting-house."

"The sea is in your counting-house because your counting-house was built on a drowned church," Maren said, "which was built on a drowned church before that. Saltmere didn't rise from the coast. Saltmere sank onto them. Ask your own stones."

He ordered her dragged out. The chapel doors opened onto three feet of green water and a Tidewrought elder waiting there with his hands folded, patient as erosion, and when the guards saw him they dropped Maren's arms and fled back inside, and she was left alone in the flood with him, and for one suspended moment the two of them regarded each other across a thousand years of wrong.

"You are choir-born," he said. "I can taste the liturgy on you."

"Yes." Her voice cracked. "Yes, I'm—my order kept the songs. Kept the memory. We thought—we hoped—"

"We know what you hoped." He smiled, and it was a terrible kind smile, the smile of someone consoling a child at a funeral. "Little priestess. We waited so long that hope became a form of drowning. And yet." He turned his head, listening to something in the water that Maren could not hear. "Yet the gate stands open. Yet the keeper comes. Tell me—is it true? Do they say the keeper walks toward us carrying the key?"

"I don't know," Maren said honestly. "I haven't seen him since the burning."

"Then pray," the elder said, "in whichever direction prayers still travel. Because the one who hunts us does not intend to let us knock again."

* * *

She heard the fires begin an hour before she saw them.

Sound carries strangely over floodwater—a flat, hard acoustics, every noise arriving stripped of distance—and so the first crackle of flame on the far side of the canal district sounded as if it were happening inside her own ears. Then came the bells, all of them at once, the alarm pattern, and then a voice amplified by speaking-trumpet, raw with authority:

BY ORDER OF THE INQUISITION: ALL PERSONS WITHDRAW FROM THE CANAL AND CHANDLERS' DISTRICTS. OIL IS BEING LAID. NOTHING THAT WALKS THE WATER MAY BE PERMITTED TO REACH THE HIGH CITY. THIS IS NOT A REQUEST.

"No," Maren said aloud, to no one. "No, no, no—"

She ran. There was no other way to say what she did next; she ran through a drowning city toward a burning one, splashing down Ramp Street against the flow of evacuating humanity, shoulder-checking past carts and cattle and weeping families, because she knew what oil on floodwater meant, everyone whose grandmother had ever worked a lamp knew what it meant, and Vane—she had never met the Inquisitor, but Tobias had described him in the safehouse with the flat voice of a man describing weather that kills—Vane had looked at a district full of grieving dead and decided the answer was fire on top of water.

The chandlers' district was already slick with it. She smelled the whale-oil reek from three streets away, saw the Inquisition crews in their ash-gray coats working the pumps, pouring rivers of tallow and lampstock into the flooded lanes where the oil spread and spread, iridescent, patient, riding the tide that rose around their boots. The Tidewrought still walked those waters. Still asked their gentle questions. A squad of soldiers herded the last human residents toward the high city at spear-point, and beyond them, unbothered, unhurried, an old Tidewrought woman stood in a doorway that had been underwater since dawn, singing—to herself, Maren thought at first, until she caught the words and understood it was a name, one name, repeated like a prayer, the name of whatever family had once lived in that house, asking for it back, asking, asking.

"Get clear!" a soldier screamed at Maren as she splashed into the cordon. "Are you mad, girl? Get clear!"

"I'm a tide-priestess," she gasped, grabbing his sleeve. "Those things in the water—you have to listen to me—they are not attacking anyone, they have hurt no one, if you burn this water you will be burning the last chance this city ever has of ending this—"

"Orders," the soldier said, and his face was young and miserable and already looking away from her. "Priestess, I've got orders. Go."

She went—not away, but around, scrambling up a rope-store wall to the rooftops, because if she could not stop the burning perhaps she could stand somewhere visible when it happened, perhaps she could be a witness, perhaps witness counted for something in a world that seemed determined to forget everything it learned.

From the ridge of the rope-store roof she saw the whole catastrophe laid out below her like a map: the flooded districts shining green-gray in the afternoon light, the dark threads of oil spreading across them, the Tidewrought walking their avenues in their slow processions, dozens of them, hundreds, heads bowed, voices lifted in that shell-sound speech, asking the drowned houses for their names. She saw the Inquisition lines drawn up at the high edge of the water, torches lit even in daylight, waiting on a signal. And at their center, on a platform of planking laid over a gun-carriage, she saw Inquisitor Vane.

Tobias had prepared her for a monster. What she saw was worse. Vane stood very straight in his gray coat, hands clasped behind him, studying the flooded district with the expression of a surgeon reading a wound—no hatred visible, no relish, only a terrible completeness, a man about to do exactly what he had trained his whole life to do. He raised one hand. The torchbearers stepped forward.

Maren screamed. She stood on the ridgeline of a burning city's roof with her arms flung wide and screamed the opening of the Drowned Choir's oldest hymn, the summons-song, the one her mothers sang to call the faithful to evening prayer, and her voice cracked across the water, and some of the Tidewrought below turned their faces up toward her, and for a heartbeat she thought—

The first torch went into the water.

Flame took the canal district the way a rumor takes a town. Fire ran across the surface of the flood in a sheet of blue-gold horror, chasing the oil-threads outward faster than anything living could outrun, and the sound was not like burning wood or burning houses; it was a roar, a single vast inhalation, the sea itself catching light. Heat rolled over Maren's face on the rooftop fifty yards away. Steam exploded upward in blinding columns where fire met tide, and through the steam she saw them—the Tidewrought, wreathed, walking still, because what is water cannot easily be taught to burn, but the *oil*, the oil clung, and their kelp-hair caught like wicks, and they did not scream.

That was the unbearable part. They did not scream, did not rage, did not fight. Burning, they continued to ask. Maren watched a figure she recognized—the elder from Chandler's Court, his robe alight, his patient face haloed in flame—stop at the base of a tenement stair and lift his charred hands toward the shuttered windows and say, in that voice like the sea in a shell, words the fire did not fully erase:

"We forgive you. We forgive you. But please—we only want our names."

The window shattered outward. Someone inside had hurled a chair through it rather than hear him.

Maren slid down the roof slope on her heels, sobbing, useless, her palms shredded on the slates, and below her the tide—which had risen all day in obedience to something older than the moon—seemed finally to understand what was being done to it. The water pulled back from the burning district in a single long exhale, dragging fire and oil and steam into the harbor, and where it withdrew it left the streets exposed, blackened, cratered with heat, littered with shapes that steamed and smoldered and did not move. And in the sudden quiet after the roar, Maren heard the remaining Tidewrought—those who had been in the far streets, those the fire had missed—begin to sing.

It was not a war-song. She wished to every salt-choked god it had been a war-song. It was a lament, low and rising, thousands of years old, the song her order had preserved a fragment of and called the Lament for the Unnamed, and now she heard it whole for the first time, sung by its own people in the smoking ruins of the city that had burned them, and it said, in the old tongue, in words she had studied all her life without understanding:

*We gave you our names so you could build.*
*Build us a door, and we will ask no more.*

The tide turned. She felt it turn—not the daily turn but something vaster, a gathering, the whole weight of the outer ocean leaning in toward Saltmere like a judge leaning toward a verdict. Out past the harbor mouth the horizon darkened from gray to slate to a color she had no name for, a color from the oldest songs, and the water began to climb the high city stairs that the morning tide had never reached, and somewhere beneath her feet, beneath the burning and the smoke, Maren felt the Undergate swing wider, and wider, and she understood with cold clarity that whatever happened next would happen within hours.

Vane's trumpets were sounding retreat to the high city. The crowds were praying, looting, fleeing, all three at once. And through it all, cutting against every current, a single figure was wading down the flooded great avenue toward the drowned cellar where the gate stood open—an ordinary man, soaked to the ribs, iron key glinting in his fist, moving with the exhausted certainty of someone walking toward the one thing left that he can still do.

Elodie Blackwood, going to pay.

Maren wiped her face with a bleeding hand, spat seawater, and went after him.
