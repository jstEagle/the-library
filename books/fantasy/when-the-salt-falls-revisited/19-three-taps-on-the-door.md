## 19. Three Taps on the Door

The water had taken the Harrow Steps by the time Elodie reached them.

He stood at the top of the stair with his mother's iron key clenched in his fist, and he watched the sea climb. It did not surge the way storm-tides surged, all fury and foam. It climbed politely, rung by rung, as if it had been invited—as if it had knocked and been answered at last. Below him, Saltmere's lower streets lay under a hand's depth of green-black water that moved without current, that moved *deliberately*, sliding up gutters and over thresholds like a guest who knew the house.

And everywhere, the singing.

It came up through the stones themselves, through the soles of his boots, a chord too low to be heard properly and too persistent to be ignored. Maren's people. The drowned choir. They were in the streets now—Elodie could see them through the rain-silvered air, far off down Cordwainer's Lane: tall shapes walking upright through the flood, their bodies gleaming like wet slate, their movements gentle, unhurried. They stopped at doorways. They knelt before weeping citizens. Their mouths opened, and what came out was not speech but something older, something that made Elodie's chest ache in a way he had no word for.

*They're asking,* Maren had told him on the sea caves' cold floor, her hands shaking around a cup of brackish tea. *They've waited two hundred years, and when they finally come home, they don't demand. They ask.*

He had promised her he would answer them. He had promised himself he would live long enough to try.

Elodie pulled his coat tighter, tucked the key against his sternum where it sat warm as a second heart, and started down into the drowning city.

* * *

Charm Row was burning.

Of course it was. Vane had ordered the district drowned in fire rather than let the tide have it, and fire obeyed orders better than water ever had. Flames leapt from the upper windows of the Luck-Knot, from old Mistress Ferrow's candle shop, from the loft where Elodie had stored twenty years of harmless trinkets with careful magic folded inside them like letters never sent. The heat reached him even across the flooded street, pressing against his face like an open oven door. Ash fell into the black water and floated there, small grey boats going nowhere.

He picked his way along the raised edge of the fish market, past stalls half-submerged, past a luck-knot he'd tied himself still hanging from a post, its threads swollen dark with seawater but holding. Holding. Everything he'd ever made held, right up until it didn't.

A Tidewrought stood in the middle of Cordwainer's Lane.

Up close they were not the monsters of the chapel murals—not the fanged, many-jointed devils the priests painted on tithing day. This one was slender and tall as a masthead, its skin the deep blue-green of water over stone, and its face—its face was *sorrowful*, carved with lines like erosion, like something that had spent two centuries being patient. It turned toward Elodie as he passed. Its eyes were the pale grey of sea glass.

It sang one note at him. Low. Questioning.

"I'm going," Elodie said aloud, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "I know. I'm going."

The Tidewrought inclined its head—a gesture so human, so *courteous*, that Elodie had to stop walking for a moment and press his knuckles to his mouth. Then it turned back to the doorway it had been kneeling before, where old Tomas the cooper stood in the floodwater to his knees, clutching a hatchet he clearly had no intention of using, weeping without sound.

Elodie waded on.

The streets below the market had no bottom anymore—or rather, their bottom had become the sea's, and the boundary between cellar and ocean had dissolved entirely. Water rose past his waist. It should have been freezing; it wasn't. It was blood-warm, bath-warm, warm as the shallows off Merrow Point in high summer, and Elodie understood with a lurch that the sea was being gentle with him. That it recognized what rode against his sternum.

Somewhere behind him, a warship in the harbor caught fire from stem to stern, a burning spine laid across the black water, and the light of it turned the flooded street the color of amber. Somewhere ahead, muffled by walls and water and distance, came the crash of the Inquisition's ram against a door that needed no ramming—the Undergate's cellar entrance, which the soldiers had found because a cartographer's honest map had shown them the way.

Three taps. That was how it had started, twenty years of silence broken by three slow knocks, like a greeting. Like a friend at a door after a long journey.

He was coming home the long way around, through fire and flood, to answer them.

* * *

They had posted six men at the cellar stairs, and five of them fled when the water reached their waists and began to sing.

Elodie heard them go—boots slapping through shallows, one of them praying, another shouting about devils—and then he stood in the dark mouth of the stairwell, chest-deep in luminous green water, and looked up at the sixth man.

Inquisitor Vane stood on the third step above the floodline, torch in one hand, sword sheathed, his soft grey coat soaked to the elbows. He did not look afraid. He looked *tired*, in the way Elodie imagined a man looks who has carried something heavy for twenty years and has just set it down to see whether his shoulders remember any other shape.

"Keeper," Vane said. His voice was quiet, pitched only for Elodie, almost lost beneath the singing water. "I wondered if you'd come."

"You knew I would."

"The gate is yours. Of course you'd come." Vane descended one step. The water took him at the knee and he did not flinch. "Do you know what I expected to find down here? A demon. A weapon. Some engine your family built to hold the sea hostage." Another step. "Instead I find a door. Just a door. And a man standing in front of it like a son outside his mother's room."

"That's nearly what it is," Elodie said.

Vane's torchlight guttered in the wet air. Behind the Inquisitor, up the stairs, the city burned orange; ahead, beneath the flood, the Undergate's iron bands glowed faintly through the water, and the knocking had become constant now—not three taps but a rhythm, urgent, a heartbeat made of salt.

"I have orders," Vane said. "Drown it or burn it, whichever the water permits. I have watched this city eat its own children for twenty years because of what sleeps under it. I have burned—" His voice caught, very slightly, and Elodie saw the ledger open behind his eyes: names written in a careful hand, page after page, and one name at the beginning of it all, in ink gone brown with age. "I have burned people who deserved none of it, because I believed the gift was a curse and curses must be cut out. I believed it was the only mercy left."

"And now?"

"Now the dead are walking through the streets asking politely for their names back." Vane laughed—one short, broken sound. "Nothing in my training prepared me for *polite*."

Elodie moved forward through the water, slowly, showing both hands, the iron key held flat on his palm where the torchlight could catch it. "Then let me show you one more thing before you decide what I deserve."

"If you work charm on me—"

"It isn't charm. It's the opposite of charm. It's truth, and it will hurt, and I'm sorry for it." Elodie stopped an arm's length away, water churning gently between them. "Your sister's name was Lira Vane. She was nineteen. She liked to stand at the harbor wall during storms. She heard the sea talking to her, and she talked back, and everyone called it madness until someone powerful enough decided it was witchcraft."

Vane had gone absolutely still. Even the torch flame seemed to steady itself out of respect. "How do you know that name? Her name was struck from every record. I paid to have it struck. I thought—I wanted to protect her from—" His voice failed.

"She was Tidewrought-touched," Elodie said softly. "Like me. Like my mother. The gift isn't a curse, Inquisitor. It's an inheritance, and it comes from *them*—from the people your ancestors stole blind and locked beneath our feet. And when the purge came for her, she couldn't be saved by hiding, so she was saved the other way. Her name was taken through the gate. It's held beyond it, with all the others. With the stolen names of a whole people. With—" his breath hitched— "*my mother's.*"

"You're lying." But there was no force in it. Vane's hand had begun to tremble, and the torchlight shivered with it. "You're lying to save your life."

"I'm not saving mine. Look at where I'm standing." Elodie lifted the key higher. "Touch the key. That's all. Touch it, and you'll hear what I hear."

For a long moment the only sounds were fire above and water below and the gate's patient knocking, three beats and a rest, three beats and a rest, like something that had learned never to expect an answer but kept asking anyway.

Then Vane dropped the torch. It hissed out in the flood, and in the sudden darkness lit only by the gate's own pale glow, the Inquisitor of Saltmere reached out with a scarred hand and laid two fingers against the iron key.

The singing rushed in.

Elodie felt it pass through Vane like a wave through a piling—felt the man stagger, felt his knees hit the submerged step, felt the great controlled machinery of him crack open along a seam twenty years old. Vane made a sound that was not a word, that was barely a sound at all, a young man's sound, and in the glow Elodie could see tears cutting clean lines down the salt-dust of his face.

*Lira.* The name went through the water like a bell through a body. *She's here. She's—she's singing. She's been singing the whole time.*

"She's been waiting," Elodie said, and his own voice was wrecked now too. "They all have. Not to rise up. Not to take revenge. To come *home*. My ancestor stole their names and built this city on top of them like a lid on a pot, and every keeper since has paid in memories to keep the lid shut, and it has bought nothing but more waiting." He knelt in the floodwater beside the shaking man, until they were level, keeper and hunter, both half-drowned, both grieving. "I can give them back. All of them. Lira too. But I can't hold the gate alone, and I won't fight you for the chance to try."

The knocking swelled. Three beats. Three beats. Three beats.

Vane looked up. In the gate's pale light his face was stripped bare of everything—rank, zeal, the whole careful architecture of the hunt—and what remained underneath was simply a brother, twenty years late, standing outside a door with his hat in his hands.

"What do you need me to do?" he asked.

"Hold it open," Elodie said, rising, turning to the gate at last, the iron bands humming under his palms, the key fitting the lock like it had been cast yesterday instead of two generations ago. "Whatever happens. However loud it gets. Hold it open."

The lock turned. The seam of the door split with a sound like the first breath after drowning, and light the color of moonrise poured up through the flooding cellar, and beyond the threshold Elodie heard—not the exiled dead, not yet—something vaster: the whole drowned choir drawing one collective breath.

Vane braced his shoulder against the door, boots planted in the rising tide, and held.

And Elodie Blackwood stepped to the threshold of everything, and opened his mouth to begin.

The first name on his lips was not a stranger's.

It was his mother's.
