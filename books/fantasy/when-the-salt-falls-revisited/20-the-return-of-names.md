## 20. The Return of Names

The water reached Elodie's knees before he found the last stair, and by the time his boots touched the cellar floor it was at his waist—cold, black, singing. Above him Saltmere burned and drowned at once: he could hear the roar of Vane's fires eating the harbor ships, hear screaming that might have been panic or might have been prayer, but down here there was only the door.

The Undergate stood in the flooded dark like a thing out of a childhood fever dream. Iron banded with silver, silver inlaid with script so old no living scholar could read it, and across its face the long scar of his mother's sealing—a seam of pale glass where her magic had fused the metal shut twenty years ago. The water pressed against it from both sides now. It had stopped being a wall between two worlds. It had become a membrane, thin as skin, and everything on either side was straining toward the other.

Three taps came through the iron. Slow. Patient. The way someone knocks when they know you're already coming.

"I'm here," Elodie said. His voice sounded small in all that water. "I brought them back."

He had the names. Not written—the vault stones had crumbled into brine behind him, Tobias hauling Maren up through the collapsing archive while Elodie pried loose what he could—but held somewhere stranger: dissolved into him the moment his bare hands touched the foundation salt, the way a charm dissolves into the one who wears it. Two hundred years of stolen inheritance, sunk into his blood like ink into paper. He could feel every one of them waiting, pressing against the inside of his ribs, wanting out.

They wanted their names back. And they wanted his.

The key was already in his hand. His mother's iron key, warm despite the cold, humming against his palm like a sleeping bird. He fitted it to the lock that had never once been opened in his lifetime, and turned.

* * *

The gate did not burst open. That was the terrible, beautiful thing about it—it opened the way a fist opens, slowly, finger by finger, the glass-seam cracking along lines of light the color of moonrise on open sea. Water poured through the widening gap, but it did not flood; it rose around Elodie like something welcoming, lifting him until his feet barely brushed the stone.

And through the opening came the dead.

Not the shambling horror the priests had promised, not the drowning tide the Inquisition had burned half the harbor to hold back. They came walking out of the deep like people coming home from a long journey—tall, gray-green, crowned in weed and pearl, their bodies trailing ribbons of luminous kelp. The Tidewrought. The exiled. Their faces were faces he knew from no painting and every dream: grief made patient, patience made vast.

One of them knelt before him in the flooded cellar. Its eyes were the exact green-black of deep water over sand, and when it spoke, its voice came not through air but through the water itself, through Elodie's own blood:

*Keeper's son.*

"I'm not—" Elodie started, and stopped. His mother's face swam up in him, unbidden. Her hands kneading dough in the shop above, her voice teaching him which knots held luck and which held sorrow. *You'll understand when it's your turn,* she'd said, the only time she'd ever spoken of the gate, and he'd thought she meant the work. She had meant this. She had always meant this.

"Yes," he said instead. "I have them."

*Then speak,* said the Tidewrought. *Speak, and be unmade.*

"Elodie."

The name cut through the water like a blade through silk—and Elodie understood, with a lurch, that it had come from behind him. He turned.

Inquisitor Vane stood on the stairs, soaked to the bone, sword drawn but lowered. Behind him the corridor blazed with reflected firelight, and beyond that, faintly, the screams of a city losing its mind. But his eyes were fixed on the Tidewrought kneeling in the water, and his face was the face of a man watching a door close forever on the last chance of his life.

"You," Vane said hoarsely. "You said she was here. You said her name went through." He swallowed. "Say it again."

Elodie didn't answer. He couldn't. Because the Tidewrought had lifted its head, and in the water-light Elodie watched it shape a word with lips that hadn't spoken above the waves in two centuries—and the word was *Sereth.*

Vane's sword hit the flooded floor with a clang that echoed like a bell.

"My sister," he whispered. "That's my sister's name."

*It is,* agreed the Tidewrought, gentle as undertow. *She has waited. She has grieved. She has asked for nothing but to be called home by her own sound.* The great head turned toward Elodie, and those fathomless eyes held him without mercy. *Keeper's son. Speak the names. All of them. And know the price.*

"The price," Elodie repeated numbly.

*Every name returned costs you its memory. Every memory given buys a soul its freedom. When the last name leaves your mouth, nothing will remain of you but the man who stands here—and even he will not know why he stands.*

Vane moved then. Elodie flinched—but the Inquisitor wasn't reaching for a weapon. He was wading forward, sword abandoned, boots dragging in the deepening water, until he stood beside Elodie at the threshold of the gate. He put his shoulder against the iron leaf as if to hold it wider, and his voice, when it came, was stripped of everything but raw need.

"Do it," he said. "Whatever it costs you. Do it. I'll hold the door."

For a heartbeat Elodie could only stare at him. Twenty years of fear, of hiding, of neighbors turning neighbor in—the whole machinery of terror this man had built and served—all of it hanging on the hinge of a single name.

"Your sister," Elodie said quietly. "She was Tidewrought-touched. Wasn't she. That's why they burned her."

"They burned her because she sang to the harbor seals and they answered," Vane said, and his voice cracked clean through. "Because she was kind. Because kindness looked like witchcraft to men who'd forgotten what the sea used to be." He pushed harder against the gate. "I spent twenty years proving to myself that the gift was a curse worth erasing. If it isn't—if she wasn't—" He couldn't finish.

"If it isn't," Elodie said, "then everything you did was for nothing."

"Yes."

"And you're standing here anyway."

"I'm standing here anyway."

Elodie closed his eyes. Opened them. And began.

* * *

The first name he spoke was the oldest one, and the memory it took was the taste of bread.

It happened like this: he said the syllables—they weren't human syllables, they were sounds like current over stone, like whale-song slowed to the speed of speech—and the Tidewrought before him shuddered from crown to tail, straightened, brightened. Somewhere far below, a voice that had been silent for two hundred years cried out in joy. And inside Elodie's chest, something simply... left. He reached for the taste of his mother's morning bread, warm and salt-crusted, the smell of the shop waking up around it—

Gone. Not hidden. Gone. A hole shaped exactly like breakfast, and no way to miss it because he no longer knew what he was missing.

The second name cost him rain on the shop roof. The third, the weight of his mother's hand on his hair. The fourth and fifth and sixth took the color of certain evenings, the sound of particular bells, the entire geography of a childhood street. Each name spoken freed a soul from the drowned dark; each soul freed hollowed him further; and still the names kept coming, rising up through him like bubbles through deep water, faster now, eager, and Elodie let them go because there was nothing else to do and no one else to do it.

Maren arrived at some point—he heard her splashing down the stairs, heard her cry out at what the cellar had become, heard Tobias behind her swearing softly at a miracle—but he couldn't stop to greet them. There was no *him* left to do the greeting. There was only the speaking, and the emptying, and the tide rising gently around a man becoming no one.

The Tidewrought crossed over in their hundreds. They passed through the gate and through the flooded streets beyond, and where they walked, the fire-water hissed and died. They gathered up the drowned and the grieving and the lost. They sang—not the hungry song from beneath the city, but a different one, an old one, a lullaby that made the burning buildings weep themselves quiet.

And through it all, Elodie spoke names.

Near the end, when the cellar was nearly empty of everything except light and water and the vast gentle press of departing souls, the last Tidewrought paused before him. This one was smaller than the others, older, wearing a cloak of woven moonlight. It studied him for a long moment.

*You gave well, keeper's son,* it said. *Now the last.*

Elodie's heart stuttered. "There are more?"

*One.* The ancient eyes glittered. *Yours.*

"I don't—" He laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "I don't even remember why I'm doing this anymore. I don't remember my mother. I don't remember the shop. I remember standing here, and I remember that I chose this. Isn't that enough?"

*It would be,* the Tidewrought said, *if choice were the same as self. But it isn't. Give us your name, Elodie Blackwood, and go free.*

And Elodie—who could not remember his mother's face, or the color of his front door, or the reason bread had once mattered—found that he could still remember how to be afraid, and how to be brave, and that these were apparently the same muscle.

He opened his mouth.

Behind him, very quietly, Maren began to sing.

* * *

She sang the Drowned Choir's oldest hymn, the one they only sang at funerals, the one that said: *we do not mourn the departed, we thank them.* Her voice caught the last name in Elodie's throat and carried it, gentled it, made of it not a theft but a gift freely witnessed. Tobias joined her, off-key and weeping. Even Vane—Vane, who had spent twenty years believing music was the first symptom of damnation—closed his eyes and hummed the harmony he'd learned as a boy from a sister who sang to seals.

And Elodie Blackwood spoke his own name into the water.

He felt it leave him the way a bird leaves an open hand. For one impossible moment he was everywhere at once—every charm he'd ever sold, every lie he'd ever told, every night he'd spent alone above a shop that smelled of rope and salt and safety—everything at once, and then, gently, none of it. The tide took his name the way the tide takes everything: completely, kindly, without apology.

When the light faded, the gate stood open onto calm water. The Tidewrought were gone. The cellar was quiet. Four people stood in a flooded room, breathing, alive, and one of them had no idea who he was.

The nameless man looked at the woman with the sea-colored eyes, and the tall man with the ruined hands, and the soldier with the wet sword, and smiled apologetically.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Have we met?"

Maren laughed—or sobbed, it was hard to tell—and took his hand.

"No," she said. "But we're about to spend a lot of time together. Come on. The city needs rebuilding, and you look like a man who knows his knots."

The nameless man looked down at his own fingers, long and clever and stained with old rope-burns, and felt something in him recognize them, though he could not say what.

"I think I do," he said, and followed her up the stairs toward the light.
