## 22. Small Charms by the Sea

The man with no past woke before the gulls, as he always did, though he could not have said why.

His room above the chandler's shop was small and clean and held nothing that remembered him. A bed. A basin. A coat on a hook, salt-stiff at the cuffs. He had owned these things for three months now, and before them there was nothing—not darkness, not pain, just an absence so complete it did not even ache. It was like being a page torn from a book: the edges of him were ragged where something had been cut away, but the paper itself was smooth.

He dressed in the dark out of habit rather than need. His hands knew the buttons without his eyes.

Downstairs, past the sleeping chandler and his snoring dog, out into the blue hour of morning. The rebuilt harbor spread below him like a promise half-kept. They were still working on it—the new pilings pale and raw against the older stone, cranes standing like patient herons—but the water was back where water belonged, lapping at the quays instead of climbing the streets. Six weeks since the floods receded. Six weeks since the burning stopped and the singing stopped and everyone in Saltmere began, carefully, to speak of it in the past tense.

He walked the length of the fish market while it was still empty, greeting the few souls already at work. Old Sefa, mending nets by lantern light. The twins who gutted herring with identical, efficient disgust. They greeted him back by no name at all—he had none to give them—and this had stopped feeling strange around the second week. In Saltmere, after everything, a man without a name was not remarkable. Half the city had lost someone or something to those last terrible days. The tide-priests said the sea took names the way it took shells: only what was freely given.

*Freely given.* The phrase surfaced in him sometimes, unbidden, like a fish rising to inspect the light. He never managed to catch hold of it.

His stall stood at the harbor's edge where the old charm district had been—Charm Row, people called it still, though the Row itself was ash and memory, its shops replaced by open stalls and honest daylight. He set up each morning the same way: a plank table, a striped awning, a wooden frame from which hung his wares. Luck-knots braided from three colors of cord. Weather-charms of glass and dried kelp. Small bells that rang sweetly when the wind turned fair. Nothing powerful. Nothing hidden. That was the strange freedom of the new Saltmere—the tide-priests had made magic lawful again, or at least confessable, and yet he found he preferred making the small things, the harmless things, the charms that worked by hope more than by craft.

Except.

Except that sometimes his hands did things he could not account for. When he braided a luck-knot, the pattern that emerged was one he had never been taught, a weave of seven strands that seemed to fold the cord through itself impossibly, and fishermen swore the knots *worked*—swore their lines came home heavier, their boats rode out squalls that should have swamped them. When he sold a weather-charm to a nervous bride, she found her wedding day bright as polished brass. He did not know how he did it. He only knew that when he finished a piece, he would stand holding it a moment longer than necessary, his throat tight, as if the little charm were a letter he had written to someone whose face he could not recall.

This morning he made eleven knots before the market filled. Eleven was somehow the right number. He stopped when his hands told him to stop, and they always told him, gently, like a mother easing a child away from a game.

The harbor woke around him. Crates thumped. Gulls argued over offal. The ferry from the southern isles slid in, and passengers spilled onto the quay blinking and stiff-legged, and among them—

Among them a woman in a gray coat, hair cropped short and sun-streaked, moving down the gangway with the easy roll of someone who had spent her life on water. She was laughing at something the ferryman said. Her laugh carried across the market the way a bell carries, and the man with no past looked up from his cords, and smiled.

He did not know why. That was the thing about himself that frightened him most some days—not the missing memories but the feelings attached to nothing, the love with no object, the grief with no wound. And now this smile, wide and helpless, aimed at a stranger stepping off a boat.

She saw him. Of course she saw him; the whole quay could probably see him. And her step faltered—one foot landing a half-beat late, her hand going briefly to her collarbone—and then she resumed walking, toward him, directly toward him, as if the stall were a destination she had always intended.

"You're new," she said, stopping at the table. Her eyes were green-gray, the color of the sea over sandbar, and they moved over his face with an intensity that made him feel *known*, which was absurd, because no one knew him. He barely knew himself.

"Since spring," he said. "More or less."

"I've been away." She picked up a luck-knot, turned it in her fingers. Her hands were work-rough, sure. "I went south. There were other harbors. Other people who needed—" She stopped herself, smiling faintly. "Forgive me. I'm Maren."

"Maren," he repeated, and the name settled into him like a key into a lock that had been waiting. Not recognition—there was nothing behind it, no face, no story. Just rightness. Just the sense that here was a word he had heard ten thousand times in a life he could not remember.

"You make these?" She held up the knot.

"I do."

"They're very good." Her voice had gone quiet. "They're better than good. Where did you learn the seven-strand weave?"

"I don't know," he said honestly. "My hands remember it."

Something happened to her face then—something she controlled quickly, but not quickly enough. Grief and joy at once, two tides meeting. She set the knot down with exaggerated care, the way you put down a thing that might break.

"How much?" she asked.

"For you? Nothing." The words were out before he thought about them. He blinked. "I—that is—it's a busy day, I couldn't possibly—"

"I'll pay." She pressed a coin into his palm and closed his fingers over it, and her touch was warm and salt-smelling and made his chest hurt in a way he could not diagnose. "You'll take payment. Trust me. It matters that you take payment."

He looked down at the coin. It was ordinary copper, worn smooth, except for a small stamped mark near the edge—a wave inside a circle. New-minted. He'd seen them around since the rebuilding; the city struck them in honor of the tide-priests, or the flood, or peace itself. He didn't follow politics.

"Thank you," he said.

"No." Maren laughed, wet-eyed, shaking her head. "No, thank *you*. For—for the knots. For all of it." She tucked the luck-knot into her coat, close over her heart, and stood a moment longer, looking at him as if memorizing him, as if he were a shore she was leaving and might never chart again. "Will you be here tomorrow?"

"Every day," he said. "Rain or shine. It's the only thing I'm sure of."

"Good," she said softly. "That's good."

She bought a weather-charm too, though the sky was cloudless and her ship wasn't sailing till evening, and she bought a small bell she plainly didn't need, and he wrapped each one in paper and twine and his hands trembled slightly and he pretended they didn't. When she finally stepped away, she walked backward a few paces, watching him, and raised one hand—not a wave exactly. More like a blessing. Or a salute.

Then the crowd swallowed her, and he stood alone at his stall with a copper coin in his fist and a smile still on his face that he could not explain and did not want to explain. Some things, he sensed, were better left unfished. Some waters you let lie.

He opened his hand and looked at the coin again. Wave in a circle. He rubbed the mark with his thumb, absently, the way you worry a rosary or an old scar.

And far beneath his feet—beneath the pilings and the silt and the sealed and sleeping dark—he felt, or imagined he felt, a faint warmth answer the touch. Like a door, somewhere deep, resting content in its frame. Like a house that knows it is loved even when empty.

The man with no past shrugged, smiled at nothing, and reached for his cords.

There were knots to braid. The tide was coming in, gentle and kind, and the day—whatever else it had been, whatever else it had cost—was a good one to be alive in Saltmere, selling small charms by the sea.

* * *

In the evening, when the market emptied, Maren came back.

He was striking the awning when her shadow fell across the table, and he turned and there she was again, and again the smile arrived unbidden, and again she returned it like an echo glad to be called.

"The shrine," she said, without preamble. "On the point. Have you seen it?"

"No."

"You should. Tomorrow, maybe. It's nothing grand—just stones and driftwood and a view of the water. We built it for the keeper." She watched his face as she said the word, and he realized she was watching, and wondered why. "The one who gave everything so the rest of us could keep ours."

"I don't know the story," he said. "People mention it. I never quite—I can't seem to hold on to the details."

"That's all right." Maren sat down on the crate across from his stall, uninvited and entirely welcome, the way old friends sit. "It's a long story. But I have time now. And you look like a man who could use a story with an ending."

The lamplighter was making his rounds along the quay, touching flame to the harbor lights one by one. Somewhere a fiddle started up in a tavern. The first star showed over the water, thin and silver as a fish-scale.

"Go on, then," the man said, sitting down across from her, and pulled his coat close against the evening chill.

Maren smiled, folded her hands, and began.
