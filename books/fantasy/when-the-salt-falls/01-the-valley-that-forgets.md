## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The salt came down at dusk, the way it always did, drifting off the white flats in slow gray flakes that settled on the rooftops of Veyra like a blessing no one had asked for.

Zephyrine stood on the guildhouse steps and let it fall on his shoulders, his hair, the backs of his hands. Around him the valley was putting itself to bed: lamplighters moving door to door with their long brass poles, the smell of bread and lamp-oil and brine, someone's grandmother calling a child home in a voice worn smooth by repetition. The Founding Rite was tomorrow. Banners of undyed linen hung from the eaves along Saltrow, stiff with starch, waiting to be marked with each family's sign at dawn. In twelve hours the whole valley would gather in the square below the guildhall and renew the oldest promise in Veyra — *we remember so that we are forgotten* — and Zephyrine would stand among them with his hands folded and his face arranged into something calm.

He was practicing the calm now. It did not come naturally.

"Your shoulders," said a voice behind him. "You carry them like a man expecting a blow."

He didn't turn. He knew the footsteps anyway; he'd known them since before he could walk, the uneven tap of his grandmother's cane on stone, left foot heavier than right, the rhythm of her as familiar as his own heartbeat. Odalys Vasquez came to stand beside him at the rail, wrapped in the gray shawl she wore eleven months of the year and retired only for the Rite itself.

"I'm fine," he said.

"You're a terrible liar, which is a virtue in this family. We have enough liars in the world." She looked out over the roofs. "Shoulders, Zeph."

He rolled them back. The salt kept falling. Below them, the last lamplighter finished Saltrow and began the climb toward the kilns, his little flame bobbing through the gloom like a firefly that had lost its way home.

"Do you remember," his grandmother said, "your first Rite? You were four. You cried when they put the salt-mark on your wrist because you thought it was a burn."

"It *was* a burn."

"A holy one. There's a difference." She smiled, but it was the thin kind of smile she wore when her mind was three steps ahead of the conversation and none of the steps led anywhere pleasant. "You'll wear the unmarked band tomorrow like everyone else. Wrists bare. And you'll keep your hands in your pockets during the Litany of Forgetting."

His stomach dropped, quiet and cold, the way a stone drops into a well — no splash, just depth. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," she agreed. "You don't. But your hands will stay in your pockets regardless."

* * *

He had touched the stone that morning. That was the thing he could not stop circling back to, all day, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

It had been nothing. It was always supposed to be nothing. He'd gone down to the eastern wall where the flats pressed against the last of the terraced fields, where the guild's oldest boundary-stone stood knee-high and crusted white, and he'd knelt there because that was his chore, had been his chore since he was eight — scrape the salt bloom from the boundary-stones so the wards could read the ground beneath. Every child of the guild took a turn. It meant nothing. It was the most meaningless labor in Veyra.

But this morning, when his palm had come down flat on the crust, something had answered.

Not light. Not sound. Something more private than either — a flicker under his skin, a warmth that started in the bones of his hand and climbed his wrist like water finding a channel, and for half a heartbeat he had *felt* the boundary. All of it. The whole ring of stones around the valley, and beyond them the deeper thing beneath, the old workmanship in the land itself, humming its single sustained note the way a struck bell holds its tone long after the strike. He'd felt the note falter.

Just once. A stutter in the hum, brief as a skipped heartbeat. Then steady again, or near enough, and Zephyrine had snatched his hand back and sat down hard in the salt grass and breathed until the warmth died in his arm and he could pretend, with effort, that it had never happened.

He was seventeen years old and he had never once done anything with the gift. He barely admitted he had it, even inside his own skull. It wasn't his, not really — it was his grandmother's, passed down the way the Vasquez line had always passed things down: silently, illegally, in blood instead of ink. No registry entry. No mark. Nothing any census clerk could find if they stripped him and searched him, because the gift lived too deep, folded into him the way a fold lives in paper even after you smooth it flat.

The Unmarked survived by being nothing. That was the first lesson of the guild, taught before letters: *be small, be dull, be forgotten*. And here he was, feeling the walls of his own prison sing against his palm.

He hadn't told anyone. He hadn't told his grandmother, which was its own strange wound, because he told her everything, because she was the only person in the world who knew what he carried and loved him anyway, or perhaps loved him partly for it — he'd never been able to untangle those two threads and had stopped trying years ago.

Now, on the steps, with the salt falling between them like ash from some gentle fire, he understood that she already knew about the morning. About the stone. Maybe not the specifics. But something.

"Grandmother." His voice came out lower than he intended. "How thin is it?"

She went still. It was a particular stillness, an Odalys stillness, the stillness of a woman deciding how much truth a moment can hold without cracking. Down in the square a dog barked twice and gave up. Somewhere a shutter banged and was latched.

"How thin is what?" she said at last.

"You know what. The valley. The protection." He turned to look at her properly. Her face in the lamplight was all planes and patience, deep-lined, brown as old wood, her eyes the same dark as his own — the family eyes, the ones the guild joked were carved from the flats themselves. "I felt it this morning. At the eastern stone. I felt it—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started smaller. "There was a gap. In the hum."

For a long moment she said nothing at all. The salt fell. A flake landed on her shawl and did not melt, because salt does not melt; it waits.

"Come inside," she said.

* * *

The kitchen of the guildhouse was the warmest room in Veyra and the ugliest, and both facts were sources of pride. Copper pots gone green at the seams. A table scarred by eighty years of knives and elbows and spilled dye. On the mantel, in a place of honor nobody questioned, sat a reliquary of green glass the size of a fist, empty, its stopper fused shut, its label written in a slanted hand that Zephyrine had known since childhood as simply *Grandmother's writing*, though he had never once seen her write anything anymore. She said her hands were past it. He had believed that when he was small. He believed less every year.

Odalys built up the fire without speaking, fed it two splits of ashwood, watched the flames take. Only then did she sit across from him, folding her ruined hands on the table, and look at him with the full weight of her attention — an attention that felt less like being seen than being read.

"Tell me exactly what you felt," she said. "All of it. Leave nothing out for my comfort."

So he told her. The warmth in his bones. The hum. The shape of the boundary laid open to his sense, ring upon ring, stone and ward and the deep old workmanship underneath — and the stutter, the skipped beat, the gap.

When he finished she closed her eyes. Not in sorrow, he thought. In arithmetic.

"The eastern stone is the oldest," she said. "It was set down by the first guild, before the Flats took their final shape. Everything else around it has been rebuilt, renewed, re-sworn. The stone has held longer than anything else in this valley, and it is tired." She opened her eyes. "What you felt was real. The protection is thinning."

"How fast?"

"Faster since spring. Slower than I feared. Faster than I hoped." She said it plainly, the way she said everything that mattered, saving her music for things that didn't. "The valley forgets, Zeph. That's its nature. That's why we chose it, generations back — a place so salt-locked, so sealed in on itself, that even magic cannot cross the flats. Gift dies at the boundary. Power can't reach us here. Whatever hunts the gifted in the wide world, it cannot follow us into the white." Her mouth twisted. "But a seal is a promise made by matter, and matter grows old. Every year the Rite renews the vow. Every year the vow holds the seal another season. And every year the holding costs a little more."

"Costs who?"

"You ask sharp questions for a boy who keeps secrets from his grandmother."

"You keep secrets from me. I learned the habit somewhere."

Something moved behind her eyes — amusement, grief, pride, all braided together so tightly he couldn't separate them. She reached across the table and took his hand, the one that had touched the stone, and turned it palm-up, studying the lines as if the morning's flicker might have left a visible track.

"Your great-great-grandmother wove the deepest thread of this seal," she said quietly. "Before you were born, before I was born, before the guild was a refuge instead of a memory. The Vasquez line has kept the knot ever since. Kept it *quietly*. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That our family does the dishes no one sees."

"That our family holds the door," she corrected, "and has never once, in living memory, told anyone what it costs to hold it." Her thumb moved over his palm, over the crease where the flicker had lived that morning. "And now the door is going soft in its frame, and the kingdom outside has gone mad, and I am an old woman with tired hands."

Outside, the wind shifted. Zephyrine heard it change before he consciously registered it — the nightly breath off the flats turning, carrying its freight of salt against the north side of the house. Tomorrow the whole valley would gather. Tomorrow the Litany of Forgetting would roll over the square like a tide, and every soul in Veyra would swear themselves to smallness one more year, and the seal would drink the vow and hold, and hold, and hold.

Unless it didn't.

"What happens," he asked, "if the Rite isn't enough this year?"

Odalys withdrew her hand. She rose, slowly, cane first, and went to the mantel, and stood looking at the empty reliquary with its fused stopper and its slanted label, the way other people looked at portraits of the dead.

"Then the valley starts letting things in," she said. "Or letting things out." She turned. "Either way, Zephyrine — whatever comes, you must not be found out. Not the gift. Not the blood. Promise me. Swear it on the Rite itself."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them a beam ticked, settling, the sound a house makes when it is thinking.

"And if the seal breaks," he said, "and the hunting comes over the flats — what good is hiding then?"

Her silence stretched long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone somewhere he rarely heard it go — low, and level, and afraid.

"Then we learn what we should have learned generations ago," she said, "which is that walls are not the same as safety. Now swear."

He swore. The words came out of him easily, the way lies do when they're built on top of truths: *I will hide it. I will keep it secret.* Because he meant them — of course he meant them — and because neither of them mentioned the other truth sitting under the words like a stone under snow: that a gift which woke at the touch of salt-crusted stone was not a gift that intended to stay asleep forever.

* * *

He lay awake most of the night in his narrow room under the eaves, listening to the salt fall.

It sounded different than it had when he was a child. That was the thought that kept him company through the small hours — that the sound had changed, or he had. Once it had been a hush, a blanket-sound, the valley tucking itself in. Now, lying on his back with his hands folded on his chest like a carved effigy, he heard it as something finer and more anxious: a sifting, a granular whisper against the shutters, ten thousand small dry voices saying *remember, remember, remember*, or possibly saying *forget*, he couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing felt like the truest description of his life.

Tomorrow he would stand in the square with bare wrists and an unmarked band and folded hands. Tomorrow the elders would speak the Founding words and the valley would renew itself and everyone would believe, for one more bright season, that they were safe inside their forgetting. He would believe it too. He wanted desperately to believe it.

But his palms remembered the stone. His blood remembered the knot his great-great-grandmother tied. And somewhere far to the east, past the white flats, in a capital he had never seen, a king he had never met was wearing a crown that Zephyrine's dreams had recently begun to trouble themselves with — black iron, heavy, older than any dynasty, though when he woke he could never say how he knew that, only that the knowing arrived the way the salt arrived: silently, from far away, settling on everything.

Near dawn he finally slept, and dreamed of the eastern stone, and in the dream his hand was on it and the hum was singing and the gap in the note had widened into a doorway, and something on the other side of the doorway was patient, and vast, and had been waiting a very long time to be remembered.

He woke to bells. Not the Rite bells — those wouldn't ring until midmorning. These came from the watchtower on the western rim, wild and arrhythmic, the alarm-pattern no one in Veyra had heard in living memory.

Down in the street, voices were rising. Someone was shouting a word over and over, and the word crossed the salt-hush and came through his window clear and cold:

"*Rider. Rider on the flats.*"

Zephyrine was at the glass before he knew he'd moved, staring west into the paling dawn, where far out on the endless white — impossibly, suicidally, where no rider in the history of the valley had ever survived to be — a dark figure was coming on.
