## 2. A Summons in White

The courier came out of the salt on the third morning of the Rite-fast, and every bell in Veyra rang at once.

Zephyrine felt it before he heard it — a tremor in the floorstones of the guildhouse, a shiver running up through the soles of his feet like something waking. He had been kneeling at the hearth, feeding the ritual fire the dried sea-lavender that made the smoke turn silver for the Founding Rite, and when the first bell struck his hand jerked and scattered lavender across the flagstones.

No rider survived the flats. That was not a saying in Veyra; it was arithmetic. The white expanse beyond the valley's rim drank everything that crossed it — water, warmth, will. Caravans had tried it in his grandmother's grandmother's time, whole companies of the gifted marching out with charms woven into their bootlaces, and the salt had returned nothing but their shadows baked into the crust, pale stains shaped like people who had once been certain of themselves.

And yet the bells were ringing. And yet, through the eastern window, Zephyrine could see the whole valley gathered along the rim road, watching a single figure walk down from the flats without stumbling, without burning, dressed head to toe in a white that was somehow whiter than the salt itself.

He left the fire half-fed. He would think about that later, about how he had abandoned a sacred duty for curiosity, about what it said about him that the first crack had shown there, in so small a thing.

* * *

By the time he reached the rim road, the crowd had gone quiet in the particular way crowds go quiet when they are afraid and ashamed of being afraid. The courier stood among them like a splinter of winter. White cloak, white gloves, a mask of pale porcelain covering the face — smooth, featureless except for two slits of shadow where eyes should be. Snow-bloom embroidery crawled up one sleeve: the crown's mark, the mark no one in Veyra had seen in living memory, because nothing wearing the crown's mark could cross the flats.

Could not. Past tense, apparently.

The courier held up a scroll sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and spoke in a voice pitched to carry, flat as the horizon.

"By decree of His Majesty, sovereign of the Nine Provinces and the Salt Marches: all persons bearing the unregistered gift shall present themselves for census within forty days. Census is mandatory. Census is mercy." The porcelain face turned slowly, taking in the crowd. "Refusal will be construed as sedition."

Zephyrine's grandmother stood near the front, her back straight as a fence post, and Zephyrine watched her hands. Old hands, knotted like the roots of a wind-bent tree. They did not shake. But her thumb pressed hard against the head of her walking-stick, hard enough that he could see the whitening of the skin even from twenty paces away, and he understood that whatever courage she wore was costing her.

The elder — Mother Ivenna, ninety-one years old and sharp as a flint knife — stepped forward. "There are no gifted persons in Veyra," she said. "You know this. The whole kingdom knows it. The valley is empty of magic. It has been for three hundred years."

"The flats have kept your secrets," the courier said. Something in the tone suggested the porcelain mouth beneath was smiling. "Secrets are a kind of debt. The crown has come to collect."

It rolled the scroll closed and set it into Mother Ivenna's hands — an oddly courteous gesture, almost gentle — and then it turned and walked back up the rim road, onto the white flats, and did not burn, and did not stumble, and grew smaller and smaller until it was a fleck, and then nothing at all.

The bells kept ringing for another hour after that. Nobody knew how to stop them. Later Zephyrine would learn that the ringer's tower had been found empty, the ropes still swinging on their own, and that detail frightened him more than anything else, though he could not have said why.

* * *

The council convened that night in the Long Hall, and Zephyrine was not invited, which meant everyone assumed he already knew, which meant everyone had been talking about him for days.

He sat outside the hall door with his ear against the wood, feeling like a thief and telling himself he wasn't one, that these were his people deciding things about his life and he had a right to hear it. The door was old oak, salt-bleached, worn smooth by generations of shoulders leaning exactly where his shoulder leaned now. He thought of all the other young guild members who must have eavesdropped in this exact spot over three centuries, waiting to learn their fates, and took a strange comfort in the company of ghosts.

Inside, Mother Ivenna's voice carried the way old voices sometimes do, thin but unbending, like wire.

"—the decree is bait. You all see that. A census is a net. They name it mercy so we'll walk into it holding the word like a lantern."

"Then we refuse," said Doss, the smith, whose voice came through the door like gravel poured slowly. "We've refused worse."

"We refused the tithe-kings, and they sent soldiers, and our walls held. This isn't soldiers. This is something that walked across the flats." A murmur ran through the room at that. "Nothing crosses the flats. We built our whole lives on the certainty that nothing crosses the flats. Whatever wears that mask has already broken the only law we trusted. Refusal means nothing to a thing like that. Refusal is just the shape our surrender takes."

Silence. Then his grandmother's voice, and Zephyrine's stomach tightened, because he knew that voice, knew the register it dropped into when she had already decided something and was merely informing the world.

"There's another option."

"Ivenna," said Doss, warningly.

"They want a gift-bearer. Fine. Give them one." A pause long enough for Zephyrine to lean closer until his cheekbone ached against the wood. "Give them one who isn't."

"You're talking about a decoy."

"I'm talking about the youngest. Unmarked by any registry, untrained past the first forms, unknown outside this valley. If the crown takes a census of him, they'll find a boy with salt in his blood and nothing else, and while their clerks write down his name, the rest of us burn the archives and scatter the lineages. He buys us time. Time is the only thing the flats ever gave us, and now the flats have failed, so we take it where we can."

"He's a child."

"He's nineteen. I was carrying salt loads at nine and binding knots at twelve. He's old enough."

Another silence, longer. Zephyrine found he had stopped breathing. Not because of the plan — some cold part of his mind was already turning the plan over, seeing its logic, admiring even its logic — but because of the voice saying it. Because it was her. Because the woman who had taught him to read knot-script on winter nights, who had held his hand over the hearth when the fever took his parents, who had told him — *sworn* to him, only last week, her palm on his chest — *whatever happens at the Rite, you belong here, Zeph, you belong here* — that woman was spending him like coin.

And the worst part, the part that would keep him awake for years afterward, was that the others didn't argue much. There was grief in the room; he could hear it, muffled sobs from someone, probably Lira, who had helped raise him too. But no one said *no*. No one said *not him*. They said things like *is it necessary* and *can we prepare him properly*, questions of method, never of principle. He had been raised by these people. Fed by them, taught by them, loved by them in the small daily ways that add up to a life. And to them, apparently, love was one ledger and survival was another, and when the accounts came due, love paid second.

He pushed himself off the door and walked out into the courtyard before he heard his own name voted on. He didn't need to hear it. Some things you know the way you know weather coming.

* * *

His grandmother found him at the salt-shrine an hour later.

The shrine sat at the lowest point of the valley, a ring of black stones crusted white, older than the guild, older maybe than the kingdom. Pilgrims had stopped coming generations ago; now only guild members tended it, and mostly they just swept it. Zephyrine sat on the innermost stone with his knees drawn up, doing the thing he did when the flicker rose in him — pressing his palms flat against the crusted surface, letting the faint warm pulse of inherited magic leak out into the salt where no one could see it, where it would sink and vanish and leave him looking exactly like what the guild believed he was: empty. Unmarked. Safe.

Except his grandmother had known. Had always known. He understood that now with a clarity that felt like a wound opening.

She lowered herself onto the stone beside him with the careful economy of the very old, and for a while neither of them spoke. Overhead the sky was clear and full of stars, indifferent, beautiful. Somewhere a nightjar called.

"You listened at the door," she said.

"Yes."

"Good. I'd have been disappointed if you hadn't."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "That my grandmother approves of my spying?"

"It's supposed to make you understand that I'm not ashamed of you, and never have been, and that includes tonight." She looked out at the dark curve of the valley, the terraced fields, the lamplit windows of the guildhouses. "I'm sorry, Zeph. I'm sorrier than you can know yet. But it has to be you, and you deserve to hear why from me and not from a vote."

"Then tell me why. Tell me the real reason, because 'you're the youngest' is a reason for sending someone expendable, and nobody sends a decoy they think is expendable. Decoys work because they're believed. So either you're lying to the council or you're lying to me."

She was quiet for a moment. In the starlight he saw something move across her face — fear, he thought, real fear, the first he had ever seen on her — and then it was folded away again, neat as laundry.

"The real reason is complicated."

"Then give me the complicated one."

"No." She said it gently, which was worse than harshness. "Not yet. Not tonight. Tonight I'll give you this much: the courier crossed the flats because the flats let it. Do you understand? The valley's protection isn't a wall, it's a choice — a very old choice, woven into the land itself, into the salt. And choices can be renegotiated. Something on the other side has opened negotiations." She reached over and covered his hand where it rested on the crusted stone. Her fingers were cold. "When you go out there — and you will go, because the council voted and because I asked them to vote that way — you'll feel things you've never felt. The world beyond Veyra is loud, child. Louder than you can imagine. Don't fight it and don't chase it. Just remember where your feet were planted first."

"Grandmother—"

"And take this." From inside her coat she drew a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord in a pattern of knots Zephyrine recognized — the Vasquez traveler's knot, the one woven for family going on journeys, the one his parents' belongings had still been tied in when they died. "Letters. Correspondence. If anyone asks, they're recipes. If anyone dangerous asks, swallow them."

"What's in them?"

"Recipes," she said, and for one heartbeat her eyes glinted with something that might have been humor or might have been grief. "Eat well on the road, Zeph. And write to me. The couriers between here and the capital are slow but honest — most of them."

She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and stood a moment looking down at him. Then she bent and kissed the top of his head, the way she had when he was six, and walked back up toward the lights of the guildhall without looking back.

Zephyrine unwrapped the oilcloth just enough to see paper, dozens of pages, folded and refolded, soft at the creases from handling. On the topmost page, in his grandmother's cramped, slanting hand, a single line was underlined twice:

*The knot remembers what the binder forgets.*

He sat alone at the shrine a long time, turning the phrase over, feeling the salt hum faintly warm beneath his palms, feeling the flicker of his secret inheritance pulse in time with it — alive, hidden, and now, somehow, summoned.

Forty days. The decree had given the gifted of the kingdom forty days to surrender.

As far as Zephyrine could tell, his guild had just given him thirty-nine fewer than that.
