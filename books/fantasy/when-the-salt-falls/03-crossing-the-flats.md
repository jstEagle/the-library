## 3. Crossing the Flats

They left before dawn, when the valley was still the color of drowned things and the salt flats had not yet begun their daily argument with the sun.

Zephyrine had said his goodbyes the night before, which meant he had not said them at all. He had stood in the doorway of his grandmother's house while she ground dried marshroot in her mortar, her back to him, her shoulders doing what they always did — pretending to be smaller than they were, so no one would ask anything of them.

"Come back before the thaw," he'd said.

She hadn't turned around. "The flats decide who comes back," she'd said, "not I." And then, quieter, into the mortar's slow grinding: "Wear your collar up. The wind out there finds every soft place."

Now the wind found the soft places anyway — the hollow of his throat, the wet corners of his eyes that he kept blaming on the cold. He walked beside Sereth through the last crooked streets of Veyra, past shuttered windows where he could feel, more than see, the guild watching. They had voted to send him. They had voted with their faces carefully arranged into expressions of duty, and Elder Vash had gripped his shoulder afterward and said *the valley endures because some of us are willing to be spent*, as if that were comfort instead of arithmetic.

Only one person had come to the gate to see him off properly: old Tam, the rope-maker, who pressed a knotted cord into Zephyrine's palm and said nothing at all, just held his hand a moment too long, his thumbs rough as bark. The cord was made of three strands braided tight — a maker's knot, the kind Tam tied into everything he loved.

Zephyrine wore it around his wrist now, under his sleeve, where he could feel it with every step like a small stubborn heartbeat.

*They spent me*, he thought, and then hated himself for thinking it, because the flats ahead of him were white and vast and indifferent, and resentment would not help him cross them alive.

Sereth walked like water finding its level. That was the only way Zephyrine could describe it — the courier never seemed to choose a path so much as agree with one, her boots finding the firm crust between the brine pits without looking down, her gray cloak moving in the wind as though it had been cut by someone who understood wind personally. She had spoken perhaps forty words since the decree. Most of them had been instructions: *walk behind me. Step where I step. If you feel the ground breathe, stop.*

"The ground doesn't breathe," Zephyrine had said.

"Everything breathes," Sereth had answered, and had not elaborated, and Zephyrine had decided that hating her would take energy he might need later.

Behind them the valley shrank. He kept turning to look — he couldn't help it — and each time it was smaller and paler, the dark cluster of rooftops and the green thread of the river and the terraced fields going gray and then going suggestion and then going memory. The mountains that walled Veyra from the world folded over it like two hands closing in prayer.

By midmorning there was nothing on any side but white.

It wasn't snow-white or bone-white; it was a color that refused to be a color at all, light bouncing off salt crust into salt haze until sky and ground met somewhere out past seeing and became one enormous unblinking eye. The silence was worse than the wind. Out here sound died young. His own footsteps arrived at his ears already half-forgotten, and twice he caught himself breathing loudly just to prove the world still had room for him in it.

He felt nothing.

That was the strange part — the thing he kept returning to, worrying at like a loose tooth. All his life, magic had lived against his skin like static before lightning. Touching salt-crusted stone, gripping an iron hinge, even pressing his palms flat against the valley's ancient boundary markers — something in him had flickered and reached, a warmth under the breastbone, a taste like struck flint. He had hidden it the way you hide a cough in a quiet room: contained, swallowed, performed over. In Veyra, the gift had been a whisper he spent his whole childhood strangling.

Out here it was silent. Utterly. As if the flats weren't merely empty but *muffling*, a hand pressed over the mouth of the world.

"It's true, then," he said, and his voice came out cracked and small. "Magic can't cross."

Sereth didn't break stride. "Nothing crosses the flats. Not rider, not bird, not spell." A pause. "Except me."

"You don't talk about how."

"No."

They walked. The sun climbed and stopped being kind. Salt glittered in it like a million tiny knives, and the glare came up off the ground and off the air both, until Zephyrine's eyes streamed and the horizon dissolved entirely and he was walking inside a pearl. He thought about the Founding Rite he should have been standing for this morning — the long robes, the salt-bread broken seven ways, the names of the dead Unmarked read aloud while children held candles that wouldn't light, because that was the point, that was the whole point of the valley: *here, the flame stays ordinary.* He thought of his grandmother's hands, flour-dusted, kneading the festival bread alone this year.

He thought: *they voted. Eleven elders, and not one voice for keeping me.* He thought: *I would have voted to send me too,* and did not know whether that made him loyal or a coward, or whether those had ever been different things.

* * *

The ground began to change in the afternoon.

It was subtle first — the crust underfoot losing its glassy squeak, taking on a faint gray bloom, then patches of darker earth showing through like bruises. Then the smell changed. Zephyrine hadn't realized the flats had a smell until they lost it: that sharp mineral bite, clean as a struck bell. What replaced it was older. Wet clay. Rot-sweet reeds. Water, somewhere, real water, living water.

"We're near the far shore," Sereth said. First words in hours. Her voice had changed too — loosened somehow, as though the flats had been a weight on her tongue and she'd finally set it down. "There's a river channel ahead. We ford it at dusk, when the tide drops. On the other side—" she glanced at him, and for the first time her face had something in it besides weather, something almost like pity, which frightened him more than anything else that day, "—on the other side, boy, you'll want to be sitting down."

"Why?"

"Because you've been holding your breath your whole life," she said, "and you're about to find out what air tastes like."

Zephyrine opened his mouth to tell her she was being dramatic, and that was when he felt it.

Not the ground breathing. *Him.*

It started low, under the sternum, in the exact place where all his life the flicker had lived — the place he'd learned to press flat, the way you press a hand over a wound. Except now the pressure was gone. Now the thing underneath had room, and it knew it, and it rose.

Heat first. A spreading warmth like swallowing sunlight, blooming outward from his center into his ribs, his throat, the roots of his teeth. Then light — not outside light, not the merciless glare of the flats, but something *beneath* his skin, behind his eyes, as if someone had lit a lamp in a room of him that had been dark since birth and he was seeing the furniture of his own soul for the first time. He staggered. The salt crust tilted. Somewhere very far away Sereth was saying his name — she knew his name, he'd never told her his name — and her voice sounded like it was coming up through deep water.

And then the gift *roared*.

There was no gentler word for it. Every flicker he had ever strangled came back at once, with interest, with centuries of interest, a debt collected in a single instant. He could feel the salt beneath the crust, millions of years of drowned sea singing its long mineral note. He could feel the river ahead, a cold bright vein, every fish in it a small quick candle. He could feel Sereth — and that was the terror of it, the true terror — he could feel that she was *hollow*, that where other living things burned she was shaped like the space around a flame, a mold, a vessel built to hold something, and whatever she was built to hold was not in her now, and its absence had a shape he could trace with his new sense the way a tongue traces a missing tooth.

"No," he heard himself say. "No, no—"

He fell to his knees. The world was too loud. Everything was too *alive*. He had spent seventeen years believing he was mostly empty with a small secret fire in him, and the truth inverted it: he was fire, all fire, and the emptiness had been the disguise, and the valley — the valley had been the lid on the pot, and now the lid was off and he was boiling over into a world that wanted people like him in bottles.

His hands dug into the crumbling gray earth and the earth answered him. He felt its roots, its worms, its patient slow mineral dreams. He felt a stand of dead reeds downstream and, without deciding to, without knowing *how*, he felt them remember being green. For one vertiginous heartbeat the dead stalks rustled though there was no wind, straightening, reaching, a field of small resurrections — and the cost of it tore through him like a hook behind the ribs, dragging something essential out through his skin—

"Stop." Sereth's hand closed on his nape, hard, and her grip was shockingly cold. "Breathe. Pull it back. *Pull it back or it will pull you out.*"

He didn't know how. That was the obscene joke of it — he'd guarded a gift he'd never once been taught to use, hoarded a coin he'd never been shown how to spend. But his body knew, some animal layer of him knew, the way a drowning man knows to kick. He curled inward. He imagined the valley — the river, the rooftops, his grandmother's mortar grinding in the blue hour — and he built walls out of the memory of it, stone by stone, and pushed the roaring fire down behind them.

Slowly, slowly, the world went quiet.

The reeds lay brown and dead again. The river sang its ordinary song. Zephyrine knelt in the bruised earth with his forehead nearly touching it, shaking, soaked in sweat despite the cold, feeling as though he'd run a hundred miles and been born and buried all in the same quarter hour.

"There it is," Sereth said softly. Almost gently. She crouched beside him, and up close her eyes were the color of the flats, and there was something in them that was not pity after all. It was recognition. "That's why they sent you."

"What?" His voice was ruined.

"A Vasquez who can wake dead reeds on his first day outside the salt." She stood, brushing off her knees. "The crown will want to look at you very closely."

* * *

They crossed the channel at dusk, as she'd promised, the tide fallen to expose a spine of black mud and shelving rock. The water came to their knees and it was bitter and it pulled, and Zephyrine — wrung out, hollowed, trembling with exhaustion — let the current shove him and barely cared. On the far bank the land climbed into dun-colored hills stitched with gorse, and beyond them, far off, he could see woodsmoke rising from some human settlement, thin gray threads against a bruise-purple sky.

Home was on the other side of that smoke. Home was a day and a half behind him across a killing white sea of salt, and the people there had voted, and his grandmother had not come to the gate, and the knot Tam gave him was cutting a red line into his wrist because he'd been clenching his fist around it for hours without noticing.

He sat down on the hillside because his legs informed him the arrangement was no longer negotiable.

The sunset came up over the hills like something rehearsed — gold going to copper going to a red so deep it was nearly a sound. And in that light, exhausted past fear, Zephyrine felt the gift stir again behind its walls. Not the roar. Just a glow. A banked hearth-glow, warm against his grief, and he understood with sudden terrible clarity that this was what he had been all along, that the boy who walked into Veyra's streets every morning half-asleep had been carrying a cathedral inside him and calling it a closet.

He wept. He didn't fight it. There was no one to see, and the salt flats had taken his tears' right to shame him.

When he looked up, wiping his face on his sleeve, Sereth was standing a few paces off, facing back the way they'd come, toward the invisible valley. The wind moved her cloak. She stood very still, the way a person stands when they're listening to something no one else can hear — or reporting to it.

"Who's waiting for us?" Zephyrine asked. His voice surprised him with its steadiness. "At the census. Really. Not the clerk's answer. The true one."

Sereth was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer at all. Then she turned, and in the dying light her eyes had gone pale as the flats, and she smiled with a kindness that did not reach anywhere near the rest of her face.

"Someone who has been waiting for a Vasquez," she said, "for a very long time."
