## 4. The Girl Who Hunts Hunters

Maren had been watching the courier party since dawn, when they emerged from the white haze of the flats like figures walking out of a fever dream.

Three riders and one boy. That alone should have been impossible. The salt flats of Veyra killed everything that tried to cross them — horses first, then men, then the memories of men, because no map in the kingdom agreed on what lay at the valley's heart. Some said the guild there had died out generations ago. Some said it had never existed. Maren had spent four years learning which rumors fed a fugitive and which ones got her hanged, and she had filed the valley away under *stories that cost too much to chase*.

And yet here came three riders wearing the grey livery of the royal post, their cloaks crusted white to the knee, their horses shod in leather wraps stuffed with something that glittered. Salt. They'd packed their animals' hooves with salt, as if the danger were the ground itself.

Maybe it was. Maren had never seen anyone come back from Veyra before. She'd never seen anyone come back from anywhere with that particular look — the look these riders wore, of men who had walked through a place where the world's rules didn't apply and hadn't yet convinced themselves the other side was real.

But it was the boy she couldn't stop watching.

He rode at the center of the party, bound at the wrists with cord that had bitten through his sleeve into the skin beneath, and he did not once look back toward the flats. Most captives spent the whole first day out turning around and around, drinking the horizon like a man dying of thirst. This boy faced forward. His dark hair was cut short in the way of people who lived by a communal blade, and his hands, even bound, kept moving — touching the pommel, touching his own forearm, touching the cord as though reading it. When they stopped to water the horses he sat very still and watched the birds.

That was how she knew. Not the binding, not the escort, not the grey livery. The birds.

There was a kind of bird in the eastern hills — she'd heard the charcoal-burners call them ash bells for the sound of their wings — that could smell a gift on the air the way sharks smelled blood. Wherever the crown's hunters passed, the bells followed, wheeling above the wagons in silent gray spirals. And here they were, a dozen of them, drifting after this party like gulls after a fishing boat, never diving, never leaving.

They were carrying a gifted child to a census house. Possibly to worse.

Maren lay flat against the ridge in the long dead grass, her stolen gift humming under her skin the way it always did when she was about to use it — a warmth behind the breastbone that felt borrowed, because it was. Four years ago she had taken it from a dying woman in a burning village: the ability to unmake knots. Ropes, locks, chains, sutures, the little knots of scar tissue that held old wounds closed. Nothing grand. Nothing the songs would ever mention. But it had opened every cage the crown had ever put her in, and lately it had been opening theirs too.

Below her, the courier party made camp in the lee of a rock shelf, close to the only road for twenty miles. The lead rider — tall, silver-haired, with the neat, unhurried hands of someone who had done terrible things efficiently for a long time — built a fire while the other two saw to the horses. The boy sat apart, exactly as far from all three of them as his tether allowed, and watched the flames without seeming to see them.

Maren waited for full dark. She was patient the way poor people are patient; it had been beaten into her early and thoroughly. She ate dried plums, counted the guards' rotations, memorized where each man set his boots when he slept. The silver-haired one slept last and woke first, and even sleeping, his hand stayed near a knife at his hip in a way that told her he would be the problem.

The other two would be easy.

* * *

She came down off the ridge an hour past midnight, barefoot, wearing the dark, and she did not so much as breathe hard.

The nearest guard lay with his mouth open and his sword three inches out of its scabbard, the posture of a man who'd fallen asleep mid-thought. Maren crouched over him and let her gift flow down through her fingers into the buckle of his sword belt, the knot of his bootlaces, the drawstring of the coin pouch at his throat. She felt them all at once, the little woven tensions of them, the way you might feel the strings of an instrument under your palm — and she pulled.

Everything loosened. The belt slithered free. The boots came off his feet without waking him, which was somehow funnier than it should have been, and Maren permitted herself two seconds of grinning in the dark before she moved to the second man.

The second man woke up. He got half a shout out — enough to turn the night ugly — before Maren dropped the thought of a knot into his mind instead of his gear. That was the part of her gift she never talked about, the part that frightened her: it worked on knots inside people too. The clenched knot of a man's resolve. The tangled knot of his attention. She felt his mind seize and slacken like a rope gone suddenly loose, and he folded forward into the grass, breathing slow.

The fire had burned to coals. The boy sat bolt upright by it, wrists still bound, staring at her with an expression she would remember for years afterward: not gratitude. Arithmetic.

"Who are you," he said. Not *help*. Not *thank you*. Just the question, low and careful, his eyes flicking from her to the collapsed guards to the tree line as if calculating whether he could outrun her.

"Maren." She kept her voice flat and her hands visible, palms out, the way you'd approach a spooked animal. "I take things from people who don't deserve to have them. Tonight that's you."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Swallowed. Whatever he'd been about to say, he rebuilt his face over the top of it, quick as a mason laying brick. "I'm not gifted," he said. "You've made a mistake. They were taking me to be tested, that's all. I'm nobody."

Behind the boy's shoulder, the silver-haired courier opened his eyes. He had been awake the whole time — of course he had — and now he rose out of his bedroll with the smooth economy of a drawn blade and said nothing at all.

Maren moved before thinking, which was a habit and also a flaw. Her hand came up and she reached for the knots in him — the tension in his shoulders, the coil of his intent, the whole braided readiness of the man —

—and found nothing to hold.

Not resistance. *Nothing.* It was like plunging her hand into water expecting rope and closing her fist on cold. The courier tilted his head, and in the coal-light his eyes were the pale grey of the flats they'd crossed, and he smiled the way a host smiles.

"Interesting," he said. "The Vasquez boy attracts thieves within a day of the boundary. The valley truly is thinning, if its walls let *you* through." His gaze moved to Zephyrine, and something in it softened horribly, the way a trap softens when baited. "Eat something, child. We ride at dawn regardless. Run if you like — the flats will have you back or the hills will have you dead, and either serves."

Then he simply lay back down, folded his hands on his chest, and closed his eyes. Dismissed. As though Maren were weather.

Her heart was slamming. Her gift had never failed her. It had *never*—

"We're leaving," the boy whispered, and his hand was on her wrist, and his face had gone the color of tallow. "Now. Please."

* * *

They ran through the dark hills until the camp was a smudge of nothing behind them and the road was a memory, and Maren chose the path entirely by rules she didn't explain: never the low ground, never the same contour twice, cross the stream three times. The boy followed well. Better than well — silently, surely, reading her choices almost before she made them, which bothered her more than she let herself notice.

Dawn found them in a hollow under a leaning birch, where a spring seeped out of mossy rock and the ash bells, mercifully, were nowhere in the sky. Maren drank, filled her flask, and finally looked properly at the person she'd stolen.

Up close he looked younger than he had at a distance, and more exhausted, and considerably less harmless. There was a fine tremor in his freed hands that he kept trying to disguise as flexing. His wrists, where the cords had bitten, were already healing — not scabbed, not raw, just faintly pink, hours ahead of any schedule nature kept.

"You're welcome, by the way," Maren said.

"For what?"

"For the rescue. Generally. You could try the word 'thanks.' I've heard it exists."

"I didn't ask to be rescued." He said it without heat, the way you'd state the weather, and then seemed to hear himself, and winced. "I mean — thank you. Truly. But those men weren't—"

"Weren't holding you captive? Your wrists disagree."

"They were protecting me." He met her eyes when he lied, which she noted with something between irritation and professional respect. Most liars looked away. He looked straight at her, earnest as a chapel painting. "There's a hunt on. For gifted people. Everyone knows it. My guild sent me out under guard because the roads aren't safe, and you've just assaulted the only people keeping me alive."

"The roads aren't safe," Maren repeated. She turned the words over like a counterfeit coin. "Right. That's why the king's couriers bound your hands. For safety. That's why the ash bells have been following you since the flats — they can smell protection, can't they."

Something flickered across his face. Gone too fast to name.

"You know about the bells," he said quietly.

"I know about everything the crown does to people like us." She hadn't meant to say *us*. It slipped out the way truth slips out at the end of long nights, and she hated it, and she watched him catch it. Watched his whole posture change — the arithmetic returning, faster now, recalcifying into a decision.

"Like us," he echoed. "So you're gifted."

"No."

"But you said—"

"I said people like us." Maren stood, brushed the moss from her knees, and gave him the story she gave everyone, the one she'd rehearsed until it had calluses: "I'm Unmarked. Tested at nine, clean paper, stamped by the local clerk. The crown doesn't hunt my kind. Which is why I can walk into places you can't, and why you should be nicer to me."

It was a good lie. It had survived border checkpoints and a census inspection in Coldharbour and one extremely awkward conversation with a priest. She believed in it the way you believe in a roof over your head.

The boy looked at her bound-paperless wrists, at her hands that had unlocked two armed men without touching them, at the empty air where his own bindings used to be — and she realized, with a lurch, that she had loosed his cords in front of him without ever laying a finger on them. From ten feet away. In the dark.

He had watched her gift work. And she had told him she didn't have one.

"You cut my ropes," he said slowly.

"Knife," Maren said.

"I didn't hear a knife."

"They're very quiet knives."

A pause. Then, to her astonishment, the corner of his mouth moved. Not quite a smile — more like the place where a smile would go if either of them could afford one. "Quiet knives," he repeated. "In the dark. From thirty feet."

"Forty."

"And they untied knots in two sleeping men's gear while you were at it, because I saw the belts on the ground."

"Thorough knives."

He laughed — one breath of it, startled out of him, immediately strangled, as if laughter were another thing his guild had taught him to ration. And Maren found, to her considerable annoyance, that the sound did something warm and unauthorized to the space behind her ribs.

"All right," she said. "We're both liars. Congratulations. Now that that's established: I'm going east to Harrowgate. There's a wagon road, a grain merchant who owes me, and a census house I intend to visit for reasons that are none of your business. You can come or not. If you come, you follow my rules, and rule one is you don't lie to me about anything that gets us killed."

"You just said we're both liars."

"Yes, but mine are *good*."

That earned her the almost-smile again, longer this time. He wrapped his healing wrists with his sleeves, deliberately, hiding the evidence, which she chose to find considerate rather than damning. "Zephyrine," he said. "Zephyrine Vasquez. Of Veyra. And I'm sorry about your men — the guards. I hope the fat one isn't angry about his boots."

"He'll live. He'll wake up confused and shoeless, which is most of humanity most of the time." Maren shouldered her pack and looked east, where the hills went gold at their crests and beyond them, somewhere, a building stood full of glass bottles with children's names written on labels. Four years she'd been working toward it. Four years of small rescues, small thefts, small knots undone, saving toward the great one.

The boy from the impossible valley fell into step beside her. He walked, she noticed, on the uphill side of her, between her and the open slopes — protective instinct, or habit, or a sentry's discipline. Another thing that didn't fit the story of a sheltered guild orphan. Another coin she pocketed without spending.

They walked all morning, and the silence between them was strange and new and not uncomfortable, which was its own kind of warning sign, and Maren ignored it with her usual skill.

Near noon, they crested a ridge above a river valley, and Zephyrine stopped as if he'd hit glass. Below them, spread along the far bank, lay a village. Or the idea of one. Roofs open to the sky. A orchard of blackened trees. And planted along the roadside at intervals, dozens of iron posts, and on every post, a small brass bell, and hanging from every bell, a strip of parchment bearing a single official seal.

"What is that," Zephyrine said, in a voice that already knew.

"Ash bell village," Maren said. "One of eleven I know of. The bells ring when a gifted person passes within a mile. The crown puts them up after a collection, so they can hear if anything comes back." She started down the slope, because there was water down there, and possibly a survivor, and because stopping meant thinking. "Welcome to the outside, Zephyrine Vasquez of Veyra. Rule two: whatever you're really running from, it's smaller than what's running from us."

Behind her, very softly, so softly she almost missed it, he said: "I'm not running from anything."

The lie was so fluent, so practiced, so exactly shaped like her own, that Maren smiled all the way down the hill — and began, quietly, to plan how she was going to find out the truth about the boy the ash bells loved, before the thing in the capital with the pale-eyed servant found him first.
