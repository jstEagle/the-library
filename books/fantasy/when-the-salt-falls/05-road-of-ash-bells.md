## 5. Road of Ash Bells

*Maren*

The first village had no name left worth saying. Maren knew it only as the place where the road bent east around a hill of slag and the ash bells began.

They hung from a gallows-frame at the crossroads — a dozen cracked bells cast from melted-down reliquary glass, the crown's cheap genius for turning cruelty into warning. When the wind moved through them they did not ring so much as *weep*, a thin glassy keening that carried for miles across the blackened fields. Every village that had given up its children had been made to hang them. Every village after heard them coming.

Zephyrine stopped dead when he heard them.

Maren watched him from the saddle — watched his shoulders climb toward his ears, watched his hand drift to the strap of his pack where he kept whatever it was he wouldn't tell her about. He'd been doing that since the flats. Since the shore where she'd found him half-drowned in his own light, glowing like a struck flint before he'd collapsed and woken up *lying* about it. She hadn't called him on the lie yet. A lie was useful. A lie told you what a person was afraid of, and Maren had built her whole survival on reading fear.

"They took children here," she said, not unkindly.

He didn't answer. His eyes were moving across the village with an expression she was beginning to recognize in herself: the arithmetic of absence. Counting doorways against doors hanging open. Counting wells against buckets left standing. Counting small shoes.

"The census riders come at dusk," she went on, dismounting. "That's their pattern. They let people see the bells first, all day, so by the time the wagons roll in nobody runs. Running gets you listed as *resistant*." She said the word the way you'd spit out a bone. "Resistant households don't get a receipt."

"A receipt." His voice came out scraped.

"For the gift taken. They write down the child's name, the nature of the gift, the date of harvest. Signed by the census officer." She kicked through ash toward the nearest house. "The families keep them. Some frame them. Can you imagine? Framing it. Like it's a christening record."

Behind her, Zephyrine made a sound that wasn't quite a word.

Inside the house there was still a table set for supper. Three bowls. One of them small enough to cup in two hands. Maren stood in the doorway and let him look, because someone had once let *her* look, in a village much like this one, and it had done more to shape her than any lesson since. You could hear about the hunt your whole life. It wasn't until you stood inside the silence of a table set for three that it became a thing with teeth.

"Her name was Osla," Zephyrine said quietly.

Maren turned. "What?"

"The girl who lived here. There's chalk on the doorframe — height marks. *Osla, age six. Osla, age seven.*" His fingertips hovered over the marks without touching them, and Maren saw — just for a heartbeat, just at the edge of her sight — a faint shimmer crawl up his wrist, like heat off summer stone. "She'd be eight now."

"Put that away," she snapped, sharper than she meant to.

The shimmer died. He pulled his sleeve down over his hand as if he could stuff the power back into his blood by force. "I didn't—"

"You *did*. I saw it. That's exactly how they find people, Zeph — gifted folk leak when they feel things strongly. Grief, anger, pity. The census officers don't carry detection charms; they don't need them. They carry dogs bred on harvested essence, and dogs smell leakage like blood in water." She stepped close, close enough to see the pulse jumping in his throat. "You want to survive the road to the capital? Then you learn to be nothing. Nothing is the safest thing to be in this kingdom right now."

Something flickered across his face — hurt, resistance, the particular stubbornness of the sheltered. But under it, something else. Something that looked, horribly, like guilt.

"All right," he said. "Teach me."

* * *

*Zephyrine*

The lesson began at dusk, in the lee of a collapsed granary, with Maren holding a shard of reliquary glass on a string like a plumb line.

"This is dead glass — emptied, spent, worthless," she said. "Which means it only does one thing anymore: it *notices*. Hold your power back and it hangs still. Leak, and it swings toward you like a compass needle." She let it dangle between them, catching the last red light. "Now. Show me nothing."

Zephyrine closed his eyes.

It was harder than he'd imagined. Back in Veyra, his gift had been a whisper under salt — a flicker, a warmth in his palms when he touched crusted stone, easy to pretend away. Out here it was a live coal behind his sternum. It wanted *out*. It rose when he was afraid and surged when he grieved, and this whole burned country was engineered, it seemed, to make him feel both.

"Your breathing changed," Maren said. The glass swayed gently toward him. "When you try to clamp down, you hold your breath, and holding your breath is itself a signal. Everything about you is shouting. Try again. Don't push the power down. Push it *inward* — make it small, make it a stone sitting on the bottom of a well. The well can be deep. Nobody sees the bottom of a deep well."

"A stone in a well," he repeated.

"Deep well. Cold water. Still surface." Her voice dropped into a cadence he almost recognized — the rhythm of his grandmother's voice over the hearth, teaching him the guild's old breathing prayers without ever naming them prayers. It startled him how much these two strangers of his life sounded alike. The hunted and the hidden, arriving at the same door from opposite sides.

He breathed. He sank. The coal behind his sternum resisted, then — slowly, grudgingly, like a cat settling — folded itself inward. Down past his ribs, down past some floor inside him he hadn't known he had, until it rested somewhere fathomless and quiet.

The glass hung dead still.

Maren stared at it, then at him, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes that she quickly buried — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of respect. "Again," was all she said. "Until you can do it bleeding."

* * *

*Maren*

Three villages in two days. Each one the same litany: bells, ash, tables set for suppers that never came. By the third, Zephyrine had stopped asking questions and started writing names — a little book he kept against his chest, filling page after page with names gleaned from chalk marks, from carved initials, from a weaver woman in the second village who talked and talked because talking was all she had left. Osla. Tam. The twins Brin and Sefa. A boy called Mouse whose gift, the weaver said, was making flowers bloom out of season.

Maren pretended not to notice the book. Pretending not to notice things was becoming her full-time occupation where Zephyrine Vasquez was concerned.

Because there were other things she was pretending not to notice too.

Like the way the blighted fields they passed — gray-veined wheat, fruit trees gone to wood-rot, whole orchards standing like skeletons — seemed to *green* along the edges wherever he'd walked the day before. She'd told herself it was coincidence. Rain. Her imagination. But tonight, at the farmstead where they'd begged a loft to sleep in, she woke past midnight to an empty bed of straw and a window open on the fields below, and she looked down and saw him kneeling in the blight with his sleeves rolled up and his hands buried to the wrist in dying soil, and he was *glowing*.

Not brightly. Not like the shore. Just a soft gold rising out of the earth itself, up through his arms, threading along the gray veins in the wheat stalks the way spring threads through ice. And the veins were fading. Row by row, in a slow circle around his knees, the blight was simply... leaving. Drawn out of the plants and into him, she realized — she could see it, a darkness moving under his skin like smoke swallowed whole — and he was taking it, all of it, letting it sit in his body like poison, because apparently that was the price of the healing and apparently he had decided, alone, in the dark, without telling her, that he would pay it.

Maren stood at the window with her heart hammering and did not go down.

Going down meant admitting she'd been watching. Watching meant admitting she already knew he lied to her about being unmarked. And admitting *that* meant deciding what to do about it, and she found, to her fury, that she didn't know. Every instinct said run — she'd survived six years on the outside by trusting no one, least of all liars. But another voice, smaller and far more dangerous, said: *he heals blight at midnight and takes the poison into himself rather than let a farmer's family starve. What kind of monster does that? None.*

Below, the glow guttered out. Zephyrine swayed, caught himself on a fence post, vomited quietly into the ditch, and knelt there a long time, shaking, before he crawled back toward the house.

Maren lay back down in the straw and stared at the rafters until dawn.

* * *

*Zephyrine*

The farmer's name was Doss, and the blight was killing him along with his fields.

Zephyrine understood that now, though he hadn't when they'd arrived. Blight was not merely rot; it was a hunger, and hungry things fed on whatever was weakest. The same gray veins that laced the wheat laced the backs of Doss's hands, crept up his forearms, shadowed the whites of his eyes. In three weeks, maybe four, it would reach his heart. The local physicker had charged a fortune to say *put your affairs in order.*

So Zephyrine had paid in secret, three nights running, kneeling in the cold with his gift drawn up out of its deep well and poured out into roots and stems and the gray map of a dying man's blood. Each night it cost more. The blight didn't vanish when he drew it out — it went *into* him, coiling behind his ribs next to the coal of his inheritance, heavy as wet sand. This morning his urine had come dark. He was careful not to mention that to Maren.

"Strange weather," Doss said at breakfast, squinting out at his fields. He was a big man gone hollow, eating like a bird. "Wheat looks better than it's looked since spring. Wife says I'm imagining it." He flexed his hands on the table, and Zephyrine watched him notice — watched him turn them over slowly, staring at skin that was suddenly, impossibly clear. "Says my color's better too."

"Good soil does that," Zephyrine said. "Around here. Sometimes."

Doss looked at him for a long moment. Farmers know land the way priests know scripture, and Zephyrine watched the man do the arithmetic — the green rows, the healed hands, the young stranger who'd arrived the very week everything turned. Watched him decide, visibly, what he would and wouldn't say aloud in a kingdom with ash bells on every road.

"Sometimes," Doss agreed at last, gruff. "Eat your eggs, lad."

Under the table, Zephyrine's hands trembled. Not from fear. From something worse — the warm, terrible swelling in his chest that came from being *thanked without being able to receive it.* Every name in his little book was a child he hadn't saved. This field, these hands, this family — this much he could do, and he could do it only in secret, only at night, only while lying to the one person trying to keep him alive. Guilt and purpose had braided themselves together somewhere on the road and he could no longer tell which was which. He only knew that the book of names got heavier every day, and the blight in his own blood was getting heavier too, and both of them were his to carry, and he would.

That evening, walking the fence line with Maren, he felt the well inside him shudder — the coal and the poison pressing against each other, restless, leaking a heat he couldn't fully cage. He clamped down hard, stone-in-well, deep-water-still.

Maren glanced sideways at him. Held the glance one beat too long.

"Tomorrow we reach Millhaven," she said. "Biggest census house in the province. If we're going to get inside it, you need to be perfect tomorrow. Not good. *Perfect.*"

"Perfect," Zephyrine echoed, and smiled, and lied again.

And behind them, far down the black road, the ash bells of the last village began — very faintly, though there was no wind — to ring.
