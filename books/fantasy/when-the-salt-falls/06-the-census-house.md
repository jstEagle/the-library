## 6. The Census House

The census house at Drenlow had once been a tithe-barn, and it still wore its old life the way a hanged man wears his last shirt. Maren crouched in the ditch behind it, rain dripping off her hood's edge into her collar, and studied the building with the flat patience that had kept her alive for three years on the run.

It was a big structure, longer than a granary, built of grey fieldstone with a roof of slate gone green at the edges. The crown's surveyors had added their own touches: a new door of black iron where the barn doors had been, hinges the size of a man's forearm, and — most telling of all — no windows at all along the ground floor. Above, under the eaves, narrow slits had been bricked shut with mortar still pale as bone. Somebody had wanted this place dark. Somebody had wanted nothing inside to see out.

"They count them by lamplight," she said.

Zephyrine lay beside her in the wet grass, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him even through the rain. He'd stopped shivering two days ago, somewhere past the burned village of Ash Bells Road, and Maren had decided not to think too hard about what that meant. People who grew up warm didn't stop noticing cold. People who grew up warm also didn't heal blight out of a farmer's field with their bare hands while claiming to be Unmarked.

She hadn't confronted him yet. She was saving it.

"Count what?" he asked.

"You'll see."

* * *

They went over the wall at moonset, when the two guards on the south side met for their shared flask and their backs were turned for exactly forty breaths. Maren had counted those breaths twice from the treeline, and Zephyrine had watched her do it with an expression she was beginning to recognize — that soft, appalled attention, like a man watching someone gut a fish for the first time. Valley-raised. Gentle in the bones. It would get him killed unless she taught him better, and some stubborn part of her had already committed to teaching him.

She went first up the drainpipe, boots finding purchase in the dark, and came over the eaves onto the roof walk. Below, through a ventilation grate she'd scouted from the treeline, lamplight rose in a thin gold seam.

The grate gave to her prybar on the third try. They dropped into darkness that smelled wrong immediately — not the rot-and-straw smell of a barn, but something dry and sweetish, like flowers pressed too long between the pages of a book. Like a room where living things had been made not-living very carefully, so they wouldn't spoil.

Maren lit the stub of candle she carried cupped in her palm, shielding the flame with fingers scarred from three years of this work, and held it low.

Shelves.

Rows of shelves, floor to rafters, running the length of the barn like the ribs of some enormous beast. And on the shelves, thousands of glass reliquaries — small ones the size of a fist, larger ones like jars for preserves, each one sealed with wax the color of dried blood and tagged with a paper label written in a clerk's careful hand.

Each reliquary held light.

Not firelight. Not lamplight. A light that moved, slow as honey poured in winter, coiling against the glass like something asleep that dreamed of swimming. Blue-white mostly, though here and there one burned amber or green or a bruised violet, and when Maren passed her hand near the nearest shelf, every vessel within arm's reach brightened — just slightly, just enough — reaching toward her stolen gift the way sunflowers reached toward sun.

She heard Zephyrine's breath go out of him.

"Oh," he said. Just that. A small, wounded sound, and Maren turned fast because she'd heard men make that sound before, usually right before they broke.

He was standing in the aisle with both hands pressed flat against a shelf's edge, and the reliquaries around him had all woken at once, dozens of them, pulsing softly like a tidepool at night. His face in their glow was young and stricken and lit from below like something in a fresco of martyrdom.

"Maren." His voice came out cracked. "What are these?"

"You know what they are." She kept her own voice level, though the shelf-light turned her shadow into a stranger's. "I told you what I do. What I did. Gifts. Taken out of people. Bottled."

"But there are so many." He walked down the aisle slowly, and the light followed him, waking shelf after shelf, hundreds of vessels kindling as he passed until he walked inside a corridor of soft blue dawn. "There must be — Maren, there must be thousands—"

"Four hundred years of census," Maren said. "Every gifted child reported by a neighbor. Every healer dragged from her village. Every storm-caller, every ember-witch, every luck-bringer. They don't kill them." She touched one label idly, reading. "Girl, nine. Gift of mending. Village of Corrow. Taken spring census. That's the whole life. Nine years old, and then a jar."

"The children—" His voice climbed. "Are the *people*—"

"Alive? Some. The taking doesn't always kill. But what's in the jar is never given back." She watched him absorb it, watched the valley-boy flinch away from the truth and then force himself to turn back and look at it straight, and hated how much she respected him for it. "That's why I steal them back when I can. Carry them out of the kingdom. There are people past the mountains who know how to rejoin a gift to its owner, if you can get both halves together in time."

"And if you don't get there in time?"

"The gift fades. Goes out like a lamp left burning with no oil." She shrugged, because the alternative was letting her voice shake. "The crown calls it preservation. I call it what it is."

Zephyrine had stopped walking. He stood before a shelf in the barn's far corner, where the reliquaries were older — wax seals gone brittle, labels yellowed, handwriting shifting across decades of clerks. And he was staring at one particular jar with an expression Maren couldn't read at first, because she'd never seen grief and recognition arrive on a face at exactly the same moment.

"What is it?" she asked quietly.

He didn't answer. He lifted the reliquary from the shelf with both hands, the way you'd lift a sleeping child, and turned it so the label caught the candlelight.

The label was old. The ink had faded to brown. But the writing on it wasn't a clerk's hand at all — it was slanted and quick and beautiful, letters leaning into each other like people sharing secrets, and Maren saw Zephyrine's thumb tremble against the glass.

"This is my grandmother's handwriting," he said.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. The barn breathed around them — the tick of cooling stone, the soft pulse of four hundred sleeping gifts, rain working at the roof high above.

"That's impossible," Maren said finally. "Your valley has been closed for generations. Nobody from Veyra has ever—"

"My grandmother traveled." His voice was strange, distant, the voice of a man reading his own death warrant in a language he'd only half learned as a child. "Before she settled. Before me. She never talked about it. She used to say—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "She used to say the world outside was a ledger, and everything you loved got entered into it eventually. I thought it was just a saying. Old women say things."

He tilted the jar toward the light and Maren leaned in and read the label over his shoulder, and the words were simple and clerical and obscene:

*Vasquez, Sera. Gift: salt-sight. Status: harvested. Intake: autumn census, year 312.*

"Harvested," Zephyrine repeated. The word came out of him like a stone dropping into a well. "Harvested. Not catalogued. Not stored. *Harvested.* You harvest a crop. You harvest—" He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once, and Maren realized he'd gotten there ahead of her, gotten there the way he seemed to get everywhere lately, straight through the middle without stopping to be afraid first. "They're not keeping these. They're *using* them. Something is drinking them."

"How do you—"

"I can feel it." He pressed the jar against his chest, and all around them the reliquaries pulsed faster now, brighter, agitated, like birds in a loft when a hawk's shadow crosses the door. "All of them. They're not asleep, Maren. They're being *drawn*. Slowly. From everywhere. Can't you feel it? There's a pull, like — like water finding a drain—"

And Maren could feel it, suddenly, now that he'd named it. Her stolen gift stirred against her ribs like a fish on a line, tugging faintly toward — she turned, following the sensation — toward the barn's north end, where the shelves ended and a new construction began. Something that hadn't been part of any tithe-barn. Something built recently, mortar still pale, walls thick as a fortress.

A vault door. Iron, banded, taller than two men. And carved above it in the lintel-stone, the crowned sigil of Veyra's royal house — beneath which someone had chiseled, in smaller letters, a motto Maren had seen stamped on census decrees in a dozen burned villages:

*ALL GIFTS FLOW TO THE THRONE.*

"They're feeding it somewhere," she whispered. "Whatever's behind that door. All of this—" She gestured at the shelves, at the thousands of dimming lights, at four centuries of stolen childhoods and stolen healings and stolen storms, all of it flowing in a slow, patient river toward one iron door. "This isn't a census house. This is a larder."

Zephyrine hadn't followed her gaze to the door. He was still holding his grandmother's reliquary, and he'd gone very still in the way Maren had learned meant his mind was moving fast and dangerous.

"Sera Vasquez," he read again. "Salt-sight. Harvested, year 312." He looked at Maren. "My grandmother's name was Sera. She died last winter. In the valley. Of age, in her bed, with her whole family around her." His hands tightened on the glass. "So either this is a mistake — a clerical error, another woman with her name — or my grandmother's gift was taken decades ago and *she lived without it*, and raised me knowing, and never said—"

The reliquary in his hands flared.

Not gently this time. It blazed blue-white, sudden as lightning, and every other vessel in the barn answered it — a thousand points of light surging awake at once, the whole harvest-shelf forest blazing like a sky full of stars brought indoors — and Zephyrine gasped and staggered and nearly dropped the jar, and Maren lunged forward and caught his elbow, and for one heartbeat the entire census house of Drenlow burned with light visible, surely, from the road, from the guards' post, from the heavens—

—and then, all together, the lights guttered.

Every single one. A thousand reliquaries dimming in the same instant, like lungs exhaling, and in the silence that followed Maren felt the pull from behind the iron door strengthen — brief, sharp, hungry — the way a sleeper might swallow harder when food passes near its mouth.

Then dark. Then quiet. Then the ordinary tick of cooling stone, as if nothing had happened.

Maren became aware that she was gripping Zephyrine's arm hard enough to bruise. She let go.

"It knows we're here," she said.

"No." Zephyrine's voice shook. He clutched his grandmother's jar to his chest. "No, it knew we were here before we came. It's known about me since the flats. Since I crossed." He looked down at the reliquary, and in the last of its fading glow Maren saw his face go white as the salt of home. "It recognized her handwriting on the label. Or it recognized *me* recognizing it. Maren — whatever my grandmother was, whatever she did before the valley — the thing behind that door already knows my name."

Above them, through the roof, came the sound of boots on gravel. Many boots. Moving quickly, and spreading out around the building in a pattern Maren recognized, because she'd fled that pattern her whole adult life.

They weren't searching the grounds. They were surrounding it.
