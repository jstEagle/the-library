## 7. What Wears the Crown

The city of Aurelmar did not sleep so much as it dreamed badly.

Zephyrine felt that from the moment they passed beneath the Gate of Bells, where a thousand iron chimes hung in rusted ranks and never rang — the wind had been stripped of whatever it needed to move them. The streets beyond were broad and gray and full of people who walked quickly and looked at no one, as though looking too long might invite a question. Every third wall bore the census placard: *KNOW THYSELF BEFORE THE CROWN. PRESENT ALL GIFTED PERSONS FOR THE KING'S CENSUS. REWARD FOLLOWS HONESTY.*

Reward follows honesty. He had turned the phrase over for three days now, since Maren had smuggled them through the eastern checkpoints in a cart of dyer's madder, her arm around his shoulders like they were lovers and he was drunk. It was a sentence built like a trap — soft on both ends, teeth in the middle. In Veyra, honesty was something you gave freely or not at all. Here it had a price tag nailed to it.

"Stop reading them," Maren murmured. She walked half a step ahead, hood up, hands in her sleeves. In the city she moved differently than on the road — smaller, quieter, a woman folding herself into the gaps between other people's attention. "You read them like they're going to change."

"They might."

"They won't. They've been up since spring." She glanced back at him, and in the shadow of her hood her eyes caught what little light the overcast offered — that flat, quick gray he'd come to think of as the color of decisions. "Rite day. Everyone's thinking about the rite. That's our whole advantage, so stop being interesting."

"I'm not being interesting."

"You're standing in the middle of the Processional Way staring at a wall like it insulted your grandmother."

He almost laughed at that — the grandmother part was closer to true than she knew — but he let her pull him into the crowd instead, into the slow river of bodies flowing toward the Palace Terrace. Today was the Rite of Sustenance, held four times a year, when the king came out among his people to be seen and to see. Maren had explained it on the road with the flat contempt she saved for things she understood too well: a pageant, an anointing, a blessing of the harvests and the harbors and the army's new banners. The king hadn't missed one in forty years.

"He's old," she'd said. "Old kings love rituals. Rituals are how you prove you're still alive."

* * *

They climbed with the crowd to the terraced gardens above the Processional Way, where the poor and the merely curious could watch from between the cypress rows, and the rich watched back from the colonnade below, close enough to smell the incense. Zephyrine found a place against a low balustrade of white stone and made himself small, which was a skill he'd had since childhood — the youngest of a dying guild learns early how to be furniture.

Maren stood beside him, not touching him, not looking at him. Three days inside the walls had worn her sharp. She kept counting exits under her breath; he'd stopped pretending not to notice.

The bells began.

Not the dead bells of the gate — these were bronze and alive, ringing down from the palace towers in a pattern that started solemn and turned, note by note, into something sweeter, something that wanted very badly to be loved. The crowd hushed the way water goes still. And then the Processional Way filled with light and motion: the Honor Guard in their salt-white cloaks — the sight of that color made something twist behind Zephyrine's ribs, homesickness wearing the mask of fear — then the censers, then the priests of the Crown Ordained in their vestments stitched with tiny glass beads that caught the sun like frost.

Then the king.

Aldric III came slowly, borne in an open litter because his legs no longer trusted the stairs. He was not what Zephyrine expected. He was not a monster, not a tyrant carved from marble and cruelty. He was a thin old man with a scholar's stoop and kind, exhausted features, waving to his people with a hand that trembled — and the crowd roared its love at him, genuine love, wave after wave of it, and Zephyrine felt the wrongness of that before he understood it. The love sounded rehearsed. Not by the people. By the air itself.

And above the king's gray head, above the paper skin and the kindly ruined face, sat the crown.

It was an old thing, older than the dynasty that wore it — black iron worked into a circlet of interlocking shapes, each link shaped like nothing so much as a knot. Not decorative knots. *Functional* ones. Zephyrine stared at it and felt a cold start in the base of his skull and slide down his spine vertebra by vertebra, because he knew those knots. He had grown up tracing their cousins in salt on the guildhouse floor, in thread on his grandmother's loom, in the boundary-marks buried at the valley's edge where the flats began. Binding-work. The crown was not jewelry. The crown was a cage, worn open-side-down.

"Maren," he said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like someone else's. "Look at the crown."

"I'm looking at the king. The king is the story."

"No. Look at—"

And then the world *tilted*.

Later he would have no words for how it happened, only that it happened the way a floor gives way — silently, totally, without negotiation. The crowd noise fell away as if a door had closed on it. The sunlight thickened. And Zephyrine saw.

Beneath the crown — inside it, through it, coiled around the cage-links the way smoke coils inside a bottle — there was a *thing*.

It had no shape his eyes agreed to hold. When he tried to look at its body, his gaze slid off into an impression of vastness, of something folded many more times than space should allow, packed into the iron circlet like a god stuffed into a thimble. It was old in a way that made the word *old* meaningless — old the way stone is old, the way pressure at the bottom of the sea is old. It was not alive the way he was alive. It was *attending*, the way he was breathing.

And it was feeding.

He saw that next, and the seeing was worse. From every direction, faint as heat-shimmer, thin threads of light rose out of the crowd — out of the people themselves, out of their chests, their throats, their sleeping and waking minds — and drifted upward like pollen and poured into the crown. Most of the threads were pale and weak, the ambient gift that lives harmlessly in everyone, the little warmth that makes a baker's bread rise better than it should, that lets a mother know her child is crying two rooms away. Harmless threads. The thing drank those by the thousand and barely noticed.

But here and there — a woman near the colonnade, a soldier along the route, a child on a father's shoulders — the threads burned brighter. Stronger. Gifted, or near-gifted, unregistered and unaware, standing in their king's sunshine while something ancient sipped at them through the soles of its crown like a tick through cloth. And further off, past the palace walls, deeper in the city, Zephyrine felt rather than saw great cold reservoirs of light — dozens of them, hundreds — banked and bottled and *stored*, and lines of that stored light running steadily, patiently, into the crown.

The reliquaries. The census houses. The harvested gifts of an entire kingdom, piped home like water.

His stomach heaved. He gripped the balustrade. The white stone under his palm was ordinary stone, blessedly mute — the valley's silence clung to nothing here — and he realized distantly that if his gift were awake the way it had woken on the far shore of the flats, this much magic would have burned him hollow where he stood. As it was, the sight alone nearly did it.

*Maren,* he thought, absurdly. *Tell Maren.* But his mouth wouldn't form the words, because the words were insane, because there was nothing to point at, because the entire crowd was cheering and weeping with love for the frail old king and none of them — none — could see the cathedral-sized hunger crouched three feet above their heads.

None of them.

The thing turned.

There was no other way to say it. It had no face, and it turned anyway, the way you turn toward a sound in a dark room — and all of its attention, all of that geological, bottomless attention, came off the king and off the crowd and settled on the terrace garden, on the cypress shadows, on the balustrade of white stone —

— on him.

Zephyrine stopped breathing.

It looked at him the way a man looks at a knot in a rope he thought he'd tied perfectly forty years ago. There was recognition in it. There was *surprise* in it, and surprise in something that ancient was the most terrifying thing he had ever witnessed, more terrifying than hunger, because it meant the thing remembered. It remembered the binding. It remembered the hands that tied it. And some thread of those hands' blood was standing in the crowd on Rite Day, unshielded in his grief and his awe, looking back.

*Little knot,* said something that was not a voice, because sound would require air and this required nothing so merciful. The words arrived fully formed inside his skull, tasting of brine and iron and enormous patience. *Little loose end.*

The cold in his spine became absolute. He thought: *I'm going to die on a sunny terrace in front of ten thousand people and they'll call it a heart failure and the rite will go on.* His fingers scrabbled at the balustrade. Beside him Maren had gone rigid too — he registered that peripherally, her hand sliding inside her coat toward the knife she pretended she didn't carry, her eyes fixed not on the sky but on *him*, on his face, reading whatever horror must have been written there.

"Zeph," she breathed. "Zeph, what do you see?"

*What do I see.* How could he answer that? *I see the reason for the census. I see why the gifts are bottled. I see what wears your king like a glove.*

The attention pressed closer. He felt it sorting through him the way a customs officer turns out a traveler's bags — unhurried, thorough, faintly bored until it wasn't. It brushed the place where his gift lived, the inherited flicker he'd spent his whole life hiding, and paused there. Tasted it. And Zephyrine felt its surprise deepen, sharpen, curdle into something like appetite, and he understood with total clarity that the flicker was not small. It had only ever seemed small inside the valley, muffled under a hundred miles of salt. Out here it rang like a struck bell, and the bell had his grandmother's name on it, and the thing below the crown knew that name the way a prisoner knows the lock.

*Found you,* it said. Or perhaps: *There you are.* Or perhaps it said nothing at all and the meaning simply arrived, the way weather arrives, the way dawn arrives, indifferent to whether anyone is ready for it.

Then the king raised his hand for the Blessing of the Harvest, and the crowd surged to its feet with a sound like the sea, and in the roar and the motion and the sudden storm of incense-smoke the attention lifted — reluctantly, Zephyrine thought; *reluctantly* — and slid back into its cage of knots, and the world lurched back into its ordinary shape.

Sound returned. Color returned. The bells were finishing their sweet, loving pattern. The king's thin voice carried thinly across the terrace: "—and may the crown keep you, as you keep the crown."

The crowd repeated it like a heartbeat. *As you keep the crown. As you keep the crown.*

Zephyrine discovered he was sitting. He had no memory of sitting. His hands were shaking so badly he had to press them flat against the warm stone to make them stop, and even then they didn't stop.

Maren crouched beside him, and anyone watching would have seen a girl tending a friend overcome by the heat or the crowds or the wine. Her voice, when it came, was low and level and only slightly too fast — the voice of a woman who had survived by deciding quickly.

"We're leaving. Now, before the crowd breaks, before the guards sweep the terraces. Can you walk?"

"I—" His throat clicked. He swallowed and tried again. "Maren. The crown. There's something *in* the crown. It's feeding on them. All of them. It was drinking from the whole crowd, and the reliquaries — the reservoirs, I could feel them, hundreds of them, it's eating everything the census takes, and Maren—"

"Breathe."

"—and it saw me." His voice cracked on it. "It looked at me. It knows my blood. It called me—" He couldn't say it. *Little knot. Little loose end.* "It knew me, Maren. Before I ever left the valley, it already knew me."

For a long moment she just looked at him. He waited for disbelief — he had earned disbelief, lying to her about his gift since the night she cut him out of Sereth's convoy, and he flinched inwardly at the thought of what she'd find if she ever went looking through his own hidden cargo. But Maren had seen too many impossible things to waste time doubting one more. That was the terrible economy of fugitives: belief cost less than caution.

"Something in the crown," she repeated quietly. Testing the weight of it. Her jaw tightened. "The old stories say the first Aldric was crowned by something that came up out of the sea. My mother used to tell them to frighten us. We thought they were just stories."

"My grandmother says stories are just histories that survived."

That earned a ghost of her crooked almost-smile, gone as fast as it came. She took his wrist — not gently; Maren was not gentle when fear was driving — and pulled him to his feet. The crowd was still kneeling for the blessing, heads bowed, ten thousand people baring the backs of their necks to the thing above them, and Zephyrine made himself look at it one last time.

The crown gleamed dully on the old king's brow. Coiled within it, patient as sediment, the presence had resumed its feeding, threads of pale light rising from the bowed crowd like steam off morning fields. It did not look at him again. It didn't need to.

But as Maren drew him away, down the back stair, into the cypress shade, one last impression followed him like a hand resting briefly on the nape of his neck — calm, certain, almost gentle:

*Come and be untied, little Vasquez. Or I will come and untie everything you love.*

* * *

They did not speak until they were six streets away, in a dye-worker's quarter where the air stank of madder and vinegar and no one asked questions. Maren paid a woman two coppers for the use of her courtyard well, ostensibly to wash road dust; really to stand in a room with a locked door and no listeners.

Zephyrine knelt at the well's edge and let the cold water run over his wrists until his hands obeyed him again. Above them, laundry lines crossed the gray sky like rigging on a sinking ship.

"The reliquaries," he said finally. "The vaults under the census houses — I could feel them today. Like lamps behind a wall. Hundreds." He looked up at her. "We have to get into one. Tonight, tomorrow, soon. If I can touch one of the bottles — if I can feel how they're linked to it — maybe I can understand what we're actually fighting. Because right now I'm not even sure it *can* be fought. Maren, it looked at me like I was a loose thread on a garment it planned to wear for another thousand years."

Maren crouched across from him, forearms on her knees, and studied his face with that flat gray attention he'd learned meant she was rearranging a plan around new information.

"There's a census house in the Cordwainer District," she said. "Small one. My contact says the vault's beneath the tithe-hall, two floors down, iron door, four guards." A pause. Her voice dropped. "She also says they brought in a new shipment last week. Bottles from the southern provinces. Dozens of them."

Zephyrine's chest went tight. He thought of his grandmother's last letter, the ink gone brown, the line he'd read a hundred times on the road: *If they ever take the gifts, look for my handwriting on the labels. I marked everything I ever made, even the mistakes. Especially the mistakes.*

"Maren," he said. "One of those bottles might be hers."

Maren went very still. Then she stood, rolled her sleeves, and checked the knife she officially didn't carry.

"Then we'd better go tonight," she said, "before the thing in the crown decides to come collect its loose end first."

Somewhere across the city, faint through the walls and the washday noise, the bronze bells began to ring again — the same sweet, loving pattern, over and over, like a lullaby sung to something that never slept.
