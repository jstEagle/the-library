## 10. Sereth's Bargain

Sereth had carried a hundred decrees across the kingdom and never once been asked to sit down.

The room the palace had given them — Zephyrine and her both, though he did not know she was his keeper as much as his guide — was small and high in the eastern wing, all pale stone and narrow windows that looked out over the census yards. From here she could see the queues: the gifted and the frightened, shuffling forward with their names on paper tongues, walking into the white-robed courtesy of clerks who never wrote anything down.

She could feel the Crown's attention through the floor. It came up through her boots like cold water rising, patient as tide.

*He is stalling,* it said, in the voice that was not a voice. *Let him stall. Stalling is how the willing convince themselves.*

"Tea?" Sereth asked aloud, because the thing liked her to speak. It enjoyed wearing human manners the way a maggot wears meat.

Zephyrine sat by the window with his back very straight, the way valley boys sat when they were pretending not to be afraid. He was thinner than when she'd pulled him out of the flats' far shore, and there was something new in him — something settled and terrible — that she suspected he didn't yet have a name for. The Vasquez knot. The last tie. She'd known since the flats what he was; the Crown had known longer.

"No thank you," he said politely. Valley politeness. The kind that meant *I see you, I don't trust you.*

"Suit yourself." She poured one cup anyway, for her own hands to have work. "You've been three days in this city and you've seen nothing but corridors."

"I've seen enough." His eyes flicked to hers. Brown, unremarkable, except they weren't — she had watched the entity look out of its own ancient dark at those same eyes during the public rite and go still, the way a spider goes still when the web hums. "The census house. The reliquaries. My grandmother's handwriting on glass."

"You grieve loudly," Sereth said. "For someone pretending not to be gifted."

He flinched. Good. Honest things were easier to move than brave ones.

*Ask him now,* said the tide beneath the floor. *Offer while the grief is wet.*

Sereth set down her cup and folded her hands. Thirty years a courier. Thirty years of roads and rain and the small mercenary kindnesses of the post, and every mile of it in service of the thing under the crown, because the thing had bought her at fourteen with a simple transaction: your sister lives, you carry my letters. She had stopped counting the letters that were sentences long ago.

"Vasquez," she said. "Let me tell you what happens next, so you understand the shape of your week. Tomorrow the census rolls close. The day after, the reliquary vaults begin their second harvest — the ones already bottled get poured into the Crown, and the ones still walking get bottled. Your guild is on the list. Veyra valley, forty-one souls, the last unregistered enclave in the kingdom. The flats won't protect them much longer. Nothing protects anyone much longer."

"They can't cross the salt," Zephyrine said. "No rider survives it. You told me so yourself, on the crossing. You crossed anyway."

"I crossed because I'm permitted," Sereth said. "And permission can be extended. Roads are only roads. The flats are only salt. What keeps the valley closed is older than salt, and it's fraying — you knew that too, didn't you? Your grandmother told you the protection was thinning. She wasn't guessing."

He stood up then, slowly, the way people stand when sitting has become unbearable. "What do you want from me?"

There it was. Not *what do you want*, but *from me.* He already understood he was the coin.

"The Crown offers terms," Sereth said, and heard how strange the words sounded in her own mouth — a courier delivering not a decree but a covenant. "Your guild is granted immunity. Permanent. Registered nowhere, harvested never. The flats remain theirs, or the valley opens and they pass freely into a kingdom where no hand will ever reach for them again. Every Vasquez. Every Unmarked elder. Even the dying ones. Written exemption, sealed in blood, unbreakable by any crown that follows — and there will be crowns that follow, Vasquez, because the thing under this one is patient about succession."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, you present yourself. Willingly. At the rite of harvest, before witnesses, you kneel, and you give what your blood carries — freely given, freely taken. No bottles. No force. The Crown prefers consent; consent tastes better, and—" she hesitated, because the next part was true and true things were dangerous to say, "—and it binds cleaner. A gift surrendered freely cannot be refused. That's the old law. Your grandmother's law, if the letters we intercepted are anything to judge by."

Silence. Through the window, the census queue shuffled forward another body-length. Somewhere below, a child was crying — the flat, exhausted crying of someone who had cried past tears into habit.

*He will ask about the price of refusal,* the tide said. *They always ask. Let him ask.*

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the harvest proceeds as scheduled. Veyra first — the valley is the last loose thread, and loose threads itch. Forty-one names, Vasquez. I've read them. I can recite them. There's a girl called Odile who mends nets, an elder with a bad hip whose name is Brannoc, a—"

"Stop." His voice cracked on it. He pressed his palm against the windowsill, against the stone, and Sereth saw — just barely, just a shimmer like heat off summer cobbles — the flicker rise in his skin and die again. He caught himself doing it and snuffed it, fast, guilty. Even here. Even alone with her. He had spent his whole life hiding that flicker behind salt walls and he could not stop hiding it now, even from the woman sent to collect him.

*Beautiful,* the tide murmured. *They hide even when hiding saves no one. It makes them soft.*

"Why me?" Zephyrine asked quietly. "The guild has stronger gifts. Older blood. Why does the Crown want the youngest?"

Because you are the knot. Because your grandmother wove the binding with her mother's mother's hands and hid the final tie inside a baby and buried the baby in a valley of forgetting. Because the thing under the crown has spent two hundred years pulling at that thread and you are the last inch of it.

But she couldn't say any of that. So she said: "Does it matter? The offer stands either way. Immunity for surrender. Safety for everything you are."

"That's not safety," he said. "That's a ransom note with better handwriting."

Sereth almost smiled. Almost. "It's the best offer anyone in this kingdom will get this year."

*

She left him after that — left him to the window and the view of the queues — and walked the long corridor to the little shrine alcove where the Crown preferred to speak with her face to face. It was a servant's chapel once, stripped bare, one candle. She knelt on stone that bit her knees and let the tide come up through her.

*He stalls well,* it said. *Stalling is thinking, and thinking is hoping, and hope is a handle.*

"He'll take time."

*Time is ours. We have eaten centuries.* A pause, and then, softer, the way it went soft when it wanted to be believed: *Show him the valley, courier. Show him what waits for Veyra if he refuses. Not cruelty. Clarity. Clarity ripens faster than fear.*

So that evening Sereth took him up onto the palace roof, which was against seventeen rules and permitted by one that outranked them all, and showed him the east.

The capital's rooftops fell away in tiers, and beyond them the land unrolled in the last copper light — fields, rivers, the grey smudge of burned villages on the horizon like bruises. And farther east, faint as a watermark, the white line of the flats, catching the sun the way only salt can, burning bright and blind.

"Do you know what I feel, from up here?" Sereth asked. "When I stand over the city with the Crown awake in me. I feel the valley. Like a hole in a tooth. Like a silence shaped exactly like home."

"It can't reach the flats."

"Not yet." She turned to face him. In the failing light his face was all shadow and stubbornness. "Understand what I'm telling you, Vasquez, because I'm breaking rules to tell it. The binding that holds the Crown together — the one your line wove — isn't a wall. It's a knot. And knots hold by tension. Every year the tension pulls a little more of the world toward it. The census houses aren't policy. They're the knot feeding. The hunt isn't the king's fear. It's hunger with paperwork." She pointed east, at the white line. "Two hundred years ago your ancestors built a valley the entity couldn't taste and put the last knot of the binding in the middle of it. The entity has wanted to swallow that valley since the day it was made. It has been *patient*. But patience ends, Vasquez. The heir is nearly ripe. The vaults are being drained. When the Crown finishes this harvest cycle, it will be strong enough to walk across salt like I do — and it will walk east, and the flats will not stop it, because nothing stops it except the thing it has hunted longest."

"Which is me."

"Which is the gift in you." She said it gently, and hated herself for the gentleness, because gentleness was the cruelest tool she owned. "Give it freely and the hunt ends — for everyone. Refuse, and you're not protecting your guild by keeping it. You're standing between them and a tide with your arms open, calling it *shelter*."

Zephyrine looked east for a long time. The salt line faded as the sun died, but he kept looking, as if he could see through darkness and distance all the way to the valley that forgot — to the rite fires, to Brannoc's bad hip, to Odile mending nets by lamplight, to a grandmother who had put her whole life into hiding a boy she must have known was a loaded thing.

"You've served it thirty years," he said at last. "Did it ever offer you terms?"

The question landed harder than he could know. Sereth thought of a girl of fourteen in a burning border town, of a sister coughing blood in a quarantine wagon, of a voice out of a broken crown saying *carry my letters and she wakes tomorrow.* She had said yes in less time than it takes to say it.

"Yes," she said. "Once."

"And?"

"And I took the bargain." She turned away from him, toward the darkening city, where ten thousand candles were coming alight in ten thousand windows above ten thousand cellars full of bottled light. "That's why I know exactly what I'm offering you. And exactly what it costs."

*

That night she reported to the tide, kneeling in the bare chapel with the single candle drowning in its own wax.

*He wept?*

"No. He thanked me. For the honesty." Her throat was tight. "That's worse."

*It is not worse. It is progress. The kind break sooner than the proud — they simply apologize while doing it.* The tide shifted beneath her, vast and pleased, and she felt it turning her words over, tasting them. *He will go to the king. He is already composing the visit — the clerk's disguise, the mercy angle. Let him. Lucidity is a door we leave open on purpose. Let him hear the beggar's version of the crown. Compassion is the finest solvent we have. He will choose surrender out of pity, and pity will feel like his own idea, and ideas a man believes are his own are the ones he defends to the death.*

"And if he doesn't?"

*Then Veyra teaches him the difference between bargaining and arithmetic.* A pause, and the tide went very cold, very still, the way it did when it was looking at something far away. *Courier. One more thing. The girl. Maren. She is in the city. She has found the thief-tunnels under the census house, and she has found allies among the gutter-gifted, and she is asking questions about the Vasquez boy in low taverns with a knife on the table.*

Sereth kept her face still. "Shall I deal with her?"

*No. She is useful. Fear without an object drifts; fear with a beloved object aims.* The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. *Watch her. Learn what he loves. And when the hour comes, courier, you will tell him the last truth — that immunity purchased with his gift covers the guild, but not the girl. The girl is a fugitive twice over. Thief of a stolen gift. The terms do not extend to thieves.*

Sereth knelt in the candle-smell and felt the trap assemble itself around two young people who thought they were building an escape, piece by patient piece, out of the oldest materials in the world: love, and need, and the willingness of good people to pay other people's prices.

*Go,* said the tide. *Sleep. Tomorrow he will ask you whether the offer holds if he brings proof of the binding. Tell him yes. Tell him whatever keeps him walking toward us. And courier—*

"Yes."

*When he finally gives it — and he will give it, they always give it, the kind ones fastest of all — do not watch his face. Watch the valley line on the horizon. I want to know the moment the salt begins to fall.*

Sereth rose on aching knees, took up her courier's coat with its hundred miles worn into the seams, and went to lie down in a bed she had not truly slept in for thirty years, knowing that tomorrow she would smile at the Vasquez boy and mean it, and that meaning it was the deepest treachery she had ever been asked to perform —

— and that somewhere below, in the vaults, three hundred small lights in three hundred glass throats were flickering, all together, like bells before an earthquake.

By morning, the eastern sky over the flats had gone the color of a healed wound, and the first refugees from Veyra were already stumbling west across the salt, carrying word that the valley's boundary had begun, for the first time in two hundred years, to let people *out*.
