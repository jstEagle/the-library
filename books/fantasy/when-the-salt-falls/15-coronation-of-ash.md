## 15. Coronation of Ash

The bells woke Maren before dawn, and they were wrong.

She had lived in cities that rang their hours, in towns that rang weddings and funerals and the small ordinary alarms of daily life. She knew what bells were supposed to sound like — bronze and patient, a voice made of metal. These bells screamed. The sound came off the palace hill in jagged sheets, one bell out of rhythm with all the others, as if something enormous had seized the tower by the throat and shaken it.

She was dressed before she finished thinking. Knife in her boot, Zephyrine's letters in her coat, the stolen gift in her chest humming its old restless hum. She found him already awake in the safehouse's narrow room, standing at the window with the grey light on his face.

"You heard it too," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone heard it." Down in the street, people were coming out of their doors in nightclothes, faces tipped up toward the hill. "That's not a summons, Zeph. That's a *declaration*."

He turned from the window. In the weeks since the vault raid — since the elder died with glass dust in her hair and three hundred reliquaries carried out through smoke — he had changed in ways Maren didn't have words for yet. Thinner. Quieter. But steadier too, the way a rope is steady when both ends are tied to something. He looked at her now and said, "The heir."

"The heir," she agreed. "It can't wait for the king to die anymore. We've starved it. It needs a new body before this one fails, so it's going to take the boy while his father still breathes."

"And if it binds itself into a young host—"

"It gets decades," she finished. "Maybe a century. Every harvest we cut off, it replaces with time." She pulled her coat on. The rain had started again, the thin cold drizzle that never seemed to leave the capital now, as if even the weather had been conscripted. "We need Sereth."

Something moved behind his eyes at the name — not anger, not quite. They had not spoken of the courier since the vault, since Sereth had stood in the burning doorway and let them pass without raising the alarm. A silence with teeth in it.

"She'll be watching the gates," Zephyrine said. "If there are gates left to watch."

There weren't. By the time they reached the lower city, the great iron doors of Ashgate were swinging closed, chains thick as a man's arm rattling through pulleys, soldiers in crown-grey pushing back the morning crowd with the flats of their spears. Merchants screamed about their carts. A woman with two children pressed against the shrinking gap until a guard took her shoulder, gently, terribly gently, and turned her back inside.

"Census order," a crier was shouting, hoarse and mechanical. "By decree of the Crown Regent: the city is sealed for the sacred coronation. No egress. All gifted persons will present themselves at the census houses for *protection*. Protection—" the word cracked, and Maren saw the crier's hands shaking around his scroll, "—protection will be provided."

"They're going to sweep the districts," Zephyrine murmured. "Door to door. Every unregistered gift in this city walks itself into a glass jar within the week."

"Then we have less than a week." She caught his sleeve. "Come on. I know where she drinks."

* * *

Sereth was not drinking. That was the first wrong thing.

The tavern under the aqueduct arches was empty except for her — the kind of empty that meant paid-for, cleared-out, *arranged*. She sat at a corner table with her back to the wall and her courier's coat folded neatly over the chair beside her, and she was looking at her own hands as though she'd never seen them before.

Maren stopped three tables away. Old habit: never close the distance until you know which way the blade points.

"You look terrible," Sereth said without lifting her head. Her voice was flat as slate. "Both of you. Sit down or don't. It doesn't matter after tonight."

"What happens tonight?" Zephyrine asked.

Sereth laughed then — a short, airless sound with nothing in it. "Tonight?" She finally raised her eyes, and Maren felt her stomach drop, because Sereth's eyes, always so level, so *managed*, were red-rimmed and exhausted and afraid. "Tonight they crown a boy who hasn't slept in four days because the thing in his father's crown won't stop talking to him. Tonight the vaults refill. Tonight everything you stole comes back tenfold, because a coronation is a harvest festival, little thief, and every soul in this city is a sheaf."

"You could stop it," Maren said. "You could open the gates."

"I could *die*, is what I could do." Sereth stood, slow, joints cracking like an old woman's, and Maren realized with a jolt how much the thing had been spending her — how long since any of them had actually looked at her? "Do you know what I am? Not the courier story. What I actually am?"

Neither of them answered.

"I'm a *vessel in progress*," Sereth said. "A backup. It poured a thread of itself into me eleven years ago when it bought my life on the flats, and every errand I run for it, the thread grows. There is enough of it in me now that if the heir dies before the rite completes—" she smiled, and it was the worst smile Maren had ever seen, "—it doesn't need the boy at all. It has a spare walking around in a courier's coat."

The rain drummed on the aqueduct above them. Somewhere far off, the wrong bell tolled again, once, like a thing clearing its throat.

"So why tell us?" Zephyrine asked quietly.

"Because you're going to kill it, aren't you." It wasn't a question either. Sereth picked up her coat but didn't put it on; she just held it, crushed in both fists. "I felt it in the vault doorway. When I let you pass. For one moment — one — I wanted you to win more than I wanted to live, and it *felt* me want that. Do you understand what that's like? To be a house that something else lives in, and to feel the tenant notice you noticing?"

"No," Maren said honestly. "But I know what it's like to carry something in your blood that isn't yours."

Sereth looked at her for a long moment. And Maren watched it happen — watched the hesitation arrive, the first crack in eleven years of service, visible as a seam in fired clay. Sereth's jaw worked. Her knuckles went white around the coat.

"The rite begins at moonrise," she said at last, each word dragged out like a nail. "The boy will be brought up the processional stair from the crypt side. The garrison rotates at the tenth hour — the rotation is real, it isn't a trap, I checked it three times because I couldn't believe it myself." She swallowed. "I'm telling you this because I hate it. Because I am so tired of being *used*. And because if you fail tonight, it puts a thread of itself into that child, and I know exactly what that child's next fifty years look like from the inside."

"Why help us?" Zephyrine asked. "Why risk it?"

Sereth flinched — actually flinched, the first time Maren had ever seen her do it — and for a heartbeat she looked not like the entity's agent but like a girl crossing salt flats alone, years ago, making a bargain she hadn't understood.

"Because last night," she said, "for the first time in eleven years, I dreamed something that wasn't its dream. And in the dream, someone opened the door for me." She shoved past them toward the door, then stopped, not turning around. "Moonrise. Tenth hour. If you're late, don't bother dying heroically — it won't matter."

The door banged shut behind her. Rain rushed into the silence.

"That wasn't a trap?" Zephyrine asked.

"Oh, it's a trap," Maren said grimly, watching the empty doorway. "It might even be one she doesn't know she's leading us into. But right now it's the only door we've got — and I don't think she lied. Did you see her face? Servants of that thing don't hesitate, Zeph. She hesitated."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Then we'd better make her hesitation worth something."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and the city spent it holding its breath.

Maren walked the processional route twice in borrowed clerk's grey, memorizing the garrison posts, the lantern spacing, the places where the crowd barriers funnelled mourners-and-well-wishers — the same crowd, she noted bitterly, that had been ordered to attend *or be counted absent from the census* — into narrow channels. The palace had hung black and gold banners for a living king's son, and the banners were already streaking in the rain, gold bleeding down the black like wounds.

Everywhere, the wrongness gathered. Dogs wouldn't stop howling near the palace hill. The reliquary wagons — six of them, escorted, tarped — rolled up the processional road at noon, and Maren felt her stolen gift recoil against her ribs as they passed, like a dog smelling its own cage.

Zephyrine worked too, apart from her, the way he'd been working ever since they decoded his grandmother's letters. He sat in the safehouse with the original knot's diagram spread around him and his grandmother's empty reliquary in his hands, and he did not talk about what he was planning. When Maren asked, he only said, "After tonight. If we get through tonight, I'll tell you everything." And she let it go, because some bargains you agree to before you hear the price, or you never find the courage to pay it.

At dusk the rain stopped, which was somehow worse. The clouds tore open along the east, and the moon rose fat and white over the sealed city, and every bell in the capital began to toll in perfect, horrible unison — no longer screaming, but *aligned*, hundreds of voices become one voice, and the voice said nothing and demanded everything.

Maren stood with Zephyrine in the shadow of the aqueduct, watching the processional stair glow with lantern-light up the palace flank.

"Tenth hour," she said.

Below them, the first ranks of the procession began to climb — and at their center, small between two ranks of crown-grey guards, walked a boy of perhaps fourteen in a coronation robe too heavy for his shoulders, his head bowed under a circlet he hadn't earned yet, his steps those of someone being led to an altar.

And beside him, unarmored, unarmed, her courier's coat hanging loose on a frame that seemed thinner than it had that morning, walked Sereth — looking straight ahead, her face a mask of perfect service.

Except her hand, Maren saw. Her hand, hanging at her side, was trembling.

"Even she doesn't know whose side she's on," Maren breathed. "Come on. We climb."

Behind them, across the darkened city, the reliquary vault doors began, silently, to open.
