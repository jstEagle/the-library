## 17. The Long Walk Up

**Zephyrine**

The palace had sealed itself like a wound closing.

Zephyrine felt it happen from the servants' stair — a shudder through the stone, deep as a heartbeat, and then the groan of portcullis chains winding taut somewhere below. The gates of Ashfall were shutting. Every gate. The entity inside the crown was pulling the city around itself like a fist.

"Move," Maren breathed at his shoulder, her hand pressed flat against the small of his back, guiding more than pushing. "Stairs first. Argue later."

He moved. His whole body still hummed with what his grandmother had poured back into him two nights ago — the fragment of gift she'd hidden in the valley's salt when he was an infant, returned to him in a rite that smelled of brine and burning cedar. He was whole now. Complete. And because of it, he was *loud*. He could feel himself the way a lighthouse feels its own beam: a great slow sweep of presence that no wall could mute and no silence could hide.

The thing on the throne knew exactly where he was. He could feel it knowing.

"Left at the landing," Sereth said ahead of them, her voice flat as ever, but her pace had changed. She had been walking like a courier all this time — measured, unhurried, a woman with nowhere she needed to be faster than duty required. Now she walked like someone late for her own funeral. "The gallery connects to the east corridor. From there, the antechamber above the throne room."

"And you're certain of the way?" Maren asked, and there was no softness in it. There hadn't been since the alley, since Sereth had stood before them in the rain of ash-fall and finally, *finally*, admitted what she was: not the king's courier, but the crown's.

"I carried messages down these stairs for eleven years." Sereth didn't turn. Her grey braid swung between her shoulder blades, silver-tipped, precise. "I know every stone that has heard my footsteps. Whether I'm certain of anything else—" A pause, one step long. "You'll see."

They came out into the gallery and Zephyrine stopped breathing.

The windows ran the length of the hall, floor to vaulted ceiling, and beyond them the sky was wrong. Not storm-wrong. *Feast*-wrong. Clouds had gathered in a slow spiral over the palace spire, turning like milk stirred into tea, and light came down through their center — pale gold, honeyed, the color of candlelight through reliquary glass. The whole city below lay silent under it. No bells. No dogs. Even the gulls had gone.

It was drinking, he thought. All that light wasn't shining *down*. It was being pulled *up*.

*That's the heir,* his grandmother had told him, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise, her eyes wet and furious and ancient. *A boy of nineteen. When it finishes feeding on him, it will have strength enough to reach past the flats. Past anything. Go now, child. Go while it's busy eating.*

He went.

* * *

**Maren**

Maren counted exits the way other people counted breaths. It was the first thing her stolen gift had taught her — not the taking of gifts, but the living-with-them, the constant arithmetic of *where do I run, where do I hide, who dies first if this goes wrong.*

She had stopped counting who died first. Now she counted everyone, and refused to rank them, and hated herself a little for how much harder that made everything.

Sereth walked three paces ahead. Zephyrine walked at her side, glowing — not visibly, not yet, but Maren had learned to see it, the way you learn to see heat shimmer off summer stone. Since the valley gave him back his missing piece he burned like a lamp behind frosted glass, and every step toward the throne room made the frost thinner.

*When we're done,* she kept thinking, absurdly, uselessly, *he'll be hollow.* She had seen hollow people. She had made some herself, back when she was the hunter instead of the hunted, prying gifts loose from sleeping travelers with hands that shook and a heart that didn't dare. Hollow people smiled too easily afterward, or never again. She had promised herself she would not love someone she was going to help empty out, and here she was, loving him so hard it felt like a second pulse, and the emptying hadn't even started.

Footsteps below. Many.

"In here." Sereth shouldered open a door — a linen closet, narrow, smelling of lavender and cold iron — and pulled them both into the dark, and shut it, and put her ear against the wood.

Through the gap under the door Maren watched boots pass. Dozens of pairs. Palace guard, marching in perfect unison, and beneath the rhythm of their steps something else, a sound like whispering, like cloth tearing slowly, and she realized with a crawling scalp that the soldiers were murmuring in their sleep. Marching asleep. Eyes open, faces slack, mouths moving with words that weren't theirs.

"It's wearing them already," Zephyrine whispered against her ear. His breath shook. "Not just the king. Anyone it can reach through the crown's oaths."

"They swore service," Sereth said quietly. "Oaths are doors. It has been collecting doors for a very long time." She turned in the dark, and Maren saw her face by the thin seam of window-light through the doorframe — grey, composed, and underneath it something moving like water under ice. "The antechamber is one flight up. After that, I can take you no further without the guards noticing me. My face is known to every oath-sworn man in this building."

"You mean they'll see what you really are," Maren said.

"Yes."

"What *are* you, Sereth? Truly. Before the end — I want the truth, all of it, no courier's half-answers."

For a moment Maren thought she wouldn't answer. Then Sereth exhaled, and it seemed to carry eleven years out of her.

"I was given to the crown at six years old. A tithe. My village owed the census houses a debt of gifts, and they had none to pay, so they paid in flesh — a child raised inside the binding, fed to it slowly, taught to be its hands when hands were needed." Her voice did not waver; that was somehow worse than weeping. "I have served it longer than I have served any living person. And I believed, until very recently, that service was the same as purpose." Her eyes found Zephyrine in the dark. "Then you looked at it in the square and did not kneel. Do you remember? Everyone knelt. You stood there like a nail standing up out of a floor, and it *noticed* you, and for the first time in thirty years I saw it want something it could not simply take."

"It can't take a freely offered gift," Zephyrine said. "Only given ones feed it truly. That's why it needs consent. Why it's spent centuries building machines of consent — censuses, oaths, tithes. It wants us to *choose* it."

"Yes. And you are going to walk up those stairs and offer it everything, freely, as a trap." Something flickered across Sereth's face that might, in another woman, have been a smile. "It is the most beautiful cruelty I have ever been asked to abet. Come. While the patrol is still counting doors below."

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The antechamber above the throne room had been built for petitioners waiting to be judged — a long room of benches and tall windows, all of it designed to make the small feel smaller. Now the benches were shoved aside, and the guild survivors were there, the last eleven of Veyra, and his grandmother stood among them leaning on a spear like a pilgrim, like a general, like both at once.

"You came up through the linen closets," she said. It was not a question. "Your grandfather hid in those closets during the first Rite of Unmarking. Seventy years ago. Some things about this palace never change."

"Grandmother." His voice cracked on the word and he let it. There was no time left for composure; he could feel the thing below them rising toward some vast intake of breath, the spiral of cloud tightening, the light thickening like honey off a spoon. "The knot-work. Tell me again. All of it."

She told him. She had told him four times now, and each time it sounded less like magic and more like carpentry, which was the truth of it: the binding was a joint, cut by his bloodline into the fabric of the world, holding the entity inside the crown the way a mortise holds a tenon. And joints, unlike cages, could be opened — but only from the inside, only by the cutter's hand, only with a gift given wholly, freely, without reserve. Not a battle. An offering. The one language the thing understood and could not refuse.

"The king will hold the knot when you loosen it," she said. "His mind surfaces when the creature feeds. It's the only hour he is himself. If you begin during the feeding—"

"I know."

"If you hesitate, it will take the offering and keep the crown, and there will be nothing after that. Not the valley. Not the girl. Nothing."

"I know." He looked at her — small and bent and iron-hearted, the woman who had let him believe he was abandoned so that he would grow up unafraid, and who was now going to stand in this room while he went down to die or be emptied or possibly both. "And you'll be here."

"I will be here." She took his face in both hands. Her palms smelled of salt and lamp oil. "Vasquez," she said, using the old name the way you'd strike flint. "We were never keepers. We were *masons*. Build true."

Below them, through the floor, something began to laugh.

* * *

**Maren**

They went down together, and Maren learned what it meant to walk beside a lit lantern into a dark that wanted it.

The great doors of the throne room stood open. Of course they did. It wanted him to come in; the invitation was part of the trap it thought it was setting. Beyond the doors the hall stretched away in columns and shadow, and at the far end, on the dais beneath the turning eye of the ceiling, sat the throne.

The king was in it. What remained of him. He had grown translucent at the edges, Maren thought — a man becoming a watermark of himself — and beside the throne, kneeling in a pool of white ceremonial light, was the heir.

Prince Alaric. Nineteen years old. He was not struggling. That was the worst of it. The light poured down into him and he knelt inside it with his head bowed and his hands open on his knees, receiving, and his lips moved in what might have been prayer, and the light drank from him the way the reliquaries had drunk — except he was not glass, he was a boy, and Maren could see the color going out of him in slow tides, warmth leaving his skin like a tide going out over sand.

Behind the throne, coiled through the shadow like smoke through a keyhole, the entity turned.

Zephyrine made a sound low in his chest — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The thing wore the king's shadow the way a hand wears a glove, and it lifted what might be called a face, and its attention came across the hall and settled on Zephyrine like a physical weight, like snowfall, like the entire white silence of the flats pressing down.

*"The Vasquez child,"* it said. Its voice did not come through the air. It arrived already inside the skull, patient, enormous, courteous as a census clerk. *"Whole at last. How kind of the valley to finish its work."*

"Let the boy go." Zephyrine's voice held. Maren marveled at it even as her own hand shook on her knife-hilt. "Your feeding is done. You've eaten a kingdom. I've come to give you the rest."

*"Freely?"*

"Freely."

*"Come forward, then, little mason. The throne room has always been a workshop. Let us see your joinery."*

They crossed the hall. Maren kept to Zephyrine's left, where she'd always kept, knife drawn though she knew knives meant nothing here — but her gift was a thief's gift, a taking-thing, and she had spent the whole road learning that taking-things had uses even against the untakeable. Distraction. A grabbed sleeve. One heartbeat of surprise bought for a dying farmer's blight, once; perhaps one heartbeat bought here, for him.

Sereth walked on his right.

At the foot of the dais the guards appeared — a dozen of them, sleep-marching, murmuring — and fanned across the steps, and Maren's heart dropped like a stone into a well.

But Sereth stepped forward first.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

Later, trying to remember it, Zephyrine could never get the order of events right. Memory broke apart at the center of things, the way a river breaks around a stone.

He remembered Sereth saying, in her flat courier's voice, "My lords, the crown's business," and stepping onto the first step of the dais, and every sleepwalking guard turning toward her with recognition — she was known, she was the crown's own, her face opened every door —

He remembered her hand going into her coat and coming out not with a seal or a decree but with the letter-knife she had used to open royal correspondence for eleven years, a slim blade of grey steel, and driving it through her own palm, and speaking — loudly, clearly, in the voice of formal renunciation that every servant of the crown learned in their first year:

*"By my own will I unsay my oath. My service was taken; I return it broken. Let the door close on the crown forever."*

He remembered the entity *screaming.*

Not aloud — inside everything, inside the walls, inside Zephyrine's teeth. An oath sworn and kept for thirty years was a door it owned, a channel, a feeding line, and Sereth had just slammed it shut from the inside with her own blood and her own will, freely given, freely revoked — and the backlash tore through her like a rope snapping under load.

The guards collapsed like puppets with cut strings. The light guttered.

And Sereth — Sereth stood swaying on the steps with her hand ruined and black veins climbing her wrist, and she turned her head and found Zephyrine in the shadows, and she was smiling, actually smiling, the first one he had ever seen on her face, crooked and human and astonished.

"The doorway's yours," she said. "Eleven years of carrying letters, and this is the only message I ever wanted to deliver." Blood ran from her palm and dripped, each drop hissing faintly where it struck the marble. "Go up. Don't look back at me. Looking back is how it gets in."

"Sereth—" Maren was already moving toward her, and Sereth snapped at her, sharp as a whip-crack: "*No.* Your place is at his left. You know it is. Girl — girl, listen — the taking-gift of yours. When he opens the knot, everything it has hoarded will try to pour back into the world at once. Someone must *hold the door of returning.* Only a thief knows how to hold a door against a flood. That's your toll. Pay it."

Maren went white. Zephyrine saw her understand — saw her understand and choose it in the same breath, her jaw setting, her hand finding his sleeve and squeezing once, hard, a whole speech compressed into five fingers.

"I'll see you after," she whispered to him. It was a command disguised as a promise.

Then she turned to the heir, and Zephyrine climbed.

* * *

**Maren**

Sereth stayed upright until Zephyrine reached the throne. Maren was sure of that much, because Maren forced herself to be sure of nothing else — no looking back, no counting the cost mid-climb. Arithmetic later. Climb now.

Zephyrine knelt beside the dying king, and laid his gifted hand over the king's translucent one, and began to speak the knot-work — the mason's words, the Vasquez words, in a voice that rang against the pillars like a bell dipped in honey. The air above the crown began to move. Threads of it, visible now, golden and taut, running from the crown into the heir's open hands, into the walls, into the earth, into eleven years of Sereth's closed door straining against its own lock.

The entity rose out of the king's shadow like a wave deciding to become a wall.

Maren planted her feet at the base of the dais, spread her thief's hands, and opened the only door she owned.

Everything came at once.

Behind her, Sereth made a sound — small, almost relieved, the sound of a woman setting down a weight carried since childhood — and Maren heard her body meet the marble, gently, the way snow meets a roof.

Maren held the door.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The knot opened like dawn.

He gave everything — the valley's salt in his marrow, his grandmother's hidden ember, the flicker he'd hidden from the Founding Rite, the roar that had awakened him on the flats, the whole bright inheritance of the Vasquez line — freely, wholly, without reserve, and the entity could not refuse it, had never been able to refuse it, and it turned from the starving boy on the floor to seize this vaster feast, and in the instant of its turning, the king's eyes cleared.

Human. Blue. Wet with gratitude.

"Hold," the king whispered — to the knot, to the dark, to Zephyrine, to no one — and his ruined hands closed around the loosened binding and *held it open*, the last act of a man who had been a door for forty years choosing, at the end, to be a hinge instead.

The entity screamed and poured itself toward the offering—

—and found the offering was a joint, and the joint was opening, and beyond it was not a cage but the whole of the sky.

Maren's voice came from below, raw and steady, holding the door of returning against the flood of freed gifts, and Zephyrine let go of the last of himself into the light, and the crown began, very quietly, to rust.
