## 18. Crown of Teeth

The throne room doors were taller than any door had a right to be, and they were already open.

Zephyrine stood at the threshold with Maren's hand on his left shoulder and his grandmother's voice in his memory, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man standing at the edge of a cliff he has walked toward his whole life, that no one was going to stop him now. The corridor behind them was littered with the cost of their passage — Sereth's blood still warm on the stones three rooms back, the elder's name unspoken between them like a stone carried in the mouth. Three hundred reliquaries burned somewhere below in the vaults, freed gifts streaming home across the kingdom like birds remembering their nests, and every one of them torn loose had made the thing above them stronger and angrier.

The throne room did not look like a throne room anymore.

The king's architects had built it to inspire awe: ribbed vaults soaring into gloom, pillars carved with the faces of dead monarchs, windows of colored glass that should have spilled afternoon light in rivers of red and gold. Instead the light came from everywhere and nowhere, a pale luminescence like moonlight trapped underwater, and the glass windows had gone white and opaque, as if the sky beyond them had been replaced by something else. The pillars' carved faces had softened, run, become suggestion rather than portrait — wax faces, drowned faces. The air tasted of iron and old salt and, beneath it all, faintly and horribly, of honey.

And there upon the dais sat the crown.

Not the king. Not yet. The throne itself was half-consumed, its oak gone gray and fibrous like driftwood, and rising out of the seat of it, coiling up through the armrests, through the floor, through the very stone of the dais, was the entity — vast beyond the room's ability to contain it, folded impossibly into the space like a hand crammed into a glove too small for it. Zephyrine had seen it once before, at the rite in the capital square, seen it with eyes only he possessed: a shape beneath shapes, a great slow serpent of pale light and darker intention, wearing the crown like a hermit crab wears a stolen shell.

Now it was not coiled beneath anything. It filled the upper air like weather. And it was feeding.

Below the dais, bound to the throne by bands of the same gray fibrous growth, the heir of Veyra screamed.

Zephyrine had met the boy only once, weeks ago, in a corridor — a solemn child of eleven with his father's tired eyes, who had bowed to Zephyrine without knowing what Zephyrine was. Now the boy hung in the entity's attention like a moth pinned to velvet, and where the pale light touched him, Zephyrine could see the child's own small gift — a flicker of warmth, a hearth-gift, nothing dangerous, the kind of gift that made bread rise and fevers break — being drawn out of him thread by thread, the way a fisherman draws gut from a hook.

"He can't hold," Maren said beside him. Her voice was steady but her hands were not; she had her knife drawn and Zephyrine knew, with an ache that felt older than love, that she was holding it not because it would help but because holding something was the only way she knew how to stand in a place like this. "Zeph. He's going to take the boy first, and then he won't need us at all."

"He needs me." Zephyrine heard his own voice come out quiet and level, and marveled distantly at it. His whole body wanted to shake. He let it want. "He's always needed me. The binding is a knot, and I'm the last finger that ever tied it."

* * *

They had argued about this. Of course they had. In the antechamber, while the palace shook around them and Sereth lay dying, Maren had gripped his collar with both hands and told him every reason it would not work, and every reason she would not survive it if it did.

"There has to be another way," she'd said. "There's always another way. You taught me that — you healed that farmer's blight when you thought I wasn't looking, you found the census house, you found the king. You find ways, Zephyrine. That's what you *do.*"

"And this is the way I found." He had covered her hands with his own, felt them trembling against his collarbone. "It offered my grandmother terms once. It offers everyone terms. That's how it feeds — it never takes what isn't given. Even the harvests, even the children — there's a consent woven into the census law, some clause nobody reads, some signature extracted under fear. Fear-signatures. Coerced gifts. They sustain it thin, like starvation rations." He had watched her understand arrive slowly, dreadfully, the way frost crosses a window. "But a gift freely given, wholly given, by the binder's own blood — that it cannot refuse. Grandmother said so. The knot was tied with a freely given gift, and so it must be untied with one. It's not just that it wants my gift, Maren. It's that it is *obligated* to accept it. The same law that lets it eat the kingdom makes it open its mouth for me."

"Open its mouth for you," she repeated, flat. "That's your comfort? That it will swallow you politely?"

"That it will have to open wide enough for everything else to get out."

That was the part he had not said aloud until now, standing before the dais, watching the heir's small flame gutter. Because the truth of the unbinding was not sacrifice alone — anyone could die nobly; the kingdom produced martyrs the way wet wood produced smoke, plentifully and uselessly. The truth was stranger and more terrible than death. To give a gift freely meant giving *all* of it, down to the root, down to the place where the Vasquez line had knotted its power into the world four hundred years ago. And when the knot opened — when the entity loosened its grip on the crown to take what it could not refuse — the knot would pull through him like a rope through a ring, dragging everything it had held with it.

The entity. The harvested gifts. The king's long captivity. All of it, out through him.

No one had ever done it before. No one had ever been both vessel and doorway. His grandmother had spent sixty years preparing him for a role she could only describe in fragments, and in all those years, in all her letters, she had never once promised he would live.

She had never once promised he wouldn't, either. That absence had kept him walking.

* * *

He stepped forward.

Maren did not follow. That was their agreement — the only part of it she had not fought, because fighting it had broken something in her voice, and he had kissed her forehead in the ash-smelling dark of the antechamber and said, *You hold the door. Whatever happens in there, you hold the door, because if the knot pulls through me and there's nothing anchoring the other end—*

*I know how knots work,* she had said, and laughed, terribly, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand. *My whole life is knots.*

So she stayed at the threshold, knife in hand, and became his anchor, and he crossed the ruined throne room alone.

The light changed as he walked. It had been diffuse, directionless, the flat glow of a thing too large to cast shadows. Now it gathered, leaned, focused — and Zephyrine felt himself become visible in a way he had never been in his life. All those years in the valley, hiding a flicker beneath salt-crusted palms; all those months on the road, shielding, smothering, lying to Maren, lying to Sereth, lying to himself. Every concealment stripped away in a single breath. The entity looked at him — not with eyes, because it had none, but with the whole of its vast distributed attention, the way winter looks at a traveler — and Zephyrine felt that gaze the way one feels a hand pressed flat against the chest: intimate, weighing, cold.

***YOU,*** it said, and the word did not come through his ears. It arrived in the marrow of him, in the salt-locked places where his grandmother's gift lived coiled around his bones. ***THE KNOT COMES TO THE KNIFE.***

"I come to the knot," Zephyrine said aloud, because speaking aloud mattered here, in this room built by human hands for human voices. "You've worn that crown for four hundred years. Do you remember whose head it was made for?"

The light rippled. Something moved in the depths of it, vast and slow, and for a moment Zephyrine glimpsed the shape beneath the shape — not a serpent, not a god, but something older and lonelier than either, a hunger that had forgotten it was ever anything else.

***I REMEMBER NOTHING,*** it said. ***I REMEMBER ONLY THE OPENING OF THROATS.***

"Liar." He kept walking. The dais steps rose before him, and each step was harder than the last, not because of any force opposing him but because the air thickened with the entity's attention, pressing on him like deep water. "You remember everything. That's your punishment, isn't it? That's what the binding does. You wear a man's crown and you feel every year of a man's life pass through you, and you feel every gift you swallow go out like a candle, and you remember all of it because the knot holds you *present*, holds you *here*, in this kingdom, in this century, in this suffering you made. My grandmother didn't cage you to starve you. She caged you to make you *witness*."

The pressure spiked. Somewhere in the vaults far below, glass burst — he felt it through the floor, a shudder like a struck bell — and the entity flared brighter, drunk on whatever it had torn free in its panic. The heir's screaming climbed a note.

***AND WHAT DO YOU WITNESS, LITTLE BINDER?*** The cold attention slid over him again, and this time Zephyrine felt it find what it sought: the inheritance coiled at his center, four hundred years of Vasquez power folded small, hidden since birth, made whole again only days ago when his grandmother returned to him the fragment she had hidden in the valley's salt. ***A GIFT THAT COULD FEED ME FOR AN AGE. A GIFT OFFERED—***

"Freely."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

Zephyrine stood at the foot of the dais, close enough now to see the king — the real king, the man, slumped in what remained of the throne, gray fiber grown through his flesh like roots through soil, his eyes closed, his breathing the shallow mechanical rhythm of a body long since abandoned by its mind. Four hundred years, the histories said. No king had ruled past his fortieth year in living memory, because the entity consumed them and wore them and moved to the heir, and the heir, and the heir. This one had lasted longer than most. Lucid, sometimes. Begging, once, in a private hour that Zephyrine had never told Maren about: *Get it out of me. Whatever it costs you. I am so tired of watching what it does with my hands.*

"You heard me," Zephyrine said. "Freely given. Wholly given. By the binder's blood, from the root. Those are your terms — *your* terms, the ones woven into the oldest law, the ones even you cannot break, because you are not above the law, you are *made of* it, you are a hunger that learned to dress itself in contracts." He began to climb the steps. "You cannot refuse me. And when you open yourself to take what I offer, the knot comes undone, and everything you've swallowed goes home."

***THE KNOT WILL PULL THROUGH YOU.***

"Yes."

***YOU WILL BE HOLLOW. OR YOU WILL BE NOTHING.***

"Yes."

***AND FOR THIS YOU TRADE—*** and here, for the first time, the vast voice faltered, or seemed to, the way a machine falters, a rhythm interrupted — ***—WHAT? WHAT IS THE PAYMENT'S NAME?***

Zephyrine reached the top of the dais. Up close, the entity was worse than terror; it was pitiable, the way a trap is pitiable, the way a mouth is pitiable that has forgotten it was once a face. He thought of the valley. Of salt falling like snow on Founding mornings, of his grandmother's hands teaching his smaller ones the binding signs, of Maren asleep against a fencepost on the Road of Ash Bells with her knife still in her grip. He thought of strangers. Of the farmer whose blight he'd healed in secret. Of three hundred bottled flames burning free tonight across the dark countryside, finding their owners' beds and hearths and hands.

He loved them. That was the whole of it. That was the entire terrible arithmetic. Not duty — duty was the guild's word, the valley's word, the word that had sent a boy out as bait. Love. For people he would never meet, whose names he would never know, whose gifts were even now flying home like exhausted birds. If his grandmother's generation had bound the entity with a gift given freely out of fear of what it might do, then he would bind its ending with a gift given freely out of love for what it had already taken.

"The payment's name," Zephyrine said, "is theirs."

And he opened his hands, and gave.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the horror of it, later — that the beginning of the ending felt like coming home.

His gift rose out of him the way heat leaves a cup of tea into cold air: gently, totally, without resistance. Four hundred years of inherited power uncoiled from his bones and stood up inside the throne room, and it was *vast*, larger than he had ever guessed, larger than the valley, larger than his whole understanding of himself, because he had never been allowed to unfold it fully and now, surrendering it, he finally saw its size. Salt-light. His grandmother's fragment and his own birthright braided together, the original knot's living thread, and it streamed toward the entity like a river finding the sea.

The entity opened.

There was no other word for it. The pale vastness dilated around the offering, drew it in, drank — and in drinking, obeyed the oldest term of its existence: to receive a freely given gift, wholly, it had to open the whole of itself. Every seal it had made on itself in four centuries of cautious feeding. Every internal lock. The knot of the binding, which was not a physical thing but a *term*, a condition written into what it was — *you may take only what is given; you may keep only what is coerced* — reversed its polarity in the moment of true gift.

Everything given could leave.

Zephyrine felt it happen through his own chest, because the knot pulled through him exactly as promised — a rope through a ring, and he was the ring, and the rope was everything. The entity convulsed. The light that filled the throne room turned inside out. And the harvested gifts — hundreds of them, thousands, four centuries of stolen fire — began to tear loose and stream *outward*, through the opening the entity could not close, through the hollowing boy standing on the dias with his empty hands still raised, out through the white windows and down through the floors, homeward.

The entity shrieked. It was not a sound. It was a subtraction — every candle in the world guttering at once — and Zephyrine, hollowing, held on.

Because that was the last term, the one his grandmother had never spoken aloud but had built into his marrow anyway: the ring must hold until the rope is through. If he collapsed, if his will broke, if he flinched — the knot would snap back, the entity would seize the opening and close it, and it would keep him. Forever. A freely given gift, half-delivered, was a door nailed shut from the inside.

He held.

He held through the tearing-out of the heir's stolen hearth-flame, which flew from the entity's grip and dove into the boy's chest like a returning swallow; the screaming stopped, and the gray fibers binding the child grayed further, loosened, died. He held through the entity's thrashing fury, through the dais cracking beneath his feet, through the sensation of his own name being pulled out of him along with everything else, thread by luminous thread. He held because Maren was holding the door below, because Sereth had bought the doorway with her life, because the elder had died with the vault bells ringing, because his grandmother had spent sixty years believing he could do this, and belief like that was not a weight. It was a hand.

And then — in the last of it, when the entity had thinned to a scream and a memory, when the light was mostly gone and the throne room was only a room again with ordinary darkness pooling in its corners — a voice spoke through the ruin. Human. Ragged. *Alive.*

"Boy."

Zephyrine turned. On the ruined throne, the gray fibers were sloughing off the king like snow from a branch, and the man beneath them was old — impossibly old, older than any king should be, a face like a map of every road his body had never walked — and his eyes were open. Clear. His own.

"The knot," the king rasped. His hands, shaking, found the air in front of his chest, and Zephyrine saw with his fading sight that the final strand of the binding ran from the entity's dissolving bulk straight through the king's heart — the host, the anchor, the first throat it had ever opened. "It runs through me. It will not finish while—" A cough, wet and awful. "While I hold it shut. So I will hold it. Boy. Binder's blood. Hold your hands up and think of them. Think of the strangers. I can feel what you fed it—" wonder cracked his ruined voice— "*love*, you fed it love, no one ever thought to feed it that—"

"It will kill you," Zephyrine whispered. His voice sounded far away to himself, thin, emptied. "Holding it open."

"It killed me four hundred years ago," the king said, and smiled, and it was a young man's smile on an ancient face. "Everything since has been paperwork. *Hold.*"

And the king of Veyra reached up with both hands and took hold of the last strand of the ancient binding, the knot that his crown had been built to conceal, and held it open with his mortal hands while the last of the entity poured through the ring of Zephyrine's hollowed body and out into the world it had been exiled from.

Zephyrine felt the moment it finished. There was no thunder. The great hunger passed through him like wind through an open window, taking everything, leaving the frame, and the silence that followed was so complete that he could hear, absurdly, distantly, from somewhere below the palace, Maren's voice calling his name.

The king's hands fell. His head tipped back. But his eyes stayed fixed on Zephyrine, and they were grateful, and they were human, and his lips moved one last time:

"Tell them I was *in* here. Tell them I tried."

Then the crown — the actual crown, the iron circlet that had sat on royal heads for four centuries — split with a sound like ice on a thawing lake, and rusted to flakes before it finished falling, and the King of Veyra closed his eyes at last with nothing wearing him at all.

Zephyrine stood alone on the broken dais, in a dark ordinary room, with empty hands and an emptier chest, listening to footsteps on the stairs — fast ones, frantic, Maren's voice getting closer, calling and calling his name —

and found that he could not remember how to answer her.

He opened his mouth. Nothing came. Somewhere very far away, the first freed gift found its owner, and a stranger in a village he would never see lit a candle with a touch, and wept without knowing why.

Zephyrine swayed. The dais tilted. And the last thing he saw, before the dark took the empty place where his gift used to live, was the door bursting open and Maren framed in it, knife still in hand, running toward him across a room that was, at last, only a room.
