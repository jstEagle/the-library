## 20. The Empty Gift

The infirmary of the royal palace smelled of boiled linen and honey-thick light.

Zephyrine had been counting the smells the way other men counted days. Boiled linen. Beeswax on the floorboards, laid down generations ago and never replaced. The faint iron tang of the river through the open shutters. And underneath it all, so faint he might have imagined it — salt. Brought in on his own clothes, his own skin, ground into the bandages by the healers who had not known what it was and had not asked. The valley had followed him here after all, clinging like a burr to everything that came out of it.

He lay in a bed too large for him, under sheets embroidered with a crest that no longer meant anything, and he tried to remember how to be a person with edges.

The gift was gone.

Not dimmed, not sleeping, not waiting behind some door in him for a better season. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from the jaw: the absence had its own shape, its own ache, its own weather. He kept reaching for it out of habit the way a tongue worries a gap. When he touched the stone sill of the window, nothing rose up to meet him. No flicker of inherited fire, no whisper of his grandmother's warmth moving through the salt-crusted rock. Just cold stone. Just a young man's hand on a windowsill in a city he did not love, holding nothing at all.

He should have felt triumphant. Maren had told him he would. *You'll wake up light*, she'd said, pressing her forehead to his the night before the Long Walk Up, when they were both pretending to believe there would be a morning after. *You'll have done the one thing no one else could do, and you'll finally get to just be Zephyrine.*

She had not been wrong about the first part. She could not have known about the second.

Because the truth was uglier than she knew, and he had never found a way to say it aloud without sounding ungrateful for his own survival: he did not know who Zephyrine was. He had never met him. For nineteen years he had been the youngest of the guild, the last leaf on a dying branch, the boy whose hands warmed when they touched white stone — and beneath that, deeper, secret even from himself until the letters told him, the final knot of an ancient binding, the reason a valley full of hidden people had hidden *him* most carefully of all. Every name anyone had ever given him had been a name with magic folded inside it like a letter in an envelope. Take the magic out and the envelope sat empty on the table, and no one, least of all Zephyrine, knew what had actually been written there.

A healer came and went. He let her unwrap his hands, clean the burns where the unbinding had passed through him on its way out of the world, rewrap them. She said words like *remarkable* and *the king's physicians will want to study*. He nodded. Somewhere below, in the wrecked throne room, men were still sweeping up rust that had once been a crown, and no one could explain why the rust kept weeping a thin red stain into the marble no scrubbing could lift. Let them study that instead, he thought. Let them study something that still existed.

When she left, he turned his face to the window.

Veyra City spread out below him in the long gold light of late afternoon, smoke rising straight from a thousand chimneys because the wind had died, bells ringing from the low tower of the Saltmarket in a pattern he was learning to read as ordinary. Not alarm. Not mourning. Just the hour, and the hour after it, time going on in a kingdom that had woken three days ago from a hundred-year nightmare and did not yet trust its own dreams.

And across that kingdom, small miracles were happening that he would never see.

He knew about them anyway, the way you know about rain falling in a place you've left — reports came up to the infirmary hourly, carried by clerks and captains and one very persistent archivist who had appointed herself historian of the Unbinding and took her self-appointment with terrifying seriousness. Her name was Odile. She read to him every evening from her ledger, and though he had never asked her to, he had learned to need it.

*A cooper in Harrow Fen woke with his mother's gift of mending wood,* she had read last night, her voice dry as the paper she loved. *His mother died in the first census taking, eleven years ago. The cooper is forty-six years old. He has not spoken since it returned; his neighbors say he sits with his hands on the table turning them over and over, as if checking they are his.*

*A girl of nine in the Ash Bells country received back the gift of finding water. It had belonged to a woman named Tessaly, taken at a census house the girl's village burned rather than surrender her. The girl has never met Tessaly. She walked straight to the dried well behind her house and called water up from a depth no diviner had ever reached, and the village has declared a feast day.*

*A soldier on the northern wall woke with a gift of quieting fear. He had been given it, unbeknownst to him, by a deserter he once refused to turn in. The reliquary bearing that label cracked open on the third night, and the soldier dreamed of a woman thanking him.*

Three hundred bottles in the vaults, saved at the cost of Elder Sefa's life. And now empty, every one, their contents gone home across the whole breadth of the kingdom like birds released, seeking — Odile swore this was true, she had checked it against eleven cases — not their nearest owners but their *rightful* ones. Gifts returning to bloodlines, to debtors, to people who had once, briefly, unknowingly, held them. As if the magic itself remembered whom it had loved.

Zephyrine lay in his enormous bed and listened to these reports and felt something move in him that was almost joy and mostly grief, and understood at last that the two things were not opposites but neighbors, sharing a wall, passing things back and forth through the cracks.

Every gift that flew home was proof the unbinding had worked. Every gift that flew home was also a small funeral for the world he had grown up believing in — the world where magic had to be hidden in salt, where the gifted were hunted, where a valley forgot its own children to keep them safe. That world was ending not with a battle but with a ledger of homecomings. It deserved better than to end quietly. It deserved someone to sit up and mourn it properly.

So he mourned it. Alone, in the gold light, with his bandaged hands folded on the coverlet like a man at his own vigil.

* * *

Maren came at dusk, as she always did, smelling of the outside — horse and road dust and, lately, ink, because she had spent two days in the census archives doing something she refused to describe and which Odile described for her anyway, at length, with footnotes.

She dropped into the chair beside his bed the way she did everything, as if the chair should be grateful, and put her boots up on the frame.

"Odile says you didn't eat."

"Odile says a lot of things."

"She made me memorize three of them before I was allowed past the door." Maren produced an apple from somewhere in her coat, polished it on her sleeve, and held it out. "Eat, or I'm legally obligated to report you to the historians."

He took it. He ate it slowly while she watched him with that look she had, the flat dark assessing stare that had once sized him up as a captive worth rescuing and now, apparently, sized him up as something harder to classify.

"You did it again," she said finally.

"Did what."

"The thing where you lie in here performing your own death so nobody asks you to live." She said it without cruelty. That was the strange, disorienting mercy of Maren — she could say the worst true thing in a voice as level as a carpenter's rule. "I've watched you do it for three days. I let you have two of them. That was generous of me. You're welcome."

"Maren—"

"No. Listen." She swung her feet down and leaned forward, forearms on her knees, and for a moment the lamplight caught the old scar along her jaw, the one she'd gotten stealing her first gift at fourteen, from a hunter who had been hunting her. "I know what you lost. Don't make the mistake of thinking I don't, because I don't carry one myself — I know you think that. But I stole mine, Zeph. I know exactly what it is to have someone else's magic sitting in your chest wearing your name. I've spent ten years trying to figure out if any part of me is actually *me* or just the residue of a dead woman's fire." Her voice stayed level. Her knuckles did not. "So when I tell you the hollow goes away, or rather — it doesn't go away, but you grow around it, like a tree grows around a fence post — I am not comforting you. I am reporting from the far side of a border you haven't crossed yet."

He looked down at his bandaged hands. The burns were healing strangely, the healers said — cleanly, quickly, as if the skin remembered how to mend without help. His grandmother would have had a theory about that. His grandmother was downstairs, arguing with the Crown Steward about the disposition of the reliquary vaults, because grief had never once in ninety years slowed Sefa Vasquez's talent for logistics.

"I keep reaching for it," he admitted. The words came out smaller than he wanted. "Not even to use. Just — to check it's there. Like touching a bruise. Except the bruise is the only part of me that ever felt finished."

"I know."

"And everyone keeps saying I should be proud. The king died human and grateful. The heir is alive. Three hundred people got their inheritance back. The hunts are ended, the decree burned, Sereth—" his throat closed on the courier's name, on the memory of her standing in the ruined doorway with the entity's attention pouring off her like heat off a forge, buying them eleven seconds with her life, eleven seconds that had been enough. "Sereth paid for it. Everyone paid for it except me, apparently, and I can't even feel glad the way glad people are supposed to feel glad—"

"Stop." Maren's hand covered his bandaged ones, careful of the burns, and her voice finally lost its carpenter's edge. "You gave it away freely. Do you understand what that means? Not sacrificed, not taken, not spent in battle. *Given.* Freely. To strangers. To a kingdom that was hunting you six weeks ago." Her thumb moved once across his knuckles. "Grief isn't ingratitude, Zeph. Grief is the receipt. It's proof the thing was real and you loved it. Anyone who tells you to skip straight to pride is trying to rob you of the mourning, and the mourning is yours. Keep it. But keep it *in here*—" she tapped his chest, gently, above the hollow, "—not lying in this bed pretending to be a ghost."

He laughed. It surprised him, coming up rough and wet, half sob, and Maren pretended very hard not to notice, studying the ceiling with tremendous interest until he had himself back.

"You're terrible," he said.

"I'm a gift-thief," she said. "It's in the job description." Then, quieter, looking at him sideways: "Your grandmother sent word up. She wants you tomorrow. Both of us. She says the guild voted."

"The guild — what's left of it? Eleven people?"

"Twelve. They counted you." Something in her face went careful, the way it did when she was afraid of giving him hope in case it broke in his hands. "They voted on something. She wouldn't say what over the message. She said—" Maren hesitated, and then recited, in a fair imitation of Sefa's dry gravel: "*Tell my grandson the valley has been having thoughts. Tell him the salt has always known more than we did, and it appears we were rude not to ask sooner.*"

Outside, the last light drained off the city, and the bells rang the hour, and somewhere across the darkening kingdom another bottle cracked open, another stranger woke weeping with a dead relative's magic singing in their blood, another small piece of the stolen world went home.

Zephyrine looked at his empty hands, and then at the girl who had taught him the outside world, and felt the grief shift its weight — not lighter, but differently balanced, the way a pack sits right when someone helps you settle the straps.

"All right," he said. "Tomorrow, then."

But sleep, when it finally took him near midnight, brought a dream he would not tell anyone for a long time: he stood at the boundary of the valley where he had collapsed on the day he left, and the flats stretched white to the horizon, and the salt was falling upward — rising off the ground in slow spirals, unhurried, like snow deciding to return to the sky. And in the dream he understood that it was not leaving. It was making room.
