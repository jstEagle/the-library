## 21. Homecoming, Rewritten

### Zephyrine

The flats had changed color.

Zephyrine noticed it before anything else — the white that had been a wall his whole life was now merely a road. Where once the salt had shimmered with a hard, exclusionary light, a light that said *no* to everything beyond the valley's rim, it now lay soft and pearly under the morning sun, streaked here and there with faint veins of blue where the meltwater ran in spring channels. It looked, he thought, like something waiting to be written on.

He stood at the boundary stone with Maren a half-step behind him and felt the strangeness of it settle into his bones. A year ago he had crossed this line for the first time, dragged by a royal decree and a guild's desperate vote, and the crossing had nearly unmade him — his gift roaring awake like a dam breaking, every sense flooding at once until he collapsed on the far shore and had to be carried. He had thought then that leaving home meant dying a little.

Now the salt parted around them like water around a prow.

"Feel that?" Maren asked quietly. She crouched, pressed her palm flat against the crust, and lifted it glistening. "It knows you."

"It always knew me." He knelt beside her. The salt was warm against his hollowed-out hands — hands that no longer carried the flicker of inherited magic, that could not heal blight or shield a mind or do any of the thousand small things the gift had done without his asking. But the salt rose to meet him anyway, a faint pressure against his skin, like a cat butting its head against a familiar knee. "It just couldn't say so before."

The valley had been built to hide him — he understood that now, had understood it since his grandmother's letters were decoded in a rented room above a census house. The Vasquez binding needed a knot, and the knot needed a cradle, and so the guild had folded itself around one boy for twenty years and called it sanctuary. The valley's silence toward all things gifted had been less a protection than an amnesia, a spell of forgetting laid over the flats so that nothing — courier, hunter, entity — could find what slept at the center.

And when the crown crumbled to rust on a dead king's brow, the forgetting had ended too. Not with a roar. With a thaw.

"You're doing the thing," Maren said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at salt like it's going to apologize." She stood, brushing white dust from her knees, and offered him her hand. Her stolen gift — a thief's gift, taken from a girl who hadn't survived to claim it back — moved under her skin like weather. She'd stopped apologizing for it months ago; the freed reliquaries had gone home across the kingdom, but hers had never had an owner to return to, only her. "Come on. If we stand here philosophizing, your grandmother will come out and drag us both in by our ears."

"She would, too."

"She threatened to, last letter."

He took her hand. Together they stepped over the boundary stone — no resistance, no roar, just the small ordinary effort of walking — and the valley opened ahead of them: green terraces stitched down the mountainsides, the white rooftops of the guild quarter, the bell tower with its rope worn smooth by four generations of hands. Smoke rose from the kitchens. Somewhere below, children were shouting.

His chest did something complicated. He let it.

They walked down through the terraces as the sun climbed, and people came out to meet them — not the wary, grief-thinned remnant who had voted to send their youngest away as bait, but a crowd. Farmers with salt stains on their sleeves. Old Ivetta, who had buried two sons in the hunt and still smiled like sunrise. A cluster of children who broke from the group and ran alongside, demanding to know if the stories were true, if the crown had really been made of teeth, if he had really talked to a god and won.

"I didn't win," Zephyrine told them, because it was true and because they deserved truth more than legend. "I gave something away. That's different."

"But you're alive," said a small girl with a gap in her teeth.

"That part worked out," he admitted.

Maren laughed behind him, and the sound carried over the terraces, and he realized with a jolt that he was *glad* — plainly, simply glad, in a body that had spent a year learning fear. The hollow where his gift had lived ached sometimes, especially at night, especially when he touched salt-crusted stone and felt nothing rise to answer. But this morning, walking home through a crowd that had come out to meet him not because he was the Vasquez knot but because he was Zephyrine, the ache was quiet.

His grandmother waited at the gate of the guildhouse, small and straight-backed, her silver hair braided in the old rite-pattern she had resumed wearing since the world changed. She did not hug him. She never had. Instead she took both his hands in hers, turned them palm-up, and studied the empty lines as though reading a book she already knew by heart.

"No regrets?" she asked. It was the same question she had asked in the infirmary, and the month after, and the month after that.

"Same answer, Grandmother."

"Good." She released him and turned to Maren, and something passed between the two women — some assessment concluded long ago, renewed without ceremony. "Both of you are late. The council convened an hour ago, and I have spent that hour preventing Aldric from drafting a charter nobody has agreed to yet."

"Aldric drafted a *charter?*" Maren said.

"Aldric has drafted six." His grandmother's mouth twitched, which for her was laughter. "Come inside. History is being argued about, and you should both be in the room for it."

* * *

### Maren

The council chamber smelled of beeswax and salt and, underneath it, ink — a smell Maren had learned to associate with the guild's slow transformation from hiding place to something else entirely. The long table that had seated thirteen elders now sat barely nine, several chairs standing empty since the raid on the vaults, since Elder Corvane's death among the glass bells. But the room was fuller than its chairs: young people crowded along the walls, the ones who had crossed the flats in the weeks after the crown fell, drawn by rumor and by the simple fact that the valley finally answered when called.

Aldric stood at the head of the table with six papers fanned before him like a card sharp.

"The question," he said, "is what we are *for.*"

Maren had sat in enough war councils in the past year to recognize when a room held its breath. This one did. Even the children at the windows — shooed inside, given bread, refusing to leave — went quiet.

"We were founded as a refuge," Aldric continued. "A place for the Unmarked to survive a kingdom that burned what it feared. For two hundred years, that was enough. It isn't anymore. The kingdom knows we exist. The flats let the gifted pass both ways now — we've confirmed it eleven times. And there are—" he consulted the topmost paper, "—four hundred and twelve freed gifts currently wandering the realm looking for owners, plus the ones whose owners are dead or scattered. Someone has to teach those people what they carry. Someone has to teach everyone else not to be afraid of it." He set the papers down. "I propose we stop being a refuge and become a school."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Maren watched faces turn. Old Ivetta, who had lost sons, shaking her head slowly. A young man named Tobin — one of the first to cross after the thaw, carrying a gift of finding lost things — leaning forward with his eyes lit. Grandmother Vasquez at the corner of the table, perfectly still, watching everything.

"A school," Ivetta said flatly. "We open the gates and invite the kingdom in. The same kingdom that took my boys."

"The same kingdom," said Tobin, "whose king died freeing them. Whose heir signed the edict abolishing the census within a month of coronation. Times changed, Ivetta."

"Times change *back.*" Her voice cracked and steadied. "I've seen it change back."

Silence. Then Zephyrine spoke, from where he sat at Maren's side, and Maren heard the careful weight he put into every word.

"My grandmother hid me here for twenty years. Kept me ignorant, kept me unmarked, kept me safe — and when it mattered, sent me out anyway, because a Vasquez raised outside fear could face what one raised inside it never could." He looked at Ivetta gently. "She wasn't wrong. But she also told me once that the valley's protection cost more than it seemed — that every year of forgetting made the world outside stranger and crueller, to us and to itself. Refuge was necessary. Refuge was also a kind of wound." He spread his empty hands. "I don't have a gift anymore. What I have is a year of roads, of burned villages, of a girl who taught me how to walk through a world that hunts people like us. That teaching saved more lives than my hiding ever did. I vote school."

"And if the hunt comes again?" Ivetta demanded. "If the next king decides gifts are harvest?"

"Then it will find us *teaching,*" said Grandmother Vasquez, and her voice filled the chamber the way it must have fifty years ago, "with four hundred trained gifts and a valley that no longer forgets. Let me tell you what I know about walls, Ivetta. I helped build this one. Walls keep things out exactly as long as it suits the wall, and not one day longer. Better to be a door. Doors can choose whom to admit — and can close."

The vote, when Aldric finally called it, was not unanimous. Nothing honest ever was. Seven for, two against, and Ivetta left the chamber with her chin up and her grief intact, pausing at the door to say, "Then teach them well. Teach them what my sons died of."

"We will," Zephyrine said. "That's the first lesson."

Maren found herself blinking hard at the table's grain. Belonging, she thought. She had stolen hers, once, from a dead girl's cooling gift — had spent years believing she was a counterfeit person wearing someone else's inheritance. But no one in this room had asked her to justify her presence. No one had checked her provenance. When Aldric read the new charter aloud — *the Guild of the Unmarked, henceforth the Veyran School, open to marked and unmarked alike* — he had included, in the founding paragraph, a line about gifts freely given and gifts freely taken being equally welcome to learn.

Nobody had objected. Nobody had even looked at her.

Home, she decided, was a place that didn't audit you.

### Zephyrine

The first students arrived before the charter ink dried.

They came in twos and threes at first — a fire-gifted boy from the river provinces whose village had finally stopped fearing him; twin sisters sharing one gift of mending between them like a coat; a gray-haired woman who had hidden her gift of hearing the dead for sixty years and wept for three days upon learning she needn't anymore. They came down the salt road, and the flats bore them gently, and Zephyrine met each of them at the boundary stone because he remembered what the crossing had cost him and wanted to be the hand at the far shore.

By autumn there were ninety. By winter, two hundred, and the terraces rang with lessons — shielding in the morning, ethics at midday (Grandmother insisted ethics precede technique, and no one dared argue), practical work in the afternoon, when the fields bloomed under tended gifts and the wells ran sweet. Maren taught evasion and survival, the curriculum of the hunted, and refused to soften a single story. Zephyrine taught the class nobody had planned: the history of the hunt, the census houses, the crown of teeth. He taught it with maps and names and, whenever possible, with survivors telling their own accounts, because Ivetta had been right about that much. The first lesson was what people died of.

On the evening of the Founding Rite's eve — the first Rite to be held as a school rather than a refuge — Zephyrine climbed the terraces alone to the boundary stone, as he did most evenings now, and found Maren already sitting on it.

"Your grandmother's looking for you," she said. "Rite preparations. Apparently you're giving the address."

"I'm giving the *address?*"

"You're the one who unmade a god. Address seems reasonable."

"I gave away a gift and survived. That's not a speech."

"It absolutely is." She slid sideways to make room, and he sat, and for a while they watched the sun go down over the flats — over the road that was no longer a wall, the white expanse now scored with cart tracks and footpaths, dotted with the lanterns of late travelers making for the valley's lights. Salt wind moved through the terraces below, carrying the smell of bread and beeswax and, faintly, of the sea, which the valley had never smelled before and which Maren swore meant the world was healing in ways no spell accounted for.

"Can I tell you something strange?" she said eventually.

"You usually do."

"When I stole my gift — when I was sixteen and starving and took it off a corpse because I thought it was the only way I'd ever matter — I used to dream about this valley. Before I'd ever heard of it. White everywhere, and a door in the middle of nothing." She turned her hand over, watching her borrowed gift move beneath the skin like light under ice. "I thought it was guilt. Now I think maybe the flats were saving me a seat."

Zephyrine considered the salt stretching away to the horizon, patient and pale and no longer keeping anyone out or in. His grandmother had told him once, in the infirmary, that the Vasquez binding had been woven from love — that all the strongest bindings were — and that when he unmade it, whatever love remained in the weave had nowhere to go except outward. Into the flats. Into the thaw.

Maybe the valley had been saving seats for everyone. Maybe that was what a door was.

"There's one more thing," Maren said, and there was something in her voice now, something carefully casual that he had learned to distrust. "A letter came today. From the capital."

"The heir?"

"The heir. Official seal, very polite." She pulled a folded paper from her coat and handed it over. "They want to send students, Zeph. Gifted children from the palace orphanages — the ones the entity got to before it fell, the ones nobody knows what to do with. Forty of them. In the spring."

He read the letter twice. The handwriting was young and earnest. The request was simple: *We have children the kingdom failed. We are told yours is the only house that did not.*

"They'll come in winter carts," he said at last. "Someone should meet them at the stone."

"I volunteer," Maren said. "Obviously."

Below them, the valley glowed with lantern-light, and the bell tower began to ring the eve of the Rite, and the salt wind came up the terraces carrying the future like seeds on the tide. Zephyrine folded the letter into his pocket, next to the space where a gift used to live, and found the space — not full, never full again — but no longer empty either.

"Come on," he said, taking her hand. "History's arguing again, and this time we're supposed to be in it."
