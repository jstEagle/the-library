## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The salt line ran like a scar through the valley's throat, and Ines Hartwell walked it the way other men walked graveyards—slowly, with respect, counting the stones.

There were forty-one of them. Grey-white pillars of crusted mineral, each taller than a man, each set by hands long forgotten at intervals of two hundred paces along the border where the Salt Valley met the world. The old folk said the first sealer-priests had raised them in a single night, singing the ground white. Ines didn't know if that was true. He only knew what every child born between the stones learned before they learned to walk: past this line, the world had teeth. Within it, the world was quiet.

He knelt at the base of the ninth stone and pressed his palm flat against it. The crust was cold even in the late summer morning, cold the way riverbed stones were cold, as though something beneath the surface drank all warmth and gave nothing back. Beneath his hand, through the salt, through the packed earth, he felt nothing at all. No hum. No whisper. The particular silence that was the valley's only miracle.

Thirteen years ago, that same silence had lived inside him, too.

He stood, brushed the salt from his knee, and resumed his patrol. Behind him the stone stood in its row like a tooth in an old jaw, holding the line against whatever waited on the far side of the world.

* * *

The Oathbound made their camp at Tarnholt Ford, where the river Sill came down out of the hills fat with meltwater and slow enough to drink. Twenty-two tents in a double ring around the cookfire, banners furled because Brannoc said a mercenary company that advertised itself was a company already half-defeated. By the time Ines came down off the ridge, the smell of barley porridge and woodsmoke had settled over everything, familiar as his own heartbeat.

"Border's quiet," he reported, dropping onto the bench beside Dree, who was mending a boot sole with waxed thread and her knife held in her teeth.

"When isn't it?" she said around the knife. She spat the blade into her palm and squinted up at him. "Eleven years you've walked that line, Hartwell. You could do it asleep."

"I have done it asleep."

"I know. I've seen you." She went back to her stitching. "That's the part that worries me."

Ines took the bowl Maren handed him without looking, the way one takes bread in church, and ate standing, watching the ford. The water ran brown-green over the shallows. On the far bank, a carter was arguing with his oxen in the resigned tone of a man who had lost this argument many times before and would lose it again tomorrow. Ordinary morning. Ordinary valley. The kind of day that made eleven years of vigilance feel like either wisdom or waste, depending on how honest he was willing to be with himself before noon.

Brannoc emerged from the command tent rolling up a map. Their captain filled a doorway the way weather fills a sky—not tall so much as present, grey at the temples now, a scar through his beard from a Halveth pike fifteen years gone. He looked at Ines the way he always did: a beat longer than necessary, something unreadable passing behind his eyes, then gone.

"Escort today," Brannoc said. "Crown rider coming down the King's Road from the pass. Herald, by the advance word. We take him to Ashford village, see him posted, see him fed, send him on. Vess's mercy, keep it dull."

"Dull is the whole religion out here," Dree said.

"It is," Brannoc said, and for just a moment his gaze found Ines again, held, released. "Which is why we defend it."

Ines felt the words settle somewhere under his ribs, where the oath-brand sat. He touched it without meaning to—a habit he despised in himself—the small sigil burned into the skin below his collarbone when he was nine years old, in the shrine above the salt flats, in the year the god stopped speaking. It was a spiral closed upon itself. A mouth, shut. He had never shown it to anyone in the company but Brannoc, and Brannoc had never once asked what it meant, which was its own kind of mercy, or its own kind of knowledge.

They saddled up within the hour: Ines, Dree, young Pell who still rode like a sack of meal, and Brannoc himself, which was unusual, which Ines noted and chose not to examine. Four riders escorting one herald down a road that hadn't seen trouble since the flood two springs back. Routine. The valley forgetting itself, as it did every day, as it had been built to do.

Ines let his horse find the pace and thought about the silence.

Not the valley's silence—the other one. The one that lived in him the way a splinter lives in flesh, worked deep, grown over. Thirteen years ago he had been a child kneeling on cold stone while the war of gods and men tore the world apart beyond the salt line, and someone—his mother? the priestess? he could no longer be sure, memory being a thing the valley eroded like rain erodes chalk—had pressed his hand to the brand and taught him the binding words. Words he had spoken once, in a voice too small to understand what it was doing. Words he could not now recall, though sometimes, at the edge of sleep, he felt them sitting in his throat like swallowed stones.

Afterward, everywhere beyond the valley, the god Vess had gone quiet. Coincidence, said the valley folk. Exhaustion, said the scholars. Grief, said the priests who still tended empty shrines in the outer lands, lighting candles for a god who no longer answered.

Ines said nothing. He had been saying nothing for thirteen years. Silence, he had learned, was not emptiness. Silence was a weight you carried, and after enough years you forgot it was there until you tried to sing, or pray, or speak certain names—and then you felt the whole shape of it, the way a man feels a missing tooth only when his tongue goes searching.

"You're doing the face," Dree said beside him.

"What face?"

"The one where you're talking to your god and he's not answering."

"He never answers."

"Then maybe stop asking." But she said it gently, the way she said most things that mattered, and nudged her horse closer so their stirrups nearly touched. "Whatever it is you carry, Hartwell, it's carried you this far. Let it rest today. It's a herald, not a harbinger."

Ines almost smiled. "You've been reading Brannoc's sermons."

"Somebody has to." She kicked her horse forward toward the front of the column, and Ines watched her go, and felt, as he often did, the strange doubled grief of loving men and women whose deaths he had sworn to prevent and knowing that love was the one contract the Oathbound never wrote down.

* * *

They met the crown rider at midday where the King's Road crossed the old pilgrimage track, under a leaning rowan tree that the salt had killed years ago but nobody had the heart to fell. The herald was younger than Ines expected—young enough that the war would have been a story told to him rather than a thing survived—with a courier's lean frame and a satchel stamped with the crowned tower of House Aldmere. His livery was immaculate. His eyes, when they swept the four mercenaries, were not the eyes of a boy. They were the eyes of a man who had counted things lately. Bad things. Many.

"Captain." He inclined his head to Brannoc, then read the company's badge off Brannoc's shoulder with a professional's fluency. "Oathbound. Valley charter, salt-line garrison. Your reputation precedes you. It is, I'm told, unusually clean."

"We try," Brannoc said.

"You'll want to hear what I'm carrying, then. All of you." And there was something in his voice—not urgency, exactly. A careful, rehearsed steadiness, the voice of a man delivering news he had delivered six times already and would deliver sixty more, and each time hoped the reaction would be smaller than the last. "By decree of the Crown, sealed under the regent's hand and countersigned by the Office of Inquiry: all persons possessing unregistered gifts of craft or nature are hereby required to present themselves at any royal seat for registration within ninety days of this edict's posting."

Dree shifted in her saddle. Small motion. Ines caught it anyway.

"And those who don't?" Pell asked, because Pell was young and hadn't yet learned which questions cost.

The herald looked at him. Just looked. Then he swung down from his horse and unrolled the posting board from his saddlebag—a slate panel, government-issued, the edict already lettered across it in black ink gone slightly blue at the edges where the paint was thin. He propped it against the dead rowan and stepped back, and the four Oathbound sat their horses and read.

All gifted persons to be registered or burned.

The words themselves were blunter than the herald's speech. Someone in some chancellery had decided the population needed plain language. Below the decree ran the seal of the Office of Inquiry, and beneath the seal, in smaller letters, a list: hedge-craft, blood-reading, weather-speech, growth-song, storm-binding, dream-walking, and seventeen other names for ways human beings had always been different from one another, now enumerated like crimes in a ledger.

"Growth-song," Ines read aloud, quietly. He didn't know why that one snagged him. Something about the phrase. Something that prickled at the back of his neck like a draft from a door left open in winter.

"A new classification," the herald said. "Recent. There have been... incidents. Children mostly." He gathered the words like a man gathering spilled grain. "The Crown's position is that registration protects the gifted as much as anyone. Documented gifts, lawful practice, crown sanction. Those registered will be safe."

"And the ones who run?" Dree asked.

The herald's careful steadiness flickered. "Those who run," he said, "will be assumed to be concealing something worth running from."

Nobody answered that. The wind came down the valley, moving the dust along the road in slow ropes, and stirred the dead rowan's branches without sound, because dead branches don't rustle—they only rock, silently, like things drowning.

"Where do you post first?" Brannoc asked.

"Ashford. Then the fishing villages down the flats. Then the shrine towns, such as they are." The herald paused. "I'll be frank, Captain, since your charter requires honesty from crown officers and I extend the courtesy back. This is the fourth valley I've posted. In the other three, I watched neighbors look at neighbors differently before I was out the gate. Whatever peace you've got here—" he gestured at the pale line of border stones visible down the valley, small in the haze, "—it's going to be tested. Ninety days. When the moon finishes its thirteenth turn, this stops being a decree and becomes law enforced by the Office of Inquiry. And the Office does not send heralds. It sends inquisitors."

Brannoc's face did nothing at all, which from Brannoc was a shout. "We'll see you posted and provisioned."

"I'd be grateful."

They rode on together, five horses and a slate panel strapped behind the herald's saddle, and Ines kept finding his eyes drawn back to the edict. Registered or burned. Growth-song. Recent incidents. Children, mostly. The wind off the salt flats tasted faintly of iodine and iron, the taste it always had, the taste of home, and for the first time in eleven years of patrols it tasted like a warning.

* * *

Ashford village received the edict the way a body receives cold water: a collective flinch, quickly mastered, then the slow business of pretending nothing had happened. The reeve—a broad, worried woman named Odger with flour on her sleeves—read the slate twice, signed the acknowledgment the herald offered, and posted it on the meeting-house door with nails driven harder than the work required.

The villagers gathered the way villagers do, at a distance that permitted reading without admitting interest. Ines watched them from beside his horse. The miller, whose daughter had once charmed a swarm of locusts into the next county and been quietly praised for it. Two brothers who fished the flats and swore their luck came from a charm their grandmother had whispered into a net-knot, a knot nobody dared untie now. Old Tam, the shepherd, who claimed no gift at all and looked, at that moment, like a man rehearsing the claim.

Neighbors looking at neighbors. The herald hadn't lied.

"You see it too," Brannoc said, coming to stand at Ines's shoulder.

"I see people afraid."

"I see a ledger opening." Brannoc's voice was low, pitched under the crowd's murmur. "And I know how ledgers end. I was at Cael Marsh, Hartwell. So were you. You remember what it looks like when a kingdom decides a category of people is a problem."

Ines remembered. Everyone remembered Cael Marsh, though the valley's forgetting had softened it, worn it smooth, filed it down to a story with the blood rinsed out. Beyond the salt line, where memory kept its edge, they would remember it sharper.

"Ninety days," Ines said.

"Thirteen turns of the moon." Brannoc was silent a moment. "You count them too, then."

It wasn't quite a question. Ines turned to look at his captain, and Brannoc looked back, and for one suspended moment Ines felt the whole submerged architecture of the man's attention—thirty years of soldiering, of watching, of knowing his people down to the grain—and understood that Brannoc had been watching him specifically, patiently, for reasons Ines could not name and did not dare ask after.

"Everyone counts moons," Ines said.

"Not the way you do." Brannoc clapped him once on the shoulder, hard, the way he ended conversations he'd decided to defer. "Come on. The herald wants to make the fishing villages by dark, and we're his escort till the crossroads. Dull duty."

"Dull is the whole religion," Ines said.

But riding out of Ashford, past the meeting house with its fresh-nailed slate, past the miller's daughter watching them from a window with an expression too old for her face, past the fields where everything grew exactly as much as it should and no more—in the valley that forgot, that was the covenant, that was the price, that was the mercy—Ines found his hand had drifted again to the oath-brand below his collarbone, pressing flat against the closed spiral, the shut mouth, as if checking that it still held.

It held. Of course it held. It had held for thirteen years, through the war's ending and the silence and the long gray peace of the salt.

But for the first time, walking the border between the world that forgot and the world that remembered, Ines Hartwell allowed himself to wonder what, precisely, he had bound—and whether the thirteenth turn of the moon, rising somewhere out beyond the hills where magic still breathed, would come to collect what he owed.
