## 2. Thirteen Turns

The herald arrived on a horse too fine for the road, and that was how Ines knew the news would be bad.

He stood at the edge of the market square in Saltmere, the valley's one true town, watching the man dismount with the careful precision of someone who had never once dirtied his boots by accident. The rider's cloak was crown-blue, stitched at the hem with silver thread that caught the flat morning light and threw it back like the edge of a blade. Behind him, two outriders in matching livery sat their horses with the loose boredom of men who had done violence often enough that they no longer needed to rehearse it.

The square filled slowly. Market mornings did not stop for anything in Saltmere—not for weddings, not for funerals, and not, the villagers clearly hoped, for crown business. Fishwives went on haggling over grayling. A cooper argued with his apprentice about staves. But Ines noticed, the way he noticed most things, that the conversations had gone quiet at their centers. People spoke to each other while looking at the herald. Their words had stopped meaning anything.

Brannoc appeared at Ines's shoulder without sound, as he always did. His captain was a head taller and built like something quarried rather than born, but thirteen years of soldiering together had taught Ines that Brannoc's real weight was elsewhere—in the way he could fill a room or empty one, depending on what he decided the room needed.

"Company formation when he's done talking," Brannoc said quietly. "I want us visible. Uniformed. Orderly."

"You think that's wise?"

"I think the crown pays our contract, and I think men who look orderly get treated like allies instead of obstacles." Brannoc's jaw worked, chewing on something unsaid. "Besides. It's just an edict. They post edicts every few years. Paper rots."

Ines said nothing. He had learned long ago that his captain talked himself into decisions the way other men talked themselves out of them.

The herald unrolled a scroll with a snap that carried across the square, and read in a voice trained to reach the back of cathedrals:

*"By decree of the Crown Regent, in the name of order and the protection of the realm: all persons possessing unlicensed gift, herein called the touched, shall present themselves for registration within three turns of the moon. Those registered shall be marked, licensed, and bound to lawful use under supervision of the Crown Office of Inquiry. Those who conceal their gift beyond this term—"*

Here the herald paused. Not from mercy. From theater.

*"—shall be declared abominations, and burned."*

The word *burned* landed in the square like a stone dropped down a well. Ines heard it fall through all that silence, deeper and deeper, and heard no bottom.

A fishwife—Maren Ostrow, who had sold Ines smoked eel every week for eleven years—asked the question everyone was thinking, her voice thin as netting thread. "Burned? For what, m'lord? For healing a cow?"

"For the crime of power held above lawful authority," the herald said, with the pleasant patience of a man reciting doctrine. "Registration is mercy. Concealment is treason against the natural order."

*Against the natural order.* Ines felt the phrase snag on something inside him, a fishhook catching cloth. He thought of the salt flats at dawn, white and endless and utterly silent, where nothing grew and nothing spoke and nothing ever would. The valley's whole existence was a monument to the natural order—the order of things without magic, without gods, without voice. He had chosen it. He had sworn his life into its silence.

So why did the herald's words feel less like protection and more like the first cold wind before a storm?

The herald continued with logistics—registration stations at Grayford and Marrowgate, penalties for harboring, rewards for informing—and then rolled up his scroll and looked out at the crowd with eyes like a ledger's margins. "The moon will complete its thirteenth turn since the god fell silent," he said. "On that night, the hunt becomes law entire. Until then, you have grace. Use it wisely."

*Thirteen turns.*

Ines had been counting those moons longer than anyone in this square. Thirteen years since Vess went silent. Thirteen years since the last battle of the god-war ended not in victory but in absence—since the god of tides and truth simply stopped speaking, mid-word, mid-world, and every temple bell in every land rang once and never again. Thirteen years since a boy knelt in a shrine beneath the border stones and swore an oath that cost him everything he might otherwise have been.

He knew exactly how many turns remained. He had counted them the way a prisoner counts bars.

***

The company formed up in the yard behind the Salted Anchor tavern, forty-two Oathbound in gray wool and oiled leather, spears grounded, faces arranged into the blank competence that was their trade's whole wardrobe. Brannoc walked the line slowly, hands clasped behind his back, and delivered the kind of speech he was famous for—the kind that sounded like weather and meant like scripture.

"You've heard the edict," he began. "Some of you are angry. Some of you are afraid. Both are permitted, so long as neither shows on your face where the crown can see it." A rumble of dry laughter moved down the line. "Here is the truth of our situation. We hold contracts. Contracts are the spine of this company. Without them we're not soldiers, we're brigands with better posture. The crown holds our contract through winter and likely through next harvest. So we comply. We register if asked. We assist the Office of Inquiry if asked. We do our work, we take our pay, and when the political winds change—as they always do—we adapt."

He stopped walking. He was standing directly in front of Ines, though his eyes passed over him like Ines was any other spear in the rack.

"Some of you took oaths before you took contracts," Brannoc said, and only Ines heard the weight drop into the words. "I know that. I respect it. But hear me now, brothers: whatever you swore at some shrine, whatever god you keep in your chest, the company comes first. The living come first. Dead gods don't feed widows."

*Dead gods.* Something in Ines's chest turned over slowly, like ice shifting in a thawing river.

"Dismissed," Brannoc said. "Drills at dusk. Dismissed."

They broke apart into the easy noise of soldiers released—jokes, complaints about drill, someone arguing that the tavern's ale had gone off. The brotherhood reassembling itself around Ines, warm and loud and utterly unaware of the crack running through the middle of it.

"Walk with me," Brannoc said, low, and led him toward the stable wall, away from ears.

"You mean to comply fully," Ines said. It wasn't a question.

"I mean to keep forty-one men fed and paid through a winter when half the free companies are being dissolved or conscripted." Brannoc leaned against the wall, arms crossed, studying Ines with the frank appraisal he reserved for tactical problems. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one you had at Kell's Ford. The one that means you're going to do something stupid out of principle." Brannoc exhaled through his nose. "Ines. Eleven years, you've been my right hand. You've never broken a rule, never missed a watch, never questioned an order in front of the line. Whatever's under your skin right now—you'll swallow it. Because I'm telling you as your captain and your friend: this hunt isn't ours to fight. It's too big. It's the crown itself."

"The crown is burning people."

"The crown is burning *gifted* people." Brannoc's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "People who can crack stone with a whisper. You know what happened at Ashfall. You saw the fields after."

Ines had seen. Everyone had. Fields glassed green. Whole villages gone silent in a single afternoon during the war's worst years, when gifted armies had fought gifted armies and ordinary soil had paid the bill. He understood the fear. He had even, in his colder hours, understood the logic.

But logic was not what lived in his chest. What lived there was older and stranger—a silence he had chosen, and a silence being forced on others, and the growing suspicion that these were not the same thing at all.

"And if the crown orders us to drag children to the pyres?" Ines asked quietly. "Say it plainly, Brannoc. Will the company come first then, too?"

His captain was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the wall, the drills started, spears knocking against shields in the rhythm they'd kept for years, the heartbeat of a family built entirely out of discipline.

"If that day comes," Brannoc finally said, "I'll answer it when it does. Not before." He pushed off the wall and clapped Ines once on the shoulder, hard enough to stagger a lesser man—an old gesture, almost brotherly, almost apology. "Swallow it, Ines. Whatever it is. Swallow it until the season turns."

But seasons didn't turn in thirteen turns. Moons did.

***

He waited until full dark to go to the shrine.

It wasn't secrecy, exactly. The Oathbound knew he made this walk—they'd known for eleven years, and had folded it into the shape of him the way a family folds around a member's strangeness. *Ines goes to the stones on moon-nights. That's just Ines.* No one mocked. No one followed. It was, in its way, the deepest kindness his brothers ever showed him: they let him be lonely in peace.

The shrine lay a mile past the last farmstead, where the valley's salt line began its slow white climb toward the border stones. It wasn't much to look at—a circle of seven standing stones, each carved with spirals worn nearly smooth by saltwind, surrounding a basin of black volcanic rock that had once, according to stories older than stories, run with the god's own tide. Now the basin held only rainwater and dead leaves. Everything here held only rainwater and dead leaves. Magic died at the salt line; even a god's abandoned furniture went mute inside it.

Ines knelt in the basin's lee, as he had knelt perhaps four hundred times before, and pressed his bare palm flat against the cold stone floor.

The oath-brand sat over his heart, under his shirt—a sigil of interlocking waves, inked into his skin with salt and ash on the day of his swearing, when he was nine years old and the war still burned beyond the mountains. Every Oathbound bore one. It was the mark of Vess's service, the sign that their voices belonged to the god of truth, that they would speak no lie and suffer no liar. Most brands were just brands now. Dead tattoos on living men. But Ines had always privately suspected his was different, though he could never have said why. Sometimes, at the edge of sleep, he thought he felt it warming. Like a banked fire. Like something waiting.

He had never told anyone. There was no one to tell.

"Thirteen turns," he said aloud, to the stones, to the basin, to the silence. His breath fogged in the cold. "The herald says the hunt becomes law when the thirteenth turn completes. Do you hear that? They're going to burn them. All the ones like me. The ones whose blood remembers what yours forgot."

Nothing answered. Nothing had ever answered. Four hundred kneelings, four hundred nights of speaking into a silence so total it felt less like absence and more like refusal—the silence of someone holding their breath behind a door.

He should have been used to it. He was used to it. And yet every time, some young and foolish part of him leaned forward into the dark between the stones and waited for the god of tides and truth to say *I hear you, child. I am here.*

"He's gone," Ines murmured, the old litany, the old anesthetic. "Whatever sealed you, wherever you went, you're gone, and I'm a man who talks to rainwater and dead leaves. That's all this is."

But his hand stayed on the stone. And the brand over his heart stayed warm. And somewhere far below the valley's salt, far below the world's attention, something vast and patient and terribly sad seemed to lean closer to listen—if such a thing could be said of silence at all.

He rose, knees cracking, and pulled his collar closed against the wind. Behind him the seven stones stood their ancient vigil, keeping a secret even they had probably forgotten.

He was halfway down the path when he saw it: a light moving along the ridgeline, northward, wrong for a shepherd and too steady for a lantern lost to wind. Riders. Many riders. Coming down toward the valley in the dead of night, when honest travelers waited for dawn.

Ines stood very still in the dark, the oath-brand pulsing warm against his chest like a second heart, and watched the lights descend.

Something was coming into the valley. Something that would change everything.

And for the first time in thirteen years of faithful silence, Ines Hartwell found himself wishing—not praying, he told himself, just wishing—that his silent god would wake up and tell him what to do.
