## 3. What the Tide Brought

The sea did not want her.

Sable understood that now, in the way a drowning girl understands anything—dimly, bodily, as a truth held in the lungs rather than the mind. The water had taken her three times and given her back three times, each time more roughly, like a hand shoving a stray cat out a door. The last wave had flung her against something hard and barnacled, scraped her cheek open, filled her mouth with a taste of copper and cold rot, and then, with what she could only call disgust, rolled her up onto a shore of grey shingle under a sky the color of an unwashed bowl.

She lay there a long while, letting the small waves slap her ribs, feeling for her heartbeat the way you feel for a coin in a dark pocket. Still there. Stubborn thing.

*Get up*, said the voice in her head that was her mother's voice, dead four years but still the strictest person she knew. *Girls who lie still on beaches get found on beaches.*

So Sable got up. Or mostly got up; her legs had opinions about it and most of those opinions were mutiny. She stood swaying at the tide line, hair plastered to her face in black ropes, and looked at where she had arrived.

A village. A poor one. A scatter of low stone houses hunched together at the foot of hills so pale they looked bleached—hills that were not green at all, she realized slowly, but white. White as bone. White as salt.

And between her and the village ran a line of stones.

They were waist-high, rounded, set in a curving row that followed the land's contour from the water's edge away into the haze, each one carved with marks too worn to read. Where they stood, the ground changed color: green grass and bladderwrack on the seaward side, and beyond them a pale plain stretching inland, glittering faintly even under the overcast, like the skin of some enormous sleeping animal.

Sable's whole body went still.

She had grown up hearing about places like this. Her grandmother—before the soldiers came for her grandmother, before the smoke and the screaming and the year Sable learned to run—had told her about the salt lands. *Magic cannot cross the salt line, little bird,* the old woman had said, turning her thimble of tea in her cracked hands. *It dies there. It drowns there. That is why your kind must never go.*

*Your kind.* As if being able to sing a seed awake made Sable a different species of animal. Maybe it did. The crown certainly thought so. The edicts nailed to every church door in three counties thought so. *All persons possessed of unnatural gifts shall present themselves for registration upon pain of purification.* Everyone knew what *purification* meant. Everyone had smelled it, once or twice, drifting from the market squares on days when the bonfires burned hotter than cook-fires should.

But here was the thing—the terrible, miraculous, terrifying thing—that Sable realized as she stood dripping on the shingle with her heart hammering salt water through her veins:

Her gift was quiet.

Not sleeping. Not suppressed the way it was when she clenched her teeth and sang nothing and felt the power build behind her breastbone like a sneeze that would not come. Quiet the way a room is quiet when the door finally closes. All her life the gift had been a second heartbeat, a hum under everything, a green whisper wanting out. When she'd been frightened on the boat—and she had been frightened on the boat for six days straight, hiding among barrels of dried cod while the ship pitched and the sailors' boots thundered overhead—it had leaked out of her despite everything. The mold in the bilge had bloomed into soft grey flowers. The weevils in the bread had hatched into moths that beat against the barrel staves all night, drawn to her like moths are drawn to lamps. The rope coils near her hammock had sprouted. The captain had begun to look at her strangely, and then the riders had met the ship at the estuary, and then—

Well. Then the sea.

Now, standing on this shore, Sable reached inside herself the way you reach for a tooth with your tongue, expecting the familiar ache.

Nothing.

Just silence. A clean, ringing, absolute silence, like snowfall, like the moment after a bell stops. For thirteen years the voice in her blood had never once stopped talking, and now it was gone, and she stood on a cold beach and felt—for the first time since she was seven years old, since the day a dying apple tree in her grandmother's orchard heard Sable humming and burst into impossible fruit—*safe*.

She laughed. It came out cracked and half-sobbed, but it was a laugh. A fisherman hauling his boat up the strand fifty yards away turned to stare at her, a wild-haired drowned thing laughing at the sky, and Sable pulled herself together enough to look pitiable instead of mad, which took effort.

"Shipwreck," she rasped, when he came closer, wary as a man approaching a beached jellyfish. "Please. I'm the only one."

He wasn't a kind man, exactly, but he was a practical one, and practical men in fishing villages know that the sea gives gifts sometimes and it's bad luck to refuse them. He wrapped her in an oilskin that smelled of tar and old mackerel, and he walked her toward the village without touching her, keeping a careful arm's length the way you'd keep distance from something you weren't sure was done being dangerous.

"What do I call you, then?" he asked.

Sable had a name ready. She'd had names ready for three years, a rotation of them, worn smooth as river pebbles from use. "Maren," she said. It was a good salt-name. Nobody looked twice at a girl called Maren.

"Maren," the fisherman repeated, unconvinced but unwilling to argue with the sea's business. "I'm Tull. You'll want dry clothes and broth, and then you'll want to be gone by morning, because this is Brackwater and we don't keep strays."

That suited Sable down to the ground. Strays who stayed got noticed. Noticed girls with green whispers in their blood got registered, or purified, and either word ended the same way.

What she didn't say—what she couldn't have explained even if she'd wanted to—was that she wasn't sure she could leave. Not yet. Because for the first time in thirteen years, the thing inside her was silent, and silence, after a lifetime of noise, was its own kind of addiction.

* * *

They gave her a corner of the drying shed.

Tull's sister-in-law, a broad woman named Greta with forearms like anchor chain, fed her fish stew and interrogated her with the efficiency of a customs officer, and Sable gave her the story she kept oiled and ready: orphan, seamstress's apprentice, bound south to an aunt in Marrowgate, ship lost on the shoals. Greta believed maybe a third of it, which was fine. A third was plenty. In villages like this, charity ran on a thin gruel of plausibility, and you only needed to seem sad and useful, not honest.

The drying shed stood at the village's landward edge, close against the first pale rise of the salt flats. It was a long low building of driftwood and stone, hung inside with nets in their hundreds—nets stiff with salt, hanging from beams like the shed had grown curtains of grey lace. Fish hung there too in silver rows, gutted and split, and the air was thick with the smell of brine and smoke and old oil. The floor was packed earth gone hard as iron.

Sable was given a straw pallet in the corner behind the net-racks, a blanket that scratched like a cat's tongue, and instructions to be invisible until she left.

She lay down that first night and listened to the silence inside herself, and nearly wept with relief.

Here is what nobody who isn't gifted can understand: the gift is not a tool. You do not pick it up and put it down. It is a tenant. It lives in you, pays no rent, follows no rules, and sings—all the time, always, whether you want it or not. When Sable was happy, things grew. When she was sad, things grew wrong. When she dreamed, the weeds outside whatever window she slept behind grew toward her in the night like supplicants, and she'd wake to find ivy curled around her wrist, gentle as a lover's hand, and have to tear it off and burn it and move on before anyone saw.

Thirteen years of that. Thirteen years of never touching soil barefoot, of singing only songs with no living words in them, of pressing her palms flat when she felt the power rise, of watching every flower she passed with dread instead of joy. Thirteen years of being a garden that walked.

And here, in this shed of salt-stiffened nets, on this poisoned white earth where nothing grew—not weed, not moss, not lichen—she was just a girl. Just a tired, hungry, half-drowned girl in a scratchy blanket. The silence wrapped around her like the blanket's rougher, kinder twin.

She slept eleven hours without dreaming, and woke feeling like a stranger in her own body—a leaner, lighter stranger, someone who might, given time, learn to rest.

* * *

Of course, relief is a debt, and debts get collected.

On the third day, working salt from her hair with a wooden comb Greta had lent her, Sable heard the horses.

You learn to hear horses when you've run as long as she had. Hooves have voices: cart horses plod, dray horses clatter, and the horses that carried crown riders came on in a rhythm she knew in her spine before her ears sorted it—four beats, hard-shod, unhurried. Men who did not need to hurry. Men whose hurry was a thing other people did.

She was through the drying shed's back gap before she'd fully decided to move, folding herself down between two great racks of nets, into a hollow behind stacked creels, pulling a folded net over her shoulders like a funeral shroud. The salt smell closed around her, sharp enough to sting the eyes. Through the gaps in the netting she could see a slice of the yard, grey sky, the heads of villagers gathering.

Four riders. Dark cloaks, the crown's antler-and-flame badge stitched at the shoulder. And leading them, dismounting now with the ease of a man arriving at his own estate, a figure in grey and bone-white whose presence made the villagers step back as if from weather.

An inquisitor. Here. In the one place in the world where her blood finally, blessedly, went quiet.

Sable pressed herself deeper into the nets and did the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the sea, harder than the running: she made herself listen to the silence and trust it.

*It can't find me here,* she told herself. *There's nothing of me here for it to find. I'm just Maren. I'm just a girl in a shed full of fish.*

But her hands wouldn't stop shaking, and beneath the shaking, deeper than the shaking, in the sealed vault of her chest where the green whisper lived, something stirred—faint, faint, faint as a moth against glass. Not waking. Not speaking. Just turning over in its sleep, the way a dog turns when it hears its master's footsteps far down the road.

Because her gift couldn't reach the salt-dried nets or the dead earth. But it could feel her fear. And fear, her grandmother had warned her, was the one seed that grew anywhere.

Outside, a clear cold voice was asking questions.

"—a survivor washed ashore three nights past, I'm told. Ship out of Greyharbour, lost on the Correnn shoals." Papers rustled. "Tall girl, seventeen, perhaps eighteen. Dark hair. There was a manifest."

"There were bodies," Tull's voice, flat and careful. "Eleven we know of. Sea keeps the rest."

"The sea does keep things," the cold voice agreed pleasantly. "Until it doesn't. I'll see the survivor."

Sable's heart became a small frantic bird battering itself against her ribs. *Greta will cover. Greta likes me, or at least Greta dislikes the crown, which amounts to the same thing in Brackwater—*

"Greta says the girl's fevered," said another voice, older, drier—the reeve, Sable guessed. "In the sick-house, under the widow Ammet's care. You're welcome to look, my lord, but fevered girls cough, and cough travels, and we've buried eight souls this winter already. Your pardon."

A pause. Long enough to be dangerous.

"Fevered," the inquisitor said at last, and the word came out with the flatness of a coin dropped on a counter. "How inconvenient. Then I'll return. I'm told these waters are rich. I intend to dredge them thoroughly."

Boots on stone. Horses wheeling. And then, just before the hoofbeats faded, Sable heard him say something else, quietly, almost to himself—words that slid through the net-gaps and found her like a splinter finding flesh:

"They always run to the salt. They think it hides them. But salt only makes them quieter." A soft breath that might have been a laugh. "Quieter things are easier to carry."

Then the yard was empty, and the wind moved through the hanging nets, and Sable lay in her cocoon of salt and silence and understood, with perfect clarity, that her relief had a deadline.

She could stay here only as long as she was nothing. And she had already, somewhere in the last three days—in Greta's kitchen, in Tull's boat, in the eyes of the village children who had stared at her over the fence—begun to become something. Someone. Maren, the shipwreck girl. People were starting to remember her face.

And worse than that: as the hoofbeats died away, deep in her chest, the caged whisper turned again toward the fading sound of riders, and this time Sable could have sworn she felt it listening.

*Soon*, it seemed to say, in a language older than words. *Soon, soon, soon.*

She pulled the net tighter over her head, closed her eyes, and began, silently, to count the hours until she could run.

She did not hear the door of the drying shed open at dusk, or the slow, deliberate footsteps of a man who moved like he owned the ground he crossed. She did not see the shape stop at the edge of her hiding place, silhouetted against the last grey light, looking down at the heap of nets that was breathing.

She only heard the voice—low, tired, and strange, with an accent she couldn't place:

"You can stop counting. I'm not one of them."
