## 4. The Girl Who Shouldn't Be Here

The drying sheds stood at the edge of Lowmere like the ribs of some great fish the sea had given up and then regretted. Ines had walked past them a thousand times without looking—salt-stained timber, nets hung in gray curtains, the whole structure breathing brine and old rope. Today he looked, because today the reeve's daughter had come running to find him with the news that something was wrong inside them.

*Something moved,* she'd said. *Something that breathed.*

He should have sent for Brannoc. He should have sent for anyone but himself. Instead his boots carried him down the shingle path alone, and he understood, with the cold clarity of a man watching his own foot slip on scree, that he already knew what he would find.

The morning fog was burning off the harbor in slow rags. Gulls screamed over the fish racks. Everything smelled of salt, and beneath the salt, faintly, of something green. Something growing.

That was the first wrong thing. Nothing grew in Lowmere that wasn't planted, watered, and prayed over. The valley saw to it.

Ines stopped at the shed door and let his hand rest on the frame. Eleven years since he'd last broken a rule. Eleven years since the night he'd sworn at the shrine above the quarry, when Brannoc had gripped his shoulder and old Mother Cass had pressed the brand to his forearm, and the pain had been nothing compared to the silence that followed—the silence where a god's voice should have been. He had kept every rule since. He had reported two drifters in his first year, both gifted, both taken by the crown's wagons before the frost came. He had told himself the valley was mercy enough: here they could not burn what could not catch fire.

He pushed open the door.

Dried kelp hung from the rafters in long brown braids. Nets sagged between posts like exhausted animals. The light came through gaps in the planking in thin gold blades, and in one of those blades of light, curled against the far wall behind a stack of creels, was a girl.

She was perhaps sixteen, maybe younger—half-drowned things were hard to age. Her hair was dark and stiff with dried seawater. She wore a fisherman's oilskin three sizes too large, and her bare feet were cut and gray with cold. She had wedged herself into the space between the wall and the creel stack as if trying to become part of the building.

And she was *blooming.*

It was small—that was the horror of it. A single white flower opening from a knot in the timber beside her hand, petals unfurling with the soft insistence of a held breath released. On the dirt floor near her knee, moss had spread in a bright impossible circle, vivid as spring. And around her wrist, wound through the grime like a living bracelet, grew a tendril of something flowering and blue.

Ines felt the floor tilt under him, though he knew it was only the world.

Magic did not enter the Salt Valley. That was not doctrine; it was physics, older than any church. The salt killed it the way frost kills flies. Thirteen years ago the whole war between gods and men had guttered out along these border stones because nothing gifted could cross. The girl should have been mute as a stone the moment she washed ashore. Whatever she was, whatever she carried, it should have died at the line.

Instead it flickered. Weakly—a candle in wind—but there.

Her eyes found him. Gray-green, wide, exhausted beyond fear into something flat and animal. She did not scream. She pulled the oilskin tighter and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"I know what I am. I'll go. Just—don't call for anyone yet. Let me walk out on my own feet."

The flower beside her hand closed its petals, slowly, like an eye deciding not to look.

Ines stood in the doorway a long moment. Somewhere outside, a gull dropped a mussel shell on the rocks, and the small sound went through him like a crack through glass.

"You crossed the salt line," he said. It wasn't a question. "Nothing crosses the salt line."

"Then I don't know where your line is." Her voice cracked on the words. "I walked until the riders stopped following. I swam the last bit. There was a river mouth—" She stopped, swallowed. "I didn't choose this place. The sea chose it for me."

The sea chose. Ines thought of the drowned temple three hundred miles south, of the god who had fallen silent there thirteen years ago, and felt something move in his chest that he had spent eleven years convincing himself was dead.

"You're hurt," he said.

"I'm *registered,*" she answered, with the bitter precision of someone who has learned the word recently and hates the taste. "Or I will be. Or burned. Whichever comes first. I heard the herald. Everyone heard the herald."

Yes. The edicts, nailed to the meetinghouse door in fresh ink: all gifted persons to present themselves for registration before the moon completed its thirteenth turn, or be burned as abominations. Brannoc had read it aloud in his flat parade-ground voice while forty Oathbound stood in the square and did nothing, because doing anything else meant losing their contracts, their bread, their brotherhood.

Ines stepped inside and closed the door behind him. The girl flinched hard enough to knock a creel over. Dried fish spilled across the floor between them like small silver offerings.

"I'm not going to hurt you," he said. "What's your name?"

A long pause. Long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer.

"Sable," she said finally. "My mother called me Sable. Before."

Before the hunt. Before the edicts. Before whatever had put her in the water with riders on her trail. Ines crouched slowly, keeping his hands visible, the way you'd approach a wounded animal or a frightened child, though she was somehow both and neither.

"Sable. I need to know how much of your—gift—you can use right now. Here. Inside the valley."

She stared at him. Then, cautiously, she lifted her hand, palm up, the way a child shows a parent it holds nothing. The blue tendril at her wrist stirred, brightened, threw off a scatter of pollen that glowed faintly in the dim shed—and then guttered, dimming to almost nothing, straining like a bird beating against the inside of a jar.

"It hurts," she whispered. "Like holding my breath underwater. Like the whole world is pressing on me. But it doesn't stop. It's never—it's never done this before. Everywhere else it just *is.* Here it fights."

Everywhere else it simply is. Ines rose, and his knees ached, and he realized distantly that he had been standing in one position for too long, that his heart was going too fast, that eleven years of discipline were balanced on the head of a pin inside him.

Because he knew what she was describing. Not secondhand. Not from stories. He knew what it was to feel a power straining against a seal, fighting to exist inside a cage built of salt and oath and silence. He had worn that cage so long he had mistaken it for his own skin.

*Report her.* The rule sat in him like a swallowed stone. Report the drifter. Report the gifted. Keep the valley clean, keep the contracts, keep the brothers. It was the simplest arithmetic in the world: one stranger against forty men who had bled beside him.

The flower by her hand opened again. Petal by petal, white as bone, patient as dawn.

Ines turned and walked out of the shed.

* * *

The reeve was a man named Aldous Pike, round as a barrel and twice as red, who administered Lowmere with the anxious diligence of someone perpetually expecting to be blamed for something. Ines found him at the fish racks, arguing with a cooper about tar prices, and waited with his hands clasped behind his back until the cooper stormed off.

"Messenger says something's in the drying sheds," Pike puffed. "Breathing. Moving things. You went?"

"I went."

"And?" Pike wiped his palms on his apron, leaving dark streaks. His eyes were small and quick and afraid. Everyone's eyes were afraid lately; the edicts had seen to that. "Gifted? You know the law now, Hartwell. Herald's orders. Anything that breathes wrong gets reported to me, and me to the riders, and no blame falls on Lowmere for it."

"There's no one in the sheds," Ines said.

The lie left his mouth with the ease of a blade drawn from a sheath kept oiled for years without his knowing. It did not even taste like a lie. It tasted like the truth he should have spoken eleven years ago.

Pike blinked. "No one? Messenger swore—"

"Drifters, most likely. Squatters moved on when they heard me coming. I checked the whole floor and the loft." Ines held the reeve's gaze with the flat calm he'd learned from Brannoc, the calm that made men believe a thing because believing it was easier than doubting a man like him. "I'll walk the shore tonight, double-check. If anything turns up, you'll be the first to know."

"Well." Pike tugged his apron, mollified and suspicious in equal measure. "See you do. Crown's paying attention to us now, Hartwell. Paying attention to *you* especially, if you take my meaning. Company of mercenaries squatting in our valley—herald asked after you lot by name." He leaned in, lowering his voice as if the crown itself might be eavesdropping. "There's talk in the low country. Talk about the Oathbound. About oaths sworn at shrines. Old shrines." His little eyes flicked to Ines's forearm, where the sleeve covered the brand. "Talk about what happened thirteen years back."

"The war's over," Ines said.

"Aye," Pike agreed, too quickly. "Over and done. Well. No one in the sheds, then."

"No one in the sheds," Ines confirmed.

He walked away along the racks, past the hanging silver bodies of the day's catch, and it took all his training not to look back toward the sheds. Behind him he heard Pike shouting at the cooper again about tar prices, and the ordinary noise of the village closed over the moment like water.

Eleven years. One lie. It hadn't even been difficult. That was the part that frightened him—not the breaking, but how naturally the rule had bent, as if it had been waiting all this time for a reason.

* * *

He brought her food at dusk: barley bread, hard cheese, a flask of watered wine. He brought a blanket and a clay pot of rendered fat for her feet, and he brought, without quite admitting why, the small tin star that the company gave its youngest recruits—the one he'd kept in a drawer for thirteen years, meant once for someone who never lived to wear it.

Sable ate like someone who had learned hunger as a craft. Between bites she watched him with those gray-green eyes, cataloguing him the way he'd catalogued her: threat assessment, escape routes, odds.

"You lied for me," she said at last. "To the reeve. I heard you through the boards."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Ines sat down across from her, his back against the post, and found he had no answer that wasn't also a confession. Outside, the tide was turning; he could hear it dragging at the shingle, patient, immense, indifferent.

"Because the law is new," he said finally, "and the law is wrong."

"That's not an answer. That's a slogan." She set down the bread. "Men don't break eleven years of rules for slogans. My father broke one rule, once, for my mother. Just one. And it undid our whole life." Her voice caught on the last word and steadied itself with visible effort. "So. Why?"

He looked at his forearm. The sleeve had ridden up; the edge of the brand showed, the sigil burned into him at seventeen—a circle bisected by a vertical line, the mark of the silent god's sworn. Sable went very still.

"I've seen that," she breathed. "In the stories. The old women tell them still, in the hill country, quiet, where the riders can't hear. The seal-mark. They say the ones who bore it silenced Vess himself. They say the god's voice didn't die—it was *locked*, and the lock is made of men." Her eyes came up to his face, and in them was no longer fear but a terrible, wondering recognition. "You're one of them. You're one of the sealers' sons."

"I'm a mercenary," Ines said.

"No." She shook her head slowly. Around her wrist, the blue tendril brightened, straining toward him like a plant toward light, and the white flower on the timber wall opened so fast he heard it. "You're the reason I can still do this. Don't you understand? Everywhere in this valley the magic dies. Everywhere except near *you.* It leaks. Through the cracks in you. Whatever you sealed—" Her voice dropped to a whisper, awed and horrified in equal measure. "Whatever you sealed, it isn't perfect. And I'm what leaks through."

The tide dragged at the shingle. A gull cried somewhere over the black water. Ines sat in the dimness of the drying shed, in the smell of salt and old rope, and felt the crack run through him—not duty splitting from desire, but the deeper fissure, the one that had been widening for thirteen years without his ever hearing it: between the man who kept rules and the man who had made them necessary.

Outside, far off on the ridgeline, a horn sounded. Once, twice, a third time—the company's signal, the one that meant riders sighted, the one that meant *come home now.*

Brannoc's horn. Calling him in.

Ines rose, tucked the blanket more securely around the girl who shouldn't be here, and said, "Stay hidden. However long it takes, stay hidden. I'll come back after dark."

And he walked out into the blue dusk, toward the horns, carrying his first broken rule like a coal cupped in both hands, wondering how long it would burn before it lit everything he touched.
