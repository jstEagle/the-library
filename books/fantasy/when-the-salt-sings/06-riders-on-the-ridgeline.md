## 6. Riders on the Ridgeline

They came down off the ridgeline at first light, and Ines knew it before he heard it, because the gulls went quiet.

That was the thing about the Salt Valley: sound carried strangely here. The wind off the flats flattened everything into a single low hush, so that a rider's hoofbeats arrived in your bones before your ears. Ines stood in his doorway with a chipped mug of barley tea going cold in his hand and watched them descend—a column of twelve, then more behind, maybe thirty in all, their banners hanging dead-still in the windless morning. Crown colors. Ash-gray and bone-white, the sigil of the burning hand stitched where a heart should be.

Behind him, from beneath the floorboards of his own cottage, came a sound so small he almost missed it. A breath, held. Then released, very slowly, the way Sable had learned to breathe these past four days—shallow and careful, like someone rationing air in a flooded room.

*Thirty riders*, Ines thought, *and I am standing here holding tea.*

He set the mug down on the sill. His hands were steady. Eleven years of mercenary work will do that for you; the body learns to keep its own counsel even when the mind is screaming. But underneath the steadiness there was something else, something that felt unpleasantly like the moment before a bridge gives way—the knowledge that the structure has been compromised, that every step forward is borrowed.

He pulled on his coat and went out to meet them.

* * *

The village of Grayharbor-on-Salt was not much: forty roofs, a reeve's hall, a chapel to no particular god because the valley's god had gone silent and the villagers had stopped pretending otherwise. The drying sheds leaned along the shore like old men telling the same story over and over. The nets hung stiff with salt, gray-white as frost.

Corvane did not arrive with the vanguard. He arrived after it, alone, on a pale horse the color of the salt flats themselves, and Ines understood immediately that this was deliberate. The riders were the announcement. This man was the sermon.

He dismounted in front of the reeve's hall with the unhurried grace of someone who had never once needed to hurry. He was younger than Ines expected—mid-thirties, perhaps, with a scholar's soft hands and a soldier's straight back—and he wore no armor, only a long coat of ash-gray wool and a chain of office made of small linked rings, each ring stamped with a tiny open eye. His face was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was a face built for trust, with laugh lines already forming at its corners, and eyes that used the laugh lines the way fishermen use lures.

"Good people." His voice carried without effort, warm as bread. "I am Corvane, inquisitor by appointment of the Crown, and I bring you no threat. Only questions."

Ines stood among the gathered villagers near the back, arms crossed, wearing the flat expression of a man who had seen a hundred such speeches and believed none of them. Beside him, Maren the net-mender shifted her weight. Old Tammas spat into the dirt. The reeve, a sweating man named Odell whose authority extended exactly as far as the valley's borders and no farther, wrung his hat in his fists.

"The edict is posted," Corvane continued, gesturing, and one of his riders nailed a fresh copy to the hall's door with three efficient blows. "All persons gifted with the old blood shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Registration is protection. The Crown does not burn what it can count."

*A lie so smooth you could swallow it whole*, Ines thought, *and choke on it later.*

"But," Corvane said, and let the word hang, gentle as a noose lowered over a branch, "the Crown also knows that gifts hide. They hide in attics. They hide in cellars. They hide—" his gaze swept the crowd, mild and terrible, "—in plain sight, among neighbors who love them and lie for them. And so I must ask each of you: have you seen anything? Heard anything? A flower blooming out of season. A bird that speaks. A child whose fevers break too fast. Small things. Miracles are never large, at first. They begin small, like rot."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on a crowd until they become one animal wanting only to scatter. Ines felt it settle over Grayharbor like weather.

And beneath it—beneath the floorboards of a cottage three streets away—he knew Sable could feel it too. He'd told her the gift would be mute here. Mostly true. But for four days he had watched her flinch at nothing, watched her press her palm flat against the salt-crusted wall of his cottage as if listening to something through it, and he had begun to understand that the valley didn't kill magic so much as cage it. And a caged thing strains. And a straining thing makes noise, somewhere, somehow, in ways men like Corvane had spent their lives learning to hear.

"Nothing?" Corvane said pleasantly, to no one and everyone. "A whole village, and nothing?"

"We're salt folk," said Maren, loud enough. "Nothing grows here but debt."

A ripple of nervous laughter. Corvane smiled with them, generous, and Ines watched the smile not reach anywhere near the eyes. "Salt folk. Yes. Which brings me to a practical matter." He turned to Reeve Odell. "Your valley hosts a mercenary company. The Oathbound. Forty swords under one Captain Brannoc. Contracted, I'm told, to protect this province's roads."

"That's so," Odell said.

"Then the company will wish to cooperate with the Crown's hunt. It would go badly for their contract otherwise." Corvane said it lightly, a remark about weather, and Ines felt it land in his chest like a hook finding meat. "I'll speak with your captain this afternoon. Where might I find him?"

"The quarry road camp," Odell managed. "Past the border stones."

"Wonderful." Corvane's gaze drifted across the crowd once more, unhurried, cataloguing. It passed over Ines and stopped—not long, barely a heartbeat, just long enough. "You. The tall one. You've a soldier's bearing. Oathbound?"

"Ines," he said. "Yes."

"And do you live alone, Ines?"

The question was nothing. It was a needle with no thread. And still Ines felt the pause—one breath too long, half a heartbeat, the space where a different answer lived. "I do."

"Mm." Corvane smiled at him, warm as banked coals. "Lonely work, honesty. Thank you for yours."

He moved on. Ines did not move until his legs decided for him.

* * *

Brannoc received the inquisitor at the quarry camp with wine and forty men drawn up in parade order, which Ines recognized instantly as both courtesy and warning: *we are many, we are watching, do not mistake us for prey.* The two commanders sat across a trestle table with the border stones visible beyond the tent flap, those ancient gray monoliths marching away toward the flats, marking where the world forgot how to be magical.

Ines stood at the back of the command tent with the other officers and listened to his captain lie.

Not badly. Brannoc was too good a liar to lie badly. He gave Corvane the company's history, its contracts, its sworn neutrality in matters of faith. "We're sword-hands, lord inquisitor, not witch-finders. We guard roads. We don't search attics."

"The edict doesn't distinguish," Corvane said.

"No," Brannoc agreed. "It doesn't." He refilled the inquisitor's cup himself. "But contracts do. We're bound to this province until thaw. If the Crown wants our swords for the hunt, it can buy them come spring, same as anyone."

Something flickered across Corvane's pleasant face—appreciation, maybe, or appetite. "And if I told you the fugitive I seek passed through this very valley? Four days ago, perhaps. Washed up on your shores like driftwood. A girl. Young. Her gift—" he paused, choosing the word with a connoisseur's care, "—*grows things*. Would your contract cover her?"

Every man in that tent went carefully still. Ines kept his breathing even and his eyes on the middle distance, on the map of the province pinned to the tent pole, seeing none of it.

Brannoc took his time with his wine. When he looked up, his face was granite. "No girl washed up here, lord inquisitor. If one had, the sea would have said so, and the sea tells us everything." He spread his hands. "You're welcome to search. Every shed, every cottage, every net-rack. My men will help. We have nothing to hide."

*Every shed. Every cottage.* Ines's heart performed a single slow, cold turn.

"Generous," said Corvane. "I may take you up on that tomorrow. Tonight my riders rest." He rose, buttoned his coat with neat precision, and paused at the tent flap. "Captain. One more thing. Your man there—Hartwell. The tall one who answered me in the square." He didn't turn around. "He hesitated. Half a breath. I collect hesitations the way other men collect debts." Now he turned, and smiled that lure of a smile. "Probably nothing. Probably loneliness. But you know your people better than I. Watch him for me?"

"He's my best sword and my dullest liar," Brannoc said evenly. "If he were hiding anything, I'd know."

"I believe you," said Corvane, in the tone of a man who believes nothing, and left.

* * *

Dusk. Ines walked home the long way, past the drying sheds, past the chapel, checking the street twice the way he hadn't checked a street since his soldiering days. Inside his cottage, he knelt and lifted the loose board by the hearth.

Sable looked up at him from the dark of the crawl-space, and his chest tightened. Four days of hiding had carved hollows under her eyes. Her hair was full of salt-dust and spider silk. She was seventeen years old and she had the eyes of an animal that had learned every exit in a room and found all of them barred.

"He knows," she whispered. "I heard him through the boards. Ines, he *collects*—"

"Hush." He helped her out. She unfolded from the crawlspace joint by joint, wincing, and stood swaying in the firelight. "Listen to me. Tomorrow they search. Every building. You can't be under the floorboards when they come—you can't be in the village at all."

"Where, then?"

"The salt pans. The old evaporation terraces past the north flats." He'd spent two nights working it out, pacing the cottage while she slept badly above him. "No one goes there. The ground is poison to crops, poison to wells, and the crust will hold your weight if you stay to the ridges. You'll be able to see the village. I'll bring food at dusk. One day. Maybe two, if the inquisitor lingers."

She wrapped her arms around herself. "It's worse out there," she said quietly. "Under the floorboards, I can almost pretend. Out there—" She pressed her palm against her sternum, where Ines had noticed her doing it before, some instinct older than sense. "Out there the silence gets *inside* you. Like the gift is drowning instead of sleeping. Like it's screaming and the salt eats the scream before it leaves my mouth."

Ines crouched so they were eye to eye. Up close he could see the faint shimmer beneath her skin when she was frightened, like moonlight moving under water—the echo of her leaking through, even here, even in the valley's dead ground. It should not happen. He had staked her life on it not happening. And yet.

"Sable." He waited until her eyes focused on him. "Whatever you feel out there—whatever the silence does—stay on the ridges. Stay small. Can you do that for me?"

She nodded. Then, because she was seventeen and honest in a way the world hadn't yet beaten out of her: "What happens after tomorrow? After the search?"

He didn't have a good answer. He gave her the truth instead, thin as it was. "After tomorrow, I figure out the next thing. That's all any of us ever do."

* * *

Before dawn, he walked her out.

The salt flats at night were another country—pale, silent, breathing fog. They moved ridge to ridge, Ines reading the crust by feel, testing each step with his spear-butt the way he'd been taught by a marsh-guide twenty years dead. At the third terrace he stopped and unrolled a groundcloth behind a leaning slab of salt-encrusted stone, a windbreak barely worthy of the name.

"It's not much," he said.

"It's perfect," she said, meaning neither.

He gave her the food, the water skin, the whistle carved from gull-bone—three short blasts meant danger, one long meant come home. She took them with both hands like sacraments. Then she did something he didn't expect. She reached out and touched his forearm, right over the oath-brand hidden beneath his sleeve, and though she couldn't possibly have known where it sat, her fingers found it anyway, the way water finds the lowest ground.

"It hums," she murmured. Not to him. To herself. "Even here. Even through everything. Why does it *hum*?"

Ines withdrew his arm, gently, and lied for the second time in eleven years: "Old scar. Nothing more."

She looked at him for a long moment with those drowned-deep eyes, and he understood that she knew he was lying, and let him, and hated it, and filed it away. Seventeen years old. He thought: *she is learning to survive men who lie to her kindly, and I am teaching her how.*

"Sunrise soon," he said. "Lie flat if you see riders. The salt hides heat, but not movement."

He left her there as the sky went gray, then pearl, then the bruised gold of dawn over the flats. By the time he reached the village, the first search parties were already moving through the lanes, and Corvane's riders were going door to door with the methodical patience of men counting coins.

* * *

They searched his cottage at midmorning.

Two soldiers and a lean, gray functionary with a slotted lantern and a mirror on a pole. They looked under his bed, in his chimney, through his stores. The functionary tapped walls, listened, made marks in a ledger. Ines stood in his own doorway and offered them tea they refused, and watched them find nothing, because there was nothing to find—because the girl was a mile out on the poison flats, lying flat behind a salt slab with her hand pressed over her sternum, holding her gift down like a lid on a boiling pot.

It nearly worked.

The failure came at noon, from a direction no one had planned for. Ines was standing in the square, enduring the slow agony of doing nothing, when he saw it: the well-water in the trough by the chapel shivering, though there was no wind. Then the salt crust along the lane's edge beginning, very faintly, to *glow*—a pearly luminescence rising off it like heat-shimmer in reverse, drifting north. Drifting toward the flats.

Toward her.

Ines's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. Because he knew what it was. He'd read the old accounts during the worst nights of his research, the ones the crown burned faster than witches: a strong gift under suppression doesn't die. It *leaks*. And leaks leave trails. And somewhere on those terraces, a seventeen-year-old girl was straining against the salt with everything she had, and the strain was writing itself across the valley in light.

He looked around wildly. Corvane stood at the far end of the lane, and he was looking at it too. The inquisitor's head tilted, slowly, like a bird's. He raised one hand, and his riders began to converge on the square, and his voice carried across the village, pleasant as ever:

"There you are," he said softly, to the shining air, to no one, to everyone. "Small miracles. Never large, at first."

Ines was moving before he'd decided to move—but between him and the north flats lay the quarry road, and on the quarry road, drawn up across it with forty men and their captain at the fore, stood the Oathbound.

Brannoc met Ines's eyes across sixty feet of frozen ground. Everything was in that look: the shrine where they'd sworn together as boys, the thirteen years of shared bread and shared graves, the lie Brannoc had told Corvane yesterday with granite calm—and the question underneath it all, the one that would decide whether Ines lived to see dusk.

His captain held the line a moment longer. Then, quietly, so only the nearest men could hear:

"Company stands down. Quarry road is closed for drill exercises. Nobody crosses till I say."

The line parted. Just wide enough. As Ines ran past him, Brannoc caught his arm, and his grip was iron, and his voice dropped to something raw and private:

"One time, Ines. I cover for you *one time*. But hear me—my loyalty ends where the contract begins, and the contract begins the moment Corvane makes it worth more than you are. Get her out of my valley before I have to choose. Because if you make me choose—" his jaw worked, "—I'll choose the brothers. Every time. Even you."

Ines nodded once. There was nothing else to say. He ran north onto the flats, boots punching through the glowing crust, toward the terraces where a girl lay flat against the salt with her gift screaming silently inside her—and behind him, faint but gaining, he could hear the hoofbeats of riders who had found the shining trail, and the pleasant, carrying voice of Corvane calling orders in a tone like a hymn.

One day. He'd promised her one day.

The sun wasn't even at its height yet.
