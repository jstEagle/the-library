## 7. The Oathbound Divide

The fire in the company hall burned low, the way Brannoc liked it—coals more than flame, so a man's face showed only in pieces and the rest was left to shadow. Lies went down easier in shadow. Truths too.

He sat at the head of the long table because twenty-two years in the Oathbound had earned him that chair, and because tonight he would have to speak words he did not want to own, and it was better to own them from a seat of authority than from among equals. Eleven men and two women of the company remained in the valley; the rest were out on contract, escorting wool factors and tin merchants along roads that suddenly mattered more than they ever had. The edict nailed to the market post three days past fluttered in his memory still, its wax seal fat and red as a wound.

*All gifted persons to present themselves for registration by the thirteenth turn of the moon. Those found unregistered after shall be subject to the purifying flame.*

"Registration," said Merrin, who handled their accounts and could smell a bad contract from across a county. She turned her cup on the board without drinking. "That's what the herald called it. Registration. Like we're registering sheep for shearing."

"That's exactly what it is," said Dolm, the crossbowman, broad hands flat on the table. "Sheep get sheared or they get eaten. We're being asked which we'd rather."

Brannoc let them talk. He had learned long ago that a captain's first job was listening—that the second was choosing which of the things he heard to repeat. Around the coals the faces came and went: young Kettrin, nineteen and hungry for distinction; old Wray with his missing fingers and his missing faith; Havel, who had not yet spoken and whose silence had a shape to it.

It was Havel finally who said the thing they were all circling. "There's a girl in this valley the crown wants burned. And one of ours has been seen carrying bread to the drying sheds at hours when no honest man carries bread anywhere."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"We don't know that," said Merrin.

"We know it," said Havel. "We've all known it since Corvane's outriders came through asking their questions. We know Ines lied to the reeve—we heard him do it, smooth as a fish slipping off a hook, and Ines Hartwell hasn't told a lie since the day he swore at the shrine. Eleven years clean and then, all at once, a lie with a girl inside it." He looked around the table, jaw working. "I'm not saying I blame him. I'm saying the inquisitor knows too, or will soon. And when Corvane comes back—and he'll come back with more than questions—he'll ask us where our loyalty sits. With our contracts and our heads, or with one man's soft heart and whatever he's hiding under his floorboards."

"Say it plain," Kettrin said. "You want to give him up."

"I want us alive," Havel answered. "I want the winter wages paid and the guild marks renewed in spring, and I want to die old and stupid in a bed instead of on a pyre built for somebody else's sin. If that means handing over a girl none of us swore to protect, then yes. Plain enough."

"She's a child," Merrin said.

"She's gifted. Which by this edict is the same as kindling."

Brannoc put his hand flat on the table. Not hard. Just once, palm to wood, the sound of a door closing. The company quieted, because twenty-two years teaches men what that sound means.

"Here is where we stand," he said. "The crown pays our guild stipend. The valley pays our escort fees. Every debt we carry, every widow we've pensioned, every apprentice we've taken on—the coin behind all of it runs through offices in the capital, and those offices answer to men who signed that edict. Defy it, and we're not heroes. We're outlaws with forty mouths to feed and no road that doesn't end in a rope." He let that sit. "Hold to it, and we hand a child to the fire. Nobody at this table wants that. But wanting and doing are different countries, and most of us will never visit the second one."

"And Ines?" Wray asked quietly. "Which country does he live in?"

Brannoc looked at the coals for a long moment before answering.

"Ines lives wherever his oath put him," he said. "And that's the problem."

* * *

They had sworn together, the two of them, thirteen years ago come autumn. Brannoc remembered it the way he remembered few other things—whole, and sharp at the edges, and smelling of salt.

The shrine stood then as it stood now: a ring of black stones above the salt flats, older than any kingdom, worn featureless by wind that carried brine instead of blessing. Vess had no temples anymore, no priests, no bells. Only the stones, and the men who came to kneel between them. Mercenaries mostly. Men whose work made prayer a practical matter—who wanted, more than anything, a god who would not demand anything back.

The rite was simple. You knelt. You spoke your name into the silence. You felt—for those who felt it—a vast attention turn toward you like the head of a slow animal, curious and sad, and then you pressed your palm to the stone, and the brand came: a circle broken by a single line, seared cold rather than hot, an ache more than a pain. The mark of the silent god. The mark that said *this one listens, and asks nothing.*

Ines had been barely twenty. Skinny. All elbows and watchfulness, fresh from some grief Brannoc never learned the shape of, wanting what every Oathbound wanted—to belong to something that would not betray him. When the brand took him, Ines had made a sound. Not a cry. More like a man exhaling after holding his breath for years.

And Brannoc, kneeling beside him, palm to the neighboring stone, had felt something pass between them through the rock—some thread of the god's attention, thin as spider silk, binding oath-brother to oath-brother. They rose from the stones bound twice over: once to the silence, once to each other.

*I will keep your quiet,* Brannoc had sworn, in the formula every man spoke. *I will hold your peace as you hold it. What is sealed, I will not open.*

Thirteen years later he sat in a hall full of men who wanted him to break that, one way or another. Either he broke faith with the god by surrendering the man who bore the seal, or he broke faith with the living by sheltering the reason the crown had come sniffing at all.

Or so it seemed, sitting there in the coal-light. Brannoc had been a soldier long enough to distrust problems that offered exactly two answers. Two answers usually meant someone was selling a third one privately, and the price was shame.

He dismissed the company near midnight, watched them file out into the cold—Kettrin glancing back, Havel not glancing at all—and then sat alone with the dying fire for a long time, turning the cup Merrin had left, empty, around and around.

The third answer, when it came, arrived the way most of his worst decisions arrived: wearing the face of a friend.

* * *

He found Ines at the border stones an hour before dawn, which surprised him not at all. There were only a handful of places Ines Hartwell could be found with certainty, and the salt line was one of them—he walked it the way other men walked city walls, checking each boundary marker with a touch that was half habit and half devotion. Brannoc came up the path quietly but not silently; there was no sneaking up on Ines, and trying would have been its own insult.

"Your fire's gone out," Ines said without turning. "I could see it from here."

"Hall fires go out. That's what makes them fires and not convictions." Brannoc stopped beside him. Below them the flats stretched pale and endless, silvered by the waning moon, dead as glass. Nothing grew on them. Nothing magic survived them either—the salt line ran invisible and absolute, and beyond it lay the world where gifts worked and gods had once walked and men had burned for both. "The company talked tonight."

"I know. Havel asked me at supper if I'd seen any pretty birds lately. It was practically a confession from both of us."

"He's not wrong, though. Is he."

Ines didn't answer. In the moonlight his profile looked carved from the same stuff as the stones—patient, weathered, impossible to read unless you'd spent eleven years learning the grain of it. Brannoc had. He could see the tension in the set of the mouth, the exhaustion banked under the eyes. A man guarding something precious and paying for it hourly.

"There's a girl," Brannoc said. Not a question.

"There's a girl."

"Gifted."

Ines was quiet a moment. Then, because lying to Brannoc was apparently harder even than lying to the reeve: "Her gift is voice. Living things grow when she speaks. She's sixteen, Brannoc. She washed up half-drowned in the nets and she's been terrified out of her mind for longer than I can measure, and if I put her name on Corvane's ledger she dies screaming. So yes. Gifted. And mine to hide, because no one else in this cursed valley will."

"The company might," Brannoc said slowly. "Some of them. For a price."

"I won't spend their honor like coin."

"You already have. You just weren't the one holding the purse." Brannoc turned to face him fully. Thirteen years of brotherhood, and it came down to this: two men on a cold hill, one of them about to become the other's enemy. He made himself say it plainly, because Ines deserved plainness if nothing else. "Corvane returns within the week. Maybe sooner. He's requested the company's assistance formally—which means contracted, which means paid, which means when he asks us where the girl is, we are bound by law and guild-oath to answer truthfully. Havel will answer. Kettrin will answer. Half the men will answer, because half the men have families in the lowlands who burn alongside traitors' kin. I can't hold them. I've tried counting heads and I come up short every time."

"So the vote goes against me."

"There's no vote. That's the mercy and you should know it—I'm not putting this to the table, because a table would make it a betrayal, and I'd rather it stay what it is." Brannoc's throat was tight. He looked out at the salt so he wouldn't have to look at Ines's face while he said the rest. "The company holds to the contract. That's decided. Come morning I send word to Corvane that we'll cooperate fully, and I put four riders on the drying sheds road by noon, because if I don't, Corvane puts forty there, and forty doesn't stop at sheds."

Silence. The wind moved across the flats with its dry whisper, the sound that gave the valley its name—the salt singing, the locals called it, though nothing sang in it that any god answered.

"You swore beside me," Ines said at last. His voice was level. That was the worst part, how level it stayed. "At the stones. Same night. Same brand."

"I remember."

"*What is sealed, I will not open.*"

"I know what I swore." And now Brannoc did look at him, and let him see everything he was feeling, because hiding it would be the final dishonesty. "And I'm telling you that I'm breaking it, or breaking everything else, and I chose the way a captain chooses—with arithmetic instead of love. Forty souls against one. Contracts against conscience. The living against the lost. I've done that sum my whole life, Ines, and it's never once come out where my heart wanted it to." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "You were the only thing I ever swore to that wasn't arithmetic."

Something moved in Ines's face—grief, maybe, or its cousin—and then settled into stillness. "Then say what you actually rode up here to say. You didn't come to tell me the vote's against me. You could've done that at breakfast."

Brannoc exhaled. Here it was, then. The private mercy. The third answer, bought with shame.

"Noon," he said. "Four riders take the sheds road at noon. Corvane's outriders patrol the south ford until dawn—they change posts at first light, and there's a gap of perhaps half an hour when the southern line walks blind. The reeve owes me a favor and I'll call it tonight; the bridge toll records will show a grain wagon going north before sunrise, and no one checks grain wagons twice." He held up a hand before Ines could speak. "Don't thank me. Don't you dare thank me, because I'm not doing it for you. I'm doing it because in thirteen years I've learned exactly two things worth knowing, and one of them is that I can't defy the crown, and the other is that I cannot stand in front of Corvane tomorrow and watch him drag you out of that cottage in chains. One night, Ines. I can give you one night without becoming the man who sold you. After that—" he spread his hands, empty, "—after that I'm just another contract with a face."

Ines looked at him for a long time. The moon was going down behind them now, and the salt below had begun to gray, and somewhere far off a bird made the first argument of the morning.

"One night," Ines said. "And the girl?"

"Is not mentioned between us again. As far as the record shows, I never knew she existed. You'll take her out over the line tonight, and whatever waits for you out there—" Brannoc gestured at the dead pale flats, at the whole wide gifted world beyond them, "—it's yours, not mine. I have a company to keep alive and a god to apologize to."

"He won't hear the apology," Ines said. "He hasn't heard anything in thirteen years."

"Maybe." Brannoc turned to go, then stopped, because there was one more thing and he would hate himself forever if he left it unsaid on this hill where they'd been brothers. "Whatever you're doing—wherever you're taking her—it isn't random. I've watched you these two weeks. You move like a man following a map he memorized in childhood. So I'll say this once, soldier to soldier: if you're walking toward the thing I think you're walking toward, then the crown isn't hunting you for harboring a witch. They'll hunt you for what you *are.* And I won't be able to ride fast enough to matter." He clapped Ines once on the shoulder, hard, the old salute, the one that meant *still yours, whatever else.* "Go before light. And Ines—if it comes to war out there—"

"Don't pick a side," Ines finished. "Pick the survivors. You always did."

Brannoc walked down the hill alone. Behind him he did not watch Ines Hartwell slip away into the last dark before dawn, taking with him a stolen girl, an eleven-year record of honesty, and the only friendship Brannoc had never had to purchase. Ahead of him lay the hall, the coals, the letter to Corvane waiting for his seal.

Love curdles slowly, he thought, descending through the cold. Then all at once, and forever after, it keeps the taste.

By noon the riders would be on the sheds road. By dusk Corvane's hawk would be on his wrist, carrying word north.

And somewhere over the border stones, in the country where gods still breathed, Ines Hartwell would be running out of night—and Brannoc would be standing at his window, watching the thirteenth moon swell at the horizon's edge, already grieving the day it came full.
