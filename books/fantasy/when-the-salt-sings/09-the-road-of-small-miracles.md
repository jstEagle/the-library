## 9. The Road of Small Miracles

The hinterlands did not forget the way the valley forgot. That was Sable's first lesson of the road.

Ines had warned her, back when they crossed the salt line and she'd wept at the sheer *loudness* of a world with magic in it again—warned her that outside the valley, every mile was a risk. But he hadn't told her that the land itself would remember her. Hedgerows leaned toward her as she passed. Frost bloomed into white flowers along her footprints if she lingered too long on a stone. Once, exhausted past caring, she'd hummed an old lullaby against a dead tree, and by morning it had budded, green shoots cracking through bark that had been grey for years.

Ines had stood over that tree for a long while before saying anything.

"You can't do that where people can see."

"There's no one to see," she'd said, gesturing at the empty hills.

"There's always someone." He'd said it without anger, but with a weight that made her stomach drop, because she understood then that he wasn't talking about eyes. He was talking about mouths. About word traveling faster than feet. About what happened when the crown heard that something grew where a girl walked.

So they kept to the high ground, the sheep tracks and drovers' paths, moving by day through country so poor and empty it barely bothered having a name. And still the land remembered her. Still things leaned after her like sunflowers following a late sun.

She should have hated it—that her body could not pass through the world quietly, that even her breath left green marks. But there was a part of her, small and shameful and growing, that loved it. For eleven days since the ford—for eleven days since Ines had bled for her and two of his sworn brothers had chosen her over their oaths—the world had been listening to her voice, and her voice had been answering.

She had spent her whole life being told to be quiet. Out here, quiet felt like a kind of lie.

* * *

They found the priest on the third day, or perhaps he found them.

Sable saw him first: a thin man sitting beside a shrine at a crossroads so minor it appeared on no map Ines owned. The shrine was old—one of the waystones raised before the god-war, its carved face worn nearly smooth—and someone had left offerings there recently. Bread gone hard. A twist of dried flowers. A stub of tallow candle burned down to nothing.

The man himself was mending a sandal with a bone needle and a length of gut, and he looked up as they approached with the unhurried calm of someone who had decided long ago not to be surprised by anything.

"Pilgrims," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Travelers," Ines corrected, hand resting easy near his knife. Sable had learned to read that gesture. It meant *I don't trust you yet*, which from Ines meant almost nothing, because he didn't trust anyone yet.

"Mm." The man bit off his thread and examined his work. "Travelers, then. You've come a long way to stand at a dead god's waystone."

"The gods are all dead," Ines said flatly. "Or silent. Same thing, some say."

"Some say." The man smiled. He had a face built for smiling and worn down by not doing it—a network of fine lines around his mouth and eyes, like paper that had been folded and refolded too many times. "I'm Merric. Formerly hedge-priest of Vess in the parish of Alderfen. Currently unemployed, unfrocked, and unrepentant. Will you share tea? I have a kettle and three cups, which is more hospitality than most shrines can offer these days."

"Why three?" Sable asked, before she could stop herself.

Merric looked at her then—really looked—and something moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice.

"Because I have always hoped," he said, "for company."

He brewed the tea on a little spirit stove, and it was terrible, bitter and oversteeped, and Sable drank every drop. While they sat, Merric talked—not about anything important, just the road, the weather, the price of wool—but Sable noticed that his gaze kept returning to her, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

It was near dusk, when the light went long and gold across the moor, that he finally asked.

"You sang, didn't you? Crossing the ford at Miller's Rise. Four nights ago."

Ines went very still. "How would you know that?"

"Alderfen is six miles downstream. The river came up singing." Merric set down his cup with great care, as though it were something holy. "Water remembers voices, you know. Always has. It carried your song to my parish, girl, and my parish carried it to me, and I have been walking upstream ever since." He leaned forward. "What are you?"

"Tired," Sable said. "And hungry. And not looking to be anyone's miracle today."

"Ah." Merric nodded slowly. "But that's the thing about miracles. They're never looking to be miracles either. They're just... trying to get somewhere dry." He rose, joints cracking, and began packing his few belongings with brisk efficiency. "Well. I'm coming with you."

"We didn't ask you to," Ines said.

"No one ever does. That's rather the point of me." He slung a threadbare bag over his shoulder. "You're going to the drowned temple. Don't bother denying it—it's written all over both of you, the way a destination gets written on pilgrims. Everyone walks toward something. Most people just don't know what." He smiled that worn-paper smile again. "And I have been waiting thirteen years for someone worth walking with."

* * *

The twins came later, and less gently.

Sable smelled the ambush before it sprang—or rather, she smelled woodsmoke where there should have been none, sweet and wrong in the middle of an empty afternoon. She had time to grab Ines's sleeve and say "*wait*" before the crossbow bolts thudded into the turf where they would have been standing.

"Not bad," said a voice from the gorse.

"Not bad at all," agreed a second voice, identical, from a different patch of gorse entirely.

They emerged together, the way twins do everything—as if choreographed by some private agreement older than language. A young man and woman, red-haired, freckled, dressed in the mismatched layers of people who own many clothes but love none of them. Each held a crossbow aimed with professional indifference.

"Wren," said the man.

"Sparrow," said the woman.

"Together," they said, "we're the Wren-Sparrow Company, purveyors of goods across borders, over borders, under borders, and occasionally through borders that didn't know they were being crossed."

"You shot at us," Ines said. His voice had gone flat and cold, the mercenary's voice, and Sable watched his weight shift onto his back foot. Beside him, the two Oathbound who had defected at the ford—Dagny and Rell, quiet as stones since joining them—moved their hands toward their weapons.

"We shot *near* you," Sparrow corrected. "There's a difference. It's called craftsmanship."

"What we want," Wren said, "is to know why the crown's riders stopped asking about a mercenary and started asking about a song."

Sable's blood went cold. "What song?"

"Oh, come now." Sparrow lowered her crossbow, grinning. "'When the salt sings, the silence ends; when the silence ends, the god ascends.' Children are skipping rope to it in Marrowgate. Fishermen whistle it in Kettleharbor. There's a version in the mines with considerably more verses and considerably worse theology."

"It's got a tune," Wren added, "that makes your teeth hum. We heard a peddler sing it in a tavern, and every candle in the room leaned toward him like he was the prettiest thing at a dance."

"That was three weeks ago," Sparrow said. "Before you existed. As far as anyone knows." Her grin sharpened. "But we know how songs start, see. Songs like that don't start. They *escape*."

Silence. Wind in the gorse. Somewhere distant, a hawk crying.

Then Merric stepped forward, hands open and empty, and said mildly: "They're with me."

"They don't look like they're with anyone," Wren said.

"I'm a defrocked priest of the silent god traveling with armed fugitives toward a drowned temple. I collect strays. It's practically doctrine." Merric spread his hands. "Kill them if you like. But then you'll never learn what the temple actually holds, and you'll spend the rest of your short, profitable lives wondering. I know you two. Wondering will be worse than dying."

The twins looked at each other. Something passed between them—some twin-frequency conversation conducted entirely in eyebrow movements and the set of a jaw.

Then, together, they broke their crossbows down and slung them across their backs.

"Right," said Sparrow. "Temple."

"But first," said Wren, "tea."

"He has terrible tea," Sparrow warned.

"I heard," said Wren. "We're hoping it's a lie."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow of an ancient hillfort, its earthworks softened by centuries into something more like a sleeping animal than a fortress. Dagny built the fire—she was good with fires, spoke to them almost, coaxing flame out of damp wood with a patience Sable envied. Rell took first watch on the rampart, a silhouette against the early stars.

And around the fire, impossibly, they talked.

Wren produced a bottle of plum brandy "liberated" from a crown supply wagon—"taxes," he explained solemnly, "collected in advance"—and Sparrow taught Sable a dice game that was definitely rigged and possibly cursed. Merric told stories about parish life before his defrocking: the old women who still left bread at the waystones, the children he'd taught to sing hymns to a god who couldn't hear them. "People say that's sad," he said, poking the fire. "Teaching songs to a deaf god. But I never thought so. A garden isn't sad because the gardener's away. You keep it anyway. You keep it *because*."

Dagny, who had spoken maybe twenty words since the ford, said quietly: "Brannoc used to say that. About the company. Keep the oath anyway. Even when it feels like no one's listening."

"And do you believe it still?" Merric asked.

Dagny turned her cup in her hands. "I believe Brannoc believes it. That's harder to fight than any belief of my own."

Sable found herself watching Ines across the fire. He sat slightly apart, as he always did, sharpening a knife that was already sharp, and the light moved on his face and made him look briefly, unbearably young. He caught her looking and, instead of turning away, held her gaze.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing. You just—you're almost smiling."

"I'm aware of the danger," he said gravely. "I'm monitoring the situation."

Laughter went around the fire like something passed hand to hand, and Sable felt it settle into her chest, warm and foreign. When had she last sat among people laughing? Before the riders came. Before her village learned what she was. Before she learned to measure every room by how quickly she could leave it.

Later, when the others slept, she sat beside Ines at the fire's edge.

"The old woman," she said. "At the ford crossing, before the fight. She grabbed my wrist and said she knew my voice. Said she'd heard it before—thirteen years ago, the night the sky went quiet." She pulled her knees up. "I told you I didn't understand what she meant. I still don't. But Merric keeps looking at me like I'm scripture he half-remembers. And the twins say my song reached Marrowgate before I did. And the river sang four nights before I ever touched it." She looked at him. "What am I, Ines?"

He was quiet a long moment. The fire cracked. On the rampart above, Rell shifted against the stars.

"When I was a child," Ines said at last, "they brought me to the shrine and put the brand on my arm, and the priest told me: you are not the god's servant, you are his *silence*. Where you walk, magic dies. You are the proof that even gods must stop." His jaw tightened. "I believed him. I built my whole life inside that sentence. And then I met a girl whose voice makes rivers sing, and I understood that everything I was told about silence might be a lie, and that the seal they put in me might not be a wall at all." He looked at her. "It might be a dam. And dams don't kill water, Sable. They just hold it back until someone builds a door."

"So I'm the leak."

"You're the sound the dam makes," he said, "when the water starts remembering the sea."

She shivered, though the fire was warm.

"Ines," she said. "The old woman at the ford. She said something else. She said: 'Tell the girl the moon is counting.' Thirteen turns, the herald said in the valley. Thirteen turns until the hunt becomes law." She hugged her knees tighter. "We're running out of moons, aren't we?"

Ines looked up at the sky, where the moon hung fat and pale, one turn shy of fullness. One turn shy of the twelfth.

"One more," he said quietly. "Maybe two. Then everything changes, one way or another." He glanced at her sideways, and there it was again—that ghost of a smile. "So we'd better gather everyone we can."

"Everyone," she repeated.

"Every stray. Every defrocked priest, smuggler, deserter, and old woman who remembers." He poked the fire, sending sparks climbing toward the stars. "Corvane thinks he's hunting a witch. Let him. By the time the thirteenth moon rises, I mean for him to find out he's been hunting a congregation."

Above them, on the dark rampart, Rell suddenly straightened and raised one hand—the Oathbound signal for *riders on the road*. Sable's heart lurched. But then Rell's other hand came up, fingers spread wide, and changed the signal to something Sable had never seen: five fingers, then fist, then five again.

*Allies coming,* Dagny breathed from across the ashes. *Many. Unarmed.*

Down in the black valley below the hillfort, lanterns were flickering to life—a whole procession of them, dozens, swaying up the old pilgrim road toward the gates that hadn't opened in thirteen years. And faintly, on the wind, Sable heard them singing.

It was her song.
