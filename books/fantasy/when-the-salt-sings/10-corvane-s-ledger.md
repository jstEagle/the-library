## 10. Corvane's Ledger

Inquisitor Corvane had a theory about villages: every one of them was a ledger waiting to be opened. The people were the entries. Their silences were the erasures, their small kindnesses the marginal notes that betrayed them more surely than any confession. He did not need fire to read a village. He needed only patience, a comfortable chair, and the particular quality of attention he had spent twenty years honing into something like a blade.

The Salt Valley's fishing hamlet of Greywater offered him its chair willingly enough. It was the reeve's own, dragged from his hearth to the meetinghouse at Corvane's request, and it creaked when he settled into it, which pleased him in a small, private way. A chair that announced its occupant. A room that listened.

He had been in the valley four days.

Four days, and he had already filled half a notebook with entries.

*Widow Tamsen — nets mended for a stranger's child, autumn past. Denies knowing why.*

*Miller Ostrec — paid a tithe twice last season. Once to the crown. Once to no one who would say.*

*Boy at the well — saw riders on the ridgeline three nights before the herald came. Counted six.*

None of it was damning. All of it was *interesting*. That was the thing his subordinates never understood, the young zealots the Office of Inquiry sent him, boys who wanted torches and screaming and the clean arithmetic of ash. Corvane wanted something else. He wanted the moment a lie changed shape — the instant when a falsehood, pressed gently from all sides, revealed itself not as a wall but as a door.

He had found such a door on the second day. It belonged to a woman named Maren Kettle, who dried fish in the sheds down by the waterline, and she had told him, with the flawless sincerity of the truly frightened, that nothing out of the ordinary had come through her sheds in all her years there.

It was the *all her years* that caught him. People who were telling the truth said *nothing much lately*, or *not that I've seen*, or gave him some small irrelevance to hold because they wanted to be helpful. Liars reached for totality. *All my years.* As though a lifetime could be swept under one sentence like dust beneath a door.

So he had gone to the drying sheds himself, alone, without his escort, and he had stood among the racks of salt-stiffened nets breathing air so thick with brine it felt almost solid, and he had noticed two things.

The first was that the salt here was old. Not laid fresh against the walls as wards were laid elsewhere — this valley simply *was* salt, ground into its stones and its soil and, he suspected, its people, generation upon generation of it, until the whole place had become one vast dead zone where the gifted could pass unnoticed and unharmed. Clever country. He could see why the crown had historically left it alone. Nothing to hunt in a field where nothing grew.

The second was a patch of floor, near the back wall, where the dust had been disturbed in a pattern too wide for a rat and too shallow for work.

Someone had hidden there. Recently. Someone small enough to fold beneath the floorboards and still enough to leave only that pale ghost of presence behind.

And someone else had known, and had said nothing, and had walked out of these sheds wearing the face of an honest man.

Corvane had closed his notebook then, slowly, and smiled at the empty shed, and felt the particular pleasure he imagined a scholar felt when a cipher finally yielded — not triumph exactly, but the deep settling click of a mechanism engaging.

* * *

The reeve of Greywater was a soft-bellied man named Doss who sweated even in the cold meetinghouse and whose eyes went, always, to the same spot on the far wall whenever a hard question arrived. Corvane had catalogued the habit within minutes. It was a kind of gift. Most subjects made him work for their tells; Doss hung his out like laundry.

"Tell me about your mercenaries," Corvane said, on the fifth morning, folding his hands atop his ledger. "The Oathbound. How long have they held contract here?"

"Eleven years." Sweat. Eyes to the wall. "Good men. Quiet. They keep the roads and see the caravans safe over the stones."

"Eleven years is a long contract."

"The valley pays well enough. Salt trade's steady."

"And their captain? This Brannoc?"

"A fair man. Strict." A hesitation, thin as onion skin. "Fair."

"And Hartwell?"

There it was. The name landed in the room like a stone dropped into a still pond, and Corvane watched the ripples move outward through the reeve's face — the blink a half-beat too long, the throat working once, the eyes flicking not to the wall now but to the window, northward, toward the cluster of low cottages at the valley's edge where the Oathbound kept their quarters.

"What of him?" Doss asked.

"He was the one who reported the disturbance at the drying sheds, I understand. Found nothing. Filed a quiet account with your clerk." Corvane turned a page he did not need to turn. "A diligent man."

"Aye. Diligent."

"Has he ever been anything less?"

The silence lasted four seconds. Corvane counted them the way a miser counts coins. Four seconds was an eternity in interrogation; most lies collapsed inside two.

"No," said Doss. "Never."

"Curious," Corvane said pleasantly, and let it sit there between them, growing, until the reeve began explaining things Corvane hadn't asked about — the harvest, the tithe, the excellent conduct of the company — and Corvane nodded along like a man listening to a favorite song.

That evening he walked out to the border stones himself.

They rose from the earth in a broken crescent along the valley's northern rim, ancient things, older than the crown, older than any kingdom he served, their surfaces furred with salt crystal that glittered dully in the last light. He had read the archives' speculation about them — natural formations, said the geographers; wardworks of the god-war, insisted the older scholars; both wrong, probably, and both right, in the way of things that predated the categories men used to contain them. He laid his palm flat against one stone and felt nothing. No hum. No residue. Whatever power had raised these markers, it was finished with them, and what remained was pure negation: a line drawn across the world beyond which magic simply stopped.

Which meant, he thought, tracing the crescent with his eyes toward the darkening hills, that anyone fleeing the hunt with a living gift would run *toward* this valley, not away from it. The stupid ones ran to cities and crowds. The clever ones ran to the one place in the world where their curse went silent.

And someone in this valley had been expecting them.

* * *

The crown archive at Vhal Morast was three hundred leagues away, but Corvane carried its most relevant shelf in his head. Twenty years of inquiry taught a man to travel light and remember everything, and what he remembered now, sitting by lamplight in the borrowed office of the valley's garrison, was a filing code he had seen exactly once, eleven years ago, when he had been a junior inquisitor assigned to inventory the seized papers of a burned hedge-coven in the Marches.

Most of that material had been garbage. Charms against toothache. Love-sigils drawn by farm girls. But one folio had been different — older, in a hand that trembled with either age or awe — and it had described, in the manner of a legend half-remembered, the thirteen families who had ended the god-war.

Not defeated the gods. *Ended* the war. There was a difference, and the folio had been very clear about the difference, in its cracked, reverent script. The gods had not been killed; killing a god was like killing the sea. They had been *sealed* — bound into silence by mortal voices sworn into mortal blood, oath-lines running father to son, mother to daughter, down through generations like veins of ore. The seal required keepers. The keepers required oaths. And the oaths, the folio said, were written into the body: brands, sigils, marks that could not be washed away because they were not on the skin but *in* it.

One name had recurred through the document more than any other, attached to the greatest of the sealing lines, the family said to have spoken the binding at the drowned temple itself.

Hartwell.

Corvane had noted it at the time with a junior's diligence and a junior's indifference — one name among dozens in a folio of legends — and filed the memory away where he filed everything, in that vast cold archive behind his eyes.

Now he sat with his own notebooks open around him like a spread of cards, and he dealt the memory out onto the table beside them, and he looked at it properly for the first time.

Hartwell. An Oathbound mercenary, eleven years in a salt valley — eleven years, almost to the season since the god-war's last embers were formally declared extinguished by royal decree. A man who had served faithfully and quietly in the one place in the world designed to smother magic. Who had found signs of a fugitive in the drying sheds and reported *nothing*. Who bore, if the records of his company were accurate — and Corvane had sent for them, and they would arrive — the oath-brand of Vess on his forearm.

Vess. The Silent God. The one whose seal had held while others frayed, whose temple drowned, whose voice had not been heard in thirteen years.

Thirteen years.

And now, in this same season, a girl comes up out of the sea into this same valley, carrying a gift that makes things grow, that makes birdsong bend light — a girl whose power, by every report filtering up the ridgeline, sounded less like witchcraft and more like *echo*. Like something lost, leaking back into the world through whatever cracks time had worn in the great seal.

Corvane rose from the desk and stood a long moment at the window, looking north, though the night hid everything beyond the lantern-light of the compound.

He had gone into the Office of Inquiry believing, as all its initiates believed, that the hunt was about safety. Keep the flame from the hay, protect the harvest of ordinary lives. But twenty years had refined that belief as fire refines metal, burning away the soft impurities until only the true ore remained, and the truth he had arrived at was harder and cleaner: magic was not a danger to the realm. Magic was an *insult* to it. Every hedge-charm, every whispered cantrip, every god's lingering breath in the world was a declaration that mankind's order was provisional — that above the crown stood powers that had never signed any covenant, that could unmake with a syllable everything law and labor had built. The hunt was not pest control. It was a war of legitimacy, fought one pyre at a time, and Corvane intended to win it absolutely. Not suppress magic. End it. Close the book entirely, so thoroughly that within three generations children would learn the word *witch* the way they learned the word *dragon*: as a story, a scare, a thing that had never truly been.

And he had thought that ending would take his lifetime and his successors' lifetimes, a slow strangulation of sparks.

But if the seal on Vess ran through a man — one man, one voice, one brandable arm —

Then magic was not a sea after all. It was a river, and rivers had sources, and sources could be dammed.

Or drained.

Corvane returned to the desk and drew a fresh page toward him. His pen moved in the small, precise hand that clerks mistook for modesty and that was in fact the handwriting of a man who never wasted a stroke.

*Subject: HARTWELL, Ines. Mercenary, Oathbound Company. Suspected concealment of fugitive (female, approx. 16-19, gift-class unknown, possibly higher).*

Beneath it, after a pause:

*Probable descent: Sealer-line of Vess. Significance: potentially terminal.*

He considered the word *terminal*, liked it, left it.

The company muster-roll arrived two days later, carried up the ridgeline by a courier who did not know what he brought. Corvane read it by the same lamp, alone, and found what he expected to find: Hartwell sworn in at nineteen, sponsor Brannoc, oath administered at the shrine below the western fell — and, entered in the margin in an archivist's faded ink, the traditional notation reserved for men of sealed blood who took the gray cloak.

*Oath-brand confirmed at induction. Sigil: the Closed Mouth.*

The Closed Mouth. The mark of the line that had silenced a god.

Corvane closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and allowed himself, for the space of ten breaths, to feel the full weight of what he was holding. Then the feeling passed through him and was gone, the way weather passes through stone, leaving the stone colder and more itself.

They had fled north, the mercenary and the girl. The tracks said north; the frightened villagers' averted eyes said north; the shape of the thing said north. North lay the high passes, the hinterlands, and beyond them, if the old charts were honest, the coast road to the drowned temple — the very anchor-point of the seal, the place where the Closed Mouth had first been spoken and where, if his rising suspicion was correct, it might someday be unspoken.

Let them run there. Let the girl's cursed echo grow louder as they went, drawing every gifted wretch out of hiding like moths to a lantern. Let the mercenary believe distance meant safety.

Corvane would follow at his leisure, with writs and soldiers and fire, and when the moon completed its thirteenth turn — the heralds had proclaimed it, the countdown was already in every mouth from here to the capital — he would stand in that drowned temple among the roots of the oldest magic in the world, and he would take from Ines Hartwell the one thing no sealer had ever surrendered.

His voice.

He snuffed the lamp himself. Outside, the valley lay silver and silent under the rising moon, and somewhere in that silence, he was certain, the god was listening — and did not yet know that help, terrible and orderly and utterly without mercy, was already on the road.
