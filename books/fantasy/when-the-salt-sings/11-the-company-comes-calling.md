## 11. The Company Comes Calling

They smelled the river before they saw it—that green breath of willow and wet stone that no salt valley ever gives you. Three days out from the ridge country, the land had begun to soften around them like a fist unclenching: grass taller, soil darker, and at last, threading through a cut in the hills, the Vael, wide and brown and patient, running toward the sea with all the indifference of water that has never been asked to choose sides.

Ines knelt at its edge and cupped a handful to his mouth. It tasted of earth and iron and distance.

"Is it safe?" Sable crouched beside him, hood back, her hair still carrying the pale streaks the frost-flowers had left when they bloomed through it on their first night beyond the stones. She'd stopped trying to hide them. He wasn't sure she could anymore.

"It's a river, not a witch." He let the water run through his fingers. "It doesn't care what you are."

"That's not what I asked."

He almost smiled. Eleven years of brotherhood had taught him to read men; four weeks of this girl had taught him to be read. "The ford is half a mile downstream. Old crossing, used by drovers and smugglers both. We cross by dark, sleep on the far bank, and by midday we're in country where even Corvane's riders move slow." He paused. "If anyone's behind us, the river buys us time."

"Is anyone behind us?"

He looked at her—sixteen years old, hollow-cheeked from hard travel, with a voice sleeping under her ribs that could make dead wood blossom—and decided, as he had decided every morning for a month, that she deserved the truth more than comfort.

"I don't know," he said. "But if Brannoc is coming, he'll come to the ford. It's the way I would come."

* * *

They crossed at dusk, when the light went copper and the water turned to hammered bronze. The twins—Dara and Devlin, who had joined them after the temple road, all sharp grins and sharper knives—went first, testing the shallows with long staves. The hedge-priest's death at the bridge two weeks past had taught them all the cost of chokepoints, and Ines watched each of them take the crossing seriously, faces set, voices dropped to nothing.

Sable waded last, Ines a step ahead of her, his hand finding hers when the current shoved at her knees. The water was cold enough to ache. On the far bank, the willows stood thick and black against the dying sky, and the ground rose into meadowland dotted with sheep-folds gone derelict since the war.

They made camp in the willows without fire. Dara took first watch on the bank, watching the way they'd come—the far shore dimming, the hills behind it going blue and then black.

Ines didn't sleep. He sat with his back against a willow trunk and cleaned his sword by touch alone, an old ritual, the whetstone whispering along steel in long slow strokes. Thirteen years of mercenary work had put a hundred notches of memory in that blade, and every one of them belonged to a stranger. The cuts coming tomorrow, if they came, would not.

"You're sharpening it wrong," Sable said quietly, from somewhere near his shoulder. She couldn't see in the dark any better than he could. She just knew him now, the way you come to know a sound in another room.

"There's no wrong way to sharpen a sword."

"There's a wrong reason." A pause. The river talked to itself among the stones. "You're hoping it won't matter. You're sharpening so you have something to do with your hands instead of your heart."

Ines set down the blade. "When did you get wise?"

"The day I stopped being able to lie to myself about what my voice does." She settled beside him, small and warm in the willow-dark. "It's Brannoc, isn't it. The one who gave you the night's head start."

"He did."

"And he's sorry for it."

"He is. That's what makes it worse." Ines stared out at the river, where moonlight was beginning to lay its first silver seams across the current. "A man who betrays you out of hate, you can fight. A man who betrays you out of love—" He stopped. Started again. "Brannoc swore beside me at the shrine. Same hour, same blood, same god. When I speak the binding words someday—if I ever do—he's the one I hear standing next to me. And he chose the contract anyway. Because forty men eat on Oathbound coin, and contracts are how we've stayed alive since before either of us could grow a beard."

"Do you hate him?"

"No." The word came out of him like a splinter. "That's the whole curse of it. I understand him exactly. That's why I can't hate him. You can only hate what you don't understand, or refuse to."

Sable said nothing for a while. Then: "My mother understood the men who burned our village. She told me once, the night before she sent me downriver. She said, *they think they're putting out a fire, little bird. Nobody hates water.*" Her hand found his sleeve. "Understanding them didn't save her."

"No," Ines agreed softly. "It never does."

From the far bank, faint but unmistakable, came the cry of a nightbird—twice, then silence. Then twice again.

Dara's voice, low and tight, drifted back through the willows: "Riders. East ridgeline. Torches doused, moving in column." A beat. "Oathbound column spacing. I'd know it drunk and blindfolded."

Ines was already on his feet.

* * *

There were eight of them. He counted torchlight ghosts moving down through the sheep-folds on the opposite bank, horses walking, unhurried—which was worse than a charge, because an unhurried enemy believes he has already won. Ines gathered the others deep in the willows and laid it out in a whisper.

"We go upstream along the far bank, find a crossing above the shallows, put the whole night between us and them—"

"They've seen our fire-smoke from yesterday," Devlin said. "That's how they tracked us. They know where we slept. They don't need to chase us upriver; they just need to hold the ford and wait for us to get hungry."

"Then we go downstream, away from them—"

"Ines." Sable touched his arm. "Look."

He looked. On the far bank, the column had halted at the water's edge, and a single rider came forward alone, out onto the shingle bar in the middle of the river where the ford ran shallowest. The moon was high enough now to give him shape: broad-shouldered, heavy in the saddle, a man built like a fortress gate. Even at fifty yards, Ines knew the set of those shoulders the way he knew his own hands.

Brannoc carried a lantern raised high in one fist. With the other, he held something white—a strip of cloth, tied to a spear haft, held up like an offering.

"A parley," Dara breathed. "That's either honest or it's a knife with manners."

"It's honest," Ines said. His mouth had gone dry. "He wouldn't waste the cloth otherwise. He knows I'd see through it."

"And if it's a trap?"

"Then it's a trap with Brannoc in the middle of it, and he's too clever for that." Ines slung his shield across his back, deliberately emptying his hands, and pulled off his sword belt. He hesitated—one heartbeat, looking at it—then handed it to Sable. "Hold this. If I raise my right hand flat, palm out, bring it to me fast."

"What are you going to do?"

"What I should have done at the shrine, maybe." He stepped out of the willows. "Talk."

* * *

The river came to his thighs at the deepest channel, cold as a confession, dragging at him like the past. He walked out onto the shingle bar with the water streaming from his legs and Brannoc waiting ten feet away, lantern swinging gently, throwing gold light up across a face Ines had trusted with his life a hundred times.

Up close, Brannoc looked older than thirteen days should allow. Grey in the beard that hadn't been there a month ago. Eyes bruised underneath with sleeplessness.

"Halloa, brother," Brannoc said. His voice was steady, but only just. "You look like shit."

"You always were the handsome one."

Something moved through Brannoc's face—not quite a smile, not quite grief, some animal caught between dens. "I gave you one night. Corvane's given me eight days. Do the arithmetic of that, Ines. I've been riding at a walk for three days hoping you'd be gone. Hoping I'd ride down to this ford and find nothing but cold ash and a story." He lowered the lantern. "You're still here."

"The girl needed rest. There was sickness in the group. We lost the priest at the bridge—you'd have heard."

"I heard." Brannoc closed his eyes briefly. "Old Tammet. Gods. He christened my daughter."

"He buried mine." The words came out flat and true, and Brannoc flinched as if struck. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the river filled the silence, talking its endless talk.

"Why are you here, Brannoc? Truly. Not the answer you'll write in the company ledger. The one you'll carry to your grave."

Brannoc's jaw worked. "Eight swords on the bank behind me. Maren, Cott, the Voss brothers, four others. All sworn. All loyal—to the contract, to the company, to the crown that pays us now. Corvane wants the girl alive. Wants you alive too, if it can be managed. Dead if it can't." He looked down at the water swirling around their legs. "I'm supposed to offer you terms. Surrender her, come home, stand trial before the guild tribunal. They'll restore your name. Your pay. Your bunk. Everything except your honor, which you'll notice isn't on the list of things they're offering back."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then at dawn we come across, and I do my duty." Brannoc's voice cracked, just once, like ice under a boot. "Ines, listen to me. Listen. I've seen Corvane's ledgers. What he means to build—a world with no gift in it at all, burned clean, salted forever. Forty years I've killed for coin and called it a living, and I never once lost sleep, because the men on the other end of my sword were men. But this—" He shook his head slowly. "This isn't a contract. This is a pogrom with a seal on it. And I signed."

"Then don't cross at dawn."

"Don't ask me that." It came out raw, almost a shout, strangled down to a whisper. "Don't you dare ask me that. I have thirty-two men who follow me because I keep us alive. Wives. Children. A winter store in Marrowgate. If I break contract, every guild in the free cities closes to us. We starve respectable. You think I haven't done that sum a thousand times on this road?" He dragged a hand down his face. "You found something worth breaking for. Good for you. I'm glad. Genuinely—I am glad, Ines. But I found forty reasons to stay whole, and I love them more than I love being right."

"So that's it." Ines felt something in his chest tearing along a seam that had been splitting for weeks. "One night of mercy and then the axe."

"One night of mercy and then my *duty*, gods rot you." Brannoc's eyes were bright and terrible in the lantern light. "Do you know what the worst part is? At the shrine, when we were seventeen, and the priest cut our palms and pressed them together over the stone—do you remember what you said? You said, *whatever comes, we stand together.* Whatever comes. Those were your words, Ines Hartwell. So whatever comes at dawn—" His voice failed him entirely for a moment. When it returned it was iron wrapped in wool. "Stand ready."

He turned his horse and walked it back toward the far bank, and the lantern light shrank and shrank until it was one gold spark among the sheep-folds, and then it was gone, and Ines stood alone on the shingle bar with the river pulling at him from both directions and nowhere left to stand at all.

* * *

They didn't run. Running was what prey did, and besides—Devlin was right—they'd be run down in open country inside a day. Instead Ines spent the black hours before dawn doing the thing he hated most: preparing to fight his family.

He positioned them well. The willows gave cover; the river gave warning; the shingle bar gave a killing ground he intended never to use. Sable he placed twenty yards upstream in the deepest shadow, with Dara beside her—not as a guard, Ines told himself, though they both knew better—but the girl had gripped his wrist before he left her and said, "Don't die being noble. Noble is just dead with better eulogies," and he had laughed, actually laughed, at the worst hour of his life, because she was right.

Dawn came grey and cold. Mist stood on the river like the ghosts of everyone who had ever drowned there. Out of the mist, at first light precisely—because Brannoc was nothing if not precise—the Oathbound began to cross.

Eight of them, in file, shields slung, swords drawn but points low. A war-party's discipline wearing a funeral's pace. Ines stood at the water's edge on his own bank, unarmed, hands open at his sides, and waited.

Maren reached the shallows first, then Cott, then the Voss brothers—big twins, bearded mirror images, men Ines had drunk with, bled with, carried wounded on his own back through the Kestrel Pass. They saw him standing there empty-handed and faltered, just slightly, the file bunching like a startled flock.

"Company halt." Brannoc's voice rolled across the water. He came last, wading out of the mist with his sword bare and his face carved from something harder than flesh. "Ines. Step aside from the willows. We take the girl and go. No one needs to bleed today."

"No one needs to," Ines agreed. "But someone will, because you brought eight swords to a conversation."

"Step aside, brother."

"I can't." He spread his arms, deliberately, terribly, making himself the only thing between the crossing point and everything he loved. "And you know I can't. You've known since the shrine. You swore the same oath I did, Brannoc—to stand between the powerless and the fire. Every word of it. You just decided the crown's coin outranks the god's silence, and I decided it doesn't, and here we are."

"Last chance." Brannoc's sword came up. Behind him, six blades rose in ragged echo, and Ines saw it plainly in Maren's eyes—the hesitation, the horror, the desperate wish that Ines would simply move. "Step aside or be moved."

"Come and find out how."

What happened next happened fast and ugly, the way real fighting always does—nothing like the ballads, all mud and screaming and short brutal distances. Brannoc came on first, because someone had to, because his men would not advance while he hung back, and Ines met him bare-handed, turning the flat of the first cut with his vambrace, taking the second along the outside of his thigh because he would not—would *not*—draw blood from these men, would not give them a single wound to avenge.

Cott's pommel cracked across his cheekbone and the world rang white. He stayed on his feet. Someone shouted his name—it might have been Maren, it might have been God. The Voss brothers took his arms and he could have broken free, could have taken Tam's throat in his teeth, but instead he let them hold him and said, into the mist, "Brothers. Brothers, look at me. Look at what you're doing."

"Shut up," Tam whispered, and his hands were shaking. "Shut up, shut up, just let us—"

And then Sable sang.

She didn't mean to. Ines understood that later, piecing it together—understood that the sight of him bleeding in his brothers' grip had torn something loose in her that no caution could hold. But in the moment there was only the sound: rising out of the willows like the dawn itself made audible, a note first, then a chord, then a *voice*, vast and young and grieving, a voice with a god's sorrow folded inside a girl's fury. The mist on the river shivered. The water flattened as if pressed by a great palm.

The ford broke.

Not eroded—*shattered*, the shingle bar heaving upward in a groaning arc, boulders rolling like frightened cattle, the whole crossing convulsing as the river remembered it was stronger than anything men had built upon it. The Oathbound scattered, swept off their feet, Brannoc plunging chest-deep into the flood with his sword spinning away glinting into the brown water. Ines tore free of the Voss brothers' suddenly nerveless grip and grabbed Tam by the collar as the current took him, hauling his head above water, holding him against the drag of the river with arms that had carried him before and would carry him again.

"Hold on," Ines snarled at him, spitting river-water and blood from his split cheek. "Hold on to me, you great idiot. I've got you. I've *got* you."

The song faded. The river gentled, sated, and the ford was simply gone—a chaos of white water and displaced stone where a crossing had been for three hundred years. Eight Oathbound staggered onto the banks, upstream and down, weaponless or witless or both.

On the near bank, Maren stood dripping, staring at Sable emerging pale from the willows with Dara's steadying hand on her shoulder—and at the flowers that had come up through the mud wherever her bare feet had fallen, impossibly, absurdly, white blossoms opening in the cold dawn like the world apologizing.

Maren unbuckled her sword belt and let it fall into the shallows.

"No," she said, to no one, to everyone. Her voice shook. "No, I'm done. I burned a village in the Ashlands for Corvane's writ. I told myself it was orders. I will not tell myself that again." She looked at Ines—at his bleeding face, his empty hands, the girl with flowers growing in her footprints—and something in her soldier's posture finally, fully broke and reformed into something else. "Wherever you're going, brother, I'm going there."

And Cott, knee-deep in the wrecked ford, laughed—a wild, cracked, disbelieving laugh—and threw his own sword after Maren's.

* * *

Brannoc stood on the far bank with the water streaming off him, alone amid his scattered command, and for a long moment nobody spoke. The river roared where the ford had been. The white blossoms nodded in the dawn wind.

"Two of mine," Brannoc said at last, and his voice was gravel and grief. "You cost me two of mine, Ines."

"I didn't cost you anything." Ines limped to the water's edge, thigh burning, cheek throbbing, heart worse than both. "Corvane cost you. The contract cost you. The world you keep choosing cost you." He held out his hand across the impossible water, across the ruin of the crossing, across thirteen years. "It's not too late, Brannoc. It's never too late until it's done. Come with us. Stand with us. Be what you swore to be at the shrine, before the coin got louder than the god."

For one suspended heartbeat—Ines would swear it all his life—he saw his brother waver. Saw the choice hang in the air between them like mist.

Then Brannoc straightened, and the wall came back up, and he became the captain again, the company man, the man who kept forty people alive by keeping his heart in a strongbox.

"No," he said quietly. "But I'll tell you this, and it's the last mercy I have left in me." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a scrap of oilcloth-wrapped paper, weighed with a pebble, and hurled it across the river. It landed short, in the shallows, and Dara waded out and retrieved it. "Corvane's not behind me anymore. He rode ahead three days ago—straight south, for the drowned temple. He knows what the thirteenth moon means. He knows what you are, Ines. What your line is. He means to be standing on that altar when the moon rises, with enough fire to burn the god's anchor out of the world and everyone in it." Brannoc mounted his dripping horse. "You're not running from the hunt anymore, brother. You're racing it. And you're three days behind."

He wheeled the horse and led his diminished command up into the hills, and Ines stood on the broken bank with the paper trembling in his hand and the blood drying on his face, and read the single line in Brannoc's blunt soldier's script:

*The twelfth moon rises in nine days. Ride.*

Behind him, Sable touched his arm, gentle as the flowers at her feet.

"Then we ride," she said.
