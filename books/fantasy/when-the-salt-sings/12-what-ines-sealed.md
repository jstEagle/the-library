## 12. What Ines Sealed

The healing fire was small, the kind of fire you made when you wanted warmth but not witnesses.

Sable sat closest to it, because her hands still shook. The river ford had been two days ago and the cold of it had not left her bones; it lived in her knuckles now, in the hinge of her jaw, a gray ache that no amount of wool seemed to reach. Across the flames, Maren the hedge-priest's widow-sister—no, that wasn't right, Maren was dead at the bridge, this was Odile the old woman who remembered the god-war—stirred a clay pot of willow bark and comfrey, and the smell of it rose sweet and bitter together into the dark.

The twins were asleep, or pretending to be, curled back-to-back against the fallen cedar like two spoons in a drawer. Tam's arm was splinted. Tor had not spoken since the ford except to say *thank you* once, in a voice like a cracked bell, when Sable's song had pulled him out of the water before it could pull him under.

And Ines sat apart from all of them, as he always did, on the far side of the fire where the light couldn't quite decide whether to claim him. He was cleaning his sword with the slow, methodical patience of a man who had done ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more, and he was doing it wrong—or rather, he was doing it without seeing it, his whetstone moving over steel while his eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance where nothing lived.

Sable had learned to read him the way she'd learned to read weather, in fragments and pressure changes. She knew the set of his shoulders when he was counting riders on a ridgeline. She knew the particular stillness of his hands when Brannoc's name came up. And she knew this: the blank stare, the stone moving on steel, the silence so heavy it bent the firelight around him.

He had been like this since the ford. Since she had sung the river apart and drowned the fight and felt, for one terrible bright instant, something answer her from inside *him*.

"You're going to wear a groove in that blade," she said.

Ines blinked. Looked down at his hands as if surprised to find them attached to him. "It wants cleaning."

"It wanted cleaning an hour ago." She wrapped her arms around her knees. The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, a nightjar called its three-note question and got no answer. "You've been staring through me for two days. Through all of us. Like we're windows and there's something on the other side you don't want to look at."

Odile, ladling willow-bark tea into wooden cups, said nothing. But Sable caught the old woman's glance—a quick flick toward Ines, then away—and understood that Odile saw it too, and had perhaps seen it before, in other men, in other wars, and knew better than either of them what came next.

"Drink your tea," Ines said.

"I'm not thirsty."

"Drink it anyway. You took river water into your lungs. I heard it."

She drank it anyway, mostly to spite him, which was a strange reason to do anything but had become, over these weeks of road and frost and flight, one of their private languages. The tea was bitter enough to make her eyes water. When she lowered the cup, he was watching her—not through her now, but *at* her, and there was something in his face that made her heart stumble. Something that looked like a man standing at the edge of a high place and deciding, finally, to jump.

"Sable," he said.

"Ines."

He set down the sword. Set down the whetstone beside it with great care, the way a man lays aside a tool he is not sure he deserves to pick up again. "Do you know why your gift frightens me?"

She did not answer. Her gift frightened everyone. That was the shape of the world now: edicts nailed to doors, names written into ledgers, pyres stacked in market squares. A voice that made things grow was not a wonder anymore. It was a crime awaiting discovery.

But he shook his head before she could speak. "No. Not the way you think. It doesn't frighten me because it's dangerous." His jaw worked. "It frightens me because I know it. I know the feel of it. When you sang the ford apart, I felt—" He stopped. Started again. "I have felt that voice before. Thirteen years ago. And I am the reason it went silent."

The nightjar called again. The fire settled into itself, sending up a fistful of sparks that rose and died like brief orange souls.

"What are you talking about?" Sable asked, though some part of her, the part that had grown things in salt and heard gods whispering through her teeth, already knew. Had maybe always known, from the first moment in the drying shed when her gift had flickered alive in a place where magic should have died, and she had wondered, half-delirious with cold, *why does the mute man hum my song back to me?*

Ines looked into the fire. "You asked me once why I know so much about hunted gifts. Why a mercenary sworn to a silent god can read an inquisitor's ledger. Why I knew the sigils on Corvane's writs before he showed them to me." He drew breath, and Sable saw that his hands, those steady killing hands, were trembling. "I'll tell you. But you should understand that after I tell you, you may want to walk into the dark and keep walking. I wouldn't blame you. Odile might. Even the twins might, if they were awake."

"We're awake," said Tor, from the shadows, without opening his eyes.

Tam snorted. So they were both awake. Sable almost laughed, almost cried; the line between had worn thin as wet paper these past days.

Ines waited until the laughter died. Then he began.

* * *

"I was seven years old," he said, "when my mother gave me to the temple of Vess."

The words came slowly, each one handled like something breakable, or like something sharp he didn't want to cut himself on. Sable pulled her blanket tighter and listened.

"Not sold—given. There's a difference, though I couldn't tell you what it was anymore. My mother was a tide-reader in the fishing villages south of here. She said the god had come to her in a dream and asked for a son with a steady heart. She believed it. Or she needed to believe it. Both, probably. They built the temple out over the shallows, did you know that? The drowned temple. It wasn't always drowned. It stood on pilings above the water, and at high tide the sea came up through the floor grates and the whole sanctuary sang. That's what they told me. I remember it singing. I remember thinking the sea had gotten into the stones and the stones had learned to love it."

His voice had gone somewhere far away, and Sable let it go, following.

"The priests of Vess weren't like other temples. We didn't beg. We didn't prophesy. Vess was the god of the hush between waves—the quiet after a storm, the held breath before a confession. Other gods spoke. Vess *listened*. That was the whole of the liturgy: you went into the water-chapel and you were silent, and you listened until you heard the god listening back. Some people never heard it their whole lives. I heard it my first morning. The priest said I had a listener's ear. He sounded proud. He sounded afraid too, though I didn't understand why until much later."

"Why afraid?" Tor asked softly.

"Because a god who listens is a god who remembers everything," Odile said, without looking up from her pot. "And memory is a dangerous thing to hold, child, when the world decides to go to war over what it would rather forget."

"Yes," Ines said. "That." He rubbed the oath-brand at his wrist—the sigil Sable had recognized weeks ago in the cottage, the coiled wave-and-key that appeared in none of the village carvings but in every forbidden story of the god-war. "I grew up in the temple. Swept floors, learned tides, learned the listening. And I learned the last lesson last, because it was only taught to a few: that Vess had a true name, and that the name was not a word but a *weight*, and that speaking it was not calling the god but *binding* him. The name spoken by a willing throat seals. The name spoken by an unwilling throat shatters. That was the oldest law of our order. The sealers' law."

"And someone sealed him," Sable whispered. The firelight swam. "Thirteen years ago. Everyone says the god just... went silent. Fell quiet like a tide going out. Nobody says why."

"Because nobody knows," Ines said. "Except me. And the men who died making sure I'd stay silent about it."

* * *

He told them about the last battle of the god-war, and Sable would carry the telling in her body for the rest of her life, the way you carry a scar.

"They call it the Battle of the Nine Shrines," he said. "History will tell you it was armies—godsworn against crown, miracle against cannon, the old war in its final spasm. And there were armies. But the war ended in one room. The inner sanctum of Vess's own temple, where the god's anchor-stone stood—the stone his presence pooled around like water finding a low place. The crown had a company of sealers. Descendants of the old order, families who'd carried the binding words in their blood for nine generations, waiting for a war to give them excuse. My bloodline was one of them. My mother's dream, my temple upbringing—none of it was accident. The order had been grooming sealer-children for decades, hiding them in plain sight among the faithful. Children with steady hearts. Children who could be carried to the anchor-stone and told to speak."

"You were seven," Sable said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "They used a *child*."

"They used the only child whose ear was true enough to hold the weight of the name without breaking." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd ever heard him make, dry and small as sand. "I remember the sanctum filling with water—the enemy godsworn had broken the seawalls, trying to drown the sealers before we reached the stone. I remember my handler wading with me on his shoulders, holding me above the flood, shouting the syllables at me over the roar. Teaching me my death as we went. And I remember the anchor-stone rising out of the black water, and the god—Vess himself, or the edge of him, the way you see the sun's edge before you see the sun—looking down at me. Looking at a seven-year-old boy being carried to his throat like a knife."

He was silent a long moment. The twins had sat up. Odile had gone still as carved wood. Even the nightjar outside seemed to be listening.

"He could have refused," Ines said. "Everyone knows gods can't be bound unwillingly—that's the law, that's why the sealers needed generations of preparation, consent engineered into consent engineered into consent, a chain of willingness stretching back nine lifetimes so the final link wouldn't snap. My ancestors chose it. My mother dreamed it and gave me gladly. Every hand that passed me down the chain had opened willingly. And at the end of the chain there was me. Seven years old, soaked to the bone, terrified, standing on an anchor-stone in a flooding temple with a god looking at me—and the god *let* me. Do you understand? He could have unmade the whole chain with a thought. Instead he went quiet, and he opened, and he let a child speak his name into him like driving a nail."

"Why?" Tor breathed. "Why would a god let that happen?"

"Because he was tired," Odile said quietly. "Or because he saw what the war would become if he kept fighting. Or because—" she looked at Ines with her old, old eyes— "because he loved us more than he loved winning. Gods are not so different from men in their foolishness."

"I've had thirteen years to guess," Ines said. "I landed on the third one. It's the only one I can live with." He turned his wrists over, showing them the brands. "These aren't punishment marks, whatever I told the reeve. Every sealer carries them. They're the chain-links made visible. As long as my oath holds—as long as I never speak the name again, never sing, never open my throat to anything truer than a battlefield command—the seal holds. The god stays silent. The world stays..." He gestured vaguely at the fire, the dark, the ruined peace of it. "This."

"But it's failing," Sable said.

Everything in her had gone very clear and very cold, the way it did when her gift was near the surface. Because she understood now—she understood with a certainty that felt less like thought than like tide—all of it. The impossible flicker of her power in the salt valley. The way Ines hummed her melodies back without knowing. The way the god's lost voice had found *her*, of all the gifted in the world, and leaked through her mouth in fragments of green and growing.

"It's failing," she said again, steadier. "Thirteen years of cracks in the seal. And the echo leaks out along the cracks. Into me. I'm not Vess's voice because the god chose me. I'm his voice because you're leaking, Ines. I'm the sound coming through the hole in you."

The words fell into the fire and burned there. Ines did not flinch, but she saw the flinch arrive everywhere else in his body—in the tendons of his neck, the clench of his jaw, the way his hands closed on his knees.

"Yes," he said.

"And the thirteenth turn of the moon. The herald's count. The hunt becomes law when the moon completes its thirteenth turn—do you think that's a coincidence? The seal was laid on a moon-turn, Ines. Seals laid on a count fail on the count. The crown isn't hunting the gifted because we're dangerous. The crown is hunting us because *someone knows the seal is about to break*, and they're trying to burn every living echo before it does."

For the first time since she'd known him, Ines Hartwell looked genuinely afraid. Not of riders, not of blades, not of burning. Afraid the way a man is afraid when the ground under his longest-held belief begins, gently, to tilt.

"Corvane," he said. "His ledger. His archives. He's not an inquisitor, he's a *counter*. Counting echoes, counting moons." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I swore an oath at eight years old that I would guard the silence with my life. I've kept it eleven years as a soldier. I killed men who sang. I reported a hedgewife in Marlow Fen and watched them take her, and I told myself it was duty, it was the chain, it was keeping the god asleep so the world wouldn't burn again. Eleven years I told myself that."

"And now?" Sable asked.

He lowered his hands. In the firelight his face was stripped bare, younger than his years, older than his grief, and Sable thought: *there he is. The boy on the anchor-stone. Still standing there.*

"Now the girl the echo leaks through is sitting across my fire," he said, "and she is the bravest person I have ever met, and the crown wants to burn her, and the god I helped bury is whispering through her teeth, and I don't know anymore which silence is mercy and which silence is just a longer way of killing." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. He didn't try to hide it. That, more than any confession, undid her.

* * *

Sable got up. Crossed the space between their sides of the fire. Sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and felt him go rigid—not pulling away, just braced, a man expecting a blow he believed he deserved.

She took his wrist. Turned it over. Looked at the brand, the coiled wave-and-key, the chain-link written in scar tissue on a child's skin by adults who called it holy.

"When my gift first woke," she said, "I was nine. I sang to a dying apple tree behind our house because I couldn't stand watching it die, and it bloomed. Out of season. Covered in blossom in the frost. And my father looked at it, and then at me, and his face—" she swallowed— "his face was the exact face you're wearing now. Like he was looking at something he loved and something that would destroy him and couldn't find the line between them. He reported me within the week. First edict, first ledger, my name in his own handwriting. I spent four years running because the man who raised me chose the law over me."

"Sable—"

"Let me finish. I'm not telling you this to wound you. I'm telling you because I know what shame looks like from the inside, and yours is wrong-shaped." She tightened her grip on his wrist, over the brand. "You were *seven*. They put the weight of a god's silence in a seven-year-old's mouth and called it destiny, and you have spent your whole life carrying it and calling the carrying duty. That's not your sin, Ines. That's theirs. All of it—the war, the seal, the hedgewife in Marlow Fen, every piece of it—they built the machine and put a child at the end of the chain and told him the child was the machine." She looked up at him. "You want to know what I think? I think the god let you seal him because he saw the same thing in you that I saw in the drying shed. Steady heart. Listener's ear. And I think he's been waiting thirteen years, patient as tide, for the boy with the steady heart to grow into a man who could choose differently. Not be forced. *Choose.*"

The fire crackled. Tor and Tam had gone absolutely silent. Odile was smiling faintly at her pot, the smile of a woman watching a prophecy fulfill itself at a campfire instead of a temple, which was, in her opinion, where prophecies belonged.

"And what would choosing differently mean?" Ines asked. His voice was rough. "Breaking the seal? Unleashing him? You've seen what happens when gods fight men. Nine shrines burned. Two kingdoms drowned. The war didn't end because anyone won—it ended because we buried the last combatant."

"No," Sable said. "I've heard him, Ines. Through the cracks. Every night since the twelfth moon started waxing, clearer. And you know what he sounds like? He sounds *tired*, and he sounds sad, and the one thing he pushes against the crack hardest, the one thing he sends through me over and over like a man knocking on a door, is a single thought." She closed her eyes and let it rise, the fragment of the god that lived behind her teeth, and spoke it aloud:

"*Not war. Witness.*"

Silence. The nightjar called. The fire breathed.

"He doesn't want to be freed to fight," Sable said. "He wants to be freed to *testify*. To stand in front of gods and men and tell the truth of what was done to him and by him and for him. Thirteen years of silence, and the only thing he's been holding onto is the chance to say one true thing out loud." She opened her eyes. "But he can't say it. The seal won't break from his side—he agreed to that. It can only break from yours. From the voice that sealed him. Willingly. The way the whole chain was built: willingly, link by link, down to you."

Ines stared at her. Then past her, at the dark beyond the fire, where somewhere east and south the drowned temple stood—or sank—waiting beneath its waters with a thirteenth moon climbing toward it.

"You're asking me to unmake the only vow I've ever kept."

"I'm asking you to make a new one," Sable said. "One you choose with your whole grown heart instead of one they poured into a child. That's not breaking faith, Ines. That's finishing it."

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the tea went cold, that the fire burned down to a red philosophy of coals, that Tam yawned and Tor pretended not to. Then Ines turned his wrist under her fingers, slowly, until the brand faced upward between them, wave and key and chain-link catching the ember-light.

"Thirteen turns," he said at last, so softly it was nearly part of the fire's noise. "When the moon rises full over the temple. If we live to get there—if the ford didn't spend us, if Corvane's ledger hasn't already named me, if my brothers don't put a blade in my back on the road—" He stopped. Breathed. "Then I'll speak it. Not as a sealer. As a man asking forgiveness."

Sable felt something loosen in her chest, something she hadn't known was clenched, and leaned her shoulder harder into his.

It was Odile who broke the stillness, rising stiffly to bank the coals, and it was Odile who paused, mid-motion, with her head cocked toward the eastern darkness like an old bird scenting weather.

"Well," she said. "You'd best hope your legs are faster than your regrets, children. Because unless my bones have forgotten how to count—and they haven't counted wrong in ninety years—" she nodded east, toward the distant glimmer of the sea, toward the drowned temple, toward the thin crescent just clearing the hills, pale and patient and one turn from full— "that's the thirteenth moon coming up. And I don't think we're the only ones watching it rise."
