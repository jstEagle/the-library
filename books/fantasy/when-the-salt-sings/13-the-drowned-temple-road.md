## 13. The Drowned Temple Road

They buried the moon-count in the way travelers do: by watching it.

Ines had learned the habit from his mother, who had learned it from a woman who remembered when the sky was full of gods instead of weather. Now he stood at the treeline above the river road with his face tipped up, counting the bite the shadow had taken out of the waning disc, and did the arithmetic he hated.

Nine nights to the twelfth turn. Then one more turning after that, and the thirteenth moon would rise over the drowned temple, and everything would be decided—by them, if they arrived first; by Corvane, if they did not.

Behind him, the camp stirred in the grey half-light. Ansel was muttering over the fire, coaxing it awake with dry moss and worse jokes. The twins—Wren and Wick, identical down to the scar on their left eyebrows—were arguing over whose turn it was to check the snares, an argument that had run for six days without resolution or casualties. Old Maren sat apart on her folded blanket, as she always did, her milky eyes fixed on the east where the hills rolled down toward the sea.

And Sable stood at the edge of the clearing, her bare feet in the frost, singing so quietly that only Ines—and perhaps whatever slept beneath his oath-brand—could hear.

The grass around her toes had come up green through the white. Little blue flowers opened along the stems like eyes blinking awake.

"You shouldn't spend it," Ines said softly, coming up beside her. "Your voice. Out here it costs you."

"I know." She didn't stop. The song had no words he recognized, but it had a shape he knew the way he knew his own heartbeat—the shape of Vess's silence, given melody at last. "But if I don't let it out a little, it builds up. Like breath held too long. And then—" She flexed her fingers. Somewhere in the canopy above, three birds lifted off in perfect unison, wheeling left on some command no hawk had given them. "Then things get strange."

"They got strange anyway."

"That's your fault," she said, and there was the ghost of a smile in it. "You're the strange one. You just keep it locked up so tight you've forgotten what color it is."

He looked at his wrist, where the oath-brand sat under its wrapping—a circle broken by a vertical line, the sigil of the sealing, burned into him at twelve years old by priests who were mostly dead now. He had worn it for thirteen years without once wondering about its color.

"We move in an hour," he said instead. "The temple's five days hard walking if we take the high fells. Three if we take the causeway through the marsh."

"And the riders?"

"Behind us, I think. A day, maybe less." He met her eyes. They were grey as the valley's salt sky, and they didn't flinch anymore, which was new, and which frightened him more than any rider. "Sable. If it comes to running again—if it comes to leaving someone behind—you go. You hear me? Whatever we are to each other by then, you go."

"No," she said simply, and walked back toward the fire, leaving small green footprints in the frost that closed slowly behind her, like a wound healing in reverse.

* * *

They took the marsh road, because Ines had lied about the fells being faster and everyone knew it.

The causeway was an old thing, older than the god-war, raised stones sinking into black water on either side, the reeds tall enough to swallow a mounted man whole. It was slow going—single file, packs high, Wren probing the mud ahead with a smuggler's pole—but it hid them, and hiding was worth more than speed now. Twice that morning they heard hoofbeats on the far bank and went flat among the reeds while Corvane's outriders passed close enough that Ines could smell the leather oil on their tack. Once, a rider paused at the waterline, scanning the fog, and Ines put his hand over Sable's mouth not because she'd made a sound but because her gift had begun to hum against her teeth like a struck bell, and he felt it through his palm.

When the riders moved on, she pulled his hand away gently. "It likes company," she whispered. "That's all. It gets lonely."

"Everything living does."

Ansel, bringing up the rear, snorted loud enough to startle herons. "Everything living does, he says. Thirteen years a monk of the silent god and he's finally worked out philosophy. Write it down, girl, before he takes it back."

"He can't take back what's true," Maren said from the middle of the line, not turning around. Her voice carried strangely across the water, the way old voices do. "I knew a sealer once. Before the last war. A woman with a brand like yours, mercenary. She used to say the quiet wasn't empty. She said it was a held breath." Maren stopped walking. The reeds hissed. "I have been wondering ever since what happens when the breath lets out."

No one answered that. The causeway groaned under their feet and the fog came down thicker, and they walked.

At midday they reached the bridge.

* * *

It was the only crossing where the causeway met the high ground—a stone span over a channel where the marsh drained toward the sea, wide enough for two riders abreast, and Ines saw the problem of it the same moment Wick did, at the far end, holding up a fist.

Horses. Six of them, picketed beyond the span. A picket post. Corvane hadn't sent riders chasing their trail; he'd sent riders ahead, to sit on the doors.

"Four men visible," Wren breathed, flat in the reeds. "Two on the bridge, two at the fire. Could be more in the trees."

"There will be more in the trees," Ines said. "There always are." He lay in the cold muck and studied the geometry of it, the angles, the distances, the work of twenty years doing its grim arithmetic. Four men. Five of them, and two of those were children and one was eighty if she was a day. "We could wait for dark."

"They'd see our fires," Wick said.

"We could go around," Sable said. "Through the water. I could—my voice, in the water, maybe it would carry us—"

"It would carry every soldier within ten miles straight to us," Ines said. "Out here your voice is a bonfire in a dark field. No." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "There's another way. One man crosses the open reedbed on the flank, makes noise on the far side, draws the picket into the trees. The rest cross the bridge while they're chasing ghosts."

"Reedbed's chest-deep," Wren said. "And freezing."

"And full of eels," said Wick, with feeling.

"And it works," Ines said, "if the man making noise is fast, and quiet, and knows how to vanish after." He looked at Ansel.

The old hedge-priest was already unbuckling his pack.

"No," Ines said.

"You weren't asking me, boy. You were telling me, and hoping I'd argue so you could feel noble about it later." Ansel set his pack down with the care of a man setting down something he meant never to carry again. His vestments—defrocked, threadbare, patched at every elbow—looked suddenly very thin against the fog. "I'm the fastest of us. Don't make that face; I ran parish borders for forty years ahead of tax collectors and bishops both, and neither caught me. And I'm the most expendable, which is the part none of you will say, so I'll say it."

"You're not expendable," Sable said. Her voice cracked on it. Around her feet, in the mud, white flowers burst open—dozens of them, sudden as a gasp. Everyone stared. She stared hardest of all. "I didn't—that wasn't—"

"I know," Ansel said gently. "That's the point, girl. That's exactly the point. Your grief has opinions now. Best not waste it on me." He grinned, and it was the same grin he'd used over every bad joke and burnt supper since Marrowgate, and Ines understood, with a sick lurch, that the old man had been saying goodbye for days. "Get them across. Get her to the temple. And when the god wakes up—" he tapped Ines's wrapped wrist with one crooked finger, "—you tell Him Ansel of the Fen Parishes kept faith longer than any priest with a pulpit ever did. Kept it with a fugitive, a smuggler, a hag, and a fool. Tell Him we were a better congregation than He ever had."

"Ansel—"

"Forty years I preached to empty rooms, Ines. Let me end loud." He turned and slid into the reeds without another word, and the marsh took him the way water takes a coin.

* * *

It worked. That was the horror of it—it worked exactly as planned.

Shouts on the far flank, a crash of reeds, Ansel's voice ringing out in the old liturgical tongue, declaiming scripture at the top of lungs built for empty churches—and the picket broke like a kicked hive, four men, then six, then eight crashing into the trees after a ghost. Ines had them across the bridge in ninety heartbeats, Sable's hand in his, Maren swearing steadily in a language older than the crown, the twins moving with the loose grace of people who had crossed other people's bridges all their lives.

From the far bank, Ansel's voice rose higher, laughing between the verses, drawing them deeper.

Then a horn blew—not the picket's horn. A deeper note, from the ridge beyond the trees. Cavalry horn.

And the shouting changed key.

They heard it happen. There was no other word for it. The sounds of pursuit became the sounds of a kill, brief and businesslike, and then there was a single cry—short, surprised, almost annoyed, a man interrupted mid-sentence—and then nothing but the horn and the horses coming down toward the channel.

"Go," Ines said. "GO."

They ran. The causeway unspooled behind them, and Ines looked back exactly once, because he had promised himself he wouldn't and because he was weak. On the far side of the bridge, torchlight bloomed in the grey day. Riders in crown black. And hanging from the bridge's near post, already, impossibly fast, a body swinging gentle as a bell—thin, defrocked, patched at every elbow.

Sable screamed. The sound tore out of her with her name for it still inside, and the channel water leapt—a wall of black spray standing up like a startled animal—and the bridge stones shivered and cracked from end to end with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

The riders reined back hard. The bridge held, barely, sagging now, a snapped spine.

"Keep going," Maren said, gripping Sable's arm with fingers like roots. "Grief walks forward or it drowns you where you stand. Forward, child. Forward."

They walked forward. Behind them, the flowers Ansel had called up in the reedbed were still blooming—white ones now, miles of them, spreading along the waterline like a procession, like a funeral that the land itself insisted on throwing.

Nobody spoke until dark.

* * *

They made camp in a ruined shepherd's cot high on the fells, past the marsh, past the reach of easy pursuit, and the silence inside the walls was a different animal from the silence outside it.

Wren sat sharpening knives that didn't need sharpening. Wick sat beside her, not arguing about anything, which was how Ines knew the twins were grieving—they only argued when the world was tolerable. Maren had taken the corner by the hearth and was doing something with knots and thread, her hands moving in patterns Ines suspected were prayers in disguise.

Sable hadn't spoken since the bridge.

She sat against the wall with her knees drawn up, staring at her own hands, and around her, in the cracks of the flagstones, a slow ring of white flowers was opening—one by one, patient, unstoppable, the way frost comes, the way rot comes, the way spring comes. Her grief leaking out of her the way light leaks under a door.

Ines sat down beside her. Not touching. Just near.

"He made me laugh," she said finally. Her voice was raw. "First night out of Marrowgate, when I couldn't sleep, he told me the story of the bishop who excommunicated him. It involved a goat. It took him an hour and the goat had a better character arc than most men." Her hands curled. "He believed in this. In us. In the temple, in the covenant, in all of it—he believed it like it was already true. And now he'll never—"

"He knew," Ines said quietly. "On the causeway. He'd known for days. He chose it, Sable. That's not the same thing as losing him."

"Isn't it?"

"No," he admitted. "But it's the only part of it we're allowed to keep."

She turned and looked at him then, and in the hearth-light her eyes were wet and furious and ancient, and Ines thought: *there she is. The echo of a god's voice, wearing a girl's face.* "Then we keep it," she said. "All of it. Every one of them. Brannoc, if it comes to that. Corvane, even—I'll keep him too, I'll keep his memory alive long enough for the world to know exactly what he tried to do." The flowers around her shivered open, all at once, a soft white applause. "We go to the temple. We wake your god. And we testify."

Ines felt the words land somewhere under the brand, in the sealed dark place where his own stolen power coiled like a held breath. *Testify.* Not wage war. Not burn the world clean. Stand up in front of gods and armies and speak the truth of what men had suffered. Ansel would have loved the word. Ansel had died for the sentence it belonged to.

"Wren," he said aloud, rising. "Wick. How far to the temple road from here?"

The twins exchanged a glance—the first coordinated movement they'd made in hours.

"Two days to the coast road," Wren said.

"One, if we don't sleep," said Wick.

"Then we don't sleep," Ines said. "Corvane thinks he's penned us between his riders and the sea. Let him. We'll come down the temple road with the twelfth moon at our backs, and we'll arrive before he does—or we'll arrive loudly enough that it won't matter." He looked around the ruined room, at the smugglers and the crone and the girl crowned in impossible flowers, at the ragged remnant of everything Ansel had believed in. "Five days ago you were strangers I was keeping alive out of habit. Tonight you're the congregation. So—congregation. Rest an hour. Then we walk."

Maren looked up from her knots, and her blind eyes seemed, for a moment, aimed precisely at him.

"The temple road," she said. "You understand what waits on it, sealer's son? The stones remember your voice. When you walk that road, the silence will recognize you."

Outside, the wind came down off the fells carrying a smell no wind should carry this far inland: salt, and deep water, and something older underneath both, patient as sediment.

The twelfth moon was coming. And on the drowned temple road, Ines could already feel it—the seal beneath his skin beginning, very faintly, to hum.
