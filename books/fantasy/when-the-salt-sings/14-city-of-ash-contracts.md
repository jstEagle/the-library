## 14. City of Ash Contracts

Marrowgate did not smell like a city. It smelled like a wound.

Ines had been in ports before—Saltmere in his younger years, the fish-rot sprawl of Cael's Landing—but Marrowgate was something else entirely. It rose out of the tidal flats like a carcass picked clean and reassembled by someone who had only heard descriptions of cities. Ten thousand rooftops of slate and copper, green with sea-verdigris. Streets that were canals half the day and mud the other half. And over all of it, the smoke: tallow-smoke from ten thousand lamps, coal-smoke from the foundries, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable to a nose trained by eleven years of salt air, the smell of burned paper.

They burned contracts here. That was the custom. When a guild died, when a house fell, when an oath was broken so thoroughly that no amendment could save it, the papers went into the great braziers at the Gate of Ash, and the wind carried the char up through the streets until everyone breathed what everyone else had lost.

"Cheerful place," muttered Wren, one of the smuggler twins, as their hired skiff bumped against the pilings of the Saltquay district.

Her brother, Corren, said nothing. He hadn't said much since the ford. Ines had noticed it on the road: the boy laughed at the right moments, made the right jokes, but there was a hollowness behind his eyes now, like a room someone had recently moved out of. The river at the ford had nearly taken him. Sable's voice had saved him, and he had not yet decided how to feel about being saved by something that frightened him.

Sable stood at the rail, hood up, watching the city with an expression Ines couldn't read.

"You don't have to come ashore," he said quietly. "Wren and I can find passage."

"And wait where?" She didn't look at him. "On the boat? With the boatman who's already looked at my hands twice? My hands, Ines." She turned them palm-up, briefly, before pulling her sleeves down. There was still soil under her nails from three days ago, from the night she'd dreamed of rain and woken to find the floor of their inn-room carpeted in white clover. "I'm not staying anywhere alone. Not again."

He didn't argue. Arguing with her about this felt like arguing with the tide.

The hedge-priest's absence walked beside them too. They had all stopped mentioning Aldous by name—the old man who had died on the bridge at Vell's Crossing, buying them twenty minutes with nothing but a stolen inquisitor's tabard and a voice hoarse from blessing things that no longer listened. Twenty minutes. It had been enough. It hadn't felt like enough. Ines caught himself reaching for the man's dry humor at least twice a day, the way you reach for a stair that isn't there in the dark.

The old woman, Maren, came ashore with them because Maren came ashore everywhere. She had survived the god-war's tail end as a child, she claimed, though her memory of it shifted like silt, and she navigated Marrowgate's alleys with the certainty of someone who had either lived here once or lied convincingly about it for sixty years.

"The Ferryman's Rest," she said, tapping Ines's arm. "If anyone sells passage east of the Sound, it's the guild brokers at the Rest. But keep your hood up, girl. Free city means free of the crown's law, not free of its coin. Half the brokers here would sell their own mothers if the price included a title."

"And the other half?" Wren asked.

"They'd want payment first."

* * *

The Ferryman's Rest was everything Maren promised and less. A long hall built on pilings over black water, lit by oil lamps turned low, thick with pipe smoke and the low commerce of people arranging things they didn't want witnessed. Sea-captains in oilskins. Brokers with ledgers chained to their belts. Two women playing knucklebones with stakes that included, at various points, a horse, a debt, and someone's name—which, Ines gathered from the snatches of talk, meant the right to collect on it.

He left Maren and the twins at a corner table with instructions: ask after ships bound past the Drowned Coast, pay no more than half up front, and above all, don't draw attention. Then he took Sable to the far end of the hall, near the kitchen door, where the smoke was thickest and the lamplight weakest.

She sat close enough that her shoulder touched his arm. He didn't move away. Somewhere between the valley and here, that had stopped being a thing either of them commented on.

"There," she murmured. "Grey hull, green trim. The broker talking to the captain keeps glancing at the door every time it opens. Nervous. Nervous means cheap."

"Nervous also means desperate," Ines said. "Desperate captains take crown contracts."

"So does every captain in a hundred miles, probably." Her jaw tightened. "We knew that coming in."

"We did." He watched the door anyway. Habit older than his oath. A mercenary learns to watch doors the way farmers learn to watch clouds, and Ines had spent the last two weeks watching every door in every town between here and the ford, waiting for red tabards, waiting for the particular way Brannoc moved through a crowd—shoulders level, hands loose, patient as a headsman.

The door opened. Cold harbor air rolled in with the smoke, and a group of four men came down the steps into the light.

Ines knew three of them by silhouette alone. Dace, with the crooked set of his left shoulder, healed wrong after Kestrel Ford eight years ago. Ollivan, tall and narrow, who sharpened his knives when he was worried and had worn three blades down to nubs since the company split. And between them—

Brannoc.

His captain looked worse than Ines had ever seen him look, and Brannoc had once fought a fever for nine days while commanding a retreat. There was grey in his beard now that hadn't been there at the ford—not age-grey; grief has its own color. His eyes swept the hall with the flat efficiency of a man doing a job he hated, and Ines had time for exactly one thought—*don't be here for us, let it be anything else*—before those eyes found his across forty feet of smoke and lamplight.

They held. Neither man moved.

Then Brannoc said something to the others, quiet, and the three of them took up positions at the bar with the studied casualness of soldiers blocking exits. And Brannoc crossed the hall toward them, alone.

"Ines." He stopped an arm's length away. Close enough to strike. Far enough to mean he wasn't going to.

"Captain." Ines rose slowly, angling himself so his body sat between Brannoc and Sable without making it obvious. Old choreography. Both of them knew it. Both of them knew the other knew.

"Not your captain anymore. You broke from the company." Brannoc's voice was low, rough as rope. "That makes you freelance. Freelance is a dirty word in this town."

"Is that why you're here? To insult me?"

"I'm here because I bought the contract on finding you before Corvane's men did." Something flickered behind his eyes—shame, maybe, or its well-practiced disguise. "Which means I get to decide how the finding goes."

Sable's hand had found Ines's wrist beneath the table. Her grip was steady. Steadier than his.

"You have one minute," Ines said, "before I remember that we're in a free city, in front of witnesses, and you can't drag me out by the collar."

"Witnesses." Brannoc almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Half the people in this room are on Corvane's ledger already, whether they know it or not. The other half would sell you for the price of a berth." He pulled out the bench across from Sable and sat down uninvited, forearms on the table, and for a moment—in the guttering lamp-light, with the smoke hanging between them—he looked less like a hunter and more like a man visiting a grave.

"One minute," Ines repeated, and sat too.

"I'll take thirty seconds." Brannoc exhaled, slow. "Corvane doesn't want the girl dead anymore, Ines. That's the part nobody tells you. Dead witches make martyrs, and martyrs make trouble. What he wants—" his gaze slid to Sable, and Ines saw her flinch under it, "—is her *registered*. Collared, inked, entered in the rolls. Alive, documented, useful. There's a whole new class of contract for it. He wrote it himself. They're calling them ash contracts, because that's what happens to the ones who refuse."

"I'd rather burn," Sable said. Her voice came out low and even, and Ines felt the table hum faintly under his palms—the salt-pale ghost of her gift, straining even here, even in a city built on water. "Tell him I said so."

"He knows you'd say so. That's why he sent me instead of soldiers." Brannoc rubbed his face with one hand, and for a heartbeat he was just a tired man in his fifties sitting in a bad tavern. "Here's what I came to say. You walk into the registrar's office tomorrow morning. Voluntary. You take the mark, the stipend, the protection detail. Corvane becomes your patron instead of your executioner. And you—" he turned to Ines, and now his eyes were wet, and he didn't bother hiding it, which was somehow worse, "—you get reinstated. Full back-pay. Your name struck from the traitor rolls. Every guild seal restored. The Oathbound take you back, Ines. Brannoc vouches for you personally. You could sleep indoors again. Eat food that isn't scavenged. Stop running."

The words landed the way a blade lands—not all at once, but in stages, each one finding a different soft place.

Indoors. Back-pay. *Brothers.* Eleven years of shared bedrolls and shared blood, of Brannoc teaching him to hold a shield-line and standing vigil beside him the night he swore his oath, of a hundred small loyalties braided so tight he'd mistaken them for his own spine. And here it all was, offered back to him, on the condition that he hand over the girl whose gift was the echo of his god's lost voice—the girl he had lied for, bled for, buried a friend for.

Temptation wearing a friend's face. It was the oldest trick in any ledger, and knowing that didn't stop it from working.

"You've rehearsed this," Ines said quietly.

"Every night since the ford."

"Does it work? The speech?"

"No." Brannoc laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "No, it doesn't work, because you're going to do what you always do, which is the stubborn thing, the thing that gets people killed, the thing that got Aldous killed on that bridge—"

"Aldous chose that."

"Aldous chose it because you asked him to!" The word came out louder than Brannoc wanted; heads turned at the bar; Ollivan shifted. Brannoc lowered his voice to gravel. "You ask, Ines. That's your gift. People follow you into fire and call it their own idea afterward. So I'm asking back. For once. Come home. Give them the girl, and come home."

Silence stretched between them, filled with pipe smoke and the slap of black water against the pilings below.

And then Sable spoke.

"I'll answer," she said, "since you're offering me like a purse of coins and haven't actually asked me." She pushed her hood back. In the dimness her eyes caught the lamplight strangely—Ines had seen it before, the flicker in them, like light through moving water. "Your Corvane wants me registered. Do you know what registration is? Have you seen one done?"

Brannoc's silence was answer enough.

"It's a binding," she said. "A little one. Enough to keep the gift quiet. And a little binding on someone like me isn't little. I'm not carrying a candle-flame, Captain. I'm carrying—" she hesitated, glanced at Ines, and Ines gave her the smallest nod, "—an echo. Of a god. And your inquisitor wants to put a leash on it and hold the handle. Think about what a man like that does with a leash on a god's voice. Think about why he wants it *alive and documented*. And then think about whether you're selling me to safety or selling the world to him."

For the first time, Brannoc looked uncertain. It lasted only a moment, and then the shutters came down—but Ines had seen it. Filed it. A crack in the wall.

"You don't know that," Brannoc said. "You're guessing."

"I'm guessing," Sable agreed softly. "So is he. We're all guessing. The difference is what we're willing to spend while we guess."

Brannoc stood. He straightened his cloak with the mechanical care of a man keeping his hands busy so they wouldn't shake.

"You have until the moon clears the harbor tower," he said. "Two days. After that, the contract passes to Corvane's own men, and I can't soften what they do. I've given you the choice. Guild law says that's more mercy than you're owed."

"Guild law," Ines said, "says a lot of things."

"Yeah." Brannoc's jaw worked. "It does." He turned, took two steps, stopped. Didn't turn back. "The shrine," he said, barely audible. "Thirteen years ago. You remember what the silence sounded like, after you swore? I've been hearing it every night since the ford. I don't know what that means, Ines. But if you figure it out before the moon clears the tower—you know where I drink."

He walked out. The door swung shut behind him, and the cold came in with the harbor smell, and Sable's hand found Ines's again under the table and gripped it hard.

* * *

Brannoc did not go back to his men.

He climbed the outside stair of the Rest to the gallery above the taproom, where the rooms were cheap and the walls were thin, and he sat on the narrow bed in the narrow room with the ledger open on his knees and did not read it.

Ollivan knocked twice—their old signal—and let himself in without waiting. He looked at the ledger, then at Brannoc's face, and whatever he saw there made him sit down on the windowsill and say nothing, which was Ollivan's particular genius: he knew when the talking was over and the deciding had begun.

"He won't take it," Brannoc said finally.

"No."

"He shouldn't take it." Brannoc closed the ledger. Corvane's seal gleamed on the cover, white wax stamped with the crown's wheel-and-flame. "That's the hell of it, Ollivan. I offered him a real thing. Back-pay, his name clean, his brothers. Any sensible man takes it. He'd be safe. The girl would be safe. Fed, housed, collared, but safe. And I sat there wanting him to refuse, and I don't know when that started."

Ollivan was quiet a moment. Down in the taproom, muffled through the floorboards, someone had begun singing—one of the river ballads, tuneless and wandering—and Brannoc realized with a cold turn of his stomach that he recognized the melody. Everyone did, lately. It had spread along the roads like spilled wine, verse by verse, campfire by campfire. The song of the silent god. The song of the god who went quiet thirteen years ago, and the child who sealed him, and the voice that was coming to wake him.

The prisoners sang it in Corvane's camps. Brannoc had heard it drifting from the pens at Pale Camp, a dozen ragged voices in the dark, and had told himself it was nothing, contagion, hysteria. But it was in Marrowgate now. It was in the taverns. It was under the floorboards of the world, rising.

"When we were young," Brannoc said, "and we took contracts, we used to ask who the contract was *for*. Not just who paid. Who benefited. Who got hurt. Remember? Before the guilds standardized everything and the asking became bad form."

"I remember."

"I stopped asking somewhere along the way. Tonight I tried to remember when." He looked out the window, at the harbor lights shivering on black water, at the moon just beginning its climb past the tower. One turn shy of thirteen. "And I couldn't find the moment. That's what frightens me, Ollivan. Not that I changed. That I can't find where."

Ollivan turned the ledger over with one finger, reading the seal upside down. "There's still time to return it unsigned."

"There isn't. Signed contracts pass at moonrise tomorrow. That's the law." Brannoc laughed, short and bitter. "Guild law. Always so tidy about exactly the wrong things."

Downstairs, the song ended. In the silence after it, faintly, from somewhere across the harbor, another voice picked it up.

Brannoc sat very still.

"Get Dace," he said. "Quietly. Tell him to meet me at the shrine of the drowned quarter at low tide. And Ollivan—" he paused, hand on the ledger, feeling the weight of it, feeling the weight of everything it had cost and everything it might yet cost, "—tell him to bring his knives."

Outside, the moon cleared the harbor tower early, silvering the smoke of the city, and in a smoke-filled tavern by the black water, a man who had chosen the contract began, at last and too late and exactly on time, to choose otherwise.
