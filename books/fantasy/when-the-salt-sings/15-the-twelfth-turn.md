## 15. The Twelfth Turn

The moon came up wrong.

Sable felt it before she saw it—felt it the way a dog feels weather in its teeth, the way old sailors feel a storm two days off. She was sitting on the seawall at the edge of the fishing hamlet where they'd sheltered, watching the water go from pewter to ink, when something behind her ribs turned over like a sleeper changing position. Not her heartbeat. Something older and slower, something that beat once for every twelve of hers.

She looked up, and there it was: full, swollen, hanging low over the sound as if the sky itself were leaning down to listen.

"Twelve," she said aloud, and hated how small her voice sounded against all that silver light.

Behind her, in the rented room above the salt-house, Ines was sharpening knives. She could hear the whisper of stone on steel, steady as breathing. It had become the most familiar sound in her world—more familiar than her own name, which she had not heard spoken kindly since the riders came to her mother's village. *Sable.* Even the smugglers only used it half the time; mostly they called her Little Voice or the Songbird or, when they thought she wasn't listening, the God-touched. That last one made her skin crawl now in a way it hadn't a month ago.

Because they were right. And that was the problem.

She pressed her palms flat against the cold seawall stones. Under her hands, the barnacles stirred—not moving, not exactly, but *listening*. Everything listened now. The gulls on the pilings cocked their heads when she hummed. The kelp in the shallows leaned toward her voice like flowers toward sun. Last week she'd sung an old work-song while washing her shirt in the stream and the water had risen up in ribbons around her wrists, braiding itself, trying to make something of itself, and she had screamed and run back to the room and Ines had held her until the shaking stopped.

That was three weeks ago. Since then it had gotten worse. Or better. Depending on who you asked.

Not her. Never her.

* * *

"Ines." She stood in the doorway of their room, lamplight behind her, moonlight ahead of her through the one small window that faced the sea. "It's rising."

He didn't stop sharpening—that was one of the things she loved about him, the way he never treated her fears as emergencies but also never dismissed them—but his eyes lifted, gray and level, taking her measure the way he took the measure of every horizon.

"How bad?"

"I don't know yet." Her hands wouldn't stay still; she folded them, unfolded them, pressed them into her skirt. "It's different this time. Before, when it surged, it was like... like a tide coming in. You could see it coming. You could stand out of its way. This is—" She searched for the word and found one she hated. "This is like someone else walking in my body and me standing at the window watching them do it."

The whetstone stopped.

Ines set down the knife with exaggerated care, the way he did everything that mattered, and crossed the small room to her. He was not a tall man, but he filled space the way a wall fills a doorway—quietly, completely. Up close she could see the oath-brand at his throat in the lamplight, the sigil of Vess rendered in old scar tissue, and beneath it, faint as breath on glass, the hairline crack that had appeared the night he'd pulled power through it at the Sound.

"It spoke?" he asked. "Vess. Did it speak?"

"Not with words. Not yet." She wrapped her arms around herself. "With *wants*. I woke up this morning already singing. I don't mean I started singing—I mean the song was happening and my mouth was just... where it came out. Tam's lullaby. The one the hedge-priest used to sing, the one we sang at his grave." Her voice cracked on the priest's name and she pushed through it, because if she stopped she would drown. "And the whole time I was singing it, I was thinking about the temple. About the drowned road. I've never been there, Ines. I don't know the way. But I could *see* it—the steps going down into black water, the cedar stumps, the bell tower with its roof caved in like a broken eggshell. Like remembering a place I've never been."

Ines was very still. Stillness, she had learned, was his version of shouting.

"And tonight," she went on, quieter, "on the seawall. There was a moment when I couldn't remember my mother's face. My own mother. But I could remember the god-war. Battles I never fought. Names of kings dead four hundred years. They're all just *there*, Ines, sitting in my head like furniture someone moved in while I slept." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, brittle as salt-crust. "What if they keep moving things in? What if one morning there's no room left for me? What if I open my mouth to say my own name and what comes out is—"

"*Sable.*"

Just her name. Nothing else. He said it the way he said everything—without ornament, without comfort's soft lies—and somehow it landed harder than any gentleness could have. She realized she was crying.

"You are not a vessel," he said. "You are not a door or a cup or a pipe. You're a girl who survived drowning, a war, an inquisitor, my cooking, and Tam's theology. Whatever leaks through you—" his hand rose, hesitated, and settled on her shoulder, careful as a man handling a bird with a broken wing, "—it leaks because you let it. Because you have a heart big enough to hold more than your own grief. That's not the same as being consumed."

"But what if it becomes the same?" she whispered. "You've seen what happens. The crown's archives are full of vessels. People who got too close to something old and woke up one day as a mouth with a person's face hung on the front of it. Corvane reads those names out at every burning. He calls it *the fate worse than fire*."

"I know what he calls it."

"He's not wrong, though. That's the worst part. He's a monster, but he's not *wrong*—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I can feel it pressing, Ines. Tonight, on the wall. It wants to come up through me like water through a well-shaft. And some part of me—some tired, frightened, stupid part—*wants to let it.* Because if it's using my mouth, then it's not my fault anymore. If the god speaks, I don't have to decide anything ever again."

The silence after that was long enough to hear the sea turning over below the window.

Then Ines knelt.

She hadn't expected it. He was not a man who knelt easily—she had watched him refuse to kneel to a guild-master in Marrowgate with a blade at his kidney. But he knelt now, on the rough boards of the salt-house floor, so that his eyes were level with hers, and the oath-brand at his throat caught the lamplight, and his voice came out low and absolute.

"Hear me," he said. "I have served a silent god for thirteen years. I gave it my voice when I was a child too young to know what I was giving. I know what it is to be used—I am the seal that keeps it mute. Every word I have not spoken for thirteen years has been a brick in its prison. So believe me when I tell you I understand the temptation of surrender. To let something bigger carry the weight. To stop being the one who decides." His jaw tightened. "And I am telling you, Sable Hartwell-of-nowhere, girl who should have drowned twice over: *no one will use you. Not the crown. Not Corvane. Not Brannoc. Not me. And not your god.*"

"Not even if it saves us all?"

"*Especially* not then. A salvation bought with a stolen person isn't salvation. It's just a prettier kind of burning." He took her hands. His palms were ridged with old callus, warm, steady. "If Vess wants a voice, it can ask. Out loud. Like anyone else asking. And you can say no."

"What if I can't say no? What if saying no isn't in me anymore by the time I need to?"

"Then I'll say it for you."

She stared at him. "You broke your oath once already—at the Sound—to save me. If you break it again—"

"I know what it costs." He touched the cracked brand, briefly, the way a man touches an old wound he has stopped apologizing for. "I spent eleven years believing that oath was the best thing about me. Then I met a girl hiding in a drying shed whose gift should have died at the salt line, and I lied to a reeve to keep her alive, and it turns out the best thing about me was always going to be the choosing, not the swearing." A ghost of his rare smile. "So I'll choose again. As many times as it takes. That's the whole secret of oaths, Sable. They're not walls. They're promises, and promises are made of choices, and I will keep choosing you until one of us is ash."

Outside, the moon climbed another finger's width, and Sable felt it climb—felt it like a hand pressing gently, patiently, on the back of her neck, urging her face upward. Urging her toward the sea, and the sea-road, and the black water where a temple waited with its broken bell.

*Soon,* something said, in a register below hearing. *Soon, little echo. Soon.*

* * *

She did not sleep.

She sat by the window with a blanket around her shoulders and watched the moon make its slow silver road across the water, and Ines sat beside her—not touching, never crowding, just present, the way he had been present through every bad night since the ford. Around midnight the pressure in her chest crested, and she felt her lips begin to move without her permission, and panic flared white—

—and then she remembered what he'd said. *Ask. Out loud.*

"Stop," she whispered, to the dark, to the moon, to whatever vast patient attention lay behind both. "Please. I'm still here. I'm still *me*. Wait."

The pressure paused. Held. Like a knock at a door, waiting to be answered rather than broken open.

And in that pause—in the terrible, tender courtesy of it—Sable understood something that undid her more than any possession could have. The god wasn't forcing. It had never been forcing. All these weeks of leakage, of borrowed memories, of songs rising unbidden—it had been knocking, and she, terrified and lonely and hungry for something ancient to love her, had kept opening the door wider on her own.

The vessel fear was real. But so was the choice. It had always been the choice.

She began, very quietly, to sing. Not a song Vess gave her. One of her own—half Tam's lullaby, half a fisher-girl's tune from the village where she'd first washed ashore, half nothing that had existed before tonight. A new song. Hers.

Under her voice, deep beneath the floorboards and the bedrock and the drowned miles between here and the temple, something vast shifted its weight and listened—and this time, listening felt less like being hollowed out and more like being accompanied.

Beside her, Ines exhaled slowly. "What did it say?" he asked.

"Nothing." She leaned her head against his shoulder, watching the moon. "It's learning to wait."

They sat that way as the silver road on the water lengthened toward morning, and neither of them spoke again, because there was nothing left to say that the night hadn't already heard.

But far down the coast, past the headlands, past the drowned road's first marker, torchlight was moving along the shore—a long line of fires where no fishers worked at night. Sable saw it first. She stiffened, and Ines followed her gaze, and she watched the blood leave his face by degrees as he counted the lights.

"That's not a patrol," he said quietly. "That's an army."

And beneath her ribs, patient as tide, the god murmured: *Thirteen days.*
