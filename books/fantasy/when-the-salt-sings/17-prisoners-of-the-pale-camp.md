## 17. Prisoners of the Pale Camp

The camp was called the Pale Camp because everything in it had been drained of color—the tents bleached by salt wind and woodsmoke, the banners of the inquisition a white so stark it looked like surrender, the faces of the prisoners gone gray as ash before their time. Brannoc had served under pale banners before. He knew what they meant. They meant that someone in authority had decided that color itself was a kind of sin.

He walked the lines at night because he could not sleep, and he told himself it was discipline, and he told himself it was duty, and by the fourth night of telling himself these things he had stopped believing either one.

The twins were held in the third cage from the river side—iron-barred wagons with wheels sunk to the hubs in mud so no cart horse could be stolen out from under them, or so Corvane's engineers said. Really, Brannoc thought, the mud was for the prisoners. A cage on wheels could be moved quickly when a burning needed doing. Mud made the waiting longer. And Corvane believed, with the whole of his cold and orderly heart, that long waiting broke people better than fire did.

The twins were not broken.

Brannoc stopped outside the bars, as he did every night, and found them sitting shoulder to shoulder in the straw, the girl Ferren with her brother's head in her lap, both of them awake because they were always awake when he came. They had named themselves for him in the first hour: *the tall one who won't look at us*. He still hadn't decided if it was an insult.

"You're late," said Ferren.

"The captain of a company doesn't run on your time."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "He runs on Corvane's. That's worse."

Her brother, Wren, opened his eyes but didn't lift his head. He had taken a beating during the taking—Brannoc had watched it happen at the coastal crossing and done nothing, had stood with his sword drawn and his orders clear and his feet rooted like posts driven into clay—and there was a bruise along his jaw that had gone the yellow-green of old ice. "Did you bring food?" Wren asked.

"I brought myself. That's more than most get."

"It is," Wren admitted. "Though I'd trade you for a heel of bread without much grief."

Brannoc crouched, slow, joints aching with an age he refused to name, and passed two strips of dried mutton through the bars. He had been doing this since the second night. He had told himself then that it was strategy—that fed prisoners talked, and talking prisoners gave up where the girl Sable had gone, and where Ines Hartwell had gone, and that every kindness was a hook. The twins ate the mutton and gave up nothing, and the hooks rusted in him instead.

"Sing us something," Ferren said, licking grease from her thumb.

"You know I don't sing."

"We know you used to." She tilted her head, studying him with those identical gray eyes, hers and her brother's, eyes that had looked at him across a hundred campfires of shared history that wasn't theirs. "Wren heard you humming on the second night. Something old. Something about a river ford."

"That's a soldier's song."

"It's a god's song," Wren said quietly. "You just put soldier words over it. They all do. Half the marching songs in the valley companies have bones that go back to Vess. Nobody remembers anymore which parts are theirs."

Brannoc stood up too fast. His knee cracked like green wood.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you," said Ferren, and smiled at him, and it was the smile that followed him down the rows of cages and back past the picket lines, through the smell of wet canvas and horse dung and banked fires, all the way to his own tent, where he sat in the dark with his sword across his knees and did not sleep, and did not hum, and failed at both.

* * *

Corvane received him at dawn in the command tent, which was the largest and whitest thing in the camp, its floor covered in woven rush mats imported from Marrowgate at obscene expense because the inquisitor believed filth was a vector of sloppy thinking. Brannoc had never liked the man. He had admired him once, distantly, the way a mason admires a well-built gallows. Now admiration and dislike had fused into something heavier that he carried behind his breastbone like a swallowed stone.

"The twins," Corvane said, without looking up from his ledger. He wrote in that ledger constantly, a slim black book bound in something Brannoc chose not to identify. Every interrogation, every confession extracted, every name gathered from every village between here and the coast went into it in a hand so fine it looked engraved. "They've spoken?"

"They speak plenty. None of it useful."

"Everyone speaks usefully eventually. It's simply a question of how much of them has to be spent first." Corvane turned a page. He was a narrow man, dry as winter grass, with a voice that never rose because it never had to; men leaned toward quiet the way plants leaned toward light. "Their gift, according to the testimony we recovered at the crossing, involves water. Currents, pressure, the breaking of vessels. Useful for interrogation, actually. Water finds every crack." He glanced up at last, and his eyes were pale as the tents, pale as the banners, a color Brannoc had decided long ago must come from staring into fires to see what burned. "But that's tomorrow's work. Tonight I want your company formed up on the south perimeter. There will be a burning."

"Both of them? At once?" Brannoc kept his face still. Eleven years of mercenary contracts had taught him that skill above all others: to keep his face still while something inside him screamed or laughed or died. "Two gifted at once draws a crowd. Half the hinterland will come to watch. Some of them will be sympathetic."

"Some of them will be *instructive*," Corvane corrected mildly. "That is rather the point, Captain. Fear is a teacher with excellent attendance. But no—not both. The boy first. The girl watches. Then, if she confesses the location of the fugitives, perhaps the boy watches her be spared. Grief and hope in the same hour. It makes the deepest grooves." He returned to his ledger. "Sundown. Have your men ready to hold the crowd line."

Brannoc saluted, which was a thing his arm did while the rest of him stood somewhere else entirely, and left the tent into the gray morning, and walked, without deciding to, toward the river.

* * *

He had sworn beside Ines Hartwell at the shrine under the salt cliffs thirteen years ago—or fourteen now, he'd lost count, another thing the years had taken—and the oath had been simple the way all terrible things are simple. *My brothers before my blood. My contract before my conscience.* The Oathbound lived by it, ate by it, buried their dead by it. It had held them together through winters that would have scattered any other company like sparrows. It had made Brannoc's life make sense after the war took everything else, and he had loved it the way you love a wall around you in a storm, forgetting that walls also keep you in.

Ines had broken the contract first. That was the ledger-entry version, the clean version, the version Brannoc recited to his loyalists when they grumbled: Hartwell went rogue, Hartwell hid a witch, Hartwell spat on the oath. But Brannoc had seen the girl's face at the river ford, seen her stand over Ines's bleeding body with a voice that tore the water open, and what he remembered most—what he had tried for weeks to forget—was that she had been crying. Not screaming. Crying. As if drowning the fight had hurt her more than it hurt them.

Contract-breakers didn't cry over the men they maimed.

And now here he was, walking the prisoner lines at midnight again, and the singing started.

It began, as it always began, from the far cages—the ones holding villagers rounded up on suspicion, hedge-folk and herb-wives and one terrified ferryman whose crime was a birthmark shaped like a wave. Someone low and cracked started it, a woman's voice, and the others caught it the way dry grass catches flame, until the whole row of cages was murmuring it, soft enough not to draw the sentries, steady enough to carry:

*When the salt sings, the silence ends.*
*When the silence ends, the Voice descends.*
*Hold your breath and hold your ground—*
*the god who drowned will not drown down.*

It was Sable's song. It had to be. Brannoc had heard fragments of it for a month now, drifting up roads and rivers ahead of them like weather, in taverns and hedgerow shrines and once, impossibly, hummed by a crown quartermaster who claimed he'd learned it from his grandmother. The girl was leaving it behind her everywhere she went, seeded into every camp and village she passed through, growing whether she willed it or not. Corvane called it contagion. The prisoners called it comfort. Brannoc, standing alone in the dark between the cages with forty years of soldiering in his body, called it by no name at all, because naming it would mean admitting what it was doing to him.

The twins joined in. Of course they did. Their voices twined together the way only twins' voices can, two threads making one rope, and they changed the last verse—added to it, sly and low:

*When the tall one walks the line,*
*and can't tell salt from brine,*
*he'll remember what he swore*
*before he swore to war.*

The other prisoners laughed, soft and startled, laughter like sparks. Brannoc stood very still.

Then he unhooked the ring of keys from his belt—the ones Corvane had given him personally, because the captain of the Oathbound was trustworthy, because the Oathbound did not break contracts—and he looked at them for a long time.

* * *

He told himself, walking back to the third cage, that he was only going to talk. That was the story he brought, the way you bring a lantern on a walk you intend to end soon. He would talk to them, confirm the time of the burning, maybe warn them—warning was not treason, warning was mercy, mercy was permitted even to contract-men—

Ferren saw the keys before he was within ten paces. Her whole body went still in a way that made Wren sit up, and then they were both looking at him, and there was no slyness left in their faces at all, just a naked, careful hope that was worse than any mockery.

"You're going to catch hell for this," Brannoc said. His voice came out rough. "When Corvane finds the empty cages."

"He'll burn something else instead," Wren said. "There's always something else. That's how men like him stay warm."

"Not tonight. Tonight he finds a broken lock and a note saying you drowned in the river trying to escape." Brannoc worked the key into the padlock. His hands were steadier than they had any right to be. "There's a fisher track downstream, half a mile past the picket line, where the bank drops to shingle. Follow the water north. Don't stop before dawn. And when you find them—" he hesitated, the padlock half-turned, "—when you find Hartwell and the girl. Tell him—" 

What? Tell him what? That I'm sorry? That I chose wrong? That I loved him like a brother and sold him like stock and only understood which of those mattered when I watched you two in a cage?

"Tell him the thirteenth turn rises in nine days," Brannoc said instead, "over the drowned temple. Corvane means to be there. He means to burn it—with the girl inside it, if he can manage it, and everyone else inside it if he can't. He has fire-priests. He has oil by the barrel. He isn't hunting her anymore, do you understand? He's hunting *her song*. He wants to burn the idea of her out of the world, and the temple is where the idea lives." The padlock clicked open. "Tell him his brother finally learned to count."

"To what?" asked Ferren.

"Count what an oath costs." He swung the door wide. "Go."

They went—but at the edge of the dark, Ferren turned back. For a moment he thought she would say something clever, and he braced for it, wanting it almost, a little barb to carry instead of the heavier things. Instead she pressed her fist to her heart, an old gesture, a god-war gesture, one that soldiers' songs had borrowed and salted down and forgotten the meaning of.

"Vess hears even the late," she said. "Especially the late. That's the whole point of him."

Then the dark took them, and Brannoc stood alone by an open cage in a camp full of sleeping men he had betrayed, feeling lighter and more damned than he had in thirty years.

* * *

The note was easy. Broken locks were easier—he'd staged escapes before, in other lives, for other reasons. What was hard was the letter.

He wrote it three times by the light of a shuttered lamp, burning scraps of his own campaign journal for paper, because no Oathbound carried blank pages and no Oathbound, ever, had done what he was doing. The first draft was an apology and he burned it unread. The second was an explanation and it was worse. The third was short:

*Hartwell.*

*Nine days. Thirteenth moon over the temple. Corvane comes with fire-priests and oil and four hundred men, and he does not mean to take prisoners—he means to end the story. I freed the twins. They'll find you or they won't. If they do, believe them.*

*I am not asking forgiveness. Forgiveness is a debt and debts are contracts, and I've finally learned what mine cost. But if you reach the temple—if you do what I think you're going to do—know that some of us will be standing behind you when the moon comes up. Not many. Enough.*

*B.*

He folded it small, sealed it with plain wax, and woke Tammas Rill—one of his own, a boy he'd recruited at fifteen off a turnip farm, a boy who worshipped him with the uncomplicated devotion that Brannoc had spent years mistaking for loyalty to the oath.

"Ride west," he told him. "Hard as you can. Find the Hartwell party. Give this to no one but Hartwell's own hand." He pressed a purse into the boy's palm. "Don't come back. Go to Marrowgate afterward. Disappear. When the thirteenth moon rises, this camp is going to be no place for anyone who ever ate at my table."

Tammas stared at him. "Captain—"

"That's not a request, boy. It's the last order I'll give you as one." Brannoc managed something that was almost a smile. "After tonight I'm not anybody's captain. I'm just an old man choosing sides too late."

"Late's better than never," Tammas said—simple, farm-boy certain, no poetry in it—and was gone into the dark with the letter against his chest, and Brannoc listened to the hoofbeats fade toward the west, toward the temple, toward whatever was coming.

Behind him, in the far cages, faint as tide through stone, the prisoners had begun to sing again.

*When the salt sings, the silence ends...*

Brannoc stood in the cold between the fires, an old man in a pale camp, and for the first time in thirteen years he let himself hum along.
