## 18. The Thirteenth Moon Rising

The temple announced itself long before they saw it.

Ines felt it first, in the brand beneath his collarbone—a slow, rhythmic warmth, like a second heartbeat waking after thirteen years of sleep. Then the land began to change. The salt flats gave way to black volcanic soil, and the black soil to a forest of cedars so old their roots had learned to walk. Mist pooled between the trunks like milk spilled from a great bowl, and in that mist, birds sang songs with no names—songs Ines's body recognized even though his mind did not.

Sable walked beside him with her head tilted, listening. She had been listening for three days now, ever since they crossed the last ridge and saw the sea glittering far below, silver-gray and patient.

"It's humming," she said quietly. "The whole valley. Can't you hear it?"

"I feel it." He touched his chest, where the oath-brand lay hidden beneath his shirt. "Like something knocking on a door I built."

Behind them came the others. Maren, the old woman who remembered the god-war, leaned on Tam's arm—the smuggler twin who had lost his brother's laugh somewhere along this road and carried it now like a stone in his pocket. Rell, the other twin, walked point as she always did, her hand never far from her knife. And behind them all, limping but upright, Brannoc, who had freed two prisoners and burned his own life down to follow them here.

They were seven. Corvane, by Brannoc's count, was better than four hundred.

"The moon rises early tonight," Maren said, squinting at the pale disc already visible above the treeline, fat and white and wrong-looking, too large by half. "Thirteenth turn. I haven't seen it since I was a girl, when the sky went quiet."

"Tell me again what happens," Sable said. Not to Ines—to Maren. She asked everyone but Ines these days, as if the truth might be gentler coming from someone whose blood wasn't the seal itself.

Maren's voice dropped into the register she saved for old things. "When the thirteenth moon rose over the drowned temple, the god Vess fell silent. Not dead. Silent. There is a difference, child, and it matters more tonight than it has ever mattered before."

* * *

The temple was drowned, but not in water.

It sat in a shallow basin at the forest's heart, its white stones gone gray with salt-frost, its columns leaning like tired sentinels. Where water should have filled the basin there was instead a sea of mist, knee-deep and glowing faintly, moving in slow tides that obeyed no wind Ines could feel. Steps descended into that mist and disappeared. Somewhere below, he knew without knowing how he knew, the sanctuary still held its shape—and within it, the silence.

Vess's silence. Thirteen years of it, sealed by a boy's terrified voice on a battlefield that had become this holy ground.

Ines stood at the top of the steps and his legs nearly failed him.

He had expected memory. What he got was presence. The seal inside him—the binding he had spoken when he was eleven years old, words fed to him by dying priests who needed small hands and an unbroken voice—resonated against the temple stones like a tuning fork struck against its twin. He could feel the shape of the lock. He could feel, with sickening clarity, how thin the door had grown.

And through the cracks, something looked back at him.

Not eyes. Not exactly. A pressure, vast and patient, pressing against the inside of his skull the way tide presses against a seawall. And threaded through that pressure—not words yet, but the *shape* of words, waiting—

*Free me, and I will give you war.*

Ines staggered. Sable caught his elbow.

"Ines?"

"He's talking to me." His own voice sounded foreign. "Or trying to. It's not... he isn't asking. He's offering."

"What kind of offer?"

Ines looked out over the mist-filled basin, at the temple where gods had once walked openly among men, and answered honestly because lying to her had become impossible somewhere on the Road of Small Miracles. "War. Fire. Four hundred men under Corvane, and every guild that named me traitor, and every crown that ever posted an edict—all of it, swept away. That's what Vess was, before. That's what they sealed. Not a god of silence. A god of judgment."

Sable was quiet for a moment. Then: "And if you free him gently?"

"I don't know. No one knows. The seal was made in fear. Everything about it was made in fear." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "I've spent my whole life in a valley where nothing magical can live, guarding a door I don't have the key to. And now I'm standing on the lock, and the thing on the other side wants out, and I'm supposed to decide—for everyone—what comes through."

"You're not deciding alone."

He turned. They had gathered behind him without his noticing—Brannoc, breathing hard; Maren with her cane planted like a standard; the twins flanking Sable as they always did now, instinctively, the way brothers flank the thing they've chosen to protect.

"No," Ines said, and meant it. "No, I'm not."

* * *

They made camp among the outer columns, in the lee of a wall carved with processions of figures whose faces time had eaten away. Rell built the fire low. Tam distributed the last of their food with the grim arithmetic of a man who had stopped pretending there would be enough.

From the ridgeline behind them, torchlight had begun to appear—first one flame, then ten, then a river of them, descending through the cedar forest like lava finding its course downhill. Drums, faint and steady. Corvane's army moved at night now. They had stopped caring about being seen.

"They'll reach us by full dark," Brannoc said. He was cleaning his sword with the methodical calm of thirty years' habit, but his hands weren't quite steady. "Maybe sooner. Corvane will want to take the temple before the moon crests—he'll think it weakens whatever you mean to do."

"Will it?"

"I don't know, Ines. You're the one with the god whispering in your head." But there was no bitterness in it anymore. Just exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion, something that might have been forgiveness, offered late and awkwardly, like a gift wrapped in burlap.

Ines almost smiled. Almost.

Inside the temple, the pressure against his mind had not relented. If anything it grew stronger as the moon climbed, and clearer. Fragments now, not just shapes:

*They burned my temples. They burned my people. Free me by force and I will remember them all. Every name. Every debt.*

"That's not justice," Ines murmured to himself. "That's just a longer list of grievances."

But even as he thought it, another current ran beneath the first—one he hadn't noticed until now, quieter, older. Grief. Beneath the god's fury was grief, oceanic and ancient, and Ines recognized it because he had been carrying his own for thirteen years. The loneliness inside brotherhood. The shrine where he heard nothing, as always. All those years kneeling before a silent altar, wondering if he'd been abandoned or if he had done the abandoning.

*You were afraid,* the god's presence seemed to say, and it was not accusation. It was almost tender. *So was I.*

Ines pressed his palms against the cold stone floor and shook.

"Ines." Sable knelt beside him. Her hand hovered over his shoulder-blade, not quite touching. "What's happening?"

"He's not what the stories say." Ines lifted his head. "Neither of us is. We're both just—" He searched for the word. "*Tired.*"

* * *

Full dark came, and with it the moon, cresting the eastern ridge like a pale eye opening over the world.

By its light, Corvane's army poured into the basin's rim. Torches by the hundreds now, ringing the temple grounds, and at their center a figure in gray-white robes who did not need fire to be seen—the inquisitor's pale armor drank the moonlight and gave nothing back.

Corvane raised one hand, and the drums stopped. Into that sudden silence, his voice carried impossibly across the basin, amplified by bound artifacts Ines could feel buzzing against his skin like flies:

"Ines Hartwell! Sworn brother of the Oathbound! Sealer's blood!" A pause, theatrical, savoring. "You stand on the anchor-stone of the god who broke the world. Give me the girl, give me your oath-brand, and die clean. Refuse, and every soul in this basin burns with you—including hers."

Beside Ines, Sable stood very straight. Her gift was rising off her skin in visible waves now—frost-flowers blooming and dying in the air around her shoulders, light bending strangely where she breathed. Since the twelfth turn, she had stopped fighting it. She had told him, in the dark hours before dawn, that it was easier to ride the wave than drown beneath it.

"He won't burn the temple," she said. "Not while you're alive. He needs the seal intact. He needs *you*."

"Then we give him a problem," Ines said. "We go inside. All of us. Let him decide whether his hatred is worth shattering the only thing keeping his enemy asleep."

"And if freeing Vess starts the war anyway?" Rell asked. Her voice was flat, practical. "If the god wakes up angry?"

Ines looked at each of them in turn—Brannoc with his scarred hands, Maren with her ancient eyes, the twins with their matched stubbornness, and Sable, whose voice was the echo of everything he had spent his life sealing shut.

"Then it falls to me to ask him not to," Ines said. "And I'm going to spend whatever I have left making sure he listens."

* * *

They descended the steps together, seven souls walking down into mist that glowed like drowned starlight.

The deeper they went, the louder the resonance became. Ines felt his oath-brand heating against his chest—not painfully, not yet, but with the insistence of a kettle nearing boil. The temple walls around them began to answer, veins of pale light threading through the salt-frosted stone, tracing patterns that matched the sigil on his skin exactly.

Maren stopped at the threshold of the inner sanctuary and would go no further. "This is where I stop, child," she said to Sable. "Old women and gods mix poorly. But listen—" she gripped Sable's wrist, fierce despite her frailty. "Whatever happens in there, whatever he offers, whatever you hear—you are not a vessel. You are a voice. Make them know the difference."

"I will," Sable said.

Tam and Rell took positions flanking the doorway, knives drawn, facing outward toward the army above. Brannoc hesitated at the threshold, then stepped inside beside Ines—because some oaths apparently survived even betrayal.

The sanctuary was round, domed, empty except for a single stone altar worn smooth by centuries of kneeling pilgrims. Above it, in the dome, stars had been painted that no longer matched any living sky. And everywhere—in the walls, the floor, the air itself—the silence pressed in like deep water.

Here, Ines thought. This is where he fell silent. This is where I made him.

He approached the altar. The pressure against his mind swelled to fill the entire space, no longer fragments but a presence, whole and enormous, and the offer came one final time, laid out before him like a general's map:

*Free me by force. Speak the name in anger. Break the seal as a weapon breaks, and I will rise with fire in my right hand and judgment in my left, and no crown will ever hunt your kind again, and no child will ever kneel at a silent shrine, and the debt of thirteen years will be paid in ash.*

All of it true. All of it tempting. And underneath it, barely audible, the grief again, the terrible loneliness of a god who had been silenced by the very people he died protecting—

"Ines." Sable's voice, soft, at his shoulder. "The moon."

Through the dome's oculus, the thirteenth moon had come fully into view—enormous, luminous, filling the opening like a coin dropped into a cupped palm. Its light fell across the altar, across Ines's hands, across the oath-brand now burning visibly through his shirt.

And outside, muffled by stone and distance, came the sound Ines had been dreading: the shriek of fire taking hold. Cedar smoke curled down through the oculus, acrid and sweet. Corvane had lit the grove.

The hinge of history creaked.

Ines closed his eyes. Beneath the smoke, beneath the drums, beneath the god's vast and wounded patience, he found the thread he had followed all the way from a valley that forgets to this room where everything would be decided: not power, not vengeance, not even duty—but a girl half-drowned in fishing nets, hiding from a world that wanted to burn her, and a mercenary who had lied for the first time in eleven years to keep her safe.

He opened his eyes.

"Hold the door," he said to Brannoc. "Whatever comes down those stairs—hold it."

Then he turned to face the altar, and the god, and the thirteenth moon, and began to speak.
