## 19. Corvane's Fire

The moon rose the color of a wound, and Corvane thought it fitting.

He had waited thirteen years for this night—waited through the long gray years of paperwork and petitions, of provincial governors who smiled at him across council tables while their eyes said *fanatic*, of inquisitors junior to him who were promoted past him because they lacked his appetite for the work's ugly arithmetic. Let them call it fanaticism. A fanatic loved something too much. Corvane did not love anything. He had simply seen, once, at nine years old, what magic did when it was permitted to be magnificent: a hedge-witch in his home village who could coax grain from stony soil, beloved by everyone, until the winter she couldn't and half the village starved anyway because they had stopped planting for themselves. He had watched grown men weep at her funeral and understood, with the terrible clarity of childhood, that wonder was a debt that always came due.

The ledger balanced tonight.

He stood on the ridge above the drowned temple with two hundred crown soldiers ranked behind him, torches banked low, and looked down into the grove where the last cedars of Vess stood black against the rising light. The temple itself was mostly ruin—a suggestion of columns beneath the brackish water, steps climbing out of the marsh like the spine of some drowned leviathan—but the grove lived. Thirteen trees, one for each year of the god's silence, or so the old texts claimed. Corvane had spent four months and three bribed archivists confirming it: this place was an anchor. The god's silence was not absence but imprisonment, and imprisonment required walls, and walls could be burned.

"Signal fires are set, Lord Inquisitor." Sergeant Hesk, a solid man with a solid man's face, stood at his shoulder. "Oil's been laid since dusk. The grove will catch on the first spark."

Corvane nodded. Below, in the shadow of the temple steps, he could see them—the remnant. The traitor Hartwell, moving between the columns like a man memorizing a room he expected to die in. The girl Sable, small and pale at the water's edge, her hair lifting in a wind that touched no one else. The old woman from the hinterlands, the twins freed from his camp by Brannoc's treachery, a handful of others. Seven souls against two hundred.

And Hartwell himself, standing now at the top of the steps, looking up at the ridge as if he could see Corvane's face in the dark. Perhaps he could. Sealer-blood saw things other men didn't. Corvane had found that in the archives too, buried in a marginal note in a dead bishop's hand: *the oath-lines keep the lock, and the lock knows its keeper*.

"Lord Inquisitor." Hesk again, quieter. "The Oathbound captain is asking for you."

Brannoc. Corvane turned. The mercenary came up the ridge path alone, unarmed as agreed, torchlight sliding off the iron-gray stubble of his jaw. He had served the crown's contract faithfully for five weeks—tracking, harrying, delivering prisoners—and Corvane had almost believed in him. Almost. Then two days ago the twins had walked out of the pale camp between one watch and the next, and the guards swore they'd heard singing, and Corvane knew exactly whose song traveled in the dark like that.

"You have something to report, Captain?"

"I have something to say." Brannoc stopped three paces away. Close enough to be courteous. Close enough to be a threat, if he intended one. "I'm not going to dress it. I took your contract because my company needed feeding and because I told myself the girl was a stranger to me. She isn't anymore than Hartwell is. I've known Ines Hartwell since we were fifteen and starving, and I swore beside him at a shrine when we both believed in something better than contracts." His jaw worked. "I'm done believing in yours."

Behind Corvane, Hesk's hand drifted toward his sword. Corvane raised two fingers—a stilling gesture—and studied Brannoc the way he studied all dangerous things: patiently, cataloguing.

"You understand what happens now," Corvane said. "You understand that I don't need your company. Two hundred men against seven. The arithmetic has never favored them."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "It hasn't. But you should know that forty of your two hundred are mine, or were, and they've been listening to the same song the prisoners sang. And you should know something else." He leaned in slightly, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was worse than any threat. "You think you're going to burn a god's grave tonight and walk away clean. But I stood in that shrine thirteen years ago. I felt what it cost to seal Him. And I'm telling you, inquisitor, whatever you're planning—you're not holding the right end of this rope."

For a moment, only the wind off the marsh, carrying the smell of salt and old stone.

"Take him," Corvane said quietly.

They took him. Brannoc did not resist—went down between four soldiers with his hands open, watching Corvane with an expression the inquisitor chose not to interpret. Bind him and hold him apart from the line, Corvane ordered. If the field turns, he dies first.

Hesk lingered after the others dragged the captain away. "My lord—if forty of ours are compromised—"

"They're not compromised. They're *uncertain*. There is a difference, Sergeant, and it is the difference between everything and nothing." Corvane drew the long knife from his belt—not the ceremonial blade, the working one, oiled steel with a plain grip. "Uncertain men need only be shown which way certainty lies. Tonight I intend to show them very clearly."

He looked down at the grove one last time. The thirteenth moon sat directly over the temple now, swollen and red, and beneath it the water around the sunken columns had begun to move—slowly, deliberately, circling counterclockwise, like a thing turning over in its sleep.

"Light the grove," said Corvane.

* * *

Fire caught the first cedar the way fire catches anything dry and ancient: greedily, with a sound like a gasp.

From the ridge it was almost beautiful. The flames climbed the black bark in orange ribbons, and the resin within the old wood popped and sang, and within moments the whole eastern arc of the grove was alight, throwing shadows that danced across the flooded temple floor. Corvane gave the order to advance and two hundred men went down the slope in ordered ranks, torches high, boots drumming the packed earth, and he let himself feel—for just one moment—the pure clean satisfaction of a design completing itself.

Then the girl began to sing.

It reached him across four hundred yards of burning air, and it should not have. That was the first wrongness. Sound attenuates; sound scatters; sound obeys. Her voice obeyed nothing. It arrived in Corvane's ears as if she stood at his shoulder, and it was not loud—it was never loud—but it was *present*, the way cold is present, the way gravity is present. A melody without words, or with words underneath the melody, words in a language that made his teeth ache to hear.

The ranks faltered. Not broke—faltered. Men slowed. Torches dipped. Somewhere in the third rank a soldier began openly weeping without knowing why.

"Hold formation," Corvane snapped, and his voice sounded thin even to himself, a paper thing against that music. "She is one girl. One frightened girl with a parlor trick and a god's reputation to hide behind. *Advance.*"

They advanced. The second cedar caught. The third. Fire raced along the grove's heart, and the smoke rolled thick and sweet, and Corvane descended into it with his men, down the slope, across the marsh margin, toward the temple steps where Hartwell stood waiting with a sword he clearly did not want to use.

"Ines Hartwell!" Corvane called, and the name carried strangely, swallowed by smoke and song. "In the name of the Crown of Ashes, surrender the witch and kneel, and I am authorized to spare every soul here who lays down arms!"

"You're authorized," Hartwell called back, and even at this distance, even through smoke, Corvane could hear the exhaustion in it, the sorrow. "That's a funny word for what you are, Corvane. You know what I learned in thirteen years of keeping a secret? That authorization is just violence wearing its good coat."

"Then wear yours," Corvane said, and signaled.

The lines met at the foot of the temple steps, and the battle among the burning cedars began.

It was ugly work, close work, the smoke pressing down like a lid, the firelight making mad carousel shadows of everything. Corvane kept to the rear of his command as doctrine demanded—he was the mind of the operation, not its sword—but he saw enough. He saw the old woman from the hinterlands stand in front of the twins with a walking staff and put three soldiers on the ground with movements too quick for her age. He saw Hesk go down with an arrow through the thigh and keep shouting orders from the mud. He saw Hartwell fight exactly as reports described: defensively, surgically, disarming rather than killing, again and again, taking a cut along the forearm rather than opening a brother-in-arms' throat. Sentiment. Sentiment would kill him tonight.

But the arithmetic was holding. Seven against two hundred was seven against two hundred, however well they fought, and Corvane watched the circle tighten step by step, the defenders pressed back and back toward the water, toward the steps, toward the burning grove—

And then the left flank turned.

Not broke. *Turned.* Forty men in crown colors wheeled as one body, and Corvane saw why a heartbeat later: Brannoc, bound hands split on a broken arrow shaft, had gotten loose in the chaos and now stood atop a fallen cedar trunk with a borrowed sword, roaring his company's old war-cry, and forty men answered it. They crashed into the flank of their own line like a landslide. The neat arithmetic of the evening tore down the middle.

"*Traitors!*" Corvane screamed, and his voice cracked on it, and he hated himself for the crack. "*Hold the line! Hold—*"

The line did not hold. Lines made of uncertain men do not hold, not when certainty arrives wearing forty familiar faces and singing a god's song. The battle curdled into a hundred private combats, crown soldiers fighting Oathbound fighting defenders, and through it all the girl's voice rose higher, and the fire climbed higher, and the water in the temple basin began to churn like a struck bell.

Corvane stood in the smoke and did the only arithmetic left to him.

The anchor was burning; that much held. Even if he lost every soldier on the field, the grove would burn to the waterline by dawn, and the texts were clear that the anchor was the trees. But the texts were also clear about the seal—that it ran in blood and voice, that Hartwell's voice had locked the god away, and that a seal broken by force released the god *into* the breaker. Into whoever broke it. He had read that marginal note a dozen times and dismissed it as superstition.

Now, watching the water rise in spirals around the temple, watching the girl at the top of the steps glow like a lamp with too much oil in it, he understood the note differently. The god wanted out. The god had wanted out for thirteen years, and every power on this field—the girl's gift, Hartwell's cracking oath, the fire itself—was a lever being pressed against the same door. Force would open it. Force was *opening* it. He could hear it in the churning water, feel it in his fillings, in the roots of his teeth: something vast pressing upward from below the world, patient no longer.

Unless the vessel changed hands.

Corvane moved. Not toward the fighting—around it, along the temple's western colonnade where the shadows ran deepest, knife in hand, an old man's quiet tread through a young god's chaos. He had spent thirteen years being the mind of operations. Minds win battles. Bodies win moments, and this was a moment.

The steps were guarded lightly—everyone was in the melee below—and he took the stairs two at a time, lungs burning with more than smoke, and came up onto the temple platform where the girl stood at the edge of the great basin with her arms spread and her eyes rolled white, singing words that were not hers. He could see that even from behind. The voice coming out of her mouth was too large for the mouth. It resonated in the stones. It resonated in *him*. She was a doorway with something enormous leaning on the far side of it, and she was holding, barely, and no one was watching her because everyone was busy killing each other over the question of her.

Everyone except Corvane.

He crossed the platform in six strides, seized her by the hair with one fist and laid the knife's edge against her throat with the other, and the song stopped.

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Corvane had ever heard. Every combatant on the field seemed to feel it at once; the clash of steel stumbled, faltered, died in patches, heads turning toward the temple steps. The fire roared on among the cedars, indifferent. But the voice—that impossible, weather-breaking, wall-shaking voice—was gone, snuffed like a candle pinched between fingers, and in its absence the whole valley seemed to lean forward.

"INQUISITOR." Hartwell's voice, raw, from below. Corvane peered down through smoke and saw him at the base of the steps, sword lowered, face streaked with soot and someone else's blood. Beside him, breathing hard, Brannoc. Between them, the whole battlefield going still. "Let her go. Whatever you think you're doing, it won't—"

"What I think I'm doing," Corvane called back, and his own voice surprised him—calm, almost tender, the voice he used over ledgers, "is ending the war before it starts. Listen to me carefully, Hartwell, because I will say this once. The seal is breaking—I can feel it breaking, we can all feel it—and when it breaks, the god pours into whatever vessel stands nearest. Your archives agree with mine on that, if nothing else. So here is the covenant I offer instead of yours: the girl comes with me. Alive. Unharmed. A vessel under *crown* authority, sealed and studied and controlled, and magic ends its war on human civilization the orderly way—or I open her throat on these steps, and we discover together whether a god can drown in one girl's blood."

Below, Hartwell had gone very still. It was Brannoc who spoke next, and there was something in the mercenary's voice Corvane had not heard before—not rage, not fear. Pity.

"You don't understand what you're holding, inquisitor."

"I understand leverage," Corvane said. "I have always understood leverage."

The girl in his grip stirred. Her head turned—slowly, with a strength that had nothing to do with muscle, until her white eyes found his over her shoulder. Her lips moved. What came out was barely a whisper, pitched only for him, and it was not her voice and it was not the god's voice either but something older that used both the way a rider uses a horse:

*"You balance your books, little clerk. But you have never asked what the debt is owed* in."

The knife trembled in Corvane's hand—one fine tremor, quickly mastered—and down among the burning cedars, the water of the temple basin went suddenly, perfectly still, like a held breath, and the thirteenth moon passed directly overhead, and every shadow on the field pointed at the temple at once.
