## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There is a particular silence that settles over the Vellum Guild after the last clerk goes home, and I have come to love it the way one loves a well-kept garden: not because it is wild, but because it is *kept*. Seven years I have balanced these accounts. Seven years since the storm, since the exile-name was burned into my tongue like a brand, since the road spat me out at the guild's door with nothing but wet boots and a talent I had sworn never to touch again.

Tonight the ledgers are kind to me.

I sit in the counting room with its single candle and its smell of tallow and old vellum, and I work through the day's entries in my careful hand. This is the work they gave me when Corvane looked at my papers — or rather, at the lack of them — and said only, *Can you keep a ledger?* I could. I could keep anything, so long as it stayed between lines.

The first entry of the evening:

*Item: one bottle, thunder, captured over the Weeping Fields, sold to Lady Ashgrave of the Salt Marches. Payment received: three casks of pickled moonfish and one apology, seven years deferred.*

Lady Ashgrave has been buying bottled thunder from us for a decade. She uses it to frighten crows, which seems extravagant, but who am I to judge what comfort people take from impossible things? The thunder itself is temperamental stock. It sulks in storage. Last spring a jar of August lightning got loose in the cellar and we spent three days finding all the places it had hidden. Old Hemm still won't go down the east stairs without a copper bell.

I enter the transaction, note the deferral against the apology column, and move on.

*Item: one debt, paid in years. Debtor: the miller of Harrow's Bend, who owed the guild four hundred crowns and could not pay. Settlement received: eleven years of his life, transferred by contract and witnessed twice. The miller will age them in our keeping as one ages wine.*

This one always makes me pause, though I would never admit it to anyone. There is something in the phrasing of the old contracts — *the debtor surrenders forthwith those years designated* — that sounds so reasonable on the page. And the miller was old anyway, he said so himself, said he'd rather have the guild's protection for his grandchildren than his own lost decades. He signed with a steady hand. I checked.

That is the thing about this work. You check, and you initial, and you file, and everything stays where you put it. Nothing in the Vellum Guild escapes its column. I have built seven years out of columns.

My exile-name is written nowhere in these books. That was the first thing I asked, before I unpacked my bag, before I slept my first night under the guild's roof. *Does anything here remember me?* And Corvane smiled his dry smile and said the guild remembers only what is written, and nothing about me had been written yet. It was the closest thing to mercy I had been offered since the storm. A person unwritten is a person who can begin again.

So I began again in ink.

*Item: one feather, grey-silver, provenance disputed. Seller claims it was molted by an Emberwing dragon, species believed extinct these two hundred years. Purchased by the guild for twelve crowns and the seller's silence regarding how he came by it. Current status: awaiting authentication.*

I turn the feather over in its glass case and I will confess something I have never written anywhere: I hope it is real. Not for the guild's sake — an authenticated Emberwing feather would fetch more than the whole cellar of bottled weather — but because the world has felt smaller every year I have kept it, and I would like there to be one dragon left in it, somewhere, molting in some high grey valley, indifferent to ledgers entirely.

The authentication will take months. Feathers of impossible birds are like that; they resist being known. I set the case back in its drawer, next to the mermaid's promissory note and the left shoe of the man who walked off the edge of the world, and I am content.

Content. I want to be fair to myself, here at the start of things, and record that I was genuinely content. The candle burned steadily. The numbers reconciled to the half-penny. Somewhere upstairs Corvane was doing whatever guildmasters do at night, and I did not wonder about it, because wondering was not in my job description and I have always been scrupulous about staying within terms of employment.

Here is what nobody tells you about willful blindness: it does not feel like blindness. It feels like discipline. It feels like finally being good at your life.

* * *

Midnight comes, and with it the last accounts — the private ones, the ones Corvane keeps in the black-bound volume that lives in the locked drawer of my own desk. I do not open that drawer often. It is not forbidden, exactly; the guild does not deal in forbidden, it deals in *fee schedules*. But the black book belongs to Corvane's side of the house, and I balance it the way one mends a rich cousin's fence: carefully, asking no questions about why the fence is so high.

Tonight, though, the black book is heavier than usual. I notice that the way I notice everything — professionally, without comment — and I open it.

Most of the entries are ordinary by our standards. A soul bought back from a river-god at a markup. Sixty leagues of fog, leased to a fishing fleet. A curse renewed annually, like a subscription. My pen moves through them in the rhythm I have worn smooth over seven years: read, verify, initial, file.

Then, near the middle of the gathering, a line in Corvane's hand that is different. Tighter. Faster. Written, I would guess, late, by candlelight, by a man who wanted the entry made and did not much care how it looked.

I almost skip it. I want to be honest about that too. My eyes actually slid past it once, the way they slide past the watermarks and marginal notes I have trained myself not to read, because reading things outside your column is how clerks get exiled from better lives than mine.

But the numbers snagged me. There were no numbers. Every other entry in the black book has figures, even the terrible ones — *especially* the terrible ones, because Corvane believes, as I believe, that a price makes a thing manageable. This entry has no price. Only words, and a date, and a counterparty noted with initials I have never seen in any guild registry.

I lean closer to the candle.

*Item: one sky, sold.*

And beneath it, in smaller script, as if the pen were embarrassed: *Terms pending. Buyer: C.B. Delivery upon alignment.*

I sit back. The candle gutters and steadies.

It means nothing, I tell myself. Guilds trade in metaphor; it is practically our coat of arms. A "sky" might be a shipment of star-metal. It might be a painting. It might be the name of a horse. Terms pending, delivery upon alignment — that is standard escrow language, I have written it a hundred times myself. One strange entry in a book full of strange entries, unpriced and unfinished, and unfinished things are not my department. Unfinished things wait for Corvane to finish them.

I copy the figures — the absence of figures — into my working summary, initial the page, and close the black book, and lock the drawer, and put the key where the key goes.

That is what I did. I want the record to show that is exactly what I did, because someday — though I could not have told you why that night — I suspected I would need to account for it.

* * *

I finish the ordinary ledgers by the second candle. Bottled thunder, reconciled. Years in escrow, accruing interest of a kind we do not advertise. Feather, dormant. Everything in its column, every column in its book, every book in its place on shelves that run the length of the counting room like a dam holding back a very organized flood.

I allow myself the small ritual I end every night with. I press my palm flat against the master ledger — the great genealogy of debts that is the guild's true heart — and I feel, faintly, the hum of it. Everything written in a Vellum Guild book stays written. That is our oldest service and our truest product: permanence. In a world where storms rise without warning and names can be burned off a person like paint, we offer the radical comfort of *it happened, and it is recorded, and therefore it cannot be undone.*

Seven years ago a storm rose when I called it, and it should not have risen, and it unmade a village whose name I still cannot write down without my hand shaking. The kingdom recorded that. The kingdom exiled me for it. And then the guild offered me the opposite bargain: write only what is finished, and nothing unfinished can ever touch you.

I blew out the candles. The dark took the room gently, shelf by shelf.

As I reached the door, I paused — I could not say why — and looked back at the locked drawer. Just a moment. The way you pause at a word you have read but not pronounced.

Somewhere far to the north, I thought I heard thunder. Which was odd, because no storm was forecast, and besides, all our thunder was bottled.

I closed the door, and went up to bed, and slept the sleep of the diligent.

In the morning, the stars would begin to go out — though that night, none of us knew it, and the sky above the guildhall was, for a few more hours, obediently full of them.

The first sign, when it came, would not be in the heavens at all.

It would be in the ledgers.
