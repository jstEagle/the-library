## 2. The Night Without Stars

I want to say I noticed the exact moment the world broke. That would be a tidy thing to put in a ledger, and I have spent seven years loving tidy things.

The truth is I was arguing with a bottle of thunder.

It had come in that afternoon from a client in the Saltmarsh districts — a widow, per the intake slip, who had caught the last clap of her husband's funeral storm in a stoppered flask of green glass and wished it held against the day she'd need to hear him again. Guild policy on bottled weather is simple: we store it, we do not open it, and if it hums after midnight we pretend it is settling. This one was not settling. It was humming the way a kettle hums, and the ledger line beneath its entry — *one grief, preserved; interest accruing* — had begun to smudge, which ledgers of ours are not supposed to do.

"Stop that," I told it, and pressed my thumb flat over the ink until the smudge gave up.

That was my life, then: a woman scolding glassware at half past midnight because the alternative was thinking about anything larger than the page in front of me. Seven years in the Vellum Guild's counting room will teach you this much — an impossible thing is very manageable as long as you only look at one of them at a time. Bottled thunder. A debt paid in years by a man who now looked twenty years older than his brother and pretended not to know why. A dragon feather, gray as old pewter, from a species everyone agreed was extinct, which meant either the ledger lied or the world did, and we had all quietly voted for the world.

One impossible thing at a time. It was not wisdom exactly. It was more like the way you can carry anything up any number of stairs if you refuse to count the stairs.

Then the silk tore.

There is no other way to describe it, though I've tried since. Somewhere far north — past the fenlands, past the border stones, past anywhere I'd ever been — something vast and woven came apart, and the sound arrived before the knowing of it did. Tearing silk, but silk the size of the horizon, and underneath the tearing a note like a struck bell made of cold. The lamp above my desk guttered sideways. The bottle of thunder went silent so abruptly my ears rang with its absence.

And every star in the sky went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*, all of them, in the same instant, like candles snuffed by a single passing hand. Through the counting-room window the night became a flat black nothing, the kind of black you get when you close your eyes too hard — except I wasn't closing my eyes, and neither was the sky.

I stood there holding a pen I no longer remembered picking up. Ink dripped off the nib onto the floor and I watched it fall, absurdly, because watching ink fall was a problem I understood.

The guildhall woke up around me like a kicked hive.

* * *

You learn things about people during a panic. Mostly you learn their prices.

The clients lodged upstairs were in the corridor before I'd gotten my coat on — a hedge-witch clutching her receipted dream like it might sublimate, a river-broker shouting about a favor the Guild owed him in *luck*, three sisters who had jointly purchased "one miracle, minor, non-refundable" and were demanding, with impressive unanimity, that it be refunded anyway. Down the great stair came the appraisers, still in their nightclothes, carrying instruments that were all pointing the same direction and all trembling.

"The wards," someone said. "The border wards are down."

"They're not down," said another voice, higher. "They're *gone*. There's nothing to raise them from—"

"The stars—" said a third, and then couldn't finish, because what do you finish? The stars were the wards' anchor. Everyone knew that the way everyone knows the sun rises: without ever once having to think about how, or on what, or what would happen if it didn't.

I should have been panicking with them. Instead I was doing something worse. I was standing in the doorway of the counting room with my ledger under my arm, listening to the guildhall scream, and feeling a small cold splinter slide into the back of my mind where the fear couldn't reach it.

Because here is the thing about the wards. I kept their accounts.

Every ward-stone renewal, every anchor-tithe, every drop of starlight the Guild purchased to feed the border — it all passed through my ledgers, in my hand, in my ink. And the accounts had been *balanced*. Perfectly. For seven years. Which meant the wards had been fed, and fed well, right up until tonight.

Things that starve fail slowly. Things that are cut fail instantly.

I did not let myself think the rest of the thought. There wasn't time, and also I didn't want to, and those two reasons felt identical in the dark.

* * *

Corvane appeared on the mezzanine the way he always did — all at once, already composed, as though panic were a weather happening to lesser buildings.

Guildmaster Corvane is — was — a tall man gone stooped in the shoulders, the way tall men go when they've spent decades making themselves smaller around other people's fears. Silver at his temples, ink permanently stained into the creases of his fingers, a voice like a drawer sliding shut. He had taken me in when the kingdom had spat me out. Seven years ago I had walked through these doors with a burned name and a storm still rattling in my teeth, and Corvane had looked at me across the intake desk and said, "We trade in impossible things, Zephyrine Marchetti. You'll fit right in."

He fit right in now. He surveyed the shrieking hall, raised one inked hand, and the noise died the way it always died, obediently.

"Nothing has happened," he said, "that the Guild cannot account for."

Which was, I noted, a strange sentence. Not *nothing has happened*. Not even *we will fix this*. Just: it can be accounted for. Even now, with the sky blind, the man reached first for bookkeeping.

"Master Corvane!" The river-broker shoved forward, waving his contract overhead like a torch. "My luck — the luck you sold me is *failing*, I lost forty crowns at cards tonight, I want—"

"You want a refund on luck," Corvane said, "on the night the stars fell out. Yes." He turned to the assembled hall, and his voice dropped into the register he used for funerals and negotiations, which in my experience were often the same event. "The Guild honors its debts. All of them. But hear me: whatever has happened tonight, it did not happen *here*. Go to your rooms. Stay indoors. Come morning, every claim will be reviewed fairly and in order."

"And the stars?" the hedge-witch asked, very quietly, in the silence. She was holding her bottled dream against her chest like a child. "Who refunds us the stars?"

For just a moment — a moment I would take out later and examine under lamplight, turn over, weigh — Corvane's face did something complicated. Something that was not surprise.

"Go to your rooms," he said again, gentler, and the hall, being frightened and obedient, went.

Then his eyes found mine across the wreckage of the atrium, and he beckoned, and my feet carried me to him the way they always did.

* * *

"Zephyrine." He spoke low, in the shadow of the mezzanine rail, while below us appraisers swept up broken glass and pretended the ceiling wasn't lying to them. "The ledgers. Seal them."

"Seal them?"

"Wax and countersignature, tonight. Every volume, current year back to founding. No entries, no withdrawals, no audits until I say otherwise."

Seven years I had kept those books open the way a heart keeps beating — constantly, unthinkingly, because stopping meant death. "Corvane, the clients will riot. Half our contracts have audit clauses. If we seal—"

"If we seal, the Guild survives the week." His eyes were steady and tired and old. "Zeph. Tonight of all nights, don't make me spend time explaining myself."

*Don't make me.* As if his reasons were coins he kept somewhere and might deign to spend.

I could have argued. I want you to understand that I could have; I was not, in those days, a coward, merely a careful woman, and careful women argue beautifully. But the hall was smoking faintly at the edges of my vision — literally smoking, someone's spilled luck curdling in a corner brazier — and the stars were gone, and the man who had saved my life was asking me for one act of trust, and I owed him seven years of them.

"Sealed tonight," I said. "But I'm writing the incident into the day-book first. Time, date, observed phenomena." Because that was the rule, the oldest rule, the one they'd taught me my first week: *if it happens, it goes in the book.* A guild that trades in impossible things stays honest by writing everything down. It was the only religion I had left, and I clung to it now like a railing in a flood.

Something crossed Corvane's face then, quick as a fish under ice.

"No," he said.

"No?"

"Not the day-book. Not yet." He put his hand briefly over mine, and his fingers were cold, colder than a man's hands have any business being in a heated hall. "What happened tonight isn't an entry, Zeph. It's a wound. You don't write on a wound. When I understand it, you'll record it, whole and true, and not one hour before. That's an order from your Guildmaster, and a request from your friend. Take whichever gets you to sleep faster."

He smiled at me. He had smiled at me a thousand times, warm as banked coals, and I had built a small life inside that warmth.

I took my ledgers and went to seal them, and told myself the chill in his fingers was the season turning.

* * *

It took until nearly dawn to wax and countersign eleven volumes. I worked alone in the counting room with every lamp lit, because the window showed nothing worth looking at — a sky like a closed eye — and because the work steadied me. Wax, press, sign. Wax, press, sign. The bottle of thunder watched me from its shelf, silent as a held breath.

It was on the eleventh volume, the oldest one, that my hand slowed.

Sealing the ledgers meant sealing *everything*. Including the pages I had never been given reason to read closely — the Guildmaster's private margin-notes, entered in his own hand, which policy allowed him and custom discouraged anyone from reading. Custom had always been enough before.

I sat there with hot wax cooling on my thumb and thought about the wards that hadn't starved. About the sound like tearing silk coming from the *inside* of the border, the way cloth tears when the pull comes from within the garment. About a Guildmaster whose first instinct, with heaven falling, had been *tell no one* and *write nothing* — the two commandments of my entire faith, broken in a single breath, by the man who taught them to me.

Custom discourages reading. Policy permits.

I opened the eleventh volume to the private margins, and I began, with a thief's care, at the back, working forward through years of Corvane's cramped, beautiful script — and stopped dead at an entry dated four months ago, in ink darker than any we stocked:

*Received in full, per agreement: one sky.*

I sat in the lamplight for a long time. Outside, the false dawn came up gray and starless over the rooftops, and somewhere below me the guildhall full of frightened people slept on, trusting the books I had just nailed shut.

Four months ago. Four months ago I had balanced that page. Four months ago I had written beneath it, in my own neat hand: *accounts reconciled*.

I had signed off on the end of the world, and called it tidy.

Somewhere in the dark beyond the window, in a direction I couldn't have named and a distance I refused to guess, something old was dying, and it knew my name, and it was about to send for me.
