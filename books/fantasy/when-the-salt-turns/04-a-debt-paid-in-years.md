## 4. A Debt Paid in Years

I left the guildhall through the coal chute, which tells you something about the dignity of my exit.

There had been no time for the front doors. Corvane's voice still rang in my ears — *seal the ledgers, tell no one* — and I had spent eleven years learning that when a man with power says *tell no one*, what he means is *no one must ever know*, and what he means by *that* is usually *kill them*. So: coal chute. It deposited me in the guildyard among the ash barrels, wearing the contents of one, and I took a moment to be grateful that my exile had already stripped me of most of my pride. There wasn't much left to lose.

The sky above Vellum Row was wrong. That was the thing I couldn't stop looking at. Not dark — dark is honest, dark is just night doing its work — but *empty*, a black so total it felt like standing inside a closed ledger. Seven years I'd lived under those stars without once thanking them, and now that they were gone I understood they had been the only thing holding the world's edges down.

And Corvane had sold them. I had read his handwriting say so.

*One sky, sold.*

I walked. I didn't run; running attracts attention, and attention was the last currency I could afford to spend. The streets of Lowmark were full of people standing in doorways, staring up, their faces lit only from below — lanterns, cookfires, the desperate glow of a city refusing to believe. Somewhere a temple bell rang without pattern. Somewhere a child was crying, and somewhere else an adult was crying in a child's register, which was somehow worse.

I went to find Sable Ives.

Here is what you need to know about Sable, and here is why telling you costs me something. Three years ago she stole something from the Guild — a vial of bottled thunder, if you're wondering, though she never learned what it was — and the penalty for theft from the Vellum Guild is written into your own account. You pay what you owe or the ledger collects. The ledger always collects. Corvane gave her a choice, because Corvane is generous the way a knife is generous: pay in coin she didn't have, or pay in memory. She chose memory. She signed away every recollection connected to me — how we met, what I did for her, why she trusted me enough to let me talk her out of worse crimes than theft — and she walked out of the guildhall lighter by one friendship and owing me, by the ledger's cold arithmetic, a favor of the first magnitude.

She remembers none of it. She knows the debt exists because the debt is written in her blood the way all guild debts are. She doesn't know *why*. Every time she sees me, I can watch her try to reconstruct a person out of nothing and fail.

And tonight I was going to knock on her window at midnight and ask her to spend whatever remained of her loyalty on a suicidal errand to the capital.

Corvane would have been proud of me. That thought nearly turned me around at her street corner. But the stars were gone, and Emberlast was dying, and guilt is a luxury for people with time.

* * *

Sable lived above a chandler's shop on Tallow Lane, three floors of stairs that smelled of tallow and old smoke. I climbed them and knocked the pattern we'd agreed on before she forgot agreeing to it — two quick, one slow, pause, two more — and heard, from inside, the particular silence of someone reaching for a weapon.

"Who is it?"

"A customer," I said. "For impossible things."

A long pause. Then: "Marchetti." The word came out flat, like a coin dropped on stone. "It's midnight."

"The stars are gone. I don't think anyone's keeping regular hours."

The lock turned. Sable Ives stood in the doorway with a knife held low and casual, the way she holds everything — as if the whole world is mildly beneath her notice. She was small and sharp-featured, hair cropped short for practicality rather than fashion, eyes the gray of coins worn smooth by handling. She looked me up and down, taking in the coal dust, the ink stains, the expression I was probably wearing.

"You look like a man who's read something he shouldn't have," she said.

"That's accurate."

"And now you've come to me. At midnight. Covered in ash." She stepped back to let me in, which was itself remarkable — trust from a woman who couldn't remember earning any. "Sit down before you fall down. And don't touch anything. Some of it's cursed and some of it's mine, and I'll expect compensation either way."

Her rooms were exactly as I remembered: neat chaos, everything valuable hidden in plain sight, a map of the capital pinned to the wall behind a tapestry of dubious provenance. I knew about the map. That was, I confess, part of why I'd come. I told myself it was because she was the only person I trusted, which was true, and also because she owned the only unregistered route into the palace district, which was also true. Both things can be true. Corvane taught me that too.

"I need to get to the capital," I said. "Tonight. Before dawn if possible."

Sable poured herself something amber from a bottle without offering me any. "That's four hundred miles."

"Yes."

"Through country where the roads have started rearranging themselves, if half of what I heard on my way home is true."

"It's worse than half."

"And you want me to take you." She sat across from me, knife still in hand, not pointed anywhere in particular, which from Sable was practically an embrace. "Why? Give me one reason I should burn four hundred miles and probably my life on a bookkeeper's emergency."

Here it was, then. The moment I'd rehearsed on the walk over and abandoned twice.

"Because you owe me," I said. "A favor of the first magnitude. It's in your blood-contract. I can call it in."

Something moved behind her eyes — not anger, exactly. Something more tired than anger.

"I know," she said quietly. "I've always known. There's a hole in me shaped like you, Marchetti. I can feel it when you walk into a room. Like a tooth that got pulled and the tongue keeps going back to the gap." She drank. "I don't remember what you did. I don't remember who you were to me. I just know that the ledger says I owe you, and the ledger—" she tapped her own sternum— "collects."

"Sable—"

"So call it in," she said. "That's what debts are *for*."

And there it was. No argument, no bargaining, no demand for explanation. Just the flat arithmetic of obligation, offered up by a woman who had paid once already and knew the exchange rate by heart. I should have felt relieved. Instead I felt something cold slide down between my ribs, and I recognized it, because I'd seen its twin on Corvane's face a hundred times: the comfortable ease of a man spending money he didn't earn.

Corvane had called in my loyalty seven years ago, when he sheltered an exile with a storm in her throat and asked, eventually, only for ledgers kept and questions unasked. He never forced me. He never had to. He simply let the debt accumulate until I would have done anything to service it. And I had told myself for seven years that this was gratitude, freely given, when really it was just interest compounding in the dark.

Now I sat in Sable's lamplight, asking her to do the same thing, using the same instrument, and calling it necessity.

"There's another way to ask," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Without the contract. I could just... ask. As a person. You could say no."

Sable stared at me for a long moment. Then she laughed — one syllable, dry as paper.

"Oh, that's *cruel*," she said. "You're giving me a choice after I've already paid for the answer?" She set down the glass. "Fine. As a person, then, since you're offering: ask me properly, and let's see what the hole where you used to be says about it."

So I asked. I told her everything — the ledgers, the entry, the Crown Below, the king, the dragon's summons folded into a moth of ash that was even now dissolving in my pocket into fine gray script. I told her the wards had fallen from inside. I told her Corvane sold the sky, and that I meant to unmake the sale or die trying, and that the road to the capital ate travelers, and that I had no plan beyond arriving.

When I finished, she was quiet for a while, turning her glass.

"The hole in me," she said finally, "is nodding. Which is the most unsettling thing that's happened to me tonight, and tonight the stars went out." She stood. "Get up. We're taking the river road as far as Marrow Ford and cutting cross-country after. And I'm bringing the map."

"The map's mine now?"

"The map's collateral," she said, pulling the tapestry aside. "You're a bookkeeper. You'll appreciate collateral." She worked the pins free and rolled the parchment with practiced hands. "Besides, you'll need someone who can steal us past the checkpoints, and you'll need someone who can read that thing, and I do both."

"Why help me? Honestly."

"Honestly?" She slung a satchel over her shoulder and paused at the door, and for a moment her face did something complicated — grief looking for its own name and not finding it. "Because whoever I was before I paid that debt, they thought you were worth it. And I'd like to meet her someday. Might as well start by finding out if she was right."

* * *

We went out through the chandler's yard and down to the river, where the water ran black and starless under the empty sky. Sable moved ahead of me along the towpath, quick and sure-footed, and I followed with a stolen map against my chest and a moth-shaped handful of ash in my pocket that whispered directions only I could hear.

Behind us, Lowmark's bells were still ringing their shapeless alarm. Ahead, the capital waited with its hollow king and its hungry crown, four hundred miles of lying road between.

Somewhere over the drowned hills, I felt — rather than saw — the first wrongness of the night's travel settle over our path, like a hand testing a lock.

The road had noticed us.

And it was already beginning to move.
