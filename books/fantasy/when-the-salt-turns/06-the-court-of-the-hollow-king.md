## 6. The Court of the Hollow King

There is a particular smell to a royal palace, and I had expected it to be power. It is not. It is lavender over rot, the way a housekeeper masks something she has been told not to name. The Vellum Guild smells of ink and tallow and Sable's terrible pipe; the Palace of Aldric smelled of a corpse that had been assured, repeatedly, that it was fine.

We came in through the front gate, because no one suspects an auditor.

This is the great secret of my profession, and I will die defending it: nobody guards against paper. Soldiers check for knives, wards check for storms — and I have been both knife and storm, in my worse years — but no ward ever built flinches at a woman with a satchel of ledgers and a look of profound apology. Sable walked beside me wearing a clerk's grey that made her look like a punishment from a minor god, and we carried letters of authorization written on genuine Guild vellum, because Corvane's seals were still good even if Corvane's soul was in escrow.

"Remind me," Sable murmured as we crossed the third courtyard, "what we're auditing."

"Grain levities. Tax disbursements. Anything with numbers."

"And what we're actually doing?"

"Finding the king."

"Right." She adjusted her false spectacles. "And when we find him, you'll do your little rite, and I'll do my little thefting, and nobody screams."

"Somebody always screams," I said. "It's in the contract."

The courtiers met us before the second bell. I want to describe them properly, because at the time I thought they were merely courtly, and it took me until evening to understand what I had actually seen. They were beautiful the way a balanced column of figures is beautiful — symmetrical, correct, and utterly without anything alive behind them. Their smiles arrived a half-second late, like thunder after lightning. Their clothes were magnificent. And when the tallest of them, a chamberlain with hands like folded parchment, asked to see our credentials, I felt the ledger in my satchel grow warm, the way it does near a debt.

*Debts given legs,* I would think later. That night I only thought: *these people have been audited within an inch of their lives, and it shows.*

"We are honored," said the chamberlain, "by the Guild's diligence."

"We are honored by the Crown's patience," I replied, which is auditor for *please don't eat me.*

They gave us rooms in the east wing. They gave us access to the treasury records. They did not give us, anywhere in the palace, a single window that faced the sky — and once I noticed this, I could not stop noticing it. Corridors turned away from the heavens the way a face turns from a debt collector. Every hall, every stair, every gallery curved its shoulders inward, as if the whole building were hunched against a cold only it could feel.

"They know," Sable said that first night, picking the lock of our connecting door out of pure habit. "The smiling ones. They know why we're here."

"Of course they know. They just don't care, or don't *dare* care, and I haven't decided which is worse." I spread the map of the palace across both our beds. "The king takes supper alone in the west tower. Every record says so. Alone, alone, alone — three separate stewards wrote it down, which means it's a lie. Truth doesn't need that much paperwork."

Sable looked at me with something I have learned to recognize as her version of admiration. "Seven years of ledgers and you never lost the thief."

"I was never the thief, Sable. I was the person who caught them and then felt bad about it."

* * *

We went up on the fourth night, during the king's supper hour, through a servant's stair that smelled of tallow and fear. Sable went first. I carried the ledger, because I did not yet know what I would find, but I knew — the way I knew about the entry I'd missed, the way I knew the wards fell from inside — that whatever it was would need to be written down honestly, and I am the only person left in this kingdom who does that.

The west tower door was locked from the outside with a lock worth more than my childhood home. Sable had it open in forty seconds and insulted its lineage the entire time.

Inside: a small round room. A table set for one, the meal untouched and congealing into a small monument to appetite's death. A bed, neatly made. A window — the only window in the palace facing up — boarded over with silver wood, nailed with silver nails. And a man sitting on the floor beside the boarded window with his eyes closed, his lips moving, counting.

"...four hundred and eleven thousand, two hundred and six. Four hundred and eleven thousand, two hundred and seven." He opened his eyes. They were clear. That was the horror of it — I had prepared myself for madness, for vacancy, for the hollow mask the pamphlets whispered about. I had not prepared myself for a tired, intelligent man who looked at two strangers in his prison and immediately began assessing them the way I assess a suspicious entry.

"You're not servants," he said. "Servants knock. Servants are afraid. You picked the lock, which means either assassins or auditors." A pause. "I hope auditors. Assassins are no use to me at all."

"You're King Aldric," I said, stupidly, because it was true and I had nothing better.

"I'm the part they didn't take." He got up off the floor with the careful movements of a man whose body has been sitting too long, again, as it does every day. "Ten minutes," he said. "I get ten minutes a day when the crown turns its attention elsewhere — it goes down into the treasury, you see, into the counting rooms, into the dreams of anyone who owes anybody anything. It feeds at dusk. So I have learned to live by the clock, and my clock says you have perhaps eight minutes left. Who sent you?"

"The Vellum Guild," I said. "Though not the Guild that knows it sent us."

Something moved behind his face — hope, I think, arriving like a debtor at the door, half-expected and dreaded. "Then tell me what you are."

"Zephyrine Marchetti. Ledger-keeper. Exile. Storm-caller, formerly." I heard my own exile-name come out of my mouth without flinching, which surprised me more than the room did. "This is Sable Ives, who is currently stealing the hinges off your door out of professional habit."

"Hinges can be replaced," Aldric said. "Kingdoms are harder. Zephyrine Marchetti." He said my name carefully, tasting it. "You're the one who called the storm."

"Seven years ago."

"Good. Then you understand weather, and weather is what I have." He crossed to the table and pulled back the sleeve of his nightshirt, and I saw the accounts.

That is the only word I have. His forearm was covered in fine lines of ink — real ink, pricked in with a needle — columns of figures running from wrist to elbow, dates and sums and tallies in a hand so precise it hurt to read. Not scars. *Ledgers.* He had been writing the royal accounts on his own skin.

"The crown cannot rewrite numbers," he said. "Understand this. It can rewrite faces, memories, oaths, histories, the color of the sky at dusk if it likes — I have watched it do all of these. But a number is a promise with nowhere to hide. Two plus two was four before the thing below the world learned my kingdom's name, and it will be four after. Numbers are the one honest language left. So I keep them." He turned his arm to me, offering the figures like a man offering his children. "It thinks I sit here empty. It checks my mouth sometimes, makes me smile at ambassadors, has me sign things. But it stopped checking my arms years ago, because what harm can a prisoner do with arithmetic?"

He laughed then, briefly, and it was the worst sound in the room.

"Check my work," he said. "Please. You are the first person in three years who might actually be able to check my work."

So I did. I sat down on the floor of a king's prison with my ledger across my knees while Sable kept watch at the door, and I compared his tattooed columns against the treasury records I had copied, and I want to tell you what I found, because it matters.

His figures were perfect. Three years of daily entries, maintained by a man with stolen minutes, and there was not a single error. But threaded through the legitimate accounts — hidden the way he must have known only an auditor would look — were corrections. Little subtractions. Payments being quietly deducted from the Crown's coffers and routed to names that appeared nowhere else: *the miller's widow on Cordwain Lane. The orphanage at Harrow Ford. The ferryman who lost his license for refusing to ferry soldiers.*

Aldric had been embezzling from the thing that wore him. Small amounts, patient amounts, for three years, feeding the people his crown was devouring.

"It's not much," he said, watching my face. "A few thousand crowns a year, misfiled. But the widow on Cordwain Lane has bread, and no one — not the crown, not the courtiers, not God — knows where it came from. Except now you." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "You understand? It took everything. My face, my days, my wife's memory of me — she visits, you know, and I smile at her with its smile, and she tells the smiling one she still loves him. Everything. But it cannot take the arithmetic. It cannot take the four hundred and eleven thousand, two hundred and seven, because I will simply count to four hundred and eleven thousand, two hundred and eight tomorrow, and the day after, and someday someone will ask why I'm counting, and maybe — *maybe* — that someone will be able to use it."

"That's why you begged a stranger to audit you," I said quietly. "You weren't asking to be rescued."

"Rescue is a fairy tale," Aldric said. "I was asking for a witness. A number unwitnessed is just scratching. A number witnessed—" he touched his inked forearm, almost tenderly, "—is an account. And every account comes due."

Somewhere below us, a bell rang dusk. Aldric's face changed — not gradually, not like a mood passing, but like a lamp being shuttered. The clarity drained out of his eyes and the smile came up over him like water rising in a cellar, and the last thing the real king did was grab my wrist hard enough to bruise and press something into my palm — a sliver of silver nail, snapped off the boards over his window.

"Beneath the throne," he said, fast, his own mouth already curving against his will. "Beneath the throne there's a door that isn't a door, and the figures I've memorized are the key, and I have been counting toward this for three years, auditor, so don't you *dare* be late—"

And then the smile finished arriving, warm and gracious and wrong as a paid compliment, and the King of the Hollow Court shook my hand and thanked me for the Guild's diligence and saw us courteously to the door.

In the corridor, Sable looked at me. I opened my hand. In it lay the silver nail, and around it, pricked into my palm where he'd gripped me, faint as breath on glass: a string of numbers.

"What is it?" she whispered.

I stared at the figures until they burned, and I understood that Aldric had not been keeping accounts to stay sane. He had been building a key, one stolen minute at a time, and he had just handed it to the only person in the kingdom fluent in the language it was written in.

"It's the combination," I said. "To the door under the throne. And Sable — he memorized it in a language only I can read."

Below us, deep beneath the floor, something vast turned over in its sleep, and every coin in the treasury rang once, softly, like a throat clearing.
