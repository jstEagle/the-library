## 7. Corvane's Confession

I have stolen a great many things in my life, and I can tell you the weight of each one. A duchess's signet ring is lighter than you'd think. A reliquary bone is heavier. But nothing I have ever lifted — not crowns, not keys, not the ceremonial dagger I once took off a sleeping bishop just to see if I could — weighs as much as paper.

Specifically, this paper. The royal ledgers of Aldric's court, seven years of accounts, and I was copying them by mothlight in a linen closet off the east gallery because it was the only room in the palace without eyes in the walls.

Let me back up.

Zephyrine had gone to do whatever Zephyrine does — that careful, terrible ritual-work they'd been sketching on scraps of map for three days, all arrows and angles and one word written so small I pretended not to read it: *unbinding*. That left me the numbers. The king, lucid for his ten minutes that morning, had pressed a scrap into Zephyrine's hand with figures scrawled across it, and those figures had to mean something, and the only place meaning lived anymore was ink.

So there I was, in a linen closet that smelled of lavender and old lies, copying entries into my memory the way I used to copy locks: by feel, by rhythm, by trusting my hands to remember what my head couldn't hold fast enough. *One sky, sold.* I'd found that entry an hour ago and hadn't stopped shaking since. Not the guild ledger. The royal one. Corvane had sold it *twice* — once in his own books, once in the king's, like a man who tells the same lie in two churches to be sure God hears it.

The door opened.

I want it noted that I did not freeze. I simply became very still, which is different, and which has kept me alive through eleven careers. I was holding a sheaf of copied pages and a stub of charcoal pencil, illuminated by a moth made of ash that had refused to leave since Emberlast sent it, and none of these things are concealable.

Guildmaster Corvane closed the door behind him and latched it.

He looked older than the man who'd signed Zephyrine's exile papers. Or perhaps he'd always looked like that and I'd never stood close enough in bad light to see it: the gray under his skin, the way his shadow pooled wrong, as if something beneath him were leaning up against the floor from underneath.

"You're the thief," he said. "Sable Ives. Paid a debt in memories."

"Allegedly," I said. "You have me at a disadvantage. You know my crimes and I only know yours, and mine are more charming."

He took the pages from my hand. I let him, because the alternative was dying in a linen closet, and I've always had standards about where I'm found.

He read them. All of them. His face did nothing, which frightened me more than any expression could have, because faces that do nothing have been emptied on purpose.

"Zephyrine sent you," he said.

"Zephyrine doesn't send anyone anywhere. Zephyrine walks toward horrible things and I follow out of what I can only assume is a brain defect."

That almost — *almost* — moved his mouth. "Yes. They were like that here too. Seven years ago. Always walking toward the fire and calling it an accounting error."

"They were exiled for a storm they didn't mean to call."

"They were exiled," Corvane said quietly, "because I needed them gone before they started asking about the weather."

The closet got very small. The ash-moth circled once, twice, settling on my shoulder like a hand.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Corvane said. "Not because you deserve it. Because no one else is left who'll understand it, and a man can only carry a thing alone for so long before he starts setting it down in closets."

* * *

"There was a famine," he said. "You wouldn't know. It's not in any history, because I bought the historians first. Seven years ago — eleven years, really, the ledgers run long — the rains failed. Not one season. Three. And the border wards, the great ones your predecessors called the Shepherd's Ring, held the weather in patterns. Kept the storms moving, the rains cycling, the whole sky behaving like a well-run account."

"And the sky stopped balancing."

"The sky went into arrears." He said it like a confession in church, flat and practiced and worn smooth from saying. "There's a mathematics under everything, thief. Weather. Luck. Stars. It all runs on credit, and the wards were how we managed the payments. And the famine — the true famine, the one under the grain famine — was a famine of *credit*. The sky was overdrawn. Every ward we fed bought us a month. Every month cost more. I was spending the kingdom's future weather, and I ran out of future."

"So you found a lender."

"I found a buyer." He corrected me the way a teacher corrects a student, gently, horribly. "The Crown Below doesn't lend. It purchases. It wanted collateral, and I had very little left. Grain reserves: gone. Guild favors: spent. And then it named its price, and thief, I want you to hear this properly, because everyone after me will tell it wrong."

The ash-moth trembled on my shoulder.

"It asked for the sky. Not the *use* of it — the ownership. The stars, the wards, the pattern of it, all of it, deeded over. And in exchange it would balance the books below: rain in the right places, harvests that held, children who ate." His voice didn't shake. That was the worst part. He'd rehearsed this until it came out clean. "Do you know what a famine costs? I do. I sat with the numbers for nine nights. Eleven thousand children, in the capital alone, by midwinter, if the sky stayed broken. I did the arithmetic a hundred times hoping it would change. It never changed. So I signed. One heaven against eleven thousand children. Thief, I would sign it again tonight."

"Eleven thousand children grew up," I said. "Under no stars."

"Yes."

"They'll have children of their own someday, under no stars."

"Yes."

"And their children."

"*Yes,*" he said, and now his voice did crack, one hairline fracture down the middle of all that practice. "That's the shape of it. That's what a debt is. I bought eleven thousand lives with every life that comes after, forever, and I knew it when I signed, and I signed anyway, and I have read the entry every night for seven years waiting for it to turn into something I could live inside." He folded my stolen pages with terrible neatness and set them on the shelf among the linens. "The villain was right once. Do you understand? That's the trap. Not the mistake. The *once*. Once you've been right when everyone called you monstrous, you stop believing anyone can judge you, including yourself. After that, every sale is easier. The second year it wanted the dreams of the border villages. Small things, it said. The third year, memories — that's where your friend Sable Ives lost hers, incidentally; the crown collects through intermediaries, and the guild was its intermediary, and I signed for that too. The fourth year it wanted the king."

"And you gave it Aldric."

"Aldric offered," Corvane said. "That's the part no one will believe. He came to me. He'd found the entries — he was a better accountant than I ever was, it's why the crown-thing wears him so well, numbers are the one spine it can't fully replace. He said a king should be the collateral of his own people. He walked into that transaction with his eyes open and his ten minutes a day intact because even the Crown Below keeps honest margins on one line item." His mouth twisted. "It honors its contracts to the letter, thief. That's what makes it a devil instead of a monster. Monsters are merely hungry. This thing *itemizes*."

I thought of the king that morning, pressing his scraps of figures into a stranger's hands, begging someone — anyone — to check his work. Doing bookkeeping in the ruins of his own skull. Keeping the one set of books his jailer couldn't touch.

"He's been auditing from inside," I said.

"For seven years. Every figure he memorizes is a line the crown cannot rewrite. He's been building the key to his own cell one number at a time, and he couldn't use it, because a key does nothing without someone outside the lock." Corvane finally looked at me, and his eyes were the grayest thing in the room. "And then a moth flew to an exiled bookkeeper, and I understood that the last dragon had decided the account was due."

"Why tell me this?" I asked. "Why not kill me and burn the closet?"

"Because Zephyrine is going to attempt the unbinding, and the unbinding requires a name freely surrendered, and when they learn whose name the rite truly needs—" he stopped himself, jaw tightening, some final door closing behind his eyes. "When they learn, they will need to choose whether to hate me. I'd rather they hate me with the whole truth. It's the only inheritance I have left that isn't counterfeit."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." He unlatched the door. Paused. "Thief. When you copy a lock, you must understand the mechanism or you'll spring it wrong. Understand this one: the crown-thing is not keeping the sky out of malice. It's keeping it because it was *sold*, honestly and legally, by a man who meant it. Contracts freely entered bind tighter than chains. To break it, Zephyrine won't need power." His hand rested on the latch a moment longer. "They'll need something freely given that outweighs a freely given sky."

Then he was gone, down the gallery, his shadow pooling wrong against the wall, and I stood in the lavender dark with the ash-moth shaking on my shoulder and seven years of stolen truth in my head.

I've carried a lot of stolen things. I always knew what to do with them: sell them, spend them, forget them in a drawer somewhere.

This one I had to carry back to Zephyrine.

And worse — far worse, the thought that made my hands unsteady for the first time in eleven careers — I knew, watching the moth burn slowly down toward nothing, exactly what Corvane hadn't said. The rite needed a name freely surrendered. Zephyrine had already decided to give their exile-name.

But the exile-name had never been theirs to give.

Corvane had written it into the guild's books seven years ago — and anything written in those ledgers belonged, by the oldest law of the Vellum Guild, to whoever held the page.

Me and my brain defect. I blew out the mothlight, tucked the copied pages against my heart where they weighed the most, and went to find the only person in the kingdom who might still forgive me for what I was about to hand them.
