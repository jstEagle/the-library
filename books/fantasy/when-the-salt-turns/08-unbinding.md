## 8. Unbinding

The throne room of Aldric's palace smelled of cold candlewax and something older underneath — old incense, maybe, or the memory of it, soaked so deep into the stone that no fire would ever bring it out again. I stood at the foot of the dais with a ledger open across my forearms like a shield, and I thought: *seven years balancing accounts in a guildhall, and this is where it ends. In someone else's books.*

Aldric sat on his throne and shook.

Not the way a man shakes when he is afraid. The way a house shakes when something inside it is trying to get out. His face — that smiling, golden, wrong face — was turned toward the great doors we had come through, toward the sound of Sable's voice rising over the courtiers' footsteps. But behind the smile, in the eyes, something else was looking at me. Something tired. Something *counting*.

"Zephyrine Marchetti," he said, and his voice was the king's voice, warm as honey poured over a blade. "The Guild sends its auditor at last."

"I'm not here for the guild." I climbed one step of the dais. The crown-thing's smile didn't waver, but the shoulders under it went rigid. "I'm here for you."

"Then you're too late," said the smile, "the accounts are closed—"

"*Eleven thousand four hundred and six,*" said the man beneath.

It came out cracked, half-swallowed, like a voice through a wall. The crown-thing's jaw clenched. For one heartbeat the smile was just a mouth, stretched too wide over teeth that were only teeth, and behind it Aldric fought to be heard.

"The grain tallies," he rasped. "Winter of the famine. Eleven thousand four hundred and six. That was the number. That was always the number. Check me against it — *check me* —"

"I know," I said. "I've got it written down."

And I did. Three nights in the royal archives with Sable picking locks while I copied figures by mothlight, and there it was, in Aldric's own boyhood hand: eleven thousand four hundred and six souls fed through a winter that should have buried them. The number he had memorized because it was the number he could not let anyone rewrite. The number the crown-thing had spent twenty years trying to make him forget, because a king who remembered what a soul cost might start asking what a sky cost too.

"You cannot unbind what is freely worn," the crown-thing said, and now it wasn't bothering with warmth. The smile stayed; everything else drained out of the voice until it sounded like wind down an empty stairwell. "He wears Me willingly. He *asked*. Ask him. Ask him if he asked."

"I did ask," I said. "Last night. For ten minutes."

I climbed another step. My knees were not happy about any of this. Exile does terrible things to your sense of ceremony; I kept expecting someone to appear and fine me for being out of bounds.

"He said yes once. Twenty years ago. To save eleven thousand people. That was a bargain, and bargains are honest even when they're ugly." Another step. "But he hasn't been allowed to say anything since, have you, Your Majesty? A contract signed under duress isn't binding. And a contract where one party has been locked in a cupboard behind his own face for two decades—" I set my boot on the last step. "*That's* not consent. That's forgery. And forgery, sir, is very much within my jurisdiction."

From below us, from the great doors, came a crash — wood splitting, and Sable's voice, bright and breathless: "Zephyrine! Whenever you're ready! Preferably before they finish inventing new numbers of themselves!"

I looked back once. The courtiers were coming up the aisle — and up was the right word for it, because they moved like things that had never quite learned how bodies worked. They wore silk and powdered faces, and every one of them had a seal stamped somewhere visible: on a throat, a wrist, a brow. Wax seals, red as old wounds.

They were debts. Of course they were debts. I'd known since the wards fell, since I read Corvane's hidden entries and understood that the Crown Below collected on everything, and interest compounds. Every favor the crown-thing had ever granted, every whispered promise to every hungry courtier — all called due at once, and walking upright.

One of them reached the dais steps and smiled at me with a face that had been beautiful about forty years ago and had been spending that beauty ever since. "Little auditor," it said. "Your name is owed too. Did you know? Seven years ago. Someone wrote it into a ledger as payment. We've come to collect."

"That entry," I said, "has been disputed."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rites of unbinding: they are bookkeeping.

All the songs describe them as swords and screaming and light pouring out of the wound in the world. And yes, there was some of that. But first there was procedure. You need three things: the true figure (Aldric's eleven thousand, held in memory where ink couldn't touch it), the original instrument (the ledger page where Corvane had recorded the sale of the sky, which I had torn out and carried against my heart like the world's worst love letter), and —

"—a name," I read aloud, my finger on the line, while below us Sable put a knife into somewhere unspeakable on a courtier made entirely of outstanding obligations, "freely surrendered, by the bound or by a surety of equal weight. The surrender must be true. It cannot be coerced, purchased, or performed. False surrenders are void and rebound upon the offeror."

"Charming," said Sable, from the bottom of the steps, backed against a pillar with three debts closing in. "Any chance the book offers tips on the fighting part?"

"No. It assumes the fighting part is someone else's problem."

"It usually is!"

I turned back to the throne. Aldric — the real one, the flickering one — was watching me through the mask of his own face, and his lips moved without sound, shaping words I couldn't catch. Then, faintly, through the wall of gold: "*Numbers.* Say the numbers first. Make it listen to arithmetic. It hates that it can't rewrite what it can't hear properly."

So I did the thing that has gotten me exiled, employed, and nearly killed, in roughly that order. I opened the ledger to a fresh page, and I began to read Aldric's memorized figures aloud in a clear, carrying, insufferably precise voice. Grain in. Souls fed. Days of winter. Deaths prevented — zero, that year, thanks to the bargain, though heaven knew what it cost elsewhere. Interest accrued on a kingdom's sky, compounding nightly, star by vanished star.

The courtiers flinched at each figure like blows. Debts hate audit. It is the one thing they cannot survive.

The crown-thing rose off the throne — actually rose, its shadow staying seated below it like a discarded coat — and the temperature of the room dropped toward the temperature of the space between stars, which was appropriate, since it had eaten ours.

"You will stop counting," it said.

"It's literally my job."

"You will stop counting or I will take his mouth away, and then his eyes, and then—"

"Then the contract's void anyway," I said, and my voice only shook a little. "You need him whole enough to be willing. That's why you keep him lucid ten minutes a day. That's why he gets to beg strangers to check the accounts. You *need* the fiction of consent. So no, you won't damage him. You'll posture. Posture away."

For a moment the whole throne room hung on the edge of itself. And then the crown-thing did the most frightening thing it could have done.

It laughed.

"Clever clerk," it said. "Corvane said you were clever. Do you know what he traded for your exile, seven years ago? Not your freedom. Your *name*. Zephyrine Marchetti died on paper that night; the person standing here is a fiction the Vellum Guild maintains for its own purposes. So tell me, little auditor — what will you surrender? What do you actually *have*?"

And that was the moment I understood that I had already decided, days ago, on a road that rearranged itself nightly, when I first spoke my storm aloud and felt it answer.

Because here is the truth about my exile-name. The guild gave it to me when they took me in: *Marchetti-of-the-Vellum*, sheltered, recorded, safe. But names aren't given. They're *kept*, the way ledgers keep entries — and the name I had been keeping, all these years, the name I balanced books under and answered to and hid inside, was the name Corvane assigned me. A holding-name. An entry pending reconciliation.

The name I surrendered had to be true. And the truest thing about me, the one entry I had never once falsified, was this: I was the storm-caller who never meant to call it. That name — the exile-name, the one shouted across a border seven years ago, the one that had cost me everything and taught me exactly what hiding costs everyone else — *that* was mine. Freely kept, all these years. Which meant it could be freely given.

"Sable!" I called down. "How long can you hold them?"

There was a pause full of tearing silk and profanity. "Define 'hold'!"

"Thirty seconds?"

"THAT'S NOT A DEFINITION!"

"Thirty seconds, then!"

I stepped up to the throne, tore Corvane's page from my coat, and laid it over the crown's reflection in the polished armrest — the original instrument, the sale of the sky, ink still remembering. Then I took Aldric's hands. They were freezing. Under the gold smile, tears were running silently down the king's face, and his mouth shaped the same word over and over: *check, check, check.*

"I'm checking," I told him gently. "Auditor's oath. Everything balances in the end."

And I spoke the rite.

"The figure stands: eleven thousand four hundred and six. The instrument stands: one sky, sold, seller Corvane, buyer the Crown Below. Consent: forged. Contract: void. And for the surety required—" my voice did not break, though it was a near-run thing, "—I surrender the name I was exiled under. Freely. Truly. Not for redemption. Redemption's for people who did it on purpose. I give it because it's *true*, and truth is the only currency this creature can't counterfeit."

The exile-name left me the way a storm leaves a coastline — all at once, and taking pieces of the shore with it. I felt it go: seven years of it, the border, the shame, the careful rule-bound life built on top of the wound. It poured out of my chest and into the ledger page, and the page drank it, and the wax seals on every courtier in the room went soft and slid loose—

Below us, the debts collapsed. All of them. Silk puddling around empty seals, obligations finally discharged, and Sable stood ankle-deep in dead paperwork saying "oh, *thank* the—" before the crown-thing screamed.

Not with Aldric's mouth. With the sky's mouth. Through the wound above the palace, through the starless hole where our heavens used to be, something vast turned its attention down upon the throne room, upon the voided contract, upon the small stubborn clerk who had audited it out of house and home—

—and the walls blew open, and the night poured in, and wings filled it. Enormous, ragged, ember-lit wings, and a voice like a mountain clearing its throat:

"*THE LEDGER CALLED. I ANSWER.*"

Emberlast had come.

And the crown-thing tore itself free of King Aldric like a hand pulling out of a glove, and rose to meet the last dragon-fire in the world.
