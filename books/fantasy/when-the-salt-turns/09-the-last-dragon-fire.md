## 9. The Last Dragon-Fire

I have flown a long time, and I have learned that the sky keeps accounts too.

Every wingbeat is a withdrawal. Every fire spent is an entry in a ledger no one audits — except me. I have been my own ledger-keeper for three hundred years, which is to say I have been lonely and precise about it. Dragons do not weep. We do arithmetic instead, and when the sums come out wrong, we burn something until they don't.

Tonight the sum was going to come out wrong no matter what I burned.

The stars had been dark for six days. Six days of flying under a sky like the inside of a closed book, black vellum stretched from horizon to horizon, and beneath it the roads had begun to hunt, and the rivers to forget their courses, and small frightened things — hares, hedge-witches, one memorable tax collector — to look up at nothing and feel the nothing looking back. I flew through it because there was nowhere left to land. A dragon without sky might as well be a very large, very disappointed lizard.

And then, over the capital, the wound opened.

* * *

You should understand what a wound in the sky looks like from above, because almost no one has ever seen one and lived to complain about it.

It did not tear. Tearing is honest work; tearing has edges you can grab. The sky *unraveled*, thread by thread, the way a cheap cloak comes apart at the shoulder seam, and through the gap came the smell of the Crown Below — cold iron and wet stone and interest accruing. Through the gap I could see the thing that had been wearing King Aldric like a coat worn so long the coat forgot it wasn't the man.

It was coming out.

I felt it before I saw it: a pressure on my old bones like deep water, like debt. It rose through the throne room roof in a column of shadow that had opinions, and the shadow spread wings it had copied from something that once flew, badly, the way a counterfeit coin copies a face. Below it, on the palace steps and the plaza and every street radiating outward, stood its courtiers — the debts given legs, as the little moth had told me, ash-voiced and patient. They did not move. Debts never need to move. They only need to wait for you.

But in the heart of all that waiting, two small figures were still working.

One stood in the shattered frame of a throne room window with her arms full of stolen ledgers and a knife between her teeth, which told me everything I would ever need to know about Sable Ives. The other knelt at the center of the plaza, chalk lines burning white around them, hands pressed flat against the stones, and around them the air smelled of ozone and rain-that-hadn't-fallen-yet.

Zephyrine Marchetti. The exile who called a storm by accident and spent seven years pretending to be an accountant.

I had sent them a moth made of my own ash. I confess I had not expected much. Moths are not reliable correspondents, and accountants are not reliable heroes, and I am an old dragon who has been disappointed by both professions. But there they were, kneeling in the chalk, holding open a door in the world with nothing but arithmetic and stubbornness, while the crown-thing poured out of Aldric like smoke deciding to become a problem.

The rite needed a name freely surrendered. That part was theirs to give. But names are only kindling, little accountant. Kindling needs fire.

It needed mine.

* * *

Here is the thing about being the last of anything: everyone assumes it means you are precious. Collectors assume it. Kings assume it. Guildmasters who sell skies certainly assume it. But lastness does not make a thing precious. It makes a thing *final*. There is a difference. Precious things are kept. Final things are spent, eventually, on whatever matters most, because keeping a final thing forever is just another way of losing it — slowly, comfortably, in a vault, alone.

I had kept my fire for three hundred years. I had rationed it through wars and plagues and one truly regrettable century of fashion, hoarding it the way misers hoard gold, telling myself some future emergency would justify the expense. And every year the vault got quieter, and the arithmetic got sadder, and I understood, somewhere under my scales, that I was not preserving a treasure. I was presiding over an estate sale with no buyers.

The crown-thing rose above the palace and began, with great ceremony, to eat what remained of the sky.

That decided me.

* * *

Diving on a god is not like diving on a knight. Knights flinch. This thing did not flinch; it *accounted* for me, the way Corvane must have accounted for me seven years ago when he sold the heavens — one dragon, aging, irrelevant, filed under assets that would never be called. I felt its attention slide across my hide like a clerk's damp finger down a column, finding my number, dismissing it.

Good. Let it dismiss me. Every fraud in history has been undone by the entry it didn't bother to read.

I came out of the dark like the answer to a question nobody asked aloud, and I opened my throat, and I gave it everything I had saved.

Not a gout. Not a breath. Three hundred years of it, all at once, the whole hoard spent in a single glorious insolvent moment — white fire, the old fire, the kind that remembers when stars were young and cheap and plentiful. It caught the crown-thing mid-unfolding, caught the shadow-wings mid-beat, caught the whole smug architecture of it and lit it from inside like a lantern made of someone else's mistakes. The thing screamed in a voice like a ledger closing. Its courtiers on the streets below simply stopped, all at once, the way debts stop when the debtor dies — not gone, mind you, never gone, but finally, blessedly, *inactive*.

And the fire did not stop at the crown-thing. Fire this old doesn't know where you meant it to go. It went up.

It went up through the wound in the sky, into the unraveling, into the torn threads of the firmament, and it caught there the way flame catches on oilcloth — running along the frayed edges, licking into every gap, filling the hole the Guildmaster's greed had punched in the roof of the world. I watched my own death climb away from me in ribbons of light and stitch itself into the dark.

The first star relit like a struck match.

Then ten. Then a hundred, then more than I could count, which for a creature of my age is saying something — stars blooming back into the black one after another, each one a small white entry posted to an account that had been overdrawn for seven years. The sky paid itself back in fire. My fire. All of it. Every coin in the vault, spent at last on the only purchase that had ever mattered.

I have done arithmetic for three hundred years. That night, for the first time, the sum came out even.

* * *

Below me, the plaza went quiet in the way places go quiet when the impossible finishes happening and hasn't yet decided what it cost.

Zephyrine Marchetti knelt in the chalk with their head bowed, and I saw — dragons see well — the small curl of ash drifting up from the circle where an exile-name used to be. Burned. Freely given. I had asked them for kindling and they had handed me the whole hearth. Somewhere in the crowd a woman was laughing and crying at once, and a child pointed up at the new-old stars and asked a question no adult could answer, and Sable Ives stood on the palace steps with her stolen ledgers clutched to her chest like a cat that had decided the theft was now a rescue.

And from the broken doorway of the throne room came King Aldric.

He walked badly. Whatever the crown-thing had worn him as, it had taken the shape along with the years; he moved like a house with the furniture removed, all rooms and no living in them. He descended the steps one at a time, gripping the rail, and stopped before Zephyrine, and I heard him speak in a voice scraped thin as vellum:

"The accounts," he said. "Did anyone check the accounts?"

"Every line," said Zephyrine Marchetti, accountant, exile, storm-caller, nameless. "They balance."

Aldric nodded, slowly, the way a man nods when he has carried something heavy for years and is permitted at last to set it down. Then he turned to face his city — hollowed, yes, emptied of himself, but standing upright, which is more than most kings manage even when full — and he abdicated. Aloud. Formally. Into the custody of the Vellum Guild, of all the ridiculous institutions in the world, because it was the guild's ledgers that had held the truth, and the kingdom had learned the hard way that truth kept anywhere else gets rewritten.

Sable caught the king when his knees went. Of course she did. Thieves always know how to catch falling things; it's the whole profession.

* * *

I was dying by then. You will want details; dragons are private about this, but I find I no longer mind.

Fire spends from the inside out. When the last of it went up through the wound, what remained of me was mostly memory and momentum, and momentum is generous but not loyal. I was gliding now rather than flying, wide circles over the capital, lower with every turn, and the stars overhead were bright enough to read by, and I found I wanted to read them. I wanted to check my work one final time.

There. The Weaver, her loom rethreaded. There, the Salt Road, shining again from horizon to horizon. And there — new, or perhaps merely forgotten, or perhaps written just now by some celestial auditor with a sense of humor — a constellation I did not know: a long serpentine figure with spread wings, head lifted, mid-flight, forever arriving.

Ah, I thought. So that's where they're posting me.

I want to be clear about this, because histories will get it wrong, histories always do: I did not sacrifice myself. Sacrifice implies reluctance overcome. What I felt was closer to what a miser feels throwing open a vault he'd forgotten the purpose of, or a singer holding a note held too long, or an accountant writing, at the end of a long dark column, the final number that makes everything above it mean something. Some prices are not endured. Some prices are *paid gladly*, with both hands, and the paying is the point, and the grief afterward is just the receipt.

I was glad. Remember that part.

My wings stopped answering somewhere over the river. I did not fight it. I tipped into the long glide, the oldest dance my kind knows, and the city below shrank to embers and lantern-light, and above me the stars burned in their hundreds, every single one of them mine, every single one of them paid in full.

The last thing I saw was a small curl of ash rising from the plaza — my moth, or what remained of it, climbing toward the constellations like a courier delivering the final page.

Deliver it well, little one. Tell them the accounts are settled. Tell them the dragon checked.

Tell them—

* * *

Far below, in a plaza chalked white with a finished rite, Zephyrine Marchetti looked up at the new constellation, then down at the open ledger in their hands, and wrote the first line of the entry the whole kingdom would be reading by morning: *Received: one sky, returned. Paid: one dragon, gladly.*
