## 10. First Honest Entry

The stars came back on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Nothing in the Vellum Guild's ledgers had ever suggested the heavens kept business hours, but there they were at dawn, scattered back across the wound in the sky like coins spilled from a torn purse, and the city below them wept and cheered and immediately began arguing about who deserved credit.

I watched it all from the guildhall steps with ink still under my fingernails and a name that no longer existed anywhere except in my own mouth. *Zephyrine* — not Marchetti, not the exile-name, just Zephyrine, the name I'd burned to rekindle the sky, the name I'd somehow been given back in the ashes, the way Emberlast had been given back as a scatter of new constellations that the astronomers were already fighting over.

They'd named one of them after me. I found this embarrassing and intended to ignore it for the rest of my life.

"You're standing where Corvane used to stand," Sable said, coming up beside me with two cups of something hot and suspicious. She handed me one. "People are going to start getting ideas."

"People have been getting ideas all week." It was true. Since Aldric's abdication — signed, witnessed, and filed, because even a hollowed king deserves proper paperwork — a steady stream of guild members had climbed those same steps to find me and say things like *the hall needs a master* and *you've earned it* and, most damning of all, *at least you're honest.* The last one was usually said in the tone of someone apologizing to a dog they'd once kicked.

"Have you decided?"

"I've decided I'm terrible at deciding," I said. "It's why I was good at ledgers. Ledgers decide themselves. Two columns, and the truth falls out."

Sable snorted into her cup. Seven years ago she had paid a debt to me in memories and remembered nothing of why; three weeks ago she'd copied royal ledgers under threat of death and held off a court made of walking debts while I unbound a king. Somewhere in between she had apparently decided she liked me, or at least found me useful, which in her accounting amounted to the same thing. She was wearing the guild's gray now, badly, sleeves rolled up like the uniform had personally offended her.

"The council's waiting," she said. "Again. Third time today. They've got the chain of office out."

"The chain is very shiny."

"It has a dragon on it. Someone polished the dragon."

I looked at the chain when they brought it up — because of course they did, they carried it up the steps like pallbearers, four senior factors with faces like men delivering bad news to themselves — and the dragon on it was indeed polished to a blinding gleam, and it was modeled on nothing so much as Emberlast, and I had to stand there while Guildmaster-elect-to-be-if-she'd-please Corvane's-successor was offered a dead friend's likeness in silver.

"No," I said.

The factors blinked. One of them, old Master Quillon, who had taught me double-entry before he taught me to fear the third column, actually looked relieved.

"Zephyrine," he began, "the guild needs—"

"The guild needs its history read aloud," I said. "In full. Every entry. Including the ones in Corvane's hand, including the ones under mine, including the seven years of entries nobody questioned because questioning entries isn't what ledger-keepers do." I took the chain from Quillon's hands — gently, because it wasn't his fault, none of it had ever been only his fault — and set it on the step between us. "I'm not refusing you. I'm declining the job you're offering and applying for the one that doesn't exist yet."

Quillon's relief curdled into confusion. "Which is?"

"Keeper of the ledgers. Just that. No mastership, no chain, no vote on trade routes. Someone whose entire duty is that the books tell the truth, and that anyone can come in off the street and read them." I paused. "And an auditor. Of impossible things."

"Auditor," repeated the youngest factor, tasting the word like spoiled milk.

"Someone checks that what we sell does what we claim. That bottled thunder thunders. That a debt paid in years was fairly weighed. That a sky sold to the Crown Below gets returned when the terms lapse." I smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, but it was an honest one, and I was learning to tell the difference. "We traded impossible things for two hundred years without once asking whether *we* were being impossible. That ends today."

* * *

The reopening of the ledgers took nine days.

Not because the writing took long — though it did; seven years of truth runs long — but because every page had to be written where anyone could see it. We moved the great ledger-stand from the vault to the guildhall's front room, under the window, and I sat there in full view of the street and wrote.

I wrote about the night the wards fell, and how they fell from inside, and how I knew and told no one for eleven hours because Corvane asked me to seal the books first. I wrote that entry in full, with the exact wording of his order, and then beneath it: *I obeyed. The delay cost lives on the border roads. This entry is my sentence for it, served publicly.*

I wrote about Sable Ives, and the debt she'd paid in memories, and — this was the hardest entry — the fact that I had never told her why, because the knowing would have hurt her worse than the not-knowing, or so I'd decided on her behalf, alone, without asking. *I used her the way Corvane used me,* I wrote. *She deserved better than my discretion. If she reads this and chooses to hate me, the guild will witness the account as settled and fair.*

She read it on the fourth day, standing over my shoulder with her arms crossed, silent for so long that a client at the counter started to fidget.

"That's why I stayed on the road with you," she finally said. "On the starless road. When the maps were lying. Something in me — some hole where the memory used to be — kept saying *stay with the ledger-keeper, stay with the ledger-keeper,* like a coin rolling around in an empty purse." She looked down at me. "You could have left it buried."

"You could have hated me properly."

"I considered it." A pause. "I've decided against. But I'm keeping the entry. Frame it. Let everyone see the auditor's first audit was of you."

So we framed it, and hung it by the door, and clients coming in to buy bottled thunder or rent a luck-month or sell a dream they didn't want anymore walked under it, and more than a few stopped to read, and some of them turned around and walked back out, and the guild's revenue fell by a third in the first month.

Master Quillon told me this with something close to joy. "A third," he said. "Corvane would have called it ruin."

"Corvane called a famine solvable by selling the sky," I said. "His arithmetic had a blind spot the size of heaven."

"And yours?"

"Mine says a third of our trade was people buying things they didn't need from an institution they trusted too much. Let them keep their coin. The ones who stay will be worth having."

He laughed — actually laughed, an old man's dry rustle — and went off to draft the charter amendment, and I turned to the day's real work.

* * *

The entry took me until evening. I wrote slowly, in the hand I'd learned at fifteen, the hand Corvane had praised, the hand that had recorded a feather from a dragon thought extinct and never once asked where the dragon was now.

*Entry 4,411, in the year the stars returned:*

*Let it be recorded that the kingdom's sky was sold by Guildmaster Edran Corvane to the entity styling itself the Crown Below, in exchange for the warding of the harvest borders during the famine years, and that the terms were never written down because the Crown Below required silence as collateral. Let it be recorded that Corvane believed at the time he was saving children, and that he was, and that he continued afterward to collect interest on a bargain he no longer controlled, and that this is what bargains do. Let it be recorded that King Aldric was taken as surety, and has been freed, and abdicates into this guild's custody not as a prisoner but as a debtor, and that this guild owes him service until the crown-thing's claims are fully discharged — which, by the Crown Below's own schedule, will take ninety-one years.*

*Ninety-one years,* I thought, dipping the pen. *Well. Someone should live long enough to see the final payment posted. It might as well be the person who found the entry in the first place.*

*Let it be recorded that Emberlast, last keeper of dragon-fire, gave the whole of its fire to reignite the sky, and died mid-flight above the capital, and is now a constellation visible from the guildhall window on clear nights, and that this guild's debt to dragons is therefore total and permanent and cannot be entered in any column, and should be remembered anyway.*

*Let it be recorded that Zephyrine — no surname, the exile-name burned, the true name returned — declined the guildmastership and takes instead the post of Keeper of Ledgers, First Honest Entry, with authority over no one and answerability to everyone.*

*Let it be recorded that the undersigned was wrong for seven years, not in malice but in comfort, and that comfort is how kingdoms get sold.*

*I sign this with my own hand, in open view, and invite correction.*

Below the signature line, someone had already penciled a note — Sable's handwriting, unmistakably, slantwise and smug: *Audited. Found honest. Don't let it go to your head.*

* * *

I blew out the lamp near midnight, when the hall was empty and the new stars were sharp through the glass. Emberlast's constellation hung low over the rooftops — the astronomers had given it a formal name, *The Faithful Scale,* which I pretended not to find moving.

There was a knock at the door. At midnight. From the street.

Sable appeared at my elbow with a knife already drawn, because she was Sable. Through the window we both saw the figure standing on the steps: small, robed in travel-gray, face shadowed — and holding up, cupped in both hands like an offering, a moth made of ash.

Emberlast's summons-moth. The kind that had come to me in the vault. Except Emberlast was dead, and ash moths don't survive their makers, and this one was beating its wings steadily, patiently, the way a creature does when it has flown a very long way and expects to be answered.

I opened the door.

The figure pushed back its hood, and the moth drifted past us into the hall, trailing a smell of cold high places, and settled on the open ledger — on tomorrow's blank page — and began, in tiny cinders, to write.

"Well," Sable said. "That's going to need an audit."

I reached for my pen.

* * *

*End of Book One.*
