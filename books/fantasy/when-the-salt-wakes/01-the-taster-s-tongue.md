## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The bell over the kitchen arch rang at half past five, as it always did, and Paloma Almeida was already awake beneath it.

She had learned years ago that the trick to surviving Vell's royal kitchens was not skill, exactly. The head cooks had skill. The pastry mistress had genius. What Paloma had was a body that refused to die, and the wisdom to make that look effortless. So she rose in the dark before the bell could finish its second chime, dressed in the plain grey wool of the lower servants, braided her hair back tight enough to last fourteen hours, and stood at her narrow window watching the valley of Vell assemble itself out of fog.

Mist pooled in the low fields like spilled milk. Beyond it, the mountains stood shoulder to shoulder all around the valley, gray-green and indifferent, closing it off from every road that mattered. Somewhere past those ridges there were kingdoms with names she'd only heard whispered by merchants who drank too much and lied too easily. Paloma had never seen one. She had been born inside this bowl of earth, like her mother, and like her grandmother before her, and the mountains did not particularly care what any of them wanted.

She went down.

The kitchen corridor smelled of wet stone and yesterday's smoke, and by the time she reached the great room, forty people were already moving through steam and shouted orders with the choreography of people who had done this ten thousand times. Nobody greeted her. That was not an insult; it was an arrangement. A food-taster who made friends made liabilities, and a taster with liabilities hesitated, and hesitation got people killed. Paloma had spent nine years becoming furniture — useful, clean, unremarkable furniture with an unusually durable stomach.

"Almeida." Master Ferren, the under-steward, didn't look up from his ledger. "Regent's table first. Full service today. She's expecting the Ostermark envoys at noon and she wants everything tasted twice."

"Twice," Paloma said.

"With the interval."

The interval meant she swallowed, waited a full quarter hour for the slow poisons, then swallowed again. It doubled the work and tripled the boredom, but the Queen Regent had not survived eleven years of ruling without her husband by trusting anyone, least of all her own cooks, and so the interval had become law.

"I'll need the small table by the east window," Paloma said. "And whatever comes off the fire first goes to me, not the warming racks. Heat masks things."

Ferren's quill scratched. He was a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the perpetual expression of someone doing arithmetic about his own disappointment. "You'll have it." Then, because he was not entirely a stone: "There's quince on the second shelf. Cook set it aside for you."

Paloma blinked. "For me?"

"For the household," Ferren corrected, already turning away. "Don't make it strange."

It was strange. Kindness in the kitchens of Vell arrived the way weather did — rarely, and usually meaning something. But she took the quince anyway, golden and heavy in her palm, and tucked it into her apron pocket where no one could see her being given things.

* * *

The Regent's breakfast was served in the small audience chamber rather than the great hall, which told Paloma the day would be long. Small rooms meant small audiences, and small audiences meant the Regent was in one of her moods — the kind where she counted grains of rice and remembered grudges with archival precision.

Paloma knelt at the side table while the footmen laid out silver. There were seven dishes: smoked river trout folded around hazelnut stuffing; a porridge of white corn with honey and brown butter; soft-boiled eggs in little woven cups; black bread with plum preserve; a compote of apples and rosemary; fresh cheese drizzled with oil; and a pot of the bitter northern tea the Regent drank all morning like other people drank water.

She began, as always, with the tea.

This was the part strangers never understood about tasting — that it wasn't one dramatic swallow in front of a nervous monarch, poison detected, villain dragged out. Mostly it was method. She held each spoonful on her tongue and read it the way a scribe reads a page. Bitterness that sat wrong. Sweetness that clung too long. A metallic ghost underneath honey. Her tongue had a vocabulary most scholars would envy; she simply applied it to breakfast.

The tea was clean. She drank a full cup and waited.

While she waited, she ate the eggs. This was the discipline nobody praised her for: the waiting. Poison was patient. Some of the best compounds Hedda the herbalist could name — and Hedda could name more than any herbalist ought to — took twenty minutes to announce themselves, and by then a careless taster was already being carried out the servants' stair. So Paloma built intervals into everything. Tea, then eggs, then trout, each separated by a hundred counted breaths, her hands busy the whole time with napkins and spoons so that no observer could tell she was, in fact, sitting very still inside her own body, listening to it.

The trout tried to kill her.

Not successfully — nothing ever managed that — but it tried. Halfway through the second forkful, her mouth went numb at the edges, and a fine heat crawled up behind her jaw, and she knew the taste immediately: wolfsbane steeped in the hazelnut oil, masked cleverly, masked almost well enough. Almost.

She set down the fork. Wiped her lips with the linen. And waited to see whether the numbness spread or retreated.

Around her, the kitchen traffic continued. A scullery boy argued with a fishmonger's man. The pastry mistress sang three notes of something filthy under her breath. Steam climbed toward the rafters. Paloma sat with death dissolving politely in her bloodstream and felt — nothing much. Not fear. Not triumph either, though there had been triumph once, years ago, when the first dish fought her and lost. Now it felt like paperwork. Her body processed the poison the way other bodies processed ale: a flush, a shiver, a slow return to ordinary. Nine years of small deaths had taught her cells their business.

The numbness retreated. She flagged Ferren with two fingers.

"The trout," she said quietly. "The oil on the stuffing."

Ferren went the color of old candle wax. "The oil came sealed from the Ostermark shipment—"

"I know where it came from. I'm telling you what's in it." She kept her voice level, pitched under the kitchen noise, giving him the mercy of privacy. "Wolfsbane. Light hand. Whoever did it knew oils, and knew our delivery schedule. Dump the lot. Every jar from that seal, not just the opened one."

"And the Regent—"

"Never touched it. I caught it at the third bite." She paused. "I'd like to speak with whoever received the shipment."

"That will be Rennick's desk."

"Then Rennick and I should talk before noon." She picked up her spoon again, because the morning was not finished, and neither was she. "Send the next course."

* * *

By seven, the Regent herself appeared.

Queen Regent Maren of Vell was fifty-one years old and looked carved rather than aged, all planes and edges, in slate silk the exact color of her mountains. She did not acknowledge Paloma — she never did, not directly, not in nine years — but she paused at the threshold of the audience chamber while her ladies arranged themselves, and her eyes swept the side table, and found the taster seated there with her untouched plate and her steady hands, and something in the carved face loosened by perhaps a millimeter. That millimeter was the entire architecture of Paloma's career. The Regent trusted her the way one trusts a wall: completely, impersonally, and without ever learning its name.

"Your Majesty." The chamberlain announced the Ostermark envoys at eight sharp, three men in dove-gray with the flat politeness of people selling something they'd rather not discuss. The poisoned oil had come from Ostermark. Paloma watched the envoys bow and smile and thought: *one of you knows.*

She tasted the rest of the service twice, with the interval, while the envoys discussed tariffs in voices designed to be overheard. Nothing else fought her. The porridge was innocent. The cheese was honest. The tea stayed bitter and true to the end of the pot, and when the audience concluded and the Regent rose, she paused — again, briefly — beside the side table.

"You are well?" the Regent asked. It was the closest thing to conversation they'd ever had. Three times a year, perhaps, she asked it. It was not concern. It was inventory.

"Well, Majesty."

"The trout?"

"Handled. Your kitchens will be cleaner by tonight."

Something flickered at the corner of the Regent's mouth — approval, filed away rather than shown. "See that it is." And then, as she turned, almost as an afterthought, which meant it was not an afterthought at all: "Tomorrow is your rest day, is it not, taster?"

"It is, Majesty."

"Mm." The Regent looked at her one moment longer than the sentence required. "Enjoy the valley."

Then she was gone, silk whispering, ladies trailing, and Paloma sat alone in the emptied chamber with seven clean dishes and a cold cup of tea and the peculiar hollow feeling of having been seen without being known.

*Enjoy the valley.* As if the valley were a place one visited. As if it weren't the whole world, walls and floor and ceiling, the bowl she'd been poured into at birth and would set in at the end.

She gathered the tasting linens for burning — protocol, even for clean plates — and let the routine carry her, because routine was the load-bearing wall of her life. Burn the linens. Log the trout incident in the taster's ledger with the date, the compound, and the suspected source. Deliver the ledger copy to Corvane's office.

Ah. Corvane.

* * *

The spymaster's office occupied a corner of the west wing that always seemed colder than the rest of the building, though the hearth was never unlit. Paloma had delivered ledgers there for six years and had exchanged perhaps four hundred words total with Lord Corvane in all that time, most of them variations on *sign here*. He frightened her less than he frightened everyone else, mostly because he had never once needed to frighten her. His attention was a currency he spent carefully, and he had never spent any on a food-taster.

Today, he was standing at his window when she entered, and he did not turn around immediately.

"Master Almeida." He used her surname. Nobody used her surname. Servants called her Almeida the way one calls a tool by its function; courtiers called her nothing at all. But Corvane said *master*, dryly precise, as if the word were a small test he administered daily.

"My lord." She set the ledger on his desk. "Trout service, seventh of Frostfall. Wolfsbane in hazelnut oil, Ostermark seal. Contained at source. Casualties: none, including myself."

"Including yourself," he repeated. He turned then. Corvane was a lean man somewhere north of fifty, with grey going systematically white at the temples and eyes the color of wet slate. People said he never blinked. People said many inaccurate things about Corvane, but this one happened to be nearly true. "Does it ever worry you? What you can swallow?"

She considered lying, decided it would insult them both, and answered honestly. "No, my lord. Worry is for things I can't survive."

Something moved behind the slate eyes — not warmth, but its distant cousin. Recognition, maybe. "A practical theology," he said. "Very well. The Regent thanks you. You'll have your rest day tomorrow, uninterrupted. Consider it earned."

"Thank you, my lord."

"One thing more." He came around the desk, unhurried. "The oil. You said whoever prepared it knew your delivery schedule. Kitchen schedules aren't secrets worth stealing. Delivery schedules are logistics, and logistics are my department." He stopped an arm's length away. Close enough that she could smell the cedar oil he used on his boots. "If you remember anything else — a face at the loading dock, a question asked too casually, anything that sits wrongly in that remarkable stomach of yours — you'll bring it to me. Directly. Not to Ferren, not to the guard captain. To me."

"Yes, my lord."

"Good." He returned to his window, dismissal complete, then added without turning: "Enjoy the valley, master taster."

The same words the Regent had used. The same strange emphasis. Paloma walked back down the cold corridor with her spine straight and her thoughts circling, trying to decide whether she had just been thanked or measured. With Corvane it was always both. The measuring was the thanking.

Outside, the fog was burning off. The valley showed itself in patches — green terraces, the brown ribbon of the river, the red roofs of the lower town stacked up the hillside like spilled fruit. Somewhere beyond the eastern ridge, three dove-gray envoys would eventually ride home to a kingdom that had just tried to poison the Regent's breakfast, or failed to, depending on which of them was lying. Somewhere beyond the western ridge, according to Hedda, there were lands that had names no one in Vell could pronounce anymore.

Paloma stood on the kitchen steps a moment longer than necessary, letting the sun land on her face.

Nine years. Nine years of swallowing death before breakfast, of counting breaths between courses, of being furniture with an immortal stomach. She was good at it. She was, if she were honest, the best in the kingdom, and the kingdom knew it the way it knew the weather, and that was the whole of what she'd built: competence so quiet it had become a kind of invisibility, loneliness so well-managed it passed for peace.

Tomorrow was hers. One whole day, granted by the Regent, confirmed by the spymaster, owed to nobody.

She went home along the river path with the quince still in her apron pocket, planning nothing more ambitious than bread, cheese, and an afternoon of reading by her window — and it was only when she reached her own gate, in the last of the afternoon light, that she saw the small shape standing at her cottage door.

Caked head to foot in white salt.

Unafraid.

Waiting.
