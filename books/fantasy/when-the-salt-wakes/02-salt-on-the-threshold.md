## 2. Salt on the Threshold

The day off was a fiction, and everyone knew it.

Paloma had learned long ago that a taster's rest was not freedom but rehearsal—the body kept on its schedule even when the kitchens were closed. So she rose at the same grey hour, drank the same barley water, and stood in her cottage doorway breathing the valley's particular air: cold stone, woodsmoke, and beneath it all, faintly, the mineral bite of salt rising from deep places. The valley of Vell smelled like the inside of a sealed jar. She had never thought much about it until that morning, when the smell seemed to lean toward her door like something listening.

She had planned nothing. That was the luxury of it—nothing. She would walk to Hedda's for comfrey for her knees, she would mend the second shutter that rattled in any wind above a whisper, and she would eat her own cooking, unsupervised, unwatched, and taste nothing in it but her own life. It was a small inventory of pleasures, and she recited it to herself as she unlatched the door.

The child was standing on her step.

Not sitting, not crouched, not huddled against the cold the way a lost child ought to be. Standing, square in the center of the worn threshold stone, facing the door as if it were an appointment they had both agreed upon. Six years old, maybe seven—small enough that the top of their head barely cleared her hip—and covered, from the dark hair down to bare feet, in white salt.

It crusted them the way frost crusts a window: thick at the edges, fine at the center, glittering where the early light caught it. Salt in their eyelashes. Salt packed into the whorls of their ears. A rime of it along the small jaw, so that when they breathed out, the breath seemed to come from somewhere colder than a child's chest. Their feet were blue with cold and utterly still, and there was not one mark of the road on them—not a scratch, not a blister, though the nearest village was half a morning's walk down-valley and the child could not have come from there without leaving a trail a blind man could follow.

Paloma did what she always did when the world presented her with something unexpected. She stopped. She observed. She catalogued.

*No fear in the posture,* she noted. *No shivering, though the ground is frosted. No tears. Eyes clear.* The eyes were the strangest part—they were watching her with the patient attention of someone who has been waiting a long time and is not surprised to finally be seen. Most people, meeting Paloma's gaze, looked away first. The courtiers did it politely, the other servants quickly, Corvane slowly, like a man measuring a doorframe. This child looked at her the way she imagined she looked at a suspicious dish: *I will wait for you to go first.*

"You're on my step," Paloma said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "It's frozen. You'll want to come in before your feet stick."

The child tilted their head—a small, precise motion, like a bird correcting its view—and spoke.

The words meant nothing to Paloma. That was her first, rational conclusion, delivered briskly by the part of her mind that had survived twenty years of courts: *foreign tongue, foreign child, someone's loss, not yours.* The syllables were soft-edged and flowing, vowels opening like water finding a channel, and there was a rhythm underneath them, a lilt, that made the sentence sound less like speech than like something being remembered aloud.

"Slowly," Paloma said, kneeling—not too close; she knew how to make herself smaller without making herself vulnerable. "Again. Slower."

The child repeated it. Same cadence, same patience. And here is the thing Paloma would spend the rest of her life trying to explain, and never satisfactorily: her mouth moved.

Not a translation. Nothing so orderly as that. Her lips shaped sounds before her mind had consented to produce them—an answer rising up through her throat like water finding an old channel, a phrase in the same flowing, soft-edged language, spoken in a voice that was hers and yet came from some cellar of herself she had never had occasion to open. Three syllables, maybe four. She felt her own tongue make a sound she could not have spelled if you'd asked her to, and then it was done, and the silence afterward rang.

The child's whole face changed.

It was not delight, exactly. It was recognition—the specific, devastating recognition of someone who has called into a vast darkness for days and hears, at last, an answer that knows their name. The salt on their cheeks cracked along two lines as the small face moved. Tears, Paloma realized. The child was crying, and the tears were cutting channels through the salt like rain through a field of chalk, and the child did not wipe them away or even seem to notice them, because the child was looking at Paloma as if Paloma were—

As if she were the thing they had crossed whatever distance they had crossed to find.

"No," Paloma said, in her own language, firmly, because the other voice in her throat wanted to say something else entirely and she did not trust it. "No. I don't know what that was. I don't speak—I've never—" She stopped, because the denial was assembling itself badly, and because the child had begun, slowly, deliberately, to lift one salt-white hand.

They pressed it flat against the doorframe. Against her doorframe, her wood, the wood Paloma had planed and painted herself eight years ago with money from her first year's wage. And where the small palm touched, the salt on the child's skin did not transfer, did not smudge—it *bloomed*. A thin lace of white crystals spread outward from the fingertips across the grain of the wood, delicate as frost ferns, precise as handwriting.

Then it faded, sinking into the wood or simply ceasing, and the doorframe was only a doorframe again, and the child lowered their hand and looked at her with those clear waiting eyes as if to say: *there. Now you understand.*

Paloma understood nothing. Paloma understood everything at once and none of it in order. She was cold. She noticed distantly that she was cold, that her breath was fogging, that she had been kneeling on her own frozen step for what might have been a minute or most of the morning, and that the sensible, survival-honed, twenty-years-in-a-poisoned-court part of her was screaming one word over and over:

*Inside. Get inside. Whatever this is, do not stand in the open discussing it.*

She rose, took the child gently by the shoulders—they weighed almost nothing, and under the salt-crusted shirt their skin was ice—and drew them over the threshold. The moment the child's feet crossed onto her floorboards, the salt on them began, very quietly, to melt. Not to dissolve—to melt, running off in fine bright streams that pooled around the small toes and then, impossibly, sank straight down through the boards as if the floor were thirsty. Within moments the child stood in a dry ring, mostly clean, blinking up at her.

Paloma shut the door. She threw the bolt, which she had never once bothered to throw in eight years, because no one in Vell locked anything against anyone.

The fire was low. She built it up with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect, set the child in her one good chair close to the flames, and put a blanket around shoulders that left faint salty ghosts on the wool. Then she put water on to heat, because there was always soup, because soup was the answer to every crisis her grandmother had ever named, and because doing something ordinary with her hands was the only thing keeping the extraordinary from swallowing her whole.

While the pot warmed, the child watched her. Not hungrily—or rather, hungrily in more ways than one, because when Paloma set down bread and broth the child ate with the focused gravity of someone who has gone hungry long enough to have opinions about waste—but also with that same unnerving patience, tracking her movements around the small kitchen as if memorizing them.

When the bowl was empty, the child spoke again. The same soft-flowing language, a different phrase this time, shorter, and pointed one small finger at their own chest.

"Issel," said the child.

Paloma frowned. "Is that your name?"

The finger tapped the chest again. "*Issel.*" Then the finger swung, deliberate as a compass needle, and pointed at Paloma.

And there it was again—that treacherous fluency. Her mouth opened and a word fell out of her, round and rolling and utterly unknown to her waking mind, a word she had certainly never said in her life and yet pronounced perfectly, with an old woman's accent she suddenly recognized with a sick drop of the stomach, because she had heard that accent every night of her childhood. Under blankets. In the dark. Hummed more than spoken.

Her grandmother's accent. Her grandmother's voice, coming out of Paloma's mouth, answering a stranger's question in a language that had no business existing anywhere inside the valley of Vell, where magic could not follow and nothing forgotten ever entered.

The child smiled. It transformed the small salt-rimed face entirely, and it was such an unguarded, exhausted, *arrived* smile that Paloma felt something crack in her chest along the same lines the tears had cut through the salt.

"That wasn't me," Paloma said quietly, to the child, to the room, to whoever might be listening. "That wasn't mine."

The child patted the arm of the chair, twice, the way you pat a horse you intend to keep.

Outside, the wind moved through the valley, and Paloma noticed—for the first time in her life, she noticed—that the sound it made against her door was not quite the sound wind makes. It was higher, thinner, threaded through with something almost like singing, almost like a choir practicing very far away, and it stopped the moment she opened the door to look, leaving behind only the ordinary cold and, on her doorstep, a fine dusting of white that had not been snow.

Salt, blooming in the shape of small footprints, pointing toward her door.

She closed it again, and bolted it, and stood with her back against the wood while the child watched her with those patient eyes, and Paloma Almeida—who had survived arsenic and nightshade and the Queen Regent's suspicion for twenty years by noticing everything—finally admitted the obvious:

Whatever had walked out of the world to find her, it had known exactly which door to knock on.
