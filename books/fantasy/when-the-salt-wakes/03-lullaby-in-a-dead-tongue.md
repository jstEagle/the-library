## 3. Lullaby in a Dead Tongue

The child slept like something that had been running for a very long time.

Paloma had seen sleep like this only once before—on a courier who collapsed in the palace kitchens after riding three days through the Ash Pass, and even he had twitched and murmured in his dreams. This child did not twitch. The child lay on her narrow bed with both hands folded on the chest, palms up, as if waiting to receive something, and breathed so slowly and evenly that twice Paloma leaned close to be sure.

Salt still clung in the fine lines of the child's skin. Paloma had wiped the face and hands with a warm cloth, and the cloth came away white, and beneath it the skin was unmarked—not chapped, not raw, not burned the way salt caked on a body should burn. She'd checked for bruises, for rope marks, for anything that would tell her where this child had come from. There was nothing. Just salt, and the smell underneath it: not the sea, exactly. Something older than the sea. Stone that remembered being water.

She had put out bread and honeyed milk on the table. The child ate standing up, watching the door the whole time, then drank the milk in three swallows and said something—two syllables, rising—and pointed at the bed.

Now the child slept, and Paloma sat at her own table with her back against the wall where she could see both door and window, because that was how she did everything. Twenty years of tasting had taught her that safety was a direction you faced, not a place you stood.

The cottage was small enough that she could hear every one of the child's breaths. Outside, Vell was settling into its evening noises—the baker raking his ovens, someone's dog complaining about the cold, the bell at the chapel tolling the hour with its usual cracked note. Ordinary sounds. Valley sounds. Sounds that had never once, in all her years here, frightened her.

But the child had spoken to her at the door, and the words were still sitting in Paloma's chest like a stone she couldn't cough up.

*Vessel issa,* the child had said. Or something near it. And Paloma's mouth—her careful, court-trained mouth, the mouth that kept itself shut around kings and poisoners alike—had shaped an answer before her mind had consented to anything at all.

*Issa velleth.*

She hadn't known what she was saying. She still didn't know. The words had come up from somewhere below thought, the way a swallowed thing rises, and she had heard her own voice say them as if from across the room.

She poured herself a cup of barley tea she didn't want and held it just to have something warm between her hands.

*Think*, she told herself. *You are paid to think faster than poison.*

Coincidence was possible. Children babbled; tongues ran together; a lost child might have picked up fragments of some trader's language from the mountain passes, some dialect from the eastern caravans. The valley saw travelers sometimes, despite everything. It was possible.

Except that the child's words had not sounded foreign. That was the horror of it, the part Paloma kept circling and refusing to land on. When the caravans came through speaking their clipped desert tongues, Paloma heard music without meaning—pretty noise, sealed shut. But when the child spoke, the meaning had been *right there*, behind a thin wall, like voices in the next room. Like hearing your own name called in a crowd and turning before you decide to turn.

*Issa velleth.* Her mouth knew those shapes. Her mouth had always known them.

She set down the cup before her hand could shake it.

* * *

Her grandmother had died eleven years ago, in this same cottage, in the bed where the child now slept. Paloma had been twenty-two, newly sworn to the crown, newly certain that her life was a corridor with two doors: the kitchens at one end and this house at the other. She had sat by the deathbed holding her grandmother's hand and listening to the old woman talk—not to her, mostly, but past her—to someone who wasn't there, in a low, sliding language that Paloma had assumed was delirium.

"Grandmother," she'd said, "who are you talking to?"

And the old woman had turned her head, eyes cloudy and bright at once, and looked at Paloma for a long moment. Then she'd smiled—the particular smile of someone deciding, again, to keep a secret—and answered in plain Vellish:

"To an old friend, little dove. Go make me the honey cake. You know the one."

Paloma had gone and made the honey cake. Her grandmother had eaten two bites and died before morning, and Paloma had grieved cleanly, the way she did everything, and had not once let herself wonder about the language or the smile or the friend who wasn't there.

That was the discipline of her whole life, if she was honest: not wondering. Wondering was a crack, and cracks admitted things. A taster who wondered too much started imagining bitterness in safe food, or sweetness in poisoned food, and then she was dead. So she had learned to taste and swallow and report, and to leave the questions outside the kitchen door like muddy boots.

But tonight the questions were inside the house, sleeping in her grandmother's bed, breathing in a rhythm Paloma almost recognized.

She got up quietly, took the lamp off its hook, and knelt at the chest at the foot of the shelf-wall. The chest held things that had no use and no price: her mother's single letter, a bundle of dried lavender gone gray, a wooden bird with one wing, and at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, the recipe book.

It wasn't much to look at. A fat ledger, boards worn round at the corners, the spine re-stitched twice with different colored thread. *Recipes of the House of Thackeray* was written on the first page in her grandmother's upright hand—but *Thackeray* was inked over something else, scratched out so thoroughly that the original name had become a dark bruise in the paper. Paloma had noticed that bruise as a girl and been told: *We took a new name when we came to the valley, little dove. Old names can be heavy.* She had accepted that the way she accepted most things. Not wondering.

She unwrapped the book now and laid it on the table under the lamp, and turned past the familiar pages—the honey cake, the barley soup, the pickled river-fish that Bram the ferryman swore by—until she reached the pages she had never quite read properly. The ones written slantwise, in faded brown ink, crammed into margins and margins-of-margins, in a script of loops and tall strokes that she had always taken for shorthand. Kitchen shorthand. Grandmothers' scribble.

The child made a small sound in her sleep.

Paloma went still, lamp raised, heart knocking. But the child only turned her face toward the wall and settled again, and her lips moved faintly, shaping dream-words.

Paloma bent close. She didn't touch. She listened.

Three syllables. Four. Sliding, rising, falling. The same tongue the child had spoken at her door. The same tongue her dying grandmother had used.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, and Paloma felt the floor tilt under her certainty.

*It isn't delirium,* she thought. *It was never delirium. She was talking in her sleep, the way this child is talking. She carried the language inside her the way I carry poison—something swallowed that never leaves.*

She walked back to the table on legs that felt borrowed, and opened the book to the margin-scribbles, and made herself look.

* * *

Here is what Paloma Almeida knew about scripts: nothing, formally. But a taster learns to read labels, apothecary marks, the coded inventories of suspicious households. She knew that writing has bones—that a script built of sharp angles comes from people who carve, and a script of loops comes from people who write fast and soft, on paper, indoors, in comfort.

This script was loops. Tall strokes with curls at the top like ferns unfurling, soft joins between letters, whole words that seemed to flow downhill like water finding its level. Whoever had written these marginalia had written them often, easily, with love. These were not notes. They were habits.

And they matched—not approximately, not loosely, but *exactly*—the sounds the child had made at her door.

Paloma pressed her fingertips flat against the page as though she could hold the book still while the world moved. Then she began, methodically, fearfully, to match sound to mark. The child had said *vessel*. In the margins, that curling fern-shape appeared over and over beside the recipes for salt-cured things. The child had said *velleth*—and there it was, tucked beside the instructions for the honey cake, repeated like a refrain down half a page.

She worked until the candle guttered, matching, guessing, failing, starting again. She was not translating. She wanted to be clear with herself about that. She was doing what she did every day at court: tasting small amounts, watching what happened, adjusting. Treating the language like a dish of unknown provenance, testing it one syllable at a time.

Somewhere past midnight, she found the page that stopped her.

It was the honey cake again—but this version was longer than the one her grandmother had taught her, and written in the margin beside it, in the looping script with a translation squeezed beneath in cramped Vellish letters, were words her grandmother had never spoken aloud:

*Bake it when someone must cross a long way home. Salt from the threshold. Honey from the last jar. Feed them before they go and they will find the road. Feed them after and they will remember us.*

Below that, in larger letters, underlined twice:

*A meal opens what walls cannot.*

Paloma read it five times. The lamp hissed. On the bed, the child slept on with her palms open to the ceiling.

*Feed them before they go and they will find the road.*

What road? What crossing? Her grandmother had been a cook—a good one, famous in the valley for her festival tables—and cooks wrote practical things. Maybe it meant nothing more than travelers' customs. Maybe every culture fed people at partings. Maybe she was a fool reading prophecy into a cookbook because a strange child had knocked on her door and her mouth had betrayed her.

Maybe.

But her eye kept snagging on the phrase above the honey cake, the heading of the whole section, written in the looping script with no translation at all—as if her grandmother had assumed anyone reading would know it already. Paloma copied the shapes onto a scrap of paper, letter by careful letter, and then, feeling utterly ridiculous, alone in her kitchen at midnight like a girl playing at charms, she sounded them out.

Her mouth knew them. That was the terrible thing. Her tongue found each shape without instruction, the way it found the shape of her own name.

*"Issa velleth,"* she whispered. *"Issa velleth."*

On the bed, the child sat bolt upright.

Paloma froze, the scrap of paper in her fist. The child stared at her across the lamplit room—not wild, not afraid, but awake in the absolute way of someone who has heard exactly what she was waiting to hear. Salt dust drifted off the blanket as she swung her legs down. She crossed the small room barefoot, took Paloma's face in both small hands—hands that smelled of stone and old water—and looked into her eyes with a gravity no six-year-old should own.

Then she said, slowly, deliberately, the way one speaks to someone learning:

*"Issa velleth. Ostra-veth."*

And she touched Paloma's chest, over the sternum, once.

Paloma's whole body reacted before her mind did—a flush of heat, a sting at the backs of her eyes, grief arriving without an address. She had tasted a hundred poisons and her body had shrugged them all off, but this—this small hand, these two words—went straight through every defense she'd spent a lifetime building.

"I don't understand," she said hoarsely. It was true and it was a lie and it was the truest lie she'd ever told. "I don't understand any of this."

The child tilted her head, considering her, the way Paloma considered an unfamiliar dish. Then she patted the sternum again, gentler, and said one more word—soft, final, unmistakably a name, unmistakably *hers*—and climbed back into the dead woman's bed, pulled the blanket up, and closed her eyes.

Paloma sat at the table until the lamp burned dry and the windows went gray.

When the light was strong enough, she gathered the recipe book, the scrap of copied script, and her coat, and woke the child gently with a hand on her shoulder.

"We're going to see Hedda," she said, not knowing why she chose the herbalist, only knowing that Hedda was old, older than her grandmother, old enough to have come from somewhere herself—and that Paloma could no longer afford not to wonder.

The child nodded as if she'd understood every word. Perhaps, Paloma thought grimly, lacing her boots with hands that wouldn't quite steady, perhaps she had.

Outside, frost silvered the lane, and for the first time since childhood, Paloma noticed that the frost along her doorstep lay in patterns—fine, branching, deliberate-looking—that looked less like weather and more like writing.

She stepped over it carefully, as one steps over a threshold, and did not look back.
