## 4. What the Valley Cannot Hold

Hedda's shop smelled the way it always did: bitter greens, drying feverfew, beeswax, and underneath it all something older and stranger that I had never been able to name—like rain on stone in a country I'd never visited.

I had known Hedda since before I could walk. She had delivered me, my mother used to say, with one hand and a cup of willowbark tea in the other, and she'd been the closest thing to a grandmother I had after mine died. She was the only person in Vell who never looked at me like a tool. When I was nine she taught me to tell hemlock from wild carrot by smell alone, and when I told her I could taste poison in anything, even masked, she hadn't flinched or cooed or crossed herself. She'd just nodded, as if I'd said I could whistle.

So it was to Hedda I went on the second morning of Issel's presence at my cottage, carrying questions I couldn't ask anyone else.

I left the child sleeping. That was its own kind of madness—I had known Issel for less than two days, and I was already leaving my door unbarred behind me because the child asked, in that wordless way children have, to be allowed to keep dreaming. But there was nowhere else to bring them. Not yet.

The bell over Hedda's door was made from a bent spoon. It clattered instead of rang.

"Paloma Thackeray." Hedda didn't look up from her mortar. She was grinding something blue-green and resinous, and her hands—liver-spotted, steady as a surgeon's—kept their rhythm while she took my measure out of the corner of one eye. "You're off rotation today. You're standing in my shop on your day off, which you never do, and you've got the look of a woman carrying water in both hands and trying not to spill."

"How can you tell?"

"Because I've got the same look most mornings." She nodded at the stool across the counter. "Sit. Tell me what you're carrying."

I sat. And then, because it was Hedda, I didn't perform. I didn't arrange the story into something tidy. I said: "There's a child at my house. Six, maybe seven. Showed up at my door caked in salt, head to foot, like they'd crawled out of the sea we don't have. No frostbite, no hunger sickness, nothing. And they talk, Hedda. They talk constantly." My throat tightened around the next part. "In a language I shouldn't know. Except I do. Every word of it. It's my grandmother's lullabies. The ones she sang when she thought I was asleep."

The mortar stopped grinding.

Hedda set down the pestle with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off. She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly. "Say some of it."

"It would sound like nonsense to you."

"Everything sounds like nonsense until it doesn't. Say it."

I closed my eyes and let my mouth remember. *Sella-vai, sella-vai, tor en dommen sel.* Sleep now, sleep now, the door is kind. I opened my eyes. "It means goodnight. Or it meant goodnight, once, to someone."

Hedda had gone very still. Not pale—not yet—but still in the particular way of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

"Where is this child now?" she asked.

"Asleep in my bed. Under three blankets. They ate an entire pot of barley soup last night and fell asleep mid-spoonful."

"And the salt? Did you taste it?"

That was the question only I could answer, and she knew it. "I tasted it," I said. "Off their collar, off the crust on their boots. Hedda—it isn't sea salt. Sea salt has a bitterness under it, iodine, rot, whatever lives in the water. This was clean. Clean like—" I searched for it. "Clean like a room no one has ever lied in. It tasted the way my grandmother's hands tasted when she'd been kneading bread. Salt and flour and nothing else."

Hedda sat down across from me. Slowly, the way old people sit when they've decided the sitting is going to be a long one.

"Tell me about the valley," I said. "Not the founding festival nonsense they teach at the schoolhouse. The real version. You know it. You've always known more than you say."

"Flatterer." But there was no warmth in it. Her eyes had gone to the window, to the mountains ringing us like cupped hands. "You're right, though. The schoolhouse version is a bedtime story. *Brave settlers found a green valley between the peaks and thanked their gods.* What actually happened is that people were running. From what, depends on which grandmother you ask—war, plague, worse things. But they all came to the same place, and the place had one quality worth having."

"The border," I said.

She looked at me sharply. "Who told you about the border?"

"No one. I'm a taster. I think about thresholds for a living." I turned my cup on its saucer. "Nothing gets into Vell that wasn't born here or walked through the passes on its own feet. No blight has ever touched our orchards. No fever epidemic, ever, in recorded history—not once. Every other valley in these mountains loses a third of its children every bad winter. We lose a handful, and always to ordinary things. Falls. Chills. Bad luck. It's like the whole valley sits inside a jar with a lid on it."

"That's one way to say it." Hedda's voice had dropped. "Here's mine. Magic cannot cross the borders of Vell. Full stop. Whatever runs the world out there—the currents, the workings, whatever word you want for it—stops dead at the treeline. It can't come in. It won't. And things that are *made* of magic, wholly made, can barely perceive us. We're a blind spot in the world's eye. That's why the refugees came. Not for the grass. For the silence. If you were being hunted by something that worked through power, you came here, and the moment you crossed the pass, you stopped existing to it."

My skin prickled. "And the cost?"

"You already know the cost. You live inside it every day." She spread her hands, taking in the shop, the town beyond it, the whole green bowl of Vell. "Nothing comes in. Which also means nothing *of* that world comes in. No wonders. No signs. No healing springs, no prophets, none of the strangeness that keeps elsewhere interesting. The founders thought that was a fair trade. Most days, so do I." She fixed me with a look. "But hear the shape of it, girl. The valley is a locked room. And someone has put a key inside my friend's house."

The words landed in my chest like cold water. "Issel."

"I don't know yet. Tell me the child's name."

"They don't say it. Not properly. There's a sound they make—" I tried to reproduce it, the soft liquid syllable the child pressed to my palm that first morning, the one I'd felt more than heard. "*Issel.* Something like that. Like water finding its level."

Hedda went white.

Not pale. White. The color drained out of her face the way color drains out of a sky before hail, all at once and bottom-up, and her hand shot out and gripped the edge of the counter hard enough that I heard the wood complain.

"Hedda—"

"*Say it again.*"

"Issel. *Iss-el.* Did I—was the stress wrong? I don't speak it, I only—"

"Stop." Her breathing had gone shallow. She pressed the back of her wrist against her lips, an old habit, I realized—an apothecary's gesture, tasting the air. "Fifty years I've lived in this valley. Fifty years, and I have heard perhaps six words of that tongue, and every one of them was from a dying woman who made me swear to forget them. That name—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "That name isn't a name the way Tomas or Marta is a name. In that language, names describe. *Issel* —it means something like *the water that remembers the way back.*"

The shop seemed very quiet suddenly. The fire ticking. The spoon-bell stirring faintly in a draft that shouldn't have reached it.

"A child named *the way back,*" I said, "walks out of the wilderness covered in impossible salt and lands on the doorstep of the granddaughter of a woman who sang in that language. In a valley where magic cannot follow." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and high. "Hedda, that's not coincidence. That's architecture."

"Yes," she said simply. "It is."

We sat with that. Outside, Vell went about its morning—a cart rattling past, someone calling a dog, the baker's boy whistling—and every ordinary sound felt like a performance staged for our benefit while something enormous moved underneath the floor.

"My grandmother," I said finally. "Maren. Before she married, before Thackeray. Who was she, Hedda? Where did she come from? Everyone says she was a refugee, everyone says *from the north,* but nobody ever says *from where,* and I was a child, I didn't press, and now she's forty years dead and I'm asking a herbalist instead of her."

Hedda was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, she chose each word like a coin counted out of a nearly empty purse.

"There was a kingdom," she said. "Once. North past the Grey Teeth, further than the fur traders go. Rich and strange and old, older than any crown that exists now. And it is gone—not fallen, not conquered, but *gone,* the way a candle goes out. No ruins. No survivors' accounts that anyone believes. The maps don't show it. The histories mention it in half a sentence and move on, embarrassed, like a man skipping a stair he knows is broken. Even the memory of it slides off people. Ask a scholar about it and they'll frown and change the subject without knowing why." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were wet. "Except the refugees. A few hundred of them scattered across the mountain countries, and they remembered. And their children half-remembered. And their grandchildren—" she touched my hand, "—hummed lullabies in a dead tongue without knowing why the syllables fit their mouth so well."

"What was it called?" I whispered.

"You know what it was called."

I did. The knowledge rose in me the way ground water rises in a well, from somewhere beneath knowing. I'd seen it, I realized—in the margins of Grandmother Maren's recipe book, in the looping script I'd assumed was shorthand. I'd seen it written and never once read it aloud, because some part of me had understood, even at seven years old, that saying it might break something.

"Ostrava," I said.

The candle flames in Hedda's shop all leaned sideways at once, though there was no draft. Then righted themselves. Neither of us mentioned it.

Hedda stood, joints cracking, and began moving through her shelves with sudden purpose, pulling jars, setting them aside—willow, valerian, something dark and honey-thick I didn't recognize. "If the child is what I think it is, you'll need these. And food that keeps. And—" she paused, jar in hand, "—and you'll need to understand what you're carrying before anyone else notices you're carrying it."

"Corvane notices everything," I said. "He noticed I was late twice last week. Two absences, and the spymaster already filed them away like specimens."

"Then be duller than you've ever been. Be boring, Paloma. Be invisible." She thrust a wrapped bundle into my arms. "And listen to me, because this is the part that matters. The valley holds magic out. That's its nature; it's why we're safe. But a lock doesn't care what side of the door it protects. If something from outside was carried *in*—born in, walked in, hidden in a child's bones—then the valley can't touch it either. Can't see it. Can't stop what it does." She gripped my wrists. "The child isn't in danger from Vell. But everything that hunted their people is patient, and the valley's lid has never once been lifted, and I am an old woman who has spent fifty years pretending not to know things, and I am telling you: whatever comes for that child will not come through the passes. It will find another way. They always do."

The bundle in my arms weighed almost nothing and felt like it weighed a life.

"Take me to them," Hedda said quietly. "After all these years, I'd like to hear the language again. Properly. From a living mouth."

* * *

We walked back through streets gone silver with the first frost of the season, and Hedda moved slower than usual, and I matched her pace and hated every step of it, because every step was a minute Issel slept alone in my cottage with a secret folded up small inside them.

Halfway down Mill Lane, Hedda stopped.

She was staring at the drainage ditch that ran along the road—just a ditch, brown water and fallen leaves, the same ditch I passed four times a week. But along its near bank, catching the low sun, ran a line of white. Bright, granular, unmistakable.

Salt. A seam of it, glittering in the frost-melt, as if the ground itself had sweated it out overnight.

"Hedda," I said. "There's no salt spring on Mill Lane."

"No," she said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used for terminal diagnoses. "There isn't."

I knelt and touched it. Tasted it, because tasting was the one thing I was for.

It was clean. Clean like a room no one had ever lied in.

Behind us, far off toward the northern peaks, so faint it might have been wind—or might have been the wind learning a new note—something began, very softly, to sing.
