## 5. The Court Summons

The summons came before the sun did.

A page in the Queen Regent's livery stood at my door in the blue-grey hour, breath steaming, knuckles raw from knocking. I knew the knock. Servants learn to read knocks the way sailors read weather: three soft raps meant a neighbor, a fist meant trouble, and this—the flat, patient percussion of someone who would wait until the house fell down—meant the palace.

I opened the door with my shawl already over my shoulders.

"Corvane's man said dawn," the page recited. "He said to say it twice. Dawn, mistress Taster."

"Tell him I know how dawn works."

The page blinked. He was perhaps twelve and had not yet learned that the palace's fear of me was supposed to make me afraid of something too. "He also said—" the boy checked his palm as though the words were written there, which knowing Corvane they might have been—"'the Regent's table waits for no one, but it has been waiting for you.'"

Which was Corvane's way of saying he'd noticed.

I fed the boy a heel of bread because he looked like no one had, and sent him off, and then I stood in my doorway watching the valley wake up grey and gold and utterly indifferent to me, and I did the arithmetic I had been doing for two days now.

Two absences. One market morning, one sick day that hadn't been a sick day at all. Two days in eleven years where Paloma Almeida, taster to the crown of Vell, had failed to appear at her station beside the Regent's breakfast. Eleven years of perfect, invisible, punctual poison-proof attendance, undone by a salt-caked child asleep in my bed inside.

I had left her sleeping with the fire banked and the latch low, which is to say I had left her exactly as safe as a child can be in a cottage at the edge of a town that had never once given me cause to lock a door. That was another thing about Vell. We didn't lock things. Magic couldn't cross the border, and so we had all quietly agreed that nothing else would either.

It occurred to me, walking the long road up to the palace with my stomach doing its cold slow roll, that I had never once lied to anyone in this valley. There had never been anything worth lying about. My whole life had been built on a foundation so boring that truth cost nothing.

Now there was a child in my house who spoke a dead language, and I could feel the first lie being born in me like a tooth coming through.

* * *

The tasting hall smelled of rosemary and dread, as it always did.

It was a long narrow room attached to the Regent's private kitchens, all stone and steam, with a single high window that let in light the color of weak tea. The serving line stood ready under linen cloths: poached river trout, honeyed oats, a custard the color of late butter, bread still hot enough to fog the air above it. Three under-cooks pretended not to watch me come in. Everyone always watched the taster come in. You spend your life proving you can't be poisoned and people develop a morbid fascination with whether you might, someday, prove otherwise.

And at the far end, seated on a stool like a man attending his own trial, was Corvane.

He wasn't dressed like a spymaster. Spymasters in stories wear black and lean in shadows; Corvane wore a good brown coat going soft at the elbows and had the face of a man who spent too many winters squinting across fields—a broad, weathered, unremarkable face, the kind you forget while you're looking at it, which is of course the entire point. He had been the Regent's shadow since before I took the post. He had signed the papers when I was apprenticed at fourteen. In eleven years he had spoken to me maybe forty times, and every conversation had been about eggs.

"Taster Almeida." He didn't rise. Rising is a courtesy between equals and we were performing no such theater. "You're well?"

"I'm well."

"You were missed on Thirdday." Not an accusation. A fact, set down gently, like a stone on a table. "And again on Fifthday. Illness, your note said."

"A fever. It passed."

"Fever," he repeated, tasting the word the way I tasted trout. "The valley hasn't seen a fever in six years. The last one was in the ferry quarter. Bram's boy." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, genuinely kind, which made it worse. "You see how it is with me. I remember everything useless. It's the useful things that slip away."

"I wouldn't know, master Corvane. My work is mostly eggs."

That got a real laugh out of him, short and surprised, and I hated myself a little for earning it, because I understood suddenly what this was. This was not an interrogation. Interrogations are for people you suspect. This was a man laying a cloth over a table very carefully, arranging everything just so, before he decided what to serve.

"The Regent is anxious this season," he said. "Solstice coming. She wants everything tasted twice."

"She always wants everything tasted twice."

"She does. But this season she means it." He gestured to the line. "Begin with the fish."

I began with the fish.

This part I could do with my eyes closed, and sometimes nearly do. People imagine tasting is dramatic—that we sip and swoon and gasp. The truth is it's bookkeeping. You take a spoonful small enough to swallow without chewing. You hold it on the tongue and count. Bitter notes bloom at the back first if something's wrong; true poison is usually faster than taste, but the court doesn't pay me for usually, it pays me for the exceptions, the slow alchemical cruelties that need a minute or an hour or a day to declare themselves. So I count. Twenty heartbeats per dish, then thirty, then I move on, and somewhere behind me a kitchen exhales and the Regent eats breakfast believing the world is held together by my tongue.

Eleven years. Four thousand mornings. My body carries more poison than most apothecaries' shelves, absorbed in doses small enough to survive and large enough, over time, to have changed me. I don't talk about what it's done. The whites of my eyes went faintly grey in my twenties. Wine tastes of nothing. Once, cut on a knife, my blood ran darker than blood should run, and I decided then and there never to look again.

The trout was clean. The oats were clean. The custard—

The custard stopped me.

Not because it was poisoned. Because it was *perfect*, and perfection in the Regent's kitchens is rare enough to be a signature. The vanilla came from the southern trade, the eggs were double-yolked, the sweetness sat precisely where sweetness should sit—and underneath it, so faint that anyone else would have called it imagination, was a thread of bitterness like the ghost of an almond.

Bitter almond. Every servant girl knows bitter almond. It's in the songs children skip rope to.

I held the spoonful on my tongue and counted, and I did not let my face change, because the first rule of my trade is that the taster's face is state property.

Ten heartbeats. Twenty. The bitterness didn't grow. It sat there, patient, precise, dosed—not to kill me. I knew the weight of a killing dose the way you know the weight of your own hand. This was a whisper of it. A seasoning. Enough that a careful tongue would find it, and a careless one would not.

Enough for a person smaller than me. A person whose body hadn't spent eleven years becoming a fortress.

Someone had meant this custard for someone else. Someone young. Someone at this table or near it—or someone who might someday sit where I sat, if the succession of tasters worked the way I suspected it secretly did.

Or—and here the thought arrived cold and complete, like a stone dropped down a well—someone had meant it for whoever was expected to taste it. And Corvane had known, and had watched me find it, and was watching now.

I swallowed.

"Master Corvane," I said. "Your custard has almond in it."

Silence along the serving line. An under-cook dropped a ladle and caught it against her apron with a clank that sounded, in that hush, like a bell.

Corvane rose from his stool for the first time. He crossed the room unhurried, took the spoon from my hand as if it were evidence—which it was—and touched its bowl to his tongue. His eyebrows drew together. Whatever else Corvane was, he had a palate; you don't run a kingdom's secrets without learning to taste them.

"Well," he said softly. "So it does."

"It's faint. Dosed low."

"Dosed low," he agreed. He looked at me then, fully, for the first time in eleven years, and I felt it like a lamp being turned toward a wall you'd believed was blank. "How faint, Taster? Faint enough to miss? Or faint enough to survive?"

The question had too many doors in it. Behind each one, something waiting.

"Faint enough that I'm still standing here," I said. "Ask me the rest over wine, if you're buying."

He laughed again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, and he waved the kitchen back to work with two fingers, and the room exhaled and resumed its noise, and the moment closed over like water.

But as I finished the line—bread, honey, the Regent's dark morning tea—I felt his attention stay on me the way heat stays in a stone after the fire's out. He stood by the window with the spoon still in his hand, turning it slowly, a man holding a key he hadn't yet found the lock for.

* * *

He walked me out. Of course he did. He fell into step beside me in the corridor like we were old friends taking air, and the corridor was long and empty and echoed our footsteps back at us doubled, four feet walking instead of two.

"You've been with the Regent's service eleven years," he said.

"Eleven in spring."

"And in all that time, I could count our conversations on one hand." He glanced sideways. "I've often wondered if that was your design or mine."

"Probably yours. You hire quiet people. Then you punish them for it."

"That's fair." He said it easily, without offense, and we walked a while. Light came through the arrow-slits in pale bars and lay across the floor like fallen lumber. "Can I tell you something unprofessional, Taster?"

"You can tell me anything. I'll taste it first."

Another laugh, briefer. Then he stopped walking, and I stopped, because the corridor had narrowed around him somehow, the way rooms do when a certain kind of man fills them.

"My mother came from outside the valley," he said.

I kept my face still. It is the one skill I have that outranks my tongue.

"Before the border was sealed. She never spoke of where. When I asked, she said the name of her home country had been taken away, and it was rude to ask a woman for something she'd lost." He looked down the empty corridor as if reading something written far along its length. "She died when I was nineteen. Among her things I found a wooden spoon carved with birds, and a song I've never found in any hymnal, and a handful of salt wrapped in wax paper, labeled in writing I've never been able to read."

The floor seemed very slightly tilted. Or I did.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. And heard, even as I said it, that I had asked the question the way an innocent woman asks it—too evenly, too carefully, the way you carry a full cup across a crowded room.

Corvane noticed. I watched him notice. It moved across his face like weather, and he chose—consciously, deliberately, I would swear it—to let it pass.

"Because you missed two days," he said, "and the Regent notices everything, and I would rather notice it first. Because there's a rumor out of the eastern farms of a stranger seen on the roads, and the description changes every telling except for one detail—white dust, they say, dried white, like the person had been pulled out of the sea." He turned to face me fully, and his voice stayed warm and his eyes did not. "And because if such a stranger ever came to your door, Paloma Almeida—and why would it, a taster, a nobody, the most invisible woman in Vell—but *if* it ever did, I would want you to come to me. Before anyone else. Before the Regent. Before the fear gets large. Because I remember what it is, a little, to have a door in your history that won't stay shut."

The corridor hummed with silence. Somewhere below, the kitchens roared on, oblivious, feeding a court that believed itself safe.

"There's no stranger at my door," I said.

And it was a lie, the first of my life, and it came out smooth and easy and cost me nothing in the mouth and everything everywhere else, and Corvane nodded slowly as if I'd handed him something delicate and he was deciding whether it was a gift or a debt.

"No," he said. "Of course not. There never is."

He walked me to the servants' stair and stopped at the top of it, and pressed into my hand the spoon—the tasting spoon, the evidence—with my fingers closing over the bowl where the poison had been.

"For your collection," he said. "Tasters keep things, I'm told. Little trophies. Proof of survival." His grip lingered a half-second past courtesy. "I'll want it back someday. Everything gets returned eventually, in my experience. Everything finds its way home."

I walked down the stairs with the spoon in my fist and the taste of bitter almond still sitting at the root of my tongue like a warning, and I thought about a child asleep in my cottage with salt dried white in her hair, and about a door that closes forever at the solstice, and about a spymaster who carried a dead country's salt in a drawer somewhere and had just told me, in the only language he had, that he was watching.

The morning was bright when I came out into it. Too bright. The kind of brightness that makes shadows sharp enough to draw blood.

I had three weeks until solstice.

And now, I understood, I had perhaps three days before Corvane came asking his next question—one I might not be able to lie my way past, because he had shown me the salt in his own pocket, and waited, patient as poison, to see what I would show him in mine.
