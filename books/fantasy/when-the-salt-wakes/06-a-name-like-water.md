## 6. A Name Like Water

The cottage smells of smoke and bread and something green growing in a pot on the sill.

Issel knows the word for it. *Herb.* One of the words that came across with them, packed tight like seeds in a pocket. The woman says the word when she waters it, moving her mouth slow, and Issel copies the shape of it inside. *Herb. Herb.* A good word. A soft word. Words here are mostly soft, like the moss that grows on the north side of stones.

The floor is wood. Not stone. Wood that creaks in three places — by the bed, by the door, by the chair where the woman sits at night with her book. Issel has counted them. Counting is safe. Grandmother said: *when you are afraid, count what is true.* Three creaks. One window. Two chairs but only one person. One fire that goes down to red coals and comes back up to yellow every morning because the woman feeds it.

The woman's name is Paloma. It took four days to learn it, because names here are spoken differently than names at home. Here they sit flat on the tongue. At home, names move. Names are like water. You say *Paloma* and it lies still. You say *Issel* —

You say *Issel* and it runs downhill, quick over stones, and lands in your chest.

That is why Issel does not say it out loud anymore.

* * *

There was a song on the road. Issel does not like to think about the road, but the song comes anyway, the way cold comes under a door.

The road went through places with no names left. That is what the Grey Choir does first. It does not take the houses or the trees or the people. It takes the *names*. It stands at the edge of a town and sings the town's name wrong — one note bent, one syllable swallowed — and the name frays like rope, like wet paper, like a spiderweb touched by a finger. And when the name is gone, everything holding the name goes too. Slowly. Politely, almost. The houses forget how to stand up. The people forget their own mothers' faces. The river forgets which way is down.

Issel remembers the river forgetting. That was the worst thing. Water flowing uphill, confused, weeping into the sky.

Grandmother held Issel's hand very hard and said: *You are going through the salt. Do not look back. Do not answer if anything calls your name in my voice.*

Something did call. Something called in Grandmother's voice, sweet and tired, from behind. Issel walked with eyes shut and counted steps instead of looking. Four hundred steps. Four hundred and eleven. The air changed. It got heavy and quiet and *flavorless*, like drinking plain water after soup, and then the calling stopped all at once, like a hand clapped over a mouth.

Then there was grass. Then there was this valley, where nothing sings wrong, because nothing sings at all. The valley has no magic in it. Issel felt it right away — felt the silence like a blanket, like a weight. Scary and safe both. The thing that follows cannot come in. But neither can anything else. Neither can help.

Neither can home.

* * *

At night Issel sleeps in the big bed and Paloma sleeps in the chair, or she pretends to, sitting up with her book. Issel wakes sometimes in the dark and watches her. She has a face that works hard even when she isn't doing anything. Her eyebrows pull together like two people trying to carry one basket.

Once, in the dark, Issel whispered the name. Just once. Just to hear it.

*Issel.*

And the word ran downhill inside the chest, quick over stones, and something far away — past the valley edge, past the salt, past the forgetting — something heard it and turned around.

Issel knew this the way you know a storm is coming before the clouds arrive: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like metal rain.

So now the name stays inside. A name kept inside is a small door kept shut. Issel holds it shut the way you hold your breath underwater.

But doors get tired. Doors want to open. Every day the name pushes a little harder against the inside of the teeth, wanting out, wanting to be water again, running downhill.

* * *

In the mornings there is soup.

Paloma makes soup with things she grows and things she buys from neighbors, and she talks while she stirs, not expecting answers, which is kind of her, though Issel doesn't know the word *kind* yet in this language. In this language Issel knows maybe sixty words now. Soup. Bread. Bed. Cold. Hot. Yes. No. *Careful* — that one is important; Paloma says it a lot, reaching toward the stove, toward the knife, toward the door. As if careful could be wrapped around a person like a coat.

This morning Paloma points at the steam rising off the bowl and says, "Steam."

Issel points at the steam. "Steam." The word comes out crooked but whole. Paloma smiles with her whole face, which changes her completely, like sun coming over the valley rim.

Then Paloma taps her own chest. "Paloma." Points at Issel. Waits.

Here it is. The question that comes every morning, gentle as a hand on the shoulder: *what are you called?*

Issel looks at the soup. The soup does not help.

There is an old rule, older than Grandmother, older than the road: *a name given freely can be used kindly; a name taken by force can be used cruelly.* The Grey Choir cannot take a name that hasn't been offered. That is why they sing instead of asking. Singing feels like asking. It isn't. Singing is fishing with your voice, dropping hooks made of sound into other people's mouths, waiting for someone to bite, to answer, to give themselves away note by note.

Issel will not bite. Issel will not be caught.

But also — also — the name wants out. Also, this woman fed Issel for six days without asking anything except *steam*, *bread*, *careful*. Also, Grandmother said one more thing, at the very end, at the salt, squeezing Issel's hand so hard the bones sang:

*Find someone whose blood remembers us. Give them the key. Trust the taster.*

*Taster.* Issel doesn't know what a taster is. But this woman tastes everything before she eats it, lips pressed to the spoon, a little pause, a listening-in-the-body. Issel has watched. This woman checks her food like a soldier checks a road.

Maybe that is the sign. Maybe Grandmother meant her.

The name pushes at the teeth. Issel opens their mouth —

— and outside, far away, down where the fields go gold and then gray toward the border, something begins to sing.

Very far. Very low. Not a melody yet. More like the memory of a melody, like hearing music through a wall, through a hill, through years. Most ears would call it wind. Issel's ears are not most ears.

The singing bends one note wrong.

Issel's spoon stops halfway to their mouth. The soup trembles in tiny rings.

It shouldn't reach in here. Nothing reaches in here; that is the whole truth of the valley, its great flat silence, its blessed nothing. But the song isn't trying to come in. The song is doing something worse. The song is standing at the border, patient as winter, singing the valley's name — *Vell, Vell* — over and over, each time a hair less correct, wearing the name down the way water wears down a step.

Paloma frowns at the window. "Wind," she says. She says it like she's convincing herself.

Issel shakes their head, small, and puts a hand flat on their own chest, over the place where the name lives, where it runs downhill over stones.

"Issel," the child says aloud, for the first time since the crossing, giving it away freely, kindly, into this warm room that smells of bread and herb — because if the Choir is already singing at the edge, then hiding the name is a door locked from inside a house already burning, and this woman's blood is supposed to remember, and Grandmother never lied about anything except being fine.

The name leaves like water. Quick over stones. Into Paloma's keeping.

Outside, far off, the wrong note bends a little further wrong.

And beneath the floor, beneath the earth, deep down where no sea has ever been, something old and salty turns over in its sleep — and begins, very softly, to hum.
