## 7. Recipes as Maps

Issel slept like something that had been running for a long time and had finally decided to stop.

Paloma watched from the doorway of the small back room she'd cleared—the one that had been a pantry until three days ago—and thought about how strange it was that a child could simply *decide* to trust you. No negotiation. No terms. Just a salt-caked kid who had walked out of nowhere, looked at her face, and said a word in a dead language that her own mouth had answered before her mind could vote on it.

*Vessa.* Home.

She'd been turning that word over for two days now, like a stone with something underneath it.

The cottage was quiet in the way she liked best: banked fire, wind worrying the eaves, the smell of yesterday's bread still hanging in the rafters. On ordinary mornings she would already be walking up to the castle, tasting the Regent's breakfast while her hands stayed steady and her face stayed empty. But today was not an ordinary morning. Today she had told the kitchen steward she was ill—her first lie in eleven years of service, and it had come out smooth as butter, which frightened her more than the lie itself.

She sat at her worktable and pulled her grandmother's recipe book out of the bottom drawer where it had lived for nine years, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling faintly of cloves and dust.

It was a plain thing. Leather gone soft as cloth at the corners, spine cracked, pages the color of weak tea. Her grandmother had called it *the book* the way other people called their children by name, and when she died it had passed to Paloma along with everything else that survived her: two spoons, a copper pot, a lullaby, and no explanations.

Paloma opened it to the first page and read the familiar heading. *Barley Soup for Cold Mornings.*

She knew this recipe by heart. She could cook it blind, half-asleep, grieving. Barley, marrow bone, three onions, a fistful of dried thyme, salt "until the spoon remembers the sea."

That last line. She'd always taken it for poetry. Grandmothers wrote poetry into recipes; it was practically the law.

But last night, humming the lullaby over Issel's sleeping body, matching syllable to syllable with growing horror and wonder, she had noticed something. The lullaby's third verse contained a word she had never been able to translate in her head—a word that lived only in the song, that had no home anywhere else.

And that word appeared here. In the soup recipe. In the instruction about salt.

*Until the spoon remembers the sea* — but written beneath it, squeezed into the margin in her grandmother's cramped hand, was the word itself, followed by a number.

A number.

Recipes didn't have numbers tucked under their flourishes. Not unless they meant something other than cooking.

Paloma fetched a candle closer, took out a fresh sheet of paper, a pencil stub, and began to copy the book—not the recipes, but everything *around* the recipes. The marginalia. The crossed-out words. The little marks in the corners of pages that she had always assumed were ink blots or idle scratches.

By midmorning her table looked like a spymaster's desk. Pages copied out. Symbols listed. And a pattern emerging that made the hair on her arms stand up despite the fire.

Her grandmother had used numbers the way cooks used pinches—but never for quantities of anything edible. Beside *three onions* there was nothing. But beside the instruction to let the soup rest *beneath the north window*, there was a small numeral: 4. Beside a note about storing pickled things *in the cool dark*, another numeral: 7. Beside a line about how bread proved fastest *near the hearthstone where the floor dips*, a numeral: 2.

Directions. Distances. Depths.

Paloma sat very still.

Then she turned to the middle of the book, to a recipe she had never once cooked, because it wasn't really a recipe at all. It was headed *White Stew, Old Country Style* and its ingredient list read like nonsense: white earth, sweet water, the long vein, the breathing mouth, the place where two salts argue.

She had assumed, all these years, that grief had scrambled her grandmother's notes. That the old woman had written down a dish from a homeland too painful to describe accurately, and what remained was fragments.

Now Paloma read it with new eyes and felt the floor tilt gently beneath her chair.

*White earth.* Salt. Not seasoning—substance. The caves under Vell were cut through solid rock salt; every child in the valley knew that; miners brought out blocks of it white as sugar.

*Sweet water.* Fresh water. The underground river. Bram the ferryman poled his night barges through caverns where the water ran clear and cold and tasted of nothing at all—which was, Hedda once said, the strangest taste there is.

*The long vein.* A passage. A corridor of some kind.

*The breathing mouth.* A cave mouth that exhaled air. Caves did that—the big ones, the deep ones. The miners called them chimneys and refused to work near them.

*The place where two salts argue.* Two veins of salt meeting. Mineral strata colliding. A landmark.

And beside each impossible ingredient, in the margins, those same cramped numerals. 4. 7. 2. And others. A whole sequence of them, marching down the page like a column of soldiers.

Paloma's hands had begun to shake, so she pressed them flat against the table and breathed the way she breathed when a dish went wrong in front of the Regent—in through the nose, slow, controlled, invisible.

Her grandmother hadn't left her a cookbook.

She'd left her a map.

* * *

The morning went to pieces productively after that.

Paloma worked the way she did everything: methodically, quietly, with an efficiency that had kept her alive at court for eleven years. She cross-referenced the numerals against the geography she knew. Everyone in Vell knew the broad strokes of what lay beneath them—the salt mines outside town, the river caverns that the ferryman's family had worked for generations—but nobody mapped them precisely because nobody needed to. The mines gave salt; the river gave passage; the rest was dark and irrelevant.

But her grandmother had numbered it all.

Four paces north of the breathing mouth—or four hundred? The units weren't given, which maddened her until she realized they didn't need to be. Whoever this book was written for would know. Would have been taught. The book assumed a reader who shared a frame of reference that Paloma had spent her whole life missing.

Except she hadn't missed all of it. That was the thing that kept catching in her chest like a fishbone. She knew the valley. She'd walked its hills, traded in its market, tasted its water in everything she'd ever eaten. And as she decoded line after line, the landmarks resolved with a certainty that made her laugh out loud, alone, in a way she hadn't laughed since she was a girl.

*The place where two salts argue* — she knew that. The miners complained about it constantly. Deep in the eastern workings, two veins of different colors met in a seam so hard that tools dulled against it, and the foreman had named it the Quarrel before Paloma was born. She'd heard old Toma describe it a hundred times, drunk, at the autumn fair: *white against grey, and neither will give, and God himself couldn't make them shake hands.*

It was in the book. It had been in the book for decades, sitting between instructions for a stew that did not exist, waiting for someone who knew both halves of the riddle.

Paloma pushed back from the table and stood in the middle of her kitchen with her heart going hard.

From the back room came the small sounds of Issel waking—not crying, never crying, just the rustle of blankets and then silence, the listening silence of a child checking whether the world was still safe. Paloma called out a word she'd learned two nights ago, *vessa*, home, and got back a sleepy answer, and the sound of it steadied her more than any breath exercise ever had.

She made porridge. She fed the child. She watched Issel eat with the total absorption of someone who remembered hunger, and she thought: whoever wrote this book wanted their people to find their way back. Wanted it enough to hide directions inside the most durable container a household possesses. Recipes get copied. They survive fires, moves, marriages, deaths. You can burn a map; you cannot burn the idea of soup.

Grandmother, what were you afraid of?

And the second question, arriving on its heels like a shadow: what were you preparing me for?

* * *

By afternoon the pattern had grown teeth.

Paloma laid out the copied marginalia in sequence and found that the numerals formed a route. Not a vague one—a precise one, a chain of bearings and distances stitched through the real bones of the valley. The breathing mouth matched the great chimney-vent the miners avoided, east workings. From there, the long vein ran northwest—four units, whatever a unit was—descending. The sweet water confirmed it: the underground river bent exactly that way; Bram had told her once, complaining about a current, that past the third landing the water turned cold enough to ache.

And at the end of the chain, after the arguing salts, after a final numeral larger than all the others, the marginalia stopped giving directions and started giving warnings.

She copied the last lines out carefully, translating the Ostravan script against the lullaby's vocabulary, guessing at gaps, feeling each guessed word land in her stomach like swallowed ice:

*Beneath the quarrel of salts, the door stands.*
*It opens for blood alone. It closes for want of it.*
*Once in a generation the lock forgets itself.*
*Do not come alone. Do not come late.*
*Bring salt from your new home. It will know the difference.*

Paloma read it five times. Then she set the pencil down, walked to the window, and looked out at the valley of Vell lying gold and ordinary in the afternoon light—smoke rising from chimneys, the castle grey on its hill, Hedda's herb garden visible two plots over, everything safe and bounded and famously, blessedly free of magic.

Magic couldn't enter the valley. Every schoolchild learned it. It was why refugees had come here for centuries, why her grandmother had come here, why nothing uncanny had ever touched Paloma's life except, apparently, a dead language sleeping in her mouth.

But the book didn't say magic was trying to get *in*.

The book said there was a door *underneath*. Already here. Patient as geology. Opening once in a generation—for blood alone.

*Blood alone.*

Paloma looked down at her own hands. Poison-proof, unremarkable, steady hands. Hands that had never once trembled at court, trembling now.

Her grandmother had written this book in a valley that magic could not touch, about a door that magic could not reach, and had hidden it in the one language the valley couldn't read and the one format no authority would think to confiscate. She had raised a granddaughter on lullabies in that same tongue without ever explaining a word of it—had sung the key into the child before the child could talk.

You don't do that casually. You don't bury a map in a cookbook and a cipher in a cradle song unless you believe that someday, somehow, it will matter desperately. Unless you're certain that the door will still be waiting. Unless you know your bloodline will still be here to open it.

*It opens for blood alone.*

Issel padded out of the back room, dragging the blanket, and stood beside her chair. The child looked up at the papers spread across the table—at the copied script, the columns of numerals, the word *door* underlined twice in Paloma's own hand—and then looked up at Paloma with an expression far too old for a six-year-old face.

Issel pointed at the underline. Said something soft in Ostravan. One of the few phrases Paloma now understood whole, because she'd said it herself, helplessly, on the first night:

*I knew you would find it.*

Paloma crouched down until they were eye to eye. Outside, the light was failing, and somewhere across the rooftops a bell rang the hour, ordinary as bread. Everything in her wanted to fold the papers away, put the book back in its drawer, go back to being the taster, the invisible woman, the survivor. That life had rules. That life had kept her alive.

But the book was open on the table, and the book had been waiting nine years for her to read it, and her grandmother's voice seemed to rise off the page like heat: *do not come late.*

"Issel," she said, and then, because some things shouldn't be said in the valley's language, not even to a child, she said it in the old one, her accent clumsy but true: "Show me the way under."

Issel's whole face changed—opened—like a door.

They would need lamps. Rope. Warm clothes, and food that kept, and a story for anyone who asked why the taster was buying miner's gear at dusk. They would need to wait for a night Bram was poling the river, because the long vein began at the water.

Three days' preparation, maybe four. Paloma blew out the candles, folded the map-pages into the oilcloth with the book, and felt something settle in her chest that she hadn't felt since childhood: purpose with an edge on it, sharp and clean.

Above them, the valley slept, believing itself sealed.

Beneath them, something hummed in the dark, counting the years since it had last been fed with footsteps.

And on the lowest shelf, wrapped in its cloth, her grandmother's book lay warm to the touch—though Paloma told herself, firmly, that it was only the fire.
