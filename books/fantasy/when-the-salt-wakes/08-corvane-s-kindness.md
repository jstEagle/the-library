## 8. Corvane's Kindness

The summons came on a Thursday, which was wrong in two ways.

First, because Thursdays were my days off—the court had learned long ago that a taster who worked seven days grew dull of tongue, and a dull taster was worse than no taster at all. Second, because the note was delivered by a boy in palace livery rather than the usual kitchen runner, and it was written in ink instead of charcoal, and it bore a seal I had never seen pressed into anything addressed to me: a black bird with its wings folded, patient as a closed hand.

Corvane's seal.

I read the note three times at my kitchen table while Issel slept in the loft above, curled under the quilt my grandmother had made from coat scraps. *The Spymaster requests the honor of your presence at midday. A matter concerning your duties.* Nine words. Nine words that meant nothing and everything, because spymasters did not request honors from food-tasters. Spymasters summoned them.

I fed the fire, left bread and honey where Issel would find it when she woke—I had taught her to wait inside, to answer no knock—and walked up through Vell's crooked streets to the palace with my stomach doing the slow cold turn it usually saved for arsenic.

* * *

Corvane received me not in his offices in the tower, where the spies and their ledgers lived, but in a small room off the west gallery that I had passed a hundred times without ever seeing anyone enter or leave. It was warm in a way the rest of the palace wasn't—a real fire, not the ceremonial kind—and there was a table set for two with an actual meal on it: roast partridge, glazed roots, bread still steaming, a pot of the dark plum preserve that came up from the southern farms only once a year.

"You'll forgive the theater," Corvane said. He was smaller than he looked from across courtyards. Everyone was, but Corvane especially—he had built himself out of posture and reputation until the man underneath was nearly beside the point. His hair was iron-grey, cut close; his hands were soft, unscarred, the hands of someone whose weapons were all made of words. "I find people speak more honestly over food. And you of all people know what honesty over food is worth."

"Your note said my duties."

"It did." He gestured to the chair opposite him. "Sit. Taste."

I sat. I tasted. Partridge—clean, though slightly overdone, the way meat gets when it has been kept waiting. Roots—clean, butter, thyme, a whisper of vinegar. Bread—clean. Preserve—

I stopped with the spoon halfway to my mouth.

Not poison. Nothing so crude. But there was something in the preserve, something folded small into the sweetness, a bitter thread like the memory of almond, and beneath it a second bitterness, greener, that I knew from years of the Regent's enemies and their inventive little gifts. Not enough to harm me. Enough to be found. Enough to be noticed by exactly one person in this valley.

I set the spoon down and looked up.

Corvane was watching me with an expression of mild, unhurried interest, the way a man watches water to see if it boils.

"Well?" he said.

"It's fine," I said. "Good preserve. The plums were late this year—they always are, after a wet summer—but they sweetened anyway. Whoever made it knows what they're about."

"And nothing else?"

"The roots are overcooked. You should tell your kitchens."

Something moved behind his eyes—not disappointment exactly. Recalculation. He took the preserve pot himself, spooned a measure onto a plate, and ate it. Chewed. Swallowed. Smiled.

"There is nothing in it," he said. "Nothing harmful. A trace of bitter extract, yes, harmless, added this morning by my own hand. Do you know why?"

"To see if I'd find it."

"To see if you'd say so." He set the plate aside. "Every taster before you, when asked directly whether a dish is clean, says yes. Every single one. It is the great weakness of your profession—you are paid to be suspicious of food but never of men. You looked at me just now as if I had put a knife on the table between us. That is the most honest thing anyone has done in this room in a year."

"I don't understand what any of this has to do with my duties."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Yet." He poured wine, two cups, and pushed one toward me. I let it sit. He drank his own without comment. "Let me tell you about your duties, Paloma Thackeray, since you seem unclear on them. You believe your duty is to die well, if it comes to that. Swallow, sicken, be carried out, be remembered briefly as a line in a steward's ledger. That is the duty you were given. It is not the duty you could have."

He leaned back. The fire cracked. Somewhere far off in the palace, a door closed with a sound like a full stop.

"The Queen Regent trusts her kitchens to you," he said. "She trusts your tongue the way a general trusts a wall. But walls can be climbed, and tongues can be bought, and the Regent knows it, and lately she has been sleeping badly. There are poisons moving through Vell that don't announce themselves. Slow ones. Ones that take a month to find the heart, so that by the time the body fails, the dish is forgotten and the cook has moved on to some other court. Your immunity protects you from dying. It does not protect you from being used."

"Used how?"

"A taster who reports all dishes clean," Corvane said softly, "is worth more than a hundred assassins. If someone bought you—if someone threatened you, or charmed you, or simply asked as a friend asks—you could pass a poisoned cup to the Regent herself and sleep soundly after. So my duty, as I'm sure you appreciate, is to make certain no one has done that. To make certain the wall has no doors in it."

"And you test this by putting bitter extract in plum jam?"

"I test it by watching. The jam was for your benefit only—it tells me you're honest about what you taste, at least when surprised. The rest—" he spread his soft hands, "—the rest is observation. Which brings me, at last, to the actual reason I've asked you here."

There it was. I had known it was coming the whole time, the way you know rain is coming from the smell of the ground, and knowing did nothing to steady my hands. I folded them in my lap like a good servant.

"You've missed days," Corvane said. "Three this month. Unusual for you. You are, by reputation, the most reliable servant in this palace—never late, never ill, never absent except on your allotted day, and even then reachable. Then this month: absences. Errands run quickly, eyes down. You've stopped taking your evening walk along the river path. You buy more food than one woman eats." He let that last sentence sit in the air between us like a fly neither of us wanted to swat first.

"I've had a poor appetite for walking," I said.

"Mm." He opened a ledger on the table—of course he had a ledger, of course it was already open to my page—and turned it toward me. "Market records. The baker on Tanner's Lane notes selling you two loaves on Tuesday. The greengrocer notes carrots, onions, a chicken—'a big one,' she wrote, 'enough for a family.' You live alone, Mistress Thackeray. Unless something has changed."

The fire popped. My heartbeat sounded loud in my own ears, absurdly loud, loud enough that I was certain he could hear it.

"You eat more when you're worried," I said. "Everyone does."

"Some do." He closed the ledger. And then his whole manner changed—shifted like weather, the sharpness folding away and something warmer coming in behind it, something almost gentle. "Paloma. May I call you Paloma? Thank you. Let me speak plainly, because I think you will respect plainness. I am not your enemy. Whatever you believe about my profession—and most of it is true, I'm sorry to say—I have spent twenty-two years keeping this valley safe, and in those twenty-two years I have learned to recognize a particular kind of person, because I am one myself. The person who serves a place that will never serve them back. Who makes themselves useful so they won't have to be loved. Who stands very still and very quiet at the edge of other people's lives, tasting their dangers, and goes home alone."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say. He had reached into my chest and described the shape of what he found there, accurately, and the accuracy felt like a kind of violence.

"I was born outside this valley," he went on. "Did you know that? In the hill country east of the passes, in a town that isn't on maps anymore either—not erased, just forgotten, which is slower and crueller. I came to Vell at nineteen with nothing, the same as half the court. I know what it costs to belong somewhere that keeps a list of what you owe. So when I see another such person—someone careful, someone invisible, someone who has survived by making themselves indispensable—I feel something like kinship. And kinship offers things. Protection. Patronage. A place closer to the fire than the edge of the room."

"Why?" The word came out harder than I intended. "What do you want from me?"

"Only answers. Small ones." He refilled his own wine. "The Regent's court has been receiving strange reports this season. Salt where salt shouldn't be. Wells turning brackish in districts that have never known the sea. Animals leaving the high pastures early. And now a taster who feeds a family's worth of food into a house that holds one woman." He looked at me, and his eyes were not unkind, and that was the worst part—that he meant the kindness, that it was real, that he was offering me genuine warmth with one hand while the other hand held a ledger with my name in it. "So I will ask you plainly, and you may answer plainly, and whatever you answer will stay between us regardless. Have you taken in a stranger? A guest? Anyone new under your roof this season?"

The silence stretched. I heard my own breathing. I thought of Issel asleep in the loft, small and salt-crusted and trusting, of the word she had taught me last night—*sennet*, she had signed against my palm, pressing her fingers there twice, and I had understood it meant *safe*, or maybe *home*, or maybe both at once, the words being perhaps the same word where she came from.

I had spent thirty-four years telling the truth. It was not virtue, whatever the steward's ledgers implied. It was policy. Truth was the safest thing a powerless person could trade in; a taster known for honesty could survive a dishonest court, the way a clear stream survives a muddy country. I had never lied to the Regent, to the steward, to Hedda, to anyone who mattered. Lying was a door, and doors, once opened, were hard to close again.

But Corvane was asking me to open a different door—to walk Issel out of my cottage and lay her on his table, wrapped in truth like a clean cloth. And I found, sitting in that warm room with its honest fire and its honest meal, that some doors open the other direction too.

"No," I said.

The word was small and enormous. It left my mouth and changed the shape of the air.

"No stranger," I said, and heard my voice hold steady, steady as it did over the Regent's cups. "No guests. I've been tired this season, Corvane—that's all. Tired and hungry and spending too much at market because cooking properly is the only thing that makes the days feel worth the trouble. If feeding myself well is a crime in this valley, write it in your ledger and I'll pay the fine."

He watched me for a long moment. Long enough for me to count four heartbeats, five, six, each one a small treason.

And then he smiled, and rose, and the warmth came back into his face like sun coming out from behind cloud, and I understood that I had passed—or failed—some test whose terms I might never learn.

"Then we understand each other," he said. "And I'm glad. Genuinely glad, Paloma. Because I have a proposal for you—one that comes with the protection and patronage I mentioned, and requires nothing further from you at present. The Regent is expanding her household security for the solstice season. She wants a second taster. Senior taster, with authority over the kitchens' procurement, a stipend, rooms in the palace if you want them." He named the figure, and it was three times what I earned, and we both knew it. "It means more visibility. More scrutiny. It means I will be watching you closely, because that is what seniority attracts. But it also means no one touches you without answering to me. No steward reassigns you. No nobleman's complaint reaches you. You would be mine to protect."

*Mine.* The word settled over me like a net laid gently over a bird—no wires touching, no weight, just the geometry of the thing, the shape of the trap visible to anyone who cared to look.

"That's generous," I said.

"It's practical. Good tools deserve maintenance." He walked me to the door himself, opened it himself, and stood close—close enough that his voice dropped to something meant only for me. "One more thing, and then I release you to your day. If you ever need help—if anything ever comes to your door that frightens you, or that you don't understand, or that you can't carry alone—come to me. Before anyone else. Before you act, before you hide, before you decide that the careful life you've built is worth less than whatever it is. Promise me that much."

"I promise I'll think about it."

His smile flickered—just barely, just at the corner—as if I had answered a different question correctly after all. "That's more than most give me. Go home, Paloma. Eat your big dinners. Walk wherever you like again." And then, as I stepped past him into the cold corridor: "The valley keeps us all, in the end. It's the only place magic can't follow. Sometimes I think that's why we love it—we're safe here from everything, including ourselves."

I walked out into the grey afternoon and did not look back.

* * *

Issel was awake when I got home, sitting cross-legged on the floor by the hearth with the honey bread untouched in front of her, arranging dried beans into patterns on the flagstones. She looked up when I came in, and her face did the thing it always did—searched mine, quickly, thoroughly, reading my mood the way I read a dish. She had learned to do that in whatever life had come before, and the learning showed, and every time it showed I wanted to burn something down.

She pointed at my face. Then she made a gesture I hadn't taught her: two fingers drawn down her own cheek, slow, like tracking a tear that hadn't fallen yet.

"I'm fine," I said.

She pointed at me again. Made the gesture again. Held my gaze with the flat, patient insistence of a child who has already caught adults lying and expects better, eventually.

"I met a dangerous man today," I admitted, kneeling down among the beans. "He was kind to me. That's what made him dangerous."

Issel considered this. Then she swept the bean pattern away—all except one bean, which she placed carefully between us on the stones. She pointed at it. Then at me. Then at the door.

"One," she said, in her soundless way. *One of us.*

"Yes," I said. "One of us now. Him and everyone else—everyone else is outside the bean."

She nodded gravely, as though I had stated something obvious, and began laying out the rest of the beans around the lone one in a wide circle, a border, a boundary with our single self safe in the middle of it. Watching her build the map of my new life out of dried beans on my kitchen floor, I felt the lie I had told settle into me fully for the first time—not as guilt, exactly, though the guilt was there, cold at the base of the throat like swallowed river water. As change. I had crossed something today. There was a before-Paloma who had never lied, and she had stayed behind in that warm room with Corvane, and the Paloma who walked home through the dusk was a woman with a secret, and secrets, unlike poisons, cannot be spat back out once tasted.

That night I bolted the door, banked the fire low, and took my grandmother's recipe book down from the shelf—the one with the marginalia in the script that matched the sounds Issel made in her sleep. If Corvane was watching me now, truly watching, then I had perhaps days before his observation became his certainty. Whatever my grandmother had hidden in these pages, in these recipes that were not entirely recipes, I needed to read it before someone else decided to read me.

I turned to the page for salt-baked fish, where the marginalia ran thickest, and by candlelight, with Issel's breath soft and even in the loft above, I began at last to translate.

The first line stopped my heart.

It was not instructions for fish.
