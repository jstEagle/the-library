## 9. Found Family, Small Hours

### Issel

The woman's house smelled of smoke and dried herbs and something underneath all of it that Issel could not name at first. It took three days of sleeping on the straw pallet by the hearth before Issel understood: the smell was *safety*. It had a smell here. In Ostrava, safety had been a sound—the low chord the door made when it sealed behind you—and then it had been silence, and then it had been running.

Here it was rosemary and woodsmoke and the woman humming while she stirred a pot.

Issel did not speak. Speaking was dangerous. Words were how they found you; words were threads, and if you pulled one out of your mouth the wrong people could take hold of the other end. So Issel held every word like a stone under the tongue and said nothing.

But the woman hummed.

And the humming was wrong in a way that made Issel's chest ache. It was a lullaby—any child knows a lullaby from the inside, the way you know your own heartbeat—but it was an Ostravan lullaby sung with valley vowels, flattened and bent, like a river photographed and then painted from memory. The words weren't quite words. They were the shapes of words worn smooth by someone who had learned them as sound alone.

*Your grandmother,* Issel thought. *She carried the song out but not the tongue.*

On the third evening, the woman set down a bowl of soup—barley, turnip, a slick of fat on top, salt—and knelt across from Issel with her own bowl, which was smaller. She did this deliberately. Issel noticed things like that. The woman always gave herself the smaller portion and pretended she hadn't.

"Paloma," the woman said, and touched her own collarbone.

Issel looked at her for a long time. The fire popped. Outside, wind moved through the valley grass with its long dry hiss, so different from the sea-sound of home that it still woke Issel at night, reaching for a coast that wasn't there.

Then, carefully, like setting down something breakable, Issel let one thread out.

"Issel." A hand on a small flat chest.

Paloma smiled, and it changed her whole face, which until then had been a face built for not being noticed—level, watchful, arranged. For a moment she was just somebody's granddaughter, somebody's neighbor, somebody who had once been a girl eating soup in this same room.

"Issel," she repeated, and her accent mangled it gently, *Ee-sell*, and Issel found that being mangled didn't hurt. Being named wrong by someone who wanted to say it right was very different from having your name erased.

They ate. The soup was good. The soup was salted exactly right, which was strange, because Paloma had told Issel—by pointing, by shrugging—that tasters were forbidden much salt, that their tongues must stay clean instruments, and yet this soup was seasoned like love.

Afterward, Paloma dipped her finger in the dregs on her bowl and drew a small shape on the table. A circle with a line through it. Then she pointed at the pot, and raised her eyebrows.

Issel knew that shape. Every Ostravan child knew it. It meant *enough*, or it meant *whole*, depending on where you drew it—in the air it meant enough, on food it meant whole, on a door it meant sealed.

Issel picked up Paloma's finger and drew the circle again, then tapped the pot, then made the gesture that meant *more*—palm opening, fingers unfurling like a leaf.

*Not whole. More.*

Paloma laughed—a startled, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years—and ladled more soup into Issel's bowl without giving herself any.

That night, falling asleep by the hearth, Issel listened to the house settle and thought: *This is what we crossed the dark to keep alive. Not the city. This. Someone giving you the bigger bowl and lying about it.*

Somewhere far away, past the valley's edge, something was singing Issel's name in a voice with no mouth. But the hearth was warm, and the lullaby hummed itself into Issel's bones even bent and broken, and for a few hours the stones stayed safely under the tongue.

### Paloma

Paloma had never shared a roof with anyone. Twenty-six years of careful, invisible living had produced a household of one, run with the precision of a court kitchen and the warmth of one too. She knew how many steps it was from her bed to the hearth (eleven), how long a loaf kept in cold weather (four days), how to be so quiet her neighbors sometimes seemed surprised she existed.

Now there was a child in her house who rearranged everything without moving anything.

Issel ate like a bird and slept like the dead. Issel woke before dawn and stood at the window watching the light come up over the eastern ridge with an expression Paloma couldn't read—longing? vigilance?—and would not be drawn away until the sun cleared the hills. Issel folded the blanket into a shape too perfect for a six-year-old, corners sharp as a soldier's kit, and it broke Paloma's heart a little each morning, because she knew what kind of place taught a child to fold like that. Places that might be taken from you at any moment teach neatness. Places that are safe teach sprawl.

They built a language between them the way you build a fire—one dry thing at a time.

Over meals, mostly. Soup became their classroom because soup required sitting still together, and sitting still together turned out to be the hardest and best thing Paloma had done in years. Paloma would point and name: *spoon. bowl. fire. cold. hot.* Issel would repeat the word once, flawlessly, file it away, and then point back and answer in the other tongue—and here was the miracle, here was the thing that made Paloma's hands shake so badly she had to grip the table: she *understood*. Not always. Not cleanly. But the sounds came into her ear and meaning bloomed behind them like ink dropped in water, unbidden, ancestral.

*Ashk.* That meant ember—or perhaps hearth-heart; the two ideas lived in the same word the way root lives under flower.

*Sennet.* Water that is given, not taken. There was no valley word for that. Paloma turned it over in her mind for a full day, marveling that a language would need it.

One night Issel pointed at Paloma's chest and said something soft and round, three syllables, and waited.

"What does it mean?"

Issel pointed again at her chest. Then at the hearth. Then, with great seriousness, at the soup pot.

"Hearth-keeper?" Paloma guessed. "Cook?"

Issel shook her head, frustrated, and tried a different tack: she mimed Paloma tasting food, waving a hand in front of her own face—*no harm*—then mimed the child eating, then clasped both hands together over her heart.

Paloma sat back. "Someone who makes sure the food is safe," she said slowly. "A guardian of eating."

Issel nodded hard, then corrected with one finger: not guardian of eating. Guardian of *the eater*.

There was a word for that. A whole word, an old word, a word for the person whose task it was to stand between a body and its poisoning. Paloma felt the floor tilt. Her whole life, Vell had called her a tool—a good tongue, a useful gut, a servant valued the way you value a lock. And here was a vanished language that had looked at the same work and seen a *keeper*, someone tended and honored, a role you gave to family.

She didn't cry. Tasters don't cry; tears salt the palate. But she had to leave the table for a moment and stand in the cold yard breathing steam, looking up at stars she'd memorized for company, thinking: *I have been lonely my entire life and I did not know it until someone handed me a word for what I am.*

When she came back in, Issel had washed both bowls without being asked and folded the cloth into that terrible perfect square, and Paloma said, "All right, that's enough of that," and mussled the folds into chaos just to see the child's scandalized face, and Issel laughed—a real laugh, high and startling, the first one—and the house felt different after that. Lived-in. Warm in a way fire alone doesn't manage.

*

The neighbors started leaving things on the third day.

First it was Marta Ostrow the baker, who left half a seed loaf wrapped in cloth and said loudly, to no one, "Oven ran hot, waste otherwise," while making direct eye contact with Paloma across the yard. Then the ferryman Bram, whom Paloma barely knew beyond nods at the landing, appeared at dusk with a string of river fish and the statement that he'd "caught too many, happens," though everyone on the Vell knew Bram never caught too many anything; the man fished like he prayed, sparingly and with intent.

By the end of the week there was a small economy on her doorstep: pickled roots from Hedda with a note in the herbalist's crabbed hand—*for the little one, and for you, and I want to know what name-sound she carries when you're ready*—a jar of honey from a farm family whose names Paloma didn't even know, a bundle of candles.

"It's because of me," she told Issel, translating herself into gestures as she spoke, an odd doubled conversation they'd developed where Paloma talked and signed and Issel answered in either tongue and somehow they met in the middle. "People don't leave food for me. People forget I eat."

Issel considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then pointed at Paloma, then at the door, then made the open-palm gesture—*sennet*, given water—and finally pressed both hands flat over her own heart.

Paloma didn't know the word for what that meant. But she understood it anyway. Some meanings cross every border.

She started cooking more than they needed. She couldn't help it. Ten years of tasting other people's food, of eating precisely measured portions under observation, had starved something in her, and now her hands wanted to make abundance. She braided bread the way she remembered seeing it done once at a festival. She made a stew with too many kinds of root vegetable because the market woman gave her a discount on the bruised ones and Paloma discovered she liked a crowded pot.

She caught herself humming while she cooked. Caught herself singing actual words, the lullaby, letting the syllables come out whole instead of worn smooth, trying them against Issel's silent corrections—because Issel *did* correct, wincing at wrong vowels, and Paloma found she wanted to get it right, wanted it with a ferocity that frightened her a little. Somewhere in her blood was a country she'd never seen, and every correctly pronounced word was a brick of it coming back.

"You're teaching me my grandmother," she said one night, and Issel tilted her head and pointed at Paloma, then at herself, then shook her head: no. Not teaching. *Returning.* She made a gesture Paloma hadn't seen before—hands cupped, then opened outward, like a bird released.

Giving something back that was always yours.

### Issel

The days took on a shape, and shapes were dangerous, because shapes could be tracked. Issel knew this the way Issel knew the tide-turn: in the body, without wanting to. Morning light at the window. Bread smell. The lesson-words over soup. Night, and the lullaby, and sleep.

It was the best shape Issel had ever lived inside. That was exactly the problem.

Every evening Issel walked the edges of it, checking. The Choir did not knock. The Choir did not need doors. You felt them first as a wrongness in music—if Paloma hummed and the tune went slightly gray, like ash settling on honey, Issel would go still and listen until the color came back. So far it always came back. The valley itself pushed the wrongness out; Issel could feel the land doing it, a slow muscular shrug along the borders, the way a sleeper pulls a blanket higher.

But the shrugs were getting tired. Issel could feel that too. And every day Issel stayed, the valley spent more strength, and every kindness—the bread, the honey, the big-bowl lie—made the debt worse, because now there was more here to lose.

So Issel began doing something secret. Each evening, while Paloma washed up, Issel practiced one word at a time, the important ones, hoarding them like coins: *run. hide. door. alone. safe.* Building the sentence that would have to be said someday, soon, before the solstice, before the gray song got through:

*I know the way back. Let me go so you can stay.*

Issel wasn't sure the sentence was survivable to say. Under the tongue, next to the stones, it already hurt.

But then some evenings the plan dissolved entirely. Some evenings Paloma pulled the stool close and showed Issel how to fold dough, guiding small floured hands, and laughed when the braid collapsed, and didn't fix it, just baked it crooked and called it beautiful, and Issel sat in the warm ruin of the plan and thought: *maybe found is a kind of safe too. Maybe the Choir cannot erase a thing that keeps remaking itself in a stranger's kitchen.*

Those evenings Issel almost believed it. Almost.

### Paloma

On the ninth night, snow threatened and didn't arrive, and the cottage was thick with the smell of the stew Paloma had left simmering, and Issel was asleep by the hearth with the blanket gloriously unsquared for once, and Paloma sat at the table with her grandmother's recipe book open to a page she'd read a hundred times and never truly *seen*.

Because now she had ears for it.

*"Take the white that remembers the sea, as much as fits a child's two hands."* Salt. Fine. But why *remembers*? *"Follow it down past the third cold, where the water runs backward."* Backward—underground rivers ran strange, Bram had said so once, drunk at midwinter. *"Speak the door-word with your mouth full, for the threshold must be fed."*

She had always read these as the ramblings of an old woman who missed a homeland she'd never described. Now, with a sleeping child who spoke the marginalia's language fluently, with a memory of a humming wall waiting somewhere under the valley like a held breath—now the recipes sounded less like cooking and more like directions.

Paloma looked at the child, at the rise and fall of small breaths, at the salt-white dust that still, faintly, no matter how often she bathed her, ghosted the creases of Issel's hands.

Then she looked at the window, and the reflection in it, and the reflection of the jar on the sill behind her—

—and stopped.

The jar of honey Bram had brought. She'd set it on the sill three nights ago. The honey inside it had been gold.

In the moonlight, its surface had gone pale. Crusted. White at the edges, creeping inward, like frost forming in a warm room.

Paloma rose quietly, crossed to the sill, and touched the glass. Cold. Colder than the night warranted. Inside the jar, the honey had stiffened into something that glittered when she tilted it, fine crystals catching the moon, and when she leaned close she could swear—she *did* swear, later, to Hedda—that it smelled of the sea.

Behind her, asleep, Issel murmured something in a dead tongue, and out past the garden fence, in Hedda's well a quarter-mile down the lane, the water began, very softly, to taste of salt.
