## 10. The First Sign

Hedda had drawn water from the same well for fifty-one years, and in all that time it had never once lied to her.

That was the whole point of a well, she'd tell the girls who came to apprentice—though none ever stayed, the work too damp and solitary for young bones. A well told you the truth about a place. It held the valley's blood in its throat. If the water went cloudy, rain was coming. If it smelled of iron, the river had shifted underground. If it turned sweet after a dry autumn, you thanked whatever old thing still kept Vell's foundations and you didn't ask its name.

On the morning after the frost came early, Hedda lowered her bucket and pulled up a handful of white.

She stood at the well's lip with her shawl still buttoned wrong and looked at what sat in her palm: coarse crystals, glittering, unmistakable. Salt. Not grit, not lime scale from the stones, not anything the water table could reasonably produce. Salt, in a valley that had never seen the sea. She was sixty-eight years old and she knew every plant, mineral, and mold within three days' walk of her door, and there was no source of brine in Vell deep enough to season a soup pot.

"Old girl's finally cracked," she said aloud, to hear a voice in the yard. The hens didn't argue. She tipped the salt into her apron pocket—because you never threw away something that might be useful later, that was another thing she told apprentices—and drew a second bucket.

The second bucket came up clear. Cold, sweet, ordinary.

Hedda drank some anyway, standing there in the grey light, letting it sit on her tongue like a rumor she was trying to catch out.

* * *

The church font was Tam's discovery, not hers, which stung more than the salt itself. Tam Wicker was nineteen and thought himself an herbalist because he could name eleven mushrooms, four of them correctly. He came banging on her door before noon, breathless, hatless, tracking mud across her clean floor.

"Hedda. Hedda, you have to come see."

"If it's a body, I charge extra for bodies."

"It's—it's the font. Father Aldric sent me. He says—" Tam swallowed. "He says the holy water's gone wrong."

The church of Vell was small and squat and older than the crown it served, built by hands whose names nobody remembered on a foundation nobody had ever bothered to excavate. Father Aldric was a mild man who believed mildly in most things, which made him perfect for a valley where belief was best kept diluted. He stood at the font when they arrived, wringing his stole like a washcloth, and he wouldn't let anyone else touch the water.

It had gone white. Not clouded—white, opaque as milk, and when Tam finally convinced Aldric to let him dip a finger in and taste it, he spat and said one word: "Salt."

"Consecrated water doesn't curdle," Aldric said faintly. "It doesn't *change*. That's rather the definition."

Hedda leaned over the stone basin. The surface was perfectly still, and beneath the white, at the very bottom, crystals were growing. She watched one form while she stood there—a slow, deliberate bloom, like a flower deciding to happen. It grew outward from nothing, branching, precise, patient.

Not forming the way minerals formed. Forming the way things grew.

She had a word for that, and she kept it behind her teeth where it couldn't do damage.

"When did this start?" she asked.

"Last night," Aldric said. "I filled the font yesterday morning, as always. It was fine at evening prayer." He hesitated. "There's something else. The candle flames last night—they burned blue for a few minutes. All together, all at once. I assumed a draft."

"A draft," Hedda repeated.

"The windows were shut," Aldric admitted.

She straightened up. Around her, the little church felt different than it had five minutes ago—smaller, somehow, the way a house feels smaller when someone unfamiliar is standing just outside it. She was not a woman who frightened easily; fear was a luxury for people who hadn't spent their youth dosing fever victims and burying the failures. But something cold walked up her spine, and it wasn't the draft.

"I'll need to take a sample," she said.

Aldric nodded gratefully, as if handing the problem to her made it smaller. People did that. It was one of the uses of being old.

* * *

Back home, she set the font-water beside the well-salt on her worktable and studied them like a physician studying two symptoms.

They matched. Of course they matched. Same crystal structure under her loupe, same faint smell—not the sharp bite of mined salt but something softer underneath, almost floral, almost like the memory of a flower. She'd smelled that undertone exactly once before in her life, and the memory of it arrived now with such force that she had to sit down.

Her mother's hands. Grinding something on a mortarstone at night, when Hedda was small enough to still be allowed in the room. A white powder, and her mother crying without noise, and words in a language that wasn't Vellish, low and rhythmic, like a rope being paid out hand over hand.

Her mother had died when Hedda was nine. Before that, she had been careful in ways Hedda hadn't understood until much later—careful about names, careful about the border passes, careful never to say certain words above a whisper. An immigrant's caution. Half the valley had it; half the valley's grandmothers had come from somewhere that no longer wanted them or no longer existed, depending on the story, and the stories were always told with a shrug that meant *don't ask.*

Hedda asked. She'd been asking for sixty years, gently, in taverns and sickrooms, collecting fragments the way other people collected buttons. Most of what she'd learned was useless. Some of it was worse.

One fragment surfaced now, unbidden, the way drowned things surface:

*Salt remembers the way home.*

An old ferryman had said it to her once, decades back, drunk on plum brandy and grief. She'd written it off as poetry. Ferryman Bram said a lot of things that sounded like poetry because his grandmother had been foreign and he liked the shape of foreign sentences in his mouth.

But Bram's grandmother, if the tavern gossip could be trusted, had come from the east. From beyond the passes. And Paloma Thackeray's grandmother—

Hedda stopped that thought where it stood. She got up instead, took down her ledger—the real one, the false-bottomed one, the one she'd inherited along with the mortarstone and the caution—and opened it to the pages she consulted perhaps twice a year.

Lists of families. Arrival dates. Original names where she'd managed to learn them, crossed-out names where the valley had renamed them. It was her life's quiet work, this ledger: a record of everyone in Vell who had come from somewhere else, going back past the founding, past the Regent's great-grandmother, past the point where the records stopped pretending to be complete.

She ran her finger down the eastern arrivals. There. Three generations back. *Thackeray — original name recorded variously as Ostravan dialect; see attached.*

Attached was a scrap of paper in her predecessor's handwriting, and on it, phonetically rendered, a name-sound. Hedda had read it a hundred times without it meaning anything. A dead kingdom's name, the note said. A place erased so thoroughly that even the erasure had been erased. Refugees had come through the eastern passes in a single terrible winter, and the valley had taken them in because the valley took everyone—that was its founding law, its founding *nature*: no magic could cross into Vell, and therefore nothing that hunted magic could follow. The refugees came because they were hunted. The valley saved them by making them safe and small.

And now salt was growing in her well.

Hedda poured herself a cup of nettle tea she didn't want and made herself think it through, step by step, the way she'd think through any outbreak. First principle: symptoms cluster around a cause. Second principle: find the earliest symptom.

She found it by asking her own memory backward. The blue candles, last night. The well, this morning. But before that—she cast further, and there, a fortnight back: Maren Cobb swearing her goats had refused to enter the eastern pasture, standing at its edge screaming. Everyone had laughed. Goats were idiots.

And before that, the child.

The whole valley had seen the child by now, or heard of it—the mute little thing found at the taster's door, caked white, half-dead, taken in by Paloma Thackeray of all people. Quiet Paloma, careful Paloma, the woman whose body swallowed poison for a living and whose face gave away nothing, ever. Neighbors left food at the cottage door now. Small kindnesses. The valley loved a stray.

Hedda had met the child once, briefly, on the lane. Issel, the neighbors called it—or that was the sound the child made when asked. A strange little sound, water-shaped. Hedda had knelt down, offered a sprig of dried mint, the universal currency between herbalists and children, and the child had looked at her with eyes far too calm for its age, and opened its mouth, and produced two syllables.

At the time she'd thought it was babble. Now, sitting in her chair with her ledger open and her tea going cold, Hedda heard those two syllables again in perfect recall, and recognized the accent.

Not the child's accent. Her mother's. The night-grinding rhythm, the soft consonants, the language that wasn't Vellish and wasn't anything else either—a language from a country that had been rubbed off the maps.

The child had spoken to her. In greeting, or in warning, or simply because children speak to anyone who kneels. And Hedda had smiled and patted its head and gone on with her day, because she hadn't known what she was hearing, and ignorance is only bliss for people who don't keep ledgers.

She put her head in her hands.

* * *

By evening she had done what she always did with unbearable knowledge: she had organized it. On her worktable lay the font sample, the well salt, her ledger open to the eastern page, and a fresh sheet where she'd written, in her cramped apothecary's hand, a list she hated:

*1. Salt blooms inland, in consecrated water and deep groundwater alike.*
*2. Candles burn blue. Livestock refuse ground.*
*3. Onset coincides (within days) with arrival of child speaking extinct tongue.*
*4. Child resides with Thackeray — descendant of eastern refugee line.*
*5. Founding law of Vell: magic cannot cross the border.*

Under item five, after a long pause, she wrote:

*6. Therefore: either the law has changed, or the law is being tested from inside.*

She looked at that sentence for a long time. A valley that repelled magic was like a body that repelled infection—sound, reliable, boring. But every physician knew about the wound that healed over wrong. The seal that looked closed and wasn't. You didn't get infection bursting through healthy skin. You got it at the gate. At the threshold. Where something had already gotten partway in.

The child hadn't climbed the passes like the refugees of legend. Nobody knew how it had arrived. It had simply been found—at a door, caked in salt, unafraid. As if it had been delivered. As if something on the other side of the world had posted it through a crack.

And cracks widened.

Hedda rose, went to her window, and looked east toward the passes, black against the last violet of dusk. Somewhere out there, if half her collected fragments were true, lay the absence of a kingdom. Not ruins—absence. A hole shaped like a country, which was a different and hungrier thing.

She thought of the crystal growing in the font, branching, patient, *deliberate*, and understood suddenly that it wasn't a symptom at all.

It was a signal. Something reaching in. Or something inside reaching out. Either way, the valley's long silence was ending, and Hedda—who had spent her whole life believing silence was safety—found she couldn't decide whether to pray it would stop or pray it wouldn't.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Near midnight she heard hoofbeats on the lane and looked out to see a rider in court colors passing at speed toward the town—Corvane's livery, the spymaster's grey-and-black. Odd hour for a message. Odder still when a second rider followed, and then a third, spaced apart like beads on a wire.

Someone at the palace was awake too. Someone was counting the same signs and arriving at faster conclusions, because that was what spymasters did: they noticed, and then they acted, and what Corvane did with his noticing depended entirely on questions Hedda didn't dare guess at.

She made a decision the way she made all decisions: quickly, practically, and with an eye to which regret she could live with longest.

Before dawn, she packed her satchel—mortarstone, loupe, ledger, a jar of the font-water stoppered tight—and set out through the freezing dark toward the taster's cottage on the eastern lane, walking fast, an old woman's breath smoking ahead of her like incense.

Halfway there, she passed the well at the crossroads. The public one, the old one, the one every traveler used.

She didn't stop. She didn't need to. The moonlight was thin but sufficient, and the bucket-chain was crusted white from lip to handle, and even at twenty paces she could see the glitter of new salt blooming across the stones like frost that had decided to stay.

The valley was waking. And whatever slept inside it was turning over, and opening one eye, and looking—for reasons Hedda could feel in her oldest bones—directly at a cottage where a mute child slept beside a woman who tasted poison for a living.

Hedda quickened her step. Whatever she was going to say when she got there, she had the sudden, terrible feeling that she was already late.
