## 11. Into the Caves

The recipe for *brined river eel with mountain herbs* called for one measure of salt from the deep shelf, two measures of silence, and a walking stick.

Paloma had read the marginalia four times before she understood what she was reading. The old script looped and coiled like water finding its way downhill, and her grandmother's hand had grown shakier toward the bottom of the page, as though whoever wrote those instructions had been hurrying, or afraid, or both. *Take the low road past the second fall of stone. Do not bring what you love. Bring only what is yours.*

She didn't know yet whether Issel was hers. She only knew that leaving the child behind felt like leaving a hand in a closing door.

"You're sure about this?" Hedda had asked, pressing a bundle into Paloma's arms at dawn: dried meat, a stub of candle, a flask of willow-bark tea, and a small knife with a bone handle worn smooth by other hands. "The caves under Vell go deeper than anyone maps. Miners won't take the lower galleries. They say the salt sings down there."

"They say a lot of things."

"They said your grandmother was mad too." Hedda's eyes were red-rimmed; she hadn't slept, or hadn't wanted to. "She came to me once, years ago, asking after certain herbs. Ones that keep the mind clear in the dark. I thought she meant for cooking." Hedda's mouth twisted. "Everything in this valley is for cooking, isn't it? Until it isn't."

Paloma tucked the bundle under her arm. Issel stood beside her on Hedda's doorstep, scrubbed clean for the first time since arriving, wearing clothes lent by the ferryman's wife—too big, sleeves rolled three times over small wrists. The child looked up at the grey morning sky the way other children looked at festival fires.

"Stay close," Paloma said, in Vellan, then again in the other tongue, the words still strange in her mouth, still tasting faintly of lullaby.

Issel answered in the same breath, both languages braided together, and took her hand without asking permission.

* * *

The entrance wasn't hidden so much as unremarked. Every map of Vell's underside marked the old salt works as exhausted, played out, a hole in the ground that had given everything it had. The miners' path descended past rotting support beams and the ghost-smell of pick work, and then kept going where no beam had ever been set—because no miner had ever bothered to cut this far.

Paloma counted falls of stone the way the recipe instructed. One: where the ceiling dipped and the dark began in earnest. Two: where an ancient collapse had left a slope of rubble they climbed hand over hand, Issel light and sure above her, moving through the blackness as though it were a familiar room.

"How do you see?" Paloma whispered, and then realized the child probably couldn't answer in words she'd understand. But Issel paused, turned back, and pressed Paloma's palm flat against the cave wall. The salt there was warm.

Not body-warm. Warm like bread held too long in the oven. Warm like something alive underneath, breathing slow.

"Oh," Paloma said quietly.

Issel nodded once, satisfied, and led on.

Her grandmother's instructions grew stranger as they descended. *Where the walls weep, turn your shoulder to them. Where the floor shines, walk soft. When you hear the third voice, you are nearly home.* Paloma repeated them under her breath like a rosary, translating each line twice—from Ostravan script into meaning, from meaning into the geography of the dark—and with every step she felt the strange double sensation she'd carried since the child arrived: that she was remembering this place rather than discovering it.

They heard the first voice an hour down. Water, falling somewhere out of sight, hitting stone with a sound almost like speech. The second voice was the wind, drawn through some high fissure, moaning across the openings in the rock so that it rose and fell in long syllables. Paloma's neck prickled. She had tasted poisons that made less noise than this silence did.

The third voice came when they stopped to drink.

It was neither water nor wind. It came from below and ahead and everywhere at once—a low, sustained tone, barely audible, more felt in the teeth than heard in the ears. Issel went rigid against her side, then, horribly, began to smile.

"What is it?" Paloma asked. "What's down there?"

Issel touched their own chest, then pointed down. Then—the gesture that stopped Paloma's heart—the child pointed at *her*, and made a shape with both hands like a door opening.

"I know," Paloma said. Her voice came out rough. "I know. That's why we're here."

* * *

The gallery opened all at once, the way a cathedral opens when you come through the wrong door expecting a corridor.

Paloma lifted the candle and could not find the ceiling. The flame showed walls of salt rising sheer on either side, translucent as frozen honey, veined with bands of white and rust and a blue so pale it might have been memory. The floor underfoot was smooth—not worn smooth, but *finished*, polished by intention, and carved into it ran lines of script wider than her arm, spiraling inward toward the center of the chamber the way water spirals toward a drain.

The humming was louder here. It came from the far wall—or from what she had taken, in the candlelight, for a wall.

It was a slab of salt taller than three men, standing free of the cavern face, and around its edges the rock had been carved with writing older than any she'd seen in her grandmother's book—older in its bones, angular where the book's script curved, as if this had been cut when the language was young and the world agreed about fewer things. The letters caught the candlelight and threw it back doubled, tripled, until the whole wall of text seemed lit from within.

"It's beautiful," Paloma breathed, and then hated herself for saying it, because beauty had never once in her life preceded anything safe.

Issel let go of her hand.

The child walked forward across the carved floor with the unhurried certainty of someone crossing their own courtyard, and Paloma's whole body screamed to reach out, to pull them back, to stand between this child and whatever waited in that glowing stone—but her feet would not move. Later she would wonder whether the room itself held her. In the moment she could only watch.

Issel raised both hands and laid them flat against the door.

The hum changed key.

It rose—a full note, then another, climbing like a kettle toward song—and light bloomed outward from beneath the child's palms in slow veins of gold, spreading through the salt slab the way dawn spreads through a window. For one suspended moment Paloma saw *through* it: shapes beyond, depth beyond, a warmth on the other side that had nothing to do with fire. Something on the far side of the door pressed close, the way a face presses to the far side of frosted glass.

Then the light guttered. The hum sank back to its long low note. The door became a wall again, and Issel stumbled backward into Paloma's arms, shaking, cold as a pulled-from-water fish, eyes enormous.

"It knows you," Paloma whispered, holding on. "It knows you're the key."

Issel shook their head. Pressed a small hand against Paloma's sternum, directly over her heart. Then pointed back at the door.

"No." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "No, you've got it backwards. I'm nobody. I'm the taster. I taste the Queen's soup and I don't die. That's all I am."

But even as she said it, her hand had risen—she hadn't told it to—and her fingertips hovered a finger's width from the carved surface, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off it like breath, close enough that the humming shifted again, subtly, leaning toward her the way a plant leans toward light.

She snatched her hand back.

Around the edges of the great slab, the old angular script seemed to watch her. And threaded through it—she saw now, seeing it properly for the first time—ran newer carvings, shallower, in her grandmother's looping hand. A line added beneath the ancient text like a footnote written centuries later:

*If you are reading this, the door has waited long enough.*

Below it, a phrase in the old tongue that Paloma's mouth shaped before her mind could stop it—a key-phrase, she understood somehow, a word meant to be spoken aloud by someone carrying Ostrava in their blood:

*Sel-va-resh. I am the meal, and I am come home.*

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

And somewhere above them—far above, up through two falls of stone and a hundred fathoms of ordinary valley earth—Paloma heard, or imagined she heard, a sound she would spend the rest of her life trying to forget: voices singing in unison, patient and tuneless, singing the name of the valley slightly, deliberately wrong.
