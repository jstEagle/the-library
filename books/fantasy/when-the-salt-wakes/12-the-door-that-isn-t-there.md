## 12. The Door That Isn't There

The cave is warm the way bread is warm. Issel knows this before anything else. Not cold like the stories said caves should be, not wet like the river-places under Ostrava's walls. Warm. The salt drinks the light and gives it back slow, and everything smells of the sea even though Issel has never seen a sea.

Paloma holds the little flame in her hand — a candle stub from her kitchen, cupped so the air won't steal it. Her face above it is all shadows and one bright eye.

"You're sure about this?" she asks. She has been asking questions like that all day, since they climbed down past the third turning, since the walls began to sing.

Issel nods. Nodding is easy. The words are still locked up tight behind Issel's teeth, all except the small ones, and Paloma doesn't know the small ones yet. But the door doesn't need words. The door needs something else.

Issel takes her sleeve. Two fingers on wool, because touching skin makes the feeling come too fast, and Paloma startles when feelings come too fast. Two fingers on the sleeve, and then walk.

The tunnel opens into a round room. The ceiling is far away and hung with salt teeth, long white ones, and the floor is smooth as a frozen puddle. In the middle of the floor there is nothing at all.

That is the important part. Issel looks hard at the nothing to make sure it is still there.

It is. A circle of empty air, taller than a person, wide as a doorway, standing where no wall stands. Behind it is more cave. Through it is more nothing. It does not shine. It does not hum. It is simply a place where the world forgot to finish itself, and most eyes slide off it like water off a duck's back. Issel watched Paloma look straight through that spot twice already today and see only rock.

But Issel sees it. Issel has always been able to see doors. Mama said so. *You were born looking at exits, little fish.*

Issel lets go of Paloma's sleeve and walks to the edge of the nothing and puts out one hand.

The nothing ripples.

It ripples the way a pond ripples when a stone goes in — rings spreading out from Issel's fingertips, rings of pale gold light that weren't there a moment ago, and now the whole doorway glows soft as a lamp behind cloth. The warmth deepens. Somewhere very far away, or maybe not far away at all, a bell sounds once. Low. Patient. Like something saying *I know you. I have been waiting to know you.*

Paloma makes a sound in her throat. Not a word. Just a sound, small and startled, the sound a person makes when a cat speaks.

"That wasn't there," she whispers. "Issel, there was *nothing* there."

*There still is,* Issel thinks. *And also there isn't.* Words are hard. Instead Issel does what works: reaches back, finds Paloma's wrist, and pulls.

She comes. She is bigger and heavier than Issel by a lot, but she comes the way she always comes when Issel pulls — trusting first, asking after. That is Issel's favorite thing about Paloma, the thing that made Issel follow her home in the first place, all those days ago, caked in salt and too tired to be afraid. Some people ask after. Paloma trusts first. Grandmother would have called that an Ostravan heart in a borrowed body. Issel just calls it *her.*

They stand together at the edge of the glowing nothing.

"Touch it," Paloma says. "Go on, if you're going to — touch it, show me what—"

Issel touches it first, to show it's safe. Fingers into the gold. The gold closes around them like water, cool, then warm, then cool again, tasting faintly of honey at the back of the throat. The bell rings again, closer now, and beneath the bell there are other sounds trying to be heard — voices, many voices, murmuring the way a city murmurs at evening.

Then Issel steps back and looks up at Paloma and points.

*Your turn.*

Paloma stares at the doorway. Then at Issel. Then at her own hand, held up between them like something she's never met before.

"I told you," she says, and her voice has gone thin and careful, the voice she uses with the Queen Regent's servants, the voice that means *I am nobody, please don't look at me.* "I'm not whatever you are. I was born here. My mother was born here. I've never — my grandmother came here, yes, but that was — Issel, magic doesn't work on me, that's my whole *life*, that's all I am. Poison slides right off me and nothing else ever sticks. No spell, no charm, no blessing. The priests tried when I was small. Nothing took. If anything, I'm the opposite of—"

Issel is tired of listening with eyes only. Issel grabs her hand — skin now, no more wool, sorry, sorry — and pushes it forward, palm flat against the light.

The light takes her.

Not all of her. Only the hand, to the wrist. And the hand does not stop at the surface the way hands stop at walls. It goes *in*, up to the wrist, and the glow climbs around it like rising water, and the bell rings a third time, and this time the sound is not patient anymore. This time it is glad. Loud-glad. The kind of glad that shakes dust from the salt teeth overhead, and somewhere deep in the round room a seam of crystal lights up blue-white, then another, then another, running along the walls like lightning learning manners.

Paloma snatches her hand back. She stumbles two steps away and stands there holding her arm like it belongs to somebody else.

Her fingers are glowing. Faintly, fading, but glowing — a pale gold wash sinking into her skin, into the lines of her palm, gone within three breaths. But Issel saw. Issel will always have seen.

"No," Paloma says.

The doorway hums. It hums a note that Issel knows, actually knows, has known since before knowing — the first note of the evening song, the one every household in Ostrava sang when the lamps were lit. *Come in from the dark. Come in to the table.* The note hangs in the warm air, and the round room sings it back in a hundred crystal voices, and Paloma stands in the middle of all that welcome with her arms wrapped around herself like winter got inside her clothes.

"No," she says again, quieter. "That's not — I tasted for the Regent this morning. I drank from a cup that killed a rat in four minutes. That's what I am. That's all I am. A mouth that poison doesn't like." Her laugh comes out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I'm not *magic*. Magic can't even find me. Hedda says I'm a hole in the world."

*You're not a hole,* Issel thinks fiercely. *You're a key. Keys look like holes to people who only know locks.*

Issel does the brave thing, the scary thing, the thing Mama told Issel never never to do with strangers: Issel speaks. Not the valley tongue — the throat won't make those shapes, not yet, maybe not ever — but the old words, the true ones, the ones that fit in the mouth like river stones worn smooth. The greeting. The one you say at a threshold, because thresholds are holy and doors are cousins of thresholds and this door especially deserves its manners.

"*Sel-venna i ora,*" Issel says. *Welcome-home, blood-of-blood.*

The doorway flares.

And Paloma flares with it — because this time the light doesn't wait for her to reach out. This time it leans toward *her*. The whole golden circle tilts in the air, impossible, graceful, the way a flower turns toward sun, and every ring of light stretches toward Paloma Almeida like hands reaching for someone they've missed, and the murmur behind the door rises and resolves, for one clear moment, into actual voices. Actual words. Old words.

Issel hears them say her name.

Not *Paloma*. The other name. The name underneath, the root-name, the one that got buried on the road out of a burning country and planted in valley soil and covered over with a new spelling until everyone forgot there had ever been anything growing there. The voices say it the old way, the full way, and the sound of it fills the round room like the smell of bread filling a house.

Paloma hears it too. Issel watches her hear it — watches her go absolutely still, the stillness of a deer that has caught a scent on the wind, watches her lips move, shaping syllables without permission, the same way her mouth shaped Ostravan answers before her mind consented, that first morning at the cottage door.

"That's—" she starts. Stops. Starts again. "My grandmother used to call me that. When she was half-asleep. I thought it was nonsense. I thought it was just — an old woman's nonsense, a sound she liked." Her voice drops to almost nothing. "It's a name. It's *my* name."

The voices behind the door keep murmuring. Welcoming. Counting, almost — Issel can feel it, the way you can feel a storm coming in your teeth. They are counting their own. The ones who left. The ones who survived. The ones who carried the language out in lullabies and recipe books and marginalia, hidden in plain sight, folded into soup and song and a taster's careful invisible life.

*One of ours,* the murmur says. *One of ours came back. One of ours grew up and did not know and came back anyway.*

Paloma sits down. Right there on the smooth salt floor, candle guttering beside her, skirts pooling, hands limp in her lap. She sits the way people sit when the ground stops being trustworthy — not choosing to sit, exactly, more agreeing to what the world insists upon.

"My whole life," she says, to the candle, to Issel, to nobody. "Do you understand what my whole life has been? Tasting other people's food so they don't die. Standing behind chairs. Being useful and invisible. I thought — I thought the immunity was just luck. A quirk. Something that happened to me." She looks up, and her eyes are wet and furious and shining all at once. "It isn't a quirk. It's *inheritance*. Everything about me that didn't fit — the poison sliding off, the words coming out of my mouth in a dead language, the lullabies, the *recipes* — it was never random. I've been carrying Ostrava around inside me my entire life and I didn't know. I didn't *know*."

Issel crouches down next to her. Close enough to share warmth. Issel knows about not-knowing. Issel spent a whole year not-knowing, back when the Grey Choir was still just a story grandfathers told to make children come indoors, back when the borders of Ostrava were still drawn on maps that hadn't burned yet.

Issel puts one small hand on top of Paloma's big glowing-faded hand.

*Now you know,* Issel thinks as hard as thinking can go. *Now you know, and the door knows, and knowing can't be put back.*

Paloma turns her hand over and holds Issel's fingers. Her grip is shaking. So is her breath. But she doesn't let go.

"Why me?" she asks, and it's not really a question, it's grief wearing a question's clothes. "Why did it pick me? There must have been others. Other grandchildren, other families, other names changed at the border. Why is it my hand that fits?"

Issel doesn't have the words for the answer. But the doorway does.

The bell rings a fourth time — and this ringing is different. Lower. Heavier. Underneath it, for the first time, there is a strain, a thinness, like a rope bearing weight near its breaking point. The gold light flickers. Once, twice. The welcoming murmur falters, and in the faltering Issel hears the other thing, the thing that chased Issel across a dying country and through a closing pass and down into a valley where magic cannot follow:

Silence. The Choir's silence. The silence where color used to be.

The light steadies. The bell finishes. But the message is plain as bread: the door is weak. The door is *old*. The door has been holding itself shut with both hands for a very long time, waiting, counting, and now it has found what it was counting for, and it does not have much season left to wait.

Issel points at the doorway. Then at Paloma. Then presses both palms together and pulls them slowly apart — the sign Mama made when talking about how much time was left, the widening gap, the shrinking gap.

Paloma follows every motion. Issel can see her following, see the taster's mind working behind her eyes, the mind that counts grains and clocks heartbeats and notices when a dish is one part in a hundred wrong.

"The solstice," she says quietly. "Whatever this is — whatever it's waiting for — it's waiting for the solstice. Isn't it?"

Issel nods.

"And if it misses the solstice—"

Issel makes the sign for *closed*. Hands flat, pressed together, no gap at all. Forever-gap.

Paloma looks at the door for a long time. The gold light lies gentle across her face, and Issel watches something happen in her expression — something settling, something being decided in a room inside her that Issel cannot see. The loneliness that lives behind her competence, the quiet one, the one she hides even from herself: Issel watches it meet its opposite. For the first time since Issel has known her, Paloma Almeida looks like a person standing exactly where she belongs.

"One of its last," she says softly. Testing the words. Turning them over like stones. "Hedda said refugees came here from everywhere magic wanted to destroy. I always thought we were the lucky ones who got out clean." She laughs, and this time the crack in the middle of it sounds almost like joy. "We weren't out. We were *seed*. Buried seed. And now the salt wakes up and — Issel. Issel, listen to me."

Issel listens. Issel would always listen.

"If this door leads where I think it leads — if your people are on the other side of it, waiting — then I'm not just going to open it." Paloma stands. She picks up the candle, and the flame steadies in her grip, and the glow of the doorway catches in her eyes until both eyes are bright. "I'm going to hold it open with my own two hands if I have to. Nobody else gets erased while I'm standing here. Nobody. I spent my whole life swallowing death for other people's tables." She looks at the humming gold, and her chin comes up, and she says the next part in two languages at once, half valley-tongue and half the old sweet stones of Ostravan, and both halves mean the same thing:

"Not this time."

Behind the door, the city murmurs. Behind the murmuring, far away, something silent begins to sing.

* * *

They climb back up through the singing tunnels as the candle burns low, and it is only at the last turning, where grey daylight leaks down through the crack in the world, that Paloma stops suddenly and goes very still.

"Issel," she says. Her voice has changed. It has the flat, quick quality Issel has learned means danger-not-here-but-close. "Someone's been in the caves. Look."

On the smooth salt floor, pressed into the white crust, there are boot prints. Soldier's boots, iron-shod, walking in and walking out. And beside them, smaller and stranger: a line of bare footprints that end in the middle of the round room's entrance and do not continue — footprints that simply stop, as if the walker stepped off the edge of the world.

Or as if the walker was taken.

Paloma kneels and touches the print lightly, the way she touches food she suspects, and Issel sees her count something — depth, age, direction — the taster's arithmetic applied to fear.

"They found the tunnel," she says. "Not the door. Not yet. But Corvane's men don't wander, Issel. Someone sent them." She looks up at the thin blade of daylight, and beyond it, toward the valley, toward the court where tomorrow she must stand behind chairs and swallow poisons and pretend to be nobody at all.

"And I don't think they were looking for you."
