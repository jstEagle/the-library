## 13. Winter Court

The summons came at dawn, written in ink the color of dried blood.

*The Lady Regent requires her taster. Come before the bells.*

Paloma read it twice by candlelight, Issel asleep in the trundle bed she'd dragged from Hedda's storeroom, salt-pale and small beneath a quilt that had been her grandmother's. The child breathed in slow, even pulls, and each exhale carried the faintest scent of the sea — though Vell had never seen the sea, and neither had Issel's skin known anything but valley water for weeks now. Some smells lived deeper than skin.

*Come before the bells.* Not *at your convenience*. Not even the customary courtesy of a time. Just the command itself, stripped bare, which meant Corvane had drafted it himself. The Regent's own hand always added a flourish, a small kindness, as if she remembered that tasters were people. Corvane's hand added nothing. Corvane's hand was all function, like a knife laid on a table.

She dressed in the dark, braided her hair tight against the cold, and stood a moment over the bed. Issel's fingers were curled around a wooden spoon — one of Paloma's, taken without asking, carried everywhere like a talisman. In the grey light the child looked carved from chalk, from the walls of the caves Paloma could not stop dreaming about. The wall that hummed. The light her hand had passed through as if it were morning mist.

*You are not merely descended from Ostrava,* the door had said, in the wordless way doors say things. *You are one of its last.*

She touched Issel's hair once, lightly, and left.

---

Vell's castle in winter smelled of resin and roasting chestnuts and, underneath it all, of stone keeping its own counsel. Solstice preparations had transformed the great hall overnight: garlands of juniper and dried orange strung along the rafters, braziers standing sentinel every ten paces, and workmen on ladders hauling up the enormous yule log that would burn from solstice night until the new year kindled. Servants swarmed with brooms and beeswax and armfuls of holly, and through it all moved the Regent's chamberlain calling out instructions in a voice pitched to carry over armies.

Paloma slipped into the current of servants the way she slipped into everything: quietly, usefully, unremarkable. That had been the discipline of her whole life. A taster who drew attention died of it eventually; there was no faster route to a poisoned cup than being noticed by someone with something to hide. She had made herself furniture. She had made herself weather.

But today the hall's noise bent around her strangely. She caught a kitchen girl staring and looking away. She heard her own name pass between two grooms in the stable yard — not *the taster*, but *Paloma Thackeray*, spoken whole, like an item on an inventory.

Corvane had been asking questions. Of course he had. He'd given her three days to surrender the child — no, that was coming, hadn't happened yet — but his men had already been to her lane, she was sure of it. Mrs. Osk the baker had left bread at her door two mornings running with a look on her face like a woman carrying a secret too heavy for her arms. Old Tam at the ferry had refused her coin. Kindness, all of it, and kindness that visible was its own kind of warning: the neighborhood knew she was in trouble, and the neighborhood was choosing sides.

She took her station near the high table's service door, where the tasting board waited under linen. Her hands did what her hands always did: arranged the spoons, the little silver dishes, the napkins warmed against her cheek so the Regent's first bite wouldn't be cold. Ritual. Ritual was armor. She had survived eleven years of this court by making each day identical to the last, and today, of all days, she needed that sameness like a drowning swimmer needs a plank.

The Regent arrived mid-morning.

Lady Regent Sarelle was not yet forty and had governed Vell for nine of those years, since her husband the King-Regent's riding accident, and she wore widowhood the way other women wore jewels — visibly, deliberately, a black gown heavy with jet beads that clicked when she turned her head. She had been beautiful once, people said, before grief planed her down to essentials. What remained was all edge and economy. She sat, she gestured, the tasting began.

Paloma worked the board. Quail in juniper glaze — clean, faintly sweet, the fat unctuous and untainted. Spiced wine mulled with cloves — clean, though the clove was strong enough to mask most things, and she said so, and the cup-bearer scowled and remade it thinner. Honeycakes from the solstice baking — clean, dense, good. Her tongue catalogued each flavor with the flat professionalism of long practice, and underneath the professionalism her mind ran its own darker arithmetic: *three days*, or however many remained now, and somewhere in the city a child who spoke a dead language slept clutching a spoon, and somewhere beyond the valley's borders a thing called the Grey Choir sang the valley's name wrong on purpose.

"Thackeray."

The Regent's voice, directly addressing her, was rare enough that the whole service passage went still. Paloma bowed.

"Your Majesty."

"The quail. Describe it."

A test? A whim? With Sarelle you never knew, and knowing the difference was survival. "Clean fat, my lady. Juniper forward, the bird basted late — the glaze hasn't scorched. No bitterness at the finish. Nothing beneath."

"Nothing beneath," the Regent repeated softly, and for a moment her eyes held Paloma's with an expression Paloma couldn't file anywhere — not suspicion exactly, not kindness. Something older and wearier than either. Then the moment passed, and the Regent waved her off, and the hall resumed its roar.

It wasn't until the noon bell that she understood what the morning had actually been: a gathering. Everyone who mattered was here. And Corvane had requested an audience.

---

She learned it because tasters hear everything; that is the second half of their trade, though no one pays them for it. Poison enters a court by mouth, and mouths talk. Standing behind the screen by the service door with the remains of the tasting board, Paloma listened to the Regent's privy council assemble in the map room adjoining the hall — she could see them in fragments through the jamb's gap, could hear the scrape of chairs, the clink of the Regent pouring her own wine because she trusted no cupbearer more than necessary.

And then Corvane's voice, smooth as poured oil, filling the room.

"Your Majesty. Before we discuss the solstice guard rotations, I must report on a matter of border integrity."

Border integrity. Paloma's stomach dropped through the floor and kept falling.

"You have my attention," the Regent said.

"For some weeks I've been receiving reports. Small ones. The kind that seem like nothing, singly." A pause, measured, theatrical — Corvane never wasted a pause. "Salt water in a well three miles from any coast. Salt crust on the font at the temple of Saint Vered, which has held rainwater for two hundred years. Livestock refusing pasture along the eastern marches. And four days ago, a shepherd on the ridge above the salt caves swore he heard singing underground. Singing, Your Majesty. In harmonies no throat should make."

Silence in the map room. Someone shifted papers.

"The valley was founded on a covenant," Corvane continued, softer now, drawing them in — he was good, she'd give him that, he was very good. "Our ancestors came here because this place rejects magic. Every refugee charter, every founding document, says the same: within Vell's borders, the uncanny cannot follow. That covenant has held for nine generations. It is why we are safe, why we are *dull*, if you forgive me, Your Majesty — dullness is our fortress. And now the fortress is sweating salt."

"And your theory?" the Regent said.

"A leak. Something inside the valley is thinning the seal. Magic doesn't seep through walls, Majesty — it comes through doors. And doors open from both sides." Another pause. Paloma realized she had gone rigid, one hand pressed flat against the cool stone of the screen. "I've traced the reports geographically. The well, the font, the restless flocks — they form a pattern. A gradient. All of it strongest near a single point." Papers rustled. "The low town. Tanner's Lane. The cottage of one Paloma Thackeray, royal food-taster, whose household recently increased by one member. A child. Unregistered. Unnamed in any parish roll. Found — or arrived — none can say which."

The silence that followed had texture. Paloma could feel it through the stone, the way you feel a storm change pressure in your teeth.

"A taster's family," someone said — Lord Aldery, she thought, the treasury man, his voice carrying the particular sneer of a man who had never tasted risk. "Come now, Corvane. Kitchen staff don't crack continental seals."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Kitchen staff don't. But families do. Families carry things. Blood carries things. Your Majesty, I have spent two months on the archives of the founding, and I'll tell you what the cheerful histories omit: the valley's first settlers didn't merely flee *trouble*. They fled specific troubles. Specific lands. Some of those lands are not simply far away." His voice dropped, and even through stone Paloma leaned toward it. "Some of those lands are *gone*. Not conquered. Not ruined. Erased. Removed from maps, from records, from memory — and no war does that. Wars leave ruins and grudges. Only one kind of enemy leaves nothing."

"You speak in riddles," the Regent said.

"I speak in the shape of a hole, Majesty. There is a shape where a kingdom used to be. Our founders' rolls list refugee families by origin, and for several entries the origin is scraped out — physically scraped, with a knife, centuries ago, by someone terrified. One of those scraped entries belongs to a family whose name was later changed." Another paper sound, delicate as a moth. "*Thackeray.*"

Paloma stopped breathing.

She had known, of course. Since the caves, since the ledger Hedda had shown her — *Almeida, altered to Thackeray* — she had known what she was. But hearing it in Corvane's voice, in the Regent's map room, wrapped in his reasonable, patient, terrible logic — hearing her grandmother's borrowed name pronounced as evidence — was different. It was the difference between knowing a knife existed and feeling it slide between your ribs.

Lord Aldery laughed, but it was a thin laugh. "You're saying the food-taster is a sorceress?"

"I'm saying," Corvane said, and now his voice lost all its silk, became simply, horribly clear, "that I tested her."

Paloma's blood went to ice water.

"Ten days ago, the dish of glazed eel served at the Ambassador's dinner. Everyone ate; no one sickened. But the eel carried hemlock extract, Majesty. My preparation. Mild enough to pass as indigestion in a healthy adult — except in one person at that table, who showed *no reaction whatsoever*. None. Not a flush, not a tremor, not the least watering of the eye. I've poisoned enough men to know what immunity looks like." A pause. "Natural tolerance fails gradually, with symptoms. Hers failed completely, instantly, as if the poison had looked at her and decided against it. As if something in her blood recognized the assault and declined the invitation."

"That's not possible," Aldery said. "The tasters are screened—"

"The tasters are screened for what poison does to ordinary bodies," Corvane said. "Majesty, I don't claim to understand what she is. I claim only what I observed, and what I observe now: salt rising in wells around her house. A nameless child speaking a language that predates our charter. And a valley seal that has held for nine generations beginning to fray *this winter*, *this season*, within weeks of that child crossing our border." His voice gentled into something almost tender, which was worst of all. "I don't believe Paloma Thackeray is our enemy. I believe she is a woman who inherited a debt she never knew existed, holding a key she never asked for. But keys attract lockmakers, Majesty. And whatever locked that door on the far side has been patient for a very long time."

Through the gap in the screen, Paloma watched the Regent set down her cup. Watched her stand, walk to the map on the wall — the old map of the valley with its borders drawn in faded gold — and lay one finger on the eastern edge, where the salt caves were marked only as blankness.

"My grandmother," the Regent said at last, and her voice had changed, become quieter, more distant, "came to Vell from across the mountains. She never spoke of where. When I asked, as a girl, she told me the road behind us exists only for people walking away from it." She turned. "What do you recommend, Master Corvane?"

"Discretion, first. The solstice approaches; panic at court serves no one. Second — bring the taster in. Gently. She has served loyally for eleven years; extend her the courtesy of loyalty returned. Ask her about the child. Ask her what her family carried across the mountains." A beat. "And if she lies, Majesty — then we will know what we are dealing with."

"And the child?"

Another silence, longer. When Corvane answered, Paloma heard him smile, and hated him for how human it sounded.

"The child, Majesty, may be the only innocent in this story. Which is precisely why whoever stands behind that door chose one to send. Nobody suspects a mute six-year-old of being a weapon of state." Softly: "Nobody but me."

---

Paloma did not remember walking out of the service passage. She remembered pieces: the yule log settling in its cradle with a groan; a servant girl asking if she was well and hearing herself answer *fine, thank you* in a voice that belonged to someone else; the courtyard's cold air hitting her face like a slap of well water.

They knew. Not everything — Corvane dealt in shapes and gradients, not certainties — but they knew the shape of her. Taster. Immune. Descendant. Keeper of a nameless child on a lane where wells ran salty. Three days, he'd told her, and the clock had started days ago.

She walked home fast, breath smoking, past the garland-draped shops, past children chalking suns and stars on the paving stones for solstice luck. The city was preparing for the longest night the way it always had: candles stockpiled, ovens heating, doors barred against the dark. They had no idea the dark was already inside, wearing a friendly face, sitting at the Regent's table, recommending *gentleness*.

Tanner's Lane. Her gate. And there, leaning against her fence post with his arms crossed and his breath steaming, waiting for her with the patience of a man who had all the time in the world and wanted her to know it —

Bram, the ferryman.

His face, when she got close enough to read it, was grey as the salt on Hedda's well.

"They came to the river this morning," he said, low, before she could speak. "Corvane's men. Asking after traffic on the underground water. Asking who'd ferried anyone strange this season." He pushed off from the post and stepped close, close enough that his voice barely crossed the space between them. "Paloma. Whatever you're planning — the boat leaves tonight. Whether you're on it or not."

Behind her cottage window, faint and warm, a lamp was already lit.

Issel was awake.
