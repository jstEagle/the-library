## 14. The Exile's Ledger

Hedda had known the moment the salt bloomed in her well that someone would come asking questions eventually. She hadn't expected it to be the taster.

Paloma Almeida stood in her doorway at dusk, holding a sleeping child wrapped in Hedda's own spare blanket—the one Paloma must have taken from the basket by the door last week, when she'd come for willow bark and left with more questions than herbs. The child's cheek was smudged white. Salt, still. Days after arriving in the valley, and the salt hadn't washed out of that small skin so much as settled into it, like it belonged there.

"You'd better come in," Hedda said, before Paloma said anything at all. "And you'd better say nothing on my doorstep. Walls have been listening lately."

Paloma's eyebrows went up, but she obeyed. She carried the child inside with the careful economy of a woman used to handling things that could kill her, and set him down on the pallet by the fire without waking him.

The cottage smelled of dried yarrow and lamp oil and forty years of steeped things. Hedda shut the door, shot the bolt—a thing she hadn't done since the Regent's mother was alive—and put the kettle on because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

"His name is Issel," Paloma said quietly.

Hedda's hand stopped on the kettle handle. She made herself finish the motion. Poured. Two cups, though her throat had closed to the width of a reed.

"Where did you hear that name?"

"He told me. In his sleep, then awake." Paloma sat across from her, spine straight, hands folded—court-trained even now, even exhausted. "That's not all. Hedda, I understood him. Before I knew I understood him, my mouth was answering. The language he speaks—it's the one my grandmother sang to me. I thought they were just nonsense syllables. Lullaby sounds." Her voice did something careful around the word *nonsense*, like setting down a dish she suspected might already be cracked. "Then I found her recipe book. There's writing in the margins. Same script."

Hedda looked at the fire for a long time.

She was seventy-one years old and had lived in Vell longer than most of its stones had been mortared, if you asked anyone, and no one asked, because asking was not something people in Vell did about each other's beginnings. That was the first law of the valley, unwritten and iron: *no one came from anywhere.* You were of Vell or you were of Vell's future. The past was a border crossing nobody acknowledged making.

But Hedda remembered the crossing. She'd been four years old. Her mother had carried her through a pass that didn't exist afterward, weeping without sound, and the last thing Hedda saw over her mother's shoulder was a horizon burning in a color that had no name—and then the valley, green and dim and magicless, and her mother kneeling in the dirt saying *thank you* to nothing, to no one, to the simple absence of miracle.

"I'm going to show you something," Hedda said. "And after I show you, you can't unsee it. And neither can I, which matters more, because I've spent fifty years keeping this where seeing couldn't find me."

"Hedda—"

"The child's asleep. The fire's low. There'll be no better night for it." She rose, knees cracking, and went to the pantry.

Not the herb shelves. Behind them. She moved jars of comfrey and nettle aside and pressed the third board from the bottom, the one that gave with the complaint of old wood, and pulled out a box wrapped in oilcloth. Inside the oilcloth: leather. Inside the leather: a book.

Not a book, really. A ledger. Fat as a brick, bound in hide gone dark with handling, its spine stitched twice where it had broken once. She set it on the table between the teacups and left her hand on the cover a moment, feeling the weight of it the way you feel a scar.

"My father made this," she said. "Or began it. Others finished it."

She opened it.

The pages were dense with columns—names, dates, crossings. Some entries in a careful merchant's hand, some scrawled, some in scripts Paloma would recognize, though the newest ones were written in plain Vell letters to be safe. Each family got a line. Each line had three parts: the name they'd been, the name they took, and the road they'd died on or lived down, whichever came.

Paloma leaned close. Hedda watched her read the way she'd once watched a girl taste for the Queen—noting everything, betraying nothing, until the moment she couldn't.

"There are hundreds," Paloma breathed.

"Six hundred and eleven families, as of the last entry. My own hand wrote the last nine." Hedda turned pages slowly. "Refugees. That's what the valley has always been, whatever the founding stories say about farmers and fortune. Magic can't cross the valley's borders—can't live here, can't follow here. So for as long as there have been wars fought with wonders, there have been people running from them. They come to Vell because Vell is blind to everything they were. They arrive and they become *of Vell* and their children never know otherwise." She tapped the page. "Except somebody always writes it down. Somebody always keeps the ledger. That's the joke of it, see? A valley that erases the past, and every generation somebody hides a book in a pantry anyway."

"Why you?"

"Because my mother wouldn't let me forget, even when forgetting would have been kinder." Hedda turned another page, and another, moving backward through decades. "I keep it because grief needs somewhere to sit. That's all a ledger is. Grief with good handwriting."

Paloma reached out and stopped the page with two fingers.

Hedda had known it was coming. She'd known since the doorway, since the name Issel landed in her kitchen like a stone dropped down a well whose bottom she'd spent half a century pretending was shallow. She watched the girl's finger slide down the column and stop, and she watched Paloma's face do the thing faces do when the ground turns out to have been sky all along.

"*Thackeray*," Paloma read.

"That's your grandmother?"

"Maren Thackeray. She raised me." Paloma's finger stayed on the entry. Her lips moved as she read the rest of the line silently, and Hedda watched the silent reading fail her—watched her go back to the beginning of the line and read it aloud instead, slowly, the way you'd handle something hot.

"*Maren. Called Thackeray in Vell. Born Ostrava, of the house of—"*

Paloma stopped.

"Say it," Hedda said gently. "It won't bite. It's only a name."

"*Vasht-irien*," Paloma said, and her accent was perfect, and her eyes went wide at her own mouth, at the sounds arriving from somewhere older than her memory. "That's—that's not possible. I've never seen that word. I don't know that word."

"You know it the way a river knows downhill." Hedda poured herself more tea she didn't want, just to occupy the pouring. "Ostravan names got ground down coming through the passes. Vashtirien became Thackeray—there were clerks at the border settlements, in the early years, who shortened everything they couldn't spell. Half the surnames in the eastern villages are Ostravan names with the corners knocked off. Ferren was Ferrindal. Bram's family—"

"Bram? Old Bram the ferryman?"

"Ferrowan, originally. He doesn't know. His grandfather does—or did; he'd be a hundred and twenty now, if his mind held, and minds that hold onto Ostrava tend not to hold onto anything else." Hedda closed her eyes briefly. "There's a reason we don't tell. It isn't shame. It's that remembering is a door, and doors open from both sides, and some of what's outside wants in."

Paloma looked up sharply. "You know what's following him. Following *Issel*."

"I know that a kingdom doesn't get erased from every map and every memory by accident. Erasure takes workers." Hedda kept her voice level. Fifty years of practice, keeping her voice level. "I know that the year I crossed, my mother wouldn't speak above a whisper for a month, and when I asked why, she said the wrong things listen for their names. I know that six hundred and eleven families ran to this valley and the ledger is the only place most of those names survive, and I have spent my whole adult life making sure that stays true."

"And mine?" Paloma's voice had gone thin. Not frightened, exactly. Hollowed. "My grandmother never said a word. She cooked, she sang her nonsense songs, she salted everything within reach of her hands—she salted *bread*, Hedda, who salts bread?—and she never once said she came from anywhere. She let me think I was nobody from nowhere."

"She was protecting you."

"She was lying to me."

"Yes," Hedda said, because lies deserved their plain name. "Both things can be true. Mothers do that. Mine did worse." She reached across the table and turned the ledger toward herself, found the entry again with an old woman's certainty of place. "Read the rest. All of it. You've earned the whole line."

Paloma read. "*Maren, called Thackeray in Vell. Born Ostrava, of the house of Vashtirien. Crossed the winter pass, age nineteen, alone. Last of her household recorded.*" She swallowed. "*Settled east quarter. Kept the bread-salt.*"

"Kept the bread-salt," Paloma repeated blankly.

"It's a phrase that recurs." Hedda's chest ached in the old familiar place, under the ribs, where her mother's silence had lived. "Crossers who meant to keep something of themselves—they marked it in the ledger so someone later might ask. *Kept the songs. Kept the bread-salt. Kept the naming-day.* Little fires banked under ash, waiting for someone to come looking for warmth." She met Paloma's eyes. "Your grandmother banked a fire in you, girl. The language, the recipes, the salt. She couldn't hand it to you openly, so she hid it in your childhood where no clerk could confiscate it. Songs sound like songs. Recipes look like recipes. And now a child walks out of nowhere speaking the tongue, and your mouth answers before your mind consents." She spread her hands. "That's not coincidence. That's inheritance doing what inheritance does. Waiting."

The fire popped. On the pallet, Issel stirred but didn't wake, turning toward the warmth with the boneless trust of the very young, and both women were quiet until he settled again.

"How many others," Paloma finally said. "How many in the valley now. Living."

"Counting you? Eleven bloodlines with Ostrava in them, that I know of. There may be more I don't—some lines married out so far the ledger lost track. But the ones who kept marks, kept fires..." She counted on her fingers, an old habit. "Five. Your line. Bram's. The Ostrey family at the mill—Ostrey, another clipped name. Widow Sarel, though she's near my age and remembers nothing, which may be mercy. And the ferryman's granddaughter, though her mark is just a song she thinks she invented."

"Eleven bloodlines," Paloma whispered. "Out of six hundred and eleven families."

"Valleys swallow. That's what they're for." Hedda shut the ledger but didn't move it, didn't wrap it back up. Some things, once opened, should stay open a while, the way a wound should breathe. "Here's what I need you to understand, Paloma Almeida—Thackeray—Vashtirien. Whichever of your names is listening hardest tonight." She leaned forward. "The child appeared at your door. Out of every door in this valley. And the salt bloomed in my well the same week. And the font at the chapel wept brine on holy water. The valley is reacting, which means the border is thinning, which means whatever erased Ostrava has noticed the thinning too. You're not carrying a stray cat home, girl. You're carrying the end of a story that powerful things paid dearly to bury."

"Then help me carry it."

"I intend to." Hedda sat back. "But first I need to teach you what your grandmother couldn't. The ledger has more than names. It has warnings. Rules the crossers learned with their skin. And rule one—" she tapped the inside cover, where a single line was inked in a dead alphabet above its plain-letter translation, "*the door opens twice: once to flee, once to return, and the second opening costs what the first one cost everyone else*"—

The knock came at the front door.

Three raps. Even. Unhurried. The kind of knocking that belongs to someone with authority and knows it.

Both women froze. On the pallet, Issel's eyes snapped open—wide, black-ringed, fixed on the door—and the child made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than crying.

Hedda blew out the lamp in one motion, the way she'd practiced for fifty years without knowing why.

Through the window, in the last grey light, she saw the figure on her path. Tall. Patient. Wearing the Queen Regent's colors like a second skin.

Corvane. The spymaster. Standing at the door of the valley's best-hidden secret, smiling at the wood as if he could smell the paper through it.

"Herbalist," his voice said, mild as milk. "May I trouble you for something for sleeplessness? It seems half the valley has it lately."

Hedda looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at the ledger.

Under the floorboards, faint as breath, the well water in the yard had begun, very softly, to sing.

* * *

"Coming," Hedda called, and pitched her voice into the register of the mildly annoyed elderly, a performance she'd rehearsed her whole life without an audience.

She put the ledger back behind the comfrey with hands that did not shake—she was proud of that, later, in the way you're proud of small survivals—and slid the shelf home as Paloma gathered the boy against her chest. There was nowhere else to put them. The cottage had one room and a pantry, and the pantry had Hedda in it now, pulling dried chamomile off a hook as if sleeplessness were truly the night's business.

She opened the door on its chain. Corvane stood in the blue dusk with his hood back and his hands visible, which was his way of telling you he'd already decided you weren't a threat, which was his way of lying beautifully.

"Herbalist Hedda. Forgive the hour."

"Forgive nothing. Half the valley can't sleep, you say, and the Regent's spymaster comes to my door for chamomile like any farmer's wife." She unlatched the chain but opened the door barely a hand's width, letting the cold in and nothing else. "What's really keeping you awake, Corvane?"

He smiled. It was a good smile. It had probably opened more doors than keys.

"Salt," he said pleasantly. "Salt keeps me awake. Salt in wells. Salt in fonts. Salt on a child no one delivered and no one claims." His gaze drifted past her shoulder, into the warm dark of the cottage, and Hedda felt the chain's iron go cold against her wrist. "You'd know something about old things, I imagine. An herbalist keeps the valley's oldest memories, they say. Roots and remedies and rumors." His eyes came back to hers. "Have you heard any good rumors, Hedda? About where children come from?"

"Children come from tired mothers and bad luck," Hedda said. "Same as ever. Now take your chamomile and let an old woman bank her fire."

He took the packet. He held it a moment longer than a man holds chamomile.

"Give my regards to whoever's visiting," he said softly, and walked away down the path without hurry, whistling something under his breath—a tune Hedda didn't know, except that some deep cellar part of her did, and it took her three full minutes to understand why.

It was an Ostravan walking song. Her mother had hummed it while packing, the day they fled.

Behind her, in the dark, Paloma whispered: "He knows."

"No," Hedda said, bolting the door, turning back to the pantry, to the ledger, to the long night ahead. "Worse. He suspects everything and knows nothing, which means he'll dig until either he finds it or the finding kills us all." She lit the lamp again. Her hand shook now; the performance was over. "Get the boy ready, girl. We have perhaps two days. Maybe less."

"Ready for what?"

Hedda pulled the ledger back out and laid it open on the table, flat beneath the light, six hundred and eleven names staring up like a congregation.

"To learn rule two," she said. "Before someone teaches it to us the hard way."
