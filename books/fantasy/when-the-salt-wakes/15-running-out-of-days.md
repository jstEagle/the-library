## 15. Running Out of Days

The candle burned down to a stub before I understood the first inscription.

I had copied the wall by lantern-light two nights ago—every character I could reach without a ladder, pressed into my notebook in graphite and guesswork—and now the copies covered my kitchen table like a spread of strange laundry. Issel sat across from me, chin propped on both fists, watching me work the way children watch water come to a boil: with total faith that something would eventually happen.

"It's arithmetic," I said, mostly to myself. "It's all arithmetic."

The recipes had taught me that much. My grandmother had hidden directions inside measurements—a pinch where there should be a handful, three turns of the spoon where any cook would use one—and the cave wall worked the same way, but vaster. Where the recipe book counted spoonfuls, the wall counted years.

There was a sequence near the top that repeated four times, each repetition longer than the last, the way a chorus grows with verses. I had marked it in red. Beside it, smaller figures clustered like minnows—numbers within numbers, a count nested inside a count nested inside a count. It took me until midnight to see what they were counting.

Generations.

Not years. Not seasons. The door opened when a generation turned over—when everyone who had been alive at its last opening was dead or dying. Twenty-five years, perhaps thirty, between breaths. A door that slept through an entire human lifetime and woke for a single night.

"Once," I said aloud. My voice sounded wrong in the small kitchen, too loud, too flat. "It opens once."

Issel tilted their head. They couldn't follow the words but they could follow the tone, and the tone made them sit up straighter. I turned the notebook toward them and tapped the red-marked sequence, then held up one finger.

Issel stared at the finger for a long moment. Then they shook their head slowly—not *no*, exactly. Something more complicated than no. They held up their own hand, fingers splayed, and folded them down one at a time until only the smallest remained, and then they curled that one closed too, gently, the way you'd close a dead bird's eye.

*And then never again.*

I sat back. The chair creaked under me. Outside, the valley was doing what the valley always did on a November night—wind combing the bare orchards, a dog barking itself hoarse two lanes over, nothing magical anywhere, nothing magical ever. That was the whole promise of Vell. Magic cannot follow you here. That was why my grandmother had walked out of a burning country and through whatever border-crossing brought refugees to this valley, carrying nothing but a recipe book and a language she'd teach only in lullabies, whispered, as if even here it might be overheard.

She hadn't been hiding the language from the valley. She'd been keeping it alive long enough to matter.

Because if the wall told the truth—if the door beneath us opened once per generation and then shut for good—then every year mattered. Every year since her crossing had been a year spent from a purse with almost nothing left in it.

I did the arithmetic again, slower, because I wanted it to be wrong.

The repetitions of the chorus-sequence were dates. I was nearly sure of that much. Four openings recorded, each followed by the same trailing mark—a spiral, tight as a snail shell—that I now understood was not decoration. It was a seal. An ending. The last spiral was cut deeper than the others, gouged into the salt by someone working in the dark, maybe, someone in a hurry, and after the final spiral the wall went blank for the height of two men. A silence carved deliberately into stone.

Four openings. Then a deliberate blank. Then—low down, at the height of a kneeling person, at the height of a child—the fresh marks Issel had shown me. Newer. Shallower. Cut recently enough that the edges still crumbled when I touched them.

Someone had come back to add to the ledger. Someone who knew the door might open again.

"When," I whispered, and pulled the notebook close.

Dates in the inscription weren't written as we wrote them—no regnal years, no numbers I recognized—but they were written relationally, the way recipes are written. *After the harvest of the white frost. When the third moon swallows the second.* And one phrase appeared just before each opening date, the same phrase every time, four times, in characters I had already met in my grandmother's margins:

*Sol-sta-va.* Winter-turning. The solstice.

Every opening had fallen on the winter solstice. Of course it had. The longest dark was when a door between worlds would be least noticed; the longest dark was when Ostrava did its business.

And the relational markers—I worked through them twice, three times, checking against Hedda's almanac and the court calendar I kept in my head because a taster must always know what day it is, what feast is coming, what dish will be served to whom—the last opening recorded on the wall, the fourth, the one followed by the gouged spiral—

It had happened seventy-one years ago.

My hands went cold around the pencil. Seventy-one years. My grandmother had crossed into Vell sixty-eight years ago, as a girl of nineteen. She had missed it by three years. She had spent her entire life—marriage, children, widowhood, the slow teaching of lullabies to a granddaughter who didn't know she was being handed an inheritance—three years too late for the last door.

Or so she must have believed. Because the fresh marks, the child-height marks, said otherwise. Said the door was waking early. Said something about the interval had changed.

I looked at Issel. Six or seven years old, caked once in salt, clean now, wearing Bram's daughter's old sweater with the sleeves rolled four times. Sent through alone. Sent because—Hedda guessed, and the guesses were all we had—the Choir was closing in, because the exiles scattered outside Ostrava's borders were running out of places to run, because this generation couldn't wait thirty more years for the door's slow patience.

The door was opening off-schedule. One last time. Out of mercy or desperation or both.

"When?" I asked again, and this time I asked it properly, in the few words of Ostravan I owned. "When-open?"

Issel's face did something complicated. They understood the question—I could see that they understood it—and they didn't want to answer it, and I watched them decide, in real time, whether a grown-up could be trusted with the truth. I knew that calculation. I had performed it my whole life, deciding which of my thoughts were safe to show the court.

Finally they pointed at the ceiling. Then they traced, on the tabletop, a shape in spilled flour: a circle, and a line drawn through it top to bottom.

A wheel turning past its lowest point. The sun standing still and beginning to climb.

The winter solstice. This one. The one the court was already preparing for—the Regent's banners ordered in crimson and gold, the kitchens doubling their bread orders, Corvane's men measuring the great hall for the yule fires. Three weeks away. Perhaps less. I counted on my fingers against the calendar in my head and came up with nineteen days, and then I counted again hoping for a different number, and got nineteen days both times.

Nineteen days.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat at the table while the candle drowned and relit another from its ember, and I made myself look directly at what the arithmetic meant, because looking away from poison has never once saved anyone.

If the door opened on the solstice and shut again—shut for good, shut past the gouged spiral, shut into that deliberate blank space on the wall—then everything on the far side of it ended. Not violently. Worse than violently: quietly. Whatever survived of Ostrava, wherever its last people hid—in stasis behind the door, or scattered in exile like my grandmother had been—would be sealed away beyond all reaching. Forever was not a word I used lightly. I tasted things for a living; I knew the difference between *this will hurt* and *this will kill*. But there was no gentler word for it. The wall had a spiral for it. The spiral meant *closed, finished, never*.

And the survivors outside—the exiles, the diaspora, the ones who had run in every direction like sparks from a dropped brand—my grandmother among them, once—would die as the last of their kind, in foreign valleys, humming lullabies to children who didn't understand them, and the language would go with them, and the name Ostrava would become exactly what the maps said it was: nothing.

Unless someone held the door open long enough for them to come home.

I thought of the child-height marks on the wall. Whoever cut them had knelt in the dark and believed in this. Had believed someone would come and read them.

Then a colder thought arrived, the way the worst thoughts do, politely, without knocking.

Corvane had found Hedda asking questions. Corvane had noticed my absences. Corvane was not a man who collected information for the pleasure of it—he collected it the way the Regent collected debts, to be spent later, at the moment of maximum purchase. If he learned what I had learned—the caves, the wall, the date—he wouldn't need to believe in magic to act. He'd need only to believe that his Regent would want the valley sealed tighter than ever, the anomaly excised, the door bricked and guarded and forgotten. Nineteen days was not much time to be secret.

And there was a worse thought behind that one, waiting its turn like a patient creditor.

The exiles outside. Where were they? How many? How did you gather a scattered people to a door in a foreign valley in nineteen days when most of them had spent lifetimes learning to hide? My grandmother was dead. Who else even remembered the way?

I put my forehead down on the cool wood of the table, beside the flour circle, and let myself feel it for exactly ten breaths—the full weight of it, too large for one woman with an immunity and a recipe book. Then I lifted my head, because ten breaths is all grief gets before it has to start earning its keep.

First: copy the wall completely. Everything above arm's reach included. Bram had ladders; Bram owed my grandmother, though he'd never said so outright—it was in the way he touched his cap when her name came up.

Second: the marginalia. There were pages of my grandmother's book I'd dismissed as nonsense—a page of spirals, a page that read like a guest list, a page that was mostly the word for *salt* repeated in patterns. Nonsense was just code I hadn't cracked yet. She'd known the door would matter someday. She'd written for the person who would need it.

Third—and my stomach clenched around this one the way it clenched around nothing I'd ever tasted—third: Issel needed to tell me more. The child knew things. The child had been sent through knowing things, sent as a message as much as a messenger. And the child couldn't speak—or wouldn't. Every question I asked in Ostravan got answered in gestures and flour-drawings and those enormous patient eyes, and I felt the missing words like a gap in a fence, something dangerous pacing on the other side.

I gathered the child's hands in mine. Small, chapped, salt still ghosting in the creases.

"Issel," I said. "*Talk-to-me. Soon.* Please." I didn't have the Ostravan for please, so I gave it in Vellan, plain and unarmored, and I think plainness translates.

Issel looked at our joined hands for a long time. Then up at me. Then at the window, where the sky was going grey at its lower edge, the false dawn that isn't dawn.

And Issel shuddered—whole body, ears to heels, the shudder of a horse scenting fire—and pulled their hands free and clamped them over their own mouth, and shook their head, hard, once, twice, eyes wide and wet over the small fingers.

*No.* As if speaking were the one thing in the world that must not be done. As if something listened for exactly that.

The fire in the grate settled with a soft collapse. Outside, far off toward the valley's rim, so faint I might have invented it, something that was not wind and was not a dog and was not any sound this valley had ever made seemed very briefly to rise—and stop.

Issel heard it too. I watched the hearing happen in their face.

They took their hands from their mouth. And began, silently, mouthing shapes—words with no voice, words aimed at me with terrible urgency, over and over, until I caught the shape of them, until I shaped them back without sound, until we stood in my kitchen at the end of the world's quietest argument, trading a sentence neither of us dared say aloud:

*They are coming. We are running out of days.*
