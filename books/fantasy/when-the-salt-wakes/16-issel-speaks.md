## 16. Issel Speaks

The soup is orange. Issel knows the word for orange now. Paloma taught it three nights ago, pointing at the fire, at the carrots, at the fox-fur collar of a coat hanging on Hedda's wall. Orange. The word sits in Issel's mouth like a warm stone.

Issel has been collecting words. That is what Paloma does not know. Every day she gives them away like bread crumbs and thinks they scatter, but Issel gathers each one up and carries it inside, in the place where the other words live, the old words, the wet dark words from before.

Tonight the cottage smells of onions and woodsmoke and the salt that never quite leaves Issel's skin no matter how many times Paloma scrubs it in the tin bath. Outside, snow ticks against the window like fingernails. Inside, the fire pops. Paloma hums while she stirs. She does not know she hums it. Issel knows the song better than she does — knows it from a grandmother's kitchen on the other side of everything, where a woman with flour on her wrists sang it to a whole room of children who are not children anymore, if any of them are still anything at all.

Issel opens their mouth.

Nothing comes out. Nothing has come out for forty-one days. Issel counts in the way the elders taught, one finger folded for each dawn: forty-one folds, both hands full twice over, one hand almost full again. On the first morning after the crossing, Issel had opened their mouth to call for Mama and the sound had gone somewhere else entirely, had poured out of them like water through sand, and what was left was this silence, this thick white silence, salt-packed, door-shaped.

But tonight something is different. Tonight the words are pushing.

Paloma sets the bowl down. "Careful, it's hot." Her voice is low the way it always is, careful the way everything about her is careful, a woman made of small steps and checked spoons. She has a spoon-scar on her thumb. Issel has memorized it. When Issel is frightened at night, in the dark behind the curtain where the pallet is, Issel touches their own thumb and pretends there is a scar there too, and it helps. A little. Not enough.

Issel eats. The soup is good. Most food here is good, though it is all wrong in small ways — the bread is too soft, the salt goes in the wrong place, at the end instead of the beginning, which is backwards and makes Issel's chest ache every single meal because salt comes *first*, salt is the greeting, salt is how you tell the house you have arrived. Here nobody greets the house. Here the houses just get lived in without being asked.

Paloma sits across from Issel with her own bowl. Steam climbs between them.

And Issel looks at her face — this woman who is not family but whose lullaby *is* family, whose grandmother sang the same syllables to different children across an ocean of erased country — and the pressure behind Issel's teeth becomes unbearable, becomes a flood knocking at a door, becomes exactly the thing Issel crossed the whole world to deliver, wrapped in a body too small to carry it.

"Paloma."

The word falls out of Issel's mouth like a dropped plate.

Paloma's spoon stops halfway to her lips. She doesn't move. She doesn't breathe. In the fire, a log shifts and sends up a fist of sparks.

Issel's throat shakes. It hurts. Speaking after forty-one days of silence is like walking on a leg that fell asleep — everything pins and stings and floods back at once. But the word is out. The first word. And once a door cracks, Issel knows — Issel *knows* — what happens next. Doors crack and then doors break.

"Paloma," Issel says again, just to feel it, just to prove the first time wasn't a dream. Their own voice sounds strange, smaller than they remember, rusted. "I can— I am—"

English. They are speaking English. The valley language. The language Issel learned in fragments during the long running years, from smugglers and ferry-people and a one-eyed woman in a mountain pass who traded bread for songs. Enough to survive. Not enough to say what must be said.

So Issel lets go of English and speaks the true tongue, the deep tongue, grandmother-tongue, Ostrava-tongue, the language that officially does not exist because the people who unmade it made very sure.

"*Nena. Nena, listen.*"

Paloma drops her spoon.

* * *

Later, Issel will not be able to say how long the telling takes. An hour. Three. All night. Time goes strange when you finally open the thing you sealed.

They move to the hearth rug. Paloma wraps a blanket around Issel's shoulders and holds Issel's hands in both of hers, and her hands are trembling, which surprises Issel, because Paloma always seems like someone nothing could surprise. But her hands shake and her eyes are wet and she says, over and over, in the old tongue, "*Go on. Go on. I'm listening,*" and the words are slightly wrong, the accent bent toward Vell the way a tree bends toward light, but they are *right*, they are the right words, and hearing them come out of another living mouth is the most beautiful thing that has happened since before the burning.

So Issel tells her.

It starts, as it must, with the Choir.

"You have seen grey," Issel says. "Here. In your valley. Grey stone, grey rain, grey wool. That is not the grey I mean." Issel searches for the words and finds them, because the old tongue is generous, the old tongue kept everything even when the maps lost it. "This grey is a *color that eats*. It walks in a line, many figures, all wearing faces that are not faces — smooth, like eggs, like river stones — and where they walk, things stop being remembered. Not destroyed. *Unremembered*. There is a difference. Destroyed leaves a ruin. Unremembered leaves nothing at all, not even the absence, because you cannot miss a thing you cannot recall ever having been."

Paloma's grip tightens on Issel's hands.

"They came to Ostrava in autumn," Issel says. The words come faster now, tumbling, forty-one days of held breath pouring out. "First the border towns stopped appearing on travelers' maps. Then letters sent from Ostrava arrived blank. Then mothers looked at their own children and felt — *almost*. Almost love. Almost recognition. Like a word on the tip of the tongue. The Choir sings while it walks. That is its weapon. A song with no melody, only removal. Each verse takes a street. Each chorus takes a name. My city's name was—" 

Issel stops. Reaches for it.

Reaches again.

For one horrible moment the silence yawns open and Issel understands, bodily, what the others went through, the ones who were caught outside when the singing started — reaching for home and finding smooth grey nothing where the memory should be —

"*Ostrava,*" Paloma says quietly. Steadily. Like setting a bone. "Your home is called Ostrava."

And the name snaps back into place, and Issel bursts into tears.

Not graceful crying. Ugly, whole-body crying, the crying of a six-year-old who has been seven kinds of brave for far longer than any six-year-old should be. Paloma pulls Issel into her lap — Issel is small enough, barely — and rocks, and hums the lullaby, and Issel cries until there is nothing left to cry with, and then, hiccupping, keeps telling, because the telling must be finished. Some things, once begun, must be completed or they fester. The elders said so. The elders said many things. The elders are ash now, or worse than ash — unremembered, which is ash plus forgetting.

"The door," Issel whispers. "You know about the door. You found the cave. Your blood opened the edge of it — I saw your hand go into the light." A shudder runs through Paloma; Issel feels it against their cheek. "There are others like me outside. Survivors. Scattered families, running for eleven years, hiding in places the Choir hasn't finished singing. Grandmothers who carry seeds. Men who carry nothing but names, saying them aloud every night so the names stay real. We are few. We were more, but—" Issel swallows. "We were more.

"The door opens once in a generation, at solstice, and only from inside needs an outside key. I am the outside key. They chose me because I am small enough to slip through the Choir's attention, and because my blood is — " Issel frowns, hunting for the phrase the elders used, the formal phrase, "*of the complete line*" — and because a child's grief is quiet in a way adult grief isn't. Adults weep loudly. Children fold up small. Small fits through cracks.

"I walked for two seasons. Boats, passes, the underground rivers. I followed the salt. Salt remembers Ostrava; wherever our people settled, the ground grows a little salty, like a scar. The salt led me here, to this valley that magic cannot enter, and I thought — *they cannot follow me where magic cannot go.* I thought the valley would be safe."

Issel lifts their head and looks at Paloma directly, and delivers the terrible part, the part they carried across the world, the part that has been sitting in their chest like swallowed stone:

"But the Choir does not give up. Ever. It has unmade nine countries. It is patient the way winter is patient. It stood at our borders for three years, singing, before Ostrava began to fade. It is standing at your valley's border *now*, Paloma. It cannot come in — not yet, not fully — but it stands at the rim of the hills and it is learning your valley's name. Saying it over and over. Slightly wrong each time. And a name that is said wrong enough times, by enough mouths, begins to fray."

Paloma has gone very pale. Firelight moves on her face and she doesn't seem to feel the cold coming under the door, but Issel feels it. Issel has been feeling it for weeks — a thin wrong note under everything, like a string tuned a half-step flat. The valley's edges itching. Hedda's well blooming salt. These are not accidents. These are symptoms.

"When the door opens at solstice," Issel says, and here their voice goes small again, child-small, because the next part is the heaviest stone of all, "the survivors outside can come *in* — to Ostrava, the preserved Ostrava, the city behind the door where time is held like breath. But a door needs a key on both sides. I was sent knowing—" The sentence breaks. Issel tries again. Breaks again. Finally says it plain, the way Mama made her practice saying hard things, look at the person, use short words: "If I open it from here, I stay on this side. Forever. The key cannot pass through its own lock. If I go home, the door never opens and everyone outside is lost. If everyone outside comes home, I am locked out of my own country until I die."

The fire pops. Snow ticks the glass.

"So the elders argued all winter," Issel finishes, barely above a whisper. "Some said send me anyway, sacrifice one child for hundreds. Some said never, never, find another way. In the end they did both. They sent me — and they prayed someone on this side would be cleverer than they were. That is you, I think. Or I hope. I ran out of hope twice on the road and had to make new hope from scratch, and it isn't as good as the original kind, it's thinner, but it works."

Paloma is silent for a long, long time. Issel watches her face do many things — grief, fury, calculation, tenderness — like weather crossing a valley floor.

Then she says, in the old tongue, carefully, formally, the way one says a vow:

"*No one locks a child out of home while I have hands.*"

Issel believes her. That is the frightening thing. Issel has learned, over two seasons of running, that believing people is the most dangerous act there is — more dangerous than the mountain passes, more dangerous than the empty roads. Believing people is how you get left behind. And yet Issel looks at this woman with the spoon-scarred thumb and the bent-accent vowels and the grandmother's lullaby in her mouth, and Issel believes her completely, immediately, the way a lock believes a key.

Something in Issel's chest unknots — a knot tied forty-one days ago at the threshold, tied maybe two seasons ago in a burning harbor, tied perhaps at birth. The catharsis is enormous and exhausting. Issel sags against Paloma like a puppet with cut strings.

"There's more," Issel murmurs, already sliding toward sleep, words going soft and round at the edges. "Tomorrow I'll tell you the rest. About the song. About how it gets louder near solstice. About the third verse, the one that takes children first—"

Paloma goes rigid.

"—but don't be scared," Issel mumbles, eyelids winning. "Scared is just the body praying."

And then, at the very last lip of sleep, Issel's ears catch something they've been half-hearing for days and dismissing as wind, as the river, as imagination — and now, with the silence broken and the senses cracked open, Issel hears it clearly for the first time:

Far away, beyond the hills, beyond the snow, thin as a hair drawn across the night:

singing.

Many voices. No melody. Patient as winter.

Saying the valley's name.

Almost right.

Almost.
