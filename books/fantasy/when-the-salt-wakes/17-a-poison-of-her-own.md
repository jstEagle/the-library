## 17. A Poison of Her Own

The summons came at dawn, folded into the beak of a grey pigeon that had no business being on my windowsill before the baker's boy had even lit his ovens.

I knew the handwriting before I broke the seal. Corvane wrote like he spoke—economical, elegant, every letter placed the way a man places a knife at a table setting. *Her Grace requires you early. The winter court begins its fasting rites. Come hungry.*

*Come hungry.* It was almost funny. I was always hungry in the small way of people who taste everything and swallow little. Ten years of eating for a living had taught me to keep an appetite like a kept animal: fed enough to stay gentle, never so full it forgot what it wanted.

I looked back into the cottage. Issel slept in the trundle bed I'd bought from the cooper's widow, curled around a pillow like it might be taken away if they loosened their grip. Salt still came off them in fine white dust when they turned over. I'd stopped sweeping it up. It felt wrong to sweep.

Three days ago, Hedda had told me what the name-sound meant. Two days ago, Corvane had asked me about strangers at my door, and I had lied to him with a steady voice while my heart hammered like someone locked in a cellar. Last night I had decoded two more recipes from Grandmother Sefa's book and learned that the underground river ran beneath the tannery road, and that the door beneath the valley could only be found by those who carried Ostrava in their blood—and that my hand passed through light as easily as Issel's did.

Today, apparently, Her Grace required me early.

I dressed in my service grey, braided my hair flat the way the palace liked it—invisible hair, invisible Paloma—and left porridge on the hearth for a child who would wake confused and patient and salt-sweet. At the door I paused. Old habit now. *I'll come back,* I said in Ostravan, because saying it in Vellan felt too much like a promise anyone could overhear and take from me.

Issel, half-asleep, answered with the word for *yes* that was also the word for *the tide returns.*

Then I walked down through the frost to the palace where I had spent ten years proving, dish by dish, that nothing they gave me could kill me.

I should have wondered why that proof suddenly mattered so much to a spymaster.

* * *

The winter court smelled of pine boughs and candle wax and, underneath it, the faint mineral bite I had been noticing everywhere lately—the smell of salt where no sea had ever been. The Regent's fasting rites meant the kitchens were running lean: broths, breads, preserved fruits, nothing rich enough to hide anything in. Poisoners hate fast days. There are only so many ways to disguise bitterness in clear broth.

Corvane met me in the small tasting chamber off the west gallery—the one with the window that looked out on the frozen herb garden, the one where he'd trained me, years ago, to read a plate like a page.

He looked well. That was the first warning. Corvane never looked well without a reason; he wore his tiredness like armor and today he'd left it off.

"Paloma." He said my name warmly, and warmth from Corvane was like honey on a blade—you noticed the sweetness precisely because you knew what was underneath it. "Thank you for coming early. I know your mornings have become... complicated."

"My mornings are the same as they've always been." I hung my cloak on the peg. "Broths, breads, fruit. Fasting fare."

"Mm." He gestured to the table. Three covered dishes sat in a row, silver domes catching the candlelight, each with the little lead seal of the kitchens unbroken. "We'll start with the quail broth. It's for the Regent's own table tonight—her first meal after the rite ends at sundown."

I lifted the first dome. Clear amber broth, a slice of root, a thread of green herb floating like a tiny drowned flag. I took the tasting spoon, held it to the light out of habit, and ate.

Nothing. Clean fat, clean bone, a whisper of thyme. I let it sit on my tongue longer than politeness required, then swallowed and waited for the count of sixty, the way I'd been taught. My pulse stayed level. My mouth stayed honest.

"Clean," I said.

"Good." He uncovered the second dish himself. Bread. A dark loaf, dense, crusted with oats—the kind baked for servants' tables on fast days, the kind nobody poisoned because nobody important ate it. "This one interests me less," Corvane said, "but procedure is procedure."

I tore a corner. The crumb was good, faintly sour, honest work. I ate it, chewed, waited.

"Clean," I said again, and something in his face flickered—not disappointment exactly. More like a man watching a lock turn.

"Excellent," said Corvane. "Now the third."

* * *

Later—much later, in the years after—I would try to reconstruct exactly when I knew. There wasn't a moment. That was the craft of it. Corvane was too good to give me a moment. What there was instead was a slow accumulation, like water rising in a cellar: the way he positioned himself between me and the door without appearing to; the way the third dome sat slightly closer to him than the others; the way he watched my hands rather than my face, because he knew my face better than I did.

The third dish was pears in wine jelly, gilded with leaf silver for the Regent's private supper. Sweet things were the hardest to test. Sweetness forgives everything.

I lifted the spoon. The jelly shivered, caught the candlelight, threw a small trembling star onto the ceiling.

And underneath the pear and the wine and the sugar I tasted it.

Not bitter. Not metallic. Nothing with a shape I could name. It tasted like nothing at all—which was worse. After ten years, I knew every flavor poison could wear in this kingdom: the almond ghost of bitterbark, the copper kiss of ratbane, the green darkness of widow's nightshade. This was none of them. This was a hole in the flavor, a place where taste simply stopped, the way sound stops at the edge of a deep well.

I had tasted that hole once before. Seven years ago. A dish intended for the old Chancellor—a fish course from a river town I'd never traced, prepared by a cook who was never found. The court physician had called it *greyveil*, written the name in his ledger, shrugged. No antidote. No explanation. The Chancellor had eaten elsewhere that night; the dish had gone to the pigs; the pigs had died quietly overnight, and everyone had agreed not to think about it.

Everyone but me. Because I had tasted it, and I had spent seven years waiting for it to come back.

My body did what ten years of training had built it to do: nothing visible. I set the spoon down. I reached for the water glass. And then—

I drank the whole spoonful.

That was the part I will never fully explain, even to myself. There was a half-second, a sliver of a sliver, in which I could have coughed, could have spat, could have cried *poison* and run for the door and been believed, because I was the taster and the taster's tongue was law. One heartbeat of freedom, offered on a silver spoon.

Instead I swallowed. Because I understood, in that cold bright instant, that the dish was the test and the test was not about pears. If I detected it and lived, I proved something. If I detected it and reported it, I proved something else—that I could identify a poison even Corvane's network couldn't source, a poison that didn't belong to any kingdom's cookbook. A poison from somewhere else. From outside the valley.

From wherever vanished things go.

So I swallowed, and put down the spoon, and said, in the flattest voice I owned:

"The jelly's fine. The pear's slightly underripe."

Corvane smiled at me across the table with something that was almost tenderness.

"No," he said gently. "It isn't."

* * *

The silence went on long enough for me to count three of my own heartbeats. Somewhere below us, the fasting court murmured its hunger-prayers. The little star of candlelight trembled on the ceiling.

"You're wondering how long I've known," Corvane said. He drew out a chair—the guest's chair, the one I'd never once been invited to sit in—and indicated it. I didn't sit. "Sit, please. You'll want to be seated."

"I'll stand."

"As you like." He settled into his own chair, folding his long hands, and for a moment he looked genuinely weary, the armor of tiredness going back on. "Seven years ago a fish course came up from the river towns with something in it we never identified. The physician called it greyveil. You flagged it. You flagged it in a report so precise that I have kept it in my files ever since—flavor profile, onset delay, residue behavior. Do you remember writing it?"

"I remember the pigs."

"Yes." His mouth twitched. "The pigs were unfortunate. The point is, Paloma, that in seven years I have never been able to find greyveil anywhere in this kingdom or any kingdom adjacent to it. I've asked smugglers. I've asked assassins. I've asked a man in Vell-harbor who claims to have eaten his way across four continents. None of them knew it." He leaned forward. "Two weeks ago, I finally learned why. It doesn't come from any kingdom. It comes from places that aren't on maps anymore."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"You're going to tell me," I said carefully, "that you think the Regent's dessert came from a country that doesn't exist."

"I'm going to tell you that I think it came from *you*."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped into still water.

"Not made by you," he went on, before I could speak. "Let's be precise; precision has always been your gift. Greyveil cannot be synthesized here. The valley won't permit it—nothing of that nature can cross the border, as I'm sure you've begun to suspect. So it must have come through. Through whatever door, whatever crack, whatever *inheritance* has recently arrived in this valley." He tilted his head. "A dish was delivered to the kitchens yesterday by a carter who swears he picked it up from a farmstead that burned down eleven years ago. The seal was genuine. The cook's mark was genuine. The jelly was flawless except for one thing, which only one person in this building could possibly have detected."

"And you wanted to know if I'd detect it."

"I needed to know if you'd detect it," Corvane corrected softly. "There's a difference. Detection is a skill. What you just did—" he nodded at the empty spoon, the untouched remainder of the jelly sitting in its silver dome like a sleeping animal, "—what you just did was choose to swallow. You tasted a poison you recognized as impossible, understood instantly that reporting it would expose more than concealing it, and you bet your life on your own body. On the immunity you've spent ten years earning, dish by dish." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know how few people in this court would have made that calculation correctly? I can count them on one hand, and I'm two of the fingers."

"Why?" My voice came out lower than I intended. "Why test me at all?"

"Because three days ago, someone told me a story." He rose, walked to the window, stood looking out at the frozen garden with his hands clasped behind his back—an orator's posture, and I hated him a little for how well it fit him. "They told me about a child. Salt-white. Speaking a dead language. Living at the cottage of the food-taster, who has taken no days off in six years until this month, who has suddenly developed errands and absences and a light burning past midnight." He turned. "They told me, Paloma, that the child speaks Ostravan. And that you answered."

The room got very small.

"That's not possible," I said. "Ostrava is a story. A fable mothers use—"

"—to frighten children about the world beyond the border, yes. I know the fable. I grew up on it too, in a port town full of sailors who swore their grandfathers had traded there." Something moved behind his eyes, brief and buried, and was gone. "But here is what I know as fact, Paloma, because it is my profession to know facts. Fourteen days ago, the wells in the low quarter began to show salt bloom. The church font crusted over. Frost forms on south-facing walls in patterns no glazier recognizes. The valley's border—the seal that has kept magic out of Vell since its founding—has developed a leak. Small. Growing. Centered, by every reading my people can take, on one small cottage on Tanner's Lane."

He let that sit. Outside, a crow crossed the white garden and its shadow crossed the parterre like a pen stroke.

"I don't care about the child," Corvane said, and I flinched, and he saw me flinch, and to his credit he didn't pretend otherwise. "No—that's a lie, and you'd catch it, and we're past lying, you and I. I care enormously about the child. I care about what the child *is*. A key, if the old accounts are right. A key to a door that opens once in a generation and closes forever at the solstice." He checked some inner calendar; I watched him do it. "Nine days from now."

"You've known about the solstice." It wasn't a question. "How long?"

"Longer than you've known about the child. I'm sorry for that. Truly." And the terrible thing was that he sounded sorry. Corvane's kindness had always been real—it was just never free. "Here is what happens next, and I want you to understand that I fought for these terms. In three days, I must present the Regent with a resolution to the border crisis. She is frightened. Frightened regents do permanent things. If I bring her the child, quietly, through me, she may be merciful. The child would be studied, sheltered, perhaps even honored—there are precedents for guests of state. If I bring her nothing, her soldiers will find the cottage themselves, and soldiers are not careful with doors, or with children, or with tasters who obstruct them."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in three days I stop being able to protect you." He said it plainly, no theater, which somehow made it worse. "Paloma. Listen to me, because I am about to say something I have never said to anyone in this court. I lost family to a vanished land too. My grandmother used to sing songs in a language she claimed was just 'old harbor talk.' I found one line of it written in a ledger thirty years ago, in a hand that matched documents from a kingdom that officially never existed." His voice roughened, barely, like cloth tearing a very small amount. "So believe me when I tell you I am not your enemy. I am the only person in this building standing between you and the Regent's fear. But protection has a price, and the price is the child, delivered to my custody, within three days. Unharmed. Unfrightened, if you can manage it. I'm told they respond well to soup."

The vulgarity of it—the *soup*, the small domestic detail deployed like a hook—made my stomach turn, and under the turning I felt the greyveil doing its slow work, a cold spreading outward from my middle like frost finding the cracks in a windowpane.

Because here was the thing he didn't know. Couldn't know. My body was already fighting it. I could feel the fight—a heat behind the cold, a stubborn animal persistence, ten years of small deaths teaching my blood how to refuse the big one. Whatever greyveil was, wherever it came from, it had never met anyone like me. Nobody had. That was the entire point of me.

I let him see none of it. Invisible hair, invisible face, invisible Paloma.

"Three days," I said.

"Three days."

"And if I need longer?"

"Then you need to be a faster liar than you were last week." He smiled again, warm as a hearth, and gathered the poisoned dessert onto its tray with his own hands, careful as a nurse. "You should eat something on your way home," he added at the door, and pressed a wrapped parcel into my hands—bread and cheese from his own table, I realized, hospitality, kindness, the friendly face wearing all its friendliest features. "Winter's coming, Paloma. It comes early this year. For all of us."

I walked out through the fasting court with the parcel under my arm and poison settling into my bones like a slow snowfall, and I did not stumble once.

* * *

Tanner's Lane was white when I got home. Not snow-white. Salt-white, a fine glittering dust along the doorstep and the sill, and Issel stood in the middle of it barefoot, waiting, the way children wait when they have learned that adults come back late and lie about why.

They looked at my face, and whatever they saw there made them step aside from the door and hold it open, and say one word in the dead language of a drowned kingdom.

It was the word for *hunger.* They were telling me to eat. They were asking if *I* was safe. A seven-year-old exile, hunted by singers who erase countries, asking after me.

I got us both inside. I bolted the door. I unwrapped Corvane's bread and cheese, set it on the table between us, and looked at it for a long time—kindness, or bait, or both—and then, because I had already swallowed worse today, I cut us each a slice.

While Issel chewed, I pulled Grandmother Sefa's recipe book from under the floorboard and opened it to the page Hedda and I had marked: the one that described, in the guise of instructions for preserving pears in wine jelly, the route to the underground river, and the door, and the words that would open it.

Nine days to the solstice. Three days before Corvane came for Issel.

I did the arithmetic twice. It refused, both times, to leave me more than three.

"Tomorrow night," I told Issel in their language, my grandmother's language, my own language, though I'd only known it a fortnight. "We go down to the river. Tonight, we pack."

Issel looked at me with those dark, salt-rimmed eyes and answered with the word that meant both *yes* and *the tide returns.*

Outside, far off toward the valley's edge, something began—very faintly—to sing.
