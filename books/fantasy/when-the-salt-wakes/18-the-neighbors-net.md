## 18. The Neighbors' Net

Marta Kell had been baking bread in Vell for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above every other rule: mind your own dough.

It was not a cruel rule. It was a survival rule. The valley kept its people safe by keeping them small—no strange questions, no strange guests, no strange anything that might make the Regent's spymaster look twice at your door. Marta had watched better women than her be ruined by curiosity. She had watched the Fenn family pack their things in the night and vanish down the river road because someone had seen a candle burning at the wrong hour. Mind your own dough. She said it to apprentices the way other people said good morning.

So she could not explain, even to herself, why on the third morning of the frost she was wrapping two loaves and a crock of butter in cloth instead of one, or why her feet carried her past her own turning to the narrow lane where Paloma Almeida's cottage sat low against the hill.

The child was in the garden.

That was the first thing. Not hiding—children who needed hiding hid—but standing in the dead rows of winter kale with salt-white hands raised to the sky, as if testing whether the grey clouds would hold still if asked politely. The child's hair was white too, or near enough. Salt-caked, Hedda would later say, though Marta didn't know that yet. All Marta knew was that no child in Vell looked like that, and no child in Vell stood so still, and Paloma Almeida—the taster, the quiet one, the woman who ate poison for a living and thanked you for the pleasure—was watching from the doorway with an expression Marta recognized because she wore it herself most market days.

The expression of a woman pretending everything was ordinary while knowing it wasn't.

"I brought bread," Marta said, because what else was there. "Yesterday's bake. It's a bit dense."

Paloma came down the path and took the bundle with both hands, the way you take something heavier than bread. "That's kind of you."

"It isn't," Marta said. "I couldn't sleep. I baked too much. That's all it is."

They both knew it wasn't. Down in the garden, the child turned and looked at Marta, and there was nothing feral in the look, nothing strange either, which was somehow the strangest thing of all. Just a child. Tired around the eyes in a way children shouldn't be.

"What's the word for bread?" Marta heard herself ask.

Paloma went very still. "Why?"

"Because if I'm going to keep bringing it," Marta said, and heard her own thirty-one-year rule crack clean down the middle like a bad loaf, "I should say it properly."

For a moment Paloma looked like she might cry, which frightened Marta more than any soldier could have. Then she said a word—a soft word, round at the front and hissing at the end—and the child repeated it, smiling, and corrected Paloma's pronunciation with the grave patience of a schoolmaster three feet tall.

Marta walked home the long way, past the well, past the church, and did not let herself think until she reached her kitchen. Then she thought: *Corvane has eyes everywhere.* Then she thought: *the flour is already measured for tomorrow.* Then she thought: *two loaves.*

Some rules break quietly, she decided, pulling out her apron again. You don't have to make a sound when they go.

* * *

Bram poling the ferry across the Vell water at dusk told himself he wasn't going anywhere in particular, which was true, and also that he hadn't altered his route to pass the taster's dock, which was a lie, and Bram had made his peace years ago with being a man who lived inside small lies the way a fish lives inside water.

The debt was old. Older than Paloma knew, probably. Thirty-odd years back, before Bram owned the ferry, before he owned the coat on his back, there had been a winter when the fever came up the river road and took people the way a net takes fish—one pull, another, no pattern anyone could see. Bram had been nineteen and burning from the inside, and a woman not much older than him had sat by his pallet for six nights running with a clay pot of something bitter and steaming, spooning it into him when he was too far gone to swallow, talking the whole time in a language he didn't know, soft and low, like a river under ice.

She'd told him it was just a recipe. Her grandmother's. Nothing special.

He'd survived. Half the lane hadn't. And when he was well he asked her name and she said Thackeray, and she married a valley man within the year and never spoke that other tongue again where anyone could hear, and Bram, who owed her his life, understood without being told that the price of the gift was silence. So he kept it. He ferried people and he minded his own business and once a year, until she died, he left a fat river trout on the Thackeray doorstep and never knocked.

Now there was salt on that same doorstep. He'd seen it two nights ago, crossing late—a fine white crust along the stone, glittering under the moon, though the nearest sea was a story mothers told and the river ran fresh as rain.

And now here was Paloma herself, coming down to his dock in the blue hour with a sack of grain she didn't need milled and a look on her face like a woman carrying water in both hands across a frozen lake.

"Mistress Almeida."

"Master Bram." She set the sack down. Neither of them mentioned the grain. It was just something to do with their arms.

"You've had trouble with your well?" he said. "The salt."

Her breath stopped. He watched it stop, watched her decide, in the space of half a heartbeat, what kind of lie to tell—and then watched her decide not to lie at all, which was, he suspected, harder for her than lying would have been.

"Yes," she said. "Salt."

"My grandmother's well went salty once," Bram said, staring out at the dark water. "Before the fever year. Before—" He shut his mouth. Opened it again. Thirty years of silence sat on his chest like a stone, and underneath the stone, the debt. "My grandmother used to say that when the salt wakes, somebody's coming home."

Paloma stared at him. In the dusk her eyes were enormous.

"I run this ferry at night sometimes," Bram said, gruff now, embarrassed, looking at the water instead of her face. "Moonless nights are best. Current's calm below the mill race, and nobody walks the towpath after the second bell. If a person needed to move quietly—not tonight, but soon—there are worse ways than mine."

"Bram—"

"Trout's running early this year," he said, picking up her sack and stowing it under the bench where sacks always went. "Thought you should know. Your grandmother liked my trout."

He pushed off before she could answer, because he was sixty pounds of sentimental fool and if she'd said thank you in that voice of hers he'd have gone to pieces right there on his own deck. The current took the boat. Behind him, on the dock, the taster stood very straight and very still until the bend of the river took her.

Mind your own business, Bram told the water. The water didn't answer. Water never did. But it ran toward somewhere, same as everybody, and some nights that felt less like a law and more like an invitation.

* * *

Hedda had known this day was coming for forty years, which did not make it easier. Knowing a storm is coming and standing in it are different trades entirely.

She sorted herbs with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be. Feverfew. Comfrey. The last of the true lavender before the frost took it. On the table beside her, unrolled, the ledger—her ledger, the one she'd copied from her mother's hand and her mother had copied from a burning city's last clerk, the list of families who had come over the mountains with changed names and empty hands and lullabies they sang only into pillows.

Thackeray. Almeida. Kell, though Marta would spit to hear it. Half of Vell, if the truth were poured out, and the truth was a liquid that ate through every vessel you tried to hold it in.

A knock. Two quick, one soft. Their new signal; the girl learned fast.

Paloma came in with the wind and the smell of snow and something else underneath—salt, faint as a memory, rising off her cloak. The child Issel followed, wrapped in one of Paloma's sweaters with the sleeves rolled four times, and went straight to the fire with the certainty of a cat returning to a hearth it had decided was its own.

"She's asking names again," Paloma said. "For everyone. Marta. Bram. Yours. She keeps them in a little book she draws in the steam on windows."

"Then she's smarter than Corvane, who keeps his in blood." Hedda poured the tisane—chamomile and something older, something from page nine of a book written in a script that officially did not exist. "Sit. Both of you. What I'm going to say, you'll want to be sitting down for, and I'll want to be sitting down for, so we may as well match."

She laid her hands flat on the ledger.

"When the Choir came for Ostrava," Hedda said, "it didn't kill everyone. Killing is quick and quick things leave traces. No—it *unmade*. Streets, songs, gravestones, the very word for bread. But a handful got out. Farmers, a clerk, a cook, a midwife, a girl with a book of recipes her grandmother wrote in the margins of. They crossed the mountains and found a valley where magic could not follow, because the valley was sealed against it like a jar against flies, and they broke their names to fit through the neck of it, and they lived." She looked up. "You know this story, girl. You've known it since before you could talk. You just didn't know you knew it."

"The ledger," Paloma whispered.

"Eleven families. Some lines died out. Some thinned till there was nothing left but a nose shape or a way with knots." Hedda's voice went dry as the herbs around her. "And some stayed strong, generation after generation, in bodies that could take any poison and shrug it off. Because a body trained to survive what's put in it—that's a body the Choir's song can't get a grip on. Your grandmother didn't just hide in this valley, Paloma. She prepared you. Every dish she ever cooked you was a lesson. Whether she meant it or the book meant it, I can't say. But here you are."

The fire popped. Issel, cross-legged on the hearthstone, watched them with the terrible patience of a child who has already understood worse news than this.

"And now the seal is leaking," Paloma said slowly. "The salt. The font at the church. Because Issel came through."

"Because the door is opening, and the valley is the doorframe, and doorframes creak." Hedda closed the ledger. "Which means Corvane will come, and sooner than we'd like. He's clever and he's kind and both those things are dangerous in a man with an empty place where a country used to be. So listen to me, both of you, because this is the part that matters." She leaned forward, and forty years of waiting made her voice as quiet and absolute as a blade going into a sheath.

"There's a net under this valley and always has been. Old debts. Old recipes. Old loves. Marta Kell is already baking double and telling herself stories about it. Bram has offered his boat—I saw it in his face at market today, the way he wouldn't look at you. The baker, the ferryman, the herbalist. Bread, passage, and knowledge. That's a whole country, child, if you squint. Ostrava fell because it stood alone and proud and let itself be picked off. We will not do it that way. We will do it the valley way: small kindnesses, stacked."

Paloma's eyes were bright and wet. "You're describing treason."

"I'm describing supper," said Hedda, "which has started more revolutions than swords ever carried." She slid the tisane across the table to Issel, who took it in both salt-white hands, and said—in careful, rusty Ostravan, the first she'd spoken aloud in forty years—"*Drink, little key. There is time yet, but less than there was.*"

Issel drank. Outside, the first snow came down, and where each flake landed on Hedda's windowsill, it did not melt.

It glittered.

* * *

By the fourth day, the net was real, though none of its threads ever touched where anyone could see.

Marta's apprentice delivered "mistakes"—burnt loaves, misshapen buns—to the taster's door every morning and was under strict orders never to say who sent them, and obeyed so thoroughly that she genuinely forgot, which was the safest kind of obedience there was.

Bram began running the night ferry "for the fishing," though everyone knew the fish slept, and left a lantern burning green on his starboard side on moonless nights, which meant *clear*, and red on the port, which meant *stay off the water*, and only two people in the valley knew the code and one of them was seven years old.

The ferryman's nephew, who swept the Regent's stables, mentioned casually to a groomsman whose cousin served in Corvane's house that the spymaster's own chimney smoked terribly and hadn't his men better things to check than a poor herbalist's shop on the south lane, and the rumor moved the way rumors do, sideways and fast, and bought Hedda three unwatched days.

And on the fifth evening, Paloma Almeida opened her door to find the whole lane quietly fed: fish from Bram, bread from Marta, a jar of Hedda's winter preserves, eggs from the widow Ost, who had never spoken ten words to the taster in her life and left no note.

Issel counted the dishes on the table with great seriousness, touching each one, saying the Ostravan word for it, and then the Vellish word, building a bridge between languages one plate at a time. "*Süka. Soup. Brot. Bread. Fisk—*"

"Fish," Paloma said, and laughed, and the laugh was the first unguarded sound she'd made since the child arrived.

It was nearly midnight, the dishes washed, Issel asleep with the steam-name book open on their chest, when Paloma blew out the lamp and saw, in the last grey light through the window, that the frost on the glass had grown in patterns. Feathers. Wings. A row of tall thin shapes standing at the edge of the field, drawn in ice by no hand at all.

Far off, at the border of the valley, so distant it might have been wind, something had begun to sing.

Paloma stood in the dark kitchen with her grandmother's book in her hands and counted what she had: a baker, a boat, an old woman's ledger, a sleeping child, and eleven days until the solstice.

The singing did not stop. It did not get louder, either. It simply waited, patient as winter, for the valley to learn its name.

And down in the town, in the smoking house of the spymaster, a candle burned long past the second bell.
