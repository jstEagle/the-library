## 19. Grey Choir at the Border

Issel wakes in the dark with their hands already pressed flat against the floor.

They don't know why at first. The cottage is warm. The fire has gone down to a red eye in the grate. Paloma sleeps in the other room, breathing slow and even, the way she does when she has worked all day and let herself rest anyway. The soup pot sits on the cold stove. Everything is ordinary.

But Issel's hands are flat on the floorboards like they are listening.

And then Issel hears it too.

Not a sound. Not exactly. More like the feeling of a sound coming from very far away, the way you feel thunder before your ears find it—a pressure in the teeth, a hum in the bones of the feet. Issel sits up in the nest of blankets Paloma made for them by the hearth and presses one hand to the floor and one to their own chest, which is what Grandmother Maretta taught them to do when the world felt wrong: *ground yourself, small bird. One hand to the earth, one to your heart. Tell me which one shakes.*

The floor shakes. Faintly. Like a plucked string that will not stop humming.

Issel knows this hum.

Issel has known this hum since the night the sky over Ostrava turned the color of old milk and the elders put Issel through the door with a bundle of bread and salt and instructions too big for a six-year-old body to carry.

*They are singing,* Issel thinks, in the language with no name here, the language that lives only in Issel's mouth and now—strangely, wonderfully—in Paloma's. *They have found the edge of this place.*

* * *

In the morning, everything looks the same, and that is the worst part.

Frost on the window. Smoke from the chimney of the baker's house down the lane. A robin doing its bold hopping walk across the yard, looking for crumbs. Paloma makes porridge with honey because it is almost solstice and she says honey is for keeping strength up, and Issel eats every drop and holds out the bowl for more, and Paloma laughs her quiet laugh, and none of it is wrong.

But Issel can feel the song now even with the sun up. It sits under everything like water under ice. It has been traveling a long time—the Choir does not hurry; hurrying is for things that can be outrun—and now it has arrived at something, and stopped, and begun to press.

After breakfast Issel goes out to the yard and stands very still in the frost and lets their senses open the way the elders taught, the way that used to make Grandmother Maretta cover her eyes and say, *you see too much, small bird, you see like the old ones saw.*

The valley opens beneath them like a bowl.

Issel feels its shape the way you feel the shape of your own mouth without looking. The hills rise on every side, folded and green-grey even in winter. The river runs through the middle of everything, underground for part of its journey—Issel can feel it down there, sliding through dark channels, and it makes the whole valley hum a little, a low wet note, friendly. There are people scattered across the bowl like seeds: hundreds of small warm points of attention, cooking, arguing, mending nets, sleeping late. And threaded through all of it, holding it like a glaze holds a pot, there is the stillness. The thing Hedda called the seal. The reason magic cannot cross the border. It feels to Issel like a skin stretched over the whole valley, thin as eggshell in places, thick as hide in others, and everywhere utterly silent. Nothing sings inside it. Nothing *can.*

That is why the refugees came. That is why Issel's grandmother's grandmother came, generations ago, before the door was hidden. People whose songs were hunted came to a place where no song could live, and the silence kept them like a jar keeps honey.

And now, at the northern edge of the bowl, where the hills climb toward a pass full of grey scree—

There.

The song.

It is not loud. That is the trick of it. The Grey Choir never sings loudly. They sing at exactly the volume of thinking, so that you cannot tell whether the words are coming from outside your head or being made inside it. Issel claps both hands over their ears and it changes nothing, because the song is not in the air. It is in the *naming.*

Because that is how the Choir unmakes a country. Not with armies. Armies are slow and messy and leave survivors, and survivors are seeds. No. The Choir sings the country's name until the name frays.

Every land has a true name, deep under its common ones—the name the stones know, the name the rain says when it lands. Vell has one. Issel can taste it faintly through the seal, round and brown and patient, a name like a root vegetable, like something that keeps well through winter. It is a good name. It is a stubborn name.

And the Choir has found the edge of it, and set itself against the border like mouths against a wall, and begun to sing Vell's name *wrong.*

*Vell.* But bent. *Vell.* But hollowed. Each repetition takes off a little shine, wears a groove in the true sound the way water wears stone—not fast, never fast, but inevitable, patient as erosion. Sing a name wrong enough times, for long enough, and the name forgets itself. And a land that forgets its name becomes soft at the edges. Becomes *suggestible.* Becomes a place where the Choir can step across as if it had always lived there, and the people will nod and make room, because who can object to a neighbor who was always there?

That is what happened to Ostrava.

Issel stands in the frost with their fists clenched and remembers—or rather refuses to remember, which is a different kind of remembering—the last morning of home. The Choir did not arrive like an army. They arrived like weather. One day the light was slightly greyer. One day the bells sounded slightly flatter. One day nobody could quite remember the second verse of the harvest hymn, and everyone laughed about it, and it wasn't funny, it was never funny, it was the first tooth of the beast going into the flesh of the world.

Then the colors went. Then the names of streets slid loose. Then Grandmother Maretta stood in the doorway with her shawl pinned wrong and asked Issel her own name, gently, twice, and got it wrong twice, and that was when the elders knew there were perhaps nine days left.

Nine days to carry a child through the salt caves to a door that opened once in a generation, and push that child through alone, into a silent valley, carrying a bundle of bread and salt and the whole surviving hope of a country.

Issel had cried. Issel remembered that honestly. Issel had cried and grabbed the doorframe and Grandmother Maretta had pried their fingers loose one by one, kissing each finger, saying *small bird, small bird, you are the key, keys must turn, keys must go where locks are* —and then the light had swallowed everything and Issel had been standing in a cold cave on the far side, alone, in a country that could not hear them speak.

Now the Choir has followed the key across the world.

* * *

"Your hands are shaking."

Paloma crouches in front of Issel in the yard, wrapping her own warm hands around theirs. Her breath makes little ghosts in the cold. "How long have you been standing here?"

Issel doesn't answer with words—they haven't yet, not fully, though words are gathering in them like a storm gathering behind a hill, Issel can feel them pressing—but they do the thing they have learned works best: they take Paloma's hand and press it flat against their own chest, over the heart, the way you'd let someone feel a drum.

Paloma goes still. She has learned to read Issel's silences better than most people read speech. "Something's coming," she says quietly. Not a question.

Issel nods.

"Here?"

Issel hesitates. Then points north. Toward the hills. Toward the pass.

Paloma's face does the thing it always does, the thing Issel has watched carefully for weeks now, learning it like a lesson: nothing moves, nothing falls, but everything behind her eyes pulls inward, tightens, becomes smaller and harder, like a fist closing around a coal. She is afraid. She simply refuses to spend her fear where anyone can see it.

"The border," she says. "You're telling me something's at the border."

Issel nods again. Then, because Paloma deserves more than nodding, because the words are pushing harder now, Issel reaches for the oldest word they know, the first word every Ostravan child learns, the warning-word:

"*Sarneth,*" they say.

Paloma's breath catches. Issel has said words to her before, single words offered like pebbles across a stream. But this one lands differently. This one makes her go pale under her wind-chapped cheeks.

"That's—" She stops. Starts again. "My grandmother sang that word. In the lullaby. The third line." She says it back to Issel, careful, testing: "*Sarneth.*"

"Yes," Issel says. Just that. Their first yes spoken aloud in this valley, and it costs them something, and it gives them something back.

"What does it mean?"

Issel looks north again. Under the frost, under the silence, the wrong-name-pressing continues, patient as tide. *Vell. Vell. Vell,* sung crooked, sung hollow, sung the way a stranger sings a name they intend to steal.

Issel takes Paloma's hand and draws one finger slowly across the palm of her other hand, a long scraping line, and then draws a circle around it, and then taps the center of the circle many times, softly, softly, softly, like rain beginning.

Paloma watches the finger-marks fade from her palm. Issel watches her understand. It happens in stages, like ice cracking: first confusion, then translation, then the terrible arithmetic.

"It's circling," she says. "Whatever it is. It's found the edge and it's... knocking. Tapping." Her voice drops to almost nothing. "How long has it been tapping?"

Issel spreads both hands: *I don't know.* Then holds up fingers uncertainly, folds some down: *since last night, maybe. Maybe longer. I slept through the beginning.*

Paloma closes her eyes for one long breath. When she opens them, the fist behind them has done its work; the fear is packed away somewhere load-bearing. "We need Hedda," she says. "Get your coat."

* * *

Hedda's house smells of dried yarrow and woodsmoke and, these days, faintly of salt—which is strange, because Hedda's house has always smelled of herbs, and herbs do not smell of salt, and yet since the week the well water turned brackish there is a white rime creeping up the stones of her doorway that she cannot scrub away and has stopped trying to.

Hedda listens to Paloma's account—Issel's pointing, the tapping, the north—with her arms crossed and her lips pressed thin, and then she does something that frightens Issel more than anything the Choir has done yet.

She takes down a jar of goose fat, greases her forearms to the elbow, and plunges both arms into her rain barrel, up to the shoulder, eyes closed, the way you'd reach into an animal to feel whether it lives.

She stays like that for a long time. Steam rises faintly off the water. The kitchen is absolutely silent except for the tick of the fire.

When she pulls her arms out, she is gray-faced.

"It's true," she says. "I hoped the child was wrong. No offense meant, small one." She dries her arms roughly. "The water tastes of the song. Faintly. Like a coin held in the mouth. That means it's not just at the border anymore—it's *soaking through.* Not entering. Soaking. The way damp comes through a wall."

"But the seal," Paloma says. "Magic can't cross it. You told me that yourself. It's the whole founding of this valley."

"And so it can't," Hedda agrees. "The Choir can't set one foot inside. Not while the seal holds." She sits down heavily on her stool, and for a moment she looks every year of her age and several she hasn't reached yet. "But a wall doesn't have to be broken to be beaten. It only has to be *outlasted.* And the Choir, child, is older than walls. Older than the idea of walls. It will stand at your border and sing your valley's name wrong until the valley gets tired of being itself."

"Can it? Can a name really—" Paloma stops herself. "No. Stupid question. My grandmother's whole country was erased from every map. I've seen what names do."

"Not erased from maps," Hedda corrects softly. "Erased from *memory.* Maps are easy. Maps burn. What the Choir does is worse—it doesn't destroy a thing, it makes the world agree the thing was never there. And it starts exactly as you say, taster. With patience. With repetition. With a song sung slightly wrong."

"How long?" Paloma asks. Her voice is steady but her hands are not; Issel sees her fold them into her apron to hide it, and loves her fiercely for it, the hiding. "How long before it frays through?"

Hedda looks at Issel. Really looks, the way she does, like reading a difficult herb label. "You would know better than I. You've heard it before, haven't you. At home."

Issel has been holding the storm of words back for weeks. The dam was built from shock and grief and the simple fact that a child who speaks summons hunters. But Hedda is asking honestly, and honesty deserves honesty, and besides—the words want out now. They have wanted out since the night the humming started. Issel opens their mouth and what comes out is not one word but a flood, Ostravan tumbling out in a cracked child's voice, faster than they've ever spoken it in this valley:

*"It took Ostrava eleven days from the first wrong note to the last bell. Eleven days, and we were a strong country, old, rooted, our name was carved in mountains. Your valley is small and your name is young and soft and the seal is already wearing thin where I stand, I am a hole in it, I brought the hole with me—"*

Issel stops. Both women are staring. Not because of the content.

Because Issel spoke. Fully, fluently, aloud.

"Issel," Paloma breathes, and kneels, and her eyes are shining in a way that makes Issel's chest hurt. "You're talking. You—you've been able to talk this whole time?"

Issel shrugs, miserable, suddenly small. *A door needs a key on both sides,* they almost say, and stop themselves, and the stopping hurts more than speaking did. Instead they sign: *speaking calls them. Speaking is calling.*

Hedda and Paloma exchange a look over Issel's head, the look of two adults sharing a whole conversation in half a second, and Issel hates it a little, being a child between languages.

"Eleven days," Hedda repeats at last, heavily. "For a mountain-named country. And the solstice is—" 

"Nine days," Paloma whispers.

Silence fills the kitchen like rising water.

* * *

That night Issel cannot sleep, and does not try.

They sit by the dead fire wrapped in Paloma's good blanket and they listen to the border the way you listen to a distant sea, and they catalog what they hear, because cataloging is a thing the elders taught the children precisely so that terror could be sorted into shelves instead of left heaped on the floor.

Shelf one: the song itself. Still faint. Still patient. *Vell. Vell. Vell,* sung crooked. It has settled into a rhythm now, and the rhythm is the frightening part, because it is not the rhythm of an attack. It is the rhythm of a lullaby. The Choir is singing the valley to sleep.

Shelf two: the seal. Issel can feel the places where it is thin. Around themselves, always—a soft hole shaped like a small mute child, a flaw the Choir can lean against. And along the underground river, strangely, the seal is thinner there too, worn smooth by centuries of water, and Issel files that away without knowing why, the way you pocket a pretty stone you might need later.

Shelf three: the valley itself. And here is the thing that makes Issel sit up straighter in the dark, here is the thing they will need to tell Paloma in the morning, if their courage holds: the valley is not entirely deaf to what is happening to its name. All day, small things have been going wrong at the edges of hearing. The church bell rang slightly flat at noon and the sexton frowned and struck it again and it rang flat again. The baker's dog would not stop facing north. Old men at the ferry landing argued about the name of the hill above the mill—one said it had always been Colder's Rise and one said it had always been Calder's Rise and neither would yield, and both were right, because the song had gotten between the two syllables and was worrying them apart like a dog with a bone.

Small things. Fraying things. The first teeth.

And shelf four—the shelf Issel saves for last because it is both the most hopeful and the most terrible:

Somewhere beneath the valley, past the underground river, behind a wall of salt that hums when touched, there is a door. And doors, Grandmother Maretta said, doors are the one thing in all the worlds that a song cannot unname. You can fray a country. You can erase a kingdom. But a door that has been fed—fed with hospitality, fed with bread and salt and a shared meal—is anchored in something older than names.

If the door can be opened, the song breaks against it like a wave.

If the door stays shut, the wave comes through.

Issel pulls the blanket tighter and looks at Paloma's closed door, and beyond it the dark hills, and beyond them the invisible mouths singing patiently in the freezing dark.

*Nine days,* Issel thinks, in two languages now, thanks to her. *We have nine days, and a door, and a woman who cooks poison without dying, and me.*

*I am the key on this side.*

*Please, whoever taught doors to remember: teach her in time.*
