## 20. What Belonging Costs

The Regent kept a small room behind the winter chapel where she went to pray to no one.

Paloma knew about it the way she knew most things at court—by watching, by keeping her mouth shut, by counting the minutes between a door opening and closing. The Regent entered through the vestry twice a week, always alone, always after vespers, and always stayed exactly forty minutes. No priest attended. No candles were lit. She went in with her hands empty and came out with them empty, and whatever happened in between belonged to her the way Paloma's tongue belonged to the crown: on loan, never owned.

She waited by the vestry door for an hour in the cold, tasting the air out of habit. Stone dust. Tallow. Under it, faintly, something sweet that shouldn't have been there—honey and bruised herbs, the smell of Hedda's shop, clinging to her own sleeves. She'd changed her clothes before coming. It didn't matter. Salt found its way into everything now.

The door opened at the fortieth minute. Queen Regent Maren stood in the frame without a lamp, which meant she'd seen well enough in the dark to know someone was there before she ever reached it.

"Taster," she said. Not a question. Of course she knew. A woman who couldn't taste her own food without dying first learned to notice everything else.

"Your Majesty."

"You're supposed to be at the evening service." The Regent's voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had spent twenty years making sure nothing she said could be quoted against her. "You haven't been to a service in eleven days. You've missed two tastings—both covered, both noted. You left the palace by the laundry gate yesterday and came back through the fish market, which no one does unless they don't want to be seen." She stepped past Paloma and lit a single candle on the altar ledge. The flame made her face older and kinder at once, which Paloma suspected was the only honest light she ever stood in. "I didn't ask. That should tell you something. Come inside before Corvane's people do the arithmetic for me."

* * *

The room behind the chapel was smaller than Paloma's cottage and barer than a prison cell. One chair, one kneeler, a window of thick glass that turned the moon into a smear. On the sill sat a clay cup with a chip in the rim, and Paloma understood—it was the kind of detail you earned only by being poor once—that this was where the Regent drank water when she didn't want to be a queen.

"Say what you came to say," Maren said. "I have until the bell."

"I'll give you my resignation."

The words came out steadier than Paloma felt. Eleven years. Eleven years of eating death in small doses so that other people could eat dinner. Eleven years of being valuable precisely because she was expendable, of standing at the left hand of every royal table while the court looked through her as though invisibility were a service like any other. She had rehearsed this offer all day, walking loops around the fish market until the salt-seller's boys stopped staring, and now that it was out it sounded impossibly small.

"Resignation," the Regent repeated. She lowered herself onto the kneeler—not praying, just sitting, her back against the altar stone—and studied Paloma with the patience of a woman who had buried a king and raised his daughter and outlived every man who'd told her how to do either. "Tasters don't resign. They retire to a house in the country with a pension and a watchman, or they're carried out. There's no third door."

"There's a third door."

"Then show me."

Paloma took a breath and laid it out plainly, the way you'd lay out dishes for inspection. "You know about the child. Don't insult us both—I watched your face at the solstice council when Corvane said the word *salt*. You know Issel is with me. You may not know why, yet, but you will within days, because Corvane is building his case brick by brick, and when he's done he'll bring it to you wrapped so neatly you'll have no choice but to act on it. So I'm here first. Turn me out. Strip my name from the household rolls. I'll take the child far from Vell tonight and you can tell the court your taster ran off with a foreign spy. My choice, my crime, my exile. The valley stays clean. Your regency stays clean."

"And in exchange for this generosity?"

"Nothing. That's the whole of it. Let the child go."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them the chapel bell began the quarter hour, and Paloma counted the strokes the way she counted everything now—against a deadline she could feel approaching like weather.

Maren didn't answer immediately. When she did, it wasn't to any of the things Paloma expected.

"Do you know how I came to Vell, taster?"

"No, Majesty."

"Nobody does. It's the one story this kingdom has never managed to invent for me." The Regent turned the chipped cup over in her hands. "I was nineteen. My father had backed the wrong claimant in a war two kingdoms away, and when the wrong claimant lost, we lost everything but our lives, and only because we ran. I crossed three borders in a winter. By the time I reached the valley of Vell I had frostbite in four toes and my brother was dead in a ditch outside Brast with a spear in him, and I swore—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "I swore I would never again be a woman who could be driven from anywhere. And then I married into this family, and the old king died, and here I am. Twenty-two years of regency. Nobody has driven me from anything."

"That's a good thing," Paloma said carefully. "It sounds like a good thing."

"It is the loneliest thing you can imagine." Maren set the cup down with a click. "Do you understand what Corvane is offering me? Not what he thinks he's offering—he thinks he's offering certainty, and certainty is worth nothing to a woman who reads people for a living. He's offering me a story. Magic leaking through the border. A foreign child. A foreign-blooded servant. An explanation for every strange thing that's started happening in my valley since the frost came early. Kings and regents run on stories, taster. Bread won't hold a kingdom. Fear of the wrong story will."

"His story is wrong."

"Is it?" For the first time something sharp came into the Regent's voice. "The well behind the temple wept salt last week. My stablemaster says the horses won't drink after dusk. Two nights ago my guards on the north wall heard singing with no singers, and three of them have asked to be relieved of duty since. I have ruled this valley by knowing what it is—a place where strange things stop at the border and die. If that's no longer true, then everything I've built for twenty-two years is standing on a floor that's gone soft, and I will want to know why, and I will want to know *now*, and Corvane is the only man in my household currently bringing me answers instead of rumors." She looked up. "So convince me his story is wrong. You've tasted a hundred poisons. Taste this one."

Paloma stood very still.

Here was the trap, and she saw it whole: the truth was fatal. Tell the Regent about Ostrava, about the door, about the solstice, about the Grey Choir fraying the valley's edges, and at best she'd have soldiers at the caves interfering with something they couldn't understand, and at worst she'd have a regent deciding that a sealed kingdom full of stasis-held survivors was a strategic asset. But lie to a woman who had survived a war on instinct alone, and Paloma would be dismissed from this room and from any chance of reaching her again.

So she gave her the truth sideways, the way you administer medicine in food.

"Your Majesty. When I taste a dish, I don't just find poison. I find intent. Arsenic in the soup means someone wants the duke dead slowly. Hemlock in the wine means haste. Every poison tells you what the poisoner fears, because people kill toward their fears, not away from them." She held the Regent's gaze. "Corvane isn't afraid of magic. He's afraid of being unnecessary. He built his life on being the man who knows what crosses borders, and right now something is crossing that he doesn't understand, and instead of saying *I don't know*—the one sentence he cannot survive saying—he's finding someone to blame. Blame him if you like. But ask yourself what happens to your floor if you pull up the boards he points at and find nothing underneath except a child who talks funny and a woman who's kept you alive for eleven years."

Silence. The candle burned. The Regent's face didn't move, but her thumb moved, rubbing the chip in the cup, around and around—the only tell Paloma had ever seen on her, and she filed it away the way she filed everything.

"The child," Maren said finally. "How old?"

"Seven. Maybe. They don't keep careful count where Issel comes from."

"Where do they come from?"

"You already know the answer to that, Majesty. You said the word yourself at council, before Corvane smoothed it over. You said it like you'd heard it before."

Something crossed the Regent's face then—quick, gone, but Paloma caught it, and it changed the shape of the room. Not surprise. Recognition. Grief, even. The expression of a woman hearing a song she'd danced to once, long ago, in a country that no longer appeared on maps.

"Get out of my head, taster," Maren said quietly.

"I'm only standing in your chapel."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then the Regent rose from the kneeler, and she seemed to grow as she stood, taking back inch by inch the height and hardness of the throne she hadn't sat on today, and when she spoke again her voice was pure court.

"Here is what I can do and what I can't. I can slow Corvane. I can bury his petitions in procedure—I've been burying ambitious men in procedure since before he had his first command, and he knows it, and it makes him careless. That buys you days. Perhaps a week. What I cannot do—" and here her voice caught on something, just barely, a splinter in smooth wood, "—is order you protected and the child taken, or the child protected and you taken, and pretend those are different choices. Because they aren't, and you know they aren't, and I did not become what I am by pretending."

"Majesty—"

"Eleven years." She cut across Paloma. "Eleven years you've eaten for my table. Do you know what that's worth? Not the gold—I mean the arithmetic of it. Every dish you survived was a plot that failed. Some of those plots would have killed more than a duke; poison doesn't read place cards. You have been the single quiet reason this court still has anyone left alive to scheme against each other, and I have never once thanked you, because thanks create witnesses and witnesses create debts." She paused. "Consider yourself thanked. Once. In a room with no witnesses."

It was the closest thing to love the crown had ever handed Paloma, and it nearly undid her. She thought of her grandmother's hands kneading dough, of Hedda's ledger with *Thackeray* written in a careful altered hand, of Issel asleep on her cot with salt still ghosting the roots of their hair. All the hidden kindnesses of hidden people, keeping each other alive in the dark.

"Then take one more debt, Majesty," Paloma said. "Not my resignation. My service. Whatever you need tasted, wherever you need me sent, however many years I owe—for the rest of my life, if you want it—in exchange for letting Issel go free. Not exiled. Free. Let the child stay in Vell, or let the child leave; let the child choose. And when Corvane comes to you with his neat little case, lose one document. Misfile one report. That's all. A queen of twenty-two years knows a hundred ways to lose a piece of paper."

The Regent laughed—one short syllable, startled out of her, gone as fast as it came.

"You'd bind yourself to this court for life," she said, "to save a child you met a fortnight ago."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And Paloma, who had lied for the first time in her life eight days ago and hated the taste of it, told the truth instead, because some audiences deserve it.

"Because somebody let my grandmother in. Somebody in this valley, a hundred years ago, looked at a frightened woman with a wrong accent and a recipe book and said *you can stay*. I exist because of a mercy I never got to thank. This is me thanking them."

The candle had burned down to a stub. Through the thick window the moon-smear had shifted; somewhere in the palace the night bell would ring soon, and with it the end of the forty minutes the Regent allowed herself to be human.

Maren picked up the chipped cup, considered it, and set it back down on the sill facing outward—a small domestic gesture, meaningless to anyone else, final as a signature.

"Go home, taster," she said. "Stay out of the fish market—you look like a woman planning a route, and planning routes is Corvane's trade, not yours. I will see what I can misplace." At the vestry door she stopped, her back to Paloma, and added, so softly Paloma almost missed it:

"My brother's name was Aldric. He died in a ditch outside Brast and no map remembers Brast anymore either. If your child's country is truly gone—" a pause, the length of a held breath, "—then I am sorry for it, and I was sorry fifty years ago, and I have never had anywhere to put that sorrow until tonight. Don't make me regret having somewhere to put it."

Then the vestry swallowed her, and Paloma stood alone in the chapel's spare room with a burnt-out candle and the wild, terrified hope that she might have just bought a week.

* * *

She walked home fast, hood up, taking the main streets deliberately—if the Regent was right about looking like a woman planning routes, the safest route was the obvious one—and she was three lanes from the cottage when she realized the streets were wrong.

Vell at night had a sound. Dogs, mostly, and the creak of the ferry rope across the river, and the distant argument of the tavern. Tonight there was none of it. No dogs. The tavern windows were dark, though it wasn't late. And the frost—

The frost had followed her home.

It lay along the gutters in a thin white line, precise as chalk, pointing the way she walked. Not ice. Salt. She knelt and touched it, and her fingers came away dry and humming, a faint vibration in the bones, like pressing a hand to a wall with music on the other side.

At the mouth of her lane, Bram the ferryman was waiting under the lamp with his coat buttoned wrong and his face grey.

"Paloma." He glanced up and down the empty street. "You need to come to the river. Now. There's something you should hear—or rather—" he wiped his mouth, and she realized his hand was shaking, "—there's something the river's been singing all night. And it's singing in your grandmother's tongue."

He turned toward the water, and Paloma followed, and behind them, in the gutter, the line of salt crept another inch.
