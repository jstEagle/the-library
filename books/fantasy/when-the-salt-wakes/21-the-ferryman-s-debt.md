## 21. The Ferryman's Debt

Bram Osk had been poling the underground river for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one rule that mattered: the dark down here is not empty. It listens.

So when a knock came at his door an hour past midnight—three soft raps, then two, then three again, the rhythm his grandmother used to knock—he set down his cup of nettle tea and sat very still.

Nobody knew that rhythm. He'd never told anyone. It was a thing between him and a dead woman, and dead women did not knock.

He crossed the room on bare feet, lifted the lantern, and opened the door a hand's width.

Paloma Almeida stood on his step with a child wrapped in her good shawl, and behind her, half in shadow, old Hedda with her herb-basket held like a shield.

"You knocked," Bram said. Foolish thing to say. But it was what he knew.

"I did," Paloma said. "I'm sorry to."

"Come in before the river hears you."

* * *

His boathouse sat at the mouth of the low tunnel where the Vell river sank beneath the hill and became something older and slower—the Undervein, his father had called it, and his father's father before him. Ferrying it was a family trade nobody envied: the court paid for silence, not service, and the passengers were few. Contraband mostly. Once, a fugitive. Twice, bodies the valley preferred not to bury above ground.

Bram poured tea into clay cups that didn't match because nothing in his house matched. The child watched him with eyes too calm for its size. Salt still ghosted the edges of its hair, white as frost, though Hedda had scrubbed at it twice.

"It doesn't talk?" he asked.

"Not in any tongue we use," Paloma said.

"It talked to me." The child had touched his sleeve when they came in—a small deliberate pressure—and something in his chest had gone strange and warm, like the first sip of strong drink. "In a way."

Hedda made a sound in her throat. "Then you understand why we're here."

"I understand why you're *here*." He looked at Paloma. "I don't yet understand what you want from *me*."

"The river," she said. "The long way. Under the hill to the salt caves."

The tea went cold in Bram's mouth. "That's a death route. The narrows under Greyfall, the sump at Tallow Bend, and past all that—" He shook his head. "Past all that, nobody goes. Not since my grandfather poled a party of six in and brought back one."

"One who owed him everything," Hedda said quietly.

Bram set down his cup. His hands weren't steady, so he folded them.

"My grandfather came back from the caves half-mad," he said. "Talking about a wall that hummed. A door in the rock. He said a woman pulled him out of the water after the boat went over—pulled him out like the current was afraid of her. She fed him. Fed him like it was a ceremony, dish by dish, seven dishes, and he swore afterward he could hear the river's whole length in his head, every stone, every bend. That's how he found his way out. That woman taught him the knock."

"And that woman," Hedda said, "was my mother."

The fire popped. The child looked up at the sound without flinching.

"No," Bram said slowly. "Your mother was Maren the herbalist."

"Maren Thackeray was the name she wore," Hedda said. "Names are coats, boy. You should know—you've worn yours your whole life without asking who tailored it." She leaned forward. "My mother was born across a border that no longer exists. And the woman who saved your grandfather, who fed him seven dishes in the dark—that was her sister. Your family's debt runs deeper than you know."

Bram looked at Paloma. She met his eyes the way she did everything: carefully, completely, holding nothing back and giving nothing away.

"You're her granddaughter," he said. "The taster."

"The taster," she agreed.

"They poison you for a living and call it employment."

"They do."

"And now you want me to take you and a salt-caked mute child down the death route, past the narrows, past the sump, to a door my grandfather saw once and never stopped dreaming about."

"Yes," said Paloma.

"Why?"

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Because someone has to hold the door open. And because I think the person who holds it has to be fed by the right hands first. My grandmother's book says so. And because—" her voice caught, just barely, the first crack he'd ever heard in it— "because I have spent my whole life being useful to people who would let me die if it suited them. I'd like, just once, to be useful to something that matters."

Bram stood. He walked to the window that wasn't a window, just a shutter over the tunnel mouth, and put his ear against the wood the way his grandfather had taught him.

The river was running high. He could hear it muttering in the dark, restless, the way it got before storms—or before changes.

"There's salt in the water this month," he said. "More than ever. My poles come up white. Fish gone from the lower pools. The river knows something's coming, even if the court doesn't."

"The Choir is coming," Hedda said. "You've heard the song at night."

"I've heard something at night." He turned back. "Singing. Wrong notes. Like a hymn played backwards through water." He rubbed his jaw. "Thirty-one years I've kept this ferry quiet and clean and none of anybody's business. I've moved smugglers and corpses and once a very embarrassed minor noble in his nightshirt. Never once asked why. That's how you survive in Vell: you don't ask."

"But?" Paloma said.

"But my grandfather died with that door on his lips. Called it his unfinished business. Made me swear when I was nine years old that if anyone ever came knocking with his grandmother's knock, I'd pole them wherever they needed going." He smiled, and it was a tired smile, worn smooth as an old oar handle. "I thought I'd made that oath to a madman. Turns out I made it to a prophet."

* * *

They loaded the boat by the last of the moon. Bram worked in silence while Paloma and Hedda carried bundles down the wet stone steps: oilcloth, lamps, dried fish, a heavy canvas sack that clinked—Hedda's herbs and glass vials, a traveling apothecary. The child sat in the stern and touched everything gently, as if memorizing it.

At the bottom of the sack, wrapped in waxed linen, Paloma carried her grandmother's recipe book. Bram noticed her hand rest on it a moment longer than the rest.

"That the famous book?"

"Famous to me."

"Grandfather said the woman who saved him cooked from memory. Seven dishes. Said each one tasted like a place he'd never been." He shrugged. "Probably nonsense."

"Probably not," Paloma said.

Bram took his place at the stern, long pole in hand. The tunnel mouth gaped before them, black and breathing. Cold air rolled out of it, carrying the mineral smell of deep stone and, faintly, unmistakably, salt.

"Rules of my river," he said. "One: no lights past the third bend—the glowworms spook, and when they go dark, things notice. Two: if I say flat, you get flat, no questions. Three—" he nodded at the child— "whatever it does, whatever sounds it makes, whatever sings back to it down there, we keep rowing. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Paloma said.

The child reached out and pressed its palm flat against the hull. Bram felt it through the wood—a pulse, faint, rhythmic, like a second heartbeat joining his own. The river, when he pushed off, seemed to take them willingly, the current easing around the hull instead of fighting it.

"Well," he muttered. "That's new."

They slid under the hill. The last grey light of the world fell away behind them, and the dark closed over the boat like a held breath.

For the first mile, Bram worked by feel alone, pole finding the channel markers his family had driven into the rock four generations ago—old iron rings, worn smooth, each one a promise: *the way is known, the way is kept.* The child hummed, very softly. Not a tune Bram knew. But the river seemed to know it; the water smoothed ahead of the bow, ripple by ripple, as though something vast below was listening and approving.

"Grandfather said the river used to sing," Bram offered, mostly to fill the dark. "Before the valley sealed itself. Said it sang in a language the stones remembered."

Beside him, the child went still.

Then, in a voice like water finding a crack, it answered—one word. Soft. Two syllables, rising, falling.

The river repeated it back.

Every stalactite overhead dripped the same two syllables. The current carried it forward and returned it changed, layered, harmonized—a thousand small voices of water saying a single word.

Bram's arms stopped moving. "What did it say?"

Paloma's voice came out of the dark beside him, hushed and certain: "*Stay.*"

The river said it again. *Stay. Stay. Stay.*

And far behind them, at the tunnel mouth, faint but unmistakable, something else began to sing—the wrong hymn, the backwards hymn, closer tonight than it had ever been.

Bram dug his pole in and pushed.

"Hold on to something," he said. "We're going to make good time."
