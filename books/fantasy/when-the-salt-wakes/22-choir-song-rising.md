## 22. Choir Song Rising

Hedda woke before dawn to a silence she had never heard in sixty years of valley mornings.

The birds were gone.

Not quiet—gone. The wrens that nested in her eaves every winter, the jackdaws that argued over the chimney of the tannery across the lane, the fat doves that bullied the churchyard—all of it, emptied out overnight, like water pulled from a basin. She stood in her doorway with her shawl clutched at her throat and listened to a Vell that had no voice in it at all. Only wind. Only the creak of her own sign, *HEDDA'S HERBALS*, swinging on its iron hook as though even the wood was nervous.

Then, far off toward the eastern hills, she heard it.

Not with her ears first. With her teeth. A hum that lived in the fillings and the bones, a note so low and so patient it seemed less like sound than like weather deciding what to become. She had heard it three nights ago, faintly, and told herself it was the river under the frost. Two nights ago it had words in it—or something shaped like words. Last night it had been singing in her garden while she slept, and when she'd gone out at dawn she had found every leaf of her sage silvered over, rimed white, as though the plant had dreamed of the sea.

Salt. Where no sea had ever been.

Now the song came again, and this time it did not stop, and this time everyone in Vell could hear it.

She watched them come out of their houses one by one—the cooper's wife in her nightgown, old Tomas the wheelwright barefoot in the frost he didn't seem to feel, the innkeeper's boy clutching a broom like a spear. They all stood in the lane with their heads tilted east, all wearing the same expression: the look of people listening to their own names being said wrong.

"That's not a wolf," Tomas said. His voice cracked in the middle. "That's not any animal I know."

"No," Hedda said. "It isn't."

She thought of Issel, small and salt-caked and silent for so long, and of what the child had finally spoken in that dialect older than the valley's stones. *The Grey Choir sings a country until it forgets itself.* And then she thought of Corvane's soldiers going door to door yesterday with their neat lists, and understood with a cold clarity that settled into her chest like a swallowed stone:

Vell had two dangers now, and only time to fight one of them well.

She went back inside and began pulling jars from her shelves. Whatever was coming, the neighbors would need courage, and courage, in Hedda's long experience, could be brewed.

* * *

Bram felt the river change before he saw anything wrong with it.

He had poled the ferry between the mill landing and the town quay for thirty years, and a man who works a river that long learns its moods the way he learns his own pulse. The Vell ran cold and quick and honest; it never lied about its depth, never sulked, never froze except in the hard weeks around solstice, and never—not once in thirty years—tasted of salt.

This morning, dipping his oar, he tasted salt.

He knelt and put two fingers in the current and touched them to his tongue, an old habit from his grandmother, who had been a fisherwoman and trusted nothing she couldn't taste. Salt. Faint, but unmistakable, the mineral tang of tide-flat and sea-cave, rising up from somewhere beneath the riverbed as if the water were passing over ground it had no business passing over.

And the fish were gone too. The grayling that usually schooled in the eddy below the landing—hundreds of them, visible from the bank on a clear morning like coins scattered in green glass—had vanished overnight. Not dead. Gone. As if something downstream had asked them a question and they had decided, unanimously, to be elsewhere.

"River's leaving," said a voice behind him, and there was the baker, Marta, flour still on her forearms despite the hour, standing on his dock with her basket hanging forgotten at her side. "My oven wouldn't light this morning. Three tries. The wood just—wouldn't take the flame. And my starter's gone flat. Dead flat. Twelve years that starter's been alive."

"Mine too," said the tanner's daughter, arriving at the quay with her buckets untouched. "The hides won't cure. The liquor's gone inert, like boiled water."

They stood there, the three of them, in a silence broken only by that low humming from the east, which by now was no longer deniable. People were gathering along the banks in knots of twos and fives, pointing up-valley, talking in the hushed urgent voices of folk who have agreed not to say the frightening thing aloud.

"It's the solstice coming," someone offered. "It's always strange near solstice."

"It's never been *this*," Marta said.

Bram looked down at his hands on the pole. He was thinking of a debt thirty years old—a night when a young woman named Thackeray had dragged him, half-drowned and fevered, out of this same river after the flood took his boat, and had sat up with him till dawn with willow bark and bad wine, asking nothing, telling him only once, quietly, where she had come from before Vell. He had promised himself then that if she or hers ever needed the river, the river would answer.

It looked very much like the river was about to be needed.

"The song," the tanner's daughter said suddenly. "Listen. It's louder than yesterday."

It was. Everyone heard it now; that was the horror of it. For weeks it had belonged to the sensitive ones—to Hedda, to the dogs, to Issel—and the rest of Vell had been free to disbelieve. But this morning the Choir's note had crossed some threshold, and now it sat inside every skull in the valley alike, patient and enormous, a hymn sung to a congregation that hadn't agreed to attend.

And underneath the note, if you held still and let your mind unclench, you could almost catch the words. Not Ostravan. Not any language. Just a name, repeated, over and over, with the tenderness of a lullaby and the precision of an executioner checking a blade.

*Vell. Vell. Vell.*

Except slightly wrong each time. A half-tone off. A syllable missing. Sung the way a stranger says your name right before they take something of yours.

"My dog wouldn't come indoors last night," Marta said quietly. "Sat facing east all night, hackles up, and at dawn she was gone. Ran. They're all running. Did you see? Every farm on the east road has empty pens. The geese went through Millbrook at midnight like a white river flying."

"Animals know," Bram said. He pushed off from the landing without quite deciding to. "Get your bread baked, Marta. Get everything baked. People are going to need feeding in the next few days, and I don't think it'll be a small number." Then, because she was staring at him: "I've got a delivery to make on the west bank."

There was no delivery. There was a cottage with a taster and a child in it, and a promise thirty years old, and a river that still, whatever else it had become, ran honest.

* * *

In the castle above the town, the Queen Regent stood at her window and did not sleep, because sleep had become impossible.

The song reached even here, threaded through the stone. Her guards reported that the horses in the royal stables had kicked two doors to splinters in the night and would not be calmed; that the falcons had broken from the mews and vanished northward; that the kennel master found every hound gone from its chain at dawn, collars lying in neat rows like shed skins. Animals did not desert a place lightly. Animals deserted a place when the place had stopped being a place.

"Your Grace." Corvane stood in the doorway, and for the first time since she'd known him, the spymaster looked tired in a way that wasn't performance. His maps were under his arm. "You've heard it."

"The whole valley has heard it. Half the valley is standing in the streets listening to it." She turned from the window. "Tell me you have more than theories today, Corvane. Tell me you have a name for what's at our border."

"I have confirmation." He spread the maps across her table, weighting the corners with candlesticks. "My riders reached the eastern ridge at moonset. They got as far as the old boundary stones." He paused, and the pause told her everything before his words did. "The stones are frosted. White to the height of a man. Salt, Your Grace—salt blooming out of bare rock in hard frost, in a valley that has never seen the sea. And beyond them—" he tapped the empty parchment space past the valley's edge, where cartographers for generations had simply written *here the world grows uncertain* —"beyond them there are figures. Standing in the snow. Singing."

"How many?"

"They couldn't count. The riders said the figures seemed to be more numerous the longer they looked. They didn't stay to verify." He straightened. "One more thing. The song—it's audible inside the border now. Yesterday it wasn't. Whatever holds this valley sealed against such things is thinning. My working theory stands confirmed: the leak is real, it's widening, and it began in the lower town. In a cottage on Tanners' Row."

The Regent was quiet a moment. Outside, the song hummed through the glass like a finger run round the rim of a cup.

"And the child?" she asked.

"Gone to ground. The herbalist's apprentice swears she hasn't seen them; the ferryman claims no crossings; the baker knows nothing. Which means everyone knows something." He gathered his maps. "Your Grace, I've given the taster three days. One remains. If she doesn't surrender the child by tomorrow evening, I take soldiers to Tanners' Row myself."

"You believe the child caused this."

"I believe the child is the key to it. Whether cause or cure, I can't yet say. But I know this much"—and here Corvane's voice dropped, and something old and grieving moved behind his eyes—"I have read every account of every land the Grey Choir has ever erased. They do not stop because a valley is stubborn. They stop because the door they follow is closed. And there is a door open in my valley, Regent, and it is singing us goodbye."

When he had gone, the Regent remained at the window a long while, watching the frost creep visibly up the eastern hills like a tide with nowhere better to be. She had come to this valley herself, once, years ago, across a border of her own, carrying nothing and belonging nowhere. She remembered what it was to be a name sung wrongly.

She did not send for Corvane to countermand him.

But she did not sleep either.

* * *

Paloma heard the animals leave.

She was awake anyway—she had been sleeping badly since the caves, since the humming wall, since Issel's confession that a door needs a key on both sides—when the geese went over. Thousands of them, a ragged white river against the pre-dawn dark, streaming west over the rooftops of Tanners' Row, crying in voices that sounded less like migration than like evacuation. After them, foxes trotted down the middle of the lane in broad view of every window, unafraid, purposeful, like refugees with somewhere already arranged. The goats from the common broke a fence and flowed past her gate without sparing a glance at the garden they usually raided.

Issel stood beside her at the window, small hand in hers, watching.

"They're going the right way," the child said. In Ostravan, then, remembering, in Paloma's grandmother's flat valley vowels: "Away from the song."

"Do you hear it stronger today?"

Issel nodded. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "It knows the valley's name now. Before, it only knew the door. That's why the animals left. When the Choir learns a name, everything living starts to feel like a guest." A pause. "Guests leave before the house comes down."

Paloma's arms prickled. Outside, the frost was doing something frost should not do: it was moving. She could see it on the cobbles, a fine white rime advancing up Tanners' Row from the east end in a slow visible tide, silvering doorsteps one by one, climbing the walls of the tannery, reaching for her own gate. Not cold-frost. Salt-frost. She knew the glitter of it now, the way it caught light like crushed pearl.

By midmorning the whole street had come out to look at it, and by noon the whole town had heard why.

Marta the baker stood in the square and told them. All of it—the caves, the wall that hummed, the door, the exiles, the Choir. She told it plainly, in a baker's voice, with flour still on her sleeves, and Paloma, watching from the crowd's edge with Issel's hand gripping hers like a vice, understood that Marta was spending her own safety to spend theirs. A woman who tells a frightened town an unbelievable truth makes herself its first suspect.

But the town believed her. That was the terrible gift of the song: nobody who had spent the night listening to a name being sung wrong needed convincing that the world had tilted.

"So we hide them," said the tanner's daughter, loud enough for all. "The taster and the child. We hide them the way our grandparents hid each other, and we hold the line till solstice."

"And if hiding fails?" someone called.

"Then we stand in front of the door ourselves," said old Tomas, "and we sing it *right*. Our valley's name, sung proper, till the frost gets tired of listening."

A rough cheer went up, thin and scared and real. Paloma felt Issel press closer against her side and felt, beneath her fear, a thing she had no leisure to examine: that in thirty-one years of careful invisibility, she had never once stood in a crowd that was willing to stand for her.

That evening, Bram's ferry crossed the river bearing no passengers, only crates—bread, salt pork, candles, willow bark, a jar of Hedda's courage-brew—and a chalked message on the top crate in the ferryman's blunt hand:

*River runs salty and the fish are wise. West bank landing, tomorrow, full dark. Bring the child. Bring the book. — B.*

Paloma read it twice, then folded it into the pocket where her grandmother's recipe book already lay, its coded pages warm as a heartbeat against her hip.

From the east, low and patient, the Choir sang Vell's name again—wrong, wrong, wrong—and the salt frost crept another yard up the lane, and somewhere beyond the hills, a door made of light waited for the solstice with its hand on the latch.

Two days.

Paloma banked the fire, woke Issel gently, and began, in the oldest language she knew, to pack.
