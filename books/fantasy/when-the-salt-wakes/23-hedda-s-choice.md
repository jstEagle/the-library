## 23. Hedda's Choice

The frost came to Hedda's window in the shape of a hand.

She noticed it at dawn, while the kettle was still warming on the hob—a five-fingered bloom of white salt creeping up the glass from the sill, delicate as fernwork, wrong as a scream. Outside, her garden of bitterleaf and feverfew wore a dusting of white that was not snow. Snow melted when you breathed on it. This did not. She pressed one fingertip to the pane and the salt sang back at her, a thin high note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass.

Hedda had been born outside the valley. She knew what salt meant when it arrived uninvited.

"Three days," she said to the empty kitchen, because saying it aloud made it arithmetic instead of dread. "Maybe four."

Corvane had given Paloma three days. Corvane's soldiers had been asking questions in the market for two. The Grey Choir had been singing at the border for longer than anyone wanted to admit—the ferryman's horses refused the river road now, and the baker's boy swore his dreams had words in them he couldn't understand, syllables like teeth.

Hedda put water on for tea she would not drink, and began, with the unhurried competence of sixty years, to prepare a lie.

* * *

Her shop smelled the way it always did: dried nettle, beeswax, camphor, the ghost of every tincture she'd ever boiled too hot. Customers said walking into Hedda's was like walking into a memory of being sick and getting better. She liked that. A good lie needed a true smell around it.

She spent the morning moving things.

Not hiding—moving. There was a difference, and Corvane would know the difference, because Corvane had been a spymaster long enough to understand that an innocent woman hides nothing and a guilty woman hides everything, so the trick was to be guilty in plain sight about the wrong things. She took down her ledger of refugee families and left it sitting open on the counter where any searching officer would find it in minutes. Let them read the names. Let them find Thackeray, altered from Ostrava's tongue. That much she could not protect anymore anyway; Paloma knew what she was now, and knowing could not be taken back into the body like a swallowed secret.

But the false trail—that she built with love.

In the back room, beneath the floorboard she'd pried up twice a year for forty years out of pure habit, Hedda kept three things: a jar of valley salt, a bundle of letters from her mother written in a language the valley had no name for, and a map. Not a real map. A map drawn by a frightened girl with a charcoal stub, showing a route through the hills east of Vell, toward a pass that didn't exist, marked with symbols she'd invented herself at eleven years old and never told another living soul.

It was the only lie she'd ever kept, and she'd kept it so long it had grown roots. Now she dug it up and laid it flat on her worktable and studied it with her reading spectacles pinching her nose, and thought: *this will do. This will do beautifully.*

A clever man like Corvane would check the obvious places first—the caves beneath the valley, the underground river, Bram's ferry. But when the obvious places yielded nothing but cold stone, he would come here, to the herbalist who'd admitted to foreign blood, and he would search her house, and he would find a child's map pointing east, and something in him—the part of him that had also lost a country, the part she'd seen flicker behind his careful face when he spoke of vanished lands—would want the east to be true. Wanting makes men credulous. She had sold herbs to wanting men for six decades.

She rolled the map, tied it with twine, and tucked it into the loose brick behind the stove where she kept the money for the tax collector. Visible to anyone who pulled the brick. Invisible to anyone who didn't think to.

Then she sat down at her table, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and wrote two letters.

The first was short, addressed to no one, and would go into the fire within the hour: *If you are reading this, I failed, and I am sorry, and the fault is mine alone.* Some letters you write just to know what you would say if you had the courage to say it.

The second took longer. It was addressed to Paloma, and it was the hardest thing Hedda had written since her mother's death-notice, thirty years ago, in a language no priest in Vell could read over the grave.

* * *

Paloma came at dusk, hood up, moving through the lanes the way she moved through the court—invisible by craft rather than nature, which is the better kind. Issel came with her, wrapped in a borrowed coat, one small salt-crusted hand gripping Paloma's sleeve. The child looked at Hedda's salt-frosted window with an expression far too old for six or seven years old, and said something soft in the dead language, and Hedda understood every word, because her mother had sung the same words over cradles before Ostrava stopped existing.

"She says the white is getting closer," Paloma translated, though Hedda hadn't asked.

"I know what she says." Hedda stood aside. "Come in before the lane does."

Inside, she fed them—barley soup, brown bread, honey she'd been saving for an occasion and decided this qualified—and watched Paloma eat like a woman who had learned hunger young and never fully unlearned it. Issel ate with both hands and total seriousness, and between bites taught Hedda the word for spoon, tapping the wooden spoon twice against the bowl's rim. *Sessu.* Hedda repeated it, and the child's whole face opened like a door.

That was the moment she nearly lost her nerve.

It would have been easy, an hour earlier, to be brave in the abstract. It was not easy with the child across the table, tapping spoons.

"You've been to see Bram?" Hedda asked Paloma.

"He'll take us tomorrow night. The river runs under the eastern ridge; the recipe-map shows where it passes closest to the caves." Paloma turned her cup, turned it again. "You're not coming."

It wasn't a question, which meant Paloma had already guessed, which meant Paloma knew her, which made this both easier and infinitely harder. "I'm not coming."

"The Regent—"

"The Regent has known my face since I set her father's broken arm with comfrey and stubbornness. I'm the last person who should be standing next to a humming wall when soldiers arrive." Hedda kept her voice level, the voice she used for delivering bad prognoses. "And someone has to stay behind and be found."

Paloma went very still. "Found doing what?"

"Being guilty." Hedda smiled, and knew it was a bad smile, and gave it anyway. "I've been preparing it all day. There's a map in my house, Paloma. A child's map, pointing east through the hills. False as a fair promise. When Corvane comes—and he'll come tomorrow, or the day after—he'll tear this place apart, and he'll find it, and while he's chasing a pass that doesn't exist, you'll be under the ground with your grandmother's book and the child."

"And when he realizes it's false?"

"By then the solstice will have come and gone." She reached across the table and covered Paloma's hand, the way she'd done when setting bones, firm and brief. "Or it won't have. Either way, I won't know until they let me know, and there's a certain peace in that."

"That's prison, Hedda."

"That's a room and meals and time to finish cataloguing my tinctures, which I have been meaning to do for twenty years." She squeezed once and let go. "I'm seventy-one years old, girl. I was born outside the valley and carried in over my mother's shoulder, and every single day since, I have lived carefully. Careful food, careful words, careful friendships, careful silences. Do you know what I've learned about care?"

Paloma shook her head. Issel watched them both, still as a held breath.

"It keeps you alive," Hedda said, "and it costs you everything else. I have been safe for sixty years. I would like, once, before the end of things, to be useful instead."

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. Paloma argued the way she tasted—methodically, finding every flaw, refusing to swallow anything unproven—but Hedda had been dismantling other people's certainties since before Paloma's mother was born, and eventually the argument ran out of floor.

What broke it wasn't argument. It was the ledger.

Hedda brought it out and set it between them, open to the page with *Thackeray* written in faded ink above a crossed-out older word. "Your grandmother's name, before this valley required a new one. And here—" she turned pages, "—my family. And Bram's people, two generations back, though he doesn't know it; his grandfather burned their papers to keep the ferry license. Half this valley is descended from people who ran from something, and agreed to forget it, and called the forgetting peace." She tapped the page. "This book is why the Regent can't ever quite throw us out, whatever Corvane whispers. We're not guests in Vell. We're its foundation, and everyone knows it and no one says it. That's worth protecting. But it isn't worth more than a child."

Issel reached out and touched the crossed-out name, tracing it with one finger, and said a word—two syllables, round and liquid—and looked up at Paloma.

"What did she say?"

"She said that was her great-grandmother's name," Paloma whispered. "Before the Choir. She says—" Paloma's voice caught, steadied. "She says the names are how we survive them. Even changed ones. Especially changed ones."

"There," Hedda said roughly, closing the ledger. "There's your inheritance, Paloma Almeida Thackeray. Not the blood. The blood is just the door's opinion of you. The inheritance is this—" she gestured at the table, the soup, the child, the ledger, the whole shabby warm room, "—what you carry forward. What you feed people. What you refuse to hand over. Your grandmother carried recipes across a border that kills magic, and hid a kingdom in the margins of a cookbook, and fed a valley for forty years without one soul knowing she was feeding it a homeland too. That's not blood. That's choice. You get to choose it every day, starting tomorrow, and tomorrow you choose it by walking into those caves and opening a door, and I choose it by staying here and lying beautifully to a dangerous man."

Paloma cried a little. She tried to hide it, tasting-habit, swallowing everything, but Hedda had spent a lifetime reading bodies and let her pretend for exactly as long as she needed to, then passed her the honey jar because some tears you sweeten rather than stop.

* * *

Corvane came the following afternoon, and Hedda was ready.

She heard the horses first—she always heard the horses; the lane announced visitors whether it wanted to or not—and by the time his knock came she had flour on her hands from bread she had genuinely been making, because the best props were the ones you used anyway. Two soldiers stayed visible at the gate, which was theater; there were certainly others she couldn't see, which was fact.

"Hedda Marsh," Corvane said, removing his hat in her doorway like a man visiting an aunt. He had kind eyes the way a hook has a worm. "May I come in? The Regent worries about you. An old woman alone, in weather like this."

"The Regent worries about nothing except her own reflection, but come in, it's cold."

He laughed. He was good at laughing. He surveyed her shop slowly, letting his gaze rest on the open ledger on the counter—she saw him register it, file it, decide to circle back—and accepted tea he wouldn't drink, and asked gentle questions about her health, her suppliers, the price of feverfew, and then, folding his hands:

"You've had visitors lately."

"Everyone has visitors. It's a shop."

"Peculiar visitors." His voice stayed pleasant as milk. "A court taster, several times. A child no one in the valley has ever seen before. Salt on your windowsill that isn't from any sea." He leaned forward slightly. "Hedda. I don't believe you're a traitor. I believe you're a historian who hasn't admitted it. There's a difference, and the difference matters enormously to me right now. Tell me where they've gone, and the ledger stays a curiosity instead of evidence."

And here was the moment. Hedda felt it open under her like ice underfoot, and she stepped onto it deliberately.

"They haven't gone anywhere," she said. "The child was sick. I treated her. Paloma brought her because the girl trusts me more than she trusts the court physicians, and God knows the feeling's mutual. They've gone home." She met his eyes and lied with sixty years of craft. "But you're right that there's something I haven't shown the court. Something older than the Regent's grandmother. Would you like to see it?"

Curiosity, meet hook. She watched it set behind his eyes.

He followed her to the stove. He pulled the loose brick himself, gloved fingers quick, and drew out the roll of twine-bound paper, and unrolled a child's map of a pass that did not exist, drawn in charcoal by a homesick eleven-year-old fifty-nine years ago, and Hedda watched his face do exactly what she'd predicted: flicker with recognition, ache with want, harden into purpose.

"This is real," he said quietly. "This is a route out of the valley."

"It's a route my mother believed in," Hedda said, which was even true. "East, through the hills. She died looking for it. So did half the names in that ledger, one way or another. You want magic leaking into Vell, Spymaster? Go east. Go look. Take your soldiers and your instruments and your theories." She folded her floury hands. "And leave a tired old herbalist to her bread."

He stood there a long moment, the map in his hands, the fire ticking, and for one unguarded instant he looked less like a hunter than like a man being handed a letter from someone he'd buried. Then the shutters came down, polite and total.

"Thank you, Hedda Marsh," he said. "For your honesty."

"For my honesty," she agreed.

They arrested her at dusk anyway. Of course they did. She'd counted on it—arrest was part of the architecture; a freed witness is a rumor, an imprisoned one is a verified source, and Corvane trusted verification the way other men trusted God. The soldiers were almost apologetic. The cell, when she saw it, had a window facing west, clean straw, and a door that locked with a sound like a full stop.

Hedda sat down on the cot, arranged her shawl, and permitted herself exactly one minute of fear—hands shaking, breath short, heart hammering like a girl's—because courage that never admits cost is just ignorance wearing armor.

Then the minute ended, and she began, silently, to catalogue the cell the way she'd catalogue a new root: dimensions, light, sounds, guard rotation, the seam in the mortar by the window that a hairpin could worry loose in a week, if a week were granted.

Out loud, to the dark, in the language the valley had no name for, she said: *"Hold, little door. Help is coming under the ground."*

And far away, she somehow knew, under the eastern ridge where no pass existed, a boat was loading in the dark.
