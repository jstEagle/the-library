## 24. Underground River

**Paloma**

The river under Vell did not exist on any map, which was fitting, because neither did the people on it.

Bram's boat sat low in the black water, and the water sat low in the world, and above us all lay forty feet of salt and stone and the whole sleeping valley that had never once thought to wonder what it was resting on. I had lived here twenty-nine years. I had eaten my way through every suspicion this court could brew, and I had never known there was a river running beneath my feet like a vein beneath skin.

"Sit still," Bram said, though he said it gently, the way you say things to people who have already been still for an hour.

I was still. It was Issel who couldn't manage it. The child knelt at the bow with both hands trailing over the side, watching the water, and every few minutes turned to look at me with that expression — not a question exactly, more like the space where a question would go if questions worked that way.

We were three hours into the journey and the lanterns had begun to matter less.

That was the thing nobody warned you about the deep salt caves: they glowed. Not firelight-glow. Something older and quieter, a pale gold light that came out of the walls themselves, out of the crystal seams, so that the cavern we drifted through looked like the inside of a lamp lit from behind its own skin. Bram called it *the river's candles* and refused to explain further, which was very Bram.

Issel saw it first, of course. Children see things first; their eyes haven't been taught to look away. The child made a sound — not a word, just a note, low and wondering — and pointed, and I followed the finger up the wall to where the salt grew in great feathered plumes, translucent, lit from within, and for one long moment I forgot to be afraid.

Then I remembered the solstice. Four days. And I stopped forgetting again, mostly.

"Grandmother," I said quietly, testing the word against the dark. "Your grandmother." It was the phrase we'd built between us over weeks, our bridge-language, half Ostravan, half valley-speech, half hands. "Did she know these caves?"

Issel considered me with those too-old eyes. Then the small hands began to move.

*Old ones knew,* the hands said. *Old ones went down when the Choir sang. Down is safe. Down is quiet.*

"And then?" I asked, though I already knew. Everyone in Ostrava had known, once, what came after down. There was only one thing at the bottom of the world worth sealing yourself behind.

Issel's hands stilled. The child looked at the water instead of at me.

I let it go. You learn that as a taster — when to swallow a question whole and carry it, undigested, until your stomach is ready.

Bram poled us around a bend where the ceiling dropped close enough that I could have touched it, and the light thickened until we were moving through gold, and I thought: *if the door opens fully, if everything Hedda said is true and the way through exists — would I go?*

I didn't know yet. That was the honest answer, and honesty has always been the one luxury my life allowed me. I held the question the way you hold a hot dish: carefully, by its edges, waiting to find out what it was going to cost.

* * *

**Issel**

The water talks.

Not with words. With the sound it makes against the boat, which is the same sound as the inside of a shell, which is the same sound as the last night in Ostrava before the door closed, everyone whispering in the amber streets, *hush now, hush now, the key is going through.* Paloma doesn't hear it. Or maybe she hears it and calls it something else, the way big people call the same rain by ten different names depending on whether they're farmers or sailors.

The glowing walls are right, though. They're *right* the way bread smells when someone who loves you makes it. The old ones' songs talk about the shining roads under the earth, the salt that keeps memory the way wax keeps a seal. I press my hand flat against the boat's edge and trail my fingers in the water and imagine I can feel the songs coming up through it, faint as heat off a stone after sunset.

Paloma watches me. She watches everything. That is her whole shape, I've decided — a person made entirely of watching, and tasting, and keeping other people alive without telling them. In the cottage she sits where she can see the door. At court she stands where she can see everyone's hands. She thinks I don't notice. But I notice. Noticing is the only thing I have left, since the words went away, and I guard it like the old ones guarded the amber city.

She asks if her grandmother knew these caves.

Her grandmother. When she says that word — *grandmother* — her face does something complicated, like a door opening onto a room that hasn't been aired in years. My own grandmother put me in the boat. My grandmother kissed my forehead with lips that tasted of salt and said the words, the key-words, and pushed, and the light took me, and I woke up in a valley where no magic could follow and nothing could follow, not even the thing that chased us.

*Down is safe. Down is quiet.* That's what I told her. It's true. What I didn't tell her is the rest of it, the part sitting in my chest like a stone I swallowed:

A door needs a key on both sides.

I look back at Paloma. She's holding the lantern even though the cave gives its own light — she holds it anyway, out of habit, the way she tastes food she already knows is poisoned. Just in case. She's a just-in-case person. Maybe that's why the old songs would like her.

Maybe that's why I'm afraid to love her, and do it anyway.

The boat goes around a bend. The river gets louder. Somewhere ahead, the water drops its voice to a whisper, and I know — the way I knew the lullaby, the way I knew her door — that we are getting close to the place where the world keeps its hinges.

* * *

**Paloma**

We ate in the boat. There was no stopping; Bram said the current wouldn't wait and the current, apparently, outranks everyone.

So I unpacked the food I'd wrapped at dawn — brown bread, hard cheese, apples, and the little jar of honey Issel had become devoted to with the single-mindedness of childhood — and we passed things hand to hand while the golden walls slid past. Eating in a moving boat is undignified. Cheese crumbles; honey finds fingers. Issel laughed twice, actual laughter, silent-shouldered, and each time I felt it land somewhere under my ribs like a coal.

"You'll ruin your appetite for the feast," I said, and then heard myself, and stopped.

Because there wasn't going to be a feast. There was going to be a wall that hummed, and a solstice, and whatever came through it. I'd been cooking sentences for a future that might not exist, the way my grandmother must have cooked recipes for a country that didn't anymore. Recipes as maps. Recipes as promises. *This is how we ate when the world was whole.*

"What?" Issel signed, catching my pause the way children catch everything.

"Nothing," I lied, softly, and gave the child the bigger apple half.

But the question I'd been carrying all day wouldn't stay swallowed any longer. The cave-light made everything feel confessional; even the river seemed to lean in.

"Issel." I kept my voice low. Bram was twenty feet back at the stern, poling, politely deaf the way ferrymen are contractually obligated to be. "If the door opened — all the way. If you could walk through and it stayed open. Would you go home?"

The child's hands hovered. Then: *Yes.*

No hesitation. No cruelty either — just truth, delivered clean, the way children deliver everything. My chest did something complicated around the word.

"And if I walked through with you," I said. "Into Ostrava. Into the amber city, or wherever the door leads. Could I live there? A woman whose blood is half-right, whose tongue is borrowed from a dead woman's lullabies. Would that country have me?"

Issel studied me for a long time. The glow moved on the child's face like weather.

Then the small hands began, slow and deliberate, choosing each shape with care:

*The door asks one question. Not "are you strong." Not "are you pure." It asks:* ***will you feed someone?*** *If yes — the country has you. That is the oldest law. Older than kings. Older than the Choir.*

I blinked. "Feed someone," I repeated. "That's the test?"

Issel nodded, solemn as a magistrate, and then ruined it completely by pointing at the honey jar with unmistakable meaning, and I laughed — really laughed, for the first time in longer than I could measure, my laugh bouncing off the salt feathers overhead and coming back strange and golden.

*Will you feed someone.* Twenty-nine years I'd spent tasting other people's food so they wouldn't die. Perhaps my whole life had been an answer to a question I hadn't been asked yet.

* * *

**Issel**

She laughed. I made her laugh.

It echoed in the cave and came back sounding like more people than us, like a hall full of laughing, and for a moment I pretended that was true — that we were in the amber city, in the long supper-hall, and all the laughing was everyone, all the ones in the boats and the ones behind the door, and nobody was missing.

Then it faded, and it was just the two of us and the quiet man with the pole, and the stone came back into my chest where I keep the secret.

I almost told her. On the water, with the gold light, with honey on my fingers — I almost told her. My hands lifted halfway and stopped, because the words for it are heavy and my hands get tired, and because of the other reason, the real reason:

If I tell her, she will try to fix it. Big people try to fix things. She'll stand in front of the door and argue with it, argue with the law older than kings, and doors don't argue, doors just close, and then maybe nobody gets through, and then I did the bad thing instead of the sad thing.

Sad is survivable. Bad isn't.

So I ate my apple and watched her laugh-fade settle back into her watchful face, and I put the secret deeper, under the stone, under the water-sound, in the place where I keep the night my grandmother's lips tasted of salt.

But the river was carrying us toward the hinge-place, and hinges don't wait, and I think the secret knows it's running out of room.

* * *

**Paloma**

The river narrowed. The glow deepened from gold toward something warmer, almost flesh-colored, and the air changed — thicker, sweeter, tasting faintly of minerals and something else, something organic and old, like the breath of a room long shut.

Bram shipped his pole. "Current takes us from here," he said, and his voice was different now, lower, the voice he probably used at gravesides. He crossed himself with the wrong hand — the old way, the pre-church way — and I pretended not to see, because pretending not to see is half my profession.

"How far?" I asked.

"To the humming place? Close now." He glanced ahead, and I followed his eyes, and my stomach tightened.

Ahead, the cavern opened out and the river slowed and spread, and far across the still water stood a wall. Even at this distance I could feel it — not hear it exactly, more like feeling it in my back teeth, in the bones behind my ears. A vibration. A patience. The way a struck bell feels in the seconds before the sound arrives.

Beside me, Issel had gone rigid. The child's hands were pressed flat to the boat's boards, and the glow of the cavern seemed to bend slightly toward the small figure, the way grass bends toward light.

"It knows you're here," Bram murmured. "Been humming since sundown yesterday, the old-timers say. Since the child crossed the border, some say louder." He shook his head. "My grandmother poled refugees down this river in the famine year. Never told a soul where to. I used to think she was ashamed." His jaw worked. "Now I think she was keeping the address."

"Bram," I said. "Why are you doing this? Really."

He was quiet for a long stretch of water. The humming grew.

"Your grandmother pulled me out of a flooded well when I was nine," he said finally. "Held me underwater half a minute getting me free of the grate, near drowned me herself, and when she hauled me up she fed me bread and honey like it was a coronation feast. Said, *no one passes my table unfed.*" He looked at me, and his eyes were wet and steady. "I've owed that meal thirty years. Tonight I pay it forward."

I couldn't speak. Some debts you can't acknowledge without breaking.

The boat drifted on. Closer. The wall resolved out of the gloom — vast, seamless, salt-white shot through with veins of that inner light — and carved across its face, rank upon rank, letters older than any alphabet I knew, and among them, unmistakable, the shapes from my grandmother's margins.

And the wall was not humming steadily anymore.

The hum was changing. Rising. Finding a rhythm — and it took me a shameful ten seconds to recognize what the rhythm was, because I'd known it all my life, sung to sleep by it, and had never once understood the words.

The wall was singing the lullaby.

Beside me, Issel stood up in the boat, small and straight and trembling, and raised one hand toward the singing stone — and the song stopped.

Mid-note. Dead silence across the whole black water.

Then, from the heart of the wall, in a voice like a thousand grains of salt shifting at once, in the language of a kingdom erased from every map — it spoke a single word.

It was a question.

And I understood it.
