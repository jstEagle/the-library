## 26. Issel's Secret

The boat rocks. The river underneath is slow and green-black and it makes a sound like someone breathing in a room where everyone else has fallen asleep.

Issel knows the sound. Back home — back *inside* — the whole city breathed like that at evening, when the amber light came down through the towers and every window went the color of honey left too long in the sun. Mama used to say the city was sleeping. Papa said the city was listening.

Issel never decided which one was true.

Paloma sits across from them in the boat, her knees almost touching theirs, because the boat is small and Bram built it that way on purpose, so nothing could shift without both of you feeling it. Her hands rest on the oars even though the river pulls them along by itself now. She keeps doing that. Holding things she doesn't need to hold. Issel has watched her do it with cups, with doors, with the strap of her bag. As if letting go is a skill nobody ever taught her.

"Are you warm enough?" Paloma asks. She says it in Vellish first, then tries the Ostravan words after, careful and round-mouthed, like carrying water in her hands. *"Su-mel, issa?"*

Warm enough. Yes. The caves are not cold the way winter outside was cold. They are cold like a cellar, like a stone step in the morning, cold that holds you instead of taking from you. The walls glow faintly where the salt catches the boat-lamp's light — pale gold, then rose when Paloma moves the lamp to look at something, then gold again. Everything here is the color of the inside of a shell.

Issel nods. Then, because nodding is a small thing and what lives inside their chest right now is an enormous thing, they say: "I have to tell you something."

Their voice comes out strange in the cavern. Small sounds travel far down here. A drip from the ceiling lands somewhere behind them and sounds like a footstep.

Paloma goes still. Not frightened-still. Taster-still. Issel has learned the difference. Frightened people freeze all over, eyes wide, breath held high in the chest. Paloma freezes like a person listening to a soup, waiting for the one wrong flavor to show itself. Her face stays soft. Only her stillness sharpens.

"All right," she says.

* * *

Here is what Issel knows, and how they know it:

They know it the way they know how to swallow. Nobody taught them. It was just already there, the way the lullaby was already there in Paloma's mouth before she knew its name.

The door needs two keys. That is the first thing. Everyone thinks a door needs one key, because most doors are made by people who want to keep things out. But the oldest doors aren't made to keep anything out. They're made to hold something safe. And a thing that holds something safe cannot be opened from only one side, or it isn't holding anything at all — anyone could come in, anyone could take it. So the makers of the old doors split the key in half. One half stays outside. One half goes inside, into the thing being kept safe, and becomes part of it. Lives with it. Grows up with it. Sleeps in its beds and eats at its tables and forgets, sometimes, on purpose, that it is a key at all.

The half inside cannot leave. If it passes through the door, the door closes forever behind it — not because the door is cruel, but because a key that walks out stops being a key. Both halves together make the lock. Take away either one and there is no lock. No door. No Ostrava.

Issel is the inside half.

Mama told them this on the last night. Not in words, mostly. In words she said: *you are going to be very brave, my su-mel, braver than brave.* But she held Issel's hands against her own sternum while she said it, so Issel could feel the words in her bones before they reached her ears, and bones don't lie the way mouths learn to. Through her ribs Issel felt the rest: *the door will open once. You will go out. You will stand on the other side and call us through, and we will come, all of us, every soul still sleeping in the amber evening, and then you will step back inside before the light fades, and you will close it behind you.*

*You will close it behind you.*

Issel was six. Almost seven. They had asked, in the smallest voice they owned, *and then? And then what happens to me?*

And Mama had done the thing parents do when the true answer is a cliff edge — she had kissed Issel's forehead and said, *and then you will be our hero, and heroes are remembered forever,* which is not an answer, which is a beautiful curtain hung over a doorway leading nowhere.

But bones had already told Issel the truth. Bones had told them for a long time. Every night since they were tiny, falling asleep in the stasis-light, some part of them had understood what the grown-ups' plan required, and had gone on breathing anyway, because children are very good at carrying enormous things if nobody makes them look at the enormous things directly.

Then the Choir came closer than it had ever come. The city began to sing itself thinner. And the elders voted — Issel heard the vote through the floor of the meeting hall, felt it in the stones — to send the key out *early*. Before the solstice. Before the plan was ready. Because the door was weakening, and a weakening door leaks, and a leaking door can be found.

So they woke Issel all the way, and put the salt-cake in their pocket, and taught them the path through the borderlands where the Choir's song couldn't follow, and pushed them gently through the closing dark toward the valley where magic cannot go.

They didn't push Issel out to find help.

They pushed Issel out because a key must be positioned. Because when the solstice came and the door opened, someone had to be standing on the outside half of the lock, calling the exiles through — and then stepping aside, and watching the last face vanish, and staying.

Forever staying.

* * *

"I wasn't sent to find help," Issel says.

The boat drifts. The lamp flame leans with the motion, and shadows slide along the glowing walls like fish.

"They sent me because—" Their throat does a thing. It closes partway, the way a hand closes around something precious. They breathe around it. "Because the door needs a key on both sides. Outside and inside. Two halves."

Paloma's hands stop moving on the oars entirely.

"And I'm the inside one." The words come out faster now, tumbling, because they've been sitting in Issel's chest for so long they've grown roots, and pulling them out hurts and also feels like finally standing up straight. "If I go through the door — if I go home — I can't ever come back out. Not ever. The elders didn't send me to get help, Paloma. They sent me to open your side and call everyone through and then stay on your side while the door shut. Forever. That was always the plan. That was always—" Their voice cracks down the middle like a dropped plate. "That was always the plan."

The drip of water somewhere deep in the caves. The lap of green-black river against the hull. Paloma's breath.

When Issel finally looks up, Paloma's face has gone through several weathers. Issel watches them pass like clouds over the salt-glow: confusion, then understanding, then a horror so large it looks almost like anger, except it isn't aimed at Issel. Issel knows aimed-anger. This is different. This is anger with nowhere to land, circling overhead like a bird that can't find a branch.

"They sent a child," Paloma says quietly, "to die on the wrong side of a door."

"Not die." Issel shakes their head. It matters, suddenly, enormously, that this part be correct. "Live. Just... live here. With you. With everyone. Only never—" Their hands make the shape of the doorway in the air, the humming wall, the place where light passes through skin. "Never there again."

The word *never* fills the whole cavern. It bounces off the salt and comes back twice.

* * *

Paloma doesn't say anything for a long time.

She does the thing she did the first night, when Issel stood at her cottage door caked white with salt — the thing where instead of asking questions she just makes room. She shifts in the boat and pulls Issel against her side, and Issel goes, because being held is a language too, maybe the oldest one, older than Ostravan, older than the door.

Her heart is beating fast under her ribs. Faster than usual. Issel can hear it the way they heard Mama's words in her bones. Hearts tell truths too.

"You could have let me believe the kind version," Paloma says into their hair. "You could have let us all believe it until solstice."

"The door would have found out," Issel says simply. "Doors always find out. Grandmother Sefi said the door is honest even when people aren't. That's why it hums. It's humming the truth all the time. We just stopped knowing how to hear it."

Paloma laughs — a wet, broken little laugh that isn't really a laugh. "Of course it does. Of course the door hums the truth. Why would anything in this story be simple?"

Issel doesn't understand all the words, but understands the shape of them. Sad-funny. The shape adults make with their faces when the world is too big to cry about properly.

They sit like that while the boat carries them through a long gallery where the salt has grown in curtains, translucent, hanging like frozen waterfalls. The lamp light goes through them and comes out the other side changed — warmer, softer, the way light looks through honey. Issel watches it and thinks about the amber evening inside the door, where everyone they love is sleeping in preserved time, waiting for a child's voice to call them out.

Waiting for a child who will never walk through.

"Mama thought I'd be glad," Issel says. The confession isn't finished; there is more of it, the soft terrible underside. "To be the hero. To live in the bright world with the new family. She packed my bag with dried plums because I like them. She spent three days teaching me forty words of Vellish. She cried the whole third day but she smiled while she cried, so I'd remember her smiling." Issel's fingers curl into Paloma's sleeve. "I *was* glad. That's the worst part. When I walked through the border and saw the valley — the real grass, the real rain, the bread smell from the baker's — I was glad. And then at night I remembered, and I hated myself for being glad, and then I was glad again in the morning. Both things. Always both things."

Paloma's arms tighten.

"I know about both things," she says.

And Issel believes her. Because Issel has seen her look at the court with her careful invisible face, seen her taste poison dish after dish for people who would forget her name by supper, and seen her also laugh with Hedda over soup, also hum lullabies in a dead tongue, also belong and not-belong in exactly the same heartbeat.

Both things. The whole valley runs on both things.

* * *

After a while, Paloma speaks again, and her voice has changed. It has the taster-sharpness in it now, the competence, but pointed outward for once instead of hidden.

"Listen to me, Issel. Listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to say it many more times until you're sick of hearing it." She takes Issel's shoulders and turns them so they're facing her, and the lamp light falls full on her face, and her eyes are fierce and shining. "No one is sending you anywhere. Not the elders, not the door, not the plan. Do you understand? Whatever happens at solstice, whatever gets decided in that cave, you will be in the room where it's decided. Your choice counts. Children's choices count. Especially yours, especially now."

"But the door—"

"The door," Paloma says, "can learn some manners."

Issel blinks. Somewhere in their chest, the enormous heavy thing — the thing they've carried since the last night, since Mama's ribs, since the vote through the floorboards — shifts its weight. Doesn't get lighter. Nothing makes that thing lighter. But it shifts, like a sleeper turning over, like it might be possible to set it down someday without the sky falling.

"You'll figure it out," Issel whispers. Not a question. A wish wearing a question's clothes.

"I'm a food-taster," Paloma says. "My entire life is figuring out what's really in a dish when everyone insists it's something else. The door is a dish. The elders' plan is a recipe written by frightened people. Recipes can be rewritten." A pause, and then, softer, almost to herself: "Mine were."

The boat rounds a bend in the underground river, and ahead the cavern opens into a vast chamber, and there — far off, a pale gleam in the lamplight — the wall. The humming wall. Issel can feel it before they can see it clearly, that low honest note in their teeth and their breastbone, the sound of the truth being sung continuously by stone.

It's humming differently today. Faster. Thinner. Counting down.

Solstice is coming.

And now Paloma knows what Issel knows, and the knowledge sits between them in the boat like a third passenger, silent and heavy, and Issel watches Paloma's jaw set as she looks at the distant gleaming wall — watches her take up the oars again, though the river needs no rowing, and pull, and pull, as if she could row them straight through the heart of the problem by force.

Issel hugs the secret's empty place in their chest, where the weight used to sit alone.

Two days until solstice.

One door. One key split in halves that love each other and cannot both survive the opening.

Unless — Paloma's oars bite the green-black water, stroke after stubborn stroke — unless somebody rewrites the recipe.

* * *

That night, camped on a shelf of white stone above the whispering river, Issel wakes once to see Paloma sitting up, the recipe book open on her knees, the lamp turned low, her lips moving through her grandmother's marginalia like a woman praying in two languages at once.

She looks up. Catches Issel watching.

"Go back to sleep, su-mel," she says — the endearment rolling off her tongue now as naturally as if it had always lived there.

Issel closes their eyes. But they listen a long time to the scratch of her pencil, and the hum of the wall far off in the dark, counting down, counting down —

— and for the first time since the last night in Ostrava, the count doesn't feel like the end of something.

It feels like the beginning of an argument the door has never lost before.

Almost never.
