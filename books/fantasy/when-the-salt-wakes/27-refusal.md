## 27. Refusal

The words hung in the cave air long after Issel said them—or signed them, or whatever it was the child did with their hands and their shoulders and their terrible patient eyes that made meaning arrive without sound.

*A door needs a key on both sides.*

*I am the inside half.*

*If I go through, I cannot come back.*

Paloma sat down on the salt floor. Not gracefully. Her knees simply stopped negotiating with her, and she folded, and the cold of the crystal came up through her trousers and into her bones, and she let it, because pain was easier to hold than what Issel had just told her.

"No," she said.

Issel tilted their head. Around them the humming wall threw its pale light across the cavern, and the carved script older than kingdoms glowed faintly where the child's fingers had touched it, and everything—the whole impossible architecture of the last month—was arranged around a single arithmetic that Paloma refused to do.

"No," she said again, louder, and heard her own voice bounce back off the salt, thinner than she wanted it to be. "You don't get to say that like it's weather. Like it's already decided. You're seven years old."

Issel held up six fingers, then corrected to seven, then shrugged, which was its own answer: even Issel wasn't certain, and somehow that made it worse.

"You crossed a border alone. You walked into a valley where magic can't follow you, which means you walked away from everything that could have protected you, to be a key." Paloma heard her voice climbing and couldn't stop it. "Who told you that? Who looked at a child and said, *you will stand on one side of a door for the rest of your life so that other people can walk through it*?"

Issel's hands moved. Slowly. Deliberately. The gestures were becoming familiar now, the way a tune becomes familiar—you didn't need to translate every note to know the song.

*Everyone I love is behind the door. If the door stays shut, I lose them anyway. This way I only lose me.*

Paloma had spent eleven years tasting poison for people who would not have spat on her if she were burning. She knew what it looked like when someone had been taught their life was currency. She had just never seen it on a face that still had milk teeth coming in.

She pulled Issel against her chest, and the child went stiff with surprise—nobody had held them in weeks, maybe longer—and then, slowly, softened, small fists gripping her shirt like someone holding onto a boat rail in rough water.

"We're not doing it," she said into their salt-stiff hair. "Whatever the book says. Whatever the door says. There's another way. There's always another way; my whole life is proof of that. People told my grandmother there was no way to survive this valley, and she survived it with a recipe book and a bad temper, and I am her granddaughter, and I am telling you—" Her voice cracked, and she was too tired to be embarrassed about it. "I am telling you we will find the other way."

Issel didn't answer. But the fists in her shirt loosened by a degree, and that loosening was the first honest thing Paloma had felt from the child since the confession—not agreement, not hope, exactly, but the specific relaxation of someone who has been carrying a weight alone and has just been made to set it down, briefly, whether or not it makes sense to.

She gave them ten more seconds of holding. Then practicality reasserted itself, because it always did, because she was who she was.

"Right." She stood, knees complaining. "We have—" She counted on her fingers. Solstice was four days off. Corvane's three days had started yesterday, which meant two. Bram was moving them by night along the underground river starting tomorrow, assuming the ferryman's nerve held and the soldiers' patrols stayed stupid. Which left tonight, and this cave, and this wall, and a dead woman's recipe book.

"Sit," she told Issel. "Don't touch anything glowing. Eat something." She pressed half a loaf and a wedge of hard cheese into the child's hands, watched them begin to eat with the methodical seriousness Issel brought to all food, as though meals were appointments, and turned to the book.

Her grandmother's recipe book. Water-swollen at one corner, grease-darkened at another, smelling of dried thyme and old smoke. The marginalia in the looping Ostravan script that Paloma could now read haltingly, syllable by syllable, the way she'd once learned her letters—a word at a time, sounding them out, her mouth remembering before her mind did.

*Salt of the valley, one measure.*
*Salt of elsewhere, one measure.*
*Fold together under a dark moon. Do not stir. Do not seal.*

That was the passage she'd read as directions to the caves. Folded salt, layered stone, the valley's geology written like a pastry crust. And beneath the caves, the wall, and inside the wall—

She'd been reading it wrong. She'd known she was reading it wrong ever since Issel's confession, and she hadn't let herself think about why, and now she made herself think about why.

Because if the recipes were maps, then somewhere in this book might be a map that led around the sacrifice. A second entrance. An alternate key. Some ingredient she hadn't deciphered yet.

And if there wasn't—

She flipped pages. Stewed eel, which was absurd because no eel had ever lived within forty miles of Vell, and therefore the eel was a river, the underground river, Bram's river. Salt-baked onions—layer after layer, something wrapped in something, hidden at the center. Honey cakes for the feast of first frost. *Feast of first frost.* Solstice-adjacent. She'd skimmed past that page twice as sentimentality.

She forced herself to stop skimming.

*Honey cakes for the feast of first frost,* the marginalia ran beneath the printed recipe, in her grandmother's hand, *because the table is the oldest door. Set it right and even strangers may enter. Set it wrong and family is turned away.*

Paloma stared at it.

*Even strangers may enter.* Hospitality. Some old rite dressed up in cake. It meant nothing. It was a superstition about good manners.

It was the most important sentence in the book, and she knew that the way she knew poison—instantly, bodily, below thought.

She didn't know how yet. But the door didn't want a child standing forever on its threshold. The door wanted—her grandmother's hand said so—a *table*. A meal shared. The oldest Ostravan rite. Issel had said it themselves, weeks ago, teaching Paloma words over soup: that in the old tongue, *guest* and *gift* were nearly the same word, differing by one breath.

A key on both sides. Or a meal between them.

"Issel," she said carefully. "When you came through the border. When you crossed into Vell. How did you get past the ward? The thing that keeps magic out? Everyone else who carries it gets turned away—Hedda said so, the founding wards push it back. But you're full of Ostrava, you should have been pushed out like a cork from a bottle. How did you get in?"

Issel swallowed their cheese. Thought. Answered with hands and, lately, with a few spoken words—they'd begun speaking again, sparingly, in the grandmother-dialect that fit strangely in a child's mouth, like a coat cut for a bigger person.

"The Choir was singing the valley wrong," Issel said. "Making a hole with the wrongness. I came through the hole. It hurt." A pause. "Like walking through a memory of cold."

"And the door in the wall. When it opens at solstice. Does it open *through* the ward, or around it?"

Issel frowned, and Paloma watched a seven-year-old reach for concepts that philosophers would have paid to discuss, and fail, and try anyway. "Doors don't care about walls," Issel said finally. "A door is a promise. The wall is just... where the promise is kept."

Paloma wrote that down. On the flyleaf of the recipe book, in plain Vellish script, because some things deserved to survive translation: *A door is a promise.*

Then she did what she always did when the world got too large. She reduced it to ingredients.

What did she have? A book of coded recipes, half-deciphered. A humming wall with an inscription she could partly read via her grandmother's key-phrase. A child who was half a key and could not pass through. A ferryman with a debt. An herbalist in a cell, if Corvane hadn't yet moved her—no, don't think about Hedda, thinking about Hedda was a hole in the floor. Neighbors leaving bread at her door. Four days. A valley whose magic-seal had held for three hundred years and was, according to everything—the salt blooming in wells, the Choir's song fraying the borders—weakening precisely because Issel was here.

And what did the enemy have? The Grey Choir outside the border, patient as erosion. Corvane inside it, with soldiers, with three days' patience of his own, with a theory he'd already confirmed: *a taster's family may be the cause.*

She closed the book. Sat with it in her lap. Listened to the wall hum.

Here was the shape of the refusal, and she examined it honestly, because she was done lying to herself even if she'd only just learned to lie to anyone else. To open the door without Issel, she needed to become, in some sense, the key herself—to carry enough of Ostrava in her body to satisfy the promise on both sides. She had the blood. She had the lullabies. She had, God help her, the recipes. Whether she had enough of anything else was a question the solstice would answer, and if the answer was no, then the last citizens of a murdered kingdom stayed sealed in amber evening forever, and Issel stayed a key without a lock, and Paloma would have traded a certainty for a gamble and lost a civilization doing it.

Or she delivered Issel through, and saved everyone, and broke the only person in this entire affair who hadn't yet been broken.

"No," she said a third time, quietly now, to the wall, to the book, to the whole machinery of fate and salt and sorrow. "Find another way."

The wall hummed. It did not sound like disagreement. It did not sound like agreement either. It sounded like a locked room waiting to hear a good argument.

* * *

They surfaced from the caves near midnight, into snow that squeaked underfoot and a sky scoured clean of cloud, every star out like witnesses. The walk back down the goat track to the river took an hour, Issel riding Paloma's shoulders part of the way, chattering—actually chattering now, a flow of small Ostravan words naming things: *star, star, cold-star, that one is a boat-star, that one is Grandmother-mira's spoon.* Paloma repeated what she could. Her accent was atrocious. Issel found this hilarious, which was new, and which she hoarded like contraband sugar.

Bram was waiting where he'd promised, at the ruined boathouse with its collapsed roof, the underground river breathing fog into the night. He had his lamp shuttered to a coin of light and worry all over his face, and the moment they stepped into that light he crossed to them fast—for Bram, who moved through the world like a man perpetually wading—gripped Paloma's shoulder, and looked her over like cargo he was personally accountable for.

"You're late," he said. "And you've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look my mother had," Bram said, "when she decided something the rest of us weren't going to enjoy." He grunted. "Your grandmother had it too. Right before she talked me out of the flood-channel when I was fourteen and stupid. So. What have you decided?"

Paloma looked at him—the big gentle wreck of a man, third-generation valley, who owed her dead grandmother his life and was paying the debt by smuggling a foreign child past armed men—and decided he'd earned the truth.

"Issel can't go through the door. If they do, they can never come back. That's what the key *is*." She watched him absorb it; his jaw worked. "So I'm not sending them. I'm going to find a way to open it that doesn't require a child on an altar, literal or otherwise. The book says something about a table. About hospitality. I don't understand it yet. But I'm going to."

Bram was quiet a long moment. Fog moved on the black water.

"There's a thing my grandmother used to say," he offered at last. "Valley saying, older than the valley probably. *Never trust a lock with only one keyhole.*" He shrugged his enormous shrug. "I always took it to mean there's more ways in than the obvious one. Might be nonsense."

"It might be the smartest thing anyone's said to me all year," Paloma said. "Get us on the water."

He got them on the water. The underground river took them sliding into the dark, lamp hooded, Bram poling with that unhurried strength of his, and Paloma sat in the bow with Issel asleep against her and the recipe book open on her knees, working by coin-light through the honey-cake page for the fourth time.

*Set it right and even strangers may enter. Set it wrong and family is turned away.*

Strangers entering. Family turned away. She'd read it as manners. But turn the phrase over—turn it the way you turned a suspicious dish, looking for what was hidden underneath—and it wasn't about manners at all. It was about thresholds. About who counted as *inside*. The rite of hospitality, performed correctly, *redrew the boundary of who belongs.* Feed someone at your table, in the old law of half the world's dead cultures, and they were no longer stranger; they were yours to protect, and you were theirs to mourn.

The door didn't want a key sacrificed to it.

The door wanted to be *fed*. Wanted the rite performed across its threshold—a meal cooked by Ostravan hands, shared with the valley, given freely, so that for the length of the solstice minute the two sides of the wall were not two sides at all but one table.

It was a guess. It was built on cake recipes and a child's grammar and a ferryman's proverb. It would very possibly kill everyone.

But it didn't require Issel to vanish behind a wall of light forever, and that single fact made it, by Paloma's accounting, worth any odds in creation.

She looked up from the page and realized Bram had stopped poling. The boat drifted. He was staring ahead into the hooded dark, and she followed his gaze, and saw it: up ahead, where the river bent toward the lake beneath the caves, the water was glowing. Faintly. Pale gold, like lamplight through honey, pulsing slow.

Not firelight. Not phosphorescence—Bram would know; he'd poled this river thirty years and he'd gone still as a post.

"That's coming from the caves," Bram said quietly. "From the direction of the door."

"Yes," Paloma said.

"It's never done that."

"No."

"And you're smiling," Bram said, with the tone of a man cataloguing evidence for a future inquiry.

"I'm refusing," Paloma said, "very loudly. Pass me the pole a moment—I want to see the wall tonight while it's awake. And wake Issel gently when we moor. They should see this."

Bram opened his mouth—she could feel the objection assembling itself, sensible, survivable, *we should keep moving, we should sleep, we are fugitives with a schedule*—and then the glow pulsed once, strong, washing up the river channel like a tide, and whatever he'd been going to say drowned in it.

They rounded the bend. The lake opened beneath the vault of salt, and the wall stood at its far edge, and the wall was singing.

Not humming. Singing. Notes Paloma almost recognized—almost, the way a face in a crowd is almost someone you loved—and beneath the notes, visible now in the gold light, the carved inscription was moving. Not changing. Moving, the way text moves when it is being *read aloud*, each character brightening in sequence, patiently, endlessly, like a message repeating itself to an empty room.

Or like a recipe being recited to a cook who has finally, finally come home.

Paloma stepped out of the boat onto the salt shore, book in hand, and began to walk toward it—and that was when Issel, awake now, gripping Bram's sleeve at the waterline, made a sound Paloma had never heard from the child before.

Fear. Pure, unambiguous fear.

And pointing past the singing wall—not at it, past it, at the darkness above and beyond, where the ceiling of the cavern met the world of stone and earth and the long miles back to the valley's border—Issel spoke one word in the old tongue, and Paloma knew this one, knew it from her grandmother's bedtime warnings the way you know a scar:

*The Choir. The Choir is singing our song back at us.*

Far overhead, muffled through three hundred feet of rock and salt, thin as a hair drawn across the world, came a sound like many throats practicing a name.

Getting it less wrong than yesterday.
