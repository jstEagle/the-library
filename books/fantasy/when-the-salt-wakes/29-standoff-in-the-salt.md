## 29. Standoff in the Salt

The soldiers came down the river path an hour after dawn, and I heard them before I saw them—thirty pairs of boots on wet stone, the ring of a sword-belt buckle, the particular silence of men trying to be quiet and failing.

I had been awake all night. Issel slept against my side with their head under my chin, breathing the slow, whistling breath of a child who had cried themselves empty. The humming wall was three chambers back, still singing its low note into the dark, and somewhere above us the solstice sun was climbing toward the shortest day of the year, and Corvane was walking toward us through the tunnels with his soldiers' torchlight bouncing off walls of salt like light off snow.

"Paloma." His voice carried ahead of him, pitched low, almost gentle. He had always known how to do that—make his cruelty sound like courtesy. "Paloma Thackeray. I know you can hear me."

Issel stirred. I put my hand flat on their small back, over the spine, where I could feel the flutter of their heartbeat like a bird's.

"Stay here," I whispered. "Behind the wall. If anything happens, touch it. It knows you."

Their eyes were enormous in the dark. They didn't want to let go of my sleeve. Neither did I, if I'm honest, but I pried their fingers off one by one and kissed the top of their salt-stiff hair, and I walked out into the main chamber to meet thirty soldiers and one man who had once called me *the most honest person in Vell*.

He stood at the mouth of the tunnel with a lantern raised, and behind him the soldiers fanned out in a half-circle, pikes down, nervous. The cavern around them was vast—a cathedral of pale crystal, columns of salt that had grown over centuries into shapes like organ pipes, like folded wings, like the ribs of something enormous sleeping. The underground river ran along the far edge, black and slow, carrying Bram's boat somewhere downstream where I prayed he was waiting.

Corvane looked older than he had three days ago. That surprised me. Spymasters are supposed to be made of stone, and yet there were new lines around his eyes, and his coat was buttoned wrong—one button off, as though he had dressed in the dark or dressed angry or simply stopped caring about buttons, which for Corvane would be a kind of collapse.

"You shouldn't have run," he said.

"I didn't run," I said. "I walked. There's a difference."

"There is." He set the lantern down on a shelf of rock with exaggerated care, the way he did everything, as though even in chaos the world owed him order. "And here's another difference. Between what I am required to do and what I want to do. You remember how I've always treated you, Paloma. With respect. With—" he searched, "—*kindness*. I offered you protection. Patronage. A place at court that wasn't the end of a fork."

"You asked me to hand over a child," I said. "That was the kindness."

"Yes." He didn't flinch from it. That was the thing about Corvane; he never flinched. "It was. And now it's worse than that, because while you've been playing in caves, the valley itself has started coming apart, and the Regent has signed orders I can no longer soften." He drew a folded paper from inside his coat but didn't open it. Just held it. "Come away from the wall. Give me the child. And I swear to you, on whatever you hold sacred—and I know you hold nothing sacred except your grandmother's kitchen—I will see them fed, clothed, kept safe in the citadel until this is resolved."

"Until it's resolved," I repeated. "Resolved how? Resolved by whom?"

"The Grey Choir is at our border, Paloma."

The name went through the chamber like cold water through cloth. Behind me, faintly, I heard Issel make a sound—not a word, not a cry. Something smaller than either.

"I know," I said. "Issel told me. They sing a country's name wrong until the country forgets itself, and then they walk in and there's nothing left to save. Ostrava. Sarnholt. The Pale Marches. How many, Corvane? Do you even know? Your own records—"

"My family came from Sarnholt." He said it flatly, without drama, and it landed harder than any shout could have. "You knew that. Everyone at court knew that, once, before we learned not to say it. My grandmother used to sing songs in a language I was told was just 'the old country.' It wasn't. It was Sarnholti, and Sarnholt doesn't exist anymore, and neither does anyone who remembers it, and I have spent my entire career serving a crown that taught me to call that *peace*." He stepped forward. The soldiers shifted. "So don't lecture me about vanished lands. I have loved one my whole life. It's a grave."

"Then help me open the door," I said. "Help me get them through. All of them—the survivors outside the border, the ones the Choir has been hunting for generations. Issel isn't a threat to Vell. Issel is the last chance any of those people have, and the door closes at solstice—"

"At solstice the Choir breaches the valley," he said. "Our scholars agree on one thing only: the child's presence, and that door's existence, are drawing them. Every day they stand at our edge singing, the seal weakens. Magic is leaking in, Paloma. Hedda's well ran salt. The church font. The livestock are fleeing inward. Whatever your grandmother built into this valley when she hid here—it's failing, and it's failing *because of that child*." He opened his hands. "Give them to me, and maybe we buy Vell time. Refuse, and we all learn what the Choir sounds like from the inside."

"They're already going to learn it," said a voice behind me.

Issel stood at the mouth of the inner passage, having disobeyed me completely, wrapped in my cloak, looking smaller and more ancient than any six-year-old should. Their voice was steady. It always was, when it mattered.

"The door isn't weakening the border," they said. "The door is the border. It's the last knot holding the valley shut. If you take me away from it, the knot unravels faster. If you close it early—if you seal it, wall it up, drown it in salt like your scholars want—" their chin lifted, "—then the valley opens like a mouth. The Choir walks in. Not maybe. Not someday. At solstice."

Corvane stared at the child for a long moment. I watched him do the arithmetic every spymaster does—the credibility assessment, the source evaluation, the terrible weight of believing a mute exile who speaks a dead language—and I watched him arrive at the answer he didn't want.

"You're certain," he said quietly. It wasn't a question, but Issel answered it anyway, with a single nod.

"So the choice," Corvane said, turning to me, "is not whether the door opens. It's who stands here when it does."

"Now you understand," I said.

"No." His voice hardened. "What I understand is that I have thirty soldiers and sealed orders, and the Regent does not negotiate with children or tasters. Open the passage. Hand over the key. That is my offer. It expires when my patience does."

"Your patience," I said, "expired the moment you brought pikes into a cathedral."

And then—because I have spent my whole life learning to taste the invisible, to read a dish by its smell, to sense danger arriving before it arrives—I felt it.

Not heard. Felt.

A pressure behind the sternum. A wrongness in the air like the moment before lightning. The salt columns around us began, very softly, to ring.

* * *

It started as a harmonic, deep in the largest pillar, a tone so low I felt it in my teeth rather than heard it. Then a second pillar caught it. A third. The whole cavern became a struck bell, and the soldiers' lanterns guttered though there was no wind.

"What is that?" someone near the rear said. "Captain—Corvane—what is *that*?"

"Hold position," Corvane said, but his hand had gone to his sword, which he almost never touched.

The song came down through the stone above us. Through two hundred feet of valley rock. Through the seal that magic could not cross—which was the whole point of the valley, the founding promise, the reason my grandmother had fled here with a recipe book and a changed name.

The Grey Choir had found the frequency of the door, and they were singing through it.

I felt it first as a wrongness in my own mouth—as though someone were pronouncing my name slightly off, *Pal-oma* instead of *PaLOma*, and each wrong repetition scraped something loose. Around me the effect was worse. One soldier dropped his pike and pressed both hands over his ears. Another began reciting his mother's name over and over, faster and faster, like a man checking that he still owned it. A third simply sat down on the salt floor and began to weep without any change in his expression at all, tears sliding down a perfectly blank face.

"Fall back!" Corvane shouted. "Everyone fall back to the—"

The nearest salt column cracked.

Not shattered—cracked, a single clean line running up its height, and through the crack seeped a color I had no name for. Not white. The absence of color, moving like smoke, like ink in reverse, drinking the lantern light where it passed. Where it touched the floor, the salt turned grey and dead and stayed that way.

The soldiers broke.

There's no shame in saying it—they broke the way any brave men break when the enemy is not a thing you can stab. Thirty of them, trained by the Regent herself, turned and shoved each other in the narrow tunnel mouth, screaming about ghosts, about the song, about a sergeant whose shadow had come off the wall and wouldn't return to him. I heard a helmet strike stone. I heard a man praying in the old valley dialect, the words tumbling out wrong, the way Issel had warned me the Choir unmade language itself.

Corvane alone held his ground. He stood in the center of the chaos with his drawn sword pointed at nothing, at everything, at the crack in the world, and his face was the face of a man watching his entire cosmology fail in real time.

"Thackeray," he said, and his voice cracked on the second syllable. "What do I do?"

For one mad moment I almost laughed. Twenty years of careful invisibility, of being furniture, of being a fork and a tongue and nothing—and now the spymaster of Vell was asking *me*.

"You put the sword down," I said. "You can't cut a song. And you come with me, because the only thing standing between that"—I pointed at the spreading grey—"and every soul in Vell is a door that hasn't opened yet. Solstice is tomorrow at dusk. We hold until then, or everyone you've ever served dies remembering nothing."

The grey reached the base of the far column. Where it passed, the humming died—the pillars falling silent one by one like a choir losing voices—and in the sudden thin quiet I heard, unmistakably now, threading down through the stone:

Singing.

Dozens of voices. Patient. Tuneless. Pronouncing a word over and over that was almost *Vell*, the way a drunk pronounces a name, the way a stranger misreads handwriting—close enough to wound, wrong enough to kill.

Issel pressed against my side, and their small hand found mine, and their other hand reached out and touched the humming wall, and the wall's note rose to meet the Choir's song like a blade meeting a blade.

"Paloma," Issel said, in my grandmother's dialect, in the voice of a people who had been erased twice already, "they're inside the border. We have until dusk."

Corvane sheathed his sword.

"Tell me what to do," he said again—and this time it wasn't a question, and it wasn't kindness, and it wasn't an order. It was surrender.

Above us, the valley's oldest promise cracked a little further, and the salt began, very slowly, to wake.
