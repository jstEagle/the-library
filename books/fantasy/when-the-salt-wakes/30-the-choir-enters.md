## 30. The Choir Enters

Issel feels it first in their teeth.

Not a sound. Not yet. A pressure, like the moment before a storm when the air goes thick and the goats stop eating and stand facing the wrong way. Issel is sitting in the salt boat with their knees pulled up, watching Paloma's hands on the little stove she brought, watching her measure out flour with her grandmother's dented cup, and suddenly all the tiny hairs on Issel's arms lift up like grass before lightning.

*They are here,* Issel thinks, in the old words, the words that taste of home. *They are through.*

Paloma looks up. She has learned to read Issel's face even when Issel says nothing at all.

"What?" she says. "What is it?"

But Issel can't answer, because the answer is coming up the river behind them, and it sounds like this:

A hum. Low. Patient. The kind of hum a person makes when they are doing something kind—a mother stirring soup, a grandmother kneading bread—and that is the worst part, that the sound is almost gentle, because Issel knows what wears that gentleness like a coat over nothing.

The Grey Choir does not shout. It never shouts. It sings the way rot smells: softly, and then everywhere.

* * *

The caverns around them begin to change color.

Issel notices it in small ways first. The amber glow of the salt walls—warm as lamplight, warm as the evening Ostrava was frozen into—goes pale at one spot, then another, like breath fogging a window from the far side. Then the fog spreads. The gold drains out of the stone the way water drains from a cracked pot, slow and then fast, and where the gold was there is grey. Not dark grey. Not storm grey. The grey of ash. The grey of a page someone wrote on and erased so many times the paper wore thin.

"Get down," Paloma says, and her voice has gone flat and hard, the voice Issel heard once through the cottage door when soldiers came to ask questions. She blows out the stove with one pinch of her fingers and pulls Issel behind a rib of salt rock taller than both of them together.

The boat rocks against its mooring line. Bram's lantern, hanging from the prow, gutters and shrinks to a bead.

"It can't come in," Paloma whispers. "The valley keeps it out. It kept everything out for three hundred years."

Issel takes her sleeve. Their mouth makes the words slowly, because some things must be said right or not at all.

"The door is open a crack now. We opened it. And when a door opens—" they search for the valley words, "—things lean on it."

* * *

The Choir comes around the last bend of the underground river, and Issel sees them properly for the first time since the night their country ended.

There are so many.

They walk on the surface of the water. They do not hurry. There are perhaps forty of them, maybe more, tall figures wrapped in choir robes that move like smoke moving wrong, and where faces should be there is only smoothness—the same grey as their robes, featureless as an egg, as a stone worn down by a river that no longer exists. They do not have mouths. This is the thing that Issel has never been able to make anyone understand, the thing that made the elders call them *unnameable*: the singing comes from nowhere. From all of them. From between them. The song lives in the space where their faces ought to be, and it fills it exactly.

And they sing names.

That is what the Choir does. That is the whole of what the Choir does. It sings the name of a place—wrongly, deliberately wrongly, changing one sound, then two, then half the word—until the place forgets its own name and, forgetting it, forgets itself. Issel has watched a village go. Watched the well keep its shape while everyone forgot why wells were dug. Watched mothers turn around twice in a square, looking for children they could not remember having, until the children themselves thinned like smoke.

Ostrava lasted longer than most. Ostrava had hidden its name inside its lullabies, inside its recipes, inside its children—which is why Issel was sent out of it, which is why Issel's own name was folded up small and hidden under their tongue where no song could find it.

*Issel.* Even thinking it now is a small defiance. *My name is Issel. My name is Issel. My name—*

The nearest singer turns its smooth face toward the salt rib where they hide.

It cannot see. Issel knows it cannot see; the Choir has no eyes, has never needed eyes, hunts by hearing names the way other creatures hunt by smell. But it turns anyway, and the song shifts key, and Issel's whole body clenches like a fist.

Because the song has found something to sing about.

* * *

The colour goes first from the boat.

Bram's lantern doesn't go out—it simply stops being orange. The flame stands there, burning, giving light, and the light is grey, and the brass of the lantern is grey, and Bram himself comes stumbling back along the bank saying something in his big valley voice, and his voice comes out grey too, flat as slate, and Issel watches him stop and look at his own hands as though he has never seen hands before and is not sure he wants any.

"Bram!" Paloma shouts, and grabs for him, and her hand closes on his wrist, and for one terrible moment Issel thinks she will pull him back and he will be fine—but the song swells, and Issel hears what it is singing now, and their heart drops through the floor of the world.

It is singing *Bram*. Not his true-name—his valley name, shortened, worn soft by use—but it is enough. It sings him slightly wrong. It sings *Bramm*, with the weight shifted, and Bram blinks slowly, and his mouth moves, testing the shape, trying to remember if that is right, if that has always been right—

Paloma slaps him. Open-handed, across the face, hard.

The crack of it cuts through the song for just one heartbeat, and in that heartbeat Bram's eyes clear like a man surfacing from deep water, gasping.

"Run," Paloma tells him. "Don't answer them. Don't think your name. Just run, you stupid wonderful man, *run*—"

Bram runs. His footsteps fade up the tunnel toward the valley, and Issel huddles smaller against the salt, and the Choir keeps walking, unhurried, forty grey shapes gliding across black water, filling the great cavern the way night fills a window.

* * *

This is what unmaking looks like, up close. Issel has seen it before but never like this, never this near, and every detail carves itself into them:

Where the singers pass, the salt walls lose not only their colour but their *history*. The tool-marks of the miners who cut these galleries soften and smooth, as if no hand had ever worked here. A rusted iron ring in the wall—an old mooring point, older than Vell—fades to a dimple, then to nothing. Somewhere above them, Issel understands, the valley is being sung too: the sheep in their folds going quiet, the church bell forgetting its note, the Regent's banners over the keep fading from crimson to a grey that no one will question because no one will remember crimson.

Memory goes next. Issel feels it plucking at the edges of their own mind, gentle as a grandmother untangling hair. Little things loosen first. The smell of Paloma's soup. The exact tune of the lullaby. The word for *door*—Issel reaches for it and finds a hole where it should be, and panic floods them so completely that they bite their own hand to stay silent.

Then a warm weight settles over them. Paloma's arm. Her body curved around theirs, her mouth pressed close to their ear, and she is whispering—not the valley tongue, not the court tongue—she is whispering Ostravan. Grandmother-words. Recipe-words. The secret syllables that were smuggled across a border in a woman's cooking and a woman's humming, three hundred years too quiet for any song to catch.

She is feeding Issel their own language, spoonful by spoonful, faster than the Choir can swallow it.

*Door,* she whispers, in the old tongue. *Salt. Stay. Mine. Stay.*

Issel holds each word like a coal in cupped hands.

*Stay. Mine. Stay.*

* * *

The Choir reaches the middle of the cavern and stops.

All forty faces, smooth and grey, tilt upward at once, toward the far wall—toward the seam in the world that Issel's people sealed themselves behind, the door that is not a door, the amber evening held in stasis where grandmothers and fiddlers and beekeepers wait in the last unbroken hour of a kingdom that fell.

The song changes.

Before, it was patient. Now it goes to work. It is a strange, awful thing to hear: the Choir singing the name of the door itself, singing it wrong on purpose, and with every wrong syllable the amber glow along the far wall flickers like a candle in a draught. The seam thins. The light bleeds grey at its edges. Somewhere behind that wall, Issel knows, the sleepers stir without waking, dreaming uneasily of cold.

"They're unwrapping it," Issel whispers, in their own language, because there are no valley words big enough. "If they sing the door wrong, the ones inside don't die. Worse. They wake up and there's no door and no outside and no them. They come apart in there. Like sugar in rain."

Paloma's arm tightens. When she speaks, her voice is very low and very steady, the way it gets when she is most afraid, because Issel has learned that about her: the steadier she sounds, the closer the knife is to her skin.

"How long?"

"I don't—"

The song rises. The grey spreads along the walls like frost climbing glass. One of the singers detaches from the line and begins to drift toward the salt rib where they hide—not searching, not hunting, simply sweeping, the way a tide sweeps a shore—and its smooth face passes within an arm's reach of Issel, close enough that Issel can see that it is not empty after all. Deep inside the grey, faint as a watermark, there is a suggestion of features. A suggestion of a person. Someone, once. Someone who was sung wrong long ago, and kept walking, and kept singing, and forgot they had ever been anything else.

Issel looks at that drowned ghost of a face and understands, in one sick drop of knowing, that this is the Choir's mercy and its cruelty in the same breath: it does not kill you. It *joins* you. Every singer was somebody's child. Every singer still knows one thing perfectly, forever—the melody. Nothing else. Only the melody.

The drifting singer pauses beside their hiding place.

Its head tilts. The song threads through the cavern, and inside the thread, thin as wire, a new note appears—a note shaped like curiosity—and Issel realizes with cold certainty that it has heard something. Not a name. Something better. Something it cannot parse.

It has heard Paloma whispering in a dead tongue to a living child, and dead tongues do not fit in songs built to erase the living.

The smooth face bends lower. Lower. Close enough now that Issel can feel the song in their chest, vibrating in the hollows of their bones, prying at the fold beneath their tongue where their name is hidden, working it loose, loosening it, *loosening—*

And Paloma stands up.

She steps out from behind the salt rib into the open grey light, empty-handed, unarmed, a food-taster in a plain dress with flour still on her fingers, and she plants herself between the singer and Issel the way she must have stood between queens and poisoned dishes a thousand mornings—small, calm, absolutely in the way.

The singer stops. The whole Choir's song stumbles—one dropped beat, one wrong interval, the first imperfect note it has sung in three hundred years—because it has met something it does not have a name for either.

A woman full of small deaths. A body that has swallowed poison every day of its life and gotten up the next morning and asked for more.

The smooth face regards her. The song gathers itself, low and questioning, and begins to reach for her—to find her name, to sing her wrong—

And Paloma opens her mouth, and answers it.
