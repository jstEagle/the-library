## 31. Taste of Ash

The Choir came into the caverns the way frost comes into a room—not walking so much as *arriving*, everywhere at once, thinning the air.

Paloma pressed herself against the salt wall with Issel behind her, one arm hooked around the child's middle, and watched the light die.

Not dimmed. Died. The glow of the caverns—the soft amber that had lit their way down from the underground river—drained out of the stone like color leaving a bruise, and what remained was grey. Not shadow-grey, not dusk-grey. The grey of a thing that had never been anything else. The walls went grey. Bram's lantern flame went grey and kept burning anyway, which was worse than if it had gone out. Even the sound of the river running somewhere below them turned grey, flattened, as though someone had laid a cloth over it.

And through it all, the singing.

She had heard it first at the valley's edge, days ago, as a wrongness she couldn't name—a hymn played half a tone flat, a name pronounced carelessly by a stranger. Now it filled the caverns, and Paloma could finally hear what the song was *doing*.

It was unmaking names.

The Choir itself stood perhaps forty paces off, at the mouth of the passage they'd come through, and her eyes kept sliding off them the way a tongue slides off a burned palate. There were seven—no, nine—she couldn't hold the number. They were tall and robed and where faces should have been there was only smoothness, and their mouths—if mouths was the word—moved in perfect unison, and every note they sang took something small away from the world.

A vein of pink in the salt wall faded to white. Then the pink had never been there at all, and Paloma felt the memory of it soften in her own mind like sugar in water.

*They don't kill,* she thought. *They subtract.*

Issel made a sound against her hip. Small. The child's fingers dug into her apron—the good apron, the one with the deep pockets she'd sewn for carrying tasting spoons—and Paloma could feel the tremor coming up through the fabric like a current.

"They're afraid of you," Issel whispered.

Paloma almost laughed. "I'm not exactly bristling with weapons."

"No." Issel's grip tightened. "Listen."

And beneath the Choir's song—beneath the vast flat drone of erasure—there was a hitch. A stumble. A single voice in the unison that kept sliding toward her and then flinching back, the way her own tongue flinched from a bitter taste before her mind knew why.

They were singing the caverns out of existence, and they could not get a purchase on *her*.

* * *

Corvane was ten paces to her left, backed against the wall with his soldiers, and his sword looked absurd. She could see him thinking the same thing. You cannot cut a subtraction. He'd drawn it anyway; men like Corvane drew steel the way other men prayed.

"Fall back," he was saying, low and urgent, to his captain. "Down the side passage—"

"There is no side passage," the captain said. "There was. I can't—" His face worked. "I can't remember which wall it was."

Because it was already gone. The Choir had eaten it. Somewhere between one verse and the next, an entire branch of the cave had stopped having ever existed, and the soldiers who'd been posted there—

Paloma didn't let herself look for them. Looking would mean learning their absence, and she suspected that was how the Choir worked best: you grieved, and grief was a door.

"Issel," she said quietly. "How long do we have?"

"The song has to reach us." The child's voice was steady in the way of children who have seen too much too young and built a floor over the hole. "It walks. Slowly. It has to sing each thing properly before it can take it." A pause. "But when it reaches us, it won't take us. It will take everyone who remembers us. That's how it caught the others. It doesn't need to touch you. It needs the world to forget you, and then you fall through."

"And me?"

Issel looked up at her, and in the grey light the child's dark eyes were the only true color left in the world.

"You keep slipping out of the song," Issel said. "Every time it tries your name, it—it chokes. Like it tasted something bad."

*Like it tasted something bad.*

Paloma stood very still.

She thought about her life. All thirty-one years of it, arranged dish by dish: the first poisoning at seven years old, a single drop in honeyed milk, her grandmother holding her hair back while she vomited into a basin and then, when the retching stopped, feeding her a spoonful more because the dose had been too gentle to teach anything. The arsenic year, when she was twelve, and her gums bled and her fingernails grew white bands like tree rings recording drought. The aconite. The hemlock, twice. The wedding feast where she'd swallowed enough bride's-bane to kill six guests and walked back to the servants' hall and ate bread.

Thirty years of small deaths. Doses measured out by queens and spymasters, each one meant to end her and each one refused, and her body learning, sip by sip, the shape of dying without ever completing it.

She had always called it a curse. The court called it a gift, which was its own kind of insult. Her grandmother had called it something else once, late at night, in a language Paloma hadn't known she remembered until this month: *salka-veth*. Salt-tongued. One who has swallowed the border.

"Oh," Paloma said aloud.

"What?" Corvane snapped. "What is it?"

"It can't unmake me," she said slowly, testing the thought the way she tested a suspect broth—one drop on the tongue, wait, watch. "Because unmaking is just another poison. It's a dose. It's the world being poisoned against you, one note at a time, until your name stops working." She turned to look at the Choir, and the fear in her chest did something strange: it settled. It became professional. "I have been taking that dose my whole life. In smaller sips."

"That's madness," Corvane said.

"Yes," Paloma agreed. "Probably. Everything I'm about to do will be mad. Stay here. Keep Issel behind you."

"Paloma—"

"Corvane." She met his eyes, and for the first time since she'd known him, the spymaster looked simply like a tired man standing in a dying cave. "You gave me three days to surrender a child. Consider those terms withdrawn. And consider—" she almost smiled "—that everything you've fed me might be worth what it cost."

Then she stepped away from the wall, and walked toward the Grey Choir.

* * *

The song hit her like weather.

That was the closest thing she could find for it. Not a sound but a climate, a pressure change, the whole grey weight of it pressing on her skin, her ears, the backs of her eyes. Up close, the Choir resolved slightly—robes that moved without wind, faces like unfired clay—and she could see now that they weren't singing *at* the caverns. They were singing the caverns' names wrong. Deliberately, patiently wrong, the way you'd mispronounce a word over and over until the word lost its nerve and became something else.

She felt them reach for her.

It came as a taste—that was the horror and the wonder of it, that even this arrived on her tongue, because her tongue was where her whole life had happened. A flavor like wet slate, like copper coins left in rain, like the smell of a cellar after a flood. It slid across her palate and searched for the groove of her, the place where *Paloma* sat in the world, the place where any name sits so the world can say it and mean something.

And it found—nothing it could hold.

She understood it then, standing inside the song, understanding arriving whole the way recognition arrives, all at once or never. Every poison she'd ever swallowed had done this same work in miniature. Arsenic had tried to unmake her cell by cell. Hemlock had sung her lungs flat. Each dose had been a small grey choir, a chorus of one, singing her name wrong in her own bloodstream. And each time, her body had learned the trick of it: *this too is a flavor. This too passes. I am still here.*

She was, she realized, the most rehearsed survivor in two kingdoms. Thirty years of dying without finishing. The Choir reached into her and found a woman who had already been erased a thousand times and returned from each one, and their song slipped off her like oil off wet stone.

The nearest singer faltered.

It was tiny—a stutter in the vast unison, a half-beat where the harmony should have been seamless—but in that dead grey place it rang out like a dropped glass. The other voices wavered, adjusted, closed ranks over the gap.

Paloma kept walking.

Her legs shook. She noted this the way she noted symptoms, clinically, the taster's habit: elevated heart rate, cold hands, the sour flood of adrenaline under her tongue. Fear present. Functioning regardless. She had functioned through worse; she had once finished a full tasting while aconite shut her muscles down finger by finger, because the Queen Regent's meal would not serve itself and fear, like poison, was something the body could metabolize if you gave it nowhere to sit.

Ten paces. Five.

The Choir's song bent around her, and she could feel them straining—she could feel their attention, vast and patient and suddenly, for the first time in whatever ages they had done this work, *interested*. They sang her harder. The slate-and-copper taste thickened until her whole mouth was grey, until she was breathing it, and somewhere behind her she heard Issel cry out and Corvane shouting her name in a voice cracked open with something that wasn't strategy.

The song tried her grandmother's name next. Tried Ostrava. Tried the lullaby, the actual melody, dragged out of her memory and sung back wrong, syllables inverted, the tender sounds made cruel.

Paloma opened her mouth.

And sang it back right.

Her voice was nothing—a food-taster's voice, unused to song, rough with smoke and fear—but she had the advantage of thirty years of swallowing things that were meant to kill her, and apparently that included this. She sang the lullaby the way her grandmother had sung it, the rising third, the falling close, the name-sound that Hedda had gone pale at, and the song of the Choir met hers in the grey air and *cracked*.

Color came back.

Not much. A coin-sized patch of amber on the wall above her head, blazing like an ember. But the Choir recoiled—all of them, a rippling stagger, robes snapping as though struck—and the hitch in their unison widened, and Paloma felt something she had never once felt in her careful invisible life at the court of Vell.

She felt *loud*.

"You've been eating the world," she said, and her voice carried strangely, doubled, as if the caverns themselves had decided to repeat her. "One note at a time. One name at a time. Well." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, the old taster's gesture, finishing a course. "I've spent my life tasting what was meant to kill me. You are not the first thing to try."

The lead singer—she thought of it as the lead, though none of them led—turned its smooth face toward her. And in the center of that blankness, for the first time, something appeared: a seam. A line, like lips parting.

Behind her, Issel screamed a warning. Ahead of her, the seam opened, and the Choir began to sing *her*—not at her, not around her, but her entire history, dish by dish, poison by poison, every small death she had survived pulled up out of her memory and performed back at her in perfect unison, and the weight of it was enormous, it was a landslide, it was every queen who had ever used her sitting on her chest at once—

Paloma held her ground.

Because here was the thing the Choir did not know, the thing no one at court had ever bothered to learn, the thing that lived in the gap between *poison-proof* and *poisoned*: surviving a thing is not the same as being untouched by it. Every dose had left sediment. Every small death had deposited its ash somewhere in her, layer on layer, thirty years deep. She was not clean ground the song couldn't grip.

She was saturated ground. Full of ash. Full of everything that had ever failed to kill her.

The song poured in and found nowhere new to go.

Paloma Almeida, royal food-taster to the court of Vell, stood inside the unmaking of the world and let it break against her like a wave against a seawall built of swallowed poisons, and the Grey Choir—for the first time in the long history of their terrible patience—hesitated.

The seam in the leader's face trembled.

And in that trembling, Paloma saw—or understood, or tasted—the truth underneath all their smoothness: they were not strong. They had never been strong. They were *hungry*, hungry the way emptiness is hungry, hungry for names because they had lost their own, and a hunger that meets a woman who cannot be digested does not grow bolder.

It panics.

The Choir's unison shattered into discord. The grey peeled back from the walls in ragged patches, amber bleeding through, and behind her Paloma heard soldiers shouting in restored voices, heard Bram's lantern flare gold, heard Issel laughing and sobbing at once.

She raised her hand—her right hand, the tasting hand—and pointed back down the passage toward the humming wall, toward the door, toward the solstice minute that was burning toward them whether the world held together or not.

"Now," she said, to Corvane, to Issel, to anyone still standing. "While they're reeling. We run for the door—and Corvane, if you're going to draw that sword again, don't. What we need isn't steel."

She was already moving, the Choir's broken song keening at her back, and in her apron pocket her fingers closed around the thing they had been seeking all along without her knowing: her grandmother's recipe book, its pages soft as cloth, its last recipe waiting.

Three ingredients. One shared meal. One pinch of valley salt.

And a table set for enemies, if it came to that—because the door did not care who ate, only that they ate *together*, and somewhere behind her, in the wreck of their song, the Grey Choir was beginning to understand that the woman they could not unmake was about to feed them.
