## 32. The Key Is a Meal

The realization comes to her the way most true things come: sideways, while she is doing something else entirely.

She is kneeling beside Issel in the blue-white glow of the humming wall, pressing a strip of her torn sleeve against the gash on the child's arm where the salt crystal bit them, and she hears herself say—without thinking, the way she says everything that matters—"When this is over, I'm making you soup. Proper soup. Not the thin stuff from the ferry. My grandmother's way."

Issel looks up at her with those too-old eyes and says, in the dialect of a drowned kingdom, *"You cannot cook for the dead."*

Paloma's hands go still.

*Say it again,* she signs, because out here at the edge of everything their words have started failing them both—Issel's voice worn to a thread by what they saw in the Choir's approach, Paloma's Ostravan still stitched together from lullabies and marginalia.

*"You cannot cook for the dead,"* Issel repeats. Then, slower, watching her face: *"But you can cook for the sleeping."*

The wall hums behind them. Somewhere above, muffled through forty feet of salt and stone, the Grey Choir has begun its evening song—the wrong-name song, the one that makes the valley flinch in its sleep. The soldiers Corvane brought are huddled at the cave mouth, refusing to go deeper, refusing to leave, doing the arithmetic of fear. And here in the dark, a six-year-old has just told her the answer she has been bleeding for.

*A meal,* Paloma thinks. *Of course it's a meal.*

It was always going to be a meal. She has spent fifteen years feeding a court that would as soon poison her as look at her, and every single day she has performed the oldest rite there is without knowing its name: *eat first, so that others may eat safely. Take the risk into your own body. Make yourself the proof that this food is good.*

Hospitality. Her grandmother's whole book is hospitality—every recipe annotated not with measurements but with obligations. *Serve warm or you shame the guest.* *Never let the bread see you hurry.* *A table set for two must hold three, in case a stranger comes.*

*In case a stranger comes.*

"Gods," Paloma breathes, and laughs, and the laugh cracks in the middle because she is so tired and so frightened and so suddenly, blindingly certain. "It was never a key you put in a lock. It's a door you open for someone. You don't sacrifice the child—you *feed* them."

*Feed them,* Issel echoes, and something in the small body loosens, like a knot finally slipping. *The Opening Meal. The First Hospitality. Every Ostravan child learns it before they learn to walk.*

"Then teach me," Paloma says. "Every step. We're going to do it right."

* *

There are complications. There are always complications; her grandmother's book never once pretended otherwise.

The rite requires specific things, and Issel counts them off on small salt-crusted fingers: bread baked within sight of the guests who will eat it. Water drawn by the host's own hand. Something sweet, grown where the host was born. Something bitter, grown where the guest was born. Salt from the threshold of the house itself. And fire—not any fire, but flame lit from the household hearth, carried unbroken.

"And a table," Issel adds. "You cannot serve a guest on stone. That is how you feed prisoners."

Paloma sits back on her heels in the dark and takes inventory of a salt cave.

Bread: she has flour in her satchel—the court habit dies hard, she has carried emergency rations since her second year as taster—but no oven, no sight-line, no hearth. Water: the underground river runs black and glassy ten paces away, but Bram's boat went back upriver with the old man an hour ago, and drawing water by hand means leaning over water that the Choir's song has already begun to touch—it shivers now, constantly, like a struck string that won't stop ringing. Sweet thing from where the host was born: Vell honey, maybe, if Hedda's last gift is still sewn into her coat lining—and gods, Hedda, sitting in a cell right now wearing a calm smile and buying them hours. Bitter thing from where the guest was born: Ostrava is a city suspended behind a wall of light; nothing grows there that Paloma can pick.

And the hearth-fire. The Thackeray hearth is a mile above her head and a world away.

"It can't be done," she says, and then hears herself, and stops. "No. That's a lie I tell when I'm tired. Try again."

Issel is quiet a moment. Then the child reaches out and touches the humming wall, flat-palmed, the way they did the first time, and the hum changes pitch—deepens, steadies, almost like a held note.

*"The door remembers its house,"* Issel says softly. *"Before it was a door between worlds, it was a kitchen wall. All doors were walls once, and all walls were homes. If you cook beside it—if you make this place into a home for one night—the door will lend what the home lacks."*

"Lend," Paloma repeats. "Not give."

*"Nothing in Ostrava is given,"* Issel says, with the gravity of a child reciting scripture. *"Only shared. You borrow from the house, and the house borrows from you. That is the whole law."*

Paloma thinks about that law for a long moment. She thinks about a valley founded on the opposite principle—*nothing comes in, nothing goes out, keep your magic and your wars and your hungry gods outside our border*—and about how she has lived her whole life inside that principle like a fly inside a jar, safe and sealed and slowly starving for something she couldn't name until a salt-crusted child appeared on her doorstep and named it.

"All right," she says. "Then we share. Help me up."

* *

Cooking in the dark is not so strange to her, it turns out. She learned her trade blind-tasting in the Queen Regent's kitchens, identifying saffron from turmeric by smell alone, telling arsenic from almond by the shape of the bitterness on her tongue. Her hands know more than her eyes ever did. She empties her satchel onto a flat shelf of salt stone and lets her fingers inventory it: flour wrapped in waxed cloth, a twist of dried plums, the honey jar sewn into her coat (Hedda's voice in her head: *a woman should travel with sweetness, girl, it opens more doors than keys do*), a knife, a tinderbox, half a wheel of hard cheese, the small clay pot of valley salt she has carried since chapter one of her life, essentially, since the day her grandmother pressed it into her hands and said *never be without salt, Pali. Salt is how you promise things.*

No hearth-fire. No bread oven. No table.

She sets Issel to work grinding dried plums with a rounded stone, which gives the child something to do besides listen to the Choir's song seeping down through the rock, and kneels beside the wall.

"I need to borrow," she tells it, feeling foolish, then deciding she doesn't care. Foolishness is a luxury she gave up years ago; she might as well take it back tonight. "Fire. A hearth. An oven, if you've got one. And—I don't know—a table. We'll return everything. Well. Most of it. Some of it gets eaten, that's rather the point."

The hum shifts. Warms. It is absurd, but the wall sounds almost amused.

Light blooms.

Not the cold blue shimmer of the door-seam—this is amber, low, hearth-colored, rising out of the salt itself the way heat rises off summer stones. Where the light touches, the cave wall *changes*. Not transforms—she watches closely, tasting the air for magic the way she'd taste soup for rot—but reveals. Faint shapes surface in the translucent salt like things sunk in ice: the ghost-outline of an oven door. A shelf. A long scar in the floor that could, if you were generous and very tired, be read as a table.

*"It's showing you the kitchen that was here,"* Issel whispers, plums forgotten. *"Before the kingdom folded itself up small. This was someone's house. Maybe my grandmother's grandmother's. Maybe yours."*

Paloma puts her palm flat on the warm salt and feels, unmistakably, the memory of fire.

* *

The bread is the hard part, and the true part.

She has no yeast. She stands in the amber light turning this problem over when Issel tugs her sleeve and points at the sourdough starter question with the practicality of a child raised in a city of bakers—or perhaps raised nowhere, raised running, but taught by someone along the way that bread is a relationship, not a product. *Ask the wall,* the child signs.

So Paloma asks the wall for leaven, and the wall—in exchange, she will understand later, for the story she tells while kneading, the whole unspooling tale of a girl in a valley who ate poisons so strangers wouldn't die—the wall gives her a fist-sized lump of living dough, cool and slightly sour-smelling, that surfaces from the salt like a pearl from an oyster. Older than kingdoms, probably. A starter passed hand to hand across the folding-up of a country, waiting in its pantry of stone for someone to come home and need it.

She feeds it flour and river water and talks to it, because her grandmother talked to hers, and some instructions you follow even past the end of the world.

While the dough rises under a cloth made from her other sleeve, she builds the rest of the meal out of almost nothing:

Honey from Vell, thinned with warm water, for the sweet-of-the-host's-home. She tastes it first—habit, sacrament—and lets it sit on her tongue a moment longer than safety requires, because tonight she is not tasting against death; she is tasting *for* life, and she wants to know exactly what she's promising.

For the bitter-of-the-guest's-home, Issel leads her to a crevice near the humming wall where something impossible grows: a low plant with silver-grey leaves, rooted in salt, that Paloma has never seen in the valley's meadows. It smells like wormwood's stern cousin, like medicine, like grief. Issel picks three leaves with ceremony and does not explain, and Paloma does not ask, because some ingredients announce themselves.

Salt from the threshold: this is easy, this is the one thing she has never lacked. She opens the little clay pot and the smell alone nearly undoes her—her grandmother's kitchen, rain on the cottage roof, a rough voice singing syllables she thought were nonsense until this week. Valley salt, border salt, the salt of a place that kept her safe by keeping everything else out. Tonight it crosses the line it was dug to defend. There's a symmetry there she doesn't have the strength to admire properly.

Water drawn by the host's own hand: this is the frightening part, and she does it anyway. She kneels at the black river, which shivers and shivers, and dips her cupped palms, and the water is cold as confession and tastes of nothing at all—which, for water that the Grey Choir's song has been chewing on for days, strikes her as a small miracle. Unspoiled. Or perhaps simply *uninterested in*. Perhaps even the unmaking cannot be bothered with something so plain. She decides to take it as a blessing either way.

And the fire. The hearth-flame, carried unbroken.

There is no flame to carry. But when she mentions this to Issel, the child walks to the revealed ghost of an oven door, presses both hands to the salt, and says something long and formal in Ostravan—a request, by the cadence of it, polite as a knock—and a tongue of amber light rises out of the stone and settles into the air like a bird landing, and stays. It gives no heat she can feel on her skin, but when she holds the flat of her knife above it, the blade warms. When she sets the risen loaf near it, the crust begins, impossibly, gently, to bake.

*"Hearth-light,"* Issel explains. *"It burns on being needed. When you don't need it anymore, it goes back to sleep."*

Everything burns on being needed, Paloma thinks, looking at the child. Everything worth keeping.

* *

The baking takes hours, or minutes; time in the caves has gone soft around the edges, stretched by the Choir's distant song into something slow and taffy-like. She works. That's the whole of it, the whole salvation of it: she works. Kneading when her hands want to shake. Measuring when her mind wants to spiral toward Hedda in her cell, toward Corvane and his soldiers at the cave mouth, toward the solstice minute approaching like a wave she can see building far offshore. The work holds her the way it has always held her. A body cannot tremble and fold dough at the same time; she has known this since she was nine years old and discovered that grief could be temporarily out-kneaded.

Issel helps, in the way of children helping—earnest, chaotic, indispensable. The child arranges the bitter leaves in a pattern on the salt-stone table-scar that Paloma recognizes, with a jolt, from the marginalia: the same spiral, the same inward turn. *Set the bitter at the center,* her grandmother had written, *so the sweetness has somewhere to go.*

"What happens," Paloma asks quietly, while the loaf sings its faint crackling song in the ghost-oven, "if we do it wrong? If I miss a step?"

Issel considers this with the seriousness the question deserves.

*"My teacher said the rite fails only one way,"* the child signs. *"If the host eats apart from the guest. Or serves from fear instead of welcome. Everything else is forgiven. Bread forgives. Honey forgives. Even the bitter leaf forgives, if you put it in the middle."*

"From fear instead of welcome," Paloma repeats.

She thinks about Corvane upstairs with his soldiers and his complicated grief, serving his poisoned dishes from fear. She thinks about the Queen Regent, who tasted every meal herself for the first year after the old king died, and called it caution, and was perhaps just very hungry and very alone. She thinks about the Grey Choir, who unmake countries, and wonders—with a chill that has nothing to do with the cave-cold—whether they began as hosts once, whether erasure is just hospitality curdled into control, a table set so strictly that anything unwelcome gets wiped from it entirely.

She looks down at the loaf, at the honey, at the three bitter leaves in their careful spiral, at the pot of valley salt open beside them, and she makes her choice the way she makes all her real choices: not with a declaration but with her hands.

"Not fear," she says. "Welcome. Even if it kills us. *Especially* if it kills us—that's rather been the family trade."

Issel laughs. It's the first time Paloma has heard it, a small cracked-bell sound, and it is the best thing she has heard in thirty-one chapters of a life she suddenly wants very much to keep.

* *

The loaf comes out golden-brown in the amber light, which is a trick of the hearth-glow and possibly a mercy, because she has no idea what color things truly are down here anymore. It smells like every good morning she has ever had, compressed into one.

She sets the table on the stone scar: bread broken, not cut—her grandmother was firm on this, *a knife at the first meal is a fence*—honey in a saucer improvised from a tin lid, the bitter leaves at the center, salt in its clay pot between them where both can reach, water in the one cup they have, which they will pass back and forth, because the rite apparently requires sharing vessels and Issel delivers this requirement with the particular relish of a child announcing homework.

Then Issel does something unexpected. The child takes Paloma's hand and turns it palm-up, and places a fifth item on it: a single grain of something, tiny and dark and gleaming, that Issel has been carrying sewn into the hem of their ruined clothes since before Paloma met them. Since before the crossing. Since the last kitchen in Ostrava-as-it-was.

*"Wheat from the door's own garden,"* Issel says. *"The host provides everything else. The guest provides this. So the meal belongs to both, and neither can say they fed the other out of pity."*

Paloma closes her fingers around the grain. It is warm. Of course it is warm. Everything in this cave runs on being needed.

Above them, muffled but no longer distant, the Choir's song swells—and this time it is not the wrong-name song. It is something faster, hungrier, a sound like frost climbing glass, like pages tearing, and the black river's shivering climbs to a visible tremble and the soldiers at the cave mouth begin shouting each other's names in panic. Corvane's voice cuts through it, sharp with command and, underneath, something that might be fear or might be recognition.

The solstice is coming. She can feel it in the wall's hum, which has quickened like a pulse before waking.

Issel looks up at her across the small stone table, in the amber light, in the mouth of a door older than memory, and asks the only question left:

*"Are you ready to serve?"*

Paloma lifts the bread.

"Yes," she says. "Let's eat."
