## 33. Solstice Opening

**Paloma**

The dark beneath the mountain tasted like the inside of a bell.

Paloma had cooked a meal in a cave, by lantern-light, on a folding iron grate Bram had hauled down the underground river on his back. She had cooked it from her grandmother's book, page by page, following not just the instructions but the silences between them—the pauses where Ostravan cooks would have known what to do without being told. Salt from the valley's own crust. Water from the river that ran under the world. Barley she'd begged from the baker, who had pressed an extra sack into her arms and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of blessing.

And honey. One jar of Hedda's wildflower honey, saved for three years against no occasion in particular.

The steam rose off the pot and did something steam should not do. It hung. It hung in the cave air like a curtain drawn aside halfway, and through it Paloma could smell—not imagine, *smell*—bread baking somewhere that was not this cave. Woodsmoke and oranges. A harbor she had never seen.

"It's starting," Issel whispered. The child's hand found hers. Small fingers, salt-cracked knuckles. "She says the table is set."

"She?"

"The door." Issel looked up at her with eyes that were far too old for the face they lived in. "Doors have names here. Hers is the Long Welcome."

Behind them, boots on stone. Corvane's soldiers had stopped pretending to be anything other than frightened men holding torches, and Corvane himself stood apart, his sword still sheathed, his face turned toward the steaming pot as if he were listening to it. He had not spoken since the Choir's song had first threaded itself into the caverns—an ugly sound, like a hymn played backward, like a name pronounced wrong on purpose.

Now the song was faltering.

Paloma felt it before she heard it: a loosening behind her sternum, the way the air changes when a held breath finally lets go. The Grey Choir had been unmaking the world at the edges—color draining from the salt walls, the memory of her grandmother's voice going thin and papery in her head—and now, now, the unmaking stuttered. Stopped. Somewhere above them, at the mouth of the valley, something vast was losing its grip on a door that had been fed.

"The meal is accepted," Issel said. The child's voice didn't shake. "It was always going to be a meal. I'm sorry I couldn't say."

"You said," Paloma answered. "In your way. You've been saying it since my doorstep." She squeezed the small hand. "Eat, little key. Both of us. That's the rite."

They ate together, one spoon between them, the way family eats when there isn't enough and there has never been enough and somehow it has always been enough anyway. The barley was good. The honey made it almost sweet.

The wall began to hum.

Not the low tremor Issel's touch had coaxed from it days ago—a true hum, a chord, a note that Paloma felt in her teeth and her ribs and the scarred old lining of her stomach where every poison she'd ever swallowed had left its mark. Light came up out of the salt itself, amber and slow, like evening poured upward. And where the humming wall had been—

Where it had been there was a street.

A cobbled street, wet with rain that fell in some other sky. Lanterns burning in windows the color of honeycomb. A city built up a hillside in terraces, every roof tiled in blue, every doorway carved with the curling script that lived in the margins of her grandmother's book. Gulls—gulls, over a sea that must lie beyond the hill—cried once, and the sound arrived in the cave whole and undiluted, as if the distance between worlds were no wider than a threshold.

The solstice minute had come. The longest night, and a door standing open in it, full of borrowed evening.

Faces appeared in the street. Ostravan faces. An old woman with flour to her elbows. A boy carrying a fishing net over one shoulder. A soldier whose uniform matched no nation Paloma knew, standing very straight, weeping without any shame about it at all. They peered out at the cave—at the torches, the soldiers, the pot, the child—and Paloma watched wonder move across their faces like weather.

One of them spoke. An elderly man, leaning on a cane, his voice arriving across the impossible distance as clearly as if he stood beside her:

"*Sul veth anna?*" Is the table laid?

And Paloma's mouth, trained all her life to answer questions quickly and truthfully, shaped the answer before her mind consent, in her grandmother's tongue, in the dialect of a dead country:

"Laid and eaten. You're welcome here."

**Issel**

Issel had crossed the world twice: once out of Ostrava, once into Vell, and both times alone, and both times the crossing had felt like being unwritten a little.

This time there were hands to hold.

The door opened the way Grandmother Sefa had promised it would in the last words anyone spoke to Issel: not with thunder, but with evening. Warm light spilled across the cold salt floor and lay there like a blanket someone had spread for a tired child, and Issel thought, *that's what it's for, doors are for coming home,* and then cried without deciding to, the way you sneeze.

The people in the street were real. Issel knew some of them. The flour woman was Tamsin, who had run the bakery on Cordwainer's Rise until the Choir came singing up the coast and the city folded itself away to keep from being erased. The boy with the net was Rell, who used to steal apples from the harbor trees. They had been waiting inside the preserved evening of Ostrava for—how long? Time moved strangely behind the door; Rell's face said years, though for him perhaps it had been a season.

Tamsin saw Issel and dropped her flour scoop.

"*Issel.*" Her voice cracked across the threshold. "*Anna sul!*" Oh, look at you!

And then the street was full of voices, all of them speaking the language that lived in Paloma's lullabies, and Issel stood in the middle of it and translated without thinking, because that was the job now—the inside half of the door, the key that had been sent out so that someone on the far side could turn the lock.

*Someone on the far side.* Issel looked up at Paloma.

The woman stood very still in the amber light, tears running down her face into the corners of her mouth, and she was answering Tamsin. In Ostravan. Fluently. With the harbor vowels and everything, the vowels Issel had been told had died out everywhere except behind the door.

"You can go home too," Issel said quietly, in the valley tongue, so only Paloma would understand. "That's what I figured out. The door doesn't want me back. It wants *you*. It wants everyone who carried the language out in their mouths and kept it alive in kitchens. You're not the outside half of me." A breath. "You're the other inside half. There were two all along."

Paloma looked down. For a long moment she said nothing, and the gulls cried, and the rain fell in the other world, and somewhere above them the Choir's song thinned to a thread.

"I spent my whole life learning how to stay invisible," Paloma said at last. "And the whole time I was a door."

"You were never invisible," Issel said. "I found you. First try."

**Corvane**

Corvane had prepared himself for sorcery. He had prepared reports, contingencies, three separate theories of the valley's border and a fourth he kept in a drawer because it sounded mad even to him. He had prepared to hate whatever opened in that wall, because things that opened in walls had cost him a brother and a homeland he could barely remember, and hatred was a load-bearing wall in the house of himself.

He had not prepared for the smell of orange peel.

It reached him across the cavern, riding the amber light, and it undid forty years of discipline in a single breath. His mother had kept dried orange peel in a tin above the stove. She had been born on the far side of some vanished border herself—she had never said which one; he had never asked, because asking meant admitting the family story had holes in it big enough to lose a kingdom through—and on winter nights she had boiled the peel with cloves and the whole house had smelled like a country that no longer existed anywhere except their kitchen.

The city in the light had terraces. He could see laundry lines strung between buildings. Someone was shaking out a rug. Ordinary life, suspended, preserved, *waiting*—and Corvane understood with terrible clarity that the spymaster of Vell was looking at the thing his entire profession existed to prevent: proof that the world was larger than the maps of it.

His soldiers had lowered their torches. One had knelt. Another was crying openly, and Corvane found he could not summon the will to reprimand him, because Corvane's own eyes were burning and his throat was closed around a word in a language he did not know he knew until it climbed out of him:

"*Sul veth.*"

The table is laid.

An old man on the far side turned toward the sound. Looked at Corvane's face—looked at his cheekbones, his coloring, the heritage written plainly on him that he had never learned to read—and smiled with unbearable gentleness.

"*Veth, miro. Sul veth anna.*" Yes, child. Your table is laid too.

Corvane sheathed his sword. It seemed suddenly like a very small object to be holding in front of an open door.

"The Regent sent me to close this," he said aloud, to no one, to everyone. His voice came out wrecked. "I don't know what I'm going to tell her yet."

"Tell her the truth," Paloma said. "It's rarer than magic in that court. It might get further."

**Bram**

Bram had poled the underground river for thirty-one years, ever since a woman with flour-white forearms and a baby wrapped in a shawl had dragged him, drowned and blue-lipped, out of the shallows below the mill race, breathed him back with methods he never learned the names of, and refused his every attempt at payment with the same phrase: *"You'll owe the next stranger instead."*

He had spent thirty-one years wondering who the next stranger would be, and whether he'd know them when they came.

Then a taster's cottage had started leaving its lamp in the window, and a child had appeared at the ferry landing smelling of salt, and Bram had known. That was the worst and best of it. No doubt at all. Just the quiet click of a debt finding its shape after decades of floating loose.

Now he stood at the back of a crowd that wasn't a crowd—one woman, one child, one spymaster, six soldiers, and himself—and watched a country come home.

He didn't understand the words passing through the doorway. Didn't need to. Bram had ferried enough strangers to read the grammar of arrival: the way the old woman reached out and touched Paloma's face like checking whether a fire was warm; the way the boy with the net laughed and the laugh echoed off the salt and came back doubled, once from each side of the door; the way the child Issel stood in the middle of it all with their chin up, doing the work of a bridge and bearing it like a crown.

People were already stepping through. Slowly. One at a time, hands first, the way you test ice. A fisherman. Two sisters holding each other's wrists. A very old man who paused at the threshold and looked back at the cave, at the valley beyond it that he had never seen, and bowed—to the cave, to the valley, to the whole stubborn poison-proof land that had hidden his people's children without knowing it.

Bram wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand.

"Thirty-one years," he said to Corvane, conversationally, because grief and joy both wanted talking and the spymaster was the only one standing near. "I owed a stranger. Turns out the stranger was a whole country."

Corvane, to his credit, only nodded. "Mine was a kitchen," he said. "Turns out the kitchen survived."

**Hedda**

Two cells away and one floor up, in the keep of Vell, Hedda felt the solstice turn.

She had no window. She had a straw pallet, a bucket, a guard named Ottel who brought her tea and pretended not to enjoy her commentary, and, in the marrow of her old bones, a certainty like a struck bell.

She had spent sixty years in this valley learning what it felt like when the border held tight: a faint pressure behind the eyes, the taste of copper on snowmelt, herbs that grew straight and never strange. All her life that pressure had been constant, so constant it became silence.

Tonight the silence broke.

It broke the way ice breaks on the thaw river—not all at once, but in long singing cracks, running away into the distance, and underneath it the water moving, moving, *moving*, alive after a frozen age. Magic, leaking gently into Vell through ten thousand softened seams. Hedda sat up on her pallet in the dark and laughed until Ottel banged on the bars and asked what was so funny.

"Everything," she told him. "Nothing. Pass us your tea, lad, and fetch a pen. When they let me out of here tomorrow—and they will, mark me—I've ledgers to correct and a Regent to educate, and I'll want my handwriting steady."

Far below the keep, under the mountain, under the river, a door stood open on a city full of evening, and Hedda, who had chosen to stay behind and pay for her friends' head start with her own freedom, felt the bill come due and found she didn't mind the price at all.

Some inheritances weren't blood. Some were just knowing which way the wind had turned before anyone else smelled it.

She drank Ottel's terrible tea and listened to the ice crack all the way to the edge of the world.

**Paloma**

The exiles came through in ones and twos, then families, then a slow river of them, blinking in torchlight, touching the salt walls like pilgrims. Some carried bundles. Some carried nothing but themselves. A girl of perhaps twelve stepped across, looked at Issel, and shouted something delighted and rude, and Issel laughed so hard they had to sit down on the cave floor, and Paloma decided she would learn what that joke meant if it took her the rest of her life.

The Regent's summons could come tomorrow. Corvane's report, whatever shape it took, would land on the Queen Regent's desk like a stone in a still pond. The valley would argue about what had happened under its skin for a generation. None of it mattered tonight.

What mattered was the doorway, and the hour, and the fact that the door would not stay open forever—it was already narrowing at the top, amber light compressing into a band, the evening on the far side deepening toward the true night of the solstice. The Long Welcome had one more gift to give and a closing hour to keep.

Tamsin the baker caught Paloma's sleeve at the threshold. Up close, the old woman smelled exactly like her grandmother's kitchen, and Paloma's heart did something complicated and painful and glad.

"*You stayed out here,*" Tamsin said, in Ostravan, wonder and accusation in the same breath. "*All these years. Carrying the tongue in your mouth like a coal under ash. Who taught you?*"

"My grandmother. Alzbeta." Paloma gave the name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "She called herself Thackeray. She baked bread for a village that never knew why her loaves tasted like somewhere else."

Tamsin went still. Then she said a word—a name, an older name—and Paloma felt it land in her chest like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was in her.

"That was her," Paloma managed. "Before Vell."

"*We have her wedding registry,*" Tamsin said. "*We kept everything. We kept everything because we believed someone would come back for it.*" The old woman's eyes went bright and fierce. "*Come and see. Come and see what you are.*"

Beyond her shoulder, the city glowed, terraced and blue-roofed and patient, holding its centuries of evening like a cupped flame. The band of light narrowed another finger's width. On the cave side, Issel stood close against Paloma's leg, and Corvane stood with his hand off his sword, and Bram stood with his pole ready, and every soul in the cavern waited to see which way the taster would turn.

Paloma looked at the child beside her. At the door ahead of her. At the whole improbable architecture of a life rebuilt in a single night.

"One condition," she said, and her voice carried to every ear in both worlds at once. "Nobody closes anything while my family's still walking through it."

She took Issel's hand.

And together they stepped toward the light.
