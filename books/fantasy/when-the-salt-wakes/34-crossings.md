## 34. Crossings

The door opened like a held breath released.

Paloma felt it before she saw it—a warmth in the salt wall, a hum climbing from the floor through the soles of her boots into her teeth, and then the light. Not firelight, not sunlight, but something older: amber evening light, the light of a place where the sun had agreed to stay low and golden forever. It spilled across the cavern floor in a long warm tongue, and where it touched, the salt crystals glittered like a city at dawn.

Issel's hand found hers and squeezed so hard the small bones ached.

Faces appeared in the light first. Then shoulders, hands, whole bodies—people stepping out of the glow as if stepping out of a doorway into a garden. They came slowly, blinking, some weeping without sound, some laughing in that language Paloma had known all her life without knowing it. An old man went to his knees on the salt and pressed his forehead to the ground. A woman carried a birdcage with no bird in it, holding it against her chest like an infant.

"They were waiting," Issel said, in the grandmother's dialect, the words coming easier now with every hour. "In the amber evening. All this time. Waiting for the door."

"How many?"

"Everyone who was left." Issel's voice caught. "Everyone who believed someone would come back for them."

* * *

They emerged by dozens. The cavern filled with them—Ostravans in clothes of a fashion Paloma recognized only distantly, the cut of a collar here, the embroidery of a sleeve there, echoes of things she'd seen in her grandmother's recipe book margins. Some were old enough to remember the crossing themselves, the day their kingdom folded itself away to escape the Grey Choir. Others were young, born inside the preserved city, who had never touched unambered daylight.

A girl of perhaps fifteen stepped through carrying nothing but a clay flute, and stopped when she saw Paloma. She looked at Paloma's face the way one looks at a reflection in water that has shifted.

"You have her mouth," the girl said. "The woman who wrote the recipes."

"You knew my grandmother?"

"She taught us songs while she could still visit." The girl's eyes were bright with tears she refused to spill. "Before the door began to close. She said her granddaughter would open it someday, though she didn't know which granddaughter, or how." She reached out and touched Paloma's wrist, lightly, formally, the way one greets in Ostrava. "My name is Vessia. I am glad you learned the words."

Paloma could not answer for a moment. Her throat had closed around something too large for speech. When she finally managed it, all she said was, "There's soup at my cottage. And bread the baker makes. If—" and then she stopped, because she was crying too, and the sentence dissolved.

Vessia smiled. "We know about your bread. The door shows us things, sometimes. Small things. We watched you taste poison for years and never flinch." She looked around at the crowded cavern, at the exiles pressing gently forward into a world none of them had stood in for a generation or more. "You fed yourself to the court so you could feed us in the end. That is very Ostravan of you."

* * *

Bram stood at the edge of the underground river with his cap in his hands, watching the light pour out of the wall.

He'd poled them here through the dark in silence, except once, when the caverns glowed with veins of luminous salt and he'd whispered, "Your grandmother showed me these same veins, forty years back. Pulled me out of the river after my boat broke. Said the valley keeps what the world throws away, and sometimes that's a mercy." He hadn't spoken since. Now he cleared his throat.

"I could go," he said, not to anyone in particular. "Through there. My mother's mother came from over the mountains somewhere east. Might be there's folk in that city who'd know her name."

Hedda would have been beside him, but Hedda was miles above them in a cell, trading her freedom for Corvane's false trail, and Bram's jaw worked around her absence like a man chewing gristle.

An exile—an old woman with hands stained blue to the wrists, a dyer, maybe—overheard him and approached. She spoke slow Valley-tongue with a thick music underneath it. "The city is full," she said kindly. "Full of everyone we saved. But there is room always for those who bring skills." She looked at his hands, scarred from rope and current. "And room for those who bring boats."

Bram looked at the river behind him—the dark water, the cold, the valley he'd ferried across his whole life, every wedding and funeral and market day. He thought of Hedda in her cell, and of the debt he owed a dead woman, and of the fact that debts are paid forward or not at all.

"Someone has to pole the next ones down," he said finally. "When they come. There'll be more. There's always more." He put his cap back on. "But I'll walk through and look. Just to look. Just to see if anyone knows the name Marren."

The dyer woman nodded solemnly, as if a great contract had been signed, which perhaps it had.

* * *

Corvane arrived at the head of twenty soldiers, and the soldiers stopped dead when they saw the light.

He had expected resistance. He had prepared speeches, threats, the Regent's sealed orders. What he had not prepared for was an old man in the crowd turning toward him with an expression of dawning recognition and saying, in a language Corvane hadn't heard since he was four years old, "Little Corvan? Little Corvan, who hid under my market stall during the last days? You lived?"

Corvane's hand froze on his sword hilt.

His memories of home were few and worn smooth from handling: a courtyard with a lemon tree, his mother's voice singing a counting song, the smell of dust and hot stone, and then the road, the road, the endless road, until the valley swallowed them and magic could follow them no further and his parents slowly forgot everything in the safety of a land where forgetting was easy. He had built his entire self on top of that absence. Spymaster. Watcher. A man who collected other people's secrets because he had lost his own.

"Your mother sang," the old man continued, coming closer, ignoring the soldiers entirely. "Badly. Off-key, always off-key, and she'd laugh at herself. She gave my wife bread when we had nothing. Is she—"

"Dead," Corvane said. His voice came out strange. Young. "Both of them. In the fever year."

The old man bowed his head. Around him, other faces turned—faces that might have known Corvane's parents, his grandparents, the lemon tree courtyard. A whole nation's worth of memory, kept safe in amber, while he had kept his fragments alone in the dark for thirty years.

One of the soldiers shifted nervously. "Sir? Your orders?"

"Wait outside," Corvane said, and was surprised at how steady it sounded. "All of you. This is—" He searched for the word and found it in the other language, the dead language that wasn't dead anymore. "*Sella.* A gathering. No soldier enters a *sella* armed."

The soldiers withdrew up the tunnel, exchanging looks. Corvane remained, sword still belted at his hip because removing it felt impossible, standing in the amber light with tears running freely down a face trained never to show anything.

Paloma approached him carefully. She had prepared for him too—prepared lies, bargains, a fight. Instead she found a man undone.

"You could go through," she said quietly. "It's open. It would take you."

"I know." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, angry at the tears. "I know what it would take."

"It wouldn't take anything. It's offering."

"That's what frightens me." He looked at the light for a long time. "I've spent thirty years being the man who lost everything. Take that away and who's left?" He laughed, a broken sound. "Listen to me. Confessing to the poison-taster."

"The food's better than you'd expect," Paloma said.

Something almost like a smile crossed his face. Then he straightened, and she watched him do it—watched him gather the scattered pieces of himself and hold them, deliberately, choosing.

"I'm not going," he said. "I have work here. A Regent to serve. A herbalist to get out of a cell, if your plan holds." He reached into his coat and drew out a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Inside lay a dried lemon rind, brown with age, and a scrap of embroidered cloth. "From the courtyard. From the tree. I've carried them since I was six and couldn't tell you why."

"What will you do with them?"

"Send them through." His voice cracked on the word *through*. "If the door accepts memories instead of men—if it took mine from me when I sent Corvane's letter of surrender through the light—" He stopped, steadied himself. "Let them keep this. Let someone remember the tree, even if the tree is gone. Let someone sing the counting song to a child and say, this belonged to a boy who made it out, and he chose to stay and guard the ones who stayed behind."

Paloma took the bundle from him. "I'll see it through."

"No." He closed her fingers back over it. "You cook. You know the rites. Do it properly. Give it to them the way your grandmother would have—with bread, and salt, and the words."

So Paloma did. She set a small table of stacked crates at the threshold of the light, and laid out bread from her cottage larder, and a dish of cave salt, and Corvane's lemon rind and cloth upon a clean napkin. The old man from the market stall stood witness. Vessia played three notes on a flute that made the whole cavern ache. And Paloma spoke the words from her grandmother's book—the hospitality rite, the oldest one, the one that had opened the door in the first place:

"*What is given freely is never lost. What is shared across a threshold belongs to both sides of it.*"

When she lifted the bundle toward the light, the light leaned in. It took the offering the way a tide takes a flower set on water—gently, wholly, without remainder. For a moment every exile in the cavern went silent, and then, softly, from deep within the amber, someone began to sing. A counting song. Badly. Off-key.

Corvane made a sound like a man being unmade and remade at once, and turned his face away, and let himself weep in front of witnesses for the first time since he was six years old.

* * *

The crossings continued through the night.

Some valley folk came down the river after word spread—word traveled strangely fast, as if the valley itself were whispering. A young shepherd named Tolo, whose family had farmed the eastern slopes for generations, arrived breathless and asked to see the door, and stood before it for an hour, and then asked, "Is it true there are mountains inside that never run out of grass?" And when Vessia answered, "True, and the wolves sing in harmony," Tolo sat down heavily on a crate and said, "Then I'm going. Someone should be there who can whistle back." His mother arrived two hours later to drag him home and stayed to argue with the dyer woman about wool dyes, and by dawn she was going too, and Tolo looked embarrassed and delighted in equal measure.

Others came only to look. A ferryman's widow touched the threshold light and pulled her hand back as if from a stove—not from pain, but from the weight of choice. A baker's apprentice peered in, sighed the sigh of the deeply practical, and said, "Who'd eat my rolls?" and walked back up the tunnel whistling.

That was the way of it, Paloma saw. The door didn't pull. It offered, and each person weighed the offer against the gravity of their own life, and most found that their lives—even hard lives, even lonely ones—held them like roots hold a hillside.

By the gray hour before dawn, the flow of exiles thinned. The last to emerge was a very old woman, carried through on a chair by two younger men, who surveyed the cavern, the river, the gathered crowd, and announced in ringing tones, "Well. It smells like salt and strangers. Perfectly acceptable." And the whole cavern laughed, Ostravan and valley-born together, the first shared laughter of a new age.

* * *

Paloma stood at the threshold with Issel as the light began to deepen, the solstice minute approaching its end.

She felt the change in the air before she understood it—the faintest cooling at the edges of the amber, the hum lowering by a half-tone, the way a song signals its final verse. Behind her, the last exiles were embracing the newly arrived, trading names and directions and promises. Ahead of her, the door waited, patient as it had been for a hundred years.

"We have until the sun stands still," Issel said softly. "Then it closes. Truly closes, this time—not sleeping. Finished."

Paloma looked back at the cavern—at Bram arguing cheerfully with the old dyer woman about river routes; at Tolo already halfway through the light with his sheepdog, who had opinions; at Vessia teaching three valley children a clapping game in two languages; at Corvane sitting apart with his empty hands open on his knees, lighter than she had ever seen him, staring at the place where his childhood had gone.

Then she looked at the child beside her, who had come out of the snow to her door caked in salt, who had chosen, impossibly, to stay.

"Whatever happens next," Paloma said, "you're not going back in there. I told you. I refuse. We find another way—or we make one."

Issel's grip tightened on her hand. But the child's eyes had gone wide, fixed past Paloma's shoulder, on the threshold of the light.

"There's someone there," Issel whispered. "At the door. On the other side. They've been waiting there the whole time—they're not leaving with the others." The child's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Paloma. They're wearing grandmother's shawl."
