## 35. Issel's Decision

The light on the other side of the door is the color of evenings that never ended.

Issel stands at the threshold with salt dust on their bare feet and the whole of Ostrava breathing out at them — warm air, bread-smell, a bell somewhere ringing a note Issel has known since before knowing. The city hangs in its amber evening like a fruit preserved in honey. People are coming through. Old people first, then families, then a man carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, and every one of them pauses when they pass Issel, and touches their head, and says something soft in the old tongue.

*Small one. Key-child. Go home.*

Home.

The word should fit like a shoe. Instead it sits beside Issel like a stranger at a table.

Behind them, the cavern is loud. Soldiers who came to drag them away are standing very still with their hats in their hands. Corvane is kneeling in the salt with his eyes wet, whispering to a country he lost when he was small. And Paloma —

Paloma is holding the door.

She has both hands flat against the humming wall, and her arms are shaking, and there is white frost creeping up her sleeves where the Choir's song still tries to reach her, and she does not let go. She said she would hold it until the last person passed. She has been holding it for what feels like all the winters Issel has been alive.

She looks over her shoulder. Not at the door. At Issel.

"Whenever you're ready," she says, and her voice cracks down the middle, and she pretends it didn't.

That is the thing about Paloma. Issel learned it over soup, over words traded back and forth across a wooden table like coins. Paloma never tells you what to choose. She just makes sure you know she'll still be there after.

* * *

Issel walks toward the light.

They have to. They have to see it up close, have to stand in it, have to let it touch them, because choosing without looking is how the Grey Choir wins — that's what the teachers said before the teachers stopped, one by one, like candles pinched out. So Issel steps through the doorway and the amber evening folds around them and Ostrava is *there*, real, the towers and the terraces and the orange trees growing out of walls, everything exactly as it was on the day the country closed its eyes.

It is beautiful.

It is also empty in a way that makes Issel's teeth ache. The streets are full of people but no wind moves the laundry. The bell rings but no pigeons scatter. The bread-smell comes from ovens no one is tending. Everything here is held, the way you hold your breath. Everyone who stayed behind chose stasis over erasure, and now they wait for the door, and here is the door, and here is Issel standing in it.

A woman approaches. She has Issel's same dark curl to her hair and Issel's same salt-pale skin, and Issel knows her without knowing how, the way you know a song from three notes.

"Mother-of-my-mother," the woman says. Her voice sounds like water finding its old channel again. "You came back."

"I was sent," Issel says. Their voice works here better than it ever did in Vell. Words come easier in this air. That frightens them more than anything else has. "I was sent because the door needed a key on each side."

"And now the door is open." The woman kneels. Her hands hover near Issel's face, not quite touching, as if she's afraid Issel might be another thing held in amber. "Come inside, little key. Come home. The waiting is over."

Inside.

Issel looks past her, down the long street, at all the held faces turned toward them, hopeful, patient, frozen at the edge of joy. If Issel walks in, the door will take them and keep them. That was the bargain written before Issel could read. A key goes into its lock. A key stays in its lock.

If Issel walks in, they will never taste Paloma's soup again. Never hear Bram sing off-key on the night river. Never see Hedda scowl at a plant and be right about it. Never learn the rest of the lullaby — the verse Paloma promised she was making up, the one that wasn't finished yet.

The amber light is kind and it smells like bread and it wants Issel the way a bed wants a tired child.

And Issel thinks: *a locked room is a locked room even if it's beautiful.*

They think: *the Choir erased our name. The valley hid our grandmothers. Everyone always decided for us where we would be.*

Not this time.

* * *

"Paloma," Issel calls, turning back to the doorway.

Her face. Issel will carry her face forever — the trying-so-hard not to hope, the hands still flat against the humming stone, the sweat and salt and stubbornness of her. She has been holding a door open for hours with her whole body and she would hold it open for years if Issel asked, and she would let Issel go without a word if Issel chose it, and both of those things together are why.

"The door needs me to stay," Issel says. "Doesn't it. If I go in."

Paloma's jaw tightens. "The inscription says the key remains. Yes."

"And you'd let me."

"I'd hate it," Paloma says. "Every day. But it's yours to give. Nobody gets to spend you but you." Her voice shakes on the last word. "Not a court. Not a kingdom. Not even a whole country waiting in the light."

The woman behind Issel makes a small sound. Grief or protest; here they sound the same.

Issel looks at her. At all of them, the held faces, the still city, the beautiful breathless everywhere. And Issel understands something suddenly, the way you understand a recipe only once you've tasted the finished dish:

Ostrava doesn't need a child locked in its lock.

Ostrava needs to be *open*. It has been closed so long that everyone forgot the difference between being preserved and being kept. The door was never asking for a sacrifice. It was asking for someone brave enough to change the terms.

Issel is six going on seven. They have crossed a country that doesn't exist, outrun singers made of forgetting, survived a winter alone at a stranger's door caked in salt. They have carried a terrible weight since before they had words for it.

They put it down.

"No," Issel says.

The word comes out small and then bigger and then enormous, filling the street, rolling past the orange trees and the held laundry and the bell that stops mid-ring to listen.

"I won't stay inside. I'm not the key that locks. I'm the key that *opens*." Issel turns to face the whole waiting city, and their voice steadies the way Paloma's hands steady a knife over an onion. "Grandmother-of-my-grandmother. All of you. You don't have to stay held anymore. You can come *out*. The world outside got softer while you slept. There's a valley that hides people and a table with too many chairs and a woman who cooks like the food itself is a language. Come out. Be new. Don't be amber."

Silence. The held kind, and then — breaking — the other kind.

The woman reaches past Issel, through the doorway, and her fingers cross into the cavern air and do not turn to dust, do not stop, and she makes a sound like laughing and crying sharing one throat. "The terms are changing," she whispers. "The door is listening to the child."

And it is. Issel can feel it — the hum under their feet shifting pitch, the amber light flickering like a candle deciding to become a fire. The door was built on an old bargain, and old bargains bend for the ones brave enough to renegotiate. The warmth of it pours over Issel like bathwater. *Accepted,* something vast and patient seems to say. *Reshaped. A key that opens may remain free.*

Somewhere deep in the stone, a new line carves itself into the inscription, letter by letter, glowing faintly gold. Issel cannot read the old script yet. But they know what it says anyway, the way you know rain is coming by the smell.

*A door held open by love stays open longer than a door held open by hands.*

* * *

Paloma lets go of the wall.

She shouldn't — the exiles are still crossing, hundreds of them, thousands maybe, blinking in the cavern light like people born again — but she lets go and crosses the salt to Issel in about four strides and picks them clean up off the ground, and Issel lets her, because some things you allow even when you're nearly seven and dignified.

"You changed it," she says into Issel's hair. "You absolute, impossible, wonderful creature. You rewrote the oldest rite in existence."

"It didn't want a prisoner," Issel says against her shoulder. "It wanted somebody to say yes differently."

Paloma laughs — a wet, cracked laugh — and sets them down, and holds them at arm's length, and looks at them the way nobody has ever looked at Issel: like they are not a key, not a tool, not a last hope, but a *person who chose*.

"So you're staying," she says. "In the valley. With—" She stops. Starts over. "With me."

"With the soup lady," Issel confirms solemnly.

"With the soup lady," Paloma repeats, and her voice gives out entirely, and she pulls Issel back in, and around them the exiles of Ostrava step through into a salt cave in a hidden valley, weeping and singing and touching the walls like they can't believe stone can be cold again.

But the celebration lasts only a moment. Because the light in the doorway gutters — just once, just slightly — and far above, filtering down through the mountain's roots, comes a sound that every exile recognizes and every one of them flinches from.

Singing. Wrong-voiced. Patient.

The Grey Choir has not left. The door stands open wider than it has stood in three hundred years, and the Choir is still at the border of the valley, and now there is nothing between the song and all these newly-waking people except a taster, a child, and whatever a door reshaped by love can do next.

Paloma looks up at the sound. Then down at Issel.

"How fast," she asks quietly, "can this door close?"

Issel takes her hand. It is trembling. So is theirs.

"Together?" Issel says.

"Together," Paloma says.

Above them, the song grows louder — and begins, for the first time in centuries, to sing their names.
