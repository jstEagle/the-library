## 36. Closing the Door

The door was open, and it was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and holding it open was killing me by inches.

I hadn't understood that last part until now. In the stories—the ones Hedda told, the ones stitched into the margins of my grandmother's recipe book—doors were things you walked through. Nobody mentioned that standing in a doorway between two worlds meant being pulled apart like dough, one hand kneaded toward each side.

The light pouring out of Ostrava was amber. Not gold—amber, the color of honey held up to a lamp, of evening that never quite became night. It spilled across the salt floor of the cavern and turned every crystal into a small burning thing, and through the opening I could see the city: towers the color of old ivory, terraced gardens going up the sides of buildings like green stairs, a sky with three moons in it, all of them full. Music came out with the light. Strings and something reedy and human voices, hundreds of them, singing in a tongue I had known before I knew my own name.

And people were coming through.

They came slowly at first, as if they didn't trust the opening—and who could blame them? The Grey Choir had spent generations teaching them that openings were how you died. But then an old man stepped across the threshold and his bare feet touched valley salt and he wept, just stood there weeping, and after him came others. A woman carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, holding the empty cage like something precious. Two children, then more children, blinking at our torches and our rough gray stone and our air that tasted of river-mud instead of spice.

Issel stood beside me at the threshold, small hand gripping two of my fingers, and whispered names to each person who crossed. *That's Maretta. She made candles. That's Oveln—he's a door-warden, that's why he keeps looking at your door like it's badly built.* Issel had been translating for hours without stopping, voice gone hoarse, and still the names came, because someone had to tell these people where they'd arrived, and Issel was the only bridge.

"You need to sit," I murmured.

"After." Issel's grip tightened on my fingers. "You're shaking."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying. You've gotten bad at it. It's almost funny."

* * *

Corvane stood ten paces back with his soldiers, and I want to record—I want this written somewhere, even if only in my own head—that none of them moved to stop the crossing. Corvane had drawn his sword when the door first opened, and then he had looked through it, and whatever he'd seen on the other side had made him sheathe it again very slowly, the way you put down a weapon when you realize you're the danger in the room. Now he watched the exiles pass with an expression I couldn't name. Grief, maybe. Or its twin, which is longing.

His lost homeland had no door left. He'd told me that in the standoff, voice cracking on it, before everything went wrong and then went right in ways none of us had planned. He sent nothing through because there was nothing to send anything *to*. Some doors close so completely that not even memory can find the seam.

The Choir had stopped singing.

That was the strangest part. They stood at the far end of the great cavern—maybe forty of them, tall and wrong, their faces smooth as unfired clay, their robes the color of ash—and they had gone silent. When the solstice minute came and the door opened true, their song had faltered, stumbled, and died. Because their purpose was erasure, and there is no erasing what has been witnessed. Every exile who crossed was a fact the Choir could not sing away. The valley's border had frayed enough to let them in; it had not frayed enough to let them win.

They waited. I didn't know yet what for. I would learn.

* * *

Hours passed. Maybe more than hours—the cave kept its own time, and the amber light didn't change. The line of exiles thinned to a trickle. An old woman with a walking stick carved like a heron. A young man carrying nothing at all, hands empty, looking around the cavern as if memorizing it. A family of six, the youngest asleep against her mother's shoulder, missing everything.

Then, from deep inside the amber light, came a sound I will hear until I die: a bell, low and patient, ringing once.

"The city," Issel said. Their voice had worn down to almost nothing. "It's telling them the door won't stay. Everyone who's coming is coming."

"Is everyone coming?"

Issel was quiet long enough that I looked down at them. Salt had crusted in their hair again—they'd crossed half the world crusted in salt, and here they were, salted twice over, as if the substance simply clung to them the way stories cling to certain children. "Some are staying," Issel said finally. "Ostrava isn't empty. The stasis is ending either way—the city wakes whether or not anyone leaves. Some would rather wake inside it than start again in a strange valley." A pause. "Grandmother Vesh is staying."

The woman who had taught Issel the key-phrase. The one who'd sent a child through a dying door alone rather than let a bloodline end on the wrong side of it. "Did you say goodbye?"

"She wouldn't let me." Issel's chin lifted in that stubborn way that had survived everything the Grey Choir had done to try to grind it flat. "She said goodbye is a word for finished things. She said—" and here Issel's voice did break, once, briefly, before being hauled back under control— "she said I should learn to cook."

I laughed. I don't know why that was the thing that undid me, but it was—a short, wet, surprised laugh that echoed off the salt walls. "Good," I managed. "Because I have a book full of recipes that are actually maps, and somebody has to help me figure out the rest."

* * *

The last exile came through near the end, and it was almost no one: a girl younger than Issel, carrying a pot with a crack down one side. She stopped on the threshold, halfway between worlds, one foot in amber light and one foot in valley dark, and froze.

"It's all right," I said, in Ostravan—the dialect my grandmother had sung into my bones before I could talk, the language I'd spoken fluently for exactly four days now and clumsily my whole life without knowing it. "Nothing here bites except the cold."

She looked up at me. "Are you the door-keeper?"

The words went through me like a fishhook. "No. I'm just—" I started to say *a taster*, which is what I had been for eleven years, which was the whole of my identity as the court had defined it: a body, an immunity, a function. But the girl was waiting, and Issel's hand was tight around my fingers, and somewhere behind me Corvane had gone very still.

"I'm the one holding it open," I said.

"That's a door-keeper," the girl said, with the merciless logic of the very young, and she stepped through onto valley salt, and she was the last.

* * *

When the final exile crossed, the door began to close.

Not fast—it didn't slam, nothing about Ostrava ever slammed—but the amber light began to deepen, thickening toward the color of resin hardening around a caught thing, and the music dimmed as though sinking underwater. And I felt the pull double. My arms shook. Sweat ran cold down my spine. Holding a door between worlds, it turns out, is not a metaphor. It is labor. It costs the body exactly what the body has.

"Paloma." Corvane's voice, from behind. "How do we close it?"

"We don't," I said through my teeth. "*I* do. It has to be sealed from this side, by blood, with—" 

With the final recipe.

I had read it in the caves three days ago, in my grandmother's cramped marginalia, and I had thought then that it was a riddle, because it wasn't a recipe at all. No quantities worth measuring, no dish worth eating. Just a list:

*A pinch of salt from the land that sheltered you.*
*A promise made aloud and meant.*
*One keeper, willing.*

Three ingredients. The simplest thing in the whole book, and the only entry written in ink instead of pencil, as if she'd known it was the one that mattered.

"Issel." I crouched down to their height, and my knees nearly buckled, and I pretended they didn't. "I have to let go of the door to seal it. Both hands. Do you understand? For a moment, nobody holds it open."

Issel looked past me at the narrowing light—at the city sinking into its own preservation, at the grandmother who had chosen to stay—and then back at me. Six years old, seven maybe. Carrying the weight of a nation in a body the size of a bread loaf.

"Then hurry," Issel said.

I let go.

The pain of letting go was worse than the pain of holding. The door lurched toward closing like a mouth snapping shut, and the amber compressed to a slit, and I heard—from the other side, faintly, impossibly—singing. Hundreds of voices. The song I'd been hearing all day resolved suddenly into words I understood, and they weren't a farewell. They were a thank-you. Sung by a city, to a food-taster from a valley that magic couldn't touch.

I snatched the pouch from my belt—the one Bram had filled from his own stores, valley salt, harvested from the underground river's banks, the same salt that had flavored every meal I'd ever tested for poison. I took a pinch. One pinch. It sat in my palm catching the last of the amber light, ordinary as anything, the most ordinary substance in the world, and I thought: *this is what saved us. Not swords. Salt and soup and neighbors leaving bread at doors.*

"A promise made aloud and meant," I said, and my voice cracked, and I let it crack. "I promise the valley of Vell will remember Ostrava. Its name, its language, its recipes, its dead and its living. As long as I keep records, it will be remembered. As long as I draw breath, it will be spoken. This I mean."

"One keeper, willing," Issel whispered, prompting me like a priest.

"Willing," I said.

And I pressed the pinch of valley salt against the threshold stone.

* * *

The seal took the salt the way dry earth takes rain. The white grains sank into the humming wall and spread—not vanishing but blooming, tracing lines through the ancient carvings until the whole inscription glowed faintly, a map of itself, a signature. The amber slit narrowed, softened, and did not disappear. It thinned to a seam, a hairline of warm light in the salt wall, no wider than a knife blade, and then it pulsed once—twice—like something settling into sleep—and held.

A seam. Not a door. A scar that remembered being a wound.

The music was gone. The city was gone. But the light stayed, faint and constant, and when I pressed my palm flat against the stone it was warm. Warm as bread. Warm as a kitchen.

"It's still there," Issel said. Not a question.

"It's still there," I agreed. "Just closed. Doors, once truly opened—" I stopped. That was Hedda's line, the one she'd said to me weeks ago over bitter tea, and apparently it had taken root in me the way everything Hedda said eventually did. "Once truly opened, never wholly closed. Your grandmother was right."

"So was yours." Issel leaned their head against my arm, and I felt the exact moment the exhaustion won—the small body sagging, the breathing going long and even. Standing upright, holding my fingers, they fell asleep in the space of a breath, the way only children and soldiers can.

* * *

The Grey Choir dissolved at dawn. There is no better word for it.

We had all been watching them—soldiers with weapons drawn, exiles huddled together, me with Issel in my arms—because forty faceless singers do not stop being terrifying just because they've stopped singing. But as the first gray light of actual morning filtered down through the cavern's high vents, the nearest of them simply came apart. Not violently. The way fog comes apart when the sun finds it. The edges went soft, then the middle, then all of it, drifting upward in threads of pale vapor that caught the light and thinned and were gone.

One by one, then all together, silent as snowfall. They had come to erase a country, and a country had refused, and refusal was the one thing they had no song for. Purpose spent, they unraveled. I thought, watching them go, that they might have been the saddest things I had ever seen: creatures made entirely of an errand, with nothing left when the errand failed.

Or succeeded. Depending on how you counted. Somewhere beyond the sealed seam, Ostrava slept safe in its amber evening, unfindable at last even by the finders. Maybe that had been the mercy all along, buried under the cruelty. I decided I was too tired to decide.

The last thread of Choir-vapor drifted up toward the vents and vanished, and the cavern was just a cavern—gray stone and white salt and the smell of the underground river and forty exhausted exiles staring at each other in torchlight, alive, arrived, beginning.

Corvane lowered his sword. He'd raised it again at some point during the dissolving, old habit, and now he looked at it with something like embarrassment and sheathed it for good.

"Well, taster," he said.

"Door-keeper," Issel mumbled, mostly asleep, correcting him out of pure reflex.

Corvane's mouth twitched. Whatever he was going to say next, he swallowed it. Instead he looked at the glowing seam in the salt wall for a long time, and then at the exiles, and then at me.

"The Regent will want a report," he said finally. "I suggest we agree on one that involves less treason."

"I'll bring the recipe book," I said. "She likes evidence."

* * *

We came up out of the caves into morning.

Real morning, valley morning, gray-gold and smelling of woodsmoke and frost, and the whole surface of the underground river's outlet was rimmed with salt crystals that glittered like someone had strung the banks with lanterns. The exiles stopped at the cave mouth and stared at the sky—one sky, ordinary, moonless, ours—and an old man knelt down and put his hand in the snow and laughed.

Bram was waiting with his ferry barge and blankets and a kettle, because of course he was. Hedda wasn't—Hedda was in Corvane's cells, trading her freedom for our head start, and the joy of the morning had a hole in it shaped exactly like her. But Corvane had already sent word ahead: the herbalist was to be released, charges suspended, pending "review." His tone had suggested the review would find in her favor. Even enemies, it turns out, can be unmade and remade, if you catch them at the right threshold.

I carried Issel up the ferry ramp. They woke just enough to ask where we were going.

"Home," I said. "Both of them. We'll take turns."

Behind us, deep in the hill, a seam of amber light burned steady and faint, keeping its promise. Ahead of us, the valley lay open under a softening sky—magic seeping back into it gently now, like water finding an old channel, the border permanently, irrevocably kind.

In my pocket, my grandmother's book weighed what it had always weighed. But I could feel, under my ribs, a new page already forming—the first entry in a different ledger, one I'd begin tonight at my kitchen table with a sleeping child and a pot of soup:

*Records of Vanished Lands, kept by Paloma Thackeray, door-keeper, volume one.*
