## 37. Aftermath in Vell

The valley smelled different on the walk back.

Paloma noticed it before she could name it—before the first rooftops of Vell town rose out of the morning mist, before the baker's chimney put its familiar woodsmoke into the air. The valley had always smelled to her like bread and river mud and the cold mineral breath of stone. Now there was salt in it. Faint, almost imagined, a ghost of brine riding underneath everything else, the way a memory rides under a song.

Issel walked beside her with one hand buried in hers and the other clutching a heel of bread from Bram's boat-stores, eating in small, deliberate bites as though food were still a miracle that might be revoked. Which, Paloma supposed, for Issel it had been, not so long ago.

"You're limping," Issel said. In Ostravan. Then, correcting, in Vellish: "Limping. You are limping."

"I'm walking carefully," Paloma said. "There's a difference."

"There isn't," Issel said, with the merciless precision of a seven-year-old, and Paloma laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she hadn't managed inside the caves or outside them, the kind that hurt her ribs where Corvane's soldiers had shoved her against the humming wall. She let herself have it anyway. Some pains were worth carrying loudly.

The road down from the cave-mouth wound through the terraced orchards, bare-branched now, solstice just behind them like a door swinging shut. Below, Vell lay gray and ordinary in the winter light: the market square, the bell tower, the palace rising above it all with its slate roofs and its narrow windows, every window of which, Paloma knew, was designed so that no one standing in a corridor could ever be seen from two directions at once. A court built by people who expected to be poisoned.

She had spent eleven years making herself invisible in that court. She had gotten very good at it. Good enough that for most of her life she had believed invisibility was the same thing as safety.

She wasn't sure she wanted to be invisible anymore. She also wasn't sure what the alternative looked like. She supposed she would find out within the hour.

* * *

They came through the market gate at mid-morning, and the valley looked at them.

That was the only way Paloma could describe it. People stopped. A fishmonger set down a crate and turned. Two children hanging off the well-rail went silent mid-shout. An old woman selling dried apples pressed her hand flat against her breastbone—not a warding gesture, Paloma realized after a heartbeat. A gesture of relief. Of welcome home.

Word had traveled ahead of them, of course. Word always traveled faster than boats, even underground ones. They would know in Vell by now: that the Grey Choir had come to the border and been turned away; that the taster's girl had walked into their song; that the door under the mountain had opened and closed, and the valley was still here.

But knowing a thing and seeing its evidence were different. And Paloma knew what they saw when they looked at her: a woman who had stood in front of something that erased color and memory and countries, and who had come back with her colors on and her memories intact and a child's hand in hers.

A man near the fountain—she didn't know his name, a carter, maybe—stepped forward and touched her sleeve as she passed. Just the sleeve. Just a brush of fingers.

"Thank you," he said, and then, visibly embarrassed, "for—I don't know exactly what you did."

"Neither do I," Paloma said honestly. "I made breakfast."

The carter blinked. Issel snorted. And the moment broke, and people smiled, and the market noise came back up around them like water filling a footprint, and Paloma walked through her own hometown feeling—for the first time since her grandmother died—seen.

She hated how much she liked it. She filed that away to examine later, possibly never.

* * *

The summons came before she'd finished unlacing her boots.

Not a summons, precisely. A page, twelve years old, solemn as an owl, standing on her doorstep with a folded paper sealed in green wax—the Regent's own seal, not Corvane's. The paper said, in a secretary's careful hand:

*Her Majesty the Queen Regent requests the presence of Paloma Thackeray, formerly Royal Taster, at the fourth bell. Alone, if she wishes.*

Alone, if she wishes. Paloma read that phrase four times. Someone had thought about it. Someone had understood that after the last month, being ordered anywhere alone would land on her like a hand on the back of her neck—and that being asked, with the option named aloud, landed differently.

Corvane wouldn't have written *alone, if she wishes*. Corvane was learning, though. He'd sent the note himself, she'd learn later; the secretary's hand over his dictation, but the phrasing his. That was its own strange data point.

"I'll be back before supper," she told Issel, crouching to eye level. "Hedda will sit with you. If anyone else comes to the door, you don't have to answer it. You never have to answer a door again if you don't want to."

Issel considered this with great seriousness. "What if the door is Hedda?"

"Then Hedda can knock until I'm back."

"What if it's the Regent?"

Paloma paused. "Then we'd both have questions."

Issel nodded once, satisfied, and went back to arranging acorns in rows on the table—an occupation that had absorbed the child completely for three days running, and which Paloma had privately decided not to ask about, because some kinds of healing needed to look like nonsense from the outside.

* * *

The throne room had been cleared.

That was the first thing wrong with it. The second thing was the Regent standing at the foot of the dais steps instead of seated above them, wearing plain wool instead of state grey-and-silver, her hands clasped in front of her like a woman waiting for a difficult guest rather than receiving a subject.

Queen Regent Isolde of Vell had ruled the valley for nineteen years through regency that everyone understood would quietly become reign, and in all that time Paloma had seen her perhaps forty times, always across a dining hall, always through the lens of a tasting spoon. The Regent had a reputation for coldness that Paloma had never quite believed, because coldness in a ruler was usually just caution wearing armor, and Paloma knew more than anyone alive about caution wearing armor.

"Paloma Thackeray," the Regent said.

"Your Majesty." Paloma bowed the precise amount required—no more. Old habits. But then, deliberately, she straightened all the way up and did not lower her eyes, and watched the Regent notice, and watched the Regent decide not to mind.

"You walked into the Choir's song," the Regent said. It wasn't a question. Reports had reached her; she wanted to hear it from the source, the way she'd always wanted her poisons confirmed by tongue rather than by rumor. Paloma recognized the instinct. It was her own.

"I did."

"Why?"

It deserved an honest answer, and honesty took a moment to assemble. "Because it was singing my grandmother's lullaby backwards," Paloma said finally. "Unsinging it. And I found I minded that more than I minded dying."

Something moved in the Regent's face—not softening exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman hearing her own private arithmetic spoken aloud by a stranger.

"They say your body refused them," the Regent said. "The singers. That the song went through you and couldn't hold."

"That's roughly what it felt like from the inside. Like trying to grip a wet spoon."

The Regent's mouth twitched. Almost a smile. Filed and suppressed in under a second. "Eleven years of poison," she said. "Every dish, every day, small doses of everything the court feared. We told ourselves we were using you. I wonder if we were tempering you. The way you temper steel."

"You used me," Paloma said, evenly. "Both things can be true. I'd prefer, going forward, if we used the other word."

Silence. Somewhere above them, snow slid off a roof and hit the courtyard stones with a soft slap. The Regent studied her for a long moment, and Paloma studied back, and something passed between them that had no protocol attached to it at all.

"Yes," the Regent said. "All right." She turned and walked, not to the throne, but to a side table where a single document lay weighted under a stone salt cellar—salt, Paloma noted; someone in the palace had a sense of humor, or a sense of history, or both. "I didn't summon you here to thank you. Thank you is too small and we'd both choke on it. I summoned you because the valley has a problem, and you are the only person in it qualified to solve it, and I intend to offer you employment rather than beg, because begging is beneath us both and besides, I've seen how you negotiate."

She lifted the salt cellar off the document and held it out—not the document, the cellar itself, a squat clay pot of coarse grey salt. Paloma took it, bewildered.

"The valley's seal is softened," the Regent said. "Permanently. Your door weakened it, and the Choir's siege frayed it further, and my advisors tell me it cannot be rewoven to what it was. Magic can cross our borders now. Slowly. Thinly. Like water through cloth. In time, perhaps, more." Her voice flattened. "Some of my advisors believe this is a catastrophe. That we were founded as a refuge *because* magic cannot follow, and that every refugee family in this valley came here fleeing exactly what can now, someday, reach us."

"And the rest?"

"The rest believe," the Regent said, "that a wall is only as safe as it is honest, and that a valley full of people pretending magic doesn't exist is a valley that can't defend itself when it arrives. I find myself among the latter." She tapped the document. "I am establishing a new office. Keeper of Records for Vanished Lands. Its charge: every country erased, every language driven underground, every people scattered—Ostrava and whatever others the Choir has eaten before it and may eat after. Their maps, their recipes, their lullabies, their names. Gathered, preserved, taught." She looked up. "You understand why it has to be you. There is exactly one living tongue of Ostrava left in this valley, and she's seven years old, and she'll need someone to teach records *to*."

Paloma's throat was tight. She made herself look at the document without touching it. Keeper of Records. Not taster. Not poison-proof body, not human cutlery-guard, not the girl who swallowed so the court didn't have to. An office. A purpose with her name on it instead of her function.

"The pay?" she said, because her voice needed somewhere mundane to go.

"Embarrassingly good," the Regent said. "You'll have clerks. Access to the archive cellars. And the authority to requisition anything you need from anyone in the valley, including me." A pause. "Within reason."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll establish the office anyway and spend years doing it badly, and we'll both know it." The Regent's chin lifted slightly. "I'd rather not do things badly. I've had enough practice lately."

Paloma thought of the ledger in Hedda's keeping—Thackeray, altered from an Ostravan name, ink faded by a grandmother's careful pen. Thought of the recipe book with its coded directions, its marginalia in a dead script. Thought of thirty-three exiles waking in amber evening light and stepping out into a valley that had never been able to hold them, and holding them anyway now, changed rules and all.

"I accept," she said. "One condition."

The Regent raised an eyebrow, the universal aristocratic signal for *you are already employed, what more could you possibly want*.

"Hedda Ostrow," Paloma said. "Released today. Full pardon, her shop restored, her ledgers returned. She stayed behind to lie to Corvane for us. She's sitting in your cells right now for the crime of being born somewhere else and refusing to pretend otherwise." She held the Regent's eyes. "That's the condition. Everything else is negotiable. That isn't."

For a moment the Regent said nothing, and Paloma braced for the machinery of court to grind—delays, conditions, the slow ransom of a favor extracted from a favor. Instead the Regent simply said:

"She was released yesterday morning. Before you came down the mountain." A beat. "I'm told she's already re-opened the shop and is telling anyone who'll listen that the Regent is a fool. I chose not to hear it."

Paloma blinked. "Then what's my condition for?"

"Leverage," the Regent said, with the ghost of genuine warmth. "Always take leverage when it's offered. You'll need it. Half my council thinks your new office is the first step toward the valley drowning in sorcery, and the other half think you should be canonized, and both halves will be insufferable." She pushed the document across the table. "Sign. And Thackeray—" a hesitation, the first unprompted one Paloma had ever seen from her, "—the child. Issel. The household accounts will show a ward of the crown, if that helps. Food, schooling, whatever's needed. No strings. Consider it..." She searched. "...a debt owed to your grandmother. I knew her, you know. Everyone pretended I didn't. She saved my life once, the year I arrived here, when I was nobody's queen and everybody's suspect. She fed me soup in a kitchen no bigger than yours and asked no questions." Her jaw tightened. "I repaid it badly for nineteen years. Let me repay it now."

Paloma signed.

* * *

Hedda's shop smelled of winter herbs and lamp oil and, faintly, of salt—which no longer surprised anyone in Vell, though it had taken a week. The bell above the door rang its cracked half-note as Paloma ducked inside, and Hedda looked up from behind the counter, where she was decanting something amber and foul-smelling into bottles, and her whole craggy face rearranged itself around a grin.

"Well," she said. "The Keeper of Records. They're saying it in the market like it's a title out of an old story. Come in, come in, you look like something the river rejected."

"I feel like something the river rejected."

"Naturally. Sit." Hedda kicked a stool toward her, poured two cups of something hot and cinnamon-dark without asking, and slid one across. For a while neither of them spoke, which was its own kind of conversation; they'd earned the right to silence together in ways Paloma was still cataloguing.

"They freed you," Paloma said at last, redundantly.

"Yesterday. Corvane himself unlocked the cell." Hedda's eyes narrowed over her cup. "Stood there a while, looking at me through the bars. Then he says, 'You lied well.' And I says, 'You believed easily,' and he almost laughed. First time I've seen it. There's a man in there still, under all that grey." She shrugged. "He lost people to a vanished land too, you know. Whole family, gone when he was young. That's why he hunts erasure so hard—he hates it worse than any of us. Just went at it crooked."

"I know," Paloma said. "I saw his face when the door opened."

"Mm." Hedda topped up her cup. "And the little one?"

"Asleep on my floor with acorns arranged in perfect spirals. Building something. Won't tell me what."

"Good," Hedda said firmly. "Children should build things adults can't audit." Then her voice dropped, and the humor went out of it like a candle pinched. "The seal, girl. It's really going, isn't it. I felt it this morning. My well water tastes of iron now—it never did. There's a hedge in my back garden that's grown three inches since solstice, in dead winter, and it's growing *wrong*, all silver along the edges. The valley's waking up. Or the world's leaking in. Same thing, maybe." She fixed Paloma with a look. "What are you going to do about it?"

Paloma turned her cup slowly on the counter. Through the shop window, past Hedda's bundled herbs and hanging bundles, she could see the mountains—the low shoulder of the ridge where the salt caves slept behind their sealed door, and beyond them, far off, the grey line of the border where a choir had sung and been answered.

"Record it," she said. "All of it. Every change, every bloom of salt, every hedge growing silver. Write it down before anyone decides whether it's catastrophe or miracle, so that whoever comes after gets to decide based on facts instead of fear." She looked up. "That's the job, Hedda. Somebody erased a whole kingdom once and nearly got away with it because nobody wrote things down. I'm going to write things down."

Hedda was quiet for a moment. Then she reached under the counter and brought up a ledger—old, swollen with damp, its spine stitched twice—and laid it between them with a care that contradicted every gruff bone in her body.

"Refugee families," she said. "Every one I ever knew of. Names, origins, what they carried. Yours is in there—third page. Thackeray. Altered from Ostravan." She tapped the cover. "Start your records with mine. First entry. Because inheritance isn't blood, girl. It's what you carry forward. And I'd take it kindly if some of what I carried ended up in the official version."

Paloma put her hand flat on the old ledger and felt, absurdly, like she was taking an oath.

"It will be," she said. "First entry."

* * *

She walked home as the light went long and gold behind the western ridge, the Regent's salt cellar in her pocket and Hedda's ledger under her arm, and stopped at the crest of the lane above her cottage.

Below, lamplight already burned in her kitchen window. Through the glass she could see movement: Issel at the table, and beside Issel—not alone, never alone anymore—the baker's youngest daughter, and old Marta from two doors down, and someone whose silhouette could only be Bram, all of them leaning over the acorn spirals while Issel gestured grandly, explaining something in a mixture of two languages, and the room laughed at whatever it was.

On the windowsill, where no one had put it, sat a small dish of salt.

Just a pinch. Grey, coarse, ordinary valley salt—or maybe not entirely ordinary; maybe nothing in this valley would be entirely ordinary again. Placed there, Paloma understood, the way you'd leave bread out for a traveler. Or a greeting for a friend far away.

Somewhere beyond the border, past the grey line, a kingdom slept in amber evening, whole and remembered. Somewhere in the palace cellars, an office waited with her name on its door. Somewhere in her cottage, a child who had crossed a dying country alone was teaching neighbors to count in a language the world had tried to forget.

Paloma stood a moment longer in the cold, committing all of it to memory—because that was the work now, and she intended to be good at it.

Then she went down the hill toward the light, and the smell of somebody's supper, and the sound of her own name called cheerfully through a window that had never once, in eleven years, been left open for her—

—and did not see, on the eastern ridge where the frost lay thinnest, a single line of salt blooming white across the stone, fresh, deliberate, shaped almost like handwriting.
