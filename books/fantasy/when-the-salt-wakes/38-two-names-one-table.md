## 38. Two Names, One Table

The cottage had never held this many people. Paloma was certain of it, standing in her own kitchen with flour on her knuckles and a wooden spoon in one hand like a scepter, counting heads the way she used to count courses.

Bram the ferryman had brought two chairs from somewhere and was wedging them into corners that had no room for chairs. The baker's wife, Marta, had arrived with a sack of dark rye flour and an expression that said she'd been baking since dawn and would hear no argument about it. Two of Hedda's nieces were fighting over who got to grind the pepper. A soldier — actually a soldier, Corporal Vess of the Queen's own guard, still in uniform with her sword propped against the doorframe — was chopping onions at the little table because someone had to and she'd volunteered before anyone could think better of it.

And Issel stood on the step-stool by the stove, solemn as a small priest, stirring honey into crushed rowan berries and watching everything with those grey eyes that had once been full of salt and silence.

"More," Issel said, in Velli now, clear as a bell. "It wants more."

"It wants *patience*," Paloma said, but she tipped in more honey anyway.

Outside, winter had loosened its grip for the first time in anyone's memory. The solstice was three weeks gone. The Choir was ash on the wind. The door was sealed, and the valley breathed easier without knowing exactly why — though every soul crammed into this cottage knew why, and had come anyway, which was its own kind of answer.

Paloma turned back to the counter and looked at what she'd built: her grandmother Thackeray's book open on the left, weighted down with the good rolling pin; the loose pages she'd copied from the cave inscriptions on the right, translated line by line through long evenings with Issel pointing and correcting. Between them, on the scarred wood, a feast that belonged to no single country.

Ostravan flatbread proofing under a cloth beside Velli black bread doing the same. A stew that had started as Bram's river fish and ended up braided with the preserved lemon and salt-cured olives her grandmother's marginalia called *the taste of home we could not carry*. Roasted roots glazed in honey and rowan — Issel's contribution, insisted upon with the full authority of a six-year-old who remembered how it was done. And in the oven, the dish Paloma had been most afraid of: the solstice pudding, the one recipe in the whole book written out complete, no codes, no ciphers, just her grandmother's hand steady and certain across the page, as if she'd always known someone would need it someday.

"You're doing the thing," Marta said, appearing at her elbow with the rye flour.

"What thing?"

"The face. The tasting face. Like you're about to be poisoned."

"I'm always about to be poisoned," Paloma said. "Habit." But she made herself unclench her jaw, and laughed when Marta snorted.

That was another new thing. Laughing in the middle of work, in the middle of a room full of people, without checking first whether anyone important was watching. She'd spent fifteen years learning to be invisible in the Queen's kitchens and halls, and it had taken a vanished kingdom, a mute child, a spymaster's kindness, an old woman's sacrifice, and the end of the world arriving in song to teach her that being seen was survivable after all.

"Corporal," she called. "Onions are crying you or you're crying them?"

Vess wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, leaving a white streak of onion juice on her cheek. "Both, ma'am. Respectfully."

"Don't call me ma'am. I serve food, I don't command it." She handed over the jar of preserved lemons. "Chop these too. Small. They're from Ostrava."

Vess went still, just for a breath. Everyone did that now, hearing the name. Then she nodded and set to it with a soldier's precision, and Paloma thought — not for the first time — that the girl had asked to be here tonight, off-duty, in a servant's crowded cottage, because she'd stood in the salt caves with her sword drawn and watched a taster walk into the Grey Choir's song, and some things a person needed to sit close to afterward.

* * *

By the time the light through the windows went amber, there wasn't a flat surface left in the cottage that didn't hold food or a child. Hedda's nieces had claimed the window bench. Three of Bram's river cousins sat on the floor by the fire, teaching Issel a clapping game that required absolutely no language, which meant Issel was winning. More neighbors kept arriving — the ferryman's widow, the farrier, a pale young clerk Paloma recognized from the records office where she'd soon begin her new work — until Bram gave up on chairs entirely and declared the floor a nation.

Paloma carried out the solstice pudding last, because her grandmother's marginalia said it should arrive last, and set it in the center of the table where the candlelight caught the glaze of honey and rowan.

For a moment, the room went quiet. Twenty-some people, packed shoulder to shoulder, looking at a pudding.

"It's Ostravan," Issel announced, from the seat of honor on Paloma's right. "But it's Velli too. It's both." A pause, then, with the grave precision of a diplomat: "Like me."

"Like all of us, little one," Bram said, raising his cup. "To both names!"

"To both names!" the room roared, and the noise that followed — spoons, laughter, Marta declaring the black bread superior while secretly taking a third piece of flatbread, Vess telling the river cousins how the soldiers had panicked in the caves and Bram saying he'd have panicked too, everyone talking over everyone — filled the cottage so completely that Paloma stood in the middle of it for a moment with her hands empty and her chest too full, unable to move.

This was the found family she'd built in Vell, dish by careful dish, year by invisible year. She'd thought she knew its size. She'd had no idea. It had edges she'd never seen, rooms she'd never entered, and it had come here tonight carrying flour and chairs and courage, crowding in around a child from a dead country as if that were the most natural thing in the world.

As if belonging could simply be widened, the way you widen a table when company comes.

Issel tugged her sleeve. "Sit. You cooked. Cookers sit."

"That's not a rule."

"In Ostrava it is." Issel patted the bench with tremendous authority. "I'm making it a rule here too."

Paloma sat.

* * *

Later, when the pudding was ruins and the fire had burned down to a friendly orange murmur, the children happened.

They happened the way children happen at gatherings: suddenly, loudly, in a pack. Marta's two youngest, the farrier's boy, a neighbor girl with a gap in her teeth — four of them, maybe five, orbiting Issel with the frank curiosity of the very young, who have not yet learned that foreign countries are supposed to be frightening.

"Say something in your language," demanded the gap-toothed girl, whose name was Pip and whose boldness was already legendary on their lane.

Issel considered this with the seriousness of a scholar accepting a commission. Then climbed onto the bench, faced the assembly, and raised one small hand.

"This is the first word," Issel said. "*Sova.*"

"*So-va*," the children repeated.

"It means—" Issel frowned, working at the translation the way they worked at everything, tongue between teeth. "It means when you give someone food and they eat it, and then they're yours and you're theirs. Forever. Even if you fight. Even if they go far away."

The adults in the room went quiet again, differently this time. They all knew now what *sova* had bought in the dark beneath the valley — that a shared meal had been the oldest rite, the key, the thing that opened a sealed door when armies and songs could not. Some of them glanced at Paloma. She met their eyes and nodded once: yes, that's true, that's what it means, eat carefully.

Pip, seven years old and undeflected by history, said: "Can I *sova* you?"

Issel's whole face opened like morning. "You have to feed me first."

"There's pudding left!" Pip seized a plate.

And that was how the rest of the evening went: four children solemnly feeding each other scraps of solstice pudding and flatbread, declaring *sova* over and over with sticky hands and absolute conviction, until the word spread outward to the adults the way words do — Bram *sova*-ing Marta over the last of the fish stew, Hedda's eldest niece shyly offering a piece of honeyed root to Corporal Vess, who accepted it with military gravity and visibly, wonderfully, did not know what to do with her face.

Paloma watched it happen and understood that this was the real ceremony. Not the one in the caves, lit by humming walls and ancient script. This one. A word passing from a dead language into living mouths, one pudding-soaked handshake at a time. Her grandmother had carried the tongue across a border in lullabies and recipe margins, folded small enough to survive, and tonight it unfolded in a servant's cottage in Vell and came out the other side alive.

Somewhere beyond the valley's edge, Ostrava-as-it-was sat in its amber evening behind a sealed door. But its language was loose in the world now. Its word for hospitality was being shouted by children who'd never seen the sea of grass or the amber towers, and would grow up thinking *sova* had always been theirs.

Paloma picked up her cup and, quietly, without calling attention to herself — old habits, but gentler ones now — toasted the room that couldn't hear her:

"To both names."

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the last guests drifted out into the frost-bright street, calling farewells, arguing about who'd take the leftover bread. Bram stayed to stack chairs he'd never explain the origin of. Vess paused at the door with her sword recovered, hesitated in a way soldiers aren't taught to hesitate, and said, "If you ever need anything, ma'am — anything —"

"Paloma."

"Paloma." A nod, almost a bow. "Anything."

Then it was quiet. Fire settling. Dishes everywhere. Issel asleep on the bench, mid-collapse, one fist still closed around a scrap of flatbread, cheeks shining with honey.

Paloma moved through her kitchen putting the room back together, and found she didn't mind the mess — that she even loved it, the way you love a battlefield when your side has won. Her grandmother's book lay open still. She went to close it and stopped, because a slip of paper had been tucked between the pages that she hadn't put there.

She recognized the handwriting instantly: cramped, dry, faintly ironic. Hedda's.

She'd read it twice through the night Hedda had given it to her, before the caves, before everything — the page from the exile's ledger, the entry that read *Thackeray, altered from Sova-Thackerane, family of the western shore*. She'd tucked it away because grief needed somewhere to go and the book was where her grandmother lived.

But there was writing on the back now. Fresh ink. Hedda had added to it — when? She'd been released from Corvane's cells only days ago, thin and stiff-jointed, waving off apologies like flies.

Paloma held the page to the candle.

In Hedda's hand, beneath the ledger line, a single note:

*Your grandmother wrote to me once, near the end. Said the recipes weren't finished. Said she'd left one blank page on purpose, for whoever came after — "because a country isn't what you remember, girl, it's what you cook next."*

*I've been thinking about that. About what gets carried forward versus what gets carried back.*

*Come see me tomorrow. There's something in my garden that shouldn't be growing, and I'd rather show you than tell you.*

— H.

Paloma stood a long time in the cooling kitchen, the page in her hand, listening to the fire tick and Issel breathe.

A blank page left on purpose. A plant in a garden that shouldn't be growing. The salt, perhaps. Or something newer. Something that hadn't existed in either country yet — something that belonged, like the feast, like the word *sova* passing mouth to mouth among children, to both.

She looked down at Issel, sleeping in the honey-light, and smiled despite herself.

"Tomorrow, little one," she murmured. "We're wanted in a garden."

Outside, far off toward the valley's edge, the frost caught the moon and gleamed white — and if you looked closely, longer than was reasonable, it seemed to gleam in patterns. Almost like rows. Almost like planting.

Almost like something waking.
