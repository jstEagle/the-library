## 39. The Lullaby Continued

The cottage had learned to hold two people.

Paloma noticed it most at night, when the fire burned low and the sounds of the house changed—the creak of the second chair being drawn in, the small wet sounds of Issel breathing through sleep, the way the floorboards had begun to memorize a second set of footsteps. A year ago this house had been a place she passed through on her way somewhere else: court, Hedda's, the market. Now it was a place that stopped her at the door and asked her to stay.

She was staying tonight. She'd banked the fire, washed the bowls from supper—Issel's bowl still smelling faintly of the river-fish Bram had brought up as a gift, because Bram brought fish now the way other men brought gossip—and stood for a while in the middle of her own kitchen with nothing left to do. That was the strangest part of peace, she thought. It left you alone with your hands.

In the corner, Issel slept in the bed they'd shared since the winter of the door. Not the big bed—Paloma had given that up without ceremony—but the wide one by the window, where the last of the salt-bloom light sometimes came through the glass even now, months later, a faint pearlescence on the sill that no amount of scrubbing would lift. The valley's magic-seal had softened permanently; Hedda said the wells tasted sweeter, the bread rose higher, the old women at the font claimed their joints hurt less. Paloma only knew that some nights, the light came through like this, patient and pale, and lay across Issel's sleeping face like a hand.

She should sleep too. She had a long day tomorrow—the Regent's new archive wanted cataloguing, three more vanished lands' worth of records arriving by courier, and Corvane had taken to showing up with documents himself, awkward and earnest, still learning how to be a man whose grief had somewhere to go. But instead of sleeping, Paloma found herself standing at the shelf above the hearth, looking at her grandmother's recipe book.

It sat where it always sat, spine cracked, cover furred with age, the pages inside soft as cloth. She had added to it over the past year—her own hand alongside her grandmother's, translations in the margins, notes in Vellan script beside Ostravan ones. *Salt before honey. Honey before grief.* She took it down now without deciding to, the way you take down a thing your body has already chosen.

The book fell open, as books do, to the page she had worn softest: the lullaby.

Her grandmother had written it out not as words but as a recipe, because everything in that woman's world could be written as a recipe if you were clever enough. *For settling a child at night:* so many measures of low sound, a slow fold of one note into another, a pinch of rising at the end so the sleeper drifts up before coming gently down. Paloma had spent years thinking it was a song about food. It wasn't. It was a song shaped like food, the way a key is shaped like a lock.

She hummed it now, quietly, barely breath. The old syllables rose in her throat without asking permission—the language that had come back to her syllable by syllable over a year of teaching and being taught, until now she dreamed in it some nights and woke not sure which tongue her thoughts had been wearing.

*Alen su, alen vey,* — rest now, rest deep — *sol mara, sol ken.*

Sleep, little boat. The water holds you. Nothing follows that the dawn won't meet first.

Her grandmother's voice lived in those syllables. Not the voice itself—Paloma had been young when the old woman died, and memory is a thief—but something older than voice. The shape of a country saying goodnight to its children across however many years and miles and erasures. Every exile who had ever sung this song had sung it to a child who might be the last one who needed it. That was what lullabies were, maybe. Promises made to futures you couldn't see.

And here was the thing Paloma had been circling for weeks now, the way her tongue circled a new flavor before naming it:

There was no verse for what came after.

The lullaby promised rest, and safety, and morning. It did not have a verse for a child who woke screaming about grey singers. It did not have a verse for doors, or for choosing, or for a family made out of strangers who kept arriving at each other's doors in the snow. Her grandmother's song ended where her grandmother's world had ended—at the edge of what she could promise.

Paloma looked at Issel, asleep, one fist curled under their chin, hair spread on the pillow like spilled ink.

They had spoken again that evening—not much, never much, but more than the month before. Over supper Issel had said, in careful Vellan, "Hedda says the Choir is gone gone. Not hiding-gone." And then, after a silence, in Ostravan: *Will you sing tonight?*

Not *sing me the song.* Just *will you sing.* As if the singing were a thing between them now, a hearth they both kept lit.

"I'll sing," Paloma had said. And then she had sat with her hands around an empty cup and understood, all at once, what she had been trying to write for weeks in the margins of the recipe book: her own verse. Not a translation. An addition. The next line of a song that had been waiting a generation for someone to carry it forward.

An inheritance, Hedda had told her once, isn't what you're given. It's what you carry forward. Blood was only half of it. The other half was what you chose to keep alive.

She sat down at the table with the book and a stub of pencil, and she began.

* * *

Writing a verse was harder than tasting poison.

That wasn't sentiment; it was craft, and Paloma knew craft. A poison hid itself inside a familiar dish—a wrongness folded into rightness, undetectable unless you knew what every rightness was supposed to feel like. A verse had to do the same work in reverse: carry something new inside something old, so seamlessly that a child hearing it would never feel the seam. It had to fit her grandmother's meter exactly. It had to rhyme in Ostravan, which rhymed reluctantly, like a cat being held. And it had to say something true.

What was true?

That the water still holds you—that part stayed, that part was eternal, that was the load-bearing wall of the whole song. But after that? Her grandmother's song promised morning. Paloma's verse would have to promise something morning couldn't cover. Because Issel had seen mornings fail. Issel had crossed a border with salt in their hair and something grey behind them, and no amount of *the dawn will meet it first* could un-know that.

So the verse had to be honest. That was the rule she set herself: it could promise anything except a lie.

*Ken ilu, ken amai,* she wrote, and struck it out. Too close to the original—it borrowed its comfort instead of earning it. *Sol vethra,* she tried. *The door stays open.* But the door had closed, sealed with a pinch of valley salt and a promise kept, and though the epilogue of that story was still being written—faint blooms at the valley's edge, greetings, not threats—the verse shouldn't lean on a door. Doors closed. That was their nature. What didn't close was—

She stopped.

What didn't close was a table.

She thought of the feast they'd thrown at midwinter's end, the cottage overflowing, soldiers and bakers and ferryman crammed shoulder to shoulder, Issel standing on a stool teaching valley children their first Ostravan word—*su*, rest—and the children shouting it back like a spell. She thought of the neighbors' food that had appeared at her door during the worst weeks, left silently, wrapped in cloth, an entire community conspiring in kindness. She thought of her grandmother feeding people in a foreign valley with recipes from a drowned country, writing directions to a magic door in the margins of a soup.

Of course. Of course. The oldest rite. Hospitality. The thing the door itself had wanted—not sacrifice, not keys, but a meal shared. Her whole inheritance, from the first page of the book to the last, was one long lesson in the same truth: *you survive by feeding people, and you are saved by letting yourself be fed.*

The pencil moved.

*Su na tel, tel na mara,*
*marana ilu, ilu ken.*
*Vessa toro, vessa ama,*
*ama na su — su na hen.*

Rest is the table, the table is the sea,
the sea holds all, and all may rest.
Wherever you wander, whoever you love,
love makes the resting — rest makes home.

It wasn't perfect. The third line's rhyme leaned on a vowel her grandmother would have frowned at, and *hen*—home—was doing double duty as both noun and verb in a way the old grammar resisted. But when she whispered it against the meter of the original, folding it in after the final *sol ken*—

it held.

It held the way a bridge holds: invisibly, entirely, once you trust it with your weight.

Paloma sat back. Her eyes were burning. She let them.

Because here was what the verse actually said, underneath the pretty grammar: *there is nowhere you can be carried where you stop belonging to the people who fed you.* Her grandmother had sung the old verses across a border, into exile, into a valley that couldn't hold magic—and the song had survived, dormant, waiting in a recipe book for a child to grow up poisoned-proof and lonely and ready. And now Paloma added four lines, and the song went on, and someday—she let herself think it, let herself want it—someday Issel would sing it to someone. A child of their own, or a neighbor's child, or some future stranger asleep at their door caked in salt. The song didn't care which. Songs never did. They just needed mouths.

* * *

She sang it that same night, of course. She wasn't going to wait for courage; courage was for courts and caves. This was just a kitchen.

She knelt by the bed first, the way she did, and brushed the hair off Issel's forehead—hair that had gone glossy and dark again over the year, the salt-scorch grown out, the child underneath it rounder-cheeked and heavier-limbed, growing the way children grow when they are finally safe enough to grow. Issel stirred but didn't wake. Children who had crossed borders in the night learned to sleep through anything, and also, somehow, to surface instantly at the sound of a certain voice. Hers, now. She was a certain voice.

She began with the old verses. Grandmother's words, grandmother's tune, the low fold of one note into another, the pinch of rising at the end.

*Alen su, alen vey, sol mara, sol ken.*

Then—breath held, heart steady, the taster's trick of keeping her face smooth even when everything underneath was moving—she came to the seam, and crossed it.

*Su na tel, tel na mara...*

Her own voice, carrying her own words, in a dead language that wasn't dead anymore because it was being sung to a living child in a warm room in the valley of Vell, where magic couldn't follow—except that it had, except that it had arrived the way all real things arrive, at a door, hungry, asking to be let in.

She watched Issel's face as she sang. Nothing moved in it. But the fist under the chin uncurled, slowly, finger by finger, and opened flat against the blanket. Open like a hand releasing something. Open like a table being laid.

When the song ended, the room held the silence the way a bowl holds water: completely, without spilling.

And then Issel's eyes opened—not startled, not crying, just open, the way you open your eyes when you've heard something you want to see the source of.

"You changed it," Issel whispered, in Vellan, and then, testing, in Ostravan: *"You put a table in the water."*

"The water was getting crowded," Paloma whispered back. "Boats need somewhere to dock."

Issel considered this with the gravity of a seven-year-old theologian. Then: "Grandmother-mine"—that was what Issel called her, *grandmother-mine*, a word that had started as mispronunciation and become a title, and which Paloma had privately decided she would answer to for the rest of her life—"will you teach me the new part? So I can keep it?"

"Keep it where?"

Issel touched their own chest, over the sternum. Where the salt had crusted thickest, that first night at the door. Where, Issel had once explained very seriously, people keep their songs so the Grey Choir can't take them, because the Choir can unmake countries but it cannot reach inside a person who has been sung to.

"Tomorrow," Paloma said, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because there was no Regent here, no Corvane, no one to perform steadiness for. "Tomorrow I'll teach you every word."

"And the day after?"

"The day after I'll teach you to cook it."

Issel smiled—sleepy, crooked, wholly theirs—and reached up with both arms, and Paloma gathered the child against her shoulder, and rocked, and hummed the whole song through once more, old verses and new, both languages braided now the way two rivers braid when they've run side by side long enough to forget which was which.

By the time she finished, Issel was asleep again, breathing slow, one hand still holding a fistful of her sleeve.

She eased free, tucked the blankets, and stood a moment in the salt-pale light from the window.

On the table, the recipe book lay open to the lullaby, her new verse sitting in fresh pencil below her grandmother's faded ink—two hands, one page, a hundred years apart and no distance at all. Tomorrow she'd copy it fair, in ink, in both scripts, the way you make a thing official by writing it down. Tomorrow she'd start teaching it word by word, the way her grandmother had taught her without either of them knowing that's what was happening. Tomorrow the archive, the couriers, Corvane's awkward visits, the whole rebuilt machinery of a life.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only this: a sleeping child, a finished verse, a fire burning low and gold.

Somewhere far away—at the valley's edge, in the dark beyond the hills—she thought she saw it again, just for a heartbeat: a faint bloom of white light, rising like breath on cold glass. Not threat. Greeting. *Somewhere, Ostrava remembers us.*

"Goodnight," Paloma murmured—to Issel, to her grandmother, to whatever was listening out there in the salt-dark. "We're keeping everything safe. Sleep well. We'll leave a lamp lit."

And in the morning, when Issel woke and padded barefoot to the table and climbed onto the chair and asked—as they asked every morning, and would ask every morning for years, and Paloma would answer every time, because some questions deserve the same faithful reply—

"What's for breakfast, grandmother-mine?"—

—Paloma Almeida, royal food-taster, keeper of records for vanished lands, daughter of Vell and daughter of Ostrava, opened her grandmother's book, and smiled, and said:

"Everything, little boat. Everything we have."

* * *

Outside, the salt-light bloomed once more at the edge of the hills—patient, pale, unmistakable—and did not fade.

It waited, the way a lit window waits.

The way a door, once truly opened, never wholly closes.
