## 40. When the Salt Wakes

The frost came first, the way it always did in the years that mattered.

Paloma felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her teeth, faint and mineral, like biting down on a spoon left too long in the snow. She had learned to trust that feeling. Her tongue had been a map of danger for most of her life, and it did not lie to her now. Something was waking at the valley's edge.

She rose from bed without waking Issel, pulled on her wool coat over her nightdress, and stood at the cottage window with her breath fogging the glass. The moon hung low and orange over Vell, and out past the terraced fields, past Hedda's herb garden gone silver with winter, past the black line of the river where Bram's ferry sat chained against the ice—the salt was blooming.

It looked, from this distance, like moonlight pooling where no moonlight should pool. A faint white shimmer along the valley border, rising from the ground in slow curls, delicate as breath on glass. Not spreading. Not creeping inward as it had during the Choir's siege, all those years ago. Just standing there. Waiting. The way an old friend waits at a gate they know is never locked.

Paloma dressed properly—trousers, boots, the heavy coat with the mended pocket where she kept her grandmother's recipe book—and went to the small bed by the hearth.

Issel was nineteen now, though they still slept curled like a child, knees drawn up, one fist beneath their chin. They had grown tall and quiet and watchful, with their mother's long hands and their own stubborn silence, which they broke only when something deserved words. Paloma hated to spend that silence on cold mornings.

But some things deserved it.

"Issel," she said, and touched their shoulder the way you touch a struck bell—one soft press, and let it ring. "Wake up. It's happening again."

Issel's eyes opened all the way at once, the way they always had, even at six years old. No drifting up through sleep for them; they surfaced like a diver breaking water. They looked at Paloma's face, read it, and were already reaching for their boots.

"The edge?"

"The edge."

They did not speak on the walk out. There was no need. After everything, the two of them had worn a path between speech and silence the way other families wore paths between houses, and both were equally warm. The night was sharp enough to bite the inside of the nose. Their boots crunched on frozen mud, then on frost, then—gradually, gently—on salt.

That was the thing about these blooms. They were never harsh. The first time, years ago, three winters after the door closed, Paloma had approached them with her heart hammering, certain that the Grey Choir had returned, that the unmaking had come back to finish its song. She had tasted a pinch of it on her gloved fingertip, because she was who she was, and tasting was how she understood the world.

It had tasted clean. Clean the way sea air is clean, the way tears are clean when they're the good kind. And underneath the salt, so faint she almost missed it, a thread of sweetness—honey and burnt sugar, the exact flavor of the last meal she had cooked in the dark of the caves, the meal that opened a country.

A greeting. That was all it ever was. Somewhere beyond the world, beyond the maps, Ostrava remembered them, and every few years it said so in the only language it had left.

* * *

They reached the border as the sky began to gray at its seams. The salt bloomed waist-high here, great pale flowers of crystal opening from the earth like something out of a dream Paloma couldn't quite hold onto after waking. Beyond them, the valley ended—not with a wall, not anymore, but with a softness in the air, a place where distance went slightly wrong, where if you squinted you could almost see the suggestion of amber evening light and rooftops that belonged to no city in any atlas.

The seal had softened permanently, the Regent's scholars said. Magic leaked into Vell now the way light leaks under a door: thin, constant, impossible to stop. Hedda, freed from Corvane's cells within the season of her arrest, had put it differently. "The valley stopped holding its breath," she'd said. "Things grow in a valley that breathes."

Paloma crouched and laid her palm flat against the largest bloom. Cold, but a living cold, the cold of a stream in January. Beneath her hand, the crystal pulsed once—slow, patient, unmistakable.

"There," Issel said softly.

Paloma felt it too. Not sound, exactly. More like the memory of sound. A syllable moving through the salt the way a word moves through a held breath. If she let her mind go quiet, she could almost resolve it into meaning. Almost.

"Say it," she said. "You're better at hearing than I am."

Issel knelt beside her, pressed their own hand to the crystal, and listened with their whole body the way they always did—eyes half-closed, lips parted, the wind moving their hair. When they spoke, it was in the old tongue, the dead language that had never actually died, only waited. Then they translated, because they were generous that way, and because Paloma had asked them once, years ago, never to leave her standing outside a sentence again.

"It says—" Issel paused, and smiled, and the smile made them look six years old again, standing salt-caked and fearless on her doorstep. "It says: *the table is still set.*"

Paloma laughed. She hadn't expected to; it came out of her rough and startled, a laugh with frost on it, and she wiped her eyes with the heel of her glove and laughed again.

"The table is still set," she repeated. Of course that was what it said. Of course. She had opened a kingdom's door with a shared meal, with hospitality, with the oldest rite her grandmother's people knew—that the stranger at your threshold is a guest before they are anything else, and the guest is fed before they are questioned. And Ostrava, suspended in its amber evening all this while, had answered in kind. Every bloom of salt at the valley's edge was a place laid at a table. An invitation renewed, year after year, without demand, without expiry.

"They're very patient," Paloma said.

"You taught them that," Issel said. "Or maybe they taught you. I've never worked out which."

* * *

They stayed until the sun came up, because you didn't leave a greeting unanswered. That was another rule, the oldest one, the one written into every recipe in her grandmother's book in the margins nobody else could read. Paloma took off her glove and pressed her bare hand to the salt one more time, and spoke into it—first in Vellish, plain and workmanlike, and then in Ostravan, her accent still carrying the lilt of lullabies learned at a grandmother's knee before she understood they were anything more than songs.

*We're well,* she said. *The valley breathes. The child chose us and we chose back. You set the table; we'll keep ours set too.*

The crystal warmed under her palm—barely, briefly, the way a hand warms when another hand covers it—and then the bloom began to subside, folding itself gently back into the earth, grain by grain, until the field lay ordinary under the morning light. But the ground where it had stood stayed white, and Paloma knew that come spring, nothing would grow there except perhaps something new. Something neither kingdom had named yet.

They walked home slowly. The sun climbed. Smoke rose from chimneys across the valley—the baker first, always, then the farmhouses, then, much later and grudgingly, Hedda's chimney, because Hedda at ninety-one considered dawn a rumor she refused to confirm.

At the top of the rise above the cottage, Issel stopped.

"Do you ever think about going?" they asked. "Through, I mean. Now that the seal is soft. Now that we could probably find the way."

Paloma thought about it honestly, because Issel deserved honesty and had earned it at a price she never intended to forget. She thought about amber evenings and streets preserved like insects in honey, a whole country paused mid-breath, waiting for its people. She thought about the faces that had peered out at them from the open door on the solstice all those years ago—frightened faces, hopeful faces, faces that had wept to see her cook in the dark.

"Sometimes," she said. "But here's what I know about doors, Issel. I spent my whole life thinking a door was something you walked through and were gone. In or out. One life or the other." She looked out over the valley—over the fields that fed it, the river that carried it, the court where she still served, though these days as keeper of records for vanished lands, a title invented specifically because the Regent had run out of ways to say *thank you* and *I'm sorry* in the same breath. "But a door that's truly opened isn't a wall with a hole in it anymore. It's a hinge. Both sides swing. Both sides meet."

She touched the recipe book in her pocket, the margins full of a dead language that had turned out to be merely sleeping.

"We don't have to choose," she said. "That was the lie we were all living under, wasn't it? Vell or Ostrava. Belonging or survival. But look—" she gestured at the white patch on the far hillside, already becoming just another part of the landscape, another oddity of the valley that children would dare each other to taste. "Ostrava sets its table at our border. We set ours at theirs. Two names, one table. That's not choosing. That's making the circle bigger."

Issel considered this with the seriousness they brought to everything, then nodded once, decisively, the way they decided things.

"Then I'm making breakfast," they said. "And you're adding the verse."

* * *

That night, long after the bread was eaten and the neighbors' borrowed stew pot returned, Paloma sat by Issel's bed the way she had when they were small and the nightmares about faceless singers came nightly. Those nights were long past—Issel slept easily now, deeply, the sleep of someone who knew exactly where they were—but the ritual remained, and rituals outlive their reasons. That was rather the point of them.

She sang the lullaby.

Her grandmother's melody first, the old syllables falling into their ancient shapes: *sleep, sleep, the salt keeps watch, the salt remembers what the sea forgot.* Then her own verse, added seven years ago on the night after the door closed, sung now hundreds of times until it sounded as though it had always been there:

*Sleep, sleep, the door stands wide,
not shut, not sealed, just set aside
the way you set a plate for one
who's coming home when day is done.
The salt will wake. The salt will say:
we kept your seat. We saved your place.
So sleep, sleep, till morning comes,
and find us both on both sides of the sun.*

Issel's breathing had gone long and even before she finished. Paloma tucked the blanket to their chin, the way her grandmother had done for her, the way her grandmother's grandmother had done in a kingdom erased from every map and memory—which was, Paloma had come to understand, not the same as being erased at all.

She crossed to the window to bank the fire's glow from her eyes and happened to look out, one last time, toward the valley's edge.

The salt was blooming again.

Faint—just a shimmer, just a breath of white against the dark hillside—but blooming, hours after the morning's greeting had folded away. As if somewhere beyond the world, someone had gotten up from the table to say one more thing before the candles went out. As if the door, wide and unhurried, had leaned a little closer to listen to the verse.

Paloma stood very still, her hand pressed to the cold glass, and smiled at it.

"Yes," she told it quietly, in both languages. "We heard you. We're here. Good night."

The shimmer held a moment longer—warm, patient, sure—and then settled into the hillside like a lamp being carried to another room of an enormous house.

Doors, once truly opened, never wholly close. Paloma Almeida went to her bed knowing it the way she knew the taste of salt: completely, and without fear, and ready for whatever came knocking next.
