## 1. The Apprentice's Ledger

The ledgers smelled of tallow and old rain, the way everything in Maren's cottage did after the third day of copying them.

Ada sat at the long table beneath the one good window, her back aching in the shape of the chair that had never fit her, and copied columns of figures into the fair ledger with a nib she'd cut herself that morning. Goose-fat. Nine coppers to the miller's boy for eel-bait. Two measures of black salt from the peddler who came through twice a year and never seemed to come *from* anywhere. It was dull work, which was precisely why Maren gave it to her — dull work kept hands busy and questions small.

Ada had stopped being able to make her questions small sometime around midwinter.

She dipped the pen. Wrote *tallow, two stone* in her careful looping hand. Reached for the next page of Maren's ledger.

And stopped.

The page was already written.

Not in Maren's cramped, crabwise script, with its abbreviations and its habit of letting the tails of letters run together like roots. The hand on the page was hers. She knew it the way you know your own face in a window at dusk — the slight forward slant of it, the way she crossed her *t*s too high because she'd learned her letters from a woman whose own crosses sat low, and had spent years overcorrecting. The loop of the *g*. The little tick she made instead of a proper full stop when she was writing quickly, and she had clearly been writing quickly, the ink drying unevenly where her wrist had dragged.

*Tallow, two stone,* said the page, in Ada Halloran's handwriting. Then: *Goose-fat. Nine coppers to the miller's boy for eel-bait.*

Then: *Bought mule from Harrow's field, bay with white socks, skittish. Overpaid.*

Ada set down the pen very carefully, as though it might go off.

She read the entry again. And again, because the first two readings had not made it more sensible. The entries matched her fair ledger exactly — matched the work she had done this very afternoon, line for line, item for item. Except that this page continued past where her copying stopped. Past today.

*Third night out,* read the next line. *Thornfell pass. Cold enough that the ink wants warming in my palm before it will run.*

*Third night out.* Out of what? Out of where? She had not spent a night away from the village since she was eleven and had the fever. Thornfell pass was a day's hard walk north, into hills shepherds avoided and mapmakers didn't bother with.

And at the top of the page, in the margin where Maren always wrote dates, was a date. Ada worked it out twice, counting on her fingers under the table like a child doing sums, because she did not want to believe it the first time.

Tomorrow.

The fire popped in the hearth. Outside, the village of Marrowgate went about its evening — someone's dog, someone's axe, the bell at Sainted Brigid's ringing the hour flat and tuneless as ever. Ordinary sounds. Sounds from a world where pages did not write themselves in the hands of girls who hadn't written them.

Ada looked toward the inner door, the one that led to Maren's working room, closed now because Maren was at the Widow Fenn's again, drawing something out of the widow's bad hip for the fourth time this season. Maren would be an hour yet. Maren was always an hour longer than she said.

Ada should have shut the ledger and gone back to her copying. That was the sensible thing. That was what four years of apprenticeship had trained into her: see nothing, note less, ask only what could be answered.

She turned the page.

More entries. Her hand throughout, growing more hurried, more slanted, as though the writer were cold or frightened or both. Fragments of places Ada had never been, described with the intimacy of memory: *the lamps in the streets are all glassed but none lit, and the light comes up from under the cobbles like the town is lit from below.* A phrase repeated three times down one page, underlined harder each time: *it knows my name, it has always known my name.* And once, in a smaller, neater script than the rest — smaller because it had been written slowly, deliberately, by someone making themselves write it:

*I am going to stop being afraid of this. I don't know when. But I am going to.*

Ada pushed back from the table so fast the chair skidded. Her hip struck the dresser; Maren's good blue jar rattled but held. She stood in the middle of the kitchen with her heart going like a trapped bird and her breath coming shallow, and she became aware, distantly, absurdly, that she was going to be sick if she didn't sit down, and that she absolutely was not going to sit down.

Because here was the thing — here was the thing she would admit to no one, later, when there were people to admit things to: some part of her had known. Not the page, not the words, but the *shape* of it. All winter she had woken before dawn with the feeling of having been running. All spring she had caught herself looking north through the gap in the hills as though something up there were calling roll and expected her present. She had told herself it was restlessness, the ordinary discontent of twenty years old and apprenticed forever to a woman who answered every question with another question or with silence.

But you did not dream of running unless part of you already knew you would.

"No," Ada said aloud, to the kitchen, to the page, to whoever had written in her hand. "No, I haven't agreed to anything."

The page did not answer, being a page. Somehow that was worse.

She heard Maren's footsteps on the path outside — that particular deliberate tread, staff-first, pause, step — and Ada did the second thing she would never explain to anyone satisfactorily. Instead of shutting the ledger, she tore the page out. Folded it small, small, small, and thrust it into the lining of her bodice, where it lay against her breastbone like a coal.

Maren came in smelling of pine resin and the widow's house, hung her staff on its peg without looking at it, and paused.

"You've stopped copying," Maren said.

"I finished the page." True, technically. Ada's voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Master — can I ask you something?"

Maren was sixty if she was a day, gray to the roots, with hands that looked soft and could crack a walnut, or a man's wrist, Ada suspected. Her eyes were the color of pond ice and about as easy to see into. Now they moved from Ada's face to the ledger, to the torn stub where a page had been, and something crossed them — quick, gone before it landed. Something that was not surprise.

That was how Ada knew, later, that Maren had known. You cannot be surprised by a thing and hide it that fast. Surprise is slow. Recognition is instant.

"Can you ask me something," Maren repeated. "You can ask. Whether I answer is a separate economy."

"The ledgers. Why do we keep them? Truly. Not the answer about accounts."

Maren took off her cloak, folded it, took her time about it. "Accounts want keeping everywhere," she said. "Debts especially. Some debts are in coin."

"And some aren't."

"Some aren't." Maren sat, poured herself watered wine from the jug, and looked at Ada over the rim with that terrible mild attention, as if Ada were a patient and the illness were interesting. "You've been at the north window all week. You watch the gap in the hills like it owes you money."

"It doesn't owe me money. Nothing owes me money. I owe—" Ada stopped. Four years of *I owe you my keep, my training, my place*, all the small currency of apprenticeship, and it came to her tongue now tasting suddenly of iron. "When does my apprenticeship end, Master? Truly. Not the answer about seven years."

Maren set down the cup. For a moment — just a moment — she looked old. Old in a way Ada had never let herself see. "When you're ready," she said, "and I will decide when that is."

And there it was. There was the wall Ada had beaten her fists against all winter, dressed up once more in kindness. *I will decide.* Not you. Never you.

"Right," Ada said. "Yes, Master."

She gathered the ledgers, squared their edges, put them on the shelf exactly where they belonged. She washed the ink pot. She ate supper with her master across the same table where the impossible page had lain an hour before, and passed the bread, and asked after the widow's hip, and was, by any measure, the picture of a dutiful apprentice.

All the while the folded page burned against her skin, or felt as though it did, and all the while a voice in her that had been whispering for months finally spoke in plain words: *You have seen the last ordinary evening you are ever going to get. What will you do with it?*

* * *

She waited until the moon was down and Maren's breathing had gone long and even behind her door. Then she got up in the dark and committed her first crime.

It did not feel like theft. That troubled her even as she did it. The grimoire lived in Maren's working room, in the iron-bound chest under the window, under three false layers of receipts and dried herbs, as though hiding it among boredom were the best disguise — and perhaps it was, because Ada had lived under this roof four years and had never once been tempted. The book itself was unimpressive: squat, swollen with damp-swollen pages, bound in leather gone dark and featureless with handling. No sigils on the cover. No title. It weighed wrong in the hand, heavier than paper had a right to be, as though it were mostly full of something denser than ink.

She had been forbidden it explicitly, exactly once, four years ago, on her first night. *Everything else in this house is yours to learn,* Maren had said, *except this. Ask me why and I'll tell you nothing. Don't ask me why and I may tell you everything.* Ada had never asked. She understood now, holding it, that this had been the whole of her apprenticeship in miniature: obedience rewarded with withheld answers, patience rewarded with patience.

She took it anyway.

She also took the things a person takes when leaving in earnest and pretending to herself she might come back: her cloak, the good boots, flint and steel, a week's bread and cheese wrapped in oilcloth, her needle and thread, the little knife Maren called Ada's inheritance and never explained. The silver hair-pin that had been her mother's, the one thing in the world older than Maren's tenancy of her life. She left a note, because some part of her still believed in fairness: *Gone north. Don't follow. I'm sorry. — A.* She looked at it, added nothing, took nothing back, and put it under the ink pot where Maren would find it with her morning tea.

Then she went out the kitchen door into the last hour of dark, and did not look back at the cottage, because she knew that if she looked she would stand there until dawn, and dawn would mean Maren waking, and Maren waking meant questions, and questions were the one currency she could no longer afford.

The smugglers' road left Marrowgate from behind the tithe barn, which told you most of what Marrowgate thought about itself. Officially it was a drover's track. Unofficially it existed so that goods could enter and leave the village without troubling anyone's ledgers, and it wound north through hazel scrub and old pasture, hugging the contour of the land the way roads do when they have something to hide. In the gray before sunrise it was the color of ash and completely empty, and Ada walked it fast, almost running, feeling with every step the strange doubled sensation of a girl fleeing a crime and a page being fulfilled.

*Bought mule from Harrow's field, bay with white socks, skittish. Overpaid.*

Harrow's field lay at the fork where the smugglers' road met the shepherd's path, three miles out. Ada had no intention of stopping. She had no intention of buying anything. Horses cost more than she had saved in four years, and mules cost nearly as much, and anyway the prophecy — and she hated the word already, hated it with her whole chest — the *page* had not said she bought a mule. Only that she would.

Harrow was in his field at first light, because Harrow was always in his field at first light, arguing with a bay mule with white socks. The animal had spooked at a fox in the night, Harrow said, and broken through the near fence, and Harrow's wife had declared that if it broke anything else it would go for slaughter meat within the week, and Harrow, who was a soft-hearted man pretending vigorously otherwise, mentioned the price in a tone of elaborate indifference.

The mule was skittish. Ada overpaid. She realized she was doing it while she was doing it — counted out coins she needed, coins that represented winters of copying — and her fingers kept going anyway, as if the transaction were happening slightly ahead of her consent, the way a sneeze happens, the way falling happens.

"There," Harrow said, pocketing it. "You're a fool, girl, but a generous one. Where's a lass like you walking to, anyhow, leading a beast at this hour?"

"North," Ada said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "To the pass."

Harrow spat, unhurried, and looked at the gap in the hills, and then — this was the part Ada would remember — he looked at her, really looked, and whatever he saw made him take half a step back. "Well," he said. "Mind how you go. Folk say there's nothing over Thornfell. Nothing worth the name."

"What do you mean, nothing worth the—"

But Harrow had turned back to his fence, and the mule was pulling at the lead, ears flat, wanting the north road as badly as Ada did, and there was no time, and she went.

* * *

By noon she was above the tree line, and by late afternoon she was in the passes proper, where Thornfell's shoulders closed around the track and the wind came down funneled and cold, carrying a smell she couldn't place — stone and snow and underneath it something faintly sweet, like a snuffed candle, like a room closed for a very long time.

She made camp in the lee of an old boundary marker, a leaning stone carved with a sigil so worn it might have been a farmer's brand or a king's mark or a child's game. She fed the mule — whom she had named Cabbage, in defiance of prophecy, because nobody writes *bought mule named Cabbage* — and warmed her hands, and ate half the bread, and told herself, in the steady interior voice she used for setting bones and dosing fevers, that she was going to open the grimoire now. Tonight. By firelight. Because not opening it had become worse than any word inside it.

The iron-bound weight of it sat in her pack all through the making of the fire, all through the settling of the dark. When she finally drew it out, the flame bent toward it. Just slightly. Just once. She chose to believe she had imagined it.

The lock was a puzzle of the kind Maren loved: not locked, exactly, but *convinced* to stay shut, a hasp that yielded only when Ada stopped thinking of it as a lock and thought of it, instead, as a hand relaxing. Four years of watching her master's fingers had taught her that much at least. The cover opened with a sound like a long-held sigh.

The early pages were what she expected of a hedge-witch's grimoire — receipts, remedies, drawings of leaves with annotations in three different hands, generations of them, Maren's crabbed script the newest layer over older strata of ink gone brown. Charms for the milk, for the fever, for the finding of lost rings. A recipe for black salt. Nothing to frighten anyone.

Then, a third of the way in, the book changed.

The handwriting became older, finer, more certain of itself. The margins filled with dates — and the dates ran backward, or seemed to, or ran according to some calendar Ada didn't know, century-old ink recording days as fresh as tomorrow. And the subject changed from milk and fevers to borders, and thresholds, and a word that appeared again and again, sometimes alone, sometimes underlined, sometimes written so hard the pen had torn the page:

*Vessany.*

Ada did not know the word. She was sure she did not know the word, and then, in the next heartbeat, she was sure that she did — that she had known it always, the way you know the shape of your own mouth — and the two certainties sat in her chest grinding against each other like millstones.

She turned the pages faster. Maps, or the ghosts of maps, countries drawn and then scratched out so thoroughly the parchment wore thin. Lists of names in columns, some marked through, some circled. And then — near the end, on a page whose margins carried a date that stopped her heart, because it was not a century old, it was not even a year old, it was *tomorrow* —

She found the passage about herself.

*The apprentice will copy the ledgers,* it said, in that fine old hand, *and find the page in her own writing, and be afraid. She will steal the book on the new moon, which she believes is her own idea. She will take the smugglers' road, which is the only road that remembers the way. She will buy the mule from Harrow's field, bay with white socks, and overpay.*

Ada read it twice. The fire popped. Somewhere off among the rocks, the wind found a hole and moaned through it, low, like a held note.

*Overpaid,* she thought, absurdly. *Even that. Even the exact coins.*

Then she did the reasonable thing, the sane thing, the thing any sensible person would do with a book that claimed to have written her before she had lived her: she pulled the page from the binding — it came free too easily, as if it had been waiting — crumpled it, and fed it to the fire.

The flames took it. Held it. Turned it gently, like a hand examining a moth.

And gave it back.

The burning page rose out of the fire, unburnt, edges glowing gold and cooling to white, and drifted once around the campsite — past the mule, who watched it without alarm, which somehow frightened Ada more than anything else that day — and settled in her lap, cool as a banknote, whole as the hour she'd found it. The words were still legible. Below them, where blank vellum had been, new ink was surfacing even as she watched, rising through the page like a bruise, in her own slanting hand, dated the day after tomorrow:

*She will try to burn the page. The fire refuses.*

Ada sat very still for a long time. Above her, between the black shoulders of Thornfell, the stars were out in their cold thousands, and the gap in the hills — the notch she had watched all winter from Maren's kitchen window — showed pale against them, a doorway's worth of darkness, waiting.

Cabbage nudged her shoulder with a nose that smelled of dry grass and patience. The page lay warm in her lap, filling quietly with her own future, line by line, and the wind through the pass sounded almost like a voice, almost like her name.

Ada looked north.

"Well," she said, to the mule, to the fire, to whoever was taking the minutes. "I hope you're writing down that I'm angry about this."
