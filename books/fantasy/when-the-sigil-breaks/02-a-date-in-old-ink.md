## 2. A Date in Old Ink

The mule had a name once — a brass tag on its halter read *Bess*, worn nearly smooth — but it did not answer to Bess, or to anything else Ada tried. It answered only to the grain sack and to its own opinions, and both of those were firmly held.

She had bought it at dusk from a drover with wine on his breath and no questions in his mouth, which was the whole reason she'd paid too much for it. A girl alone on the smugglers' road wanted a beast that invited no conversation. Bess stamped now at the edge of the firelight, ears flat, staring into the dark of the Thornfell passes as if the dark owed her money.

"Go on, then," Ada muttered. "Stand there being offended. See if I care."

Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. Three days out from Hollowmere and her defiance was already wearing thin at the elbows, like an old coat she'd stolen instead of packed. She fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb. Above her the pass narrowed to a slot of sky stitched with early stars, and the wind coming down off the high rocks smelled of iron and old snow and something else — something faintly sweet, like paper left too long in the sun.

The grimoire sat in her saddlebag the way a sleeping animal sits: heavily, and with attention.

Ada had not opened it since the second night, when she'd read the first page by candle-stub and felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth. She had told herself then that she'd imagined the shiver. She was good at telling herself things. It was, Maren always said, her chief talent and chief fault — *you can argue yourself into anything, girl, that's why I keep the ledger locks*.

Well. She'd argued herself out of three locked doors and a window latch, hadn't she?

She hauled the saddlebag over and set it across her knees. The leather was colder than the night deserved. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth she'd oiled herself two years ago for exactly this purpose though she could not have said why, the grimoire waited — thick as a brick, bound in hide gone the color of tea leaves, its spine cracked in seven places like an old man's knuckles. There was no title on the cover. There never needed to be. Books like this knew what they were.

"Right," she said to it. "Let's see what you think you know."

The clasp was silver, tarnished green at the hinge, and it gave with a sound like a held breath released. The first pages were herbals — feverfew for the swelling of humors, wormwood laid under the tongue against seeing what should not be seen — copied in four different hands across what must have been a hundred years, each scribe correcting the last with little marginal huffs of ink. Then a treatise on sigils that used words Ada half-knew from Maren's forbidden shelf: *binding, witness, covenant*. Then, near the middle, the handwriting changed.

This hand was confident and narrow, slanting left, as if the writer leaned away from their own words even while setting them down. The ink had gone brown with age. The page header read, in letters more ceremonial than the rest:

*Of Her Who Comes After the Erasing.*

Ada's thumb had found the edge of the page before her mind caught up with her eyes, and for a moment she simply breathed, the way Maren had taught her to breathe before handling anything that might be listening.

"Listening," she repeated aloud, and laughed, because the laugh came out wrong. "It's a book. Books don't listen."

The fire popped. Bess snorted from the darkness like a disapproving aunt.

She turned the page properly.

The prophecy — if it was a prophecy, and not just some mad monk's riddle, and she told herself both of those things in strict rotation — was written in verses separated by small drawn doors. Not illustrations, quite. Little doorways, each one shut, each rendered with a care that unsettled her more than any monster would have. Whoever drew them had known doors. Had stood before them.

*A child of neither tower nor hearth shall take what is not hers to keep,* she read, *and carry it toward the mountain's missing tooth.*

Hedge-witch's apprentice. Stolen grimoire. The Thornfell passes. She could feel each word finding its match in her life the way a key finds wards in a lock — small clicks, one after another, precise and horrible.

*She shall ride a beast that fears the road ahead.*

Bess chose that moment to shy violently at nothing, jerking her picket line taut, and Ada very nearly threw the book into the trees.

Instead she made herself keep reading. This was the trouble with her, she thought wildly; she kept reading. She'd kept reading Maren's private correspondence when she found it, kept reading the ledgers she was meant only to copy, kept reading until she'd found tomorrow's date written in her own hand and understood that either she was going mad or her life had been transcribed in advance by someone with terrible penmanship and excellent information. Reading was how she'd gotten here. Reading was the only tool she had.

The verses went on. The road through Thornfell, named pass by pass — *the Cut of Shepherd's Sorrow, the stair called Nine Knees* — and she recognized every name, because they were the names the smugglers used, names that appeared on no map she'd ever seen, names you learned only by walking the road itself. Whoever wrote this had walked it. Or would walk it. The grammar of the thing refused to sit still in time; verbs slid between past and future like fish through reeds.

And then, halfway down a page, boxed in by two of those patient little doors:

*On the third night she will make her camp below the split crag, where the wind speaks in a woman's voice, and she will read these words by her own small fire, and she will not believe them.*

Ada looked up.

Above her, black against deeper black, the crag overhanging her campsite was split clean down its face, a fissure wide enough to put her fist in. The wind came through it with a low, rising moan — *ooooh, ooooh* — a woman's voice, pitched exactly the way her mother's had been when calling her home at dusk thirty miles and thirteen years behind her.

The fire cracked. Sparks went up. She sat absolutely still and let the moment close over her head like cold water, and down there in the dark under the water a small, clear voice said: *third night. Tonight is the third night.*

"It's coincidence," she said. Her voice sounded fine. Reasonable. A person in complete command of herself. "Passes have split crags. Winds have voices. Any hedge-witch knows—"

Any hedge-witch knows that coincidences are the world's way of keeping its real work quiet. Maren said that too. Maren said a great many things, and Ada was beginning to suspect that every one of them had been a wall built around a door.

She flipped forward, fast now, hungry and frightened in equal measure, scanning for dates. And there — in the margin beside a verse about a border that was not a border — a notation. Not in the old left-slanting hand. In a hand she knew better than her own reflection: the tight, downward-hooked *a*, the crossbar on the *t* that never quite crossed.

Maren's hand.

The notation was a date. Tomorrow's date. The same date Ada had found copied onto the ledger page in Hollowmere, in handwriting she had never written and could not deny was hers.

Below the date, in Maren's cramped script, three words: *She takes the road.*

* * *

She did not remember deciding to burn it.

That was the honest truth, and she held onto the honesty of it like a handrail: whatever happened next, she had not decided it, it had simply arrived in her body the way sneezes arrive, and so she could tell herself afterward that she had acted freely. That mattered to her enormously, though she couldn't have said why. Perhaps because everything else in the last hour suggested she had never freely done anything at all.

She took the grimoire by its spine, found the page with tonight's campfire verse — *she will read these words by her own small fire, and she will not believe them* — and held it flat over the flames.

"Let's see," she said, "how true you are."

The fire did a thing fire does not do.

It bent. Not blown aside — the air was still — but bending *away*, the way grass bends from a boot, the tongues of flame curving outward from the page in a wide respectful arc, so that the heat fell on her wrists and her sleeves and the backs of her hands and nowhere, nowhere at all, on the paper.

She pushed the page closer. The flames leaned further. The wood beneath them glowed and spat and did its honest work, and the fire itself seemed to step back like a servant declining an order, and the page lay in the light untouched, its little drawn doors as crisp as the hour they were inked.

"No," Ada said. It came out as a whisper first, then again louder, absurdly loud in the pass, startling Bess into a fresh fit of stamping. "No. You're paper. You're *old paper.* Everything burns. Everything—"

She fed the fire. She got it roaring, a proper blaze, sparks streaming up through the split crag like a column of escaping stars, heat pushing at her face hard enough to dry her eyes. She held the page over it until sweat ran down her forearms. The flames parted around the paper the way a crowd parts around someone important.

Then, slowly — with the unhurried insolence of a thing doing her a favor — one tongue of fire reached up and licked the very bottom corner of the page.

A single corner curled. Browned. Caught, briefly, a coin-sized ember glow —

— and went out. Snuffed, cleanly, from inside the burn itself, as if the fire had changed its mind mid-lick. The corner settled back flat, cool to the touch before she could even flinch away, marked only by a faint scorch-shadow in the shape of nothing at all.

The wind through the split crag moaned on. *Ooooh. Ooooh.*

Ada lowered the page into her lap and sat with her arms wrapped around the grimoire like a child holding a cat she both loved and feared would bite, and she shook. Not sobbing — shaking, a fine continuous tremor that started in her teeth and worked outward, and she let it happen because fighting it would have required believing something, and belief had become the enemy of everything she owned.

Because this — this was worse than being chased. If Maren had sent riders after her, if the magistrates of three counties had put a price on her head, all of that would have been *now*, would have been hers, would have been a fight she could run from or bargain with or lose. But this was not pursuit. This was *precedence*. Someone had stood at this exact fire before she existed and written down her shivering. Somewhere in this book her fear was already described, already annotated, already *old ink*, and she was only the latest copyist laboring through a text whose meaning had been fixed a century before her grandmother was born.

She thought of the ledger page in her own handwriting, dated tomorrow, and felt suddenly certain — with the terrible certainty that arrives at two in the morning far from home — that she had not written it yet but *would*, that somewhere ahead of her sat a desk and a pot of ink and her own obedient hand, and that everything she experienced as choice between now and then was just scenery on the road to a sentence already dried.

"That's not living," she said to the fire, quietly, reasonably. The fire burned on, chastened, ordinary again, as if ashamed. "That's copying. That's all I've ever done. Copy his ledgers, copy her recipes, copy — copy a life somebody else drafted a hundred years ago." Her laugh cracked in the middle. "I ran away to be free and I'm following a *route map*."

Because that was the worst of it, she realized. She had fled north along the smugglers' road precisely because the prophecy — no. No, she had fled north because the passes were unwatched. Because Hollowmere's constable knew her face. Because the Thornfell route was the fastest way out of territory where anyone cared who she was.

Wasn't it?

She cast back through her three days of travel and found, to her horror, that memory itself had become unreliable testimony. Why had she taken the eastern fork at Shepherd's Ford, the harder climb? Because it avoided the tollhouse. Why had she camped *here*, below this crag, rather than pressing to the summit path? Because Bess was lame-spooked and the light was failing.

Or because the book said so. Because some part of her — the part that had oiled oilcloth two years early, the part whose handwriting showed up in places it had never been — had been reading ahead all along.

The wind sighed through the split stone, and Ada heard, or imagined she heard, in its long vowel, something almost like patience. Like a reader waiting for her to reach the next page.

"I don't believe you," she told the book. Firmly. Twice. The little drawn doors stared back with their patient shutness, and she understood, dimly and with gathering dread, that disbelief was not going to be simple — that you could not un-believe a thing that had already proven itself, that refusing a prophecy was not like refusing an offer but like refusing gravity, possible only so long as you never stepped off the ledge.

She was going to have to step off the ledge. She could feel that much already, the way animals feel weather. Somewhere ahead of her in this book there would be a verse so plain, so exact, that to defy it she would have to do something enormous, and enormous things done in defiance turn out, more often than not, to be exactly the thing written down.

Unless she got off the page entirely.

Ada looked east, where the pass floor dropped away into darkness, and then west, where no route went at all — where the mountains folded together in a jumble the maps left blank or decorated with hatched lines and the single word *UNRELIABLE*. Smugglers didn't go there. Nobody went there. Which meant no prophecy could bother to be written about it, because whoever wrote prophecies wrote about roads, and roads were just other people's decisions hardened into geography.

"Fine," she said. She stood, joints aching, and began pulling up the picket line. Bess rolled her eyes and endured it. "We're not going where the book says. We're going where the book *can't* say. There has to be a way through the west folds — there's a way through everything if you're stupid enough, and I've been told I'm very—"

The word died in her mouth, because she had picked up the grimoire to stow it and the book had fallen open — fallen, or turned, or simply been waiting — to a page she had not read, and lying in the firelight was a verse beginning:

*When she turns from the written road toward the blank country west of maps—*

She slammed it shut.

For a long moment she stood in the ringing silence of the pass, the grimoire clutched to her chest, the mule breathing hard, the fire burning its ordinary gold, and above her through the split crag the wind went on saying that single patient syllable, over and over, like someone kindly, terribly, waiting for her to finish.

She did not open the book again that night. She did not sleep, either. She sat with her back to the warm stone and watched the east pale toward a dawn she had not been given a date for, and rehearsed, in a whisper, the only sentence left that belonged wholly to her:

*I am going where I am not written.*

By the time the light came, she was already moving — not east along the smugglers' road, but west, up the trackless fold, leading a stubborn mule toward the one place in the world with no road, no name, and no words waiting at the end of it.

Behind her, in the cold ashes of the fire, the grimoire lay closed and content, and if books can be said to smile, it smiled.
