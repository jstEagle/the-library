## 3. The Road That Wasn't There

The pass was not on any map. Ada had checked three of them — Maren's own survey charts, the cartographer's folio she'd bought with her last honest coin in Millbrook, and the smudged road-map pinned inside the grimoire's back cover, which showed every smuggler's cut and goat-track for forty miles and still showed nothing where she was going.

That was how she knew it was right.

The prophecy had told her so, in ink old enough to crumble: *On the third night she will turn east where the maps end, above the tree-line, where the stone remembers a different name.* She'd read it by firelight with her heart hammering like a trapped bird, because it was one thing to find tomorrow's date in a century-old book and quite another to find *yourself* in it, described with the weary precision of someone who has watched you do a thing before you have done it.

So she went east, above the tree-line, where the stone remembered a different name.

* * *

The mule — Biscuit, because naming him something she might have to abandon later felt like cruelty — picked his way up the scree with the offended patience of his kind. The air thinned as they climbed. Below them Thornfell's passes unspooled in blue evening shadow, and somewhere down there, surely, Maren had discovered the empty ledger shelf and the gap on the grimoire's chain. Ada tried to imagine her master's face and could not; Maren's face had always been a thing that changed depending on what it needed to be.

*She will send no one after you,* the grimoire had said. *She knows this road.*

Ada had decided not to think about that either. There were many things she had decided not to think about, and they were stacking up behind her eyes like stormclouds looking for a place to break.

The trail, if it was a trail, appeared only when she stopped looking for it. Twice she lost it entirely among fields of broken slate, and twice it reassembled itself ahead of Biscuit's nose as though embarrassed to have been caught missing. The stones were warm under her palms when she steadied herself against them — warmer than an autumn evening had any right to make them, warm the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies.

"Where does the heat come from?" she asked the mountain, because talking to animals was a hedge-witch habit and talking to mountains was apparently what happened when you ran out of sensible company.

Biscuit flicked one ear. He did not answer, but he also did not balk, and a mule's opinion of a road is worth more than most men's.

Near dusk they came over the shoulder of the ridge and Ada saw the valley, and she forgot, for one long breathless moment, to be afraid.

It lay below her like a cup of held light — no, not light. She squinted. Not light exactly. It was more that the valley refused the darkness properly, the way a room refuses it when lamps are lit, except there were no lamps. Streets ran out from a walled town in pale ribbons. Fields lay in neat strips beyond the walls, harvested or waiting. A river stitched silver through it all. And over everything hung a faint glow without source, like the memory of daylight that had forgotten to leave.

A town. A whole living town, in the middle of the Thornfell wilderness, where three maps agreed there was nothing.

Ada got out the grimoire with hands that wanted to shake and made them not.

*At the ridge's crown at sundown she will see the city that is not there,* said the page dated today. *She will be afraid. She will go down anyway.*

"I hate this book," Ada told it conversationally, and went down anyway.

* * *

The wrongness arrived gradually, the way cold water arrives around your ankles before you notice you've walked into the sea.

The first thing was the silence. Not emptiness — the valley was full of sound, of wind in barley and water over stones — but the absence of a particular sound, one so constant Ada had never once thought of it as a sound. No bells. No dog barking at their descent. No distant hammer, no cart-wheel, no voice calling a child home. Towns made noise the way rivers made wet. This one didn't.

The second thing was the gates. They stood open — not broken, not abandoned, simply open, the way a door stands when someone has just stepped through it and expects to step back. The wood was sound. The iron hinges bore no rust. And set into the arch above, worn nearly smooth by weather that somehow hadn't touched the rest, was a sigil: a circle crossed by seven rays, like a wheel seen through water.

Ada knew that shape. Her stomach dropped slowly, the way a stone drops in deep water.

It was burned into the grimoire's cover.

She led Biscuit through the gate because the alternative was standing outside it until fear won, and she had spent nineteen years learning that fear always wins if you give it the time. Inside, the streets were cobbled and clean and utterly empty. Lamps hung from wrought-iron posts every thirty paces — dozens of them, hundreds, a city built for lamplight — and every single wick was dark.

Not burnt out. Unlit. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole horror of it. Burnt-out lamps are finished. These looked *waiting*, glass panes polished, oil reservoirs full, wicks trimmed and fresh, as though a thousand hands had prepared them yesterday for a lighting that never came.

"Hello?" Ada called, and her voice went down the street and came back thinner, and nothing else answered.

She dismounted and walked, leading Biscuit, past shuttered shopfronts whose painted signs were legible and strange. THE GILDED SPOON. VESSANY & SONS, SURVEYORS. A dressmaker's window displayed gowns in fashion a century dead, dustless, colors bright as the day they were dyed. Ada pressed her fingers to the glass. Warm again. Everything here was faintly warm, like a body.

Vessany. The name surfaced in her memory from some fireside lesson years ago — geography, Maren had said, is mostly a list of things that used to exist. *Vessany was annexed after the Succession War,* Maren had said, in the tone reserved for facts too large for grief. *Its lands are part of Corvane now. Its people scattered. There is nothing left to know.*

There was quite a lot left to know. It was all standing right in front of her, holding its breath.

* * *

She found the people in the square at the city's heart, and the finding was worse than the emptiness had been.

They were going about an evening. That was the only word for it. In the amber half-light that came from nowhere, a woman drew water at a fountain and carried it away. Two men argued amiably over the loading of a cart. Children played a game with chalked squares, laughing — actually laughing, the sound bright and ordinary and terrible — and none of them looked at Ada. None of them looked at Biscuit, though he clopped loudly across the cobbles and drank from the fountain trough with slurping disregard for dignity.

They moved through their motions like dancers who had rehearsed so long the dance had eaten the dancing. The woman with the water passed Ada within arm's reach, close enough that Ada caught the scent of bread and lavender and underneath it something faintly mineral, like struck flint. The woman's eyes went through her. Not past her — *through* her, the way light goes through a window.

"Please," Ada said. "I'm sorry. I'm lost. Can you—"

The woman paused at the mouth of an alley. For a heartbeat — one heartbeat — her gaze snagged, caught on Ada like wool on a nail, and her face did something awful and small, a flicker of confusion, almost of pain, the look of someone waking briefly inside a dream and feeling the dream's edges. Then it smoothed away and she turned down the alley and was gone, and Ada stood with her hand pressed over her own mouth.

*They can't see you,* whispered a voice that was her own voice and yet not, the voice she was starting to recognize as the grimoire's residue, the prophecy soaked so deep into her thoughts it had its own tone now. *Or rather: you cannot be seen. You are not written here. Yet.*

"I didn't ask," Ada said aloud, furiously, to nobody, and two children playing near the fountain glanced up sharply as if they'd heard something, and Ada's blood went cold, and then their heads tilted back down to their chalked squares.

But they had heard. For half a second, they had heard.

She made herself walk the square. She counted lamp-posts — forty-one, each dark. She noted the well, the granary, the wide stair leading up to a hall whose roofline wore the same seven-rayed sigil in copper gone green. She was a hedge-witch's apprentice; observation was the trade; you catalogued even when you were drowning. Catalogue the river while you drown, Maren used to say. The river will kill you either way, but you'll die knowing its name.

*Vessany,* she wrote later, in her own little travel journal, by the light of her own small fire in a doorway that felt safer than the open square. *City of unlit lamps. Population: I don't know. They move but they don't live. Or they live but they don't move. The warmth comes from everywhere. The light comes from nowhere. The lamps are ready. Someone has kept them ready for a hundred years.*

Then, because her hand seemed to move without consulting her:

*Who trims the wicks?*

* * *

She found out at moonrise, or what would have been moonrise anywhere else; here the sky held a moon-like disc that shed light without quite committing to it.

He came down the street with a ladder on his shoulder and a brass oil-can in his other hand, an old man in a coat the color of wet slate, and he stopped beneath the first lamp-post and set the ladder up with the economy of ten thousand repetitions. He climbed. He opened the lamp's little door. He checked the wick with a fingertip, closed the door, descended, folded the ladder, shouldered it, and walked to the next post.

He did not light it.

He tended it. He groomed it. He performed every office of a lamplighter except the one that mattered, and he did it in perfect silence, and as Ada watched, she understood with a slow rolling dread that this was not one man's madness. This was the whole city. This was what a country became when the thing that lit it went out but the country refused to stop being a country. Every harvest gathered, every floor swept, every wick trimmed — a hundred years of maintenance performed in the dark, by people who faded from the world rather than stop keeping faith with it.

Ada sat down hard on a doorstep and hugged her knees and let herself, finally, feel the size of it.

Because she had run from Maren angry about ledgers and secrets and a page in her own handwriting. Small angers. Human-sized angers. And the road had delivered her to this — to grief with a skyline, to sorrow old enough to have cobbled streets named after it. She was a thief in a cathedral of patience, and the book in her satchel had known, had *written*, that she would sit exactly here on this exact doorstep, and she hated it, and she was beginning to suspect that hating it was also part of the script.

"No," she said quietly, to the grimoire, to the prophecy, to whatever patient authorial thing had arranged her life like furniture. "No. You got me here. Fine. But you don't get to tell me what happens next."

Somewhere across the square, a latch clicked.

Ada froze. Biscuit lifted his head from his nosebag.

Down a narrow lane between the granary and the surveyor's office, a door stood open that had been shut, and in it stood a figure — young, slight, holding a hooded lantern whose flame was the first true flame Ada had seen since crossing the ridge. Real fire, guttering gold, throwing real shadows.

And the figure was looking directly at her. Not through her. At her.

"You're not one of ours," the figure said — a young man's voice, sharp with suspicion and something underneath it, something like awe, like hunger. "You cast a shadow."

Ada looked down. In the lantern-light, her shadow lay long and black across the cobbles, unmistakable, undeniable — the only shadow in the whole city of Vessany.

The young man took a step forward, and the lantern shook very slightly in his grip. "Nobody's cast a shadow here in a hundred years." His voice cracked on the number. "What in the names of the Seven Rays *are* you?"

Ada, who had asked herself the same question every night since she'd found tomorrow's date in a dead woman's book, had no answer ready.

Behind him, far off across the rooftops, the first of the unlit lamps flickered — once, softly, as if something enormous had drawn breath.
