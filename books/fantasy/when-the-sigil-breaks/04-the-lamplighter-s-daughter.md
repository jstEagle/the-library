## 4. The Lamplighter's Daughter

Ivo had been mending the same lamp for three days, and the lamp knew it.

It sat on his workbench like a sulking animal — a brass thing from the Third Ring, older than his grandfather's grandfather, its wick curled and black as a burnt eyelash. No oil would stay in it. He'd poured in the good whale-oil twice, the stuff they rationed down from the Court stores, and both times the lamp drank it and gave back nothing but a smell like cold churches. The flame wouldn't take. In Vessany, a lamp that refused to burn wasn't broken. It was grieving.

"You're talking to it again," said Pellin from the doorway, not looking up from the ledger he was copying. "People will say you're mad."

"People already say I'm mad."

"They say worse about me, and I'm only your father." Pellin turned a page with a dry whisper of sound. "The lamp is dead, Ivo. Let it be dead."

"It's not dead. It's waiting." Ivo pressed his thumb against the glass, leaving a smudge in the dust that had settled there since before he was born. Everything in Vessany waited. That was the whole country, really — one long held breath. The lamps along Corvane Street hadn't been lit in ninety years, but every one of them still stood at its corner, straight-backed, ready, as if light were a rumor they'd heard once and refused to disbelieve.

Outside, the evening came down over the city the way it always did: slowly, and without any color to speak of. There was no true sunset inside the border. The sky went grey, then greyer, then a grey so deep it felt like standing inside a closed fist, and then it was night, and the stars came out wrong — too few, too sharp, arranged in patterns no sailor outside had ever charted. His mother used to say the stars over Vessany were the old ones, the true ones, the ones the world had forgotten how to see. His mother had said a lot of things before the outer ring took her street.

Ivo set down the lamp and pulled on his coat.

"Pellin," he said — because everyone called his father by his name, even his sons; *Father* was a word the cell saved for the Cause — "I'm doing the rounds."

"The rounds," Pellin repeated, in the tone of a man filing something away. "The eastern terraces again?"

"There was a light out there last week. Near the old customs house."

"A reflection off the dragon's scales, most likely. Or your eyes." But Pellin's pen had stopped moving, which meant he was listening harder than he let on. "Take the short way. And if you see anything —"

"I know. Don't touch it. Mark the spot. Come home."

"Come home," Pellin agreed, and went back to his copying, and Ivo went out into the grey.

* * *

He took the long way, of course.

You couldn't grow up in Vessany and not hunger for the Outside. It was the first story anyone told you, told in whispers, told in pieces: *out there* the sun was gold instead of pewter, *out there* rivers ran fast and loud and didn't end in streets that had stopped existing, *out there* strangers' faces did things — laughed, wept, aged — while here everything moved through time like light through honey. The Outside was where his grandmother had been born, though she'd been carried across the border in her mother's arms before the closing, and remembered nothing, and still spoke of it the way other people spoke of lost lovers. The Outside was in every prayer the cell whispered, though officially the Cause was about the sigil and the fire and the Bearer-to-come, not about doors. Officially.

Unofficially, Ivo had spent his whole life collecting Outside things. A brass button, military, real — traded from a smuggler who claimed (probably lying) to have crossed. Half a newspaper, water-ruined, dated forty years after the closing, which raised theological questions nobody in the cell wanted to answer. A song, hummed to him once by a dying lamplighter, that supposedly came from a country called Osterhagen, all about a girl and a river and a heartbreak so ordinary it made Ivo ache for a week. Ordinary heartbreak. Imagine having enough of a life to break your heart over a girl instead of over history.

So when the light appeared again — third night running, out past the customs house where the streets thinned into memory — Ivo didn't go home. He went toward it.

The eastern terraces were Vessany's oldest lie to itself. Streets still ran there, cobbled, curbed, lined with house-fronts whose windows had gone blind as boiled eggs. You could walk them, if you walked carefully. But the houses had no insides anymore. Peer through a doorway and you saw not rooms but a kind of pale fog, thick as milk, and sometimes — this was the part Ivo hated — sometimes shapes in the fog, moving with the slow patience of fish under ice. People who'd been caught mid-life when their street forgot itself. Nobody said their names aloud anymore. Naming them was supposed to tug at the thread.

The light was ahead, near the customs house: small, unsteady, warm. Warm. That was what stopped him in his tracks, halfway down Terrace Row. Nothing in Vessany burned warm anymore except the deep-down heat of the dragon, and that came up through the stones in faint pulses, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. This light flickered. This light *danced*. This light was the color of a thing alive, and Ivo stood in the dark with his heart going like a hammer and understood that he was looking at the first honest fire he had ever seen.

He should have marked the spot and gone home. Pellin's voice said so, sensible and cold, somewhere behind his ribs. But Ivo had inherited exactly none of his father's coldness and all of his stubbornness, and besides — the light was moving. Moving away from him, up the terrace steps, quick and fumbling, and whoever carried it was breathing loud enough to hear, and crying. Crying, quietly, the way you cry when you've been crying for hours and have given up hiding it.

A smuggler, then. Smugglers came sometimes — or rather, sometimes the border thinned enough that things slipped through, and mostly those things were goods, and rarely they were people, and never, in Ivo's nineteen years, had one of them cried.

He followed it up the steps, keeping to the shadowed side of the street, and caught her at the top, where Terrace Row ended at the old customs house and the fog began.

She was younger than him. That was the first absurdity. He'd built the trespasser out of smuggler-stories: hard-faced, knife-carrying, grown. Instead there was a girl of maybe seventeen, mud to her knees, a satchel clutched to her chest like a shield, holding up a storm-lantern whose flame bent toward her face as if it loved her. Her hair had escaped its braid entirely. She looked, all things considered, less like an invader than like someone who had run away from something, which — Ivo would learn — was precisely what she was, though the something turned out to be much larger than a household.

"Don't move," he said, and stepped out of the dark with his father's iron poker raised, because the cell had no better weapons than pokers and conviction.

She screamed. Then she stopped screaming with a violence that was somehow worse, going rigid, staring past him down Terrace Row with an expression he'd never seen on a living face — the look of someone seeing a place they had no possible reason to recognize and recognizing it anyway.

"This isn't—" she started. Stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "This street. This is Corvane Street. Except it isn't, the numbers are different, the fountain's gone, but the *shape* of it—"

"Who are you?" Ivo kept the poker up. His hands were shaking and he prayed she couldn't tell. "How did you get in? Who sent you?"

"No one sent me." She was backing away, and behind her was the fog, and Ivo watched her realize it — watched her understand, with dawning horror, that the way she'd come in had already softened behind her like a footprint filling with snow. "There was a pass. There was a road. I was following — I was trying to *outrun*—"

"Outrun what?"

She looked at him then, properly, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified in equal measure, and she said, with the flat precision of someone reciting a sentence she'd read rather than lived:

"*And on the third night she shall come up the eastern terraces, carrying a light that loves her, and the lamplighter's daughter shall be watching from the shadows.*"

The poker dropped six inches before Ivo caught himself.

That was — those words were — every child in Vessany could recite fragments of the Prophecy of the Return, the way children outside learned prayers or songs. It was stitched into banners, carved above the door of every safehouse, read aloud each year on the Night of Embers while old men wept. *The lamplighter's daughter.* Ivo had heard that phrase ten thousand times and it had always meant somebody else, somebody far away and future, somebody important.

But his father's cell met in the back room of a lamp-oil shop. His father was a lamplighter, third generation, licensed by a crown that no longer existed. Ivo himself trimmed wicks and hauled oil. The lamplighter's daughter — well. There was no daughter. There was only Ivo.

"That's not funny," he said, and his voice came out strange.

"I'm not being funny." She hugged the lantern to her chest. The flame leaned toward her, gentle as a hand on a shoulder. "I don't want to be here. I don't know what this place is. I stole a book and I ran away and now the book says—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if the next word cost money. "What *is* this place?"

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. At the lantern burning warm and alive in a country where nothing burned. At the mud on her, the exhaustion, the utter lack of guile. Spies, his father said, came smooth and prepared. This girl looked like a rabbit that had outrun the dogs straight into a temple.

"You're in Vessany," he said finally.

"I don't — that's not a real country. My master's maps—"

"Your master's maps are wrong." Something hot was rising in Ivo's chest, something he usually kept banked like a covered fire. "Vessany is real. We're real. We've been real for a hundred years while the whole Outside decided we weren't worth remembering." He lowered the poker, mostly because his arm was tired. "Come with me. If you run, the fog takes people. If you stay on the streets, you'll be safe until morning. And if what you just said is what I think it is—" He couldn't finish it. Saying it aloud felt like striking a match indoors.

"Is what?"

"Come with me," Ivo said again, "and find out."

* * *

The lamp-oil shop sat in the Second Ring, on a lane so narrow two people couldn't walk abreast, behind a door painted the green of old bottle-glass. Ivo made her wait in the alley while he checked the street, checked the window, checked the three chalk-marks on the lintel that meant all quiet, all safe, all ours. Then he brought her in through the cellar, past casks of oil that hadn't been oil in decades — the stock had gone thin and grey as dishwater, like everything else that depended on the Outside for its nature — and up the back stairs into the room where the Cause lived.

His father sat at the head of the table, as always, copying. Around the table sat eleven others: Dagny with her scarred knuckles, the twins Hesta and Brannic, old Sorrel dozing upright as usual, and the rest, the faithful, the patient, the buried-alive. Lamplighters all, or children of lamplighters, keepers of a trade that had outlived its country's right to exist. On the wall behind Pellin hung the banner: black cloth, silver thread, the broken circle of the sigil with a single point re-forged, the promise they'd all been born inside of.

*One day the Bearer comes. One day the fire relights. One day Vessany steps back into the world.*

Every head turned when the girl came up the stairs.

"She crossed at the eastern terraces," Ivo said. "Near the customs house. Alone. No gear worth mentioning, no weapons. She was crying." He hesitated, hating the next part, unable to stop it. "She quoted the text. The third-night passage. Word for word."

The silence that followed had texture. Old Sorrel woke fully for the first time in a year. Dagny's scarred hands went flat on the table. And Pellin rose slowly from his chair, setting down his pen with the care of a man handling something explosive, and looked at Ada — Ivo had gotten her name in the alley, Ada Halloran, she'd given it like throwing down a card — looked at her the way he looked at a lamp that might yet be coaxed to burn.

"Say it again," Pellin said quietly. "All of it. Exactly."

"I don't know what it means." Ada's voice shook and she pushed it straight through the shaking, which Ivo noted, unwillingly, took a kind of courage. "I found it in a book I stole from my master. I've been reading it for two days trying to escape it. Every page is — it's my life, written down before I lived it, my route, my mule, tonight's date, and now this, this passage, and I don't know what a sigil is, or a bearer, or why your wall has that symbol on it, except that it was on the book's cover too, and I want to go *home*—"

Her voice cracked on the last word. The lantern in her hands guttered, steadied, guttered — and then flared, sudden and tall, throwing her shadow huge up the cellar wall, and every person in that room saw a flame burn bright and warm and *hungry* in a land where fire had forgotten how, and eleven grown rebels and one lamplighter's son stopped breathing at the same time.

Pellin came around the table. He knelt — Pellin, who knelt for no one, who'd once told a royal tax-collector's ghost to get out of his shop — he knelt on the floorboards at Ada's feet, and took her free hand in both of his scarred ones, and bowed his head.

"Sigil-Bearer," he said. "Welcome home."

Behind him, one by one, the others followed. Dagny. The twins. Old Sorrel, lowering himself down with creaks like ship's rigging. Eleven rebels kneeling in lamp-oil grease and candle-smoke, heads bowed, and Ivo — Ivo stood frozen by the stairs with the poker still in his hand, watching the girl he'd dragged in at poker-point become, in the space of a single flare of impossible flame, the most important person in a country that technically did not exist.

Ada looked wildly around the room. At the kneeling rebels. At the banner. At the lantern blazing in her grip like something with opinions. Her eyes found Ivo's, and what was in them wasn't triumph or destiny or any of the words on the banner. It was the look of a drowning woman being handed a crown instead of a rope.

"Please," she said, to the room, to the ceiling, to Ivo. "Somebody tell me what's happening."

Nobody answered her. Nobody, that is, except the grimoire in her satchel, which — though no one touched it — opened softly by itself to a page near the middle, and lay there glowing faintly at the edges, like a lamp remembering how.

Old Sorrel, still kneeling, began to laugh — a dry, wondering sound, half joy and half terror.

"The fire knows her," the old man said. "The fire has been waiting for her. Gods help us all — the waiting's over."

Ivo set the poker down against the wall very carefully, the way you put down something you might need again, and found that his heart had split cleanly down the middle: half of it shouting *this is the Cause, this is everything, this is the night we've prayed for*, and the other half, quieter and more stubborn, looking at the terrified girl with mud on her knees and thinking: *she didn't ask for this either.*

He didn't kneel. Not yet. But he didn't leave the room, either.

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the dead lamp on the workbench — the one that had refused to burn for three days — flickered once, twice, and lit.
