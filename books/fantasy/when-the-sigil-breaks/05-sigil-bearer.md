## 5. Sigil-Bearer

Ivo had caught trespassers before. He had dragged poachers out of the orchard rows, hauled a smuggler's boy off the old customs house roof, once wrestled a half-mad lamplighter's apprentice down from the Spire of Small Hours where the wind sang through empty lamp-glass. Every one of them had fought him, or wept, or lied with their teeth showing.

The girl did none of these things. She walked beside him through the dark streets like a woman being led to her own funeral, clutching a book to her chest as though it were the only solid thing left in Vessany.

And maybe it was. The book had come from Outside — Ivo could smell the world on its leather, rain and woodsmoke and turnpike dust, all the smells his father's cell kept locked in jars and passed around on feast days like relics. Nobody brought things into Vessany. Things went out, or they faded. A book that crossed the border in living hands was either a miracle or a weapon, and Ivo had learned at his father's knee that miracles were just weapons nobody had aimed yet.

He said none of this. He walked, and she walked, and the lamps along Candlemakers' Row guttered as they passed, leaning their small flames toward the stranger like flowers toward an unexpected sun.

She noticed. He saw her notice — saw her flinch when the third lamp bent its fire sideways to watch her go by.

"They do that?" she asked. Her voice was thin but level. "Follow you?"

"They don't follow me," Ivo said. "They've never done that for anyone."

* * *

His father's cell met above the tannery, in a room that still remembered being a counting-house: ledgers gone ghost-pale on the walls, a table scarred by a century of elbows, and eleven rebels who looked up when Ivo pushed the girl through the door and immediately began arguing about what to do with her.

"—a spy, obviously, sent down from the Ember Court—"

"—Sera has no need of spies, she has need of nothing, that's rather the point—"

"—she came over the pass. Nobody comes over the pass unless something called them—"

"Something did call her." That was his father. Aldous Bramm filled a doorway the way other men filled a room, and he filled this one now, looking at Ada Halloran the way a carpenter looks at timber — measuring her for something already built. "It called her by name."

Ivo felt the girl go rigid beside him.

"You know my name?" she said.

"It's written in your own hand on the flyleaf of that book." His father held out his palm. Not commanding. Worse than commanding — certain. As if the book had already agreed to be handed over. "Give it here, girl, and let's see what else is written."

For a moment Ivo thought she'd bolt. Her knuckles went white around the grimoire; her eyes flicked to the window, the door, the shuttered lamp, cataloguing exits the way he himself did. Then something in her face closed, and she set the book on the table with exaggerated care, like a card player laying down a losing hand.

"Careful," she said. "Some of the pages bite. I mean that literally."

A ripple of unease went around the room. His father opened the cover without hesitation.

Ivo watched him read. Watched the certainty in Aldous Bramm's face change texture — not soften, never that, but deepen, the way iron deepens when it goes into the forge. His father turned pages with fingers that trembled only slightly, and stopped, and read aloud:

"*In the hundredth year of the fading, when the last fire leans toward sleep, the Bearer shall come from beyond the border, walking a road unmarked, bearing the word made ink.*"

Silence. In the tannery below, someone dropped a hide-frame, and the bang of it seemed to knock every rebel back a step.

"That's tonight," whispered Marla Quill, the oldest of them, who remembered the first years of the fading because her grandmother had told her. "That passage — my mother taught me that passage — we thought it was a figure of speech. A hundred years, give or take—"

"It isn't figurative." His father turned the page again, and Ivo saw what was on it and felt his stomach drop through the floorboards of the world.

There was a sigil drawn there — the Sigil, the great interlocking wheel-and-flame that hung faded on every wall in Vessany, worn smooth on every coin, stitched into every flag nobody flew anymore. And beneath it, in fresh ink that gleamed wet even now, ran a line in handwriting that was unmistakably the girl's: cramped, slanted, letters leaning east like trees on a windy coast.

*Ada Halloran, born under a waning moon, keeper's blood, bearer's hand.*

"That's not my writing," the girl said. Too fast. Too loud. "That's — I copied pages out of my master's ledger two days ago, that's my hand, but I never wrote *that*, I've never seen this book before this week, I stole it, I'm a thief and a runaway and my master is going to flay me alive if you don't—"

"She quotes the text," Marla Quill murmured, wonder in her voice, "and doesn't know it."

"I don't quote anything! I'm telling you I'm nobody!"

"You," said Aldous Bramm, closing the book with a sound like a tomb lid, "are the Sigil-Bearer."

* * *

What happened next, Ivo would remember for the rest of his life, and he would remember most clearly how fast it happened — how one sentence fell into that shabby room and eleven grown men and women went down like wheat.

His father knelt first. Aldous Bramm, who had not knelt to the crown when Sera's line still wore theirs, who had spat on the word *majesty* since Ivo was a boy, folded down onto both knees on the dusty counting-house floor and bowed his head. And then Marla Quill, and then the Ferro brothers, and then all of them, a rustling collapse of coats and reverence, until the only people standing in the room were Ivo and the girl.

"Don't," Ada said. Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Don't — get up. Please get up. You've got the wrong person."

"The book does not err." His father spoke with his head still bowed, and Ivo had never heard that tone from him, had never heard *anyone* speak that way — the voice of a man setting down a weight carried so long he'd forgotten his own shape without it. "A hundred years we have read those lines to our children at midwinter. A hundred years we have been told the fire would lean toward sleep, and a stranger would come, and the word would be made ink. Girl — Ada Halloran — look at the lamps behind you."

She looked. So did Ivo, and his breath caught, because every lamp flame in the room was standing straight up, tall and steady and gold-edged, burning brighter than any lamp in Vessany had burned in his lifetime. They threw real shadows. They lit the far corners. They looked, for the first time Ivo could recall, like light instead of memory.

"I can't do that," Ada said. "Whatever that is, I didn't — I copy ledgers. I mix poultices. I mended my master's fence three times last autumn. I'm a hedge-witch's apprentice from Thornfell and I ran away because I found a page in my own handwriting dated *tomorrow*, and I want to go home and pretend none of this ever happened."

"Tomorrow," repeated Marla Quill softly, from her knees. "The texts say the Bearer will arrive knowing and unknowing. Written and unwritten."

"Your texts say a lot of things!" She spun on them, and Ivo saw the fear in her transmute, briefly, into fury — and the fury was more honest, and he liked her better for it. "Do you know what it's like, reading a book that already knows everything you'll do? Do you know what that's like? I tried to burn a page of it last night. It wouldn't burn. My whole life I've been chafing under one secretive old woman, and now you want me to be — what, exactly? What do you want me to *be*?"

His father rose. Slowly, joints cracking, an old soldier standing up from a long siege.

"We want you to be the end of it," he said. "Or the beginning. The words are close cousins in the old tongue. The dragon-fire gutters, girl. Streets at the city's edge are going out — not dark, *out*, gone, as if they'd never stood. We have perhaps a year. Perhaps less. For a hundred years we have hoped, and hope without a face starves. You are its face."

"I'm seventeen," said Ada Halloran.

"So was the first keeper," said his father gently. "So was the first keeper."

* * *

They gave her the best room — which is to say the least faded one, above the cooperage, with a bed that remembered being slept in and a window facing the direction of the Ember Court, where the palace glowed faintly on its hill like a coal wrapped in ash. They gave her bread and salted plums and a jug of small beer, and left her alone, and posted no guard, because — as his father said — you do not guard the answer to a hundred years of prayer.

Ivo found her on the roof.

He hadn't meant to. He'd told himself he was checking the sightlines, checking the streets, doing his job; and then his feet had carried him up the cooperage stair and out through the trapdoor, and there she sat on the ridge tiles with the grimoire open across her knees, silhouetted against a sky that held too few stars. Vessany's stars had been dimming too. Nobody talked about that.

"You should be sleeping," he said. "If I'd walked a border pass today I'd sleep like the dead."

"If I sleep, I'll dream about that room full of kneeling people." She didn't look around. "Do they do that often? Fall down like that?"

"Never. Not once. My father hasn't bent his knee in forty years. He broke a royal bailiff's nose for expecting it." Ivo climbed up and settled himself a careful arm's length away on the cold tiles. "You should understand what you're looking at. Those people aren't worshipping you. They're starving, and you're the first food in a century. There's a difference. But it won't feel like a difference, after a while. To them, or to you."

"Comforting."

"I wasn't hired for comfort."

"What *were* you hired for?" Now she looked at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and frightened, and underneath all of it, reluctantly, sharp as a new knife. "You dragged me here by the collar. You're his son. Are you watching me?"

"Yes," Ivo said honestly. "But right now I'm mostly wondering why the lamps follow you."

She glanced down into the street. Below, along the cooperage lane, the lamp flames leaned toward the building, all of them, straining upward like grass toward light, and as she watched they brightened — just a little, just enough to make the shadows sharper.

"Make them stop," she whispered.

"I don't think they're asking your permission."

"That's what I'm *afraid of*." She shut the grimoire and hugged it, and for a moment she looked so young, so ordinary, a runaway girl on a rooftop with a stolen book, that Ivo nearly reached out and took it from her. Nearly offered to throw it off the roof into the canal. "Everyone keeps saying *the Bearer*, *the Bearer*. Nobody's asked whether I *want* it. Nobody thinks that matters."

"It matters," Ivo said. "To some of us."

"To you?"

He considered lying. It would have been easier, and kinder, and it was what a spy would do. Instead he said, "My mother believed in the prophecy. She died believing it — not because of it, just — believing it, the way you believe in weather. My father believes in it because the alternative is admitting he's spent his life guarding a country that's already dead and simply refuses to lie down. I believe in it the way you believe in a ladder when the floor's burning. I don't love the ladder. I just need it to hold."

Ada was quiet a while. The lamps below slowly gave up their leaning and returned to their ordinary guttering, and the dark came back in around the edges of the street, patient as ever.

"And if I'm not the ladder?" she said finally. "If the book's wrong, or I'm wrong for it, or I just — refuse?"

"Then Vessany finishes dying," Ivo said, "and everyone in it goes out with the lights. Except—" he hesitated.

"Except?"

"Except the prophecy also says the border opens for the Bearer. Both ways. In, and out. Which means if you're real, you're the first person born Outside who could walk out of here again." He looked at her sidelong. "Which makes you the most dangerous person in this country, Ada Halloran. To the crown, to the rebels, to the dragon. Everyone will want to aim you. Including the people who just knelt to you."

She stared at him. Somewhere below, a night-watch bell rang the hour — the ninth hour of the Small Watch, a sound that had rung in Vessany for four hundred years and would ring whether anyone heard it or not.

"That's the second time tonight someone's told me I belong to them," she said.

"No," Ivo said, standing, brushing tile-dust from his knees. "That's the first time someone's told you the truth about the people who told you the first time. Get some sleep. Tomorrow my father takes you to the Ember Court. Queen Sera will want to see the book."

"Queen of a country that isn't on any maps."

"Queen of a country that's still *here*," Ivo said. "Ask her how much longer. Ask her what the fire costs. And when she answers — watch her eyes, not her mouth."

He went down through the trapdoor and left her on the roof with the stars and the book and the lamps that loved her without asking. And as he climbed down into the dark of the stairwell, he heard, faintly, behind him — the sound of the grimoire's pages turning on their own, one after another, like something settling in to stay.
