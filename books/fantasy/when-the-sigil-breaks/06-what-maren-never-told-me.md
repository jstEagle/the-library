## 6. What Maren Never Told Me

They gave her a room in the house behind the tannery — a narrow bed, a washstand, a window that looked out on a street where the lamps had never once been lit and yet the shadows fell as if they had. Ada sat on the edge of the bed with the grimoire open across her knees for a long time before she dared touch it again.

The rebels had left her alone after the kneeling, which was somehow worse than if they hadn't. Kneeling she could have argued with. Silence felt like agreement, and agreement felt like a door closing softly behind her.

Outside, someone was singing. It took her a while to place the tune, and longer to understand why it unsettled her: it was an old harvest song from home, one Maren hummed over the kettle in winter — except here the words were different, older, sung in a language Ada almost knew. The melody had crossed the border and come back changed, the way a letter comes back when you send it to someone who has moved.

She turned the grimoire to the page where the prophecy named her — *the Bearer comes by the smugglers' road, with ash under her nails* — and made herself look at the margins.

She had been avoiding the margins since the Thornfell passes. There was something in them, some faint brown ghost of ink beneath the prophecy's black, like writing written over writing, palimpsest-fashion, and every time she looked at it too directly her eyes slid off. That was the tell. Hedge-witches learned early to notice what magic wanted you not to see; Maren used to say that concealment was just emphasis turned inside out. *If something hides itself hard enough,* she'd said once, scraping dried feverfew from a mortar, *it's because it matters more than the thing it hides.*

Ada wet her thumb in the washwater and touched it to the margin beside the line about the smugglers' road.

The brown ink woke. Not all at once — it came up slowly, the way a bruise surfaces, letter by letter, as though the words had been waiting years for exactly this small permission. And the hand was familiar before she could name why, familiar the way the harvest song had been familiar, and then she did name it and her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

It was Maren's handwriting.

Not the ledger hand — not the neat, patient script in which Maren recorded purchases of beeswax and sal-ammoniac and the occasional discreet fee from farmers whose cattle needed unhexing. This was Maren's other hand, the one she used for working notes, cramped and slanted, letters leaning like people hurrying against wind. Ada had seen it a thousand times on scraps tucked into herb drawers, on the inside of the workshop door where Maren kept her true recipes away from casual eyes. She had copied it so often, imitated it so often in practice, that she could have forged it blindfolded. She knew its moods. She knew how the *f*s went spidery when Maren was tired and how the crossbars thickened when she pressed hard because she meant what she was writing.

These crossbars were very thick indeed.

*She came by the road that is not a road* — the margin read — *but I brought her there, or she brought me, I no longer know which way the debt runs. The pass opens only for the one who carries the book. Remember this, whoever reads: the book chooses its courier, and the courier is chosen long before she knows there is anything to choose.*

Ada read it three times. The room got colder around the words, or she got colder; it amounted to the same thing.

*I*, she thought. *I brought her.* But who was *she*? Not Ada. The ink was decades old — she could feel it now, the age of it, the way you can feel the age of bread by its smell. This note had been written thirty years ago at least, maybe more, in a margin of a book that had supposedly sat sealed and untouched in Maren's locked cabinet until the night Ada stole it.

Unless it had not sat untouched. Unless the cabinet, the lock, the whole careful theater of secrecy had been something else entirely — not a wall but a slow drip, Maren feeding the book to her apprentice one borrowed glance at a time until the day came when the girl would steal it whole and think the theft her own idea.

Ada set the book down on the blanket, flat, as if it might behave better lying down.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, the detachment she'd learned over fevers and difficult births and the time the Rill boy had come to them blue-lipped from the millpond: *the body panics first, then the mind follows, and if you keep the hands busy the mind has nothing to follow.* So she kept her hands busy. She turned pages. She worked thumb-wet water along the margins of the prophecies, page after page, and the brown ink surfaced everywhere, Maren's voice threading through the old text like roots through a wall.

Beside the passage about the mule: *She will bargain badly and be cheated and it will save her life. Let her.*

Beside the description of Vessany's dark streets: *Do not let her see the outer ring first. The emptiness will frighten her more than any enemy. Take her in by the lamplighters' quarter, where the houses still remember being loved.*

And beside a passage Ada had skimmed past twice without reading closely — a short, strange verse about *the keeper who goes out and the debt she leaves glowing behind her* — Maren had written only four words:

*I left my heart here.*

Ada stopped.

The lamp — the single candle, rather; Vessany's electricity, whatever had powered it once, was a memory even the buildings seemed embarrassed by — guttered in a draft she couldn't locate. She pulled her cloak around her shoulders and read the verse properly.

It was not really a prophecy. Prophecies pointed forward; this pointed backward, the grammar of it all wrong for prediction, full of *has been* and *was given up* and *remains.* It read like an epitaph composed by someone still alive to hear it recited. *The keeper who goes out carries the fire's last argument with her. What she leaves behind remains alight, and waits, and does not forgive.* Below it, in a different, much older hand — the same hand, presumably, that had written the rest of the book, the first keeper's hand — a single line in a language Ada didn't know but could suddenly, horribly, half-translate, because Ossuary's fire had burned it into her during the lessons: *the debt is paid in kind or in blood.*

Maren had been here.

Maren had stood in this city of unlit lamps, in this country erased from every map Ada had ever memorized, and she had gone out through the border carrying something — *the fire's last argument* — and she had left something behind that glowed and waited and did not forgive. And then she had gone home to a village in the green hills beyond Thornfell and taken an apprentice, and taught that apprentice herbs and ledgers and the ethics of hexing, and said nothing, nothing, nothing, for fifteen years, while all the time —

All the time what?

Preparing her? Penance?

The two words circled each other in Ada's chest like cats deciding whether to fight. If it was preparation, then everything Ada had ever believed about her own choices was a fiction with a very patient author. The day she'd arrived at Maren's door, orphaned and eleven, furious with grief — had Maren seen her coming? Had the old woman already known, the way the grimoire had known about the mule? Had every kindness been curriculum? The extra honey in her tea during lesson weeks. The way Maren had taught her Latin script before she'd taught her local runes, saying the runes would come later, they always do. The way Maren had flinched, just slightly, just once, the afternoon Ada had idly drawn a spiral sigil in spilled flour and asked what it meant — and Maren had wiped the flour away with her apron harder than flour deserved, and said, *Nothing. It means nothing anymore,* and gone out to feed the chickens in the rain.

Penance, then. Penance meant Maren had done something here that needed undoing, and had spent thirty years failing to undo it, and had finally settled on the monstrous arithmetic of raising a child toward the undoing instead.

*Or both,* whispered a voice in Ada that sounded unpleasantly like Sera. *Preparation as penance. You were the apology. People make apologies out of other people all the time. It's cheaper than making them out of themselves.*

She hated the voice. She hated more that she couldn't refute it.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around what passed for midnight — the sky over Vessany held stars in fixed positions, she'd noticed, like jewels sewn to a cloth that no one shook out anymore — she heard footsteps on the stair and put the grimoire under her blanket just as Ivo knocked.

"You're awake." He didn't sound surprised. He leaned in the doorway, lamplighter's coat over his arm, soot to the elbows. In the candlelight he looked younger than he had in the cellar where they'd caught her, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "My father sends his compliments and asks whether the Bearer requires anything."

"The Bearer requires," Ada said, "to know how long your father has known I was coming."

Something moved behind Ivo's eyes — quick, guarded, quickly shuttered. "The prophecy says—"

"I've read the prophecy. The prophecy says a great many things it doesn't mean. I'm asking about your father."

"Then ask him."

"He kneels too fast," Ada said. "Men who kneel that fast have usually decided in advance what kneeling buys them."

For a moment Ivo almost smiled, and she saw that he was someone who rationed smiles, spent them carefully, like a man paying out rope over a long drop. "You should sleep," he said. "Tomorrow the Court's answer comes. Whatever it says —" he hesitated, and she understood he was deciding whether to spend another smile or something costlier — "whatever it says, don't drink anything at the Ember Court until I've drunk it first. Old custom. Royal households poison for reasons of state; rebel households poison for reasons of spite; either way, it's the guest who dies."

He left before she could thank him, which she suspected was deliberate. Kindness, delivered and withdrawn before gratitude could form a claim on it.

Ada lay in the dark with the grimoire's weight against her ribs like a second heartbeat and turned Maren's marginalia over and over. *Let her. Take her in by the lamplighters' quarter. Do not frighten her.* As if Maren had been here ahead of her — as if these notes weren't thirty years old at all but instructions, a route map, a mother-bird's flight plan laid over the prophecy's iron rails. Every hardship foreseen and softened in advance. Even the cheating mule-dealer had been permitted, budgeted, *let her.*

And yet the notes were brown with age. Thirty years brown. Which meant either that Maren had known all of it three decades ago — every step, every spooked animal, every rebel cellar — or that the notes were not notes at all but part of the book's own machinery, wearing Maren's hand the way the border wore the shape of a road.

Both possibilities were unbearable. Ada chose, for the sake of sleep she wouldn't get anyway, to believe the crueler one, because the crueler one at least kept Maren human. A woman who had erred. A woman who had come here young — she had seen her, in the fire-lessons, *glimpsed Maren young standing in this hall*, dark-haired and terrified and burning with purpose — and had broken something, or been broken by something, and had carried the wound home and wrapped an apprenticeship around it like a bandage.

*The debt is paid in kind or in blood.*

What had Maren left behind? *My heart here,* the margin said, and Ada did not think it was sentiment. Hedge-witch language was literal language. When Maren wrote *heart* she meant the organ, or the thing the organ stood for in the old craft — the seat of binding, the anchor-point of every lasting spell. A hedge-witch bound a sickroom with a pinch of the patient's hair and a word spoken over the patient's heart. To leave your heart somewhere was not poetry. It was collateral.

Maren had left her heart in Vessany. And Vessany had kept it, glowing, unforgiving, for thirty years — and now the book had fetched Maren's apprentice across the border to collect the arrears.

*Paid in kind or in blood.* In kind would mean giving back what was taken: going to wherever Maren's heart was kept and returning it, and heaven knew what that would loose. In blood meant the Sigil-Bearer's death, the sacrifice Sera's courtiers spoke of in hushed, hungry tones.

Ada pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw constellations that at least moved, unlike the ones outside.

*You were the apology.*

"No," she said aloud, to the dark room, to the book, to the woman thirty years and one impossible border away. "Whatever you meant me to be, you don't get to have decided it. I decide what I am. That's the whole — that's the entire point of being a person, Maren. You taught me that. Or you taught me to think you taught me that, which may be the cruelest trick of all."

The grimoire, under the blanket, was warm. It had been warm all evening, faintly, patiently, like a sleeping animal, and she realized with a lurch that it had been growing warmer as she read — warming to her attention the way the margins had warmed to her thumb. The book was listening. The book had always been listening.

She pulled it out and opened it one more time, to the epitaph-verse, and found — where hours ago the old hand had written only *the debt is paid in kind or in blood* — fresh ink. Black. Wet. In her own handwriting.

*Then let her pay in kind,* it said. *Bring me what she left. Bring it to the mirror at the border house, on the night the fire gutters. She will understand what must be done. She has always understood. — A.H.*

Ada stared at the letters until they blurred. Her own initials. Her own slanting *A*, her own cramped *H*. Dated tomorrow.

She remembered the ledger, the first impossible page, the entry dated a day ahead that had started all of this — and understood now that it had not started all of this at all. It had merely been the earliest visible stitch in a seam that ran back thirty years, through a locked cabinet and a stolen book and a village girl's entire childhood, back to a young woman with dark hair standing in a dragon's hall, making a bargain whose terms she was still, three decades later, trying to dodge.

The candle went out. In the sudden dark, the grimoire pulsed warm against her knees, twice, like a patient heart.

Like a heart waiting to be returned to the body that owned it.

Ada sat in the blackness and made herself say the next sentence out loud, because saying things aloud was the only magic she had ever fully trusted:

"I have to find the border house."

Somewhere below, in the tannery yard, a lantern came alight — one of the few lit lamps in all of Vessany — and began, unhurried, to move toward the city's western edge. Toward the ruins. Toward the road out.

Toward, though Ada could not yet know it, a mirror, and a face on the far side of the glass that she had spent fifteen years calling *master.*
