## 7. The Ember Court

Sera Vessan-Amaranth had not been summoned anywhere in thirty-one years. She was the one who did the summoning, insofar as anyone in Vessany still bothered with the forms of a court that had no country left to govern — and so it was with a particular, carefully banked irritation that she read the rebels' message twice, then a third time, holding the paper close to the brazier because the ink seemed to shrink from her eyes.

*A girl has come through the passes. She carries the grimoire. The text names her.*

No signature, of course. Ivo Lamplighter's hand, though — she recognized the careful, resentful precision of the letters, the way he pressed too hard on the downstrokes, as if each word were a debt he was being forced to acknowledge. His father's son, and hating it.

Sera set the message down on the table before her and looked at the fire.

The Ember Court had once held four hundred candles at every sitting. She knew this the way she knew everything about the palace: not from memory but from *habit*, which is what memory becomes when it outlives the thing remembered. Now there were eleven candles that mattered, arranged in the old pattern on the long council table, and one brazier that never went out, fed by coal that arrived by means no one could explain, burning with a heat that carried, faintly, the smell of ozone and old blood. Dragon-heat. Ossuary's fire reached even here, thinned to a warmth, the way starlight reaches a cellar.

The palace itself held together the same way she did: out of stubbornness and residual heat. Walls stood where walls had always stood, though some mornings Sera found new corridors where none had been, and others found whole wings gone soft at their edges, like bread left in rain. The tapestries in the great gallery depicted battles no veteran could confirm; the portraits' eyes followed visitors for exactly as long as courtesy required and then stopped bothering. Outside the windows — the windows that still showed anything — lay streets that existed only between dawn and dusk, and beyond those, the gray nothing where Vessany's borders used to be, where the world had quietly agreed to forget them.

A century erased. A dynasty reduced to one woman who kept the forms of rule because the alternative was admitting the forms were all there was.

And now this. A girl. A grimoire. *The text names her.*

"Names are cheap," Sera said aloud, to the empty hall, in the voice she used when she wanted the emptiness to know it was being watched. "Anyone can be named. I was named heir to a kingdom the day before it stopped existing."

The emptiness, as usual, declined to argue.

She rose. Her joints announced themselves — thirty-one years inside a dying country will do that, will write themselves into you like water writes itself into stone — and she crossed the hall past the portraits of her ancestors, each of whom had ruled a Vessany that appeared on maps. She stopped, as she always did, before the last of them: her grandmother, painted in the final year, wearing the sigil of the realm at her throat. The sigil itself was kept now in a locked case in the vault below, cold as a dead coal, and Sera had not touched it since she was nine years old and her father had put her small hand on it and said, *Remember what we are owed.*

She had spent her life remembering what they were owed. It had made her neither rich nor loved. It had made her precise.

"Send word to the rebels," she told the door-warden — an old man named Corvel who had held his post so long that the doorway had begun to grow into him, ivy-like, and who may have been more door than man by now. "The girl comes to the Ember Court tomorrow, at the hour of the second bell. Alone. Tell them—" she paused, weighing how much leash to give, "—tell them the crown extends its protection to the Sigil-Bearer."

The words tasted like ash and possibility. She had waited thirty-one years for a reason to say them. She had also spent those same years learning that every gift the prophecy offered came wrapped in someone else's ambition.

* * *

They brought the girl in at the second bell, and Sera's first thought was not *prophetess* or *savior* or even *impostor*, but: *she's frightened.*

Good. Frightened people were honest people, or at least people whose lies had visible seams. The rebels had dressed her — she could see it, the borrowed clothes sat wrong on her shoulders, a coat cut for someone broader, boots that didn't know her feet — and they'd scrubbed the road off her, but nothing could scrub the road out of her eyes. She walked into the Ember Court looking at everything: the candle-pattern, the brazier, the soft-edged walls, the portraits. Looking like a person taking inventory of an unfamiliar house. Which was either the behavior of a spy or the behavior of someone genuinely seeing Vessany for the first time.

Or, Sera reminded herself, the behavior of an excellent spy.

"Your name," Sera said, from the throne — not seated on it; she never sat on it, the throne had gone strange decades ago and hummed faintly when occupied — but standing beside it, one hand resting on its arm as if in casual ownership.

"Ada Halloran." The girl's voice cracked on the second syllable and she pushed through it. "I'm a hedge-witch's apprentice. From Thornfell. Outside."

"Outside." Sera let the word hang. "Do you know what we call the Outside here, Ada Halloran?"

"No."

"*There.*" Sera stepped down from the dais, slowly, giving the girl time to study her as she was being studied. Let her see the gray in Sera's hair, the crown-shaped shadow the years had worn into her posture. "Not 'the rest of the world.' Not 'beyond the border.' *There.* As if we were the only place that counted. That's what a century of erasure does to grammar." She stopped three paces away. Close enough to see the girl's pulse jump in her throat. "You crossed into a country that does not exist, carrying a book that should not be in your hands. Explain yourself."

"I stole it," Ada said, chin coming up with a defiance that cost her something — Sera could see the cost, the way you can see the effort behind a lifted drawbridge. "From my master. Maren. I ran away, and I took it, and I didn't know what was in it until I read it. And then I tried to follow the pass that wasn't on any map because I thought—" she faltered, restarted. "I thought if I got ahead of the prophecy, maybe it would stop being true."

Something turned over in Sera's chest, slow and cold, like a stone shifting in a streambed.

Because that was not what messiahs said. Messiahs said *I have come.* Impostors said *I have come.* This girl said *I tried to outrun my own story,* and Sera had lived long enough among desperate people to know that desperation invented better lies than ambition ever managed — but it also occasionally produced, by pure accident, the truth.

"The grimoire," Sera said. "Show me."

Ada fumbled at the satchel — the rebels had let her keep it, another mark of their certainty — and drew out the book, and Sera felt the room change before she understood why. The eleven candles leaned, all together, all in the same direction, like grass under wind. The brazier's heat pressed outward. Even Corvel, motionless in the doorway behind them, made a sound like a hinge.

The girl noticed too. Sera watched her notice, watched her go pale in the specific way of someone confirming a fear rather than encountering a surprise.

"You've seen it do this before," Sera said.

"It did it when I read the page with tonight's date on it." Ada held the book out flat on both palms, an offering or a surrender. "It's been doing it for days. It knows things. Things that haven't happened. My route. My mule. The night I crossed over. It's a hundred years old and it's writing my life and I want you to understand that I hate it."

*I hate it.* Not *it is holy.* Not *I am chosen.* 

Sera did not reach for the book. Instead she said, "Read to me. The passage that concerns you. Concerns us. Word for word — do not paraphrase, do not summarize. Precision matters."

Ada wet her lips, opened the book to a ribbon-marked page, and read:

"*When the fire gutters low and the border thins to breath, the Bearer shall come from There, bearing the book against its own current, and the sigil shall wake upon her, and the dragon shall know her name before she speaks it, and the crown shall kneel—*"

"Stop." 

Sera had raised one hand without deciding to. The hall was very quiet. In the quiet she could hear the palace settling, that endless faint creak of habit reasserting itself, stone remembering to be stone.

"'The sigil shall wake upon her,'" Sera repeated. "Upon her. Not *in* her. Not *through* her. Upon." She began to walk a slow circle around the girl, and Ada turned to track her, warily, like a cat in a room of rocking chairs. "Do you understand what a flexible word *upon* is? It can mean the sigil rests on her. Marks her. Chooses her. Or it can mean it merely *alights* — touches her briefly, the way a bird touches a branch, and moves on. And '*the crown shall kneel*' — kneel *to* her? Kneel *with* her? Kneel *because the crown must die*?" She completed the circle and came to rest facing the girl directly. "Who wrote this?"

"I don't know. The marginalia is in my master's hand. Some of it. The oldest parts aren't in any hand I recognize — or they're in every hand, it changes."

"Yes." Sera's voice came out flatter than she intended. "It does that. The grimoire was written by committee, in a sense. By everyone who needed it to say something specific." She had promised herself she would not tell the girl that. It slipped out anyway, and she watched Ada register it, file it, hold onto it — sharp, this runaway, sharper than the rebels had guessed.

"And what did *you* need it to say?" Ada asked. Quietly. Not insolent — or insolent only in the way honesty is insolent.

Sera considered several answers: the gracious one, the royal one, the strategic silence. What she gave her, because the girl had earned it with that single question, was something closer to the truth.

"I need it to say that Vessany comes back," she said. "That my country returns to the world, that the dead streets live, that the people who faded — my mother among them, she simply grew transparent one winter and was gone by spring — that all of it can be undone. I have wanted that for thirty-one years with a hunger that would embarrass us both if I described it accurately. So when a girl walks in quoting a text that promises restoration, do you imagine I am capable of judging her claim objectively?" She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "I am telling you this so you understand that everything I am about to do, I do knowing I want it too badly to trust myself."

"That's not reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be. Reassurance is what liars sell." Sera gestured toward the far end of the hall, where a side door stood half-open on darkness. "You will stay in the Court tonight. Tomorrow you go to the dragon."

At the word *dragon*, the girl's face did something complicated — fear braided with something else, something almost like recognition, and Sera noted it and did not yet ask.

"And if the dragon doesn't know my name?" Ada said. "If your prophecy is wrong?"

"Then you are a hedge-witch's runaway who trespassed in a dead country, and I will send you home with apologies and an escort, and I will come back to this hall and stand alone with eleven candles and wait for the next hundred years." Sera heard her own voice and disliked its steadiness, its practiced quality, the sound of a woman who had rehearsed disappointment until it fit her like tailoring. "But if the dragon *does* know your name, Ada Halloran — then everything changes. For you most of all. I want you to hear that from me plainly, tonight, while you can still walk away from it. Whatever the rebels have told you, whatever the book promises, being the answer to a hundred years of praying is not a blessing. It is a weight, and the ones who carry it rarely get to set it down."

Ada looked at her for a long moment. In the candlelight her eyes were the ordinary brown of good soil, of hedgerows, of a world that still had weather — and Sera felt the full strangeness of it, of standing in a palace held up by habit, speaking to the first living thing from *There* to cross her threshold in thirty-one years.

"You're warning me," Ada said slowly, "and testing me at the same time."

"I am doing both because I don't know which I'll need more tomorrow." Sera turned back toward the throne, toward the portraits, toward the vault where a sigil lay cold as a drowned coin. "Corvel will show you to your rooms. Eat. Sleep if you can. The dragon's lessons—" she caught herself; she had not meant to reveal that she knew of the lessons already, and covered it, barely, "—the dragon does not wait on anyone's comfort."

Behind her, she heard the girl pause at the doorway. Heard her say, not to Sera but perhaps to the hall, to the candles, to the century of listening dark:

"*The crown shall kneel.* You know that line cuts both ways."

Then footsteps fading, and the door, and Sera alone with the fire.

She stood a long while before the brazier, watching the dragon-heat breathe its slow ozone breath, and admitted to the emptiness what she could not admit to the girl: that *upon* might mean *briefly*. That *kneel* might mean *mourning*. That the prophecy's authors — whoever they were, whatever they wanted — had built their promise out of words with trapdoors in them, and she had just gambled her country's last hope on a frightened child's ability to land on the right side of every ambiguity.

Desperate gambles, she thought, were the only kind left. All the safe bets had died with the maps.

Above her, faint through floors of habit and stone, she could feel the palace leaning — very slightly, very patiently — toward the mountain where the old fire guttered, and where tomorrow a hedge-witch's runaway would stand before the last dragon in Vessany and be asked, in one form or another, the only question that had ever mattered:

*What will you burn?*
