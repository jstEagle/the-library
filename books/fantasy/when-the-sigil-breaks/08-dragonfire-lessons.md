## 8. Dragonfire Lessons

The dragon's hall had been a throne room once, or a cathedral, or something that had needed to make people feel small on purpose. Ada couldn't tell which. The vaulted ceiling was lost in shadow and old smoke; the pillars were carved with processions of figures so worn they might have been kings or saints or simply people walking somewhere in a hurry. The floor was black stone shot through with veins of dull red, like a hearth that had gone cold but not forgotten how.

Ossuary lay coiled at the far end of it, beneath the one intact window, where grey light came down through glass the color of old honey.

Up close, the dragon was worse than the rumors. Ada had heard *old*, had heard *dying*, and had imagined something grand in its ruin — a magnificent wreck. What she saw instead was thinness. The great wings folded along Ossuary's flanks were membranes stretched over ridges of bone, translucent at the edges, veined like the skin of an elderly hand. Scales that must have been bronze once, or copper, had dulled to the color of ash that hasn't decided whether to be grey or brown. When the dragon breathed, the whole hall breathed with it — a slow tide of warmth rolling out from the vast body, then receding — and in the hollow of each exhale Ada could hear a faint crackle, like a fire burning down to embers in a room no one has tended.

"You stare," Ossuary said. The voice came not from the mouth but from everywhere at once, resonating up through the floor, through the soles of Ada's boots, through her teeth. It was a dry voice. A patient one. "Most of them do, their first hour. The rebels send me their boldest and I spend the first hour being looked at."

"I'm sorry," Ada said, because she couldn't think of anything else.

"Don't apologize. Curiosity is fuel too." One eye opened — a slit of molten gold, heavy-lidded, ringed with wrinkled scale — and fixed on her with the weary attention of someone who has graded ten thousand examinations. "Though yours is a thin sort of curiosity. Frightened underneath. That will do. Fear burns longer than wonder."

Ada's hands found each other and held on. She had been brought here at dawn by two rebel women who spoke to her the whole way as if she were already elsewhere — *the Bearer will need her strength, the Bearer should eat, mind the step, the Bearer* — and every courtesy had landed on her like small stones. Now, alone with the last dragon of Vessany, she discovered she would have preferred the crowd.

"Maren said you'd come eventually."

Ada went still. "You knew Maren?"

"I know everyone who stands where you're standing. Eventually." The golden eye closed again. "We begin."

---

There was no ceremony to it. That was the first cruelty. Ada had expected ritual — words, gestures, something to hold onto with her hands while her insides rearranged themselves. Instead Ossuary told her to sit on the black stone three paces from the dragon's snout, cross-legged, palms open on her knees.

"Now hold out your hands," the dragon said, "as if you were asking for bread."

"That's it?"

"That's everything. Everything else is mine to give and yours to survive." Something shifted in the coils; heat rolled off the old body in a wave, and Ada felt it pass over her face like standing near an oven door. "I am going to put a little of myself into you. Not much. A coal's worth. You will try to keep it lit. Most people cannot. The fire does not live in the body — it lives in the *remembering*. Whatever you are made of, that is what it will burn."

"Burn my what—"

The flame entered her through her palms.

It did not feel like fire. It felt like being *read*.

---

Later — she never learned how long later — Ada was on her side on the warm stone, shaking, her palms pressed flat against the floor as if she could pour whatever remained of the coal back out through them. The heat inside her chest wasn't pain exactly. Pain would have been simpler. It was more like every memory she owned had been taken down from its shelf, opened, and held over a candle to see how fast it caught.

Her mother's kitchen. The smell of yeast and wet wool. It burned slowly, sweetly, and she had felt it going and could not stop it, could only watch from the inside as the memory turned gold at its edges.

Maren's voice saying *"copy this ledger again, girl, your hand is sloppy"* — that one blazed up hot and fast and furious, and the coal inside her *fed* on it, grew fat on her anger, and for one terrible moment Ada had understood that if she let it, it would eat her whole life this way, memory by memory, until there was nothing left to be anyone with.

"Enough," Ossuary had said, and drawn the flame back, and left her empty and ringing like a struck bell.

Now the dragon watched her recover with the unhurried gaze of something that measured time in centuries. "You lasted longer than most."

"How long did I—"

"A quarter of an hour. The baker's daughter managed four minutes. The king's son, the last heir before Sera's line thinned, managed eleven seconds and fainted." A sound moved through the coils that Ada slowly recognized as laughter — dry, rustling, like paper in wind. "You hate being remembered wrong. I could taste it. That's good fuel, that kind of hating. Terrible for the soul. Excellent for the fire."

Ada pushed herself upright. Her palms were unmarked. That seemed obscene somehow — all that burning, and nothing to show for it. "What happens to the memories? The ones it ate. My mother's kitchen — is it—"

"Gone?" Ossuary considered. "No. Dimmed. The fire takes the top layer, the shine of it. You'll remember the room but not the smell. In time, perhaps, the smell comes back. Perhaps not." The golden eye regarded her without apology. "You asked to learn. This is the tuition."

*I didn't ask,* Ada thought. *They knelt at me until asking was the only door out of the room.* But she didn't say it, because the dragon already knew, and because some stubborn part of her — the part that had copied ledgers neatly even when her hand ached, the part that kept walking after the mule spooked — wanted to try again anyway. That frightened her more than the burning did.

"Again," she said.

---

The second lesson took her mother's laugh. The third took the exact blue of the sky over Thornfell on the morning she ran. By the fifth, Ada had learned to brace, to choose — to shove a memory forward like a shield when the coal reached hungrily for something she loved. She fed it Maren's scoldings. She fed it the taste of the rebels' gruel. She fed it the long grey afternoon of copying ledgers, hour upon hour of it, and the fire accepted these offerings with the indiscriminate appetite of a beast that has stopped caring what it eats so long as it eats.

She also learned what Ossuary meant about *holding*. Holding the flame was not gripping it. Gripping made it flare, and flaring cost her more. Holding was closer to carrying water across an unstable floor — attention, balance, a continuous small decision not to spill. Every heartbeat she chose to keep it. Every heartbeat it tested the choice.

"You see now," Ossuary said during the rest between the sixth lesson and the seventh, "why we need a Bearer and not merely a brave person. The fire does not want strength. Strength runs out. The fire wants *continuance* — someone willing to pay daily, forever, in the only coin it accepts." The great head lowered until the molten eye was level with her, close enough that Ada could see her own tiny reflection swimming in it. "Tell me, hedge-witch's runaway. What would you pay forever?"

"Nothing," Ada said. "Forever is too long to agree to."

Something flickered behind the golden eye — surprise, maybe, or approval. "Careful," the dragon murmured. "Answers like that get people written into prophecies."

---

It happened during the ninth lesson, though Ada would count the lessons afterward and never be certain of the number, because the burning blurred them together like rain on ink.

She was holding the coal — truly holding it, balanced, breathing, the way Ossuary had shown her — when the fire went deeper than it ever had before. Past her shields, past her offerings, down to a stratum of memory she hadn't known she was guarding. And it *showed* her something instead of eating it.

The hall. This hall. But whole — the pillars bright-carved, the window unbroken, light pouring through it in bars of amber. Courtiers in colors that don't exist anymore, dyes lost with the country that made them. And at the center, where Ada sat now, stood a young woman in an apprentice's smock with ink stains on her fingers and her dark hair escaping its braid, arguing — actually *arguing*, chin up, furious — with a dragon whose scales were still the color of new copper.

The woman was Maren.

Not old Maren with her bent back and her secrets. Young Maren, thirty years younger, alive in a way Ada had never seen her, standing in this hall as if she belonged to it, as if she had stood here a hundred times — and turning, suddenly, in the vision, to look directly at where Ada was looking from, with an expression Ada knew intimately because she'd seen it every day of her apprenticeship: the look of a master who has just realized her student has read further in the book than she intended.

The coal guttered. The vision snapped out. Ada came back to herself gasping on the black stone, the hall dim again, the present pressing in — and Ossuary watching her with an expression she had never seen on the dragon's face before.

Guilt. Old, layered guilt, the kind that has had decades to fossilize.

"She stood there," Ada said. Her voice came out cracked. "In this hall. You taught her. Like you're teaching me."

The silence stretched long enough that Ada heard the fire crackle low in the dragon's chest, and heard, beneath it, the fainter sound — the one she'd been hearing all day without naming — of the city outside, of lamps failing street by street, of a country remembering itself out of existence.

"She was better than you," Ossuary said at last, softly. "Faster. Braver. She held the flame for a full hour her first day, and she paid gladly, and that was the tragedy of her — she paid *gladly*. People who pay gladly never ask what they're buying." The dragon's coils shifted; ash sifted from the folds like grey snow. "I taught her everything I am teaching you. And then I let her leave believing she had failed. Believing it was kinder than the truth. She carried that failure out through the border and built a life on top of it, the way you build a house over a well."

"What truth?" Ada's heart was slamming. "What did she fail at?"

Ossuary closed the golden eye. When it opened again, all the irony in it had burned away, and what remained was only age, and sorrow, and something that looked unbearably like fear.

"Ask her yourself," the dragon said. "Because she is coming back, Sigil-Bearer. I can smell her on tomorrow's air. And when she arrives, you will both discover—" the great head sank toward the stone, weary as a closing book, "—that the page she tore out of that grimoire of hers was never missing at all. I kept it. All these years. Waiting for one of you to be brave enough to read it."

* * *

Outside the hall, dusk had thickened to true dark, and Ada stood a moment in the corridor letting her eyes adjust. The lamps of the Ember Court were lit — half of them. The other half stood cold, wicks unburned, as if the city could no longer quite remember how many lights it used to own.

She flexed her scorched-clean hands and started toward the rebel quarters, and it was only halfway there, passing a window overlooking the dark ring of the outer city, that she stopped.

Below, at the far edge where the streets grew thin and uncertain, a lamp was moving. A single traveling light, bobbing along the smugglers' road that existed only inside Vessany, coming toward the palace with the steady, unhurried pace of someone who had walked a very long way and refused to hurry at the end of it.

Ada knew that walk. She had followed it down village lanes for eleven years.

The lamp was coming, and tomorrow — whatever Ossuary had kept, whatever page, whatever truth — tomorrow it would arrive at her door.
