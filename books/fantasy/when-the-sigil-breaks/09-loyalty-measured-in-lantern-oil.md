## 9. Loyalty, Measured in Lantern Oil

The order came the way all of Tam Ashgrove's orders came: not spoken aloud, but written in lamp oil on the back of a shutter, where only a son who had been reading his father's hand since he was six could make it out.

*Follow her. Report. Every mistake.*

Ivo stood in the lamplighter's storeroom beneath their house on Candlemaker's Row, holding the shutter at arm's length as if distance might change the words. Below him — three floors down, past the cellar where his mother's wedding lantern still hung unlit — Ada Halloran slept in the guest room they'd given her, in a bed that hadn't held a sleeper in ninety years. The mattress had been re-stuffed with fresh straw that morning. His father had ordered that too.

Everything for the Bearer. Everything *for* her, and everything *around* her, and nothing ever simply *with* her.

He put the shutter back on its hook and did not look at it again.

* * *

Spying, it turned out, was easy. That was the shameful part.

Ada made mistakes the way rivers made water — constantly, generously, without effort. She called the Ember Court a palace when every child in Vessany knew it was only called that because the word *palace* had been one of the last things to fade. She tried to pay for bread with Outside coin, and the baker — old Wrenna, whose hands were more flour than flesh these days — had stared at the foreign silver like it was a dead bird. She walked into the Street of Glasswrights looking for a chandler and came out with a crowd of silent, hopeful people following her, because in Vessany a stranger walking your street was a festival and a stranger asking questions was scripture.

All of it, Ivo reported faithfully. He kept a ledger, actually — a real one, bound in grey leather, with columns ruled in his own neat hand. *Day eleven: mistaken about the Court. Day twelve: gave away her cloak to the widow Sorrel. Day thirteen: wept behind the tannery and denied it.* His father read each entry with the particular attention he gave to accounts of oil and wicks, weighing them, entering them into some larger arithmetic Ivo was not permitted to see.

What Ivo did *not* enter into the ledger was this: that on day twelve, after giving away her cloak, Ada had gone back to the widow's house in the dark and swept her step, mended her shutters, and left a charm against rheumatism woven out of fence-twine and stubbornness — hedge-witch work, small and homely and utterly useless by any doctrine of the Sigil, and the widow had wept over it anyway. That on day thirteen, when she wept behind the tannery, she wept because she'd found a child's chalk drawing still visible on the stones — a dragon, lopsided and beloved — and had known its name without being told, because the grimoire had told her first, before the child had even lived.

Those things went into a different ledger. One with no columns. One that lived somewhere behind Ivo's sternum and grew heavier every night, so that by the third week he was carrying it up and down the hills of Vessany like a man hauling water in a bucket full of holes.

"You're limping," his father observed one evening, watching him come down the stairs.

"It's the ledger," Ivo said, before he could stop himself.

Tam looked at him for a long moment. "What ledger?"

"The oil counts." Ivo heard his own voice go smooth and easy, a smuggler's voice, and hated how good he'd gotten at it. "We're short again. The outer ring takes more than it gives."

His father nodded, satisfied, and wrote something down. And Ivo climbed the stairs to check on the girl he was betraying in one direction and deceiving in the other, and wondered, not for the first time, which betrayal was worse.

* * *

She caught him at it, of course. She was a hedge-witch's apprentice; noticing things was half her trade.

"Ivo." She was standing in the doorway of the lamplighter's loft, where he went some evenings just to be above the streets, above the fog of forgetting that rolled through the lower city on nights when Ossuary's fire dipped. She had her arms crossed and her chin up and she was doing her best to look formidable, which would have worked better if her hair weren't full of straw from the mattress and her eyes weren't red-rimmed. "You're following me."

"I live here."

"You've been following me since the second day. You always walk four paces behind and to the left, you never let me take the same street twice, and yesterday you steered me away from the Border Gate like it was on fire." She came closer. "My master used to say liars are easiest to catch because they plan. Honest people improvise. You plan, Ivo."

He should have lied better. Instead he said, "Would you believe I'm protecting you?"

"From what?"

"That depends on the day." He sat down on the loft's low sill, where the glass was thick and green and showed the city below in drowned colors. "Sit down. I'm going to teach you something, and then you can decide whether to hate me."

She sat. Close. Closer than was strictly necessary, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and hedge-herbs on her, and he filed the observation away in the ledger behind his sternum and resolved never to take it out again, an oath he broke within the hour.

"This city has names," he said. "Real ones. The grimoire calls it Vessany, and the prophecy calls it the Land Under the Flame, but nobody who ever lived here called it either." He pointed down through the green glass. "That square there — the one with the broken fountain? The maps call it Sigil Square. We call it the Duck Pond, because before the border closed, the fountain ran shallow and warm and ducks nested in it, and the children fed them crumbs off the fountain rim until the whole square smelled like a farmyard. There hasn't been a duck in a hundred years. But it's still the Duck Pond."

Ada leaned toward the window. "And that street? The long one going down toward the dark part?"

"Ropewalk. Because they stretched rope along it, end to end, a quarter mile. When the wind came off the passes you could hear it humming. My grandmother remembered the sound." He swallowed. His grandmother had died when he was nine, and had spent her last coherent year teaching him every name she could remember, faster and faster, like a woman bailing out a boat. "That dark part is the Tannery Quarter, except we don't say tannery, we say the Stink, affectionately. And the hill beyond it —"

"— the one shaped like a sleeping dog?" Ada said softly. "That's Grieve Hill. It says so in the grimoire."

"The grimoire says a lot of things." He turned to look at her, and the words came out of him sideways, the way true things do when you've been carrying them too long. "Here's what my father wants, Ada. He wants the fire relit and the border opened and Vessany restored — with him standing beside the throne, holding the ledgers, controlling the oil and the grain and the gates, because someone has to be practical while prophets dream. He wants you crowned or enthroned or whatever the prophecy does to you, because a living saint is worth ten armies. And he wants reports on whether you're strong enough to be useful, or weak enough to be steered."

She didn't flinch. He'd expected flinching. What she did instead was worse: she nodded slowly, like a woman confirming a diagnosis she'd already suspected.

"And what do *you* want?"

The question hung there. Down in the Duck Pond, wind moved through the empty fountain and made a sound almost — almost — like rope humming.

"I want you to know what the streets are called," Ivo said finally. "Because everyone here is going to spend the next months telling you what you are. Bearer. Queen. Sacrifice, probably, though nobody will use that word yet. And nobody — *nobody* — is going to ask you what anything is called. So." He pointed at the window again, and his voice steadied. "Grieve Hill. Ask me why."

"Why?"

"Because after the border closed, the families who lost people on the Outside — traders caught abroad, soldiers, one entire wedding party — they climbed that hill to look north, toward where the passes used to be. Every evening. For years. Until looking became mourning and mourning became habit and the hill got its name." He shrugged, suddenly embarrassed. "That's why. Not prophecy. Just grief, with geography."

Ada was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "My master Maren had a rule. Never learn the true name of a place unless you're prepared to owe it something."

"What do you owe Grieve Hill?"

"Nothing yet," she said. "But I'm keeping track."

* * *

After that, the treacheries began in earnest — small ones, daily ones, the kind that never make songs.

He taught her the names in secret, on rooftops and back stairs, in the intervals between her duties and his spying. He showed her the Lamplighters' Walk, the high road his family had tended for nine generations, where every lamp post had a name and most of the names were jokes. He taught her which doors in the old quarter opened onto rooms that no longer existed — you'd turn the handle and step through into a parlor furnished in a century gone, warm and lit, occupied sometimes by people who had faded so gently they hadn't noticed — and which doors opened onto nothing at all, and how to tell the difference (the nothing-doors were cold, and slightly damp, like the inside of a cheek).

He covered for her mistakes. When she lit a candle wrong-side-first in the Ember Chapel and the flame burned blue and spoke in her grandmother's voice, Ivo told the deacons it was a test of faith, and they believed him because he was Tam Ashgrove's son and Tam Ashgrove's sons were assumed to be serious. When she gave bread to a faded man who had forgotten how to chew, and the rebels muttered that the Bearer was squandering stores, Ivo entered it in the grey ledger as *distribution of alms per ancient custom*, which was even true, in a way — it had been a custom, once.

And once — only once — he lied outright to his father's face.

It was the night Ada went missing. She'd slipped the house at dusk without a word, and by midnight Tam had half the cell turning the city over, and Ivo stood in the kitchen watching his father's face do arithmetic with panic and ambition and something that might have been love, in the way that a cage might be said to contain a bird.

"She'll be at the Dragon's Hall," Tam said. "Or the border. If she runs, everything —"

"She's asleep," Ivo said. "I checked an hour ago. She's restless — new place, bad dreams — but she's asleep."

His father studied him. And here was the terrible thing, the thing Ivo would carry for the rest of his life however long that turned out to be: his father believed him instantly. Without hesitation. As if the possibility of Ivo lying had simply never been entered into the great ledger of Tam's mind, because sons who fetch oil do not lie, sons who keep columns do not lie, sons are not separate accounts at all but extensions of the father's own hand.

"Good," Tam said. "Watch her closer, though. Something's coming. The dragon's fire dipped tonight — did you see the fog on Ropewalk? Two more dips like that and the outer ring goes, and with it our numbers, and then the crown moves first and we lose everything." He tapped the table. "The girl is the only door out of this, Ivo. Doors get used. That's what doors are *for*."

Ivo nodded and went upstairs, and did not sleep, and at dawn Ada came back smelling of cold stone and old paper and something else — something like Maren, though Ivo wouldn't learn until later why the name made his chest tighten when he thought of it in connection with her. She had ink on her fingers. She wouldn't say where she'd been.

He entered *slept poorly, rose early, mood improved* in the grey ledger. In the other one, the one behind his sternum, he entered: *she knows now. Whatever it is she knows, she went and found it alone, and she didn't trust me with it.*

That entry hurt worse than the lie.

* * *

They fell into a pattern, those weeks. By day, spy and subject. By dusk, on the rooftops along the Lamplighters' Walk, two people lighting lamps together and pretending that was all it was.

There was an art to it, lighting lamps in Vessany. Each one had to be coaxed — the wick turned three times sunwise, a word spoken (any word; the lamps weren't picky, they just wanted to be addressed), and then the small blue pilot-flame that lived in the post's heart would lean toward yours and the lamp would wake. His grandmother had sworn the lamps remembered being loved. Ivo had thought that was sentiment, until the evening Ada touched a lamp on the Walk and it flared so bright and so glad that moths came up out of the fog from three streets away, arriving like pilgrims.

"They *do* remember," she whispered.

"They remember being tended," Ivo said. "There's a difference. Anything remembers being tended." And then, because the moment seemed to require honesty or ruin: "That's why my father keeps you comfortable. Tending. It works on people too."

She looked at him across the small flame between them. "Is that what you're doing?"

"No," he said. "I'm teaching you swear words. Here — that street, the crooked one? We call it Sorry Row, because it's too narrow for two carts, so drivers met head-on and one of them always had to back all the way down and apologize to the whole street. There's a version of it in the grimoire. The prophecy calls it the Path of Humility." He grinned despite himself. "Prophets have no sense of humor. It's practically a job requirement."

Ada laughed — really laughed, the first time he'd heard it, a rusty sound like a gate opening after years — and the lamp between them burned steady and gold, and Ivo understood, with the clarity of a man stepping off a roof, that he was going to lose something. Either his father's world or this one. Either the cause or the girl. The ledger behind his sternum had run out of blank pages.

That was the night the summons came.

A runner from the Ember Court, breathless on the stairs, bearing Sera's seal — the sigil itself, pressed in wax that glowed faintly, as if the wax remembered being royal. Tam took the message, read it, and smiled the smile he saved for balance sheets that finally tipped the right way.

"They want her," he said. "The heir herself. An audience." He turned to Ada, and his voice went soft and reverent in a way that fooled everyone except, perhaps, the two people standing closest to him. "You see? Even the crown believes. This is it, child. The beginning."

Ada looked at Ivo — one look, quick, searching, a hedge-witch checking her protections — and Ivo gave her back the only thing he had left to give: a small nod, and the memory of a joke, and the name of a street where two carts once backed apart instead of colliding.

*Path of Humility*, he thought at her. *Remember which street you're on.*

Then his father's hand closed around his shoulder, fond and heavy as a yoke, and they went down together into the lamplit streets, three figures climbing toward the Ember Court through a city that remembered being tended — and above them, far up on Grieve Hill, the fog gathered thicker than Ivo had ever seen it, rolling down from the direction of the Dragon's Hall, as if something very old and very tired were breathing out its last, or breathing in preparation to speak.
