## 10. The Prophecy's Second Reading

The Ember Court kept its coldest room for its warmest purpose.

Sera had learned that in her grandmother's time, or rather in the years after her grandmother stopped speaking and began simply waiting, hands folded in the high seat while the dragon-heat thinned another degree. The Archive of the Relighting sat at the palace's northernmost point, where the walls were thickest and the warmth arrived last, if it arrived at all. It was where Vessany stored the things it could not bear to lose and could not bear to look at: treaty copies with nations that no longer remembered signing them; a child's shoe recovered from the outer ring before the outer ring stopped being anywhere; and the prophecy itself — not the clean liturgical copy the rebels kissed, but the working drafts, the quarrelsome, human mess of a text that had been *made*.

Sera went there alone, as she always did, because the things she did in that room were not yet crimes and she wished to keep them so only by secrecy rather than innocence.

She carried a lamp. That was the joke of it, the private joke she permitted herself on the walk down: heir to a nation whose fire was failing, holding a little brass lamp burning whale oil from a sea none of her people had smelled in a hundred years. The oil came through the smugglers' roads, paid for in sigil-coins that Outside merchants accepted as curiosities. Her whole country ran on being mistaken for something charming.

Inside the Archive, the cold was honest. She set the lamp on the reading table, pulled off her gloves finger by finger — a ritual, though she'd have denied calling it that — and unlocked the third drawer of the iron cabinet with the key she wore on a ribbon beneath her collar, against her sternum, where it had pressed its small teeth into her skin for eleven years.

The drafts of the prophecy filled seven boxes.

Everyone in Vessany knew the prophecy as a single thing, seamless as poured glass: *When the fire gutters, a Bearer shall come from beyond the border, unwritten by our grief, and shall take up the Sigil and relight the flame, and Vessany shall stand again among the nations.* Children recited it before they could read it. The rebel cell in the Tanners' Quarter had embroidered it inside their jackets. Sera herself had memorized it at seven years old, kneeling on cold stone, believing with the whole ferocious weight of her small body that salvation was simply a sentence someone had already finished writing, and all she had to do was wait inside it.

Then, at seventeen, she had found the first draft. And learned that sentences are built, and builders make choices.

* * *

Tonight's business concerned the fourth box, and the folios within it dated to the prophecy's ninth year of composition — the period the marginalia called *the year of the argument*, when the circle of keepers around Ossuary had evidently disagreed about what they were writing and why. Sera knew the names of the arguers now better than she knew the faces of her own court: First Keeper Ysolde, whose hand dominated the early pages; Brannoc the surveyor, who mapped streets that would later stop existing and annotated the maps with the furious precision of a man watching his work become fiction; and Maren — no surname given, young hand, impatient loops — who appeared in the record only briefly and then vanished from it entirely, which had struck Sera for years as the strangest fact in a room full of strange facts.

She drew out the folio marked in Brannoc's blocky capitals: **SECOND READING — FOR THE CIRCLE ONLY — NOT FOR THE PEOPLE**.

She had read it twice before. Once at nineteen, when she had been too frightened by it to do anything but lock it away again and tell no one. Once at twenty-four, when the guttering had eaten the Weavers' Row and forty-one people with it, and she had come down here in the ash-smelling dark looking for a reason to believe the sacrifice the text implied might be avoidable.

Both times, she had closed the box.

Tonight she smoothed the vellum flat under her palms and made herself read it the way she imagined its authors had written it: not as scripture, but as a decision.

*Let it be set down plainly,* Ysolde's hand said, in ink gone brown as old blood, *for whoever comes after, that the fire is not a coin to be spent twice.*

The variant text was short. That was the cruelty of it — the whole architecture of Vessany's hope, and the correction to that hope took less than half a page. Sera read it aloud to the empty room because silence made it worse, and her voice sounded steadier than she felt, which was the entire art of ruling:

*"The Bearer takes up the Sigil and the flame passes into them — not through them. Through is passage. Into is residence. The fire does not borrow a keeper; it keeps one. When the Sigil relights, the Bearer becomes its wick. They will burn as long as the country burns, conscious, bound to the hall, unable to die while the fire lives and unable to live while it dies. This is the cost. We struck it from the common text because a hope with a price at the end of it is a hope few will carry. But the fire will know the truth regardless, for the fire cannot be lied to. Whoever comes: let them come knowing."*

Below that, in the margin, in a different ink, younger and sharper: *"Ysolde says omission is mercy. I say omission is theft. We are stealing a life and calling it destiny."*

That marginal note was signed with an M.

Sera sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, the palace settled on its bones with a sound like a sigh, held together still by Ossuary's fading heat — heat that was failing faster now, everyone said so, the outer ring thinning like breath on winter glass.

*They will burn as long as the country burns.*

And the girl — Ada Halloran, hedge-witch's runaway, thief of grimoires, the most reluctant messiah ever to be knelt to in a tanner's cellar — was currently in the guest apartments eating bread and honey and asking Ivo Ferren questions about lamplighters' routes with the hungry curiosity of someone who intended to leave.

Sera thought about the word *leave*. It sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

Here is what Sera would have told you, had anyone been allowed to ask her honestly, which no one was: she did not hate Ada Halloran.

She envied her the way a starving woman envies the smell of bread through a window — totally, helplessly, with a hatred that is just love wearing armor. Because Ada could say *I'm not your Bearer* and mean it, and the saying of it cost her nothing but the rebels' disappointment. If Sera stood in the Tanners' Quarter and renounced her own claim, she would be renouncing the last living argument that Vessany deserved to exist. Her blood was the receipt for everything the country had lost. She had been born a proof.

Ada had been born a person.

Sera understood, with the cold clarity that came to her only in this room, in this cold, that she had spent her whole life waiting for the prophecy the way other people waited for permission to die. Not passively — never passively; she had drilled the royal guard with rusted pikes, she had memorized trade routes to markets that no longer traded, she had learned four dead dialects so that the language wouldn't finish dying on her watch. She had done everything except the one thing the prophecy forbade, which was hope without conditions. And now the condition had arrived at her table in the shape of a folio, and it was asking her a question she had spent eleven years avoiding:

If the Bearer must burn forever — if the girl from Outside must be fed into the flame like wick into wax — did Sera tell her?

The honest answer, the answer she wrote nowhere and admitted to no one, not even to the portrait of her grandmother in the high seat, was: *it depends on what telling costs.*

She turned the folio over. Brannoc's hand continued on the reverse — the surveyor had been the practical one, the one who asked *and then what?* after every noble sentiment:

*"Assuming the Circle accepts Ysolde's omission: anticipate the following. The Bearer, once chosen and marked, will resist. Resistance may include flight. Therefore the door should be tended by those who love the country more than the Bearer, and the Bearer should be loved — genuinely, strategically, thoroughly — so that when the hour comes, they stay not because they are forced but because leaving means abandoning us. A willing sacrifice holds longer than a chained one. Chains fray. Love, properly managed, does not."*

Sera read that paragraph three times.

*A willing sacrifice holds longer than a chained one. Love, properly managed, does not.*

She thought of the rebel cell, of old Ferren with his plans and his ledgers, of Ivo — earnest Ivo, who had caught the trespasser and delivered her like a gift and then, Sera had heard it from three separate spies, had begun teaching her the city's forgotten street names, walking her through the ghost-quarters with the particular tenderness of a man showing someone his childhood home. She thought of how quickly the whole city had arranged itself around Ada like iron filings around a lodestone: the kneelings, the gifts of bread left at her door, the women in the market who touched her sleeve for luck.

None of them knew. None of them could know. Every act of love in Vessany was, whether it meant to be or not, an act of preparation. They were fattening a sacrifice with affection and calling it reverence, exactly as Brannoc had predicted, exactly as Ysolde had designed, a hundred years before any of them were born — and the worst of it, the part that made Sera press her knuckles against her mouth, was that it was *working*. Ada was softening. Ada was staying. Ada had stopped flinching when they called her Bearer.

Love, properly managed, does not fray.

*But,* said a voice in Sera that was not Brannoc and not Ysolde and not duty, *but she didn't agree to this. She doesn't know. Knowing is the whole difference between a queen and a butcher, and you know it, you have always known it—*

"Be quiet," Sera said to the empty room, quietly, because even alone she governed her voice.

* * *

What tipped her — what finally moved the question from her head to her hands — was the smallest discovery of the night, and she nearly missed it. Turning the folio's final leaf, looking for Ysolde's closing seal, she found instead a loose scrap tucked into the gutter of the binding: a fragment, torn roughly, in that same young, impatient hand. Maren's hand.

*"—will not write the ending we choose onto someone else's life. Strike my name from the circle. I am going to the border house. If the fire truly needs a keeper, then let it ask, and let the one it asks be free to say no. Anything else is just—"*

The rest was torn away.

Sera held the scrap under the lamp for a long time. So. There had been a defection, a century ago. A keeper who walked out — the same name, she realized with a slow cold turn of her stomach, that was scrawled in the margins of the grimoire the girl had carried across the border. Maren. The runaway apprentice had a master who had once run away from *this*, from the very circle, from the very sentence. Which meant the woman who trained the Sigil-Bearer had known — had perhaps always known — what the Sigil-Bearer was for.

Had she told her? Had Maren sent her student across the border blind, as an instrument, the way Sera was considering doing? Or had she sent her as a warning wearing an instruction?

It changed nothing, Sera told herself. It changed nothing about the arithmetic. Forty-one people on Weavers' Row. The outer ring thinning by the week. A nation of ghosts, and one girl who could unghost them, and a price that had been struck from the text so that the text could be believed.

Arithmetic did not care about consent. Arithmetic had never cared about anything.

But Sera was not arithmetic. Sera was the last of a line that had ruled by habit and dragon-heat, and if Vessany was restored, she would rule it again — really rule it, thrones and taxes and treaties, the world remembering her name — and every instinct of her office, every lesson of her grandmother's folded hands, whispered the same counsel: *a crown recovered over a grave lasts longer than a crown shared with a living rival. The girl is not of the blood. Let the blood profit.*

Duty, she had always believed, was love that had grown up and stopped making excuses. Tonight, holding the folio, she felt duty change sex and temperature in her hands — felt it sharpen, felt the fine edge of it, felt how close cruelty lay to it, closer than a blade's width, separated by nothing but whether she spoke one sentence aloud.

The sentence was six words long: *The fire will keep you forever.*

Or its opposite: *Run, girl. Run tonight, and don't look back.*

One sentence saved her throne. The other saved nothing but a stranger's soul — and cost Vessany its only Bearer, because Ada Halloran, told the truth, would do exactly what she had done her whole life, which was run.

Sera locked the folio back in the drawer. She put the key beneath her collar, where its teeth found their old places against her skin. She trimmed the lamp and picked it up, and stood a moment in the honest cold, listening to her own heart conduct its small treacherous argument.

Then she went upstairs to compose a summons — warm, gracious, full of the courtesy of the Ember Court — inviting the Sigil-Bearer to take supper with her tomorrow night. Alone.

There were things, the summons would say, that a queen owes a guest.

She did not specify which things.
