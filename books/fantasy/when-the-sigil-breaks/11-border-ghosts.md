## 11. Border Ghosts

The marginalia led her north.

Ada had copied the symbols onto a scrap of her own paper before leaving Ivo's father's house — she trusted her own hand more than she trusted the grimoire now, and besides, carrying the book itself felt like carrying a mouth that might speak at the wrong moment. The marks were small, cramped in the gutter of a page describing the first failing of the fire: *the border house, where the road remembers being a road.* Beneath them, in ink gone brown as old blood, someone had written a single word in Maren's sharp, slanted script.

*Come.*

Not an instruction to anyone. A message left for whoever came after. For Ada, though Maren could not have known it would be her — or perhaps, some cold voice in Ada suggested, she knew exactly.

She told no one where she was going. That was its own kind of treachery, and she was learning to carry those lightly; Vessany ran on small treasons the way its lamps ran on oil. She told Ivo only that she needed a morning alone, which was true, and watched him decide not to press her, which was its own kindness. His father's eyes had followed her all week with a weight she didn't want to name yet. Some things were better carried into empty country.

The outer ring of the city ended the way everything in Vessany seemed to end — not in ruins but in hesitation. Streets grew narrower and then uncertain, cobbles giving way to packed earth giving way to grass that grew in straight lines because it half-remembered being a hedgerow. Beyond that, the land unrolled in long grey-green folds toward hills she had never seen on any map, because there was no map. There had been no map of Vessany for a hundred years.

She walked through country that flickered at the edges of her vision. Not vanishing — not yet, not out here where the dragon-fire still reached, however thinly — but *diluted*, like ink washed too many times. A stone wall appeared when she looked at it directly and softened into a suggestion when she glanced away. Once she crossed what must have been a bridge over a dry streambed, and for three steps she heard water underneath, clear and bright, and then it was gone, and she grieved for it without knowing why.

The border house sat where two hills folded together, in the crook of a valley that the afternoon light couldn't quite enter. It was smaller than she'd imagined — a farmhouse, really, stone and slate, with the patient ruined look of a building that has been abandoned by everyone except itself. Ivy had taken one whole wall. A chimney stood against the sky like a raised finger.

And the door was open.

That stopped her longer than anything else. In a country of fading things, in a house a century dead, the door stood open exactly wide enough for a person to pass through, and the hinges did not creak when she pushed it wider, because someone had oiled them recently enough that the oil hadn't yet faded.

Someone had expected her.

Inside, dust lay thick on every surface except a table in the front room, where the dust had been disturbed — not recently, not yesterday, but within years rather than decades. Rings marked where jars had stood. A chair bore the worn crescent of a body that had sat there many times, over many seasons, doing something repetitive and careful.

Writing. The desk against the far wall still held it.

Ada approached the way you approach a sleeping animal. The notes were stacked in loose bundles tied with string gone soft as cloth, and each page was covered in Maren's handwriting — younger here, the letters more upright, less bowed by thirty additional years of stooping over ledgers. Dates in the margins. Dates from a century ago, corrected and annotated, cross-referenced against dates from thirty years ago, when Maren herself had sat in this chair with these same bundles and read them the way Ada read everything: hungrily, and then with growing horror.

The first bundle was observation notes. *Fire level steady. Outer ring stable. Lamps burn low but true.* The second was theory — pages of dense argument about the nature of the sigil, the binding, the way belief braided itself into the border until no one inside could leave and no one outside could remember. The third bundle made Ada sit down in the worn chair without meaning to.

It was a draft. A partial draft, in different hands, some pages copied, some original — but she knew the cadence of the prophecies instantly, the rolling formal periods, the *and it shall come to pass* architecture of it. Here it was in pieces, in working drafts, with corrections. With *choices*. A line struck through and rewritten. A prophecy about the Sigil-Bearer that had once said *she* and been changed to *they*, and then changed back again. Marginal arguments between at least two writers, one of whom kept insisting the sacrifice clause be removed, and the other who kept restoring it in a hand that Ada recognized from the grimoire's title page.

The first keeper. Ossuary had told her — *written by the dragon's first keeper, to give the country a reason to keep hoping* — and here was the hope being manufactured line by line, argued over, amended, *authored*, in a farmhouse at the edge of a dying country while the ink was still wet on the world outside.

They wrote me, Ada thought. Before I was born, they wrote me. They argued about my pronouns.

She laughed, and it came out wrong, and she pressed her fist against her mouth until it turned into something else.

At the bottom of the last bundle was a letter, unsent, addressed to no one. Dated thirty-one years past.

*I have found the counter-spell. It will cost everything I have and it is not enough — the binding cannot be broken from this side while the prophecy holds the believers, and it cannot be broken from your side at all. But there is a door in the mirror. If I go through, I do not know what I will find, or whether I can come back. If you are reading this, then either I failed and never left, or I succeeded and left everything behind, including you.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. Both are honest.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Ada knew the shape of that apology the way she knew the shape of Maren's silence, the locked chest under the apprentice bed, the questions that were answered with chores.

Maren had sat in this chair. Maren had read these notes. Maren had walked to the mirror — and Ada turned, slowly, and saw it for the first time, though it had been in the room the whole while, against the eastern wall where the light through the ivy-choked window fell across it in green stripes.

It was a tall glass in a frame of dark wood carved with interlocking lines — the sigil, she realized, the actual sigil of Vessany, repeated around the border of the frame like a fence of written promises. The mirror did not show the room.

It showed a kitchen. Sunlight through clean windows. Copper pots. A kettle just coming to the boil, steam rising in the flat, unhurried way steam rises when nothing magical is happening at all. An ordinary room in an ordinary world — the Outside, rendered so vividly it hurt, because Ada had forgotten that the world could look like that, lit and level and *unhaunted*.

And standing in the kitchen, looking back at her through the glass, was an old woman.

Ada's body knew before her mind did. Her legs went stiff; her breath snagged on something. Because the woman was bent and grey and her hands were spotted with age, and none of that was Maren — and yet the way she stood, hip cocked against the counter, head tilted, watching, was so precisely the posture Ada had studied across thirty years of ledgers and lessons that recognition arrived whole, before thought, like a blow.

"Maren," Ada said. Her voice cracked the word in half.

The woman in the mirror went very still. Then her hand rose — slowly, the way the very old move, as if the air were honey — and pressed flat against the inside of the glass.

Ada crossed the room in four strides and put her own palm against the other side. Cold glass. Ordinary, indifferent glass. And beneath her hand, faintly, a warmth that was not the glass at all.

"You're old," Ada said, and hated how small it sounded, how much it gave away. "You're *old*."

Maren's mouth moved. No sound came through. Of course no sound came through. Thirty years on one side, a century on the other, and the mirror offered nothing but light. Ada watched the old lips form words, watched them repeat, slower, exaggerated, and caught them at last like stones fished from a river.

*You — shouldn't — be — here.*

"Neither should you!" The fury arrived all at once, fully grown, as if it had been waiting thirty years for the door to open. "Thirty years! You left me a *letter*? You couldn't even sign it? You let me copy your ledgers for a decade, you let me think — I thought you were teaching me hedge-craft. Boils and birthing charms and weather-sense. You were *preparing* me. Every lesson, every chore, every time you wouldn't answer — it was all leading here, to this house, to that *book*, and you knew, you *knew*—"

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and even distorted through old glass and older magic, Ada saw the grief in them and hated that she saw it, because grief was a claim, and Maren had forfeited every claim.

The old woman spoke again, deliberately, shaping each word.

*Read — the — last — bundle.*

"I've read everything." Ada's palm was still flat on the glass. She couldn't make herself take it away. That was the worst part, that some traitor part of her wanted the contact, wanted the warmth, wanted her master on the other side of anything at all rather than nowhere at all. "I know what they did. I know the prophecy was *drafted*. I know the sacrifice clause was put back in on purpose. I know you helped — or they helped you, or whatever happened here, I know you were part of it. Was that why you took me? Was I practice? Penance?" Her voice climbed. "Was I ever your apprentice, or was I always just the next page?"

Something moved through Maren's face that Ada had no name for — not guilt, or not only guilt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman seeing her own younger fury walking around in someone else's body.

Then Maren held up one finger. *Wait.* She stepped out of view, and Ada's heart lurched stupidly at the empty kitchen, at the thought of losing even this — and then she returned, moving slowly, carrying something flat and wrapped in oilcloth. She unwrapped it on the counter where Ada could see it.

A book. Small, plain-bound, nothing like the grimoire. Its cover was scarred and water-stained, and Ada knew it anyway, knew it the way you know a face in a dream.

The counter-spell. The thing the letter mentioned. *It will cost everything I have and it is not enough.*

Maren laid her hand on the cover. Looked up. And her mouth formed words with the terrible patience of someone who has spent thirty years rehearsing a single conversation.

*The — prophecy — binds — only — the — believing.*

"I know." Ada surprised herself with how steady it came out. "Ossuary told me. The keeper wrote it to give people hope, and hope became a cage. I know."

Maren shook her head, sharp, impatient — thirty years of impatience compressed into one gesture — and tried again, pressing each word through the glass like coins through a slot.

*Only — the — believing. Not — the — doubting. You — must — stop — believing — completely. All — of — you. Every — soul — in — Vessany.*

Ada stared. "That's not possible. They've believed for a hundred years. Their grandparents' grandparents believed. Sera believes. Ivo's father would die for it. You can't unbraid a century of faith from—" She stopped. Something cold slid down her spine, some understanding arriving ahead of its own explanation, the way the sound of water had arrived under the bridge before the water vanished.

Maren was nodding. Slowly. Watching her arrive at it.

"That's why the border is failing," Ada whispered. "That's why the outer ring is going dark. It's not the dragon running out of fire. It's the belief running out of — of *fuel*. People are forgetting to believe. Children are being born who don't." Her hand trembled against the glass. "The prophecy isn't holding them. It's *propping them up*, and the prop are rotting, and if it falls—"

If the prophecy broke cleanly, freely, chosen — the fire might accept something new. But if it simply collapsed, rotted through, while ten thousand people still clung to their half of it —

Maren's expression said she had done this arithmetic thirty years ago and had never once stopped doing it.

*The — counter-spell,* she shaped, slow and distinct. *Unwrites — one — believer. Only — the — one — who — casts — it. I — could — not — free — myself — and — stay — here. So — I — left. To — keep — it — safe. To — keep — *myself* — free — of — it.* Her jaw tightened. *So — no — one — could — use — me — to — finish — it.*

"And instead you hid it in a village and taught a girl to read," Ada said, "and hoped she'd steal the right book."

Maren didn't deny it. Didn't confirm it. Just looked at her across thirty years and a century and a sheet of silvered glass, and in that look Ada found something worse than betrayal or love: *trust*. Enormous, undeserved, unbearable trust, the trust of a woman who had gambled a child on a prophecy of her own making and lost track of the stakes.

"I'm not your penance," Ada said quietly. "Whatever I do next, I'm not doing it for you."

For the first time, the old woman smiled. It transformed her face, folded the decades away for a single heartbeat, and Ada saw the young Maren from the dragonfire visions — the one who had stood in Ossuary's hall with ink on her fingers — and understood that the girl in the vision and the woman in the glass had both been running from this farmhouse, in opposite directions, for the same reasons.

The smile faded. Maren leaned closer to the glass, close enough that her breath fogged it from the far side, a tiny ghost of cloud that bloomed and vanished. Her eyes went past Ada suddenly — to the window, to the valley beyond, to something Ada couldn't see — and the calm in her face fractured into urgency.

*Time,* she mouthed. *Little — time. Listen.*

She set her palm flat again, and began, fast and deliberate, to spell out words, and Ada bent close, heart hammering, catching each one:

*The — fire — does — not — need — a — death. It — needs — a — choice — renewed — daily. But — Sera — knows — the — variant — text — and — she — has — decided — what — you — are — worth.*

"Sera," Ada breathed. "What does Sera—"

But the mirror shivered.

Not the image — the glass itself, a fine tremor running through it like a struck bell, and the kitchen wavered at its edges the way streets wavered in the outer ring, sunlight stuttering, copper pots blurring. On the far side, Maren staggered, caught the counter, and looked down at her own hands with an expression Ada had seen on the faces of Vessan lamplighters watching a flame gutter.

No, Ada thought. *No.* You're on the Outside. The Outside doesn't fade. The Outside is the one place that—

But the border was failing everywhere, wasn't it. And Maren had stepped through it once, thirty years ago, carrying a piece of it inside her like a splinter, and splinters work outward.

*Maren!* Ada hammered the glass with the flat of her fist. "Look at me — what do I do? What's the choice? How do I renew it? *Tell me how—*"

The old woman steadied herself. Straightened, as much as her spine allowed. And with the last of the image's clarity, she pressed her hand to the glass one final time and gave Ada not spells or instructions but five words, formed slowly, unmistakably, with the full weight of thirty silent years behind them:

*Burn — the — book. Free — them.*

Then the kitchen folded inward like a closing eye, and the mirror showed only the dusty room, and the worn chair, and Ada's own face — white, furious, grieving — framed in the carved sigil like a portrait of someone the prophecy had gotten almost right.

She stood there a long time. The light through the ivy went from green to gold to nothing.

When she finally gathered the bundles and tucked them inside her coat — the drafts, the arguments, the unsigned letter, proof that destiny had authors — her hands were steady. Outside, the valley had gone blue with evening, and far off across the fading fields she could see the city's lamps, and among them, brighter than any other, the ember-glow of the Ember Court burning on the hill like a wound that refused to close.

Sera had summoned her for tomorrow. *The Court will receive the Bearer formally,* the messenger had said, with a courtesy that had felt, even then, like a lid settling onto a pot.

Ada touched the mirror once, lightly, goodbye or promise or both.

"Burn the book," she said aloud, testing the words, finding them heavier than any spell she'd ever copied. "Free them."

Then she pulled her coat tight and started down through the darkening valley toward the court, toward the crown, toward whatever Sera had already decided she was worth — carrying in her pocket, folded small, the knowledge that somewhere on the other side of a thinning world, an old woman was fighting to stay real long enough to see it done.
