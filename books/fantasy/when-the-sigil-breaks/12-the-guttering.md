## 12. The Guttering

I have counted ten thousand dawns from this hall, and I will tell you what no one asks a dragon to tell: they are not worth counting anymore.

The humans think fire is simple. Light, heat, hunger, end. They build their little lamps and their little hearths and imagine that flame is a servant, something that waits in the wood until summoned. They do not know what it is to *be* a fire — to feel every wick in a country as a nerve ending, every hearth-coal as a heartbeat you did not ask to carry. I am not a creature who breathes flame. I am a creature who is breathed through. Vessany runs through me the way blood runs through a wound, and for a century now I have been the wound and the bandage both.

My name was given by the first keeper, who found me already old in the mountains before there were maps. She said I should be called Ossuary, because I had a way of collecting dead things — dead oaths, dead years, dead names — and keeping them warm. It was not a compliment. Dragons do not receive compliments; we receive diagnoses. I have lived with mine for longer than her country existed, and I will outlast its forgetting too, if my fire permits, which lately it does not.

Here is what a dying god's morning sounds like: a creak in the deep ribs of the palace, which are my ribs, which are also, if you follow the heat-veins far enough, the ribs of every house in the middle ring. A dryness in the throat of the world. And then — this is the part they never put in songs — a silence where a street used to sing.

* * *

It happened on the night the trespasser arrived, though she did not cause it. I want that recorded somewhere besides the prophecy, which records nothing true. The girl with the stolen book came over the threshold smelling of Outside rain and hedge-craft and fear, and while she was still being dragged through the rebel quarter by the lamplighter's boy, I felt the outer ring begin to go.

Not burn. Not crumble. *Go.*

You must understand how a kept country dies. Vessany was erased — unmade from the world's memory, struck from maps and ledgers and grandmothers' stories — and a country cannot survive being forgotten unless something holds its shape against the forgetting. That is my work. My fire is a needle, and reality here is a hem coming undone, and every day I stitch. The inner ring, near my hall, holds best; the fire is thick there, close to the source. The outer ring has always been the hem's edge, where the stitches are longest and the thread thinnest.

That night I felt three streets thin past bearing. Marrow Lane, where the tanners used to live when there were tanners. The Weeping Stair, which descended to a canal nobody remembered digging. And Copperfield Row, forty-one houses, a bakehouse, a shrine to a saint whose name I alone still carried, because I carry everything.

I reached for them. This is not metaphor. When a street begins to gutter, I reach down the heat-veins the way a mother reaches into a dark crib, and I feed it flame, and usually the street sighs back into being, the way a candle remembers itself when cupped against wind.

Copperfield Row did not sigh back.

I felt the people first — eleven souls awake at that hour, one of them a child with a fever, one an old man who had spent his whole life inside the forgetting and had never once seen the Outside he told his grandchildren about. Their warmth passed through me like coins through a beggar's hands. And then the row's shape failed, not all at once but corner by corner, door by door, the way frost leaves a window, and the eleven souls went with it. Not dead. I would have felt them die; death has a taste, iron and salt. They went *elsewhere*, into the same nowhere that took the country's name, and whether anywhere receives them, I do not know. Even gods are permitted ignorance. It may be our only privilege.

The bakehouse was last. Its oven had been lit for ninety years without pause — habit upon habit upon habit, the mortar of this place — and I had loved that oven the way you love a faithful dog. It went out between one heartbeat of the city and the next, and the dark that rushed into the space it left was not the dark of night. It was the dark of *never*.

Afterward I lay in my hall with my chin on my foreclaws and did not move for six hours, which for a dragon is a kind of weeping.

* * *

The girl came to me three days later.

They had named her Sigil-Bearer by then, of course. Humans are quick to kneel; kneeling costs less than thinking. The rebels brought her up the processional stair with candles, as if candles meant anything to me, and Sera watched from the high gallery with her cold heir's eyes, doing sums. And Ada Halloran stood at the foot of my dais with soot on her sleeves and a stolen grimoire hugged to her chest like a shield, and she looked at me — really looked, the way no one had looked at me in decades, not with worship or terror or appetite, but with the plain horrified curiosity of one tired animal regarding another — and she asked:

"Are you the one who keeps the fire, or is the fire keeping you?"

I liked her immediately, which should have warned us both.

"Ask the better question," I said, and my voice made the gallery dust dance. "Ask why the fire needs keeping."

She didn't flinch. Hedge-witches' apprentices never flinch at riddles; they've been fed them since childhood along with their porridge. "Because the country's forgotten," she said. "And something has to remember it hard enough to keep it real."

"Closer." I let a curl of flame play along my jaw, mostly out of vanity. "Now ask who wrote the rules."

"The prophecy?"

"The prophecy."

She hesitated, and in the hesitation I saw the thing she had been carrying since Thornfell: the claustrophobia of a life pre-written, a route mapped before her feet set out, a mule purchased because ink said she would purchase it. I have read that fear on many faces across ten thousand dawns. It is the oldest fear in Vessany. We were all written before we were lived.

"Sit," I told her, because dragons do not beg, but we can offer chairs. There is a stone bench below my dais, worn smooth by a century of petitioners' backsides, and she sat on it like a girl on a well-curb rather than a messiah at court, and I decided — privately, in the ledger I keep behind my eyes — that I would tell her the truth. Someone had to. The truth in this country is like water in a drought year: hoarded, rationed, and finally more precious than any crown.

"You want to know about the prophecy," I said. "Very well. But understand first what you're asking. Every faith in this land rests on that book. Take it from them and half these people will lie down in the street and wait to gutter, because hope with its scaffolding removed collapses faster than a building."

"I'm not asking you to take anyone's hope," she said. "I'm asking what's true."

"Ah." I closed one ancient eye, the amber one, the one that still sees clearly. "You say that so simply. You say it the way your master Maren once said it, standing exactly where you sit."

Her whole body changed. I felt it in the air — the small storm a human makes when grief and fury braid together. "You knew Maren."

"I knew a young woman with burned fingers and a thief's conscience who stood in this hall thirty years ago and asked me the same question in the same voice." I let the smoke of my breath drift toward the vaulted dark. "And I lied to her. Or rather, I told her a smaller truth than the one she'd earned, because I was a coward, and because the country could not afford the larger one. Tonight I am dying faster than usual, and dying makes honest men of everyone. So. The truth, girl, entire."

* * *

Hear it as I gave it to her, and judge me as she judged me.

"The prophecy was written by my first keeper," I said. "The woman who named me Ossuary. You think of her as a prophetess, a seer, a saint with ink-stained fingers. She was none of those things. She was a woman watching her country die."

I told it plainly, because ornament is for priests. A hundred years ago, Vessany lost a war no one remembers — the victors saw to that — and the price of surrender was erasure. Not conquest. Conquest leaves ruins, ruins leave stories, stories keep a place half-alive. No: the treaty required that Vessany be *unwritten*. Maps redrawn. Records burned. Names forbidden. The victors understood something cruel and clever: a country is not its land. A country is its mention. Remove the mention and the land remains, but empty, and the emptiness spreads inward from the borders like rot through fruit.

"My keeper fled here with the survivors," I said. "A few thousand souls in a land that was being deleted around them. And she came to me — I was old even then, older than her kingdom, sleeping in the roots of the mountains — and she begged fire of me. Not to burn anything. To *hold* things. To be the memory the enemy couldn't confiscate."

"And you agreed."

"I agreed, and it nearly killed me, and I agreed anyway, because I am a fool with a generous throat. But fire alone wasn't enough. Fire can hold a shape, but shapes decay without meaning. The people needed a reason to keep lighting lamps in a country the world insisted did not exist. They needed tomorrow to have a face." I opened both eyes then and looked at her fully, letting her see the weariness pooled in them like sediment. "So my keeper sat down with ink and wrote them a tomorrow. She wrote a Bearer. She wrote a sigil. She wrote that a stranger would come over the broken border and relight the great fire and restore Vessany to the world. She invented hope, girl, the way smiths invent horseshoes. Practical. Necessary. And fitted to a living creature who would feel every nail."

Ada's knuckles had gone white on the grimoire. "She wrote *me*. A hundred years early. She wrote my name —"

"She wrote a *shape*, and the shape has waited ever since to be filled. Do you know how prophecies actually work? Not the way your masters teach. A prophecy is a hook baited with certainty, cast into the river of events. Most hooks catch nothing. But hers was anchored to my fire, and my fire reaches everywhere in Vessany, and so the hook caught whatever crossed the border carrying enough desperation to fit the shape." Smoke curled from my nostrils. "Maren fit. Thirty years ago, Maren fit, and my keeper's successor showed her the book, and Maren looked at those pages and saw herself written in century-old ink, and she ran. Just as you ran. Just as you were written to run."

The bench creaked under her grip. "Then it's a lie. The whole thing. The Sigil-Bearer, the sacrifice —"

"I did not say lie. I said *invention*. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Listen to the last of it, because this is the part I withheld from Maren, and withholding it broke something in her that thirty years has not repaired." I drew a long breath, and the hall's candles leaned toward me like a congregation. "Your death is not required by the fire. Hear me. The fire requires a keeper who chooses, freely, every day, forever. That is the true cost. Not one glorious sacrifice — a thousand ordinary mornings. My first keeper knew this, and she feared no one would pay it. Who volunteers for a lifetime? So she wrote the cheaper story instead: one Bearer, one flame, one death, salvation complete. She gave them a martyr because martyrs recruit, and lifetimes do not."

"And the variant text," Ada whispered. "The one Sera found. The omission."

"Sera found nothing I haven't known since the ink dried. Yes, the fire as currently built would consume its relighter — that flaw is real, a scar from the first lighting, when my keeper bound the flame too tightly to a single bearer's life. But flaws can be reworked. Bonds can be shared. The fire could take many keepers instead of one corpse, if anyone dared rewrite the binding." I let the silence sit a moment, heavy as gold. "No one has dared. Rewriting means admitting the scripture was written by a frightened woman with a pen, and Vessany would rather die on a beautiful lie than live on an honest correction. Your rebels kneel to you, girl. Your queen schemes around you. And every one of them would burn you gladly if the pages seemed to demand it, because the pages have become more real to them than the people."

Ada was quiet for a long time. In the quiet I listened to my own fire guttering in the outer ring, another stitch failing somewhere in the hem of the world, and I thought: *tell her about Copperfield Row. Tell her what the beautiful lie costs.* But I had spent my honesty for one evening. Even a dying god rations.

Instead she asked, softly: "Why tell me? Why now, when you lied to Maren?"

"Because Maren ran from the truth, and you," I said, "have not yet decided what you are. And because tonight I felt eleven people stop existing, and a bakehouse oven that burned faithfully for ninety years go out forever, and I am tired, girl. Tired of holding a country together with a story. Tired of being the only one who remembers the saint on Copperfield Row — her name was Halvene, she cured fevers with willow bark and stubbornness, write it down somewhere, *please* — and tired of watching hope function exactly like the chains it replaced." I lowered my great head until my breath stirred her hair, and the amber eye filled most of her vision, and I spoke gently, the way one speaks to the last warm thing in a cold room. "You asked if the fire keeps me or I keep the fire. Here is the answer neither your rebels nor your queen will give: soon it will be neither. Soon there will be no fire, and no me, and no Vessany — only the question my keeper tried to answer with ink. Whether a thing must be believed in to exist. She answered yes. I have spent a century hoping she was wrong, because if she was right, then freedom and survival are the same choice, and no one in this country has had the courage to make it."

Ada stood. She held the grimoire against her chest, and I saw her thumb move along its edge — not reverently. *Measuring* it. The way you measure a lock before you pick it, or a rope before you cut it.

"Ossuary," she said — my name spoken aloud for the first time by someone who wasn't praying to me or cursing me — "if I burned the book—"

"Then come back tomorrow," I said, "and bring the question again, and I will answer it differently, and mean it just as much. That is what freedom feels like, girl. It feels like having to choose again. Every day. Forever." I settled my chin back onto my foreclaws. "Most people prefer the chain. At least the chain tells you who you are."

She walked down the long hall toward the small human light of the doorway, and I watched her go, and behind her my fire guttered low in the veins of the city — and in the outer ring, in the dark where Copperfield Row used to be, something new moved. Not a ghost. Not a resident. A *draft*, cold and patient, flowing inward from the hem of the world toward the warm center, tasting the streets as it came.

The forgetting had noticed us noticing it.

And it was walking toward the Ember Court.
