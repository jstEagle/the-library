## 13. Sera's Bargain

The Ember Court kept its own hours, and none of them were hours Sera had chosen.

She woke — if what she did could be called waking — to the sound of the palace breathing: the slow tick of stone expanding in dragon-heat, the sigh of tapestries that had not been woven so much as remembered into being. Outside her window, the ninth district was still there this morning. That was worth noting. Two days ago it had been the eleventh. The city was being eaten from its edges inward, one street at a time, and every morning Sera performed the same arithmetic of survival and found the sum smaller.

She dressed without a lady-in-waiting because she had no ladies left who were not also soldiers, farmers, or ghosts. Her grandmother's gown fit her badly at the shoulders; Vessany's royal line had been dying of smallness for a hundred years, each generation a little less substantial than the last, until Sera herself sometimes felt she could pass a hand through her own reflection. She wore it anyway. A queen was mostly costume, her father had told her, and costume was mostly stubbornness. He had said it the year before he faded at his own coronation table, mid-toast, smiling, gone before the wine touched his lips.

In the antechamber, her steward waited with the day's ledger. The ledger was short. It was always short now.

"The outer ring is quiet," he said, which meant the outer ring was smaller. "Ossuary has not flown since the Guttering began. And the rebels request an audience for their Bearer."

*Their* Bearer. Sera noted the possessive and let it pass. "Send word. I'll receive her alone."

"Alone, Majesty?"

"Alone." She smoothed the gown over her hollow hips. "What I have to say to her should not be witnessed by anyone who might be tempted to call it treason."

He bowed and withdrew, and Sera stood a long moment in the antechamber, looking at the portrait of the first keeper of the flame — the woman who had written the prophecy, or commissioned it, or perhaps merely transcribed it from Ossuary's dying dictation. History had sanded her features smooth. That was convenient. It was easier to make bargains with the dead when you could put any face on them.

Sera had spent three nights with the variant text.

It lived in the sealed archive beneath the Ember Court, in a codex older than the grimoire the rebels venerated — the *first* reading, unedited, unsoftened, written in the hand of the keeper herself. The rebels' copy said: *When the fire fails, a Bearer shall come from beyond the border, and shall carry the flame anew.* The variant said: *and the flame shall take her, wholly, as fuel takes wood, and she shall not rise from the pyre of her own making.*

Wholly. Not rising. The authors of the popular text had cut those lines the way a butcher cuts gristle from a loin — necessary work, best done out of sight of the customer. Perhaps they had believed the omission merciful. Perhaps they had simply needed the prophecy to work, and no one walks willingly toward a pyre knowing they will not walk back out.

Sera had read the variant eleven times. On the third reading she had wept. On the seventh she had caught herself calculating — *if the girl dies on the solstice, the border holds through winter, and we have a generation to rebuild* — and had been so disgusted with herself that she'd vomited into the archive basin. On the eleventh reading she had understood something worse: that she would do it anyway. That love of country, at sufficient altitude, looked exactly like murder from below.

So she had decided on honesty. It was, she told herself, the only clean thing left to offer. She would tell Ada Halloran everything — the sacrifice, the omission, the choice — and let the girl decide like a person and not like a lamb. If Ada refused, Sera would grieve. If Ada consented, Vessany would live.

That was the story she told herself. She told it very carefully, the way one tells any story one intends to survive.

* * *

Ada arrived at the Ember Court flanked by Ivo and two of his father's men, and Sera watched from the dais as the guards were dismissed at the threshold — over Ivo's objection, over the rebels' murmured protest. Let them grumble. What passed between queens and their sacrifices should be conducted in private, even when the sacrifice did not yet know the word for herself.

Up close, the hedge-witch's runaway was younger than the prophecies required. That was the obscenity of it, Sera thought. Prophecies never specified age. They said *Bearer*, and everyone imagined someone tall, someone grave, someone with weather enough on her face to justify a death. They got instead a girl with ink stains on her fingers and a grimoire clutched to her chest like a shield she didn't trust.

"Majesty." Ada dropped into something between a curtsy and a stumble.

"You've been learning to hold a flame," Sera said. "Ossuary reports progress."

"He reports pain," Ada said. There was spirit in her; good. Spirit made the bargain harder and the consent more real, if consent came. "Your Majesty wanted to see me."

"I wanted to show you something." Sera descended the dais slowly, letting the silence stretch. She had learned statecraft in an empty court, performing ceremony for an audience of fading elders, and she knew the value of a pause. "Not here. Below."

The archive beneath the Ember Court was warm the way a hearth is warm an hour after the fire dies — heat without life. Sera lit no lamps; the walls themselves gave off a faint amber glow, stored sunlight from a century of summers that had happened somewhere else. She led Ada past shelves of codices to the iron case at the room's center, unlocked it with the signet she wore on a chain against her heart, and laid the first reading open on the reading stand.

"This is the prophecy your rebels know," Sera said, turning the grimoire's familiar verses toward her. "A Bearer comes from beyond the border. She carries the flame anew. Vessany returns to the world." She let Ada nod. "Now read what they cut out."

Ada bent over the older codex. Sera watched her face while she read, and it was a study in small disasters: the furrow, the blink, the moment the eyes stopped moving forward and began moving backward, rereading, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something survivable.

"*And the flame shall take her, wholly,*" Ada read aloud, her voice thinning, "*as fuel takes wood, and she shall not rise—*" She stopped. Her hand came up to her mouth.

"You understand," Sera said quietly, "why they cut it. No army can be built on that verse. No hope. Hope requires a shape with room in it for the hopeful."

"They knew." Ada's voice was almost too low to catch. "Whoever wrote my copy — whoever carried it across the border — they knew, and they hid it, and they sent me anyway." She laughed, a broken little sound. "Maren. My master. She must have known. Everything she ever taught me—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Sera let her grieve for exactly as long as grief took to become leverage, and hated herself for keeping the count.

"There is another thing you should know," she said. "The Guttering accelerates. My stewards measure the city each dawn. At the current rate, Vessany will not last the season. The outer districts go first — the people there don't die, they simply stop having ever been, and those of us who remember them lose the remembering a few days later. I have forgotten my aunt's face. I know I had an aunt. I know she sang. The song is gone." She stepped closer. "You are the only door out of that arithmetic, Ada Halloran."

"And if I refuse?" Ada's hands had dropped. Her chin came up, and Sera saw the defiance arrive, raw and necessary. "If I run? You said yourself the rebels would drag me to the fire if—"

"No one will drag you." Sera held her gaze and made the lie as beautiful as she could, because a lie offered gently was still kinder than truth delivered cruelly. "I am telling you the whole of it precisely so that no one ever drags you anywhere. Here is your choice, plainly stated. Stay, learn the flame, and on the solstice give yourself to it — and Vessany lives, all of it, every street and every soul, restored to the world by morning. Or refuse, and leave. The border still opens for you — the prophecy holds you, but it holds you *toward* the fire, not away from any road. You could walk out tonight. Back to your master. Back to a world where you are nobody's scripture." She paused. "And we gutter out behind you. Quietly. Completely. As though we had never been."

The silence afterward had weight, like water.

"That's not a choice," Ada whispered. "That's a knife with two edges."

"It's the only honest thing anyone has offered you since you crossed the border," Sera said. "Think of it however you must. But think of it *knowing*. The rebels knelt to you without telling you the price. Your master trained you without telling you the purpose. Even your dragon teaches you with jokes, I'm told, and never once says *by the way, the lessons end in ash*. I am the only one standing in front of you saying: this is what it costs, and here is the door, and it is open." She drew breath. "I tell myself that makes me better than the others. Perhaps it does. Perhaps it only makes me the one who kills you with courtesy."

Ada stared at her. Something moved behind her eyes — not hatred, which would have been simpler, and not gratitude either, but the terrible arithmetic of a person realizing that honesty can be a weapon too, that kindness can corner you more efficiently than cruelty ever could.

"You want me to choose it," Ada said. "Freely. Because a free choice binds the fire better. Doesn't it? That's why you're being honest. Not mercy. *Mechanics.*"

Sera did not answer immediately, and the not-answer was itself an answer.

"A willing flame burns longer," she said finally. "Yes."

* * *

Afterward, Sera walked the long gallery alone, past the portraits of kings whose names the world outside had already lost, and permitted herself, briefly, to feel like what she was.

Not a monster. Monsters never did sums. She was a queen doing sums, and the sum was a kingdom against a girl, and every ruler since the first ruler had done that sum and called the answer duty.

She had told herself: *honesty, not manipulation.* And she had been honest. Every word true. But she had chosen the hour of the telling — after the lessons, when the fire had already begun threading itself through Ada's memories, when the girl had already learned the city's forgotten names from a lamplighter's son with soft eyes. She had chosen the place — the archive, where the old codex sat heavy as a gravestone. She had chosen to dismiss the guards, so that Ada's tears would have no audience to harden in front of. Honesty, yes. Honesty deployed like artillery, at the coordinates where it would do the most damage to the enemy's resolve — except the enemy was a homesick apprentice, and the resolve being shattered was the resolve to live.

*I am not manipulating her,* Sera told the empty gallery. *I am giving her the truth.*

The gallery, being made of habit and heat, declined to argue.

At the gallery's end stood the window that faced the dying districts, and Sera stood before it as she did each evening, watching the horizon where streets used to be. Tonight there was a new absence — she could feel it, a smoothness in the skyline where a bell tower had been that morning. Somewhere in that vanished ring, someone had hung washing, argued with a spouse, taught a child the names of birds. The birds remained. The child was a rumor now.

The solstice was nineteen days away.

Nineteen days for the girl to make peace with the pyre. Nineteen days for Sera to keep telling her story — *honesty, not manipulation* — until the telling wore grooves deep enough to believe in.

Behind her, soft footsteps: her steward. "Majesty. Word from the rebel quarter. The lamplighter's son — Ivo — was seen leaving the Court at speed, heading for the border house. And the Bearer..." He hesitated. "The Bearer asked the guards which road leads out."

Sera closed her eyes. Part of her exulted — *she'll refuse, she'll run, and my hands stay clean* — and a larger part, a part she had no throne for, felt the city tilt another degree toward darkness.

"She asked which road?" Sera said.

"Yes, Majesty."

"Then she hasn't decided," Sera said, opening her eyes. "A girl who has decided doesn't ask. Find out where the roads go. All of them. And double the watch at the border house — quietly. If she runs, I want to know before her dust settles." She turned from the window. "We are not losing her to a doorway. Not when the fire is this close to fed."

She heard her own voice say it and understood, distantly, that somewhere along the way the honesty had ended and the trap had begun — and that the trapper and the trapped had changed places so gradually that neither of them had noticed the exact moment of exchange.

Nineteen days. Somewhere below the city, an old dragon breathed shallowly in the dark, waiting to learn whether his last meal would arrive walking or dragged.

Sera went to prepare the solstice rites.
