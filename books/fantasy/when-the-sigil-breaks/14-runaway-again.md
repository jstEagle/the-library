## 14. Runaway, Again

Ada had run away once before, and she remembered thinking it was the bravest thing she'd ever done — the stolen grimoire heavy in her satchel, the smugglers' road pale under the moon, the whole of her future folded up small enough to carry. She had been wrong about that. Running from Maren had been easy. You could run from a person. A person had edges.

The Ember Court did not.

She left at the hour when even habit slept — the dead hour Ossuary had named, between the last lamp's gutter and the first keeper's waking — with a knapsack of bread, a flask of lantern oil Ivo had pressed into her hands without explanation, and the grimoire against her ribs like a second heart. She told herself it wasn't running. She told herself it was reconnaissance, a survey of the border, a hedge-witch's due diligence before she made her decision. She was very good at telling herself things. Maren had taught her that much, if nothing else.

But her feet knew what her mouth refused to say. Her feet were running.

The city let her go the way a sleeping man lets go of a blanket — without resistance, without waking. Down the Ember Stair where the banisters were worn smooth by a century of hands that no longer existed anywhere but here. Through the Lamplighters' Row, where every window stood dark because dark was all Vessany had left to give its lamps. Past the fountain in Sorrow Square, dry these hundred years, its stone maiden still holding out her empty jug to a sky that had forgotten how to rain on this place.

And at the square's edge, where no one should have been, someone was.

"I counted your heartbeats from the wall," Ivo said. He was sitting on the fountain's rim as if he'd grown there, lamplighter's coat buttoned wrong, a shuttered lantern at his feet. "You breathe loud when you're frightened. Did you know that? Like a kettle deciding."

Ada's hand went to her chest, absurdly, as if she could quiet herself by pressure alone. "Ivo—"

"Don't." He stood. "Don't do the thing where you explain and I pretend to believe you. My father has six men watching the Ember Stair and you walked past two of them because they were watching for someone *sneaking*, and you weren't sneaking. You were leaving. There's a difference. Sneaking has a shape. Leaving just looks like a girl walking away."

"Then you understand why I have to."

"No," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "No, I understand why you think you have to. Those are different things too."

* * *

They went down through the city's bones — not the streets but beneath them, the old smugglers' roads, and this was the thing Ada could not stop noticing, the thing that kept snagging at her like a loose thread: the roads were *changing*. In Thornfell, the smugglers' ways had been real the way stones are real, indifferent to who walked them. Here they breathed. A stair that had existed when she entered Vessany now ended in raw wall, and Ivo simply turned left where there had been no left, and there was a corridor.

"The outer ring is going," he said, when she asked. "When the fire dips, the farthest streets forget themselves first. The smugglers' roads used to run out under the border — my father says they went all the way to Thornfell, once. Now they only exist inside." He glanced back at her. "Like everything else."

"And if the fire dies entirely?"

"Then Vessany becomes a story someone half-remembers. A name on the tip of the tongue. People wake up Outside with no country and no memory of having lost one." He said it lightly, the way you recite something learned young, and Ada understood suddenly that he'd been taught this prayer of despair since childhood, the way Outside children were taught their letters.

The tunnels smelled of cold iron and old smoke. Their footsteps came back to them doubled, then tripled, as though the passages were full of other runners — or as though the road itself were rehearsing them, trying on their escape the way it must have tried on a hundred others. Ada thought of the prophecy's margins, of all those dates in old ink, and wondered how many girls had walked this exact route before her, certain they were the first.

"You could come with me," she said, because the dark made her honest. "Through the door. There's a whole world out there, Ivo. Bread that isn't a hundred years stale. Rain. *Names.* Your city could remember its own names out loud again."

She heard him stop walking. In the blackness she felt him turn.

"My father would call that desertion. Sera would call it theft — stealing Vessany's last hope and walking off with her under your arm." A pause. "I'd call it the only offer anyone's made me since I was born."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest answer I have. Ask me again when we're standing in front of the door." His lantern hissed open, throwing a cone of amber light that bent strangely in the tunnel air, as if the dark were thick enough to swim through. "Come on. We're losing the road."

* * *

The border house sat in a fold of land where three hills met, and Ada knew it before she saw it — knew it the way you know a face in a dream, from the inside. Maren's marginalia had described it: *the house at the meeting of waters, door facing north, threshold worn hollow by those who crossed and those who wished they hadn't.* The hills around it were wrong in a way she couldn't fix with words. They had the quality of things remembered rather than seen, their outlines soft at the edges, their shadows falling in directions the moon didn't dictate.

The house itself was small, stone, ordinary — a toll-house or a watch-house, built by some kingdom that had since redrawn its maps. Its door faced north exactly as promised, and the wood of the threshold was indeed worn into a shallow bowl, polished by feet.

On the door hung an iron handle, green with age.

Ivo set his lantern down. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere above them, so faint it might have been imagination, the sky flickered — the dragon's fire dipping, the way it had dipped twice already tonight, each time a little longer, a little lower. Each time, Ada had felt it in her teeth.

"Do you want me to look away?" Ivo asked quietly.

"No." Her voice came out smaller than she meant. "Yes. I don't know. Stay."

He stayed.

She climbed the two worn steps. She put her hand on the handle, and the iron was warm — not sun-warm, not fire-warm, but warm the way a held hand is warm, and she understood, with a lurch, that the door knew her. That it had been waiting for her the way the grimoire had been waiting, the way the rebels had been waiting, the way everyone in this beautiful dying country had been waiting, patient as ink.

She turned the handle.

It didn't move.

Not locked — locked would have been kinder. Locked implies a key, a force outside yourself, something to pick or break or bribe. This was different. The handle turned a fraction and stopped, the way a word stops when the mouth goes dry, and Ada felt the refusal travel up her arm and settle behind her sternum like a swallowed stone. She pulled. She pushed. She braced her foot against the doorframe like a burglar, which was, after all, what she was, and hauled until her shoulder screamed.

Nothing.

"It opened for Maren," she whispered. "Thirty years ago it opened for Maren going out and coming back. It opened for smugglers. It opened for *anyone*—"

"Not anyone." Ivo hadn't moved from the bottom of the steps. He was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read — pity and terror braided together, and underneath both, something that might have been awe. "Ada. Look at your hand."

She looked. In the light of Ivo's lantern, across the back of her right hand — the hand on the handle — faint as breath on glass, a mark was showing. A sigil. The same sigil that burned on the grimoire's cover, on the Ember Court's oldest wall, on the banner Sera had unfurled at the relighting rite. It glowed softly, patiently, the way coals glow under ash, and when Ada snatched her hand back and pressed it against her coat, she could still feel it shining through the wool.

"No," she said. "No, no — I haven't accepted anything. I haven't taken the oath, I haven't touched the fire, I told Sera *no*—" 

"You ran," Ivo said softly.

"What?"

"You ran." He came up the steps slowly, and stopped one below her, so that they were nearly eye to eye. "That's the reading none of us saw. Not the coronation. Not the sacrifice. The prophecy doesn't say the Sigil-Bearer *reigns*. It says she *flees to the border and is turned back*. It's in the old cant, in the part Father Pellam never reads aloud because it sounds like failure." He recited it, halting, translating as he went:

"*She shall run from the crown, and the crown shall be glad; she shall run from the fire, and the fire shall be fed; she shall stand at the north door with her hand upon the iron, and the door shall know her, and keep her; for a Bearer who may leave is no Bearer at all, and Vessany cannot afford a hope with a back to it.*"

The words settled over Ada like snowfall. Cold. Weightless. Everywhere.

"So even this," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, as if someone else were speaking through her mouth. "Even running. Even sneaking out at the dead hour and dragging you through tunnels and putting my hand on this stupid, stubborn door — it's all just — the next line. I'm not escaping the story. I'm *performing* it. Every choice I make, I'm making it because the ink told me to a hundred years ago, and the ink is always right, and—"

She stopped. Because that wasn't quite true either, and the part of her that was Maren's apprentice — the part that read ledgers twice and checked sums against receipts — caught the discrepancy and worried at it like a dog with a bone.

"The ink is always right," she repeated slowly. "Because we believe it. Ossuary told me — no. No, he hasn't yet. But I can feel it, the shape of it. Maren's marginalia. The way the fire reads memories. Everything in this country runs on being *believed*, Ivo. The lamps. The roads. The border. The prophecy is just the biggest belief of all, and I've been feeding it every step I take, because every time I choose, I choose along its lines, because I half-believe it too—"

Her hand was shaking. On the back of it, the sigil pulsed gently, in time with her pulse, patient as tide.

"So stop," Ivo said.

Ada laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "Stop what?"

"Believe something else. Just for one minute. One minute where you're not the Sigil-Bearer, not the runaway, not the hedge-witch's thief, not any character in anybody's book. Stand at this door and be nobody, and see if the handle turns for nobody."

"And if it doesn't? If it turns out there's no 'nobody' under all of this? If the prophecy is just — what I am?"

"Then we'll know," Ivo said. "And knowing is worth one minute."

So Ada closed her eyes. She let go of the handle. And for sixty seconds — she counted them, the way Maren had taught her to count, one for each beat of her own unremarkable, unwritten, mortal heart — she believed nothing. Not the grimoire. Not the rebels kneeling. Not Sera's cold courtesy or Ossuary's weary irony or Maren's thirty years of penance. Not even the fear. She emptied herself out like a cup, and stood at the north door as Ada Halloran of Thornfell, who had once spilled ink on her master's best ledger and been forgiven, and who liked bread with honey, and whose mother's face she could no longer fully recall, and who wanted — more than she wanted to save anyone or fulfill anything — to go *home*.

She put her hand on the handle.

It turned easily, sweetly, the way a well-oiled lock turns, the way doors turn in dreams of home —

and stopped. Halfway. Open a crack. Cold Outside air threaded through the gap and touched her face, smelling of rain and pine and a world that had never heard of Vessany, and it was so close, so *achingly* close, close enough to taste —

and then the sigil flared on her hand, white-hot, and the door slammed shut hard enough to rattle the stones, and the boom of it rolled away over the remembering hills like a funeral bell.

Ada slid down against the door and sat on the threshold worn hollow by the crossed and the uncrossed, and she did not cry. She was past crying. What came instead was worse: clarity, arriving cold and clean as well-water.

Running was also a role. Flight was written as surely as sacrifice. The prophecy had a line for her fear and a margin-note for her desperation, and every exit was an entrance, and every road out was the road the ink had drawn. There was no direction in Vessany she could walk that wasn't toward the fire.

Unless—

Unless the ink needed her to believe. Unless the whole architecture of destiny rested, like the lamps and the roads and the border itself, on the load-bearing faith of the people inside it. The rebels believed. Sera believed. Ivo's father believed. Even Ossuary, perhaps, believed — a dying god keeping alive the story that gave his death meaning.

And a story believed by everyone could be starved by no one — or starved by *everyone*, all at once, if someone were brave enough, or foolish enough, to stop believing out loud.

"Ada." Ivo crouched beside her. "Your hand."

She looked. Under the fading glow of the sigil, the skin was unmarked — but the mark hadn't vanished. It had gone *inward*, sunk beneath the skin like a seed, and she could feel it there, germinating, patient.

"They'll have found my empty bed by now," she said. "Your father's men. Sera's riders. They'll be following us down the smugglers' roads, and the roads will let them, because the roads want this scene — the Bearer dragged back to the fire, willing or not." She looked up at him, and found that her eyes had gone dry and steady and strange. "Ivo. When they take me back — and they will take me back — I need you to do something for me."

"Anything."

"Get word to Ossuary. Tell him—" She searched for the words and found, to her surprise, that they had been waiting in her all along, in the blank pages at the grimoire's end where her handwriting dated tomorrow. "Tell him the Bearer has finally read the whole book. And tell him I'm done running — not because the prophecy says I'll fail, but because I've decided the prophecy doesn't get to say *anything* anymore."

Far off across the hills, torchlight bloomed — a dozen flames swaying in a long line, descending toward the border house, and at their head a figure in a lamplighter's coat that was not Ivo's.

His father had come himself.

Ivo stood up slowly, and Ada watched him make a choice — watched it happen in his shoulders, his jaw, the hand that closed around his lantern's handle — and whatever he chose, he chose it looking at her.

"They're going to ask whose side you're on," she whispered.

"I know," said Ivo. "That's the easy question."
