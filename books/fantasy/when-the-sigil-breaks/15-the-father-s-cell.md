## 15. The Father's Cell

The cell had been a wine cellar once, back when Vessany still had vineyards and someone to drink the wine. Ivo knew its every stone from childhood games of hide-and-seek, from the winter his father had locked him in for a night to teach him that fear was a tool like any other. He was eleven then. He had learned the lesson well enough to hate it.

Now there were two of them behind the door.

He could hear Ada moving — the small restless sounds of her, the scrape of her boot against the floor, the soft clink of the lantern she'd been allowed to keep because his father was not cruel, only certain. Certainty, Ivo had learned, could be crueler than cruelty. Cruelty at least admitted what it was.

"You're pacing," he said through the dark.

"I'm thinking."

"You're pacing *while* thinking. There's a difference. One of them wears grooves into the floor."

A pause, and then, quieter: "Your father is going to drag me up that mountain whether I say yes or not. Isn't he."

It wasn't a question, so Ivo didn't answer it. He answered it anyway, because she deserved better than silence. "Yes."

* * *

His father's name was Dannel Ferrow, and for as long as Ivo could remember, the name had meant two things at once: the man who taught him to read by lamplight, and the voice of the resistance, the one that stood on the steps of the old customs house every Founding Eve and recited the names of the streets they had lost. Ring by ring. Year by year. The outer districts went first, when Ivo was small — whole neighborhoods simply ceasing, their residents gone between one dawn and the next, not dead, just *unwritten*, the way a word is unwritten when the page burns. His father had lost his own mother that way. Ivo had never met her. She existed now only as a name in a recitation and a chair no one sat in.

So Ivo understood the arithmetic his father lived by. One girl against a city. One stranger's life weighed against ten thousand fading ones. It was not even a hard sum. Any child could do it.

What his father had never understood — what Ivo had spent twenty-three years learning in the spaces between his father's certainties — was that people were not sums. That Ada Halloran, hedge-witch's runaway, thief of grimoires, terrible liar and worse rider, was not a number to be carried up a mountain. She was a person who flinched at loud noises and laughed at her own bad jokes before finishing them and had once, in the lamplighter's loft, fallen asleep mid-sentence with her head on a stack of rebel pamphlets because she hadn't slept properly in four days and trusted him enough to do it.

You did not spend a person like coin. And if you tried, you found out too late that you were the one who'd gone bankrupt.

* * *

They came for him at the change of watch. Two men from the cell — Brannic, who had carried Ivo on his shoulders through the Lamplight Festival when Ivo was six, and a younger one whose name Ivo didn't know, which told him everything about how far the cell had rotted while he wasn't looking.

"Your father wants you upstairs," Brannic said. He wouldn't meet Ivo's eyes. "For the vote."

"There's a vote?"

"The cell votes on the Bearer question." Brannic's jaw worked. "Whether to move her tomorrow or wait for the fire to dip further."

"And if the vote says wait?"

Brannic finally looked at him, and there was an apology in it, old and tired. "Then your father overrules the vote. You know how this goes, Ivo. You've always known."

Upstairs, the customs house smelled of tallow and old paper, the way it had all his life. The map of Vessany covered the far wall — the great one, inked a century ago, with the lost districts shaded out in gray year after year until more than half the country was ghost-colored. His father stood beneath it with his hands clasped behind his back, and for a moment, looking at him, Ivo was six years old again, watching that broad back recite the dead streets, believing with his whole chest that his father could hold back the gray by sheer force of naming things.

"Dannel Ferrow," Ivo said, using the formal address, the one that put a hand's width of air between them.

"Ivo." His father turned. Up close, the gray had gotten into him too — at the temples, in the deep lines around his mouth. "You know why you're here."

"To watch me vote, and then to ignore me."

"To make sure you understand what happens next, so that when it's done, you don't spend the rest of your life telling yourself you weren't part of it." His father's voice was level, patient, the voice he used for teaching. It had always made Ivo feel handled. "The fire dips tonight. Ossuary is dying faster than the prophecies allowed. We have perhaps three days before the third ring goes — the market streets, the schoolhouse, the harbor quarter. Three days, and then thousands of our people stop existing, and you want me to wait on the conscience of a girl who stole her way into our country by accident?"

"She didn't come here by accident. She came here running from a prophecy, which means she knows better than any of us what it costs to be written into one."

"She came here," his father said, "because the prophecy said she would. Read your texts, boy. Even her flight serves the design. The Sigil-Bearer was always going to stand before the fire. The only question left is whether she stands there willing or unwilling — and I have decided that I cannot afford the luxury of waiting to find out which."

There it was. Plain, at last, stripped of all the ceremony and the kneeling and the sacred language. His father had decided. The cell would ratify it, or the cell would learn what its opinions were worth.

"And if she refuses?" Ivo asked. "If she fights?"

His father's face did something complicated and quickly hidden. Grief, maybe. Or its fossil. "Then we do what must be done, and afterward I will carry her name in the recitation with the rest. Every Founding Eve. For as long as I have breath."

"That's your plan for her. A place in a list of the dead."

"It is the only honest thing I can offer anyone anymore." And the terrible thing, the thing Ivo would turn over in his mind for years afterward, was that his father believed it. Believed it the way bedrock believes in weight.

* * *

The vote happened in the map room at dusk, forty-odd faces lit by lamps that burned oil brought in from Outside, smuggled through passes that existed only inside Vessany, which Ivo had always thought was the purest joke the border ever played on them. His father laid out the case in the flat voice of a man reading a ledger. The fire dips. The rings fall. The Bearer must reach the mountain. Motion carried or denied.

Ivo stood when it was time to speak, and his legs felt like borrowed things.

"I've known most of you since I was born," he began. "Brannic pulled me out of the canal when I was nine. Maretta taught me cipher. Half of you have eaten at my father's table, and I've eaten at yours, and everything I am, this cell built." He paused. Somewhere in the room, a lamp guttered, and forty people flinched — the flinch of a city that had learned what darkness cost. "So believe me when I tell you I am not doing this lightly, and I am not doing this because I've stopped loving any of you."

"Then say your piece, Ivo," his father said gently, "and sit down."

"I'm voting no. And if the vote carries anyway — if my father does what we all know he intends to do — then I am done." He let that land. Let the silence stretch until it creaked. "Not done with Vessany. Never that. Done with *this*. With deciding for other people what they're worth. My father wants to save the city by spending a girl who never agreed to be currency. And maybe it works. Maybe we get our sigil relit and our borders opened and our streets back, and maybe we all live to see Vessany on maps again. But we'll be a country that bought its resurrection with a murder, and I have read enough of the old texts to know what kind of fire runs on that fuel. It doesn't warm. It consumes. We will have traded one prison for another and called it freedom."

"The alternative," someone called from the back — Halvard, old Halvard, who had buried two children in the gray — "is that we all fade quietly while we debate ethics."

"The alternative," Ivo said, "is that we ask her. Truly ask. And accept her answer either way. Because if her answer is yes — if she chooses the fire freely — then it holds. The old keeper wrote that much plainly, and my father has read that passage a hundred times and cut it out of every copy he controls, because a free choice might be no, and he cannot live in a world where the answer might be no."

His father moved then, quick as a striking snake for a man his age, and took Ivo's wrist. Not violently. The way you hold a child crossing a street.

"Ivo," he said, low, just for the two of them, though the whole room leaned in. "She is one girl. They are ten thousand souls. I have carried this city on my back for thirty years and I am telling you, as the man who taught you to walk, that there is no version of me that lets it fade while I have hands to stop it. Come with me. Stand with me. When it's done, when the light comes back, you'll understand. You'll thank—"

"No," Ivo said. "I won't."

And he saw it hit — really hit, land somewhere behind his father's ribs where no argument had ever reached — and he hated himself for the satisfaction and felt the grief arrive right behind it, huge and cold, the grief of a man demolishing his own house because the foundation was rotten.

He turned to the room. "My father will take her up the mountain tomorrow regardless of this vote. Some of you will help him, because you're frightened, and fear is honest, and I don't scorn it. But I am asking — I am *begging* — anyone who has ever loved this city for what it was rather than for what it's about to become: don't lay hands on her. Don't be the ones who carry her. Let the gray take my name first, but don't be the hands."

Silence. Forty faces. In the gray-shaded map above them all, the lost districts watched like closed eyes.

His father released his wrist. Stepped back. And when he spoke again, his voice was the recitation voice, the Founding Eve voice, iron wrapped in sorrow:

"Ivo Ferrow is removed from the cell. Confine him with the Bearer."

* * *

The guard who walked him down was young Maretta's son, and he couldn't look at Ivo either. Nobody could look at anybody tonight. The door closed behind him with the sound of a book shutting.

Ada was sitting against the far wall with the lantern between her knees, and she looked up, and whatever was in his face made her go very still.

"They voted," she said.

"They voted. It doesn't matter. He'd already decided." Ivo slid down the wall beside her, close enough that the lantern light pooled around both of them, and heard himself laugh — a short, broken sound with nothing in it. "He's locking me in with you. As punishment. Thirty years of building this cell and he thinks putting me in a room with you is a punishment."

"What did you do?"

"I chose." He looked at her — really looked, the chapped lips, the ink stain on her thumb she'd never managed to scrub off, the stubborn terrified ordinary human shape of her — and said the truest thing he had ever said aloud: "I chose you over the cause. Over blood. Over the version of myself he raised. And I'd do it again in the morning, and that's the part that frightens me, Ada. Not the cell. Not him. That it wasn't hard."

She was quiet a long moment. Then: "Ivo. If they take me up that mountain—"

"They won't." He reached over and took her hand, ink-stained thumb and all. "Because I know these walls, and I know the watch rotations, and I know that Brannic still can't look at me, which means Brannic's conscience is awake, which means Brannic is ours. Tomorrow, when they come to move you, there are going to be exactly two guards on this door instead of four, and one of them is going to find something wrong with his keys."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we improvise. You're a hedge-witch. Improvising is the whole trade."

But even as he said it, even as her grip tightened on his hand, Ivo heard it — faint, through the stone, through the floor, rising from the direction of the mountain like a long slow exhalation at the end of a very long life.

Ossuary's fire, dipping.

And beneath it, closer, from the customs house above: his father's voice, giving orders, steady as bedrock, already counting the hours until dawn.
