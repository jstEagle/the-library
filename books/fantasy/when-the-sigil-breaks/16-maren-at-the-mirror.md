## 16. Maren at the Mirror

The mirror had been waiting for her longer than she had been willing to look into it.

Maren Halloran kept it in the back room of her cottage in Lowmarsh, under a sheet of grey linen that she washed every season and never once let anyone else touch. Thirty years, and still there were mornings when she stood before it with her hand on the cloth and could not make herself pull. Not because she feared what the glass would show — she knew precisely what it showed; she had chosen the glass for exactly what it showed — but because looking was a kind of knocking, and knocking meant admitting she was not yet ready to be answered.

This morning, the third of Frostwane, with sleet ticking against the shutters and the kettle muttering on the hob, she pulled the cloth away.

Vessany looked out at her.

Not Vessany as it was — she had stopped being able to bear that years ago — but Vessany as it now was: dimmed, thinned, its colors gone the grey-green of moss under ice. The mirror faced the old border house from the inside, propped where a window should have been, and through it Maren saw the great room she remembered young: the hearth big enough to roast an ox, the rafters hung with drying herbs, the long table scarred by a century of elbows. All of it still there. All of it *less*. The fire in the hearth burned without warmth. The herbs on their hooks had faded to suggestions of themselves. And the light — the light came from everywhere and nowhere, the way things looked in dreams, because the sun over Vessany had not risen in a hundred years and the dragon's ember did not distinguish morning from night.

She watched the room the way she always watched it: patiently, guiltily, like a woman reading letters addressed to someone else.

And then a girl walked into the frame.

Maren's breath went out of her all at once.

The girl was young — sixteen, seventeen, no older — with mud to the knee and a hedge-witch's satchel riding crooked across her back, and she moved through the border house the way trespassers move, quick and low, touching nothing, everything in her posture saying *I don't belong here and I know it.* She went straight to the hearth, crouched, and began going through the loose stones with a thief's practiced fingers.

Maren knew those fingers. She had taught them.

"Ada," she said, though the glass carried nothing either way. Her voice cracked down the middle of the name. "Oh, you foolish, brave, *stupid* child."

Because Maren knew what was hidden in those stones. She had put it there herself, thirty-one years ago, in another life, with hands that had not yet learned to shake: her notes, her maps, her confession written out in ink she'd mixed with her own blood because she'd believed, then, that contrition needed a bodily cost. Everything Ada would need to understand what Vessany was and who had helped unmake it. A trail of breadcrumbs leading home to a sin.

The girl found the cache. Lifted the packet free. Turned it over in her hands.

And then she looked up — straight at the mirror, straight through a hundred years and a failing border and all of Maren's careful distance — and their eyes met.

Maren did not move. Some animal part of her wanted to duck beneath the window's line, absurd as that was; the rest of her held still, because this was the moment she had rehearsed for three decades and rehearsal had never once survived contact with the real thing.

The girl stared. Maren stared back. She saw recognition arrive in stages — first the simple shock of a face in a dark glass, then the narrowing of attention, then something worse: the slow, dawning horror of a student recognizing her teacher's eyes in a stranger's frame. Ada raised one hand toward the surface. Maren raised hers to match, palm to palm across the impossible distance, and felt nothing but cold glass, and saw the girl feel nothing too, and understood that they were both pressing their hands against the same wall from opposite sides.

Then Ada said something — Maren read the shape of it rather than heard it, two words, bitten off — and snatched up her satchel and was gone from the frame, running, and Maren was alone again with a room full of fading furniture and her own reflection ghosted faintly over Vessany's dusk.

"*Coward*," she told herself, or perhaps the girl had said it. It fit them both.

* * *

That had been eleven days ago. Eleven days of watching the mirror go strange.

The strangeness came gradually, the way a fever comes, and Maren might have missed it if she had not spent thirty years learning the glass the way other scholars learned dead languages. First the edges of the view softened, as though breath were fogging it from the far side. Then whole corners of the border house simply stopped resolving — she would look at the doorframe and find her eye sliding off it, unable to say whether the wood was oak or ash or whether there was wood there at all. On the ninth day, the road outside the house flickered. There, then not there, then there again, like a candle guttering in wind only it could feel.

The border was closing. Not slowly, not on any schedule a sane person could plan around — but closing, the way a wound closes, the country drawing itself shut around its last heat. And somewhere inside it, a girl who had run away from her was running out of world.

Maren packed in an hour, because thirty years of preparation meant there was very little left to decide.

She took the grimoire's twin — no, not the twin; the *original*, the thing everyone else believed was legend, the book from which the famous grimoire had been copied by hands that thought they were making a fair copy and were instead making a cage. She took it wrapped in oilcloth, bound with three straps, and she did not open it, because she had opened it enough times for one life and knew every page the way you know a scar.

She took the counter-spell.

It lived in a lead box the size of a prayer book, and the box lived against her sternum on a leather cord, and she had worn it so long that she sometimes forgot it was there until it knocked against her collarbone when she bent — a small cold heartbeat, reminding her. Inside, folded eight times, was a single sheet of vellum covered in the handwriting of Aldous Vane, first keeper of the dragon Ossuary, the man who had written the prophecy in the first place — not as prophecy, Maren had learned too late, but as *instruction*. A script. A set of stage directions dressed up as fate, so that the people of Vessany would have a reason to keep tending a fire that asked, eventually, for a life.

And tucked into the same fold, in her own younger hand, the amendment she had stolen from Vane's private papers the last time she crossed: the working that unwrote the writing. The counter-spell. The sentence that could end a sentence.

She had stolen it at twenty-four, standing in this same border house with soot on her sleeves and Vane's ghost in every corner, and she had told herself she stole it to destroy it. That had been the first lie. Then she told herself she kept it to study it. The second lie. Then that she kept it because destroying it would be admitting what she'd done. The third lie, and after that she had stopped lying to herself about the lies and simply admitted the truth, which was that she was afraid — afraid of the spell, afraid of Vessany, afraid most of all of walking back through a door she had once walked out of carrying someone else's future in her pockets.

Thirty years was how long fear had taken to burn down to something she could carry.

* * *

The smugglers' road to the old border ran through country that remembered her.

That was the trouble with hedge-craft: it rooted you. Every mile of the Thornfell passes had a stone she had slept under, a spring she had drunk from, a farmer whose warts she had charmed away for the price of bread and gossip. She traveled hooded and slow, an old woman with a mule named Turnip who had opinions about bridges, and at each familiar place she felt the land take note of her passage — not hostilely, but the way an old dog lifts its head when someone crosses the yard. *You again,* the hills seemed to say. *Still coming back here.*

At the shrine above Wolf's Gully she stopped, because she always stopped, and found fresh offerings: a ribbon, a heel of bread, a stub of candle burned halfway down. Someone had passed recently. Someone heading the same direction.

She knelt, pretended to pray, and examined the ground instead. Boot prints, small, uneven — favoring the left foot, the mark of someone who'd twisted an ankle and kept walking anyway. And beside the boot prints, pressed deep, the round iron shoe of a mule.

"She bought a mule," Maren murmured. "Where did a runaway get coin for a mule?"

But she knew. She had paid the girl's wages herself, every quarter-day, into a jar on the high shelf that Ada had certainly believed was locked. Maren had left it unlocked on purpose. She had left a great many things unlocked on purpose, in those last years, arranging her apprentice's escape the way she'd once arranged a ritual — and hating herself for it, and doing it anyway, because the grimoire's pages had shown her the girl leaving and Maren had long ago learned that some currents you swim with even as you curse them.

Except that wasn't quite true either, and she made herself hold the truer version while the sleet came down: she had *wanted* the girl to go. Wanted it selfishly. Because the prophecies needed a Sigil-Bearer, and Maren had spent thirty years making sure that when the Bearer came, she would come already loved, already taught, already half-made by Maren's own hands — so that whatever happened at the fire, some part of Maren's penance would be standing there in the flame-light wearing a young woman's face.

"You used her," she said aloud to the shrine, to the rain, to whatever god handled old women's confessions. "You groomed a child for a pyre and called it apprenticeship."

The shrine said nothing. Shrines are honest that way.

She got up, wet to the waist, and led Turnip on.

* * *

The border house stood where it had always stood, which is to say it stood in a place that was not entirely anywhere.

From the outside it was a ruin — everyone outside said so; Maren had heard hunters and shepherds say it a hundred times, a burnt-out shell, nothing in it, don't waste your light. But the burning had happened on the far side of the glass, in the year Vessany closed, and the Outside had simply agreed to stop perceiving the interior, the way crowds agree not to see the beggar they've decided not to see. Maren pushed the door and it swung inward on hinges she had oiled every visit for thirty years, into a great room where the hearth-fire burned without warmth and the light came from everywhere and nowhere.

Inside, the air tasted of the past. That was the cruelest thing about Vessany's edge: it smelled like her twenties. Woodsmoke and beeswax and the particular green scent of the herb bundles she herself had hung from these rafters, back when hanging them had been part of the work — part of the *script* — and she had not yet known what the work was for.

The mirror waited where it always waited.

She did not need it anymore, but she looked anyway, one last time, the way you look back at a house you're leaving. Through the glass, the border country lay in its final dusk, and she could see now — with the sight of someone standing on the threshold itself — how bad it had become. The outer ring of the city was simply *gone*. Not ruined: gone. Where streets had been there was a smooth grey absence, like the space behind a painted backdrop, and her stomach turned over as she counted the gaps. Three streets. Five. The tannery district, which had housed four hundred souls when she was young, was a hole in the shape of itself.

And the road between here and the city flickered as she watched, there/not-there/there, a candle in a draft.

"Not much time," she said. Her voice sounded loud and wrong in the empty house. "All right. All right, old woman."

She did the things that had to be done. She fed Turnip and turned him loose with his lead tied high — he'd find his way to the Widow Cress's pasture within the day; she'd paid the widow to watch for him, years ago, against exactly this morning. She built a small fire in the border house's hearth, not for warmth but for witness, because the old crafts held that no crossing should be made from a cold room. She took out the lead box and opened it and read the counter-spell through once more, all of it, every clause, mouthing the words until they sat smooth in her mouth.

It was not a grand working. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she'd laughed alone in her kitchen a hundred times: the undoing of a nation's fate fit on one page and required no circle, no sacrifice, no dragon. Only a speaker who meant it. Vane had built the prophecy out of belief — belief harvested, refined, bound — and so belief was the only solvent. You had to stand inside the story and stop believing it, out loud, with your whole weight, and the story would tear along the seam of your doubt.

Which meant the hardest component was not the vellum. It was her.

Maren sat down on the floor of the border house, back against the cold hearthstone, an old woman in a wet coat with a book in her lap, and let herself remember — properly, without flinching — what she was walking in to undo.

She had been twenty-three. Brilliant, hungry, certain that the masters at the capital had overlooked her because she was poor and plain and from the fens. Aldous Vane had found her — or let himself be found; she suspected now it was the latter — and he had shown her Vessany, and the dragon, and the beautiful problem of a country whose fire was guttering, and he had wept real tears as he explained that the prophecy was mercy, that a chosen death gave meaning to a dying land, that hope was a medicine that required a dose. And she, twenty-three and flattered to be handed the pen, had helped him write it. Had drafted clauses. Had suggested, gods forgive her, the *wording* of the sacrifice — had argued that "willing" was stronger than "chosen," that the Bearer must walk to the fire unforced, and Vane had smiled at her and written it down.

Willing. Unforced. Free.

Every trap she had ever sprung on herself, seeded in that one adjective.

"I didn't know," she said to the empty room, and then, because the room deserved better: "No. I didn't want to know. There's a difference, and I've had thirty years to learn it, and I'm done pretending otherwise."

She got up. She took up the grimoire's original and the lead box and she slung her satchel across her back the way a much younger runaway wore hers, crooked, and she stood before the inner door — the true door, the one that existed only because a hundred years of belief had agreed it did — and found, when she reached for the handle, that her hand was shaking.

Not fear. Or not only fear. It was the shaking of a muscle asked to do the one thing it had been trained never to do: she had spent three decades keeping the border's secret, guarding the knowledge, curating the silence — and now every instinct of custody screamed at her to turn around, go home, keep the ledger balanced, wait for a better hour.

There was no better hour. There was only the guttering, and a girl on the wrong side of it, and a debt.

"Penance isn't feeling sorry," she said. Her mother had said that, in the fen dialect, over a mended net. "Penance is walking upright into the weather you made."

Maren Halloran opened the door.

The border took her the way deep water takes a diver — pressure first, then silence. For one endless moment she stood in the seam between agreeing and refusing, in the thin country where the Outside's disbelief pressed against Vessany's fading conviction, and she felt both of them testing her: the world outside asking *why are you going back*, the world inside asking *who dares come now*, and she answered neither. She walked.

Then grass was under her boots. Real grass, Vessany grass, silvered with light that came from no sky. The road stretched ahead of her toward the distant smudge of the city, flickering at its edges like a thing trying to remember itself, and the air smelled of woodsmoke and beeswax and her own lost youth, and somewhere ahead — a day's walk, if the road held; less, if it didn't — a girl was running out of world with Maren's confession in her satchel.

Maren settled the strap on her shoulder, touched the lead box at her sternum once, like a salute, and started walking.

Behind her, faint but distinct, the border door swung shut. When she turned to look, there was no door. There was no house. There was only the road, and the dusk, and the long grey holes where streets used to be — and, far off, moving fast along the flickering way, a small figure with a crooked satchel, and a second figure running to catch up with her, and between them, closing like a hand, the last mile of a country that had almost run out of time.

"Hold on, child," Maren said, and broke into a run. "Hold on. I'm coming — and I'm bringing the ending with me."
