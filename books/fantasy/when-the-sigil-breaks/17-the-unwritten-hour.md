## 17. The Unwritten Hour

The mirror-house smelled of dust and cold ash, and of something else beneath it — a scent Ada had not known she remembered until it filled her lungs: tallow, dried yarrow, the bitter green bite of feverfew. Her master's workroom. Her master's smell. It reached into her chest and squeezed.

Maren stood on the far side of the border door, and then, as the old woman pressed one weathered palm flat against the wood, she was on this side of it — stepping through a threshold that should have refused her, that had refused *Ada* only days ago, with the ease of someone returning to a house she'd never truly left.

She looked terrible. That was Ada's first thought, and her second, and her third, because there was no room in her for anything else. Maren had always been old in the way that mountains are old — permanent, unimpressed by time. Now she looked *spent*. Her hair, once iron-grey, had gone the white of paper left too long in sun. The hand that gripped her walking-staff trembled at the wrist. She had crossed thirty years of exile and a dying border to stand here, and every year of it was written on her face.

"You're smaller than I imagined," Maren said. Her voice was rough, like a hinge unused for decades. "In my head you were still eight years old, burning your fingers on the kettle."

Ada did not move from where she stood among the ruined shelves. Behind her, somewhere down the smugglers' road, Ivo was holding off his father's men — holding off them *for her* — and ahead of her stood the woman whose lies had shaped her whole life. She could not decide which direction hurt more.

"Thirty years," Ada said. "You were gone thirty years, and you told me you'd never been here. You told me Vessany was a story hedge-witches tell to frighten apprentices away from the deep passes."

"I told you what kept you safe." Maren lowered herself onto an upturned crate with the care of someone whose joints had stopped negotiating. "Sit down, child. What I have to say will want sitting for."

"I'm not your child."

"No," Maren agreed, and something in her face flickered — grief, or its ghost. "No, you never were. That was the point."

* * *

The border house had once been a customs station, Maren said. The last official building of Vessany, and the first unofficial one. Its shelves still held ledgers — customs manifests, toll records, the mundane arithmetic of a country that had believed itself permanent. Ada sat among them with her knees drawn up while Maren talked, and the talk went on a long time, because the truth, it turned out, had thirty years of sediment in it.

"The grimoire's first keeper was named Ellery Vance," Maren said. "She was Ossuary's keeper in the last years before the erasure — the woman who tended the dragon-fire when the borders were being drawn shut around us. And she wrote the prophecy. Not received it. *Wrote* it. With her own hand, in ink she mixed from soot and her own blood, because she knew that a prophecy written in blood is believed more deeply than any spoken promise."

"Why?" Ada's voice came out small. "Why would she do that?"

"Because Vessany was dying, and hope was the only medicine left. The country wasn't conquered, child. It was *forgotten* — unwritten from the world's memory, maps and minds alike. The dragon's fire held what remained together, but fire needs feeding, and belief is the only food that crosses a closed border. So Ellery wrote a future in which Vessany was saved. A Sigil-Bearer who would come from Outside, rekindle the flame, restore the nation. She gave the people a reason to keep believing, and their belief kept the streets standing." Maren's hands folded over her staff. "It worked. For a hundred years, it worked. But a lie that keeps you alive is still a lie, and it grows teeth."

"And me? Where do I come in?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped, as though the walls might be listening. "I came here when I was younger than you are now. An apprentice myself, full of the same restless hunger I later saw in you. I found the grimoire. I read the prophecy. And I believed it — oh, I believed it with everything in me, the way only the young believe. I thought I was the Sigil-Bearer. I climbed to the dragon's hall. I held the flame." Her eyes, when they met Ada's, were terrible with honesty. "And then I learned what Ellery had hidden in the variant text. That the fire, relit by a single Bearer, requires that Bearer's death. The keeper becomes the fuel. I ran, Ada. I took the counter-spell from Ellery's papers and I ran, and I let the fire gutter rather than feed it myself, and I have spent thirty years telling myself that running was mercy — mine, and everyone else's."

The words landed in Ada like stones dropped down a well. She heard them fall and fall and never quite touch bottom.

"So the sacrifice is real," she whispered. "Sera wasn't lying."

"Sera wasn't lying. But she wasn't right, either." Maren leaned forward. "Listen to me now, because this is the part that matters, and I am old and tired and may not say it twice. Ellery's prophecy binds only those who believe it. That is its power and its prison. The rebels believe, so they kneel. Sera believes, so she schemes. The dragon believes, poor creature, because it has believed nothing else for a century. But belief is a door that swings both ways. If no one believes the prophecy — if the Sigil-Bearer herself stops believing — then the binding unravels. The fire does not need a death. It never needed a death. It needs a *keeper*. Someone who tends it freely, every day, forever. One person choosing, morning after morning, to feed a flame. Or—" and here Maren's voice caught, "—or the courage to let it go out. To let Vessany end gently instead of violently. Both are freedom, child. Only the prophecy pretends otherwise."

"That's why the door wouldn't open for me." Ada was thinking aloud now, the pieces turning over. "At the border. Ivo said the door refuses anyone the prophecy still holds. Because I believed in it. Even running, even hating it — I believed it was real, believed it had power over me—"

"You were fulfilling it even as you fled it. Running is also a role, if the script says you'll run." Maren smiled, and it was a broken, beautiful thing. "Ellery wrote you before you were born. Every step you took inside her ink felt like fate because you carried her book and her certainty with you. The page wouldn't burn because *you* believed it couldn't."

Ada pulled the grimoire from her satchel. It sat in her lap, heavy as a sleeping animal, its pages whispering with futures. Her thumb found the edge of the prophecy — the page that had named her route, her mule, tonight's date — and she felt the old claustrophobia rise in her throat, the terror of being written before being lived.

"If I stop believing," she said slowly, "the people lose their hope. The streets are already falling. Ivo's father wants to drag me to the fire whether I consent or not. Sera wants a martyr. And you're telling me the answer is to simply — stop? To unbelieve? Like blowing out a candle?"

"I'm telling you it's harder than dying." Maren's voice was gentle and merciless. "Anyone can die for a destiny. It's a fine way to go — the songs write themselves. But to stand before a dying god and say *no script, no sacrifice, no crown* — to offer the fire nothing but a free choice, and accept whatever it decides — that takes a courage I didn't have at your age. I chose the door instead. I chose thirty years of exile and a stolen spell and a child raised on lies, because freedom frightened me more than fate did."

The silence between them stretched. Somewhere beyond the walls, faint as a memory, Ada could hear the city — the outer ring groaning as its streets thinned, the sound a country makes when it is deciding whether to exist.

"I'm afraid," Ada said at last. It was not what she'd planned to say. "Everyone keeps handing me things — a destiny, a throne, a pyre — and they're all terrible, but at least they're *shaped*. At least I'd know what I was dying of. You're asking me to walk into the dragon's hall with empty hands and no idea what happens next. That's worse. That's so much worse."

"Yes," Maren said. "It is."

"That's it? *Yes, it is?*"

"What would you have me say? That freedom is comfortable?" Maren reached across the space between them and, for the first time in Ada's memory, touched her — two fingers against the back of her hand, light as ash settling. "Freedom is the terror of standing at a crossroads with no signposts. Fate at least holds your hand. But hear me, Ada Halloran: the fear you're feeling now, the one with no shape and no name — that fear is *yours*. It belongs to no prophecy, no queen, no rebel cell. It is the first thing you have ever felt that was entirely your own. Wear it like a crown, if you must wear anything at all."

Ada looked down at the grimoire. At the sigil pressed into its cover, worn smooth by a century of desperate thumbs. *The Sigil-Bearer.* She had hated the name and feared it and, in her worst, most exhausted moments, wanted it — wanted to be the girl in the story who mattered, whose suffering meant something.

That wanting, she understood suddenly, was the deepest hook of all.

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night by the light of a single candle Maren had carried across the border, hoarded like treasure. The counter-spell was not a spell, exactly — that was the first thing Ada learned. It was a *reading*. A way of seeing the prophecy's words as ink rather than iron; a discipline of attention that loosened belief the way rain loosens soil.

"It won't feel like anything," Maren warned, guiding Ada's finger along the first line. "Unbelieving is quiet. You'll expect trumpets."

The words swam. Resolved. Swam again. Ada read *a Bearer shall come from beyond the border* and thought, for the first time, not *that's me*, but *someone wrote that. Someone afraid, a hundred years ago, writing hope in blood because she had nothing else to give.* The sentence stayed a sentence. It did not reach for her. It lay on the page like any other dead thing humans leave behind.

Her breath came easier. Then harder. Then easier again. Grief, she discovered, lived inside belief too — she was mourning the girl in the story, the chosen one, the self she might have been. Letting her go felt like setting down a weight and losing a limb in the same motion.

Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges when the door burst open.

Ivo came through it like a thrown stone — bloodied at the temple, breathing hard, his lantern swinging wild shadows across the ruined shelves. His eyes found Ada and something in his face cracked open with relief before he mastered it.

"They're coming," he said. "My father, Sera's guard, half the rebel cell — they know where you are. They're coming to carry you to the fire, willing or not." He stopped, saw Maren, saw the open grimoire, saw — somehow, the way he always somehow did — the shape of what had happened in this room. "What did you do?"

"Nothing yet," Ada said. She closed the book. It weighed almost nothing now, or her arms had grown stronger; she couldn't tell which. "But I'm about to do the hardest thing anyone's ever asked of me."

She stood. Slung the satchel across her chest. Looked at Maren, who nodded once — not encouragement, exactly. Witness.

"The hall," Ada said to Ivo. "Take me to the dragon. And clear the way, because I'm done being carried anywhere."

Down the smugglers' road, torchlight was already moving — dozens of flames converging on the border house through streets that flickered like a candle in wind. Ada stepped out to meet them with an unwritten hour in her chest and no destiny at all, and found, to her astonishment, that her legs held.
