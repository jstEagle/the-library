## 18. What the Fire Asks

The Ember Court had not hosted a war council in ninety years, and it did so now the way a body remembers a fever — badly, and with every old scar aching.

Sera stood at the head of the long table beneath the sigil of her house, that broken circle of eight points that had once meant everything and now meant only habit. The dragon-heat came up through the floor as it always did, a warmth like a sleeping animal's breath, and the banners on the walls stirred in a wind that had no source. She had dressed for the occasion in her grandmother's regalia: the high collar of blackened silver, the mantle worked with ash-grey thread. It fit her poorly. Everything about this day fit her poorly.

She had told herself, all through the sleepless hours before dawn, that what she was about to do was honesty.

*I am giving them the truth,* she had said to the empty chamber, to the portraits of ancestors whose faces the mirror-glass had half-eaten. *Ivo's father would have hidden it forever. Ada deserved to know.* And it was even true, in the way a knife is true. The truth was simply that she had chosen the moment of telling as one chooses a battlefield — after the girl had learned what the fire asked of her, before she could run far enough to matter, when the rebels' love had already turned to expectation and expectation is heavier than chains.

Let the Sigil-Bearer die. Let Vessany live. Sera had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn smooth as river stones: one life against a nation, a nation against a century of dark. No honest ruler could hesitate.

No honest ruler, perhaps. But her hands hesitated anyway, resting on the table's edge, and she looked at them as though they belonged to someone weaker.

"Your Majesty." Captain Vell, commander of what remained of the palace guard — eleven soldiers and a great many uniforms kept in good repair out of stubbornness. He stood at parade rest beside the doors, and his face held the particular blankness of a man who has already decided he will obey whatever happens next and does not wish to think about it. "The rebel delegation is crossing the Ashen Square."

"How many?"

"Forty. Perhaps fifty. They've brought torches, though it's morning." A pause. "And weapons under their coats. We counted the bulges."

Sera almost smiled. Fifty rebels against eleven guardsmen, in a palace held together by habit and dragon-heat, over the question of whether to burn a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice alive. It would have been funny if the arithmetic hadn't been so grim. "And the other party?"

"Ivo Rennar's people. They came ahead. Twenty or so, no torches." Vell's jaw tightened. "They're standing between us and the square. Your Majesty, I don't — I'm not certain my soldiers will raise arms against Rennar's lot. Half of them have sisters in that crowd."

*Half of everyone has a sister in that crowd,* Sera thought. That was the trouble with a country small enough to be erased from maps: there were no strangers left in it. Every quarrel was a family quarrel. She had spent her whole reign-in-exile — reign-in-waiting, her loyalists called it — believing that when the moment of restoration came, Vessany would rise as one body. She had never imagined the rising would split along its own seams, crown against lamplighter's son, and both halves holding knives over the same frightened girl.

"Then we do not ask them to raise arms," Sera said. "We ask them to witness. Open the doors."

Vell stared at her. "Witness?"

"When the fire relights," Sera said, and heard her own voice go cold and ceremonial, the voice she had practiced into mirrors since she was nine years old, "every soul in Vessany should see it done. That is not a threat, Captain. That is an invitation. There is a difference, and I intend to spend everything I have making sure they can tell which one this is."

It cost her something to say it, because part of her — the part that had done the arithmetic at three in the morning — knew exactly how much force waited behind the invitation if the girl refused. But she let the doors open, and she folded her hands, and she waited.

* * *

They came into the Ember Court like two tides meeting in a harbor too small for either.

First Ivo's people, twenty-odd lamplighters and dock-hands and one ancient fishwife Sera recognized from the outer ring, walking shoulder to shoulder with the deliberate calm of people who had decided to be unmoveable. Then the rebel column from the square, forty strong, torches still lit and guttering in the dragon-warm air, and at their head Tam Rennar himself, Ivo's father, broad as a door and wearing his authority the way other men wore armor. His eyes found Sera's across the length of the court and held them, and what passed between them was not hatred. It was worse. It was recognition. Two people who wanted the same thing by different roads, each certain the other's road led off a cliff.

And then, between the two columns, walking as though she were going to market, came Ada Halloran.

Unarmed. Alone. No escort, no banner, not even the grimoire that had named her. Just a girl in a road-stained coat with straw still in her hair from wherever she'd slept, walking down the center line between fifty drawn faces as though the space between armies were the safest place in the world.

Sera felt the whole room lean toward the girl without meaning to. She felt herself leaning too.

Ada stopped perhaps ten paces from the dais and looked up at Sera, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and she said nothing. She simply stood there, in the exact place where a supplicant would kneel, and did not kneel.

"You know why you've been summoned," Sera said.

"I know what you told me." Ada's voice was hoarse but clear; the acoustics of the Ember Court carried it to the last row, to the fishwife and the captain and every soldier with a sister in the crowd. "That the fire needs a death. That the prophecy says the Bearer burns when the flame relights. You told me that in your private chamber, alone, where no one could hear you say it." A breath. "So I've come to say it here, where everyone can."

Murmurs ran the columns like wind through wheat. Tam Rennar's face went carefully blank, which was how Sera knew he had not known she'd told Ada privately — or had suspected and hoped otherwise.

"That was mercy," Sera said.

"That was strategy." No anger in it. That was the terrible thing, Sera thought; the girl wasn't angry, she was *accurate*. "You gave me a choice between dying and running, and you told yourself it was honesty. Maybe it was. But honesty given to one person in a locked room isn't truth. It's leverage." Ada turned, slowly, so that she faced the whole court, the whole divided city, her back to the throne. "So here is the truth, for everyone. My name is Ada Halloran. I am a hedge-witch's apprentice. I stole a book and ran away, and a country I'd never heard of until last month decided I was its salvation. I never asked for this. I never believed in it. And I am not going to burn for it."

"So you'll let us all fade instead?" Someone shouted it from the rebel ranks — not Rennar; one of the dock-men, his torch shaking. "You'll stand there while the streets go out street by street? While our children stop remembering there ever was an outside?"

"No," Ada said. "I'm going to walk into the dragon's hall and ask the fire what it actually wants. Not what a book written a hundred years ago says it wants. What it wants." She lifted her chin. "And any of you who want to come and watch are welcome. All of you. Both sides. Bring your torches if they make you feel brave. But nobody brings a weapon past me, and nobody forces anybody. If the answer is my death, I'll hear it myself, on my feet. If it isn't—" her voice cracked, just slightly, the first human fracture in all that steadiness, "—then we've all been ready to murder a girl over a misunderstanding, and I think we should sit with that for a while."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears.

Sera sat very still on her ancestor's throne and understood, with a clarity like cold water, that she had lost. Not to Ivo, not to Rennar, not to any faction. She had lost to a girl who had walked unarmed between two armed crowds and made the walking itself the argument. Every plan Sera had built — the summons, the private confession, the arithmetic of sacrifice — lay in pieces around the simple fact of Ada Halloran standing in the middle of the floor, refusing to be either martyr or fugitive, insisting on being *witnessed*.

Tam Rennar recovered first, because he was a man built for recovery. "The Sigil-Bearer speaks bravely," he called out, "but bravery isn't—"

"She's right," Ivo said.

He had been standing at the head of his twenty, and Sera realized she had not even seen him arrive; he was a thin young man with lamp-oil stains on his cuffs, easy to overlook, which was evidently the family talent. Now he stepped forward into the open ground between the columns, beside Ada, facing his father across ten feet of polished stone.

"The fire doesn't need a ceremony, Da. It needs a keeper. It's needed a keeper for a hundred years and we gave it a book instead of a person." His voice shook. Sera watched him master it. "If Ada walks into that hall, I walk with her. Unarmed. Anyone who wants to draw steel will have to do it past a lamplighter's son, and I'll thank them to remember I've mended every lamp on their streets."

"And if the fire takes her?" Rennar's voice was low and dangerous. "If the old words hold? Will you stand in the flames beside her, boy? Will your twenty?"

"If the old words hold," Ivo said, "then the old words are wrong, and someone should be standing there to say so."

For a heartbeat Sera thought her court would break into open violence. She could see it gathering — the dock-man's knuckles whitening on his torch, Vell's hand drifting toward his sword, the fishwife planting herself like a stump against the tide. One shout, one shove, and Vessany's restoration would begin as a massacre in its own throne room, brother against brother in front of the dying god, and whatever fire rose afterward would rise over corpses.

Then Ada moved. She didn't speak. She didn't raise a hand. She simply took one step further toward the inner doors — the doors to Ossuary's hall, the deep doors, the ones that breathed heat — and stopped, and waited, looking back over her shoulder at all of them with an expression that was not defiance and not pleading but something more unbearable than either: patience. The patience of someone who has decided that whatever the people she loves choose to do next, she will watch them do it, and remember.

And the violence, finding nowhere to land, broke apart like foam.

It was the fishwife who moved first — old Marta Ostrow, whom Sera had last seen at her father's funeral thirty years ago. She shuffled forward out of the rebel column, leaned her torch against the wall with great deliberation, and took her place behind Ada. Then a lamplighter. Then one of Vell's own guardsmen, unbuckling his sword-belt as he crossed the line. Then another. The crowd dissolved and reformed the way water finds a new channel: not ordered, not commanded, simply deciding, one stubborn body at a time, that they would rather follow the girl on foot than fight for her at a distance.

Rennar stood alone in a thinning crowd, his forty reduced to a dozen, his face working through fury and grief and something that might have been the first crack of doubt. He looked at his son. His son looked back. Neither of them spoke, and Sera understood that the worst wound in the room was that silence.

"Captain Vell." Her voice surprised her by being steady. "Open the deep doors. All of them. And let anyone who wishes to enter do so — disarmed, in peace, as witnesses."

Vell saluted. Around him, his eleven soldiers became nine, then seven, as they too chose sides — not hers, not Rennar's, but the girl's.

Sera rose from the throne of a country that had erased itself rather than see this day, descended the dais, and walked — mantle dragging, collar heavy, every eye upon her — to stand before Ada Halloran.

"You understand what you're doing," Sera said quietly. Only for her. "If the fire refuses you, there may be no second chance. If it accepts you—"

"Then we'll finally know what it accepts me *as*," Ada said. "Not what a book decided. Not what a queen calculated. What the fire asks." She glanced at Sera's regalia, at the mantle stitched with ash. "You could take it off, you know. Come as a person. Everyone else did."

Sera looked down at her grandmother's mantle, at the eight-pointed sigil gleaming dull silver over her heart, and felt the full weight of everything it had cost her to wear it into this room. For one long moment she genuinely considered it — shedding the mantle, the title, the arithmetic, walking into the dragon's hall as nothing but a woman named Sera who was afraid.

But the throne behind her was empty now, and empty thrones invite worse things than proud queens, and someone had to remain to hold the shape of the law while the rest of them gambled with miracles.

"No," she said. "Someone must be queen today, in case there's a country left tomorrow. Go. I'll follow."

Ada studied her for a moment — and Sera had the uncomfortable sensation of being read as easily as a ledger — then nodded once, and turned, and walked toward the deep doors, and the people of Vessany parted before her and closed behind her like a tide closing over a stone, and Sera stood in her regalia at the mouth of the dragon's hall and watched the girl she had prepared for slaughter walk unarmed into the dark, carrying nothing but her own stubborn, unwritten self.

The deep doors swung wide. Heat rolled out, ancient and enormous, and somewhere in the dark below, something vast drew a breath that had taken a century to finish.

Then the light changed.

* * *

It began as a flicker at the edge of vision, gold threading the dark of the descending stair, and Sera followed the crowd down into Ossuary's hall with her hand pressed flat against the wall to keep her balance, because the floor was trembling — not with earthquake but with attention, the whole palace leaning inward like a listener.

The hall opened around them, vast as a cathedral hollowed from a mountain's heart, and at its center coiled the dragon: immense, tarnished, wings folded like a ruined sail, scales gone grey as old ash. Ossuary's single eye, large as a carriage wheel, tracked Ada as she walked the last stretch of floor alone, the crowd halted behind her at some invisible boundary none of them had agreed upon.

And above the dragon, where the great brazier of the sigil-fire had burned for a thousand years and guttered to a coal the size of a fist, the light was *moving* — pulsing, quickening, flaring in rhythm like a heartbeat remembering itself.

Ada stopped before the dying god. She did not kneel. She tipped her head back to look up into that ancient eye, and though the hall was huge and her voice small, every word reached every ear as though the walls themselves had chosen to carry it.

"All right," Ada Halloran said to the last dragon in the world. "I'm here. Nobody's forcing me, nobody's sacrificing me. So tell me the truth. What does the fire actually ask?"

The coal above the dragon flared white.

And Ossuary opened her jaws, and spoke — not in the thunder Sera had braced for, but in a voice like banked embers settling, old and tired and, unmistakably, amused:

"At last. Someone asks."
