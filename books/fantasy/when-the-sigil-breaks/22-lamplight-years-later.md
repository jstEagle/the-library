## 22. Lamplight, Years Later

The lamp at the corner of Ash Row and the Street of Nine Names was the first one Ivo had ever lit, and it had become, without anyone deciding it, the most important lamp in Vessany.

Not the largest — that honor belonged to the great lanterns of the Ember Court, which Sera's stewards trimmed every evening with ceremony and silver poles. Not the brightest — the harbor beacons outshone it tenfold since the fire had spread from Ossuary's hall into a hundred thousand wicks across the city. But when people spoke of *the first lamp*, they meant this one: squat and iron-bellied, mounted on a post that leaned two degrees east because Ivo's father had installed it crooked in another century, on a street that had then been called something else entirely.

Every dusk, Ivo climbed the three worn steps to its base with his brass ladder-hook, his oil can, and his cloth, and he performed the small liturgy of keeping. He wiped the soot from the glass. He checked the wick for fraying. He poured the oil — real oil now, pressed from rapeseed grown in fields that had spent a hundred years as meadow-scrub and were farmland again — and only at the very last did he touch the flame-tongue of his taper to the wick and watch the light take hold like a held breath released.

Six years of evenings. Two thousand dusks, give or take. He had never missed one, not even the week he'd spent abed with marsh-fever, when his cousin Tansy had climbed these same steps grumbling and swearing she'd tell no one that the Lamplighter's Daughter — that was what they called him now, though he was thirty and his father had been dead four years — had needed someone else to do his work.

*Hope as a daily practice.* That was what Ada had said, once, standing in this exact spot with her hand flat against the cold iron of the lamp's belly. *Not a prophecy. A practice. You don't believe your way into a future. You tend your way into it.*

He thought of her most evenings. Not with the raw ache of the early years — that had worn down into something smoother, a stone carried in the pocket until it went round — but steadily, the way the lamp burned. She was there whenever he struck the taper. It was impossible that she shouldn't be.

Tonight the autumn came early off the Thornfells, and the wind smelled of woodsmoke and coming rain. Ivo lit the lamp, watched the flame steady itself against the glass, and was turning to go when he saw the figure waiting at the bottom of the steps: a boy of perhaps eleven, soaked to the knees, holding a satchel above his head as if it might bite him.

"You're the lamplighter," the boy said. "The one on the smugglers' post."

"I'm the one."

"Then you've got mail." The boy thrust the satchel forward. "From Outside. Old Ferren says it goes to you or nobody."

Ivo took it with both hands. Smuggler's post arrived in Vessany perhaps four times a year, riding the roads that existed only because Ada had stopped believing in her own destiny hard enough to tear a door in the world — roads that now ran both ways, thin and uncertain and utterly unregulated, carrying letters and seed catalogues and once an entire piano disassembled into nine crates. Most of it went to the trading house by the border house. Almost none of it came addressed to him.

But he knew the hand on the wrapper before he read a single word. The letters leaned right, urgent, as though their writer feared the sentence would end before she finished thinking it. The ink had smudged where rain had found it somewhere in the passes.

He stood beneath his own lamp like a fool and broke the seal with his thumb.

---

*Ivo —*

*The ember still burns. I know you'll want that sentence first, before any of the rest, so there it is. Six years and one season of terrible storms and one incident involving a goat that Ferren will have told you about by now if he tells true, and the thing sits in its lantern on my mantel glowing like a cat that got the cream. Maren says embers from Vessany are stubborn. She says most good things are.*

*The book is filling.*

*That's the second thing, and I've started this letter three times trying to decide how to say it, so I'll just say it badly. You remember the grimoire. You remember the last pages blank — how after I broke the sigil the whole back half of the book went empty, not blank like unwritten but blank like unfallen snow. For years nothing. I kept it shut mostly. It seemed rude, somehow, to stare at all that possibility.*

*Then this spring I was at the kitchen table arguing with Maren about whether hedge-witches owe taxes (we do; she maintains we don't), and I looked down and there was writing on page two hundred and forty-one. My handwriting. Words I hadn't written yet — no. No, that's the wrong way to say it, that's the old way, the way the grimoire used to work, prophecy sliding backwards through time to write you before you lived. This is different. This is the opposite of different.*

*I wrote it. That morning. I wrote it that morning and forgot, and it turned up in the book as if the book were simply a place I keep things now. A record. Nothing more. Page 241 says: "Maren insists the eastern villages pay a tithe in charms against bog-fever. Argued until dark. She makes the best plum brandy in three counties and uses it as a diplomatic weapon." That happened. That's all it does — happen, and then get written, in the order it happens, by the only person who was there.*

*A ledger. The grimoire has become a ledger. All those centuries of telling people who they were going to be, and now it just writes down what they did.*

*I laughed until I cried, Ivo. Then I cried until I laughed, which Maren says is the correct order for a witch and the wrong order for everyone else.*

*There's more room in it than one life. I've noticed that. Pages turn up describing things I never saw — a wedding in the Street of Nine Names, a new lamp lit somewhere I couldn't read clearly, a woman teaching children letters in a courtyard full of white blossom. I think it's yours. I think it's ours. I think when the fire took the city instead of a sacrifice, it took the book too, and made it wide enough to hold whatever we choose to put in it. So I'm putting this letter in, in advance, so to speak. Consider yourself recorded.*

*Tell Sera the trade terms are unacceptable and also accepted, because she'll know what that means. Tell Tansy her fever-charms sold out in a fortnight and she owes me a cut. Tell Ossuary — tell Ossuary I said hello, and that I saw a dragon-shaped cloud over the sea last month and didn't cry, much.*

*And tell yourself, from me: the lamp on Ash Row is still the first one. Everything else grew out of that. I want you to know that I know it.*

*I'll come through the border at midwinter, if the roads hold. I always mean to come more often and I never do, and I've stopped apologizing for it, because living a life takes time and mine keeps being full. But midwinter. Save me a walk along the harbor wall. Wear the grey coat.*

*Yours across the threshold, always,*

*Ada*

---

Ivo read the letter twice under the lamp, and a third time walking home, and a fourth time sitting on his bed with the shutter open so the light from Ash Row fell across the page. Outside, the evening shift of lamplighters passed his window in a loose procession of tapers, each one pausing at each post, each small flame caught and handed onward. He could hear old Wenna singing to herself two streets over, badly, the way she always did. Somewhere near the harbor a dog was barking at gulls, and the gulls were winning.

A wedding in the Street of Nine Names. He smiled at that. There had been one — Ferren's daughter, last spring, marigolds everywhere, and he had danced with Sera of all people, the queen counting steps under her breath like a girl, and neither of them had mentioned the Ember Court or bargains or the price of thrones even once.

A woman teaching letters in a courtyard of white blossom. That was Tansy, surely. Or maybe it hadn't happened yet. That was the strange freedom of the new book — he couldn't tell memory from future, and it didn't matter, because either way it wasn't written *at* anyone. It was just written *down*, afterward, by the world, patiently, in the only direction time allowed.

His father would not have understood it. His father had wanted a country you could grip, a prophecy you could steer, a Sigil-Bearer you could hold by the wrist while history poured through her. His father had died still believing the fire should have had one keeper, one throat, one name — and Ivo had spent four years learning to forgive him for it, which was its own nightly practice, harder than lamps. But even his father, Ivo thought, would have understood the ledger. His father had kept ledgers all his life. He had simply believed the ledger should come first, and the living after.

Ada had turned it around. Turned everything around. You lived, and then the book agreed with you.

He got up, found pen and ink, and sat at the table by the window. On the paper he wrote:

*Midwinter. The coat is ready. The harbor wall hasn't moved. Come.*

He considered it, then added, because it was true and because truth deserved recording even when it was inconvenient:

*I light your lamp first every evening. Everyone thinks it's for the city. It isn't. It never was.*

He sealed it, addressed it to the smuggler's post at Thornfell, and set it on the sill for morning.

Then he took up his oil can and his taper and went back out into the autumn dark, down the lane, past Wenna singing and the gulls and the dog, to the corner of Ash Row and the Street of Nine Names, where the first lamp in Vessany was burning exactly as he'd left it. He stood a moment with his hand on the warm iron, feeling the small stubborn heat of it, six years deep and showing no sign of stopping.

Then he walked on to the next post, and the next, lighting them one at a time, in order, the long way home.
