## 1. The Ledger Opens Both Ways

The cellar beneath the Guildhall of Impossible Things smelled of tallow smoke and old bone, which was to say it smelled like home, if home were something Felix Voss had ever been permitted to keep. Three lamps burned low in their niches—tallow, never oil; the dead distrusted anything that burned too clean—and their light pooled amber across a workbench scarred by forty years of knife-work that had not all been Felix's own.

Felix did not know whose hands had worn those grooves before theirs. That was one of the many things they did not know about themselves, and had learned, through long practice, not to mind before breakfast.

The ledger lay open on the bench's clear half: *Accounts Receivable, Quarter of the Drowned Moon*. The guild's books balanced two ways at once—the living column and the dead column—and keeping them both true required a steady hand, a colder eye than most possessed, and the ability to hear what most people were mercifully spared.

Felix dipped a pen and made the morning's first notation in ink mixed with grave-dirt. *Widow Anselm, paid in full. Debt discharged: three secrets owed to her drowned husband, delivered over seven nights. Token to be returned.*

Then they took up the bone token that represented the widow's debt—a small oval of sheep femur, drilled with a single hole, carved with the sigil of the guild—and set it against the whetstone wheel. It would be ground smooth and blank again, ready for whatever impossible thing came next. That was the whole architecture of the trade, elegant as a pulley: when a client wanted something impossible done, they received a token of carved bone. When the debt was paid, the token came back and was erased. The guild's ledgers never closed so much as turned over, page after page, year after year, like a body rolling in its sleep.

Felix had been balancing these accounts since they were nineteen years old and had woken in the guildhall infirmary with no memory at all—not even their name, which Odile had supplied from a slip of paper pinned to their coat. *Felix Voss, bone-carver.* The handwriting on that slip had unsettled Felix for years, until they'd learned to stop reading it, the way you stop touching a bruise.

They had other talents besides bookkeeping. That was the trouble.

The knife in their right hand moved without instruction, turning a fresh token of calf-bone into the shape of a debt not yet incurred: a coiled serpent, because the client coming tonight would be paying in something that bit. Felix watched their own fingers work with the mild detachment of a man watching weather. The cuts were clean, confident, economical—better than Felix could do if they thought about it. So they didn't think about it. They hummed instead, an old saltmarsh air with words they'd never learned, and let the hands remember what the head could not.

Above, through the floorboards, the guildhall woke. Boots crossed the common room; someone cursed the coffee; the day-clerk, Tam, began his ritual argument with the stove. Felix tracked it all the way a sailor tracks rigging sounds—with the ear, but also with something else. The building had its own pulse. And threaded through that pulse, faint as a hair caught in a door, were the others.

The bound ghosts. Nine of them, though the ledger said seven, which was itself an entry awaiting reconciliation. They lived in the guildhall's walls the way heat lives in brick, drawn there over a century by the guild's founding covenant—the Sigil, the great binding worked into the foundation stones, the thing every apprentice learned about in one dry lecture and then never thought of again. The ghosts kept mostly quiet. They murmured. Sometimes, when the wards thinned on fog-nights, they sang.

Tonight was a fog-night. Felix could feel it in their teeth.

* * *

The knock came at dusk, exactly as arranged, and Felix climbed the stairs to receive the client in the receiving room, because clients were never brought down to the cellar. What happened down there was guild business, and guild business stayed buried.

Tam admitted the visitor: a broad man in a fish-oil slicker, salt-crusted to the elbows, with the particular gray complexion of someone who had recently stood very close to something he should not have seen. He introduced himself as Corben Hale, master of the oyster dredge *Patient Ann*, and he laid his problem on the table between them like a fish he wanted gone.

"My brother," he said. "Drownded off the Shallows two springs back. Body never found." He wet his lips. "He's been talking to me."

"Through what medium?" Felix asked, in the tone of a clerk confirming a delivery address.

"The shells." Hale reached into his slicker and produced a handful of oyster shells, which he spilled onto the table with a clatter. "Every shell I dredge up near where he went down. I put 'em to my ear like a girl at a fair, and there he is. Talking. Won't stop talking."

"What does he say?"

Hale's jaw worked. "That he wants to come home. That he's cold. That—" the man's voice cracked and he hated himself for it, visibly— "that it's my fault. I cut his line. There was a squall, and I cut his line to save the boat, and I've told myself a hundred times it was the only thing to do, and he tells me otherwise, every night, otherwise."

Felix studied the shells. Ordinary to the eye—pearl-gray, ridged, unremarkable. But Felix did not deal in eyes alone.

They picked up one shell and held it to their own ear, more out of professional thoroughness than expectation.

A voice poured through, thin as thread pulled through a needle's eye, and it stopped Felix's heart mid-beat, because Felix knew that voice. Not the brother's voice. A voice from the far side of their own missing years—a voice that had sung to them across the dark of wherever they had been before the guildhall infirmary, before the slip of paper, before *Felix Voss*. It hummed the same saltmarsh air Felix hummed at the workbench, the one with the words they'd never learned, and now here it was, rising out of a stranger's grief like a face surfacing under ice.

"—and the tide takes back what the tide is given, and given, and given—"

Felix set the shell down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might be a trap.

"Your brother's voice," they said, and were relieved to find their own voice level. "Is distinct. I can work with this."

Hale exhaled like a man surfacing. "You'll do it? You'll get him to stop? Or—or tell me how to make it right?"

"I'll take your case. The guild's fee is standard: one favor, unnamed, callable within the year, or coin at triple the going rate for the labor." Felix slid a fresh-carved token across the table—the coiled serpent, because grief was a thing that bit when handled wrong, and Hale should know what he was carrying. "This binds the account. Keep it on your person. If we discharge the debt, you bring it back, and it goes in the erasure bin, and your nights go quiet."

"And if it doesn't—if you can't—"

"Then the token returns to you, and the guild owes *you*, which has happened four times in the last decade, and each time we hated it." Felix allowed themselves the smallest of smiles, because clients needed to believe the person behind the desk was more stone than meat. "Come back in nine days. The moon will be right."

Hale took the token, thanked Felix twice in ways that suggested he hadn't entirely heard either thanks, and left. Tam showed him out. Felix sat alone with the shells and did not move for a long moment.

Then they gathered them up, carried them downstairs to the workshop, and held the largest one to their ear again, alone, in the lamplight, with no client to perform steadiness for.

"—and the tide takes back what the tide is given—"

Not the drowned brother. Not any voice belonging to Hale's grief at all. This was older. This was the voice from the hole in Felix's life, the one that sang behind the locked door of everything they'd lost, and it had never once come to them through a medium before. The dead spoke to Felix constantly—it was the family affliction of anyone born with the hearing—but this voice had always kept its distance. Polite. Absent. Gone.

Now it was in an oyster shell dredged from the Shallows, humming Felix's own private tune.

"Who are you?" Felix whispered.

The shell answered with the melody's next phrase, patient as water wearing stone, and then, so softly Felix might have invented it:

*"Carver. Come count what's owed."*

* * *

Sleep did not come easily on fog-nights, so Felix gave up on it around midnight and went back down to the ledger. Work was the only prayer they trusted. By lamp-light they entered Hale's account in both columns—*living debt: one favor unnamed; dead claim: passage or silence, TBD*—and it was while blotting the entry that they noticed the discrepancy.

The page before Hale's was dated tomorrow.

Felix frowned, checked the quill, checked the ink. No—they had written tonight's date correctly at the top of the folio. But beneath it, in a hand that was unmistakably their own, in ink already dry, ran an entry dated the following evening: *Received: one (1) prophecy, aged one hundred years. Status: pending audit.*

Felix stared at it. Their heart performed a slow, deliberate stumble.

They did not write prophecies. They wrote debts. They had never once, in eleven years of balancing these columns, entered a single word ahead of its hour—no clerk of the impossible could afford to, because the moment your ledger started predicting, it stopped recording, and the guild's entire covenant rested on the difference between the two.

And yet the entry was there. In their own hand. In ink gone properly dry, dried the way ink dries over days, not hours.

Felix pressed their fingertips flat against the page, feeling for warmth, for trickery, for the oily residue of a haunting. The paper was cool. The dead did many things, but they did not forge handwriting; forgery required intent, and intent was the one cargo souls always left behind at the border.

Which meant the hand was Felix's. Which meant the hand had written it. Which meant—

Upstairs, faintly, one of the bound ghosts began to sing, and it was not the saltmarsh air. It was a warning-melody, the kind the eldest of them used when the wards thinned past comfortable, and Felix sat very still in the lamplight, listening to a dead woman sing danger into the bones of the building, while on the open page before them their own future handwriting waited like a hook baited with tomorrow.

*Pending audit,* the entry said.

There was only one place in the guildhall where a hundred-year-old anything could be found, and Felix had been putting off the sealed archive's inventory for a month, telling themselves there was no hurry.

There was a hurry now.

They closed the ledger gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping animal, took up a lamp, and went to find out what their own hand had promised them.
