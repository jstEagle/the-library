## 2. Tonight, Written Long Ago

The sealed archive sat beneath the guildhall like a held breath.

Felix Voss had been inside it four times in seven years—once for the audit of the Wishing Debt, once when a ledger tried to eat its own clerk, twice for funerals that required the reading of founding oaths. Each time, the door at the bottom of the spiral stair had taken its toll: three drops of blood pressed to the lock-bone, and the sensation of being *counted*, the way one counts coins into a purse, deciding whether each is true.

Tonight the lock lingered over them longer than usual. The bone at the center of the door was warm as a sleeping animal's flank, and when Felix pressed their thumb to it they could have sworn it pulsed once, deliberately, like a heartbeat acknowledging another.

"You know me," Felix murmured, absurdly, to a door. "You've counted me before."

The lock opened anyway. But the feeling of having been weighed stayed with them all the way down, into the cold, into the dark where the guild kept its impossible accounts.

Lamps first. That was discipline. Odile's predecessor, Guildmaster Corvane, had died down here—or rather, had *stopped* down here, in the way that people who spent too long among unbalanced books sometimes stopped—and the story went that he'd walked into the deep shelves with a single candle and simply never come back out. So Felix lit four lamps on brass stands, spaced evenly, and set a fifth on the reading table, and only then allowed themselves to look at the shelf where the discrepancy lived.

It was the ninth shelf of the western wall, the one that held the guild's founding documents: charters, covenants, the great leather ledgers whose entries were written in ink mixed with grave-dust so that debts to the dead would stay legible past death. The audit had flagged it three days ago. A simple thing, really—a column that wouldn't sum. The Ledger of Standing Obligations listed the guildhall's holdings, and one line item, *Archive, subterranean: contents, 1,447 volumes*, disagreed with the physical count by exactly one book. Not lost. The ledger didn't say lost. It said, in the cramped hand of some clerk dead two hundred years:

*Contents: 1,447 volumes, of which one is not ours.*

Felix ran their fingers along the spines now, counting under their breath the way their mother had taught them to count stitches—though they couldn't actually remember their mother teaching them anything, couldn't remember her face or her voice or whether she'd ever existed outside of the fact of Felix's own existence. That absence was an old companion. It sat beside them in the lamplight like a small cold animal and said nothing, as always.

One thousand four hundred forty-six. One thousand four hundred forty-seven.

There.

The book hadn't been there yesterday. Felix was certain of it—not because they had memorized the shelf, though they nearly had, but because dust did not lie. Every spine on the shelf wore its gray pelt of settled years. The new book was clean. It stood among its neighbors like a living thing in a row of skulls, and it was thin, and it was bound in something that wasn't leather, and Felix's hands began to shake before they even touched it.

Because they recognized the binding material. Any bone-carver would. It was human femur, split and planed and polished into covers, and the workmanship was—

*Good.* Better than good. The grain of the bone had been followed, not fought; the polish had been brought up slowly over years of handling, the way only a book that was *read* got polished, worn bright along the edges where thumbs had turned pages ten thousand times. Felix knew that wear. They saw it in their own pieces, the tokens they carved for clients who came to pay what they owed the dead. A well-loved bone told you everything about the hands that loved it.

These hands had been narrow. Strong. Left-handed—the wear was heavier on the left cover's lower corner, where a left thumb rests while the right turns pages.

Like Felix's own hands.

"No," Felix said aloud, and the word fell flat in the archive air, swallowed by shelves. They were alone down here. Officially.

Unofficially, the archive was never empty. The guildhall's bound spirits drifted where they pleased, and the deeper rooms were favorites of Maren, eldest of the ghosts, who liked the dark and the smell of old paper and claimed—when she bothered to claim anything—that she had helped shelve half these books herself. But tonight Maren was elsewhere. Tonight the archive held only Felix and the lamps and the book that smelled, faintly, of fresh ink.

That was the wrong part. That was the part that made Felix's skin crawl up their arms like rising water. Old bone had a smell—dust and calcium and centuries. Old paper had a smell. This book had both, underlaid with something else, something that belonged to a scribe's desk an hour ago: iron-gall ink, sharp as a struck match, wet-seeming in the nose though the covers were dry as winter grass.

Felix carried it to the reading table. Set it down. Stared at it for a long moment, the way you stare at a door you're sure was locked.

Then they opened it.

* * *

The first page was a title page, and it was not gentle about its business.

*THE BURNING FORETOLD: Being a True Account of What Shall Come, Set Down by Order of the First Compact of Carvers, in the Year of the Sigil's Peace.*

A century ago. Felix did the arithmetic the way they did all arithmetic—automatically, in the margins of their mind, the habit of a life spent balancing accounts. One hundred years since the Sigil's Peace. One hundred years since the covenant had been forged that bound the living realm to the dead one, that let the dead speak through bone and token, that made the Guild of Impossible Things possible at all. Every charter, every oath, every debt in every ledger downstairs rested on that peace like a house on its foundation stones.

And someone, a hundred years ago, had written a book predicting its burning.

Felix turned the page.

The early chapters were dense, formal, almost liturgical—the kind of prose written by people who believed that prophecy was a craft like any other, requiring tools and patience. *The Sigil shall stand a hundred years,* one passage read, *for a hundred years is the term of a bone's memory, and no pact endures past the forgetting of its makers.* Another: *Three things were given to seal it: the Key, the Oathbone, and the Nameplate. Three things shall be gathered to break it, and three things to mend what breaks.*

Felix read that line twice. Then a third time, lips moving. Gathered. By whom? For which purpose? The text was maddeningly silent, the way prophecy always was—every road open, every road signed in letters too smudged to read until you were already walking.

They turned pages faster now. The middle of the book was given to signs and portents, most of them useless—comets, floods, a plague of birds with white eyes that Felix sincerely hoped was metaphorical. But near the end, the handwriting changed.

Not degraded. Changed—tightened, quickened, as if the writer had stopped composing and started *transcribing*, taking dictation from somewhere. The letters leaned forward. The ink went darker.

And the dates began.

*In the ninety-ninth year, the ledger shall miscount by one.* Felix's breath stopped. Their eyes went, involuntarily, to the doorway, to the stair, as though the clerk who wrote this might be standing there with an apology and an explanation. Nothing. Lamps. Shelves. The sound of the guildhall overhead, distant and ordinary, someone laughing in the common room.

*In the hundredth year, the wards shall be tested by ash.*

*In the hundredth year, the carver shall find this book.*

Felix looked at their own hands. Narrow. Strong. Ink-stained at the cuticles from tonight's ledger work, bone-dust worked so deep into the whorls of the fingerprints that no washing ever fully removed it. Hands that had been doing this work for as long as Felix could remember—which, if they were honest, was not very long. Seven years. Seven years since they'd woken in this city knowing the trade but not its teacher, fluent in a craft with no memory of learning it. Everyone had gaps in their childhood. Everyone said so. Felix had learned to say so too.

*The carver,* the book said again, further down the page, in that leaning urgent hand. *Whose bones remember what their mind does not. The carver shall stand before the Sigil when it burns, and the burning shall know their name, and by their hand it shall be either mended or ended, and the realms shall hold or fall accordingly.*

"Either," Felix whispered. "Either mended or ended."

The prophecy gave no instruction for choosing. Of course it didn't. Prophecies were contracts drafted by lawyers who had died before the signing; every clause had a loophole and every loophole led downward.

But none of that—not the title, not the changed hand, not the word *carver* landing on Felix's chest like a dropped stone—none of it compared to what came next. Because the final entry was short. Four lines. And it was dated.

Felix had been dreading the date since the title page. Some part of them, the part that balanced impossible accounts for a living, had already done the terrible math and was waiting, resigned, for confirmation. Books like this dated their endings in ways that were conveniently far away, decades off, generations—the comfortable distance at which doom could be admired like a painting.

The date on the final page was today.

Not the season. Not the year. The *day*. Written a hundred years ago in ink that still smelled fresh, in a hand that leaned forward as if reaching through the century to grab the reader by the collar:

*Tonight. The night of the finding. The carver reads these words beneath the guildhall, alone, by lamplight, and the ink is not yet dry, and will never be dry, and this is how they will know.*

The lamps guttered. All five of them, together, in a room with no draft.

Felix sat very still. Cold moved through them—not the archive's honest cellar cold, which could be endured with a coat and stubbornness, but a different cold, a cold that started behind the sternum and spread outward, the cold of water closing over the head. Dread, arriving exactly as dread always arrived for Felix: not gradually, not reasonably, but all at once, like stepping off a stair that wasn't there.

The ink was not yet dry. They touched the final line with one fingertip, carefully, the way they'd touch a client's payment-token to hear what voice it carried—and the ink *was* wet. It gleamed. A hundred years old, and it came away on their finger like blood from a fresh cut, and it did not smear. It sank into their skin instead, a fine dark thread vanishing into the whorls, gone as if swallowed.

Somewhere above, faintly, through floors of stone and timber, Felix heard the guildhall's wards hum—a low, resonant note, the sound the protective circles made when they were working hard. They hummed rarely. They hummed when something tested them.

Felix closed the book. Their hands wanted to shake and they did not let them, because shaking hands drop things, and dropping things was for people who still believed they had time.

"Maren," they said quietly, to the dark between the shelves. "I know you're listening. You're always listening."

The dark did not answer, but it changed quality—the way a room changes when someone enters it, though nothing visible moves. And then she was there, the eldest ghost of the guildhall, coalescing out of shadow the way frost forms on glass: a woman of perhaps sixty, rendered in grays and silvers, wearing the shape of clothes a hundred years out of fashion. Her eyes found the book on the table immediately. Felix watched something cross her face that they had never seen there before, in all seven years of their acquaintance.

Fear.

"Felix Voss," Maren said, and her voice was the voice of a woman trying very hard to be calm. "What have you found?"

"A book." Felix's mouth was dry. "It knows my name. It knows tonight. Maren—it knows *me*. There's a page in here that describes this exact moment, this exact—" They stopped. Started again. "The ink is still wet."

Maren drifted closer, close enough that the lamplight thinned passing through her, and looked down at the open page for a long, long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped to nearly nothing.

"I shelved books on that wall," she said. "When I was alive. I shelved them, and I counted them, and I can tell you to the number what that shelf held, child, and I am telling you now that your book was not among them. Not when I died. Not in all the years since." She lifted her gaze to Felix's face. "Which means either the count was wrong for a century—"

"The ledger says one volume isn't ours," Felix said. "It's said so for two hundred years."

"—or the book has been waiting somewhere else," Maren finished, "and chose tonight to arrive. Chose *you* to find it." She was silent a moment, and then she said the last thing Felix expected, the words falling from her like something heavy finally set down:

"Burn it, Felix. Don't read another page. Don't ask me what I know—I'm asking you not to ask. Take it upstairs and put it in the stove and stand there until it's ash, and then take the ash to running water. Do it tonight, do it now, and maybe—"

"*Maybe what?*" Felix heard their own voice crack, sharp with fear sharpened into anger. "Maybe what, Maren? You've been dead a hundred years. You were alive when this was written—you just said so. You know who wrote it. You know what the Sigil is, what happened to it, why every ghost in this building goes quiet whenever anyone mentions the forging." They stood, and the chair scraped, and in the lamplight their shadow fell across the open page across the words *the carver shall find this book*. "Someone carved my name into a prophecy a century before I was born. Someone bound this book to wait for me. And the one spirit in this hall old enough to remember is telling me to burn the evidence and go to bed."

"Yes," Maren said simply. "That is precisely what I am telling you."

"Why?"

For a moment Felix thought she would not answer. The ghost looked at the book, and then at Felix's hands—looked at them a long time, the way one looks at a familiar object that has suddenly shown an unfamiliar face—and when she finally spoke, her voice was so soft the lamps seemed to lean in to hear it.

"Because I knew the carver," she said. "I knew her better than anyone in this world. And Felix—the book is right. Your hands are her hands. And if the Compact learns that you exist, if they learn that she came back, there will be no ward, no wall, and no grave deep enough to keep them from you."

She began to fade even as she finished speaking, pulling back into the shadows as though the truth itself were a weight she could no longer bear upright, and her last words came from everywhere and nowhere in the dark archive:

"They burned her once. They'll not need a second invitation."

The lamps steadied. The archive was still. On the table, the prophecy book lay open to its final page, and the ink of the last line glistened, freshly wet, patient as a trap.

*This is how they will know.*

Above Felix's head, faint and rhythmic, the wards went on humming—and Felix noticed, with a coldness beyond all the others, that the note had changed. It was no longer the steady tone of protection holding.

It was rising. Getting louder.

Getting closer.

Felix picked up the book, snuffed four of the five lamps, and took the stairs two at a time.
