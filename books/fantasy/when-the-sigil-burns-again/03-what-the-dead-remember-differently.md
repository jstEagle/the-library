## 3. What the Dead Remember Differently

The guildhall's ghosts kept their own hours.

Felix had learned this in their first year of service, when Odile handed over the keys and the ledgers and a single instruction: *The dead are clients too. Treat their accounts with the same respect.* The spirits bound to the Guild of Impossible Things did not drift through its halls the way stories promised ghosts would, moaning and translucent and lost. They worked. They kept to schedules older than the building's foundations. And they gathered, every night between the last bell and the first grey hint of dawn, in the long gallery above the counting room—a chamber Felix thought of privately as the waiting room, because that was what it had become: a place where souls sat with their unfinished business spread out before them like patients in a physician's hall, each waiting for a turn that might never come.

Felix climbed the stairs at midnight, the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth and pressed against their chest like a wound they were trying not to aggravate. The stairs groaned in familiar complaint. The gallery door stood open, as it always did for Felix—Odile needed three knocks and a spoken name, but the dead had decided long ago that the bone-carver was allowed passage anywhere. A courtesy. Or perhaps a claim. Felix had never been certain which.

Tonight the gallery was fuller than usual.

Perhaps two dozen spirits occupied the long room, arranged in their habitual clusters: the merchants near the eastern windows where they could watch the trade roads even though no road had run past the guildhall in sixty years; the drowned sailors from the river debts hovering low along the floorboards, close to the memory of water; the old soldiers by the cold hearth, standing at something like attention because they had never learned another posture. Candlelight passed through them rather than illuminating them, so the whole room seemed lit from beneath, lit by something buried.

The murmuring stopped when Felix entered.

It always stopped, briefly—the way a market quiets when a magistrate walks through—and then resumed. But tonight it did not resume. Tonight two dozen pairs of dead eyes turned and stayed, and the silence thickened until Felix could hear the building settling on its stones, could hear their own pulse ticking in their ears like a metronome counting down to something.

"You've heard," Felix said. Their voice came out smaller than intended.

"We don't hear," said one of the merchant ghosts, a woman named Corvel who had died owing the guild a debt she'd spent forty years refusing to acknowledge. "Hearing is for ears." She drifted closer, her form flickering like a candle in a draft. "We *remember*. And tonight everything remembers too loudly."

"The book," said a sailor. His voice carried the gurgle of his drowning still caught in his throat. "You found the book."

Felix unwrapped the oilcloth without answering, and the murmur that went through the gallery then was not sound at all but pressure—a change in the air, a shift in weight, the way a room feels just before lightning strikes somewhere nearby. Several spirits drew back. One of the soldiers actually saluted, which made no sense and frightened Felix more than anything else that night.

"I need to know about the Sigil," Felix said. "The forging. A century ago. What happened."

* * *

Corvel remembered a forge.

She told it standing at the eastern window with her back straight and her merchant's pride intact, hands folded before her in the gesture of a woman presenting accounts. "I was not there," she admitted. "I died forty years after. But my grandmother sold iron to the forgers, and she told me, and I tell you: there was a forge built on the border itself, half in this world and half in the other, and its fire burned without fuel. Nine masters hammered together—one hand from among the living, one hand from among the dead, joined at the wrist like a handshake that never ended. They forged the Sigil in a single night, from sunset to sunrise, and when it was done the border closed like a well-healed scar, and everyone wept because they were relieved and ashamed of being relieved in equal measure."

"That's how my grandmother told it," Corvel finished. "Clean seams. A mended thing."

"And you?" Felix asked a soldier.

His name had been Aldric once; now he was mostly posture and discipline, a shape of duty that had outlived the man. He remembered fire.

"There was no forge," he said flatly. "There was a battlefield. The border wasn't closed—it was *cauterized*. The dead had been pouring across for years, hungry, wrong, wearing faces they'd stolen. The living couldn't fight them and the dead couldn't stop themselves. So the nine masters took the covenant—whatever pact held the worlds loosely together—and drove it into the ground like a spear, and burned it white-hot, and the burning sealed the wound. It wasn't mending." His voice cracked along some old fracture. "It was amputation. We lost the ones already crossing. My brother was halfway over when the Sigil flared. He is neither here nor there to this day. That is your clean seam, carver. A man sawn in half by a closing door."

Felix looked at the drowned sailor, whose name was Pem. Pem had been dead the longest of the three—ninety-one years—and he floated with his head tilted as if perpetually listening to water.

"Neither of those," Pem said softly. "It was an agreement. A treaty, signed in blood and bone, both sides consenting. There was a ceremony. There were vows. The dead agreed to stay because staying was better than what lay beyond either shore—I remember the negotiations lasting years, envoys going back and forth across the border like ferrymen. The Sigil wasn't a wall or a wound. It was a *signature*. The last signature on a very long contract." He paused. "And the contract had terms. Terms no one alive has ever read."

"Terms?" Felix leaned forward. "What terms?"

Pem's form wavered. "Passage," he said. "That's all I know. Passage was part of it. Somewhere in the wording of that covenant there was supposed to be a door."

"Aldric says amputation. Corvel says mending. You say a contract with a door in it." Felix set the book on the table between them, still unwrapped, its cover dull under the candlelight. "Three ghosts. Three forgings. How?"

"Memory is an account," Corvel said, with a shrug that rippled her edges. "And every account is kept from one side of the ledger."

But that wasn't an answer, and Felix knew it. Ghosts did not forget the way the living forgot—fuzzy, softened, rewritten by vanity and grief. The dead remembered with terrible precision; it was one of the few mercies of their condition, and one of the cruelties. Three eyewitness traditions could differ in emphasis. They could not differ in kind. A forge was not a battlefield was not a signing ceremony. Someone was wrong, or someone was lying, or—

Or the event itself had been different things to different witnesses, which should have been impossible, and which made Felix's skin prickle the way it did when a ledger refused to balance no matter how many times the columns were summed.

"One more question," Felix said. "Who carved the Sigil? Who was the carver?"

* * *

The silence that followed was unlike any silence Felix had ever heard.

Silence usually had texture—the hush of snowfall, the held breath of an audience, the dead air of an empty house. This silence had *teeth*. It snapped shut around the question and swallowed it whole. Corvel's flickering stilled. Aldric's spine locked. Pem sank toward the floorboards as if the memory of water could hide him.

Even the spirits who had not been part of the conversation—the clusters by the hearth, the merchants at the windows—all of them went rigid and remote, the way a household goes quiet when the master's footsteps sound on the stair.

"Maren," Corvel said at last, and her voice was careful, the voice of a clerk passing a dangerous parcel. "Ask Maren."

"Why can't you answer?"

"Because we are bound," Aldric said. His jaw moved stiffly, as if the words cost him effort to force out. "All of us in this hall are bound to the guild, and the guild is bound to the covenant, and the covenant—" His form shuddered. "The covenant does not permit us to speak that name. Try asking whether the sky is blue. We will answer freely. Ask about the carver, and it is like pressing on a bruise inside the world."

"Then ask me nothing," said a voice from the doorway.

Maren had been the guildhall's ghost longer than anyone could account for—not merely the eldest spirit bound there, but quite possibly the eldest *thing*, if you counted generously. She had been old when the founding guildmasters were young; Odile herself consulted her the way one consults a load-bearing wall, carefully and with respect for what happens if it shifts. She appeared as a woman in her sixties with sea-grey hair and hands knotted by decades of rope-work, dressed in the oilskin of a harbor pilot, and she moved through the guildhall the way weather moves through a coastline: slowly, inevitably, touching everything.

She crossed the gallery now, and the other ghosts parted for her the way water parts for a hull.

Felix had known Maren for eleven years. She had taught them the binding-words for difficult hauntings, the etiquette of negotiation with the recently dead, the trick of listening for a ghost's true grievance underneath its stated one. She was the closest thing Felix had to family in the guildhall, which was to say she was stern, undemonstrative, and reliably present, like a lighthouse.

Maren looked at the prophecy book for a long time. Then she looked at Felix, and what was in her face made Felix's stomach drop before she said a word—because Maren's face, usually weathered into calm, showed fear. Not the theatrical dread of a haunting. Real fear, worn plainly, the way a workingwoman wears her tools.

"Where did you find it?"

"The sealed archive. Third shelf, behind the tax disputes from the founding era."

"And the date?"

"Circled. In ink that hasn't dried in a hundred years."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them again the fear was still there, banked but burning. "Listen to me, bone-carver, and listen properly, because I will say this once and then I will say nothing more tonight, because the covenant is already leaning on my tongue and by morning it will lean harder."

"I'm listening."

"Burn the book."

Felix blinked. "Burn it? It's a guild asset, it's—"

"It is a hook," Maren said, "baited a century ago and left dangling in the water ever since, and tonight, Felix Voss, the fish has finally arrived. Burn it. Burn it in salt-fire, burn it outside the wards, burn it where the ash can scatter, and then take a month's leave and let the guildmaster handle whatever comes next. That is my counsel. That is the counsel of the oldest dead thing in this building, who has watched every guildmaster come and go and has seen exactly one constant among them." Her voice dropped. "They all survived by doing nothing. Do nothing, child. Nothing is a shelter. Nothing has kept this hall standing for a hundred years."

"But the date—"

"The date is ink." Maren's voice hardened. "Ink is not fate. Prophecy is a story told by someone who wanted it believed, and every storyteller wants an audience, and the cruelest trick a story can play is to convince its listener that listening obligates them to perform." She reached out—not touching, ghosts could not touch, but her hand hovered over the book's cover the way a hand hovers over a flame to test its heat. "Do you know why none of us can speak of the carver?"

"No."

"Because the carver is the one question the covenant cannot afford. Everything else it can digest—forgings, burnings, borders, dates. But ask who held the knife, and the whole contract clenches around the answer like a fist around a blade." Maren withdrew her hand. "Which tells you something, doesn't it. The covenant isn't afraid of the question being asked. It's afraid of the answer being *known*. There is a difference, and the difference has teeth."

"Maren, please. If the Sigil burns tonight—if the prophecy is true—"

"If the prophecy is true, then it was true before you were born, and your burning yourself alive to prevent it will change nothing except the amount of ash." She softened, barely, the way a harbor softens behind its breakwater. "You are a good carver, Felix Voss. You balance accounts no one else can read. You give the dead their dignity in small carved tokens, and they rest easier for it. That is real work. That is enough work for one life. Do not go looking for the largest account in the ledger simply because your name appears somewhere in the margin."

"*My name appears,*" Felix said quietly. "*The carver.*"

Something moved through the gallery then—not wind, not draft, but recognition. Every ghost in the room felt it pass. Corvel looked away. Aldric fixed his eyes on the middle distance. Pem studied the floorboards as though accounts were written there.

Only Maren held Felix's gaze, and only Maren's expression gave anything away: a flicker of grief so old and so deep that it predated Felix's birth, predates the guildhall, possibly predated the word *guild*.

"Yes," Maren said. "It does."

And then she turned away—which Maren never did, Maren who faced down poltergeists and plague-dead and once stared a demon of debt into dissolution—and drifted toward the window, and her voice when it came was thin as thread pulled taut:

"Burn the book, Felix. While your hands still belong to you."

* * *

Felix did not burn the book.

They told themselves later that it was principle—that a ledger was a ledger, that guild property was guild property, that destroying records was the first act of every coward and every tyrant. All true. None of it was the truth. The truth was simpler and worse: Felix sat alone in the gallery until the candles guttered, with the prophecy book open on their knees, reading the entry again and again while the dead kept their distance and the silence kept its teeth.

*On this night the Sigil shall burn, and the carver shall stand at its heart, and the carver's hands shall choose.*

*Choose.*

The word had been underlined twice. By whom? By the same hand that had circled the date? The ink smelled fresh—fresh as the payment Felix had taken from a client only days ago, fresh as the voice that hummed up through their own bones when they worked late. Felix pressed their palm flat against the page and felt nothing, which was somehow more unsettling than feeling anything at all. A book that haunted should at least have the decency to be cold.

Outside, the bells rang the hour. Two o'clock. The night was moving whether Felix moved with it or not.

They closed the book. They stood. And as they turned toward the stairs, movement below caught their eye—through the gallery's interior window, down in the moonlit counting room, a figure stood among the ledgers. Tall. Cloaked. Wrong in the specific way that things are wrong when they do not disturb the dust they walk through.

Not a ghost. The ghosts were all up here, pressed against the walls of the gallery, watching the intruder below with expressions Felix had never seen on the dead: naked, unguarded terror.

The figure lifted its head. Even at this distance, even in shadow, Felix felt it look directly at them—and felt, rising through the floorboards and up through the soles of their feet and into the marrow the bone-carver had always trusted, a single sensation, unmistakable, ancient, and horribly familiar:

The smell of fresh ink.
