## 4. The Rival's Card

The knock came at the hour when the guildhall's lamps burned lowest, and Felix knew before answering that it was trouble, because honest clients never knocked like that—twice, precisely spaced, with the patience of someone who had already been paid to wait.

They had spent the evening in the cellar workshop with a client's payment humming against their wrist: a finger-bone token carved from a drowned man's knuckle, still whispering of cold water and the particular loneliness of sinking. Felix had meant to set it in the ledger as settled debt and be done with it, but the voice inside it kept snagging on something—a name, half-formed, like a word spoken just beneath the surface of a river. *Maren*, it might have been. Or something older wearing Maren's shape.

Felix set the bone down when the knock came again.

Upstairs, the guildhall proper was quiet in the way it only ever was between the last client and the first ghost's nightly circuit—the dead who lived in these walls liked to walk the corridors after midnight, checking doors, straightening chairs, keeping their small habits alive because habit was all they had left. Felix climbed past them now, past old Corvus drifting along the gallery rail where he'd fallen a hundred years ago, past the twins who flickered in the stairwell like moths deciding whether to land. They watched Felix pass with expressions that were almost curiosity. Almost.

The door.

Through its iron-bound oak, Felix could see nothing—but they could *hear* the visitor, and hearing was worse than seeing. There was no heartbeat on the other side. Every living thing had a heartbeat; even the guildhall's rats drummed their tiny anxious rhythms through the floorboards into Felix's bones. This person stood utterly silent, and silence, for a bone-carver who heard the dead in everything, was its own kind of announcement.

*Nothing living knocks like that.*

Felix opened the door.

The envoy stood in the lamplit street like an illustration cut from a finer book than this neighborhood deserved. Tall, gray-cloaked, with the kind of face that seemed designed to be forgotten—pleasant, symmetrical, entirely unremarkable until you looked at the eyes, which held the flat patience of a well. In one gloved hand they carried a card of pale ash-colored stock. In the other, nothing at all, which somehow frightened Felix more.

"Voss," the envoy said. Not a question. "Bone-carver. Keeper of ledgers." A thin smile. "And reader of sealed things."

"I don't read sealed things," Felix said. "That's rather the point of sealing them."

"Of course." The envoy extended the card between two fingers, formal as a duelist offering terms. "I'm called Wren Ashfall, envoy of the Ashen Compact. I've come to make you a very simple offer."

Felix did not take the card. Taking the card would mean admitting to a transaction, and transactions had rules, and rules were the only solid ground Felix had ever stood on. "We're closed."

"The Compact has been looking for a certain book for sixty-three years," Wren continued, as though Felix hadn't spoken. Their voice was smooth and dry, like paper sliding across a desk. "Fourteen of my predecessors have walked into cities, crossed borders, bribed archivists and threatened librarians and burned three separate buildings to their foundations. And here it turns up—" the smile widened by a single tooth "—in the cellar of a minor trade guild, in the keeping of a carver who balances accounts for ghosts. Fate has a sense of humor, Voss. It usually isn't kind."

"The Compact," Felix said slowly. "Ashen Compact. You're the ones who buy bone futures in the eastern markets. Who fund the quieting houses." Everyone in the trade knew the name; few admitted to knowing what the Compact actually *did*. They dealt in death the way other institutions dealt in grain—vastly, impersonally, always turning a profit.

"We do many things. Tonight we are doing one." Wren produced a second object from within the cloak: a purse that clinked with the unmistakable weight of sigil-silver, coin struck from metal that remembered being part of the covenant itself. "One hundred marks for a book you have no use for. Two hundred if you hand over whatever else was shelved beside it. No questions. No records. As far as your ledger is concerned, tonight never happened."

Everything in Felix went very still.

It wasn't the money. It was the *precision* of the offer—the way Wren had said *tonight never happened* with the confidence of someone who already knew exactly which shelf, exactly which night, exactly which book. Which meant the Compact had eyes inside the guildhall. Or ears. Or something worse than either.

"You know what's in the archive," Felix said quietly.

"I know what's in *you*," Wren replied. "You opened it today. Sealed bindings leave traces, Voss—residue in the air, a taste like copper and old smoke. My predecessors taught me to smell it from the street." The envoy's flat eyes glinted. "So. Shall we discuss price?"

"No."

The word came out smaller than Felix intended, so they said it again, firmer. "No. The book is guild property. Its disposition isn't mine to sell, and even if it were—" Felix thought of the fresh-smelling ink, the circled date, the words *the carver* written a century before Felix was born, and felt the dread rise again like cold water filling boots. "Even if it were, I wouldn't. Some debts can't be paid forward. That book is a debt."

Wren tilted their head, and for a moment the pleasant mask slipped, and underneath it Felix saw something ancient and tired and *hungry*. "A debt," the envoy repeated softly. "Yes. We know all about that debt. Sixty-three years, Voss. Do you know why we've hunted it so long? Because every generation, someone finds that book, reads that page, and decides they're the one the prophecy means. They go looking for relics. They go poking at the Sigil. And then people burn." The smile returned, thinner. "We're not trying to steal your destiny, carver. We're trying to prevent you from having one. Two hundred marks. Last offer."

"Guild property," Felix repeated. "Good evening."

They began to close the door. Wren did not move to stop it—simply stood there, letting the oak swing toward them, unbothered as weather. At the last inch, when only a sliver of lamplight connected them, the envoy spoke once more.

"Maren will tell you to burn it," they said. "She always does. She told the last carver the same thing, seventy years ago. He didn't listen either."

The door shut. Felix stood with their back against it, heart hammering, listening to the street outside. No footsteps receded. No carriage rolled away. When Felix finally pulled the door open again, the lane was empty, the lamps guttering, and on the threshold lay the ash-gray card, placed there without sound.

Felix picked it up with tongs, carried it to the workshop hearth, and burned it. The card resisted the flame longer than paper should—curling, blackening, hissing—and as it died it gave off a smell Felix recognized from the prophecy book's pages.

Fresh ink. Old ash.

* * *

Felix did not sleep. They sat in the workshop with the ledger open and the lamp trimmed low, and they did the thing that had always steadied them: they worked. Bone tokens for debts owed, debts paid, debts deferred. The drowned man's finger-bone hummed under their carving knife, and Felix listened to it properly for the first time that night, letting the dead man's memory wash over them like tidewater.

*Cold water. Sinking slow. A lantern above, shrinking to a coin, to a spark, to nothing. And then—not darkness. Something else. A vast gate of light and bone, and a voice saying: not yet, not yet, the covenant holds, back you go—*

Felix's knife stopped.

The covenant. The Sigil. Even the drowning dead passed through its shadow on their way to wherever the dead went—or failed to go. Everything touched it. Everything had *always* touched it, the way fish are touched by water, invisible because omnipresent. And somewhere in the sealed dark of the guild archive sat a book that claimed tonight was the night it might all come apart.

*The carver,* the page had said. Felix flexed their hands, scarred and steady, hands that had carved ten thousand tokens and never once questioned why the work came so easily, why bone seemed to open to them like a letter to its addressee.

Below the floorboards, faint as breath, the guildhall's ghosts had gathered. Felix could feel them through the stone—a pressure like standing water, dozens of the dead assembled in the cellar stairwell, drawn by something they sensed and Felix did not. Corvus's presence, dry and papery. The twins, flickering. And beneath them all, oldest and heaviest, Maren—the woman who had been the guild's first ledger-keeper, dead two centuries, bound to the hall by an oath she'd never explained.

*Maren will tell you to burn it,* the envoy had said. *She always does.*

Which meant the Compact knew Maren too. Knew her habits, her counsel, her warnings—as if she'd given the same advice before, to other carvers, in other years. Seventy years ago. A hundred and thirty. How many times had this wheel turned? How many carvers had stood where Felix stood, holding the same book, hearing the same warning?

Felix blew out the lamp and went down to ask her.

* * *

The cellar stairwell was full of ghosts, and they parted for Felix in silence, which was itself an answer. The dead chattered among themselves constantly—gossip, grievance, the endless replay of old memories—but now they pressed against the walls of perception and said nothing at all, watching Felix descend with the solemnity of mourners.

Maren waited at the bottom, by the archive door.

In life she must have been small and sharp; in death she was both, a figure of gray light with ink-stained fingers and eyes like pinpricks burned through cloth. She had been the one to teach Felix the ledger's deeper grammar, back in the apprentice years—how to record a debt owed by a ghost, how to balance an account when the debtor was a memory. Of all the dead, Maren was the one Felix trusted most. Which made what she said next feel like the floor giving way.

"Burn the book," Maren said, before Felix could speak.

"You don't even know why I've come."

"I know exactly why you've come." Her voice crackled like a quill scratching parchment. "Gray cloak. Card of ash-paper. Smell of old fire. The Compact found you." She drifted closer, and the lamplight passed through her without warming her. "Burn it. Burn the page, burn the binding, scatter the ashes in running water. Tonight, Voss."

"The archive seal—"

"I *wrote* the archive seal." For the first time, something moved behind her burnt-through eyes—something that might, in a living face, have been fear. "Two hundred years ago I locked that book away and told the guildmasters it was account-keeping from a mad predecessor. A lie. A necessary lie. Because that book doesn't predict the future, child. It *remembers* it. Every word on that page has happened before. The burning. The tearing. The carver with borrowed bones. It happened a century ago, and the century before that, and it will keep happening as long as anyone reads it and thinks themselves chosen."

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "The Compact's envoy said—she said a carver came before. Seventy years ago."

"He lasted eleven days after the Compact found him." Maren's ink-stained fingers curled. "He read the page. He decided he was the one. He went looking for the relics, and the Compact burned the safehouse around him with the Covenant Key inside, and the fire took four city blocks, and the Key has not been seen since." Her voice dropped to ash. "Do you understand? The prophecy doesn't need you to believe in it. It needs only for you to *act*. Every attempt to fulfill it or prevent it feeds the same fire. The only winning move is oblivion. Burn it."

"And if the prophecy comes true anyway?" Felix asked quietly. "If the Sigil burns whether I act or not?"

Maren was silent a long moment. Around her, the other ghosts hung motionless in the dark like moths frozen mid-flight.

"Then it burns," she said at last, "and the dead spill through, and everything ends, and none of us can stop it—least of all a bone-carver with a ledger and a lamp." She turned toward the archive door, and her gray form dimmed. "But it won't come true. It can't. Because you're going to burn the book, and go back to balancing accounts, and live a long unremarkable life, and die, and join me here, and we'll drink ghost-wine and complain about the new apprentices." A ghost's attempt at humor, thin as smoke. "Promise me, Voss."

Felix looked at her—at the oldest friend they had, dead before Felix's grandmother was born—and lied.

"I promise," Felix said.

Maren's form flickered—she heard the lie, of course she did, the dead always heard lies—but she let it stand, the way one lets a dying patient keep their comforting story. She faded up through the floor, and the other ghosts dispersed after her, and Felix stood alone in the cellar with the archive door and the truth sitting behind it like a coiled thing.

They didn't burn the book. Instead, Felix took it from its shelf, wrapped it in oilcloth, and hid it inside the hollow base of the ledger-stand itself—where a thief would never think to look, because who hides a treasure inside the furniture of ordinary work?

Then Felix went back upstairs, barred the door, and finally slept, fitfully, with a carving knife under their pillow and the drowned man's bone humming on the desk like a distant bell.

* * *

Felix woke to the smell of scorched stone.

Not wood-smoke—stone-smoke, the mineral stink of wards burning out, sharp as struck flint. Felix was upright before fully awake, knife in hand, taking the stairs two at a time, and flung open the guildhall's front door onto a morning gone wrong.

Every ward-stone set into the guildhall's outer wall had been seared black. Not broken—*seared*, each one branded with a single mark: a circle bisected by a line. The Compact's sign, burned into the guild's protections with a precision that said *we could have done worse*. The stones still smoked faintly in the gray dawn light. Across the lane, the baker's boy stood staring, and two of Odile's clerks had gathered at the corner, murmuring. Word would reach the Guildmaster within the hour.

Felix walked the wall slowly, reading the damage the way they read bone. The wards hadn't been attacked—they'd been *measured*. Tested. Touched just hard enough to prove they could be broken. A message in the Compact's native language, which was arson withheld.

At the base of the westernmost ward-stone, half-buried in frost, lay a second card of ash-gray stock. Felix crouched and turned it over with a knife-tip.

On its face, in ink that smelled freshly of old fires, someone had written a single line:

*Sixty-three years, fourteen envoys, and now a carver who lies to ghosts. We'll return when you're ready to talk about the Key.*

Felix stood very still in the smoking morning.

*The Key.* The Covenant Key—the relic Maren said had burned in a safehouse seventy years ago, lost beyond finding. Except the Compact's message implied otherwise. Implied it was *findable*. Implied they knew something about it that Felix didn't—which meant, somewhere in the Compact's long gray memory, there was a thread leading to the first relic of the original pact.

And Felix, despite everything—despite Maren's warning, despite the scorched wards, despite the prophecy's cold water rising in their chest—felt the thread pull tight inside them. Because the drowned man's bone was still humming upstairs, and in its song, beneath the cold water and the shrinking lantern, there was a gate of light and bone, and a voice saying *not yet, the covenant holds*—and Felix had begun, terribly, to wonder what else the dead remembered that the living had paid to forget.

Behind them, the guildhall's door opened. Guildmaster Odile stood on the threshold in her nightclothes, staring at the blackened ward-stones, her face draining toward the same color as ash.

"Voss," she said quietly. "My study. Now."
