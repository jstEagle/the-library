## 5. Guildmaster's Silence

The guildhall smelled of smoke by morning.

Not hearth-smoke, not the honest reek of tallow and bone-dust that had soaked into these stones for a hundred years, but something sharper—something chemical and wrong, like lightning bottled in a jar and left to spoil. Felix Voss stood in the entryway with their cloak still dripping salt-rain and watched the wards along the eastern wall. They had been silver thread worked into the mortar between each stone, humming faintly, always, the way a struck tuning fork hums long after the ear stops listening. Now they were black. Scorched into little rivers of carbon that traced the old sigilwork exactly, as if someone had drawn its shadow where its light used to be.

Whoever had done this hadn't broken the wards. They had *signed* them.

"You're back early."

Felix turned. Sable, the night porter, hovered at the foot of the stairs with a candle he'd forgotten to light, or perhaps was afraid to. His eyes went to the scorched wall, then away from it, the way people's eyes had been going away from things all morning.

"Someone left a card," Felix said. "Ashen Compact. On my workshop floor, under a locked door, inside warded walls." They held up the black-edged rectangle they'd found at dawn, though they didn't hand it over. The offer written on it in copperplate—*the book, any price, name your terms*—felt like something that could be transmitted by touch.

Sable swallowed. "The Guildmaster wants you. Upstairs. She's been asking since before the bells."

Of course she had. News traveled downward through this building the way water traveled through chalk, finding every crack, arriving everywhere eventually and slightly changed. Felix climbed the stairs with the Compact's card burning a hole in their pocket like a coal they couldn't take out.

* * *

Guildmaster Odile received petitioners in a room designed to make them feel small without ever saying so. The ceiling arched high enough that voices got lost on their way up; the single window faced the grey end of the harbor, so that visitors stood always in half-light while she sat in full. She was at her desk when Felix entered—a narrow woman of sixty-odd years with iron-colored hair pulled back so severely it seemed to argue against softness on principle, and hands folded in front of her ledger of office the way judges fold theirs over verdicts.

She did not offer a chair. She never did. It was part of the room's architecture.

"The wards on my workshop held," Felix said, before she could arrange the conversation to her liking. "But the ones on the east wall didn't. And the Compact knew about the prophecy book before I did, which means someone told them, which means—"

"Sit down, Voss."

There was no chair. There never was. But there was a stool by the door for the messenger who brought Odile her evening tea, and Felix dragged it across the floor with a sound like a bone breaking quietly, and sat, and waited.

Odile studied them for a long moment. Felix had seen her do this to debtors, to thieves, to guild members caught selling impossible things to people who couldn't afford the interest. It was the look of a woman weighing what a person knew against what it would cost to let them keep knowing it.

"You audited the sealed archive last night," she said. Not a question.

"It's my job. The ledgers don't balance themselves, and the archive ledgers haven't been touched in—"

"I know what your job is." Odile opened her desk drawer—not the one on top, the one beneath, the one Felix had always assumed was empty or full of nothing more dangerous than sealing wax—and lifted out a book.

Felix's stomach dropped through the floor, through the cellar, through the bedrock, all the way down to wherever dead things kept their accounts.

It wasn't the same book. This one was older—the cover cracked leather gone the color of dried liver, the spine repaired twice with different generations of stitching. But Felix knew what lived inside books like that. You learned to feel it in the guild trade, the way a banker learns to feel counterfeit coin. Weight without substance. Words that pressed outward against their binding.

"There are two copies," Felix said quietly.

"There were always two copies." Odile set the book on the desk between them, square and deliberate, like a piece on a game board. "One in the archive, for whoever finally went looking. One here, for whoever understood what it meant. Every guildmaster has kept the second. Passed it to the next with the seal of office and a set of instructions I can recite from memory, because I have recited them, to myself, many nights, for eleven years."

Felix looked at the book. Looked at her. The half-light from the grey window made everything in the room look already embalmed.

"And what did the instructions say?"

"That we do nothing." Odile's voice didn't waver. That was the worst of it. Eleven years of carrying this, and her voice didn't waver once. "That we read it, and we understand it, and then we close the drawer, and we go on balancing accounts for clients who want love tokens carved from their mothers' finger bones. That is what the instructions say, Voss. They say nothing, eloquently, in an official hand, and then they are countersigned by every guildmaster back to the fourth generation."

"But tonight—" Felix heard their own voice climb and forced it flat. "Tonight is circled. In ink that hasn't dried. A century old and it hasn't *dried*. The prophecy names me, or names someone, 'the carver,' and the Sigil burns tonight unless—"

"Unless what?" For the first time Odile leaned forward, and Felix saw the thing underneath the institutional calm, the thing she had spent eleven years pressing down into the bottom of herself: fear. Old fear, worn smooth as a river stone from handling. "Unless the carver does something? Tell me what, Voss. You've read the entry. You've read the whole book, if the reports of your audit habits are accurate. Show me the passage that says what restores the covenant. Show me the one that says what destroys it. Go on. I'll wait."

Felix opened their mouth.

And closed it.

Because they had read the whole book, twice, in the lamplit hours after finding tonight's date, and the prophecy gave no instructions. It gave only a date, a title—*the carver*—and an image: the Sigil at the borderland's heart, wrapped in flame, and the border itself opening like a wound whose stitches have been cut. Restorer or destroyer, the book didn't care which. Fate, apparently, was illiterate when it came to motive.

"You see," Odile said softly, "there is nothing to act *on*. There is only something to be afraid of. And every guildmaster before me looked at this exact page and decided that fear was a private matter, to be managed privately, and that the guild's business—the ledgers, the tokens, the debts owed to the dead and collected from the living—would go on. Because it must go on. Because four hundred souls depend on this hall eating regularly." She sat back. "I am the fifth guildmaster to know, Voss. I will not be the first to pretend otherwise."

"The Compact isn't pretending anything. They burned our wards last night. They offered to buy the book before breakfast. Whatever they're planning—"

"The Compact has been planning for longer than I've been alive. Their founders survived the last burning. Do you understand what that means?" Odile's hands unfolded at last, and Felix saw they were trembling very slightly, which somehow made everything worse. "It means there are people alive—or people whose griefs are still alive—inherited like property—who remember, or believe they remember, what happens when that border tears. They think the covenant is a leash on a wound. They may even be right. And you propose to walk into that with a book you barely understand and a date on the calendar."

"I propose to walk into it with the truth," Felix said. "Which is more than this office has done for a hundred years."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like bone dust settling.

When Odile spoke again, her voice had cooled into the register she used for expulsions and foreclosures. "Here is what happens now. The prophecy book stays in this room. Both copies. Your audit access to the sealed archive is suspended pending a review I will conduct personally and conclude never. If the Compact contacts you again, you will bring the correspondence to me unopened. And you will not leave the city. Is that understood?"

"You can't—"

"I am the Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things, Voss. I can forbid you from acting on a prophecy. What I cannot do is make the prophecy false, or make you safe, or make the Compact lose interest. Those impossibilities are above even my pay." Something moved behind her eyes—briefly, horribly, it might have been apology. "You are a good carver and a better auditor and the loneliest person in this building, and I have watched you for eleven years the way one watches weather. I am telling you, as the only act of kindness available to my office: sit this out. Let tonight pass unremarked. Most prophecies fail, Voss. They fail because ordinary people refuse to perform them. Be ordinary until tomorrow. Please."

*Please.* Felix would turn that word over many times in the years that followed, wondering whether it had cost Odile more than everything else in the room combined.

They stood. The stool scraped. At the door they paused, hand on the frame, not trusting their legs yet.

"The guildmasters before you," Felix said, without turning around. "Five of them, reading that page, closing the drawer. Did it ever occur to any of you that the prophecy says *the carver*? Not *a* carver. Not *the Guildmaster*. Someone with tools and bone dust under their nails, someone who hears the dead whether they want to or not. You've known it was someone like me for a century. You just hoped it would never be anyone in particular."

Behind them, the Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things said nothing at all, and that silence answered everything.

* * *

Felix did not go back to the cellar workshop. Not immediately.

They walked the long way instead, through the guildhall's upper galleries where the resident ghosts kept their accustomed stations—the gray lady who had died waiting for a letter and now read every incoming parcel's address aloud, the drowned pilot who stood eternally at the map table pointing at coastlines that no longer existed, the small child-shape in the stairwell that no one spoke of and everyone stepped around gently. All of them felt thinner today. Washed-out. As though the scorched wards on the eastern wall had taken something from the air that the dead needed too.

Maren was in her usual place by the archive door, folded into the corner of the alcove like a garment hung up carefully. The eldest ghost of the guildhall had been a wardkeeper in life, three centuries dead, and she manifested mostly as a suggestion of shawled shoulders and two points of attention where eyes should be. Last night she had told Felix to burn the book. She had refused, with the other ghosts, to say why, and had gone silent as a locked crypt when asked about the carver.

"Maren."

The suggestion of shoulders shifted. The points of attention fixed on Felix, and in them Felix read something they had never seen in a ghost before: pity.

"She showed you hers," Maren said. Her voice was dry paper, tide-flattened weed. "The second copy. Yes. I felt the drawer open. We feel everything in this building, carver. Every drawer. Every lie. Every silence, most of all—the silences ring loudest."

"Then tell me what the silence is hiding. Five guildmasters have known. A hundred years. And none of them—" Felix stopped, pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until sparks bloomed. When they lowered them, the ghost hadn't moved, but she seemed closer, the way ghosts did when they were gathering themselves to say something heavy. "You told me to burn the book. I'm asking you plainly: burn it and then what? Tonight still comes. The date doesn't care what I do with paper. So either you tell me what you know, Maren, or you watch me walk out of this building and do something stupid and brave and probably fatal, alone, and you'll feel that too, won't you? Every minute of it."

For a long moment the eldest ghost of the guildhall said nothing. Then, softly, in the voice of someone setting down a burden carried three hundred years:

"He had your hands."

Felix went cold from the soles up. "Whose."

"The carver. The one who broke it last time, at the last burning, when the covenant was forged and then betrayed in the same hour—I don't remember his face. That's the mercy or the cruelty of being dead; some things are taken from us whether we consent or no. But I remember his hands." Maren's own rose, translucent, turning slowly in the alcove's dimness as if cupping something invisible. "Steady. Precise. A scar across the left palm, here—" the ghost's finger traced a line across her own ghost-palm, "—from the first time a blade slipped, which every carver gets, which you have, which I have watched you rub absently when you balance difficult accounts." The points of attention lifted to Felix's face. "He stood where you're standing. He heard us the way you hear us. And he went out that door toward the borderland carrying relics he barely understood, because a book told him to, and what came back—" the voice frayed, "—what came back was not enough of him to bury."

The gallery was very quiet. Somewhere below, the drowned pilot pointed at his vanished coastline, faithful beyond death to a job nobody paid him for anymore.

"The guildmasters' silences," Maren said, "are cowardice. Mine is grief, and it is older than theirs, and I am done paying its interest." She drifted nearer, and the cold of her passed through Felix like winter remembering them. "You will open the archive again tonight. You will find what five guildmasters chose not to look for, because looking requires admitting the drawer exists. Start with the personal effects ledger. The oldest volume. The one bound in the same hide as the prophecy book, because they were made by the same hands, in the same year, by a carver who knew exactly what was coming and left instructions anyway."

"Why didn't you tell me this last night?"

"Because last night I still believed the fifth guildmaster would be braver than the first four." The ghost's attention dropped, briefly, to Felix's left palm. To the scar. "I'm sorry, carver. Burn the book if you can. But if you can't—if it's truly tonight—then go find what he left you. Before the Compact finds it first."

* * *

The cellar workshop received Felix like a lungful held too long, finally exhaled. Bone dust in the lamplight. The familiar clutter of chisels and half-finished tokens, debts to the dead pinned in rows like moths. Here, at least, nothing had changed. Here, at least, Felix knew their own hands.

They took the keepsake bone from around their neck—the femur sliver they'd carried so long it had worn warm against the sternum, the one piece of their past before the guild that they owned outright, carved with a pattern they'd never been able to identify—and set it on the workbench under the lamp.

Maren had said: start with the personal effects ledger. The oldest volume.

That would mean going back up. Past the scorched wards, past the gray lady and her endless parcels, past Odile's door with its drawer of eloquent nothing. That would mean defying the Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things within sight of sundown, on the very date circled in ink that wouldn't dry, with the Ashen Compact's card still smoldering in their pocket and assassins' patience wearing thin somewhere across the city.

Felix looked down at the keepsake bone lying in the pool of lamplight. And frowned.

The lamp sat where it always sat. The angle was the same as ten thousand evenings before it. But the carved pattern on the bone's face had thrown its shadow differently tonight—or perhaps Felix had simply never looked properly, never looked with an auditor's eye instead of a mourner's—and in the raking light the grooves stood up proud and unmistakable, and Felix saw, for the first time, the fine parallel chatter inside each cut.

Tool-marks. Their own marks—no mistaking them; every carver's hand stutters in a signature deeper than habit, down in the wrist, down in the marrow. The spacing. The pressure. The tiny tremor at each stroke's end that Felix had been told for twenty years was theirs alone.

Except these cuts were older. The bone's surface had yellowed and microscopically slumped around the grooves, the way carved bone does over decades, over a lifetime, over a century.

Felix picked it up with fingers that did not feel like their own, and held their inheritance to the light, and somewhere far above, muffled by floorboards and a hundred years of institutional silence, the evening bells began to count down what remained of the day.
