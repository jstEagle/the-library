## 6. Bone That Isn't Mine

The cellar workshop had its own weather. Rain above, in the world of the living, meant damp rising through the stone below, and the small iron brazier Felix kept burning did little against it except push the chill into the corners where it waited like a patient creditor. Felix had worked in this room for eleven years—long enough to know every crack in the flagstones, every place a draught slipped in to gutter the candle—and tonight they knew none of it was enough. The wards on the guildhall were scorched. The Ashen Compact knew their name. And Guildmaster Odile had said *do nothing* in a voice that expected obedience the way a coffin expects a body: passively, permanently.

So Felix carved.

It was what they always did when thinking failed them, when dread sat too heavy on the chest. Bone-work was rhythm, and rhythm was shelter. They had laid out the night's materials on the scarred workbench: three ribs from a cow that had died of old age and been paid for honestly, a bowl of saltwater, the fine steel burins rolled in flannel, and the token itself half-born under their hands—a protective piece for a fishmonger's widow on Chandler's Lane whose dead husband had taken to standing at the foot of her bed and weeping without sound. Grief-hauntings were common and mostly harmless, but this one had begun to frost the windows from the inside, and frost in summer frightened people. A warding token would settle him. It would not send him on—the guild did not traffic in sending-on; that was precisely the covenant's whole impossible business—but it would give him a boundary, a courtesy of distance, until something kinder could be arranged.

Felix held the rib up to the lamplight and turned it, reading the grain the way a sailor reads water. There. A faint seam where the marrow had run thin. The design should follow the flaw, not fight it. They set the burin's tip against the bone and began the first cut of the Lattice of Quiet Hands, the oldest pattern in their repertoire, the one Master Corvane had drilled into them in their first year: *the tool moves like breath, Voss. In. Out. Never hold it. Held tools slip, and slipped tools teach.*

The burin whispered along the bone. Curl after pale curl fell away. Felix breathed with the cuts, and the breathing worked as it always worked, narrowing the world down to the width of an edge and the length of a stroke, until the scorched wards and the fresh-inked prophecy and Odile's closed door all shrank to things that existed somewhere far above, on the other side of the ceiling, in another country entirely.

*In. Out. Never hold it.*

They were four rows into the lattice when their hand stuttered.

Not a slip—the blade never touched flesh—but a hitch, a half-beat where the fingers moved before the mind had told them to. Felix stared down at the token. The fourth row of the lattice was perfect. Better than perfect. It was not the way Felix carved the Lattice of Quiet Hands. Every apprentice learned the standard form: seven strokes to a row, the seventh a closing hook that locked the pattern down. What lay under their burin now had eight strokes, the eighth a small open spiral, left unfinished by design, a door deliberately left ajar.

Felix had never seen that variation in their life.

Except—they had. Somewhere. The shape of it sat in their hands the way a path sits in the feet of someone who has walked it home ten thousand times, and the knowing did not feel like learning. It felt like remembering. Their pulse ticked up, and they made themselves finish the row slowly, watching their own fingers the way one watches a stranger who has begun doing something alarming in a public street.

The hands finished the spiral and reached, without instruction, for the keepsake.

Felix's other hand was already resting on it. That was the strange part—not that they had picked it up, but that they could not remember picking it up. The keepsake bone lay across their palm where it always lay during hard nights: a finger-length sliver of femur, polished by decades of handling, worn smooth as riverglass. It was the oldest thing they owned. It had come to them, as near as they could reckon, with themselves—with whatever ragged self had woken at fourteen in Master Corvane's workshop with no history, no family name worth keeping, and a single bone clenched in one fist so tightly the marks of their own teeth were dented in the wood of their own thumb. *Yours*, Corvane had said, when asked. *It came with you. Bones usually do.*

Felix had carved their first tokens holding it. Had slept with it through every fever. Had pressed it to their lips, once, shamefully, at nineteen, the way other people kissed relics of saints. It was the only proof they had ever existed before the age of fourteen, and they had never once thought to examine it closely, because you do not interrogate the ground you stand on.

Now, with the eighth-stroke spiral still tingling in their fingertips, they carried it to the lamp.

They held it under the focused flame-light, turning it, and fetched the loupe from its drawer—the brass-ringed lens they used for verifying forgeries—and looked.

Bone carries its history the way parchment does. Every tool that touches it leaves a signature, as individual as handwriting: the angle of attack, the depth of the stroke, the tremor or steadiness of the hand, the particular way a carver eases pressure at the end of a cut. Felix had spent eleven years reading these signatures in other people's work. They could match a token to its maker across a crowded market of fakes. It was, in fact, the skill the guild valued most in them, and the reason a nobody with no past had risen to keeper of the ledgers at twenty-five.

Under the loupe, faint as breath on winter glass, the keepsake bone bore marks.

Tool-marks. Old ones—not the polish-worn surface but beneath it, in the deeper grain, ghost-signs of carving done long ago and later smoothed away, the way a palimpsest remembers its first text. Someone had carved this bone, and then someone had sanded those carvings out, carefully, lovingly, leaving only these shadows.

And the shadow-signature was Felix's.

There was no mistaking it. They checked three times, then a fourth with the lamp brought close enough to sting. The angle of attack was theirs—slightly steep, left-handed compensation for a childhood scar on the right thumb. The easing at the end of each stroke was theirs, that almost imperceptible lift in the last hair's-breadth that Master Corvane had called *your one virtue*. Even the tremor was theirs, the tiny hesitation before curved cuts that Felix had never managed to train out and had long ago stopped apologizing for. These were not marks made by a similar hand. These were marks made by *this* hand—or by a hand so exactly its twin that the distinction stopped meaning anything.

The bone was old. Older than Felix. Older than Corvane, likely. The femur of a person long dead—and yet it carried the handwriting of the living woman holding it, written before she was born.

Felix set the loupe down very carefully, as if it might go off.

*You are tired*, they told themselves. *You have not slept properly since the archive. You are seeing your own fears in sandpaper scars.* But even as they thought it, their thumb moved over the bone's surface, tracing the ghost-spiral near its base—an eighth-stroke spiral, open, unfinished, identical to the one their hand had just invented—and the touch went through the mark like a key going through a lock.

The cellar went away.

---

Lamplight—but not theirs. Colder light, white as scraped tallow, falling from somewhere high, and beneath Felix's feet a floor of black stone so polished it showed the sky. There was a sky to show: vast, starless, the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges, and across it, filling half of heaven, stood the Sigil.

They had never seen it. No one living had. The guild taught its apprentices the covenant's doctrine in careful abstractions—the Sigil as concept, as equation, as the great sign burned into the border between the living realm and the dead, the seal that let the two touch without tearing. But abstraction was nothing against the thing itself. It rose from the borderland like a tree of fire that had forgotten how to burn, a sigil indeed in the oldest sense—letters interlocked, a word written in a language made of light, each stroke of it tall as a cathedral and humming with a note felt in the teeth rather than heard by the ear. It was beautiful the way a wound in the world is beautiful. It was the hinge of everything.

And Felix stood before it, and knew it—not as a scholar knows a famous building, but the way you know your own front door. Their feet ached. They had walked a long way to reach it. Their right hand hung heavy at their side, and when they raised it, they saw the burin there—not the steel burin of the workshop but a greater thing, a carving-tool with a haft of grey bone and an edge that gave off no reflection at all, drinking the light instead of returning it.

Someone was speaking. A voice beside them, an old voice, cracked and fond: *It isn't too late to turn back, carver.*

*I know,* Felix heard themself answer—and the voice that answered was theirs, and was not, was lower and rougher, worn at the edges like a coin passed through too many palms. *That's why I came.*

*The covenant holds. It has held a hundred years.*

*A hundred years is long enough to ask what it costs.* Their hand tightened on the impossible tool. *You've seen the ledgers same as I have. Debts owed to the dead that can never be paid because the dead can never leave. We built a door and threw away the key and called it mercy. It isn't mercy, Maren. It's a cage with the latch painted on.*

*Maren.* The name rang through the fragment like a bell through fog, and Felix, suspended in the memory, felt their living heart lurch—for Maren was the eldest ghost of the guildhall, the one who had told them, only days ago, to burn the prophecy book, the one who had gone silent when asked about the carver—

The old voice again, sorrowing: *And if you're wrong?*

*Then I'll be wrong,* said the other-Felix, the older voice, the one wearing Felix's hands, *and I'll pay for it. That's what a debt is.*

They stepped forward. The Sigil's hum filled their skull. They set the edge of the unreflecting tool against the nearest stroke of burning light, and the light parted for it like flesh for a knife, and Felix opened their mouth—to cry stop, to warn, to do anything at all—

The cellar came back.

---

Felix was on the floor. They did not remember falling. The brazier had burned low; the candle had drowned in its own wax; the half-carved token lay somewhere under their hand, and the keepsake bone was still clenched in their fist so hard the edges had printed their palm red. Outside, rain still fell. The guildhall creaked above them, ordinary and oblivious. Time had passed—how much? An hour? Minutes? The memory had taken them whole, the way deep water takes a stone.

They sat up slowly, and their body reported itself in detail: shaking hands, a mouth dry as old paper, tears on their face that they had no memory of making. On the floor beside them, the warding token glinted up with its eight-stroke spirals, five rows of a pattern that did not exist, carved in a hand that was and was not theirs.

*Maren*, Felix said aloud, and their voice cracked in the empty cellar. *Maren, you knew me.*

Or knew *him*. Knew the other one. The carver. The one the ghosts would not speak of, the one the prophecy book named, the one who had stood before the Sigil a hundred years ago complaining about ledgers—in Felix's own cadence, with Felix's own habit of hiding fear behind bookkeeping—and had cut the binding light anyway, believing themself merciful, believing themself right.

And the Sigil still stood. Felix had seen it whole in the memory. So whatever the carver had done that day, it had not ended the covenant—only wounded it, perhaps. Only started a debt that had come due tonight, on a date circled in ink a century old.

Felix got to their feet by way of the workbench, legs unsteady, and stood a moment with both hands flat on the scarred wood, breathing the way they told frightened clients to breathe. The keepsake bone lay on the bench among the shavings. They did not pick it up. They could not, quite yet. It sat there gleaming in the brazier-light like something that had been waiting a hundred years for them to finally look at it—which, they understood now, was exactly what it had been.

Every fact they had about themselves rearranged itself, quietly and horribly, into a new order. Woken at fourteen with no past. A talent for bone that had arrived fully formed, needing no teaching, only refining. An instinct for reading tool-marks so uncanny that Corvane had joked—had *joked*—that Felix must have carved half the tokens in the city in some former life. And the bone that came with them, pre-polished by decades of a stranger's handling, carrying their own signature in its depths.

Not a keepsake. Evidence.

Felix crossed to the ledger shelf—the true ledgers, the ones only they maintained—and pulled the oldest volume, the one recording debts inherited rather than incurred, obligations passed down through generations of the guild's clients. It was guild doctrine that such debts bound bloodlines, not souls. But doctrine had been wrong about everything else this week.

Their own name was not in it. Of course not; they had no lineage, no name worth the ink. But their thumb found the page-edge on its own, the way their hand had found the burin, and stopped at an entry near the middle, in faded brown ink, dated a hundred years and some months past:

*Voss, Vesper — carver, second chair — account closed by act of the account-holder. All debts transferred. Let the record show she knew the cost and chose it freely.*

Below, in a different hand, cramped and hurried, as if written by someone afraid of being interrupted:

*She will come back to finish the work. They always do.*

Felix read it three times. The rain kept on. Somewhere above, the guildhall clock struck two, and the sound fell down the stairwell into the cellar like a stone dropped into a well.

Vesper Voss. Voss was the name Corvane had given them, pulled off a shipping manifest because an orphan needed something to be called. But it was also—apparently—a name with a history, a name that had stood in this guildhall a century ago and held a chair at the carvers' bench and closed its own account with a knife of grey bone.

*I'll be wrong, and I'll pay for it. That's what a debt is.*

The debt had come due. The transfer had been executed. Felix looked down at their own hands—carver's hands, calloused in the old pattern, scarred on the right thumb since before they could remember acquiring the scar—and understood, with the cold clarity of a ledger finally balanced, that they were not being hunted for what they knew.

They were being hunted for what they owed.

And if they wanted to learn the size of the debt—if they wanted the rest of the memory, the ending the vision had refused them—they would have to ask the one person in the guildhall who had stood beside the carver on that final day, and who had spent the last hundred years refusing to speak of it.

Felix banked the brazier, took up the lamp, and started for the stairs, toward the cold gallery where the eldest ghost kept her silence.

Maren owed them an answer. And Felix Voss—whoever Felix Voss was—had come to collect.
