## 7. Flight Across the Saltmarsh

The wards went first.

Felix felt it in their teeth—a high, sour note like a tuning fork struck against bone, rising from every threshold of the guildhall at once. They had been in the cellar workshop for six hours, carving a debt-token for a drowned fisherman's widow, and the knife was still in their hand when the note broke and became something else: a sound like a great door being kicked open somewhere far above.

The token dropped. The knife clattered into the shavings. Felix stood very still in the lamplight and listened the way they had taught themselves to listen since childhood—not with ears, but with the thin membrane of attention that separated them from the dead. Through it now came not voices but pressure. A crowd of the living, moving with purpose through rooms that had been quiet for a hundred years.

*Compact,* thought Felix, and the word tasted of scorched wardstone.

They had known this might come. Since refusing the envoy's velvet-voiced offer, since waking to find the guildhall's protective sigils blistered and black as if something had licked them with fire, some part of Felix had been waiting. Waiting, and doing nothing—because Odile had forbidden action, and Felix had obeyed, and obedience sat in their stomach now like a swallowed stone.

The lamp guttered. Not wind—the flame leaned *away* from the cellar door, toward the stair, as if trying to flee ahead of what was coming down it.

Felix grabbed the ledger.

It was instinct, pure and stupid, the reflex of a lifetime's bookkeeping. Then they caught themselves—*the ledger is worthless, the ledger is only debts*—and reached instead for the prophecy book where it lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard, beside Maren's warning and three nights of sleeplessness. Their hands shook so badly the wrapping tore. The book came free smelling faintly of fresh ink, that impossible freshness, tonight's date still circled inside like a noose drawn around a throat.

Above them, footsteps crossed the workshop floor. Deliberate. Unhurried. Whoever walked up there knew the building was theirs already.

There was one other way out of the cellar. Every old guildhall had one; the Guild of Impossible Things had simply never bothered to wall off theirs because, as Odile liked to say, no one sensible flees into the saltmarsh. The trapdoor behind the drying racks opened onto a crawl-space that surfaced in the tannery shed at the marsh's edge. Felix had used it twice before—for smuggling, in their younger years, when the guild's accounts required certain transactions to happen outside the light of its own windows.

They took the Covenant Key from its case on the shelf.

It was smaller than legend suggested—a rod of grey-green metal no longer than a forearm, cold enough to burn, wrapped in leather so ancient it crumbled at Felix's touch. When their bare fingers closed around the metal core, the dead spoke. Not words. A single sustained tone, low as a bell heard through water, as if every ghost the Key had ever bound to the living world were humming through it at once.

*Take me home,* the tone seemed to say. Or perhaps: *I am the way home.* The Key said both things at once, the way all relics did, the way the prophecy book dated itself a century back and smelled of ink mixed an hour ago.

Boots on the cellar stairs now. Three pairs, maybe four.

Felix shoved the book inside their coat, wrapped the Key in the ruined leather, and went through the trapdoor into the dark.

* * *

The crawl-space was clay-walled and root-ribbed and smelled of the marsh even here, forty feet inland. Felix moved on elbows and knees, the Key pressed against their sternum, its cold bleeding through wool and linen into skin until it felt less like carrying an object than carrying a second heart—one that beat slow and glacial and utterly indifferent to fear.

Behind them, muffled through earth, the guildhall made noises Felix would remember for the rest of their life. Furniture overturned. Glass burst. And then a voice, raised in command, flat with bureaucratic cruelty:

"Search the ledgers first. She'll want the accounts."

*She.* The envoy, then. Or whoever the envoy served. Felix crawled faster, elbows scraping root-cuts, and tried not to think about the ghosts bound to those halls—the eleven spirits who kept the archive honest, who argued over the Sigil's forging in their different remembered tongues, who had each gone silent when asked about the carver. The Compact would unbind them or worse. There were worse things than unbinding. Felix had carved the tokens for such rites once and never spoken of it.

A memory surfaced unbidden: Maren, the eldest ghost, her form like smoke poured into the shape of a woman who had died old and angry and right. *Burn the book,* she had told Felix, her voice the sound of pages turning in an empty room. *Whatever you think you are in it, burn it.*

Felix had not burned it. Felix was crawling through mud with it against their ribs instead.

The crawl-space ended at a wooden hatch crusted with salt-bloom. Felix pushed it open into the tannery shed, into air that hit like a slap—brine and rot and the sweet-rotten stink of the kelp vats, and under it all the vast wet breathing of the saltmarsh itself, miles of it, silver-grey under a moon half-eaten by cloud.

No pursuit yet. But the guildhall's windows glowed wrong—too bright, flickering in rhythms fire does not make—and silhouettes moved across the glass with the unhurried confidence of people doing thorough work.

Felix ran.

The marsh took them the way it took everything: gradually, then completely. The ground went from firm shingle to sucking peat within fifty paces, each step a negotiation with mud that wanted to keep what it caught. Channels of black water threaded everywhere, some ankle-deep, some bottomless. Salt-crusted grass hissed against Felix's trousers. The moon gave just enough light to show the next ten feet and no more, and beyond that the marsh simply dissolved into fog and the sound of water moving in places water should not be.

Twice Felix went down. Twice the mud tried, gently, patiently, to swallow them, and twice Felix hauled themselves out by fistfuls of samphire and kept running—because behind them now, faint but unmistakable, there were lights in the tannery shed. Lanterns. Voices carrying flat and clear across the water.

"They went for the marsh." Almost amused. "Let the marsh have them, then. We take the hall and wait."

But a third voice, quieter, said something Felix couldn't hear, and the lanterns did not turn back.

* * *

Felix stopped running when the guildhall's glow sank below the horizon-line of reeds, which took longer than it should have, because the marsh is flat and grief makes distance strange. They stood calf-deep in a channel, chest heaving, and let themselves feel it in pieces, because feeling it whole would have put them face-down in the water.

The guildhall. Eleven hundred years old. The archive with its sealed shelves, the workshop with its bone-dust smell, the ledger-room where Felix had spent more waking hours than anywhere else on earth. Ransacked. Held. *She'll want the accounts*—as if a century of impossible debts were loot.

And Maren. The Compact would find Maren first, if they could see ghosts at all, and Felix did not know whether they could. Some of them could. The envoy had looked at Felix with eyes that tracked slightly too far left, as if watching someone stand just behind the shoulder.

"Don't," Felix said aloud, to themselves, to the marsh, to whatever was listening. "Not now."

Because the Key had gone warm.

Against their chest, through the ruined leather, the Covenant Key had shifted from cold to a heat like a held breath, and its hum—that low submerged bell-tone—had changed key. It rose. It resolved. And Felix, who had spent their whole life hearing the dead as a background weather, realized with a lurch that the Key was not humming to itself.

It was answering something.

Ahead of them, out in the fog where the marsh met the borderland, something hummed back.

* * *

Maren found them an hour later, or an hour after that; time moves strangely in saltmarsh, stretched thin between tides. Felix had waded to a shingle bar—a rare dry spine of land—and sat with their back against a leaning marker-stone older than any guild, one of the boundary stones from the original pact, its carvings worn to suggestions. The prophecy book lay open on their knees despite everything, because dread is a reader too and keeps turning pages. The blank leaves at the end stared up at them. After tonight, nothing. As if the century-old prophet had run out of certainty precisely where Felix was standing.

"You're sitting on my grave-marker's cousin," said a voice like dry paper. "Third stone to the left. I helped carve it, before I needed it."

Felix's head came up so fast their neck cracked. "Maren."

She stood at the edge of the shingle bar where the fog began, and she was thinner than Felix had ever seen her. Ghosts were always thinning and thickening with the years and the remembering, but Maren looked like smoke after the fire is out—present, coherent, and visibly spending herself to be so.

"You shouldn't be out here." Felix stood, mud sucking at their boots. "You're bound to the hall. The binding—"

"The Compact broke my binding an hour ago." Maren smiled without warmth. "They meant it as violence. They pulled every anchor-thread they could find in the archive. Most of the others scattered—back to their haunts, their graves, wherever the dead go when you cut their leash. I stayed near. I'm good at staying near. I've been staying near things for two hundred years."

"But your binding—"

"I unbound myself, child. Before they could do it badly." She drifted closer, and where she passed the salt-grass bent though there was no wind. "There's a difference between being freed and being torn loose. I chose. It costs. Everything costs—you know that better than anyone, keeper of unpayable accounts."

Felix's throat closed. "How much?"

"Enough that I won't be lingering after this conversation." Maren said it lightly, the way she had delivered every hard truth since Felix first met her ghost in the archive stacks, nineteen and terrified and pretending otherwise. "So listen. You have the Key. Good. Don't open it, don't answer it, don't hum along no matter how it begs. It's been calling to you since you picked it up because it knows what you are—or what you were, or whose you are—and relics are terrible gossips."

"It called to something out there too. In the fog. Toward the borderland."

"That will be the ferryman." Maren's form flickered, and for a moment Felix saw through her to the reeds bending behind. "He'll want a toll. He always wants a toll, and his tolls are always the same currency, and you're carrying less of it than you think."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean I've watched you for years, Felix Voss, and I've seen the shape of what's missing. You remember your childhood in pieces that don't fit together. You remember learning to carve but never learning it. Your memories have seams, and seams mean patching, and patching means someone took the original cloth." Her voice gentled into something almost like tenderness. "When the ferryman takes his toll, he won't take at random. He takes what's loosest. What's borrowed. And you need to survive him anyway, because the Key has to reach the Sigil, and the Sigil stands past his crossing, and I am about to spend my last coin telling you why."

The fog pressed close. Somewhere out in it, faintly, the Key hummed against Felix's chest, and something answered.

"Maren—" Felix's voice cracked. "Don't go."

"I've been dead for two centuries, dear. Going is the one thing I haven't tried." But she drifted nearer still, close enough that her cold washed over Felix like the shadow of a cloud, and her smoke-thin face arranged itself into the expression Felix had trusted most in the world: the look of a woman about to be exactly right. "Three things. First: the Compact wants the covenant broken, but their masters want it broken *by you*. The prophecy names the carver as restorer or destroyer, and either ending serves them while the book stays closed. So open it. Read it wrong if you must, read it sideways, but don't let them choose your chapter for you."

"Second?"

"The other ghosts went silent when you asked about the carver because we weren't silent. We were *silenced*—bound by the same old oath that binds the guildmasters to inaction. Odile isn't a coward, Felix. She's a lock. And locks can't tell you what's inside them." Maren's edges were fraying now, wisps of her peeling away into the fog and not returning. "Third thing. Last thing."

Her voice dropped to almost nothing, and Felix leaned in, straining, mud cold around their ankles, the whole grey marsh holding its breath.

"When you meet the carver—and you will meet the carver—don't ask what they did. Ask what they saw. Everyone else asked what they did."

"Maren, wait—"

"I bought you an hour once, in the archive, when the envoy came sniffing. I told you I'd mislaid a ledger. This is the last hour I can buy." She was mostly fog now, a shape like a woman remembered rather than seen, and her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from two hundred years of staying near. "Grief is just love with nowhere to put itself, child. So put it somewhere. Put it in the walking. Put it in the work. Sharpen yourself on it and come out the far side of this marsh with an edge, because what's waiting past the ferryman will test every account you've ever kept—"

The fog took the rest. Or the tide did. Felix stood alone on the shingle bar with the marker-stone at their back and the prophecy book open to its blank final pages, and the place where Maren had been held the faintest warmth, like a lamp recently carried through a cold room.

Felix did not cry. They had learned long ago that crying in the saltmarsh wastes water the body needs. Instead they closed the book, pressed it flat against the Key, and stood for a long moment with both hands over their heart as if swearing an oath on them both.

Then they spoke to the empty fog, quietly, in the tone they used for closing accounts.

"Debt acknowledged. Maren. Two hours' counsel, one final unbinding, freely given." Their voice steadied with each word, the old ledger-rhythm, the litany that made unbearable arithmetic bearable. "Payment pending. To be rendered at the reforging. Interest: I will finish what you started."

The marsh said nothing. But the Key, against Felix's chest, hummed a low agreement—and far out in the fog, where the black channels braided together into water that reflected no moon, a lantern kindled. One small amber point, swaying at the rhythm of oars.

A bell rang once, patient, across the water. The sound of a fare being requested.

Felix Voss buttoned their coat over book and relic, turned toward the light, and began to walk.

* * *

The ferryman's boat emerged from the fog before the ferryman did: a long dark hull of wood gone silver with age, riding low in the black channel, lantern hanging from a curved prow-post carved—Felix's stomach tightened—in the shape of a hand holding scales. The boat arrived without wake, without sound of oars, though the oars were there, resting in their locks, dripping.

The ferryman stood at the stern. He wore the marsh the way Maren had worn death: completely. Reeds grew from the shoulders of his coat. Water beaded on him without ever falling. His face was the difficult part—it was everyone's face, faintly, the way a crowd is every face, and looking at it too long made Felix's own reflection swim up in memory uninvited.

"Passenger," he said. His voice was the bell, the bell was his voice. "Bound for the borderland."

"The Sigil," Felix said.

"The Sigil stands past the borderland. The borderland stands past my crossing. Nothing stands past the toll." He extended one hand, palm up, and it was an ordinary gesture, a toll-keeper's gesture, and it was the most frightening thing Felix had seen all night. "Payment."

Felix's hand went to the coat—to the book, to the Key—and stopped. *His tolls are always the same currency.* Memory. The loosest, most borrowed thing aboard them.

"How will I know," Felix asked slowly, "what I've paid? Afterward?"

"You won't," said the ferryman, with what might have been mercy. "That is the design."

Out in the fog behind him, something vast shifted—the borderland breathing, or the Sigil burning already in some tense that had not happened yet. The lantern swayed. The oars dripped. And Felix Voss, keeper of impossible accounts, stepped forward to name their price before it could be named for them.

"One payment," Felix said. "Freely given. Witnessed by the Key. Now row."
