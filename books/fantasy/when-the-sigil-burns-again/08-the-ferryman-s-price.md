## 8. The Ferryman's Price

The saltmarsh ended where the world forgot to be solid.

Felix had walked since the grey hour before dawn, boots sinking into peat that sucked at each step like a reluctant goodbye, and all the while the marsh had grown quieter in the wrong way. Not silent—saltmarshes were never silent, full of reed-whistle and bird-argument and the tick of brine in the mud—but quieter *inside*, as though the land were pressing a finger to its own lips. By midmorning Felix realized they had not heard their own footsteps for an hour. The ground swallowed sound the way it swallowed water, and it was swallowing something else too: memory, maybe. Certainty. The particular confidence of a person who knew where they were going.

They still had the Covenant Key wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, warm as a sleeping bird. They still had the prophecy book, dry despite everything, in its satchel at their hip. And they had Maren's last words, spent now like a coin they could never take back—*go north until the water forgets which way is down*—which sounded like ghost-nonsense until Felix crested the final low ridge of the marsh and saw exactly what she'd meant.

The water did forget.

Ahead lay a wide estuary, flat and pewter-colored, and it did not flow toward the sea or away from it. It simply lay there, perfectly still, holding no reflection of the overcast sky. Instead it held reflections of things that were not present: towers with lit windows, a road crowded with figures, a garden gone to seed under a sun that wasn't shining. The borderland, glimpsed sideways, the way you glimpse your own face in a spoon. Where the estuary met the shore stood a single wooden jetty, black with age, and moored to it a boat that looked less built than *grown*, its planks curving like ribs, its prow carved into the suggestion of a face with closed eyes.

And on the jetty sat the ferryman.

He was small—smaller than Felix expected, a wiry old man in a coat of layered oilskins gone green with weather, mending a net with fingers that moved without hurry, without pause, as if he had been doing this since before nets were invented. He did not look up when Felix's boots crunched onto the shingle. He did not look up when Felix stopped three paces away. He finished his knot, tested it, and then said, in a voice like gravel poured slowly:

"You're the third carver I've ferried."

Felix's mouth went dry. "The other two—"

"Are not your business." He looked up then, and his eyes were the color of the estuary: pewter, flat, reflecting things that weren't there. "Your business is the toll."

"I have coin."

"Coin." He said the word the way a physician says a symptom. "Coin buys passage on rivers that go somewhere. This one goes between. Between costs more than metal." He set down his net and folded his hands, and Felix noticed that his knuckles were scarred in long parallel lines, like someone had tried to carve him once and given up halfway. "It costs a memory. One. Chosen by me, not you. You'll cross knowing what you paid only in the way you know a tooth is missing—the gap aches, but the tooth is gone."

Felix had known this was coming. Maren had told them enough about the border crossings, in the years of quiet conversations in the guildhall's upper rooms, and the old stories all agreed. But knowing and hearing were different animals, and hearing made Felix's hands cold inside their gloves.

"What if I refuse?"

"Then you walk the shoreline until the shoreline walks you," the ferryman said placidly. "There are people on that beach who've been circling for thirty years. Wave to them sometime. Some of them wave back." He jerked his chin toward the far end of the shingle, and Felix, following the gesture, saw them—shapes standing at the tideline, motionless, facing the water. At this distance they might have been posts. They might have been anything.

Felix breathed out slowly. Thought of Odile forbidding them to act. Thought of the wards scorched black, of assassins' boots in the guildhall corridor, of Maren unraveling into light with her hand pressed to Felix's shoulder blade, pushing them out the door. Thought of the prophecy book against their hip, tonight's date circled in ink that smelled freshly cut.

"One memory," Felix said. "Chosen by you."

"Chosen by me." The ferryman rose, joints crackling like green wood in fire, and studied Felix with unnerving thoroughness—the face, the hands, the chest where the Key lay hidden. His gaze lingered there, and something flickered across the carved prow of the boat, as though it too had opened one eye. "You smell interesting, carver. Like a house where someone's moved out but left the furniture." He smiled, and the smile had too many practical uses for the teeth involved. "Board."

*

The crossing was worse than the waiting.

The boat slid off the jetty without sail or oar, drawn by nothing, and the moment it left the shallows the estuary stopped pretending to be water. It became a mirror lying on its side, and the boat traveled along the surface of every reflection at once. Felix watched towers pass beneath them—real towers, lit windows, faces looking up from streets—and understood that these were the memories of everyone who had ever crossed here, sunk into the water like coins into a well. The dead river kept what the living paid.

The ferryman rowed anyway. Felix suspected he rowed out of habit, or ceremony, or simple hatred of idleness. The oars bit into reflections and came up dry.

"It doesn't hurt," he said conversationally, as though continuing a conversation begun hours ago. "That's what people always ask. Does it hurt. No. That's the trap of it. A pain would teach you something. This just—" he made a gesture with one scarred hand, opening it like a flower, empty, "—leaves."

"How do you choose?" Felix asked. "Which one to take?"

"Experience." The flat eyes glinted. "You've been losing already. Don't look so surprised—I can read a ledger as well as any guild clerk. There are gaps in you, carver. Big ones, recent ones, and older ones underneath, worn smooth like stones in a streambed. Someone's been skimming your account for years." He tilted his head. "Or you've been spending it. Hard to tell the difference, sometimes. From outside."

Felix's hand went to their chest, to the keepsake bone they wore beneath their shirt—the bone with tool-marks in a hand identical to their own, older, the bone that had shown them a fragment of a life that was not theirs: standing before the Sigil itself, stone towering, hands raised. Their hands? Someone's hands.

"I know what I've lost," Felix said carefully. "Recently. Since the book. But before—"

"Before is none of my affair." The ferryman shipped the oars. Ahead, the far shore was resolving out of haze—a shoreline of dark stone, and beyond it a country the color of ash and ember both, a city built on the slope of a dead volcano or a living one, chimneys bleeding smoke the wrong direction, upward and inward at once. Ashen territory. The Compact's city. "Though I'll say this for free, because I'm generous and it amuses me: most people who come through here asking after their past are running from it. The clever ones ask after someone else's."

The boat touched the far shore with a sound like a book closing.

The ferryman stood. He was suddenly much taller than he had been, or the shore was lower, or perspective had simply given up on this side of the water. He extended his scarred hand, palm up, and waited.

"Now?" Felix's voice came out smaller than intended. "Here?"

"The toll is taken at the threshold. Always has been. It's cleaner that way—you pay on arrival, so you can't flee home thinking better of it." His fingers curled slightly, beckoning. "Give me your hand, carver. And think of nothing. Thinking only tells me where to dig."

Every instinct Felix owned screamed at once. Every story, every warning, Maren's voice in the archive saying *burn the book*, Odile's silence, the whole accumulated caution of a person who had survived by keeping accounts exact. To open oneself to a stranger on the threshold of the deathlands, to let him reach into the one vault that could not be relocked—

But the Sigil burned tonight. The prophecy had been patient for a hundred years and would not extend its deadline for cowardice.

Felix pulled off the glove and took the ferryman's hand.

His grip was dry and strong and *cold*, cold as the space between stars, and it did not hurt—it was true, it didn't hurt, that was the horror of it—there was only a gentle pressure behind the eyes, like a librarian's finger tracing spines, considering, selecting. Felix felt him browse. Felt attention slide across childhoods, across the cellar workshop and its lamplight, across the ledgers and the tokens and the humming client-payment, across Maren's light going out—

*Not those,* the pressure seemed to say, almost regretful. *Those are load-bearing.*

And then it settled, with the delicacy of a pickpocket lifting a watch, on something older. Something Felix had not thought of in years precisely because they had never thought of it at all—it surfaced whole and bright for one instant, one single instant of perfect recall, like a window flung open in a wall Felix hadn't known was there:

A woman's kitchen. Steam on the glass. Someone singing badly, off-key and joyful, a song about a fisherman and a faithless sea. The smell of bread. A name being called—not Felix's name, another name, a name that fit like a key fits a lock, and Felix was turning toward it, turning toward the voice, turning toward—

Gone.

The window shut. Not slammed—shut, gently, and where it had been there was now smooth wall, and worse, there had *always* been smooth wall; Felix could feel the absence already healing over, the mind doing what minds do, papering over the gap with the confident plaster of *nothing was ever there*. They knew, in the way one knows a tooth is missing: something had been taken. A kitchen. A song. A name. The contents were already dissolving even as they reached for them, sand through fingers, and within three heartbeats Felix was left holding only the shape of the loss, like the impression left in a bed by someone who rose before dawn.

"There," the ferryman said softly, releasing their hand. He looked almost kind. Almost. "You see? Didn't hurt a bit."

"What did you take?" Felix whispered. Their own voice sounded distant, as if heard through the healed-over wall.

"A kitchen," he said. "A song sung badly. A name called from a doorway." He turned back toward his boat, already untying the painter. "Small things. Warm things. I find the small warm ones hold a crossing best—they burn slow." He paused, one foot on the gunwale, and added, without turning around: "For what it's worth, carver, whoever sang that song will sing it again somewhere. Songs don't die when we forget them. Only the remembering does."

The boat slid back out onto the mirror-water. Felix stood on the ash-colored shore of the borderland, holding the shape of a loss, watching the towers of remembered lives drift beneath the hull.

*Someone's been skimming your account for years.* Or you've been spending it.

The ferryman's words followed them up the black stone path like a second toll, unpaid.

*

The city of the Compact announced itself in bone.

Not skeletons—Felix had seen enough of the dead's leavings to know the difference—but worked bone, carved bone, bone used the way brick and slate were used elsewhere: lintels and signboards and gutter-channels, doorposts incised with names, whole façades latticed with femurs and ribs polished ivory-pale. It should have been obscene. It was instead, disturbingly, beautiful—the way a well-tended graveyard is beautiful, the way grief becomes architecture when enough time passes. Every piece of it had been loved by somebody's hands first.

The streets smelled of tallow and coal-smoke and something sharper underneath, a mineral tang Felix couldn't place until they passed an open workshop doorway and saw a broker weighing finger-bones on a brass scale, each one tagged with a paper label in a cramped, meticulous hand. *Ledgers,* Felix thought. *They keep ledgers here too.* Everyone traded in the impossible; the only question was the terms.

They kept their hood up. They walked like a person with errands. Inside their shirt the Covenant Key beat against their sternum like a second heart, and Felix had begun to suspect it was guiding them—not pulling, nothing so dramatic, but warming when they turned the right way at a corner, cooling at the wrong ones, a compass of temperature. It wanted to go deeper into the city. Toward the vault district, if the map Maren had burned into their memory in the guildhall's last minutes was accurate. Toward the Oathbone.

Toward the people who had tried to kill them yesterday.

Felix found a room above a chandlery, paid with a coin they bit first, and sat on the narrow bed with the prophecy book open on their knees, reading the entry for the hundredth time. *Tonight the Sigil burns. The carver stands at either hinge.* Tonight. Hours away, now—the light through the shuttered window had gone the color of old honey, and somewhere below a bell was counting out the afternoon.

They tried to remember the kitchen.

They sat very still and reached for it the way you reach for a dream on waking, gently, before the mind's daylight dissolves it—and got nothing. Worse than nothing: they got the *feeling* of reaching, the muscle-memory of retrieval finding no shelf where the thing should be. And in the silence where the kitchen should have been, another absence shifted, older and smoother, worn like a stone in a streambed. How many gaps did they carry? When had they started? Had there ever been a complete Felix Voss, or had they always been a ledger with pages razored out, balancing happily because they'd never seen the original totals?

The keepsake bone lay warm against their skin. Tool-marks in their own hand, older than their own hand. A fragment of memory: the Sigil towering, arms raised. Whose arms?

*A house where someone's moved out but left the furniture.*

Felix closed the book. Below, the bell struck the hour, and with it—faint, from the direction of the vault district, from the direction the Key was warming toward—came a sound that did not belong to any bell: a deep, resonant *humming*, felt in the teeth rather than heard, the unmistakable vibration of worked bone singing to its kin.

The same hum Felix had heard in a client's payment, weeks ago, in a cellar workshop that felt like a lifetime ago. Or several lifetimes.

The Oathbone knew they were coming.

Felix pulled their hood lower, tucked the prophecy book against their spine, and went down the stairs into the ash-lit streets, following the warmth in their chest toward the vault, the relic, and whatever version of themselves was waiting there—aware, with the clarity of a person reading the last line of a page, that the ferryman had been right about one thing:

The clever ones ask after someone else's past.

And Felix was beginning to understand that they had been carrying someone else's past for a very long time indeed.
