## 9. Ashen Territory

The city announced itself by smell before Felix ever saw its walls: a reek of scorched marrow and hot lime, the stink of bone boiled clean. Cindral, the Compact's seat, sat in the crook of a river gone gray with ash-runoff, and even from the saltmarsh road, three days out, Felix could taste it on the wind—something burnt at the root of everything, as if the whole city had been smoked like a ham left too long over coals.

Felix walked through the western gate at dusk, hood up, the Covenant Key wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, the prophecy book strapped beneath their coat where it lay heavy as a second heart. The gate guards barely glanced at them. That was the first surprise. In every story the guild told of the Ashen Compact, its capital was a fortress of suspicion, a place where strangers were turned inside out and their bones counted twice. But the guards were bored, half-asleep on their feet, and one was arguing quietly with another about the price of lamp-oil.

*They aren't looking for a stranger,* Felix realized, passing under the portcullis. *They're looking for no one. Because no one comes here.*

The streets beyond were narrow and close, built of a stone so dark it seemed to drink the lamplight. And everywhere—everywhere—the dead were absent.

That was the thing Felix could not stop noticing. In Vellharrow, in any guild-city of the living realm, ghosts drifted through the evening crowds like fish through kelp: the recently bereaved walking beside shades only they couldn't see, old grievances murmured from corners, a child laughing at something invisible on a stairwell. The world was porous. The covenant saw to that. Even the guildhall had its bound spirits, Maren foremost among them, and Felix had spent their whole life hearing the dead the way other people heard rain.

Here there was nothing. The silence pressed against Felix's ears like deep water. No whispers in the alleys. No cold spots at thresholds. No names scratched into doorposts to keep remembered kin close. The doorposts of Cindral were scrubbed bare.

Felix stopped in the mouth of an alley and pressed a hand to the dark stone. It wasn't silence, exactly. It was more like a held breath—a city clenching itself shut against something, so long and so thoroughly that the clenching had become its natural state.

*They built this,* Felix thought. *A whole city shaped like a wound.*

A woman jostled past carrying a basket of turnips, and Felix caught the edge of her sleeve: embroidered along the cuff, small and precise, was a pattern of interlocking circles with a line struck through them. The Sigil, broken. Worn openly. Not hidden, but displayed, the way other folk wore saints' marks or lovers' knots.

Felix pulled their hood lower and followed her toward the market district, because if you wanted to understand a city, you went where it traded.

* * *

The bone-market opened at nightfall and did not close until dawn. It filled a square called the Cinder Ring—a plaza paved in pale stone that might once have been beautiful, now stained in great dark fans where fires had been set and stamped out, again and again, over years. Stalls lined the ring in concentric circles, and every stall sold bone.

Not trinkets. Not charms. Raw material. Femurs stacked like firewood, ribs bundled like kindling, skulls arranged by size and species on shelves with the tidy horror of a grocer's fruit. Brokers in ash-gray aprons called prices over the heads of buyers: ox-bone by the span, human bone by the piece and provenance, whalebone for those with deep pockets and darker purposes. Carvers worked at open benches, chisels flashing, shavings curling into braziers where they burned with a smell Felix knew intimately and had never thought to question until this moment.

It smelled like their own workshop. It smelled like home.

*Of course it does,* Felix thought, and felt the ground tilt under them. *I learned my trade somewhere. I just never asked where.*

They had coin enough—not guild scrip, which would be questioned, but honest silver from the emergency purse Odile had pressed on them back when Odile still pretended to be an ally—and they bought a stall's worth of credibility: a bundle of cow femurs, a honing stone, a roll of waxed cord. The broker who sold them barely looked up, and when he did, his eyes went first to Felix's hands.

"Carver," he said. Not a question. "Guild-trained?"

"Independent," Felix said, and the word came out smoother than they expected. It was almost true. "Working the marsh towns."

"Marsh work." He nodded slowly. "You people still take commissions from the dead, don't you? Down south." His tone was carefully neutral, the way a man talks about a neighbor's embarrassing religion.

"Sometimes."

"Hm." He weighed Felix's silver, bit one coin, and pushed the rest back. "Fair warning, southerner. If you're planning to carve anything that *speaks*, do it outside the walls. The Wardens burn unlicensed resonant work. Fine's your stock. Second time it's your bench. Third time—" he drew a thumb across his throat, not threateningly, just informationally, like reporting weather.

"I'll remember."

"See you do." He glanced at the oilcloth bundle at Felix's chest—one heartbeat too long, Felix thought, one heartbeat too knowing—and then waved them off toward the next row.

Felix found a corner stall renting space to transient carvers, spread out their purchases, and began to work. It was the oldest trick they knew and the safest: a carver bent over bone is furniture. Nobody interrogates furniture. They carved simple things—buttons, a spoon, tokens for debts nobody owed here—and listened.

Bone-markets are gossip engines. Every city has one organ like that, and Felix had spent years learning to read Vellharrow's pulse through its ledgers. Here the blood ran different, but it ran all the same, and by the third hour they had assembled the shape of the city's grief from fragments dropped between haggling voices.

"—third Warden purge this season, my cousin says, pulling resonant stock right off the benches—"

"—the Vigil's next month, you'll see the whole city out, everyone wears the broken circle, even the magistrates—"

"—my grandmother was there, at the Burning, she said the sky over the shrine went the color of a scald—"

Felix's chisel slipped. A thin white shaving curled away from the button they were ruining.

*At the Burning.* Everyone here used the phrase like a date, like a name for God, like a scar they touched in the dark. Felix had grown up on the guild's version: a century ago, the Sigil had stood at the borderland shrine, binding the realms, and something had gone wrong—or someone—and the fire that followed had been catastrophe, tragedy, the great wound the guild's whole existence existed to prevent. The guild spoke of it the way priests speak of the Fall.

But the guild hadn't lost anyone. The guild had only lost *access*.

These people had lost everything else.

Felix kept carving. Kept listening. And slowly, across the night, the ledger of Cindral's history balanced itself in their head, entry by entry, and the sum made them sick.

The Compact had not been founded by zealots or ambition-mongers. It had been founded by *survivors*. Farmers whose fields had gone to ghost-light when the border tore. Mothers who had spent decades unable to bury their dead properly because the covenant kept the departed lingering, half-aware, tethered to places and grievances, neither at peace nor present. Whole villages that had emptied themselves into the borderland during the last burning, chasing relatives who had spilled through the tear and never come back—and the ones who stayed behind had built this city on the ruins of that hope, and sworn, with ash rubbed into their foreheads, that no living person would ever again be asked to share the world with the dead.

*They're not wrong,* Felix thought, and hated themselves a little for thinking it. *Maren lingers. All the guild's ghosts linger. We call it communion. From the other side of the table, what is it?*

What was it, to be dead and unable to leave? To be a debt the living refused to let come due?

Felix's hands kept moving. Their mind kept balancing. And somewhere around the fourth hour, an old broker with a face like crumpled parchment settled onto the stool beside Felix's bench, laid down two coppers, and said, "Carve me a token, will you? Nothing fancy. Just something to hold."

"For whom?" Felix asked, before they could stop themselves. Old habit. Ledger habit. *Every token needs a debtor and a creditor.*

The old man looked at Felix then, really looked, and his eyes were wet and furious and very tired. "For no one," he said. "For the ones who can't collect anymore. That's the whole point of this city, boy. We finally paid our debts to the dead by making sure they'd never come to collect." He tapped the counter. "Token. Plain. Please."

Felix carved him a plain token. And while they worked, they understood—with the cold clarity of a ledger closing—that they were not hiding among enemies.

They were hiding among mourners.

* * *

Dawn came gray to the Cinder Ring, and with it came trouble, because dawn always did.

Felix had rented a cot in the loft above a chandler's shop near the market's edge, paying in carved buttons, and they were asleep maybe two hours when the shouting started. Not panic-shouting—procession-shouting, the organized bellow of men in uniform. They leaned out the loft window into the smoky morning air and saw Wardens moving through the Cinder Ring in files of six, ash-gray cloaks, iron badges stamped with the broken circle. They weren't raiding. They were *posting*. Nailing notices to every stall post and doorframe in the market, and the crowd gathering to read them was quiet in a way that frightened Felix more than screaming would have.

They dressed, pocketed their tools and the Key, and went down into the street.

The notice was printed in blocky type, hundreds of copies, and Felix read it over a turnip-seller's shoulder:

*By order of the Ashen Compact and its Wardens: sought for questioning in matters touching the Borderland Shrine and the coming Vigil—one person, unknown name, southern accent, guild-trained carver, seen working the Cinder Ring. Said person may be carrying artifacts of the Old Covenant. Report sightings to any Warden post. Reward: fifty crowns and remission of tithe.*

Below the words, an engraver's approximation of the Covenant Key.

Felix read it twice. Then they did the arithmetic they should have done days ago, standing at the gate guards' bored faces: the Compact had known someone was coming. Had perhaps known for weeks. The prophecy book was not a secret sealed in Vellharrow; it was a rumor with roots here, in the city built on the last burning, and the Compact's founders had spent a century waiting for the book to surface and the carver with it.

*I'm not a stranger blundering into their territory,* Felix thought. *I'm a prophecy arriving on schedule. Again.*

They moved. Quickly, calmly, the way you move through a market where every face might be worth fifty crowns to its owner. Out of the Cinder Ring by the eastern arcade, down a stair cut into the city's bone-dark bedrock, into the lower warrens where the chandlers and tanners worked and the notices hadn't reached yet. There, in the shadow of a tannery's dripping eaves, someone grabbed Felix's arm and pulled them into a doorway that smelled of lye and hides.

Felix's knife was out before their breath was. The hand on their arm released, raised, palm open.

The woman attached to it was thirty-something, broad-shouldered, with a carver's calluses and a Compact badge torn off her coat at the shoulder-loop, leaving a rectangle of unfaded cloth. Her eyes were the pale gray of old ash.

"You're the carver," she said. "Don't argue. I watched you work last night. Your grip's wrong for a Compact-trained hand—you carve *toward* yourself, like the old guilds teach." She glanced up the stairwell, listening, then back. "Wardens posted your face an hour ago. By tonight they'll have it in every ward. You need to be out of Cindral before the Vigil, and you won't manage alone. So either you trust me, or you stay and become kindling."

"Who are you?" Felix kept the knife low but didn't sheathe it.

"My name's Ilsa." She said it flatly, like a weight set down. "Until four days ago I was a Warden-sergeant. And I'm the only person in this city who wants you to reach the Sigil alive."

"Why?" Felix searched her ash-gray eyes for the trap, the tell, the ledger's hidden line. "Everyone here burned their bridges to the dead. Why would you want the covenant reforged?"

Ilsa's jaw tightened, and for a moment she looked less like a defector than like a woman standing in front of a grave she'd dug herself.

"Because I buried my sister twice," she said. "Once when she died. And once when the Compact's rules wouldn't let her *leave*." She stepped back out of the doorway, gesturing down the warren with a jerk of her chin. "Come on. The vault where they keep the Oathbone opens at noon shift-change, and we've got a long walk first. Ask your questions moving."

Felix hesitated one breath longer—long enough to feel the prophecy book warm against their ribs, long enough to hear the memory of Maren's voice saying *burn the book, child*—and then followed her down into the dark.

Somewhere behind them, a Warden's whistle split the morning air, high and sharp, and began to move closer.
