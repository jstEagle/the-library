## 10. The Second Relic

The Compact's vault sat beneath a bone-broker's counting house like a swallowed tooth—buried deep, aching when the weather turned.

Felix had spent three days learning the building's rhythms from the loft Ilsa kept above a tallow-chandler's shop two streets away. From its single window they could see the broker's yard: the carts arriving before dawn with their cargo wrapped in sailcloth, the brokers' clerks carrying ledgers out to weigh against the light, the way everyone in Ashen Territory handled bone as if it might still complain. Which, Felix had learned, some of it could. The city hummed with it. Every gutter and stairwell whispered with the residue of carved dead, a low chorus that made the guildhall's ghosts seem almost companionable by comparison.

"You keep staring at the vault door," Ilsa said. She was sharpening a boning knife on a whetstone worn concave by generations of hands, though she never seemed to need the blade for anything she actually did. "Staring doesn't open it."

"Ledgers do." Felix turned the page of the notes they'd been keeping—not a plan exactly, but an accounting. *Debts owed: one ferryman, one memory, amount unknown. Debts owed to me: one truth, held by Ilsa, interest accruing.* "Every vault is a ledger, Ilsa. It records what someone was afraid to lose. You just have to read what the fear was shaped like."

Ilsa snorted. In the four days since she'd appeared at Felix's rented room—uninvited, unarmed, saying only *you'll want the Oathbone and you'll want me to get it*—she had been consistently unimpressed by everything Felix said, which Felix found, to their private irritation, steadying. Maren would have liked her. The thought arrived unbidden and left an ache behind it, sharp-edged as river ice. Three nights now Felix had woken reaching for the eldest ghost's voice and remembered, again, the way her unbinding had felt from the outside: a candle going out not with smoke but with *relief.*

"The fear," Ilsa said, laying down the knife, "is shaped like a door with three locks and a warden who sleeps sitting up because he's terrified of dreams. The locks are clockwork, blood, and prayer, in that order. The clockwork resets every ninety-one minutes. The blood wants Compact blood specifically—the founder's line, which means mine, because apparently being a traitor runs in families along with everything else. And the prayer—" She paused, and something crossed her face that wasn't quite a smile. "The prayer wants someone who can hear the dead. So. Congratulations. You're the most expensive part of this burglary."

Felix closed their ledger. Outside, the chandler's vats sent up their sweet-rotten smell, and somewhere below a cart of femurs rattled over cobblestone, singing faintly in a register only Felix could hear.

"When?" they asked.

"Tonight. During the changing of the wardens, when the old one's dread is thickest." Ilsa stood, rolling her sleeves with the economy of someone who had done terrible things efficiently for a long time. "Bring your knife, carver. And bring whatever you say to bones to make them honest. We're going to need both."

* * *

They went down through the coal chute.

It was, Ilsa admitted, undignified. Felix agreed, black to the elbows, knees ringing on iron rungs, the prophecy book strapped against their chest beneath their coat where it lay warm as a sleeping animal. Above them the counting house settled into its nighttime creaks; around them, older than any of it, the vault's foundations breathed cold air that tasted of chalk and lye.

At the bottom, a corridor lit by lamps of rendered fat—Ilsa had explained, without apology, whose fat, and Felix had decided not to ask—and then the door.

It was smaller than Felix expected. Vault doors in stories were always cathedral-huge, banded in iron, shouting their importance. This one was waist-high, oak, plain as a pantry door, and somehow worse for it. Humility, in a thing built to hold a relic of the founding covenant, suggested shame.

"Clockwork first." Ilsa knelt, produced a set of picks finer than anything Felix had seen outside a jeweler's, and set them against the lock's face. Through the wood came a ticking, patient and insectile. "Ninety-one minutes. When I nod, you take the blood lock. Don't think about what it wants. Just give it your hand."

"My hand?"

"It pricks. It tastes. It decides." Ilsa's picks moved with a delicacy that contradicted everything else about her. "It will decide no. That's fine. While it's deciding, it isn't listening to the third lock, and the third lock is yours anyway. Two liars telling the truth at the same time—the door won't know which betrayal to believe."

"That's not how locks work."

"In this city it is."

The clockwork surrendered with a click so soft Felix nearly missed it. Then Ilsa nodded, and Felix pressed their palm to the second plate, and the pain was brief and bright as a struck match—a needle, a sip, a pause long enough to hold a whole life in. Felix imagined the lock reading them: Voss, the name said. Bone-carver. Ledger-keeper. And underneath, in ink the lock had no right to see: *something borrowed, something old.*

The needle withdrew. The plate went dark. Whatever the blood had decided, it had decided quietly, and Felix found their own heart hammering as if they'd gotten away with something enormous.

Then the third lock.

It wasn't brass or steel but a small recessed hollow shaped like a listening ear, and from it came a sound Felix knew intimately: the murmur of bone wanting to be understood. Not words yet. A waiting.

Felix knelt and put their lips close and did what they had done over ten thousand tokens and debts and grave-goods. They listened first. That was the whole craft, the thing Odile had never grasped and the guild's founders had known in their marrow: you didn't carve meaning into bone, you coaxed out what was already there, the way you'd coax a frightened animal from under a floor.

"I'm here," Felix murmured. "I hear you. What do you remember?"

The lock's murmur rose. And what poured back, in a voice like dry leaves and old water, was not a password or a hymn but a *question*, returned:

*"Who holds the debt? Who pays the covenant? Name yourself, name yourself, name yourself—"*

And Felix, without thinking, said: "Voss."

The lock exhaled. The door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, and the cold air of the vault rolled over them, smelling of centuries.

Ilsa stared. "It's never opened that fast. The last man who tried took six hours and lost his voice doing it."

"Maybe it liked my name." But Felix's mouth had gone dry. Because the voice behind the door hadn't been asking a stranger. It had been asking the way family asks across a crowded room—impatient, certain of being heard.

* * *

The vault was a single round chamber, and the Oathbone stood in its center on a plinth of black glass, upright, displayed like a saint's finger or a war trophy—which, Felix supposed, it had been both.

It was a femur. Human-sized, or near enough, polished to a deep amber shine, carved from crown to shaft with script so dense it looked at first like grain, like the natural figuring of the bone itself. Only when Felix drew closer did the letters resolve: the Old Covenant tongue, the language the guild's oldest contracts still used when the stakes were too high for living speech. Around the base of the plinth, offerings had piled up over decades—wax stubs, milk teeth, folded papers covered in names. People had come here to swear things. Felix could feel the residue of all those oaths clinging to the chamber like fog.

"Well," Ilsa said. "Take it. Before I remember why my family keeps this thing locked up instead of ground to meal."

Felix reached out—

—and stopped.

Their hand was trembling. Not from fear. From recognition, rising through the arm like water through chalk. They had held a bone shaped like this before. They held one now, every day: the keepsake they wore at their throat, the one piece of their past that had survived whatever theft had emptied them, the femur fragment they'd carried so long they had stopped seeing it. They pulled it out now on its cord, and held the two bones side by side in the lamp-light.

The amber matched.

Not approximately. Not in the way all old bone matches. The grain ran identically, whorl for whorl, flaw for flaw—the same pale streak branching like a frozen lightning bolt through the shaft of the Oathbone and continuing, seamlessly, onto the fragment at Felix's throat, interrupted only by the century between them. Where the keepsake ended, the relic began. Two pieces of a single whole.

"They broke it," Felix whispered. "When they shattered the Sigil. Someone took the femur and split it, and half became this—" they lifted the Oathbone from its plinth, and it came free with a sigh, as if relieved, "—and half became mine."

"Yours." Ilsa's voice had gone very flat, the flatness she used when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Carver. Explain that sentence slowly."

"I don't know how it happened. I don't remember getting it. That's the point—that's the whole cursed point of me, Ilsa. My memories were taken before I ever woke up as myself. All I have is this." Felix turned the fragment in their fingers. Under the lamplight, the tool-marks on its inner curve caught the glow: fine, confident cuts, a master's hand, a carver's hand. *Their* hand. They had known that much since the workshop, since the night the prophecy book opened its mouth. "But whoever I was before—they held this bone while it was still whole. Maybe they're the one who split it. Maybe they're the one who swore on it. The Oathbone isn't a symbol of the covenant, Ilsa. Look at the script."

They held it up. Now that Felix was listening—really listening, with the sense the ferryman's toll had somehow left intact—the carving spoke, and what it said was not law or doctrine.

It was a promise, in a living person's voice, addressed to the dead. *I will keep the door between you and forgetting. I will carry your names. I will not let the living world pretend you were never in it.* Signed at the base, in letters small enough to require closeness, tenderness, intent:

*Vesper Voss.*

The name landed in the vault like a dropped lantern.

"Voss," Ilsa repeated. Her eyes had gone from the signature to Felix's face and back, doing arithmetic she clearly hated. "That's your name."

"It's the name I woke up with."

"And the bone at your throat is half of the bone our entireCompact was built to guard—or destroy, depending which faction you ask—and it was cut from the same leg, by the same hand, as the relic that founded us." Ilsa laughed once, short and joyless, and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Do you understand what this means? The elders say the Oathbone was taken from the Sigil-breaker as punishment. Ground from their body after death, so their oath would die with them. But this—" she gestured at the seamless grain, the matching flaw, "—this was one person's bone, whole, and they *gave* it. Split it themselves, or had it split. Half to bind the covenant. Half to—" She looked at the keepsake again. "Half to keep. To remember by. People don't split their own bones to punish themselves, carver. They do it to leave a trail."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere overhead, the counting house ticked through its ledgers; somewhere below even this, the city's drowned dead murmured in their soaked and crowded beds. Felix stood holding a stranger's oath and their own missing history in the same two hands, and felt the shape of the trap they'd been walking inside of since the archive: not hunted *toward* the relics, perhaps. Hunted *home* to them.

"The prophecy called me restorer or destroyer," Felix said. "But maybe there was never a choice written there at all. Maybe the book was just... reading the grain. The way I do." They looked down at the Oathbone, at Vesper's careful, loving, damning script. "Whoever I was, I made a promise to the dead with half my own body. And then I lost the memory of making it. And now the promise has come due, and it sent a courier wearing my face."

"That's the most unsettling sentence anyone has said to me, and I grew up among people who tan with ghost-fat."

"I'm full of them lately."

Above them, muffled through stone and timber, a bell began to ring—not the counting house's hour bell but the ward-bell, high and urgent, the sound Ashen Territory used for fire or breach or treason. Ilsa went rigid, head tilting, counting the strokes. Four. Five. Six.

"That's the district bell. That's *our district*." She snatched up her picks, her knife, moving already toward the coal chute with the terrible calm of someone reordering a lifetime's plans mid-stride. "Nobody rings that for a burglary. Somebody rang it for *us*. Or somebody wanted it rung, and paid well enough that the question didn't matter."

Felix slung the strap of the prophecy book higher on their shoulder and followed, the Oathbone wrapped in their coat against their chest, where it lay warm and heavier than bone had any right to be. At the foot of the chute they paused, one hand on the iron rungs, and looked back once at the empty plinth, the guttered offering-lights, the little folded papers with their patient, unanswered names.

"We're leaving," they told the vault softly. "But I'm taking the debt with me. Whoever holds it—it comes due soon. I want to be there when it's paid."

Then they climbed, up into the coal-dark and the ringing bell, and as they emerged into the alley behind the counting house, Ilsa caught their sleeve and pointed down the street, where torchlight was blooming around the chandler's shop—their loft, their notes, the window they'd watched the vault from for three days.

"They're not hunting the thief," Ilsa said quietly. "Look where they're standing. They knew exactly which door."

And through the crowd, moving against the current of running clerks with the unhurried certainty of someone who has never once been chased in her life, came a figure Felix recognized from a scorched doorway and a refused offer: the Compact's envoy, smiling through the torchlight directly at them.

"Carver!" the envoy called, pleasant as a hostess. "Wonderful. You've got it. Shall we discuss terms—or shall we discuss the defector's price?"

Beside Felix, Ilsa had gone white as tallow.
