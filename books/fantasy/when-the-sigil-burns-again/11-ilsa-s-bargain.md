## 11. Ilsa's Bargain

The safehouse was a bone-broker's back room, and it smelled like every lie Felix had ever told.

Not that Felix had told many. That was the trouble with hearing the dead: they heard you right back, and lies curdled in the air between a living mouth and a listening ghost like milk left out in summer. So Felix had built a life on careful truths instead—truths measured out in ledger-lines and carved tokens, each one balanced, each one paid. It had kept the loneliness at arm's length, which was the most any honest person could hope for.

Now they sat on a stool in the corner of a room stacked floor to ceiling with femurs and ribs and vertebrae, all of them tagged and catalogued, all of them *somebody*, and watched Ilsa Vane turn the Oathbone over in her scarred hands.

She hadn't given it back yet.

"You keep looking at me like I'm going to run," Ilsa said without looking up. The lamplight caught the pale seam of a scar that ran from her ear down into her collar, old and badly stitched. "I carried this thing through three checkpoints sewn into a coat lining. I'm not running."

"I wasn't thinking that."

"You were thinking it loudly." She set the relic down on the table between them at last, and Felix let out a breath they hadn't known they were holding. "You flinch when you look at it. Same way you flinched at your keepsake bone. Why?"

Felix turned their own keepsake over in their pocket, its familiar curve pressing against their palm. Two relics now, cut—no, *carved*—from the same body. From a body that was, by every account that mattered, theirs. Or had been someone's. Or had been waiting a century for someone to walk into it and start living.

"The Compact vault had wards keyed to bloodline," Felix said. "Yours opened them. But the Oathbone hummed when *I* touched it. Not yours. Mine."

"Because it knows you." Ilsa said it simply, the way she said everything, as if the terrible things were just inventory to be counted. "That's what I came here to talk about. Not the bone. What you're going to do with it."

Felix looked at her. In the two days since the theft—the alarms, the rooftops, the long cold wait in the drainage culvert while Compact hunters passed close enough to hear breathing—Ilsa had been brisk, competent, almost warm in the way of people who have decided to be useful rather than liked. But she had not once said what she wanted. And Felix had learned, balancing other people's impossible accounts, that everyone wanted something. The dead wanted rest or revenge. The living wanted the same things wearing better clothes.

"So ask me," Felix said. "Whatever it is. Ask."

Ilsa pulled a second stool to the table and sat, elbows on her knees, and for a long moment she studied the Oathbone as if it were a map of a country she'd been exiled from.

"When the covenant holds," she said, "what happens to the dead?"

Felix blinked. "They... stay. That's the whole point. The Sigil binds the border so souls can't be torn loose, can't be scattered, can't be dragged where they shouldn't go. The dead persist. Safely. The living can speak with them, contract with them—"

"*Keep* them," Ilsa said. "Bind them. Ledger them. Guilds like yours hold ghosts in cellars and guildhalls and call it safety. My grandmother died in the Salt Fever year. Do you know what happened to her?" She didn't wait for an answer; her hands flattened on the table, and Felix saw they were trembling, very slightly, the tremor of a woman holding something down. "Nothing. That's what happened. Nothing. A hundred years and she is still standing in the doorway of our family house in Ashen Territory, because the covenant won't let her cross and won't let her fade. She stands there. Every night my mother goes to speak with her, and every morning my mother comes home having spoken with her, and neither of them has moved one inch toward peace in sixty years. That is not mercy. That is a door welded shut with the people you love still knocking on it."

Felix thought of Maren. Of the eldest ghost's voice fraying at the edges after two hundred years of guildhall service, of the way the spirits had gone silent when asked about the carver, of Maren's final unbinding buying Felix time with the last of herself. *Go, carver. Finish it or fail it, but stop letting us wait.*

Maren had waited two centuries. Felix had called it loyalty. Standing in a room full of catalogued bones, it began to sound different.

"And your version," Felix said quietly, "is the Compact's version. Burn the Sigil. Cut the border. Let everything through."

"No." Ilsa's voice went flat and hard. "No, that's where you're wrong, and it's where the Compact is wrong, and it's why I defected. The Compact wants the realms severed forever. No passage either way. The dead locked out, the living locked in, grief amputated instead of healed. They call it mercy too. Everyone calls their cage mercy." She leaned forward. "I don't want the covenant burned, carver. I want it *rewritten*. One clause. One single clause added to a pact written by frightened people a thousand years ago: that the dead may *choose* to pass on. A door home, with a handle on our side of it. Those who want to stay, stay. My grandmother could finally leave that doorway—or stay in it, if staying is truly what she wants—but she'd get to *decide*. Nobody's decided anything about death in this city for a thousand years except whoever wrote the original pact, and I'd bet my other kidney they weren't asking the dead's opinion."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the stacks of bone, a femur ticked against its neighbor, settling, the way bones do when a resident ghost shifts in long-held sleep.

Felix found their thumb moving over the keepsake bone, back and forth, the habit of a lifetime—or of two lifetimes, though only one of them knew it yet. "The reforging needs three relics and a binding," they said. "The Key opens the pact. The Oathbone carries the original oath-taker's word. The Nameplate holds the names. If we rewrite—"

"If you rewrite," Ilsa corrected, "then the new terms are whatever the hands that forge it agree to. That's why I stole nothing and gave everything to get you here. The Compact thinks you'll restore the old covenant because you're a guild man and guild men preserve. The Compact's hardliners hope you'll destroy it because then their war has a purpose again. Both of them are wrong about you. You're a bookkeeper. You know that contracts aren't sacred. Contracts are *negotiated*, and renegotiated, and amended, and the moment you treat one as untouchable you've stopped being its keeper and started being its prisoner."

"That's heresy where I come from."

"I know. Your guildmaster forbade you to act at all, didn't she? Odile. Sat on this prophecy for years and called it prudence." Something flickered across Ilsa's face—not quite a smile. "We have files on her. Long ones."

"Everyone has files on everyone," Felix muttered. "That's the age we live in."

* * *

They worked through the evening, or tried to. Ilsa had brought documents—Compact intelligence, fragments of pre-burning records, copies of copies of the original covenant's public clauses—and spread them across the bone-tables until the room looked like a scholar's fever dream. Felix read with a carver's eye and a ledger-keeper's discipline, and slowly, clause by clause, the shape of the old pact assembled itself in their mind.

It was worse than the histories taught.

The covenant bound the dead *individually*, soul by soul, name by name—a great registry written into the Sigil itself, each spirit pinned to their entry like a moth to a card. Safety, yes. Permanence, absolutely. But also: no exit. The registry admitted and never released. A thousand years of the dead accumulating like water behind a dam, and every generation of guilds calling the dam a blessing.

"How many," Felix asked, "do you think are held? In total?"

"No one counts anymore. The Compact stopped counting around the time counting became an accusation." Ilsa tapped a yellowed page. "But here—this fragment predates the last burning. Estimates then ran past four million in the eastern reaches alone. And that was a hundred years ago, and nobody has passed on since, and everybody who has died since has *added*."

Four million. Felix thought of their cellar workshop, of the tokens carved for debts owed to the dead, of the small tidy world of accounts where every haunting had a line item and every voice had a client number. Four million clients. Four million debts the living owed and would never, under the current terms, be permitted to pay in full.

And beneath the arithmetic, quieter and colder, another thought: *the dead I hear. All of them. Every voice I've ever balanced a ledger against. None of them ever got to leave.*

"Why can *you* hear them?" Ilsa asked suddenly. "Most carvers feel echoes. You have conversations. Full recall. Names, dates, debts. I watched you work in that culvert—you recited the Compact hunters' patrol rotations off a ghost who's been dead forty years, and he remembered them like yesterday. That's not craft. That's architecture."

Felix's hand closed around the keepsake bone. "I don't know."

"Yes you do."

"I know what the elder said." The words came out dry, reluctant, dragged up from the place where Chapter Twelve of Felix's life was waiting to happen. "Vesper Voss. The carver who broke the Sigil. Who paid with her memories. Who—" Felix's throat tightened. "Whose unfinished life I apparently am. Or carry. Or continue. I haven't decided which sentence to finish yet."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. Then: "Does it change what you want?"

"It changes what I *can* want." Felix stood, restless, and walked the narrow aisle between bone-stacks, fingers trailing along the tags. "If my wanting is hers—leftover, inherited, carved into the marrow before I was born—then how do I know any of this is mine? The guild. The ledgers. The instinct to keep accounts instead of burning them. Maybe that's Vesper's caution sitting in my chest wearing my name. Maybe I don't have a name. Maybe I'm a ledger-entry myself, a debt carried forward a century."

"Or," Ilsa said, "you're the first person in a hundred years with the standing to amend the contract, and the fact that you might be partly someone else doesn't make your signature less valid. Signatures renew. That's the whole point of them."

Felix stopped walking. "When did you become a philosopher?"

"When my grandmother's ghost told me, on my sixteenth birthday, that she had forgotten my mother's face and spent every night since trying to relearn it and failing, because a century of standing in a doorway erodes a person down to the doorway." Ilsa's voice cracked, just barely, and was immediately repaired. "Grief is a fine education, carver. Better than the Compact's academies."

* * *

Past midnight, the broker upstairs went to bed, and the room settled into lamplight and dust. Felix sat back down across from Ilsa, and laid the keepsake bone on the table beside the Oathbone. Side by side, the match was undeniable even to a stranger's eye: same grain, same faint honey-gold color, same tool-marks spiraling at the ends in a hand identical to Felix's own.

"One body," Felix said. "Two relics. And somewhere in the borderland, a third—the Nameplate. Guarded, according to your files, by a ghost who attacks anyone who comes for it."

"Not anyone," Ilsa said. "Thieves. Desecrators. People who come to take." She met Felix's eyes. "A hundred years she's stood guard over her own name. What do you suppose she does when the person who comes walking up to her shrine is wearing her face?"

"I've been asking myself that since the elder grabbed my wrist in the market." Felix laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as spun glass. "Do you know what the worst part is? Not that I might be her. That part is almost simple—it's a fact, facts can be carried. The worst part is that if she shattered the Sigil on purpose, if she believed the realms should be severed—then some part of me may believe it too. Some part of me already voted, a century ago, and I never got to hear the debate."

"Then hear it now." Ilsa leaned forward, lamplight carving her face into planes and shadow. "Because that's the bargain I'm actually offering, underneath the relic and the route and the safehouse. Not 'restore the covenant.' Not 'break it.' I'm asking you to do the thing nobody in a thousand years has had the courage or the standing to do: sit down at the table with both sides of the border and redraft the terms. Mercy isn't a wall and mercy isn't a gate thrown open in a panic. Mercy is a *door*, with a latch, and rules, and someone trustworthy keeping the key. I want you to write those rules. You, Felix Voss—whoever else that name contains. Because you're the only person alive who hears both sides, and the only person alive whose hands the relics will answer."

"And if the hands won't answer? If the binding demands something I don't have?"

Ilsa paused. For the first time since Felix had met her, she looked away first.

"Then you'll find out at the shrine," she said. "With her ghost watching. With the Compact's fire-brigade three days behind us and closing. With the prophecy's date already circled and tonight already spent." She pushed the Oathbone gently across the table until it touched the keepsake bone, and the two fragments seemed to lean together like old friends—or like a wound finding its other half. "I'm not promising you a clean choice, carver. I'm promising you a real one. Restore it, and four million souls stand in doorways forever. Break it, and the border tears and everything spills through hungry and unsaved. Rewrite it, and maybe—*maybe*—my grandmother gets to decide whether she stays or goes, and your Maren gets to finish whatever she was waiting to finish, and the dead become people again instead of inventory."

Inventory. The word landed in Felix's chest like a coin dropped down a well.

They thought of the cellar workshop. Of tokens carved for voices that could never be released from their accounts. Of the guildhall's ghosts, patient and fading and *thanked* and never *freed*. Of Odile's face when she'd forbidden action—cowardice dressed as stewardship. Of Maren's last words, spending her final unbinding on a stranger's escape.

*Finish it or fail it, carver. But stop letting us wait.*

"I need to think," Felix said.

"Of course." Isla rose, stretched, and gathered the documents into her satchel with quick soldier's movements. At the door she paused, hand on the frame. "For what it's worth—I don't think your wanting is borrowed. I watched you read those covenant clauses tonight. You didn't read them like a guild man defending property. You read them like a ferryman who's been rowing the same river so long he forgot the far bank existed. Whatever Vesper put in your bones, the outrage is yours."

She left. The bolt slid home behind her.

Felix sat alone among the bones with two relics touching on the table before them, and took out the ledger they always carried, and opened it to a fresh page.

This was the discipline that had kept them sane: when a decision loomed, write both columns. Debits and credits. Costs and payments. They dipped the pen.

In the left column, under *Restore*: **stability. safety. the border holds. no one torn apart.** And beneath it, in smaller letters that their hand almost refused to form: **four million souls. standing in doorways. forever.**

In the right column, under *Break*: **an end. freedom, perhaps. the door destroyed.** And beneath that: **the tear. the hunger. the dead spilling through unchosen and unfreed—cages opened by smashing, not by unlocking.**

Then, below both, they drew a third column, which no ledger-keeper was taught to draw, and hesitated over it, pen hovering.

Under *Rewrite*, they wrote: **a door with a latch.** Then, after a moment: **her grandmother's choice. Maren's rest. an unwritten future.**

And then, because honesty was the only currency they had ever traded in, they wrote the cost line, small and clear:

**price unknown. payable by: me.**

The lamp guttered. Down in the stacks, a bone ticked softly, and Felix could have sworn—though they were alone, though Ilsa's footsteps had long since faded—that something in the room exhaled, patient and vast, like four million souls turning over in a sleep that had lasted a thousand years.

They closed the ledger. Tomorrow they would cross into the borderland, and find the shrine, and meet the ghost wearing their face, carrying their memories, guarding the last relic with a fury a century deep.

Felix pressed their thumb once against the keepsake bone—against tool-marks made by their own hand in a life they couldn't remember—and whispered to the dark, to whichever of the dead might be listening:

"Tell her I'm coming. And tell her I'm not coming to restore what she broke."

The silence answered, in the way silence does when it is full: with the faintest hum from the Oathbone on the table, rising and falling, rising and falling, like a heartbeat that had waited a hundred years to be recognized.

Like something remembering its name.
