## 12. Whose Hands Carved Me

The elder's house smelled of tallow and old smoke, the way every house in Ashen Territory did—smoke soaked so deep into the plaster that no amount of scrubbing would ever draw it out. Felix had noticed it their first night in the city, lying awake on a bone-broker's cot, and had decided it was simply what a whole district smelled like when its founders had all survived a fire.

Now, standing in the elder's sickroom with Ilsa at their shoulder, Felix understood that the smell was not an accident of geography. It was a choice. These people had built their homes inside the memory of the burning and never once opened a window.

"You shouldn't be here," Ilsa murmured for the fourth time since they'd slipped through the rain-slick streets. She kept her voice low, though there was no one in the narrow room to hear but the dying woman propped against her pillows and the small brass brazier guttering on the floor. "Sable Voss does not receive visitors. She has not received visitors in eleven years. If the Compact learns you came—"

"Then we'll be quick." Felix flexed their fingers inside their gloves. The stolen Oathbone rode against their ribs beneath their coat, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping bird. It had been warm ever since Ilsa pulled it from the vault. Warm in a way that made Felix think of hands.

Ilsa had explained it twice already, in the flat voice she used for things that frightened her: Sable Voss was the last living witness to the Sigil's shattering. A hundred years old, maybe more—no one counted precisely anymore, because the Compact's calendar began at the burning and the years before it were called only *before*. She had been a child then. She remembered the light. She remembered the dead pouring back across the border like water finding a crack in a dam, hungry, confused, wearing faces their families had buried.

She was also, Ilsa said, mad as a gull in a storm, and spoke mostly to people who were not in the room.

"She won't know me," Felix said. "Why are we doing this?"

"Because you asked." Ilsa's jaw tightened. "And because I've watched you carve three tokens since we left the vault, and your hands shake every time, and I want to know why too."

* * *

Up close, Sable Voss looked less like a woman than like something the river had returned after a long journey. Her skin was thin as vellum, mapped with veins the color of drowned ink. Her hair had gone white and fine as bone-dust, spread on the pillow around a face that might have been handsome once, in the way that certain cliffs were handsome—something about the architecture of it, the stubborn angles.

Her eyes were open. They had been open, Felix suspected, for hours before anyone arrived.

"Ilsa Renn." The voice was dry leaves, but it carried. "The defector comes home to watch an old woman die. How economical."

"I brought someone who wanted to meet you," Ilsa said carefully. "A carver. From the other guild."

Something moved behind the old woman's eyes. Not interest. Something older and colder, the way ice shifts on a pond before it breaks.

"A carver," Sable repeated. "From the Guild of Impossible Things. Still keeping books, are they? Still weighing debts? A hundred years and they still haven't learned that some accounts can't be balanced. Some accounts can only be burned."

Felix stepped forward into the brazier's light. "Madam—"

They got no further. The old woman's hand shot out from under the blanket with a speed that belonged to a much younger body, seized Felix's wrist through the glove, and held it. Her grip was astonishingly strong. Her eyes went wide, and then wider, and Felix watched recognition arrive in them like weather—not gradually, but all at once, a storm front crossing a plain.

"No," Sable whispered. "No. That isn't possible. That is not *possible*."

"Madam, you're hurting—"

"*Take off the glove.*"

"Sable—" Ilsa started.

"*Take it off or I will scream this house down and every broker on the street will come running with knives.*"

Felix looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked back, and in her face Felix read the same arithmetic they were doing themselves: the door was locked, the street was watched, and a screaming dying woman could end everything they'd crossed half the territory to attempt. Slowly, watching the old woman's face the entire time, Felix worked the glove loose and drew it off.

Sable Voss turned Felix's bare hand over in both of hers, gentle now, reverent, the way one handled relics—and Felix supposed that was exactly what she was doing. Her thumb traced the callus at the base of Felix's forefinger. The groove worn into the middle finger by a lifetime of gravers and gouges. The old scar along the heel of the palm that Felix had never been able to explain and had stopped asking about years ago, because the guild's healers had no record of any injury and Maren's ghost, when asked, had gone quiet in that particular silence she kept for questions she refused to answer.

"Where did you get this hand?" Sable demanded. Her voice cracked in the middle. "Whose hand is this?"

"It's mine," Felix said. "It's always been mine."

"Your name." The old woman's fingers were shaking now, and tears stood in the seams of her eyes without falling, as if even her grief had forgotten how to finish. "Your true name. Not the guild-name. Your mother's name for you."

"Felix. Felix Voss."

The name landed in the little room like a dropped lantern.

Sable made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a sob—a sound Felix had heard before, actually, had carved tokens for widows making it over the bodies of husbands dead forty years. She released Felix's hand and pressed both of her own to her mouth, and above them her eyes stayed fixed on Felix's face as if she were trying to memorize it before it could be taken away.

"Voss," she said through her fingers. "You have his chin. You have his chin and her brow, and you have—" the hand came down, trembling, pointing at Felix's bare right hand— "*her hands.* You have Vesper's hands. The carver's hands. I'd know those hands anywhere in the world, child, because I watched them work for thirty years, because those hands taught me to hold a graver before I could write my own name."

The brazier popped. Somewhere below, in the street, a cart rattled past through the rain, and Felix heard none of it.

"My grandmother," Felix said slowly, because that was the only story that fit, the safe story, the ledger-entry story. "You knew my grandmother?"

"There was no grandmother." Sable's voice had gone very quiet, which was somehow worse than the shaking. "There was Vesper Voss. Bone-carver of the Guild of Impossible Things. Keeper of their ledgers. Restorer of the covenant—or destroyer of it, the prophecy couldn't be bothered to choose, and neither could she, in the end. There was no grandmother, child, because Vesper Voss never married. Never bore children. Vesper Voss walked up to the Sigil a hundred years ago tonight and broke it with these hands—" she squeezed Felix's wrist again, hard— "and the breaking took everything she was. Every memory. Every year. Every name. The covenant doesn't just bind realms, girl. It keeps accounts. And when you break an account, the debt goes somewhere."

"I don't understand."

"No," Sable said. "Of course you don't. That's rather the whole of it, isn't it?" She sank back against her pillows, exhausted by the effort, but her eyes never left Felix's face, drinking it in with an urgency that frightened Felix more than any assassin's blade had. "You're younger than she was. Younger by decades. And yet here you sit, wearing her craft in your fingers, wearing her face coming apart at the seams, carrying the Oathbone under your coat where I can feel it humming like a struck bell." Her gaze flicked to Ilsa. "You told her to come here. Clever, cruel girl. You wanted her to hear it from me."

"I wanted her to hear it from someone who loved her," Ilsa said.

"Loved her?" Sable laughed, wet and broken. "Child, I *hated* her for sixty years. We all did. She broke the world and then she vanished into the marshes like smoke, and we were left to dig our families out of the ash and build walls against the dead. But hate is just love that found nowhere to put itself down." She turned back to Felix. "Come closer. Closer. Let me see you."

Felix leaned in. This close, the old woman smelled of camphor and candle smoke, and beneath it something else—the same scent Felix had caught in the sealed archive, on the century-old pages of the prophecy book. Fresh ink. Fresh as yesterday.

"When the Sigil broke," Sable said, each word laid down like a stone in a wall, "the border tore. And everything that was Vesper—everything she remembered, everyone she had been—was pulled through the tear, because the covenant takes what it is owed. But debts are strange things, child. Debts want to be paid, and a debt unpaid doesn't vanish. It waits. It circulates. Like coin." Her hand rose, feather-light, and touched Felix's cheek. "Somewhere in the years after, a child was born in the saltmarsh villages who could hear the dead before she could speak. Who drew spirals in the mud that matched the Sigil exactly, though she'd never seen it. Who was given to the guild at six years old because no village could keep her. Who grew up carving bones she had no memory of learning to carve."

The room tilted. Felix gripped the bedframe.

"That's a story," they heard themselves say. Their voice sounded far away, underwater. "That's a ghost-story grandmothers tell."

"I am a grandmother," Sable said gently. "Or I would have been, if my daughter hadn't died in the second burning scare, the winter the wards failed. I am the last person alive who saw Vesper Voss with these eyes. And I am telling you, carver, keeper of impossible ledgers: you are not her granddaughter. You are not her descendant. Descendants get chins and tempers and bad knees. You got her *hands*, her *face*, her *craft*, and a hole where a life should be." Her fingers curled against Felix's cheek. "The debt came back around. The covenant paid itself. You are what was left of Vesper Voss after the fire took the rest. You are her unfinished life, walking around wearing new years, and you didn't know, and oh—oh, *child*—you didn't know."

* * *

Afterward, Felix could not have said how long they stood there while the old woman talked. Time had come unstuck, the way it did in the borderland, the way it did around the dead. Sable Voss talked and talked, hoarse and unstoppable, a dam finally broken after a hundred years, and Felix stood in the flood and let it move through them because moving was better than drowning.

She talked about the day of the first burning. She was seven. Her father had been a warden of the borderland shrine, one of the keepers who fed the Sigil its small sacrifices—names, mostly, spoken aloud, so the dead would remember they had been people. She remembered Vesper arriving at the shrine in the grey hour before dawn, alone, carrying a case of tools, and she remembered thinking the carver looked calm. That was the detail that had haunted Sable her whole long life. Not rage. Not grief. *Calm.* The calm of a woman who had decided something.

She talked about the light. White, then gold, then a color she said had no name in any living language, and the sound—she mimed covering her ears even now, a hundred years later, her thin arms shaking—not a crash but a *tear*, vast and intimate at once, like the sky being unseamed.

She talked about the weeks after. The dead walking. Her father among them, standing at their garden fence in his burial clothes with mud on his boots, saying nothing, only looking at her with an expression she had spent a century failing to describe. Hunger, she said finally. But also confusion. Also, terribly, hope. As if he had come back because someone had left the door open, and he wasn't sure whether to thank them or blame them.

"And Vesper?" Felix asked. Their voice was a stranger's. "What happened to her? To—to me?"

"No one knows." Sable shook her head. "She walked into the marshes the morning after, and the marsh doesn't give things back. Some said the dead took her. Some said she went to undo what she'd done and failed. Some said—" the old woman hesitated, and her eyes, for the first time, slid away from Felix's face— "some said the guild found her. Empty. A body with no one home. And that the guild, being what it is—keepers of impossible accounts—found a use for an empty vessel."

"Maren," Felix whispered. The eldest ghost. The one who warned them to burn the book. The one whose silences had shapes and edges, if you'd only thought to trace them.

"What?"

"Nothing." Felix straightened. Their legs felt borrowed. Everything felt borrowed. That was the joke of it, wasn't it—it always had been. "Keep talking. Please. I need—I need all of it."

But Sable was fading; Felix could see it happening, the storm of her recollection spending itself like a candle burning down to the socket. She caught Felix's bare hand again and pressed something into it—small, hard, wrapped in rotted silk. Felix unfolded the cloth with fingers that did not feel like their own and found a bone token. Old. Very old. Carved with a spiral that Felix recognized the way you recognize your own signature: the mark of the Covenant Key.

"She gave me this," Sable whispered. "Before she walked into the marsh. She knelt down—she knelt to a seven-year-old, do you understand, the woman who broke the world knelt in the mud to a child—and she put this in my hand and she said, *if a girl with my hands ever comes to you, give her this, and tell her the third relic remembers being loved.*" The old woman's breath rattled. "I kept it a hundred years, waiting for a thief with her face. Tell me, carver. Was she right? Do you remember being loved?"

And Felix, holding a hundred-year-old token in a hand that had carved it in some life they could not access, standing in a dying woman's smoke-dark room in a city built on grief, opened their mouth to say *no*—

and instead said, helplessly, truthfully, in a voice that cracked down the middle:

"The marsh smells like rain. When I dream—which I don't, I don't dream, I've never dreamed—but when I wake with the feeling of having dreamed, it always smells like rain on the saltmarsh. And there's someone singing. Someone singing to me. I don't know the song. I don't know her voice. But I know—" Felix's vision blurred. "I know she meant it kindly."

Sable Voss smiled. It transformed her face entirely, the ruin of it, and made her suddenly, unmistakably, the seven-year-old at the garden fence.

"There," she said softly. "You see? Nothing is ever really lost. It's only filed somewhere you can't reach." Her grip weakened. Her eyes were closing, the long vigil of her attention finally ending. "Go to the shrine, Vesper. Go home. And when the third relic refuses you—and it will refuse you, child, it is *her*, she guards herself, she always was a stubborn piece of work—tell it Sable kept the faith. Tell it the last Voss kept the faith."

"Wait," Felix said. "The third relic—who is *she*? What do you mean, it's *her*? Whose Nameplate is it? Whose name—"

But Sable Voss had gone wherever a hundred years of waiting goes when it finally ends, and her hand slid from Felix's wrist onto the blanket, and outside the rain kept falling on a city that had been built, stone by stone, out of the ashes of a decision Felix had made in another life.

Ilsa touched their shoulder. "We need to leave. Now. Before anyone finds her cold and starts asking who visited."

Felix didn't move. They were looking down at their bare right hand, at the calluses and the grooves and the inexplicable scar, at the old token resting in the palm like an egg in a nest.

"Ilsa," they said quietly. "When we stole the Oathbone. When I touched it. Did you see what I carved afterward? On the vault wall, without knowing I was doing it?"

Ilsa went still. "Yes."

"Say it anyway."

"You carved a spiral," Ilsa said. "And underneath it, in a script nobody uses anymore, you carved two words."

"Which words?"

Isla's voice dropped to almost nothing. "*Not again.*"

Felix closed their hand around the token until the old bone bit into flesh that had once been someone else's, and understood, with a certainty that felt less like knowledge than like gravity, that they had done all of this before—that the prophecy, the relics, the burning, the terrible arithmetic of the covenant were not ahead of them but *around* them, a wheel they had been turning for a century.

One question remained, and it was the only question that mattered now, and Sable Voss had died before Felix could ask it:

If the third relic was guarded by someone Vesper had loved—if the Nameplate bore a name Vesper had cherished enough to kill the world over—

then who, in all the borderlands of the dead, was waiting at the shrine for Felix to come home?
