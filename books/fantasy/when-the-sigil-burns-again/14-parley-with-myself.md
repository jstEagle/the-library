## 14. Parley with Myself

The Nameplate lay on the shrine's stone table between them, and neither of them touched it.

Felix had retreated to the far wall of the ruined sanctuary, where a fallen beam made something like a seat and the broken roof let down a column of moonlight wide enough to read by. The prophecy book sat open on their knees—not for comfort, but because it was the only thing in the borderland that felt entirely real. Its pages smelled of ink that should have dried a hundred years ago and hadn't.

Vesper's ghost stood at the opposite end of the shrine, her form guttering like a candle in a draft. She was Felix's height. She had Felix's hands—broad-palmed, scarred across the knuckles from decades of the carver's blade—and when she moved, the air around her remembered her shape for a heartbeat after she'd left it.

"You keep looking at my wrists," she said.

"I'm looking at the marks." Felix turned their own wrists over in the moonlight: two pale crescents each, old scars worn smooth. "I've had those since before I can remember. Which isn't saying much."

"No," Vesper said quietly. "It isn't. I know exactly how much it is."

Outside the shrine, the borderland breathed. It was not living country and not dead country but the seam between—a grey plain where grass grew upward and downward at once, where the sky held two moons and refused to say which one belonged to whom. The Sigil's light pulsed on the horizon like a slow heart, dimmer than any ledger entry said it should be, and every pulse sent a shiver through the ground that Felix felt in their teeth.

They had been circling each other for an hour since the ghost's fury had burned itself out. An hour of accusations thrown like knives and caught like gifts. Now the silence had gone soft at the edges, the way silence does when both parties are too tired to keep sharpening it.

"Ask me," Vesper said finally. "Whatever you came here to ask. Ask it properly, and I'll answer properly, and then we'll see whether either of us can live with the answers."

Felix closed the book. "Why did you break it?"

* * *

Vesper had waited a century for someone to ask her that question without already knowing what they wanted the answer to be.

The guildmasters who had come sniffing at the borderland over the years—all three of them, cowards to a woman—had asked it the way you ask a locked door to open. The Compact's agents had asked it while reaching past her for the relics, as if she were furniture. But this one, this thief wearing her bones, had asked it the way Vesper herself would have asked it: as if the answer might actually cost something.

"The covenant," Vesper began, and her voice did the thing ghosts' voices do, doubling under itself, the words arriving a half-breath late from somewhere deeper. "You've read the ledgers. You know the official version. The first pact was forged so that the dead could tend the living and the living remember the dead, a great circle of mutual care. Beautiful language. I helped write some of it, in fact—I carved the Oathbone with those very words running down its length like marrow."

She drifted closer. Not threateningly. Wearyingly.

"What the ledgers don't record is the price. A covenant is a binding, and bindings hold because something bears the strain. Do you know what bore it?"

Felix's hands went still on the book. "The dead."

"The dead," Vesper confirmed. "Every soul that crossed took up a share of the weight. That was the bargain: passage home in exchange for service. You cross, you carry your portion of the Sigil's burden, forever. No rest. No passing on. The dead don't haunt the living realm, Felix—the living realm haunts *them*. They stand in the dark holding up the sky and they call it an honor because the alternative is admitting they built their own prison and signed it in bone."

"And you decided that was wrong."

"I decided that was a lie dressed as mercy." Vesper's image flared, and for a moment Felix saw through the composed ghost-shape to whatever was underneath: a woman, gaunt, her eyes ringed with the particular exhaustion of someone who has listened to too many voices for too long. "I was a listener, like you. Like us. I heard what the dead said when they thought no one living could hear. And do you know what they said? Not gratitude. Not wisdom. They said *let go*. They said *we are tired*. They said, some of them, in voices worn down to thread—"

She stopped. Her hands, Felix's hands, curled into fists.

"They begged," Vesper finished. "A hundred years of dead souls begging to be released from a contract they never understood they were signing. And the guild called it the Great Covenant and put it in gold letters and taught children to revere it. So yes. I broke it. Deliberately. With these hands." She held them up, palms out, the way you show a magistrate empty pockets. "I shattered the Sigil, and I paid the price the breaking demanded—my whole past, every memory I owned, poured into the fracture to seal it halfway shut. Enough to keep the realms apart. Not enough to heal either of them."

The moonlight column flickered. Somewhere out on the plain, something that might have been wind or might have been weeping moved through the grey grass.

"You knew the realms would tear eventually," Felix said. "Half-mended. That's why tonight is in the book. That's why the Compact exists—you didn't just break a covenant, you started a countdown."

"I started a *conversation*," Vesper snapped, and her voice cracked like green wood. "One the guild refused to have for four hundred years. Yes, the seal was imperfect. Yes, someday it would fail unless someone reforged it properly. I knew that. I chose it anyway, because a flawed peace with an expiration date buys time to build something better, and an eternal prison builds nothing at all." Her gaze fixed on Felix, and it was unbearable, looking into her own eyes. "But tell me, carver-who-isn't. Tell me what the dead sound like in *your* cellar. Tell me what they ask you for. Because if they ask for anything other than release—if even one of them thanks the covenant for keeping them—then I'll kneel here in this ruin until the Sigil burns and admit I was wrong about everything."

* * *

Felix thought about the cellar.

They thought about the tokens laid out in rows on the workbench, each one humming faintly with the voice of a debt owed. They thought about old Corvin Ashgrave, dead eleven years, whose payment had come in last week humming a lullaby Felix had recognized—recognized the way you recognize a room you've never entered—and how, when Felix had held the token to their ear, the voice inside had said not *avenge me*, not *remember me*, but *is she eating? My daughter. Is she eating?*

And beneath that, always, underneath every voice in every token, a sound Felix had spent years telling themselves was the building settling. A vast, low groan, like a ship's hull under strain. The sound of weight being carried.

"They ask about the living," Felix said slowly. "That's most of it. Small things. Whether the harvest came in. Whether someone forgave them. One woman asks me every month if her dog remembers her." They looked down at their hands. "But under all of it—there's a sound. Like something bearing load. I used to think it was the guildhall settling."

"It's the Sigil," Vesper said. "Or rather, it's everyone holding it up. You hear it because you're half theirs already. That's what listeners are—the covenant's own ears, grown on the living side of the fence." She paused, and when she spoke again the anger had drained out of her, leaving something more dangerous behind: hope, unguarded. "So they love the ones they left. Of course they do. Love was never the question. The question is whether love requires servitude. Whether the only door we could build them was a door that locks from the outside."

Felix opened their mouth to answer and found, instead, a memory surfacing.

Not their memory. It arrived the way the others had been arriving for weeks now—sideways, unwilled, with a texture the present didn't have. Salt air and tallow smoke. The great vault beneath the old guildhall, before it burned. Their hands—or Vesper's hands, the same hands—holding the Covenant Key above the Sigil's central node while a crowd of robed figures chanted, and in the chanting, threaded thin as wire, that same groaning sound, and one voice rising above it, clear and furious:

*You're building a dam and calling it a river.*

Their own voice. Or hers. Saying it then, too.

Felix gasped. The vision broke. When they looked up, Vesper had crossed the shrine and was standing over them, her face lit with something between alarm and recognition.

"You saw it," she whispered. "The forging. You were there—in me, I mean. You have pieces of me." She laughed, and the laugh was terrible and bright. "Do you understand what that means? You aren't my descendant, whatever the elder told you. Descendants inherit blood. You inherited *me*. The price of my breaking scattered what I was, and some of it—some of it grew into a new life rather than dissolving. You are what became of the parts of Vesper Voss that the Sigil couldn't digest."

"That's not possible."

"Neither am I, technically. Here we are."

Felix stood, needing to stand, needing to put the length of the shrine between them again. The moonlight column fell between them like a border of its own. "Then answer me this. If I'm carrying pieces of you—if I'm your unfinished business walking around in borrowed flesh—why did you attack me? Why try to drive me away from the Nameplate?"

"Because you came to restore it." Vesper's voice dropped low. "Because I watched you gather the Key and steal the Oathbone, and I thought: they're rebuilding the prison. They're doing it with my own hands. And I would burn this shrine to ash before I let that happen." She lifted her chin. "But you haven't rebuilt anything yet. So I find myself wondering what, exactly, you intend."

* * *

The night wore on, and they talked the way water wears stone.

Felix told Vesper about the guild as it was now—the impossible accounts, the bone tokens, Odile's careful institutional silence. Vesper told Felix about the guild as it had been at its founding: seven carvers in a rented room, taking debts no court would recognize, believing with a ferocity that embarrassed them later that fairness could outlast death.

They argued. They agreed. They argued about agreeing.

When Vesper described the moment of the breaking—the hammer-fall, the white light, the sensation of her own history pouring out of her like water from a cracked jug—Felix found tears on their face and could not say whose grief they were grieving. When Felix described Maren's final unbinding in the saltmarsh, how the eldest ghost had spent her last coherence screaming *run, child, run*, Vesper pressed her insubstantial hands over her mouth and shook.

"She was there at the forging," Vesper managed. "Maren. She voted for the covenant. She believed in it harder than anyone. And at the end she—" The ghost's form guttered wildly. "At the end she died trying to save the thing I shattered, and her reward was a hundred years of standing guard in a guildhall, watching the burden grow heavier, and her last act was to protect *you*."

"Not me," Felix said quietly. "The ledger-keeper. Whoever held the office. I think she was protecting the truth of the accounts. Making sure someone would finally audit them."

"Audit them," Vesper repeated. She turned the phrase over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. Then, slowly: "Read me the prophecy. The actual entry. All of it."

Felix opened the book to the page with tonight's date circled in impossibly fresh ink, and read aloud:

*"On the night the moon stands doubled, the Sigil shall burn again, and the carver shall stand at its heart. What the carver carries shall decide: restore the old bond and the burden grows eternal; sever it wholly and the living world forgets its dead; or forge anew, and pay what has never yet been paid."*

Silence.

"'Pay what has never yet been paid,'" Vesper echoed. Her eyes—Felix's eyes—went distant, calculating. "Four options, and the third one isn't restore or destroy. It's rewrite." She looked up sharply. "There was a girl in the Compact city. The defector. Ilsa. You spoke of her at the shrine's edge before I drove you off—I heard. She wants the covenant rewritten so the dead can pass on."

"So the dead can choose," Felix corrected. "Door home, or door onward. Her word was mercy. Mine was choice. I think they might be the same door."

"And the price?" Vesper's voice had gone very quiet. "The book says *what has never yet been paid*. When I broke the Sigil, I paid my past. When you crossed the ferryman's water, you paid memories you can't name. The pattern is plain even to the dead, carver. This covenant will want a whole life as its binding. A complete memory, given freely. And there are only two people in all the worlds who own such a thing between them."

Felix felt it land, the way cold water lands. "Yours."

"Mine. All of it. Everything the Sigil failed to digest, still hoarded in me like a dragon's grief." Vesper spread her hands. "I have carried my past for a century with nowhere to spend it. Perhaps that was the point. Perhaps the ledger balances in directions none of us planned."

Dawn was a rumor on the edge of the borderland's doubled sky when Felix spoke again.

"If I take your memories—if the reforging takes them—then whoever walks away from that shrine won't be me. And won't be you. It'll be some third person wearing my body and holding your grief." Their throat tightened. "I spent this whole year learning that my past was stolen. I will not build a new covenant out of theft, even willing theft. There has to be another way."

"There isn't. I've had a hundred years to look for one." Vesper drifted closer, until the moonlight column divided her like a curtain divides a stage. "But hear me, Felix Voss, whoever you are, whatever you are: I will give them gladly. Every memory. The good ones, which hurt, and the bad ones, which hurt worse, and the ordinary ones, which hurt most of all because they're the ones that make a life feel real. Gladly—on one condition."

"Name it."

"Not mine. *Ours.* Whatever we forge tomorrow must include the girl's mercy. A true door. Not a burden traded for passage—a threshold the dead may cross toward rest, freely, when they are ready, and no power in either realm may bar it. Swear it as a ledger-keeper swears: as a debt entered honestly, to be paid in full."

Felix looked at the ghost who was also, in some unfathomable arithmetic, themselves. They thought of Maren screaming *run*. Of the woman asking after her dog. Of the groaning weight beneath every voice in every token, a hundred thousand shoulders under one sky.

"I swear it," Felix said. "Entered in the ledger. Witnessed by the book."

For the first time since the shrine, Vesper smiled, and the smile was young, and it was Felix's smile, and seeing it was like looking at a photograph of a house that had burned down long ago, still standing, full of light.

Then the smile died.

Her head snapped toward the eastern horizon, where the borderland met the living world, and every line of her ghost-body went rigid.

"Felix," she said. "Something is coming up the causeway. Many somethings. And they're carrying guild wards."

Felix turned. Far off across the grey plain, a line of torches had appeared, moving fast, and above them floated a light Felix knew intimately—the cold silver gleam of a guildmaster's authority-seal, burning like a small deliberate star.

Odile had found them.

And behind the guildmaster's torchline, farther back, a second light crawled along the causeway: red-orange, hungry, the color of fired pitch.

The Compact was coming too.
