## 16. The Sigil Burns Again

The fire came at dusk, which Felix should have expected. Everything in the prophecy had arrived on schedule so far—why not this too?

They saw it first as a smudge of orange against the bruised violet sky, far off across the borderland flats where the shrine stood hunched against the wind like an old woman waiting out a storm. The smoke rose straight up, unnaturally vertical, as if the sky itself were drawing it in on purpose.

"No," Ilsa said beside them. Not a denial. A measurement. She was counting something—the speed of the spread, perhaps, or the seconds they had left—and her face was doing arithmetic that ended badly.

Odile stood three paces away with her hands folded and her expression carved smooth as a ledger's cover. "I told you it would come to this."

"You *arranged* it to come to this," Felix said. The words tasted of ash already, though the fire was still a mile distant. "You sold our route. You let them follow us here."

"I let events find their natural weight." Odile's voice was the one she used for settling disputes between members who both owed the guild money—patient, faintly bored, immovable. "A century of guildmasters did nothing, and the covenant rotted quietly while the dead piled up at the border like letters no one would deliver. Something had to break, Felix. I merely chose the hour."

The heat reached them before the light did. It came across the flats in a slow wave, carrying the smell of burning juniper and old bone—the shrine's incense stores going up, the reliquary shelves, the dried offerings hung from the eaves by pilgrims who had believed, once, that their small gifts mattered.

Felix ran.

Not because running could help. Because their body refused to do anything else. The saltmarsh mud sucked at their boots, then gave way to the harder gravel of the borderland path, and Ilsa fell in beside them without being asked, and behind them Odile walked—not ran, *walked*—with the unhurried gait of someone attending a ceremony she had personally scheduled.

* * *

The shrine was already half-consumed when they reached it.

Felix had imagined it a hundred times during the flight from the guildhall: the low stone sanctuary where the Sigil lived, the great circular carving set into the shrine floor itself, cut into the bedrock of the borderland where the living world thinned to a membrane. They had expected grandeur. What they found instead was humility—a plain room, a plain floor, and the Sigil: an interlocking pattern of lines no more elaborate than a carver's practice board, worn shallow by centuries of feet walking over it, because the original makers had wanted it *used*, not worshipped.

And it was burning.

Not the wood around it. Not the roof beams crashing down in fountains of sparks. The Sigil itself burned—every line of the carving filled with flame the color of old honey, burning without fuel, burning downward into the stone. Where the fire touched, the rock went soft as wax, and through the softening stone Felix could see... movement. Shapes pressing up from beneath the world like hands against a sheet.

"Vesper," Felix breathed.

The ghost was there. Of course she was there; she had been bound to the shrine since her death, since her shattering of the covenant a century ago. But she wasn't fighting the fire. She stood at the Sigil's edge with her translucent hands pressed flat against the burning lines, and she was screaming—no sound coming out, nothing but the shape of it, her mouth open wide enough that Felix could see straight through her jaw to the flames beyond.

She was trying to hold the tear closed with her bare hands.

"The Compact fired it from outside," Ilsa shouted over the roar. She had stopped at the threshold; the heat made the air shimmer like water. "Fire arrows soaked in ash-oil. They've been preparing the mixture for decades—it burns what isn't burnable. It burns *boundaries*."

Through the ruined doorway, Felix could see them: figures in grey ash-cloaks arranged around the shrine in a careful ring, loosing arrow after arrow into the blaze, feeding it. At their center stood a tall figure Felix didn't know, arms raised, voice rising and falling in a cadence that was almost liturgy.

But Felix knew some of the others.

They were faces from the ash-city. The bone-broker who had sold Felix a cup of broth and charged them fairly. The woman who had mended their coat sleeve in a doorway and asked for nothing. Survivors' grandchildren, standing in a ring, feeding a fire that was about to eat the wall between every dead thing and every living one.

"They don't know," Felix said.

"They know exactly what they're doing." Ilsa's voice was flat. "This is the rite. This is what the Compact has trained toward for three generations. They believe when the border tears, the dead will finally be *free*—released, dispersed, gone wherever things go when there's nowhere left to linger."

"And if they're wrong?"

"Then we learn why the original pact was made." Ilsa drew a short knife. "I've read the survivors' accounts, Felix. All of them. Do you know what none of them describe? What the world was like *before* the first burning. Not one of them remembered. That's what frightened me. Not the fire. The forgetting."

The roof groaned. A beam the thickness of a man came down in a column of embers, and through the gap it left Felix could see the sky—and the sky was wrong. Stars showing through it that weren't stars. Or rather, stars that were also something else: pale, patient, *numerous*, pressing close like faces at a window.

The dead were gathering above the tear.

* * *

Felix crossed the burning floor because there was no version of this story in which they didn't.

Heat seared through their boots. Embers landed on their sleeves and they beat them out without slowing, eyes fixed on the honey-colored blaze in the floor, on Vesper's ghost kneeling at its edge with her hands sinking into stone that no longer remembered how to be solid. Up close, Felix could see that she wasn't only screaming. She was *carving*. Her fingers moved along the burning lines in patterns Felix knew—knew in their wrists, in the heels of their palms—in the wordless grammar their hands had been speaking all their life.

She was trying to recut the Sigil even as it burned away beneath her.

"It won't hold!" Felix shouted, though whether a ghost could hear them over the fire, they didn't know. "You can't hold it alone!"

Her head turned. Through the distortion of the heat-haze, her face found theirs, and Felix watched recognition arrive in her the way cold water arrives in a boot—slowly, then all at once. She looked at Felix's hands. At the keepsake bone strung at their throat. At their face, which was her face, which was the face she'd worn before the first fire took everything she remembered.

Her mouth shaped a name. Felix couldn't hear it. Perhaps it was *Vesper*. Perhaps it was the other name—the one the dying elder had given them, the name that belonged to whoever Felix had been before they were anyone at all.

The first tear opened then, and thinking became impossible.

It happened at the northern arc of the circle, where the carving had burned clean through. The stone didn't crack so much as *thin*, going translucent as oiled paper, and then it simply wasn't there anymore—a hole in the floor that was also a hole in the world. Through it came a sound Felix had spent their whole career hearing secondhand, filtered through bone tokens and client confessions: the true voice of the unquiet dead, the sound they made when no ledger stood between them and their hunger.

It was not a moan. It was not a scream. It was a *crowd*—thousands of voices speaking at once, each one individually reasonable, collectively unbearable, saying *mine, mine, mine* in a hundred different languages about a hundred different stolen things: lives, names, children, hours, breath.

The first of them came through.

It had been a person once—you could see the architecture of a person still, the way you can see the architecture of a house after fire. It pulled itself up out of the hole with terrible patience, joints bending in directions grief had taught it, and it turned its face toward the nearest living thing—which was one of the Compact archers, a young man whose hood had fallen back, whose bow dropped from his hands.

"*Mine,*" the dead thing said, in the young man's own voice. "*Give it back.*"

He screamed. Everyone screamed. The neat ritual ring of the Compact broke apart into scattering, stumbling shapes, and the fire they had tended so carefully now stood between them and escape.

More came through. A second tear opened at the southern arc, then a third, and the holes began to widen as the membrane between worlds lost its tension entirely—like fabric giving way along a seam, slow and then suddenly all at once.

Felix grabbed Vesper's wrist—or passed their hand through it, caught it, held it somehow, in that half-real way ghosts permit—and dragged her back from the widening blaze.

"The relics!" they shouted. "Key, Oathbone—we do it now, we reforge it now, before the tear gets past—"

"*No.*" For the first time, Vesper's voice carried—thin and cracked, but hers, arriving inside Felix's skull like a thought that wasn't quite their own. Her eyes were fixed on the pouring dead with an expression Felix had never seen on any face, living or otherwise: guilt so old it had become geology. "*Not like this. Reforging under a burning Sigil, with the border already torn—the covenant will seal the tear with whatever it can reach. It'll bind them in mid-passage. It will make them part of the wall.*"

Felix looked at the dead spilling through the holes. Looked at the softening stone. Understood.

"That's what happened last time," they whispered. "That's why the dead hate the covenant. The first reforging—your reforging—it sealed them *in*."

"Some of them." Vesper's hands were shaking. A century of guilt moved behind her eyes like weather. "Some of them, yes. I told myself they'd pass on eventually. They never passed on. They just... hardened. You've heard them, carver. Every token you've ever carved, every voice in your ledgers—that's the sound the wall makes when it thinks."

Above them, the sky-tears widened. The honey-fire in the Sigil's lines guttered, flickered—and Felix felt it, felt the exact moment the prophecy finished keeping its promise. Tonight's date, circled in ink that smelled fresh. *The Sigil burns again.* Done. Accomplished. On schedule, to the hour.

Everything after tonight was blank pages.

The tallest of the tears split with a sound like a ship's mast breaking, and through it poured not one dead thing but dozens, a gray flood of them spreading across the shrine floor and out through the broken walls, flowing downhill toward the living lands beyond the borderland—toward the saltmarsh, the ferry, the towns with their lamps lit against the dark, all those warm rooms full of breathing people who had no idea what was crossing the flats toward them at the speed of hunger.

Ilsa appeared at Felix's shoulder, soot-streaked, bleeding from a cut above her eye. Behind her, at the edge of the light, Odile stood watching the catastrophe she had purchased with perfect composure—though Felix noticed, with a strange distant pity, that her hands were no longer steady.

"Well?" Ilsa demanded. "You have the Key. You have the Oathbone. The Nameplate is ten feet from you, under that altar stone. Reforge it. Seal it. *Now.*"

"And wall them in," Felix said. "All of them. Forever. That's the price written into the old covenant, and we only learned it tonight."

"Then rewrite the price! You're the carver—carve something new—"

"I can't carve a new covenant out of a burning building with the dead pouring through the walls!" Felix's voice cracked. "There has to be a binding. There always has to be a binding—Ilsa, the covenant runs on memory, on a whole life given whole, and I don't *have* one—"

They stopped.

Because Vesper was looking at them. And her expression had changed—guilt still there, grief still there, but underneath both, quiet and enormous, something that looked terribly like relief. Like a debt-holder hearing, at last, that someone had come to settle accounts.

"I have one," she said.

* * *

The dead reached the threshold of the shrine as the three of them—two living, one less than living—turned to face the altar stone together.

Beyond them, the fire climbed the sky, and the blank pages waited.

The largest tear yawned wide, and out of it stepped a dead thing wearing a face Felix knew—not from the ash-city, not from the guildhall, but from their own ledgers. A client. Someone whose debt Felix had balanced years ago, whose bone token still hung in the cellar workshop of a guildhall half a world away.

It looked at Felix. And in a voice gone thin as paper, it said:

"*Carver. You keep the books. Tell me—what do I still owe?*"

And Felix, with the fire roaring and the world tearing and Vesper's hand closing around theirs, understood that the answer to that question was the only thing left that could save anyone—and that they had until the last line of the Sigil burned away to find it.
