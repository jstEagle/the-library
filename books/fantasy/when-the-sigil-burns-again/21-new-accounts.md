## 21. New Accounts

The guildhall smelled of new timber and old smoke, and Felix Voss stood in the middle of it with a ledger open across both arms, trying to decide which of those two things should be trusted less.

Three weeks had passed since the saltmarsh. Three weeks since the Sigil burned and held, since the tearing sky had knit itself shut over the borderland like scar tissue, since Felix had woken face-down in the reeds with three relics fused into one smooth length of bone and no memory older than this year. Three weeks of rebuilding. Carpenters swarmed the guildhall's scorched east wing by day; at night, when they left, the ghosts came back.

That was the difference. That was everything.

Before, the dead had clung to the guildhall like barnacles to a hull—Maren in her corner chair, the nameless child on the third stair, a dozen others pressed into walls and rafters, unable to leave, unable to rest, lingering the way water lingers in a wound. Now they came and went. Felix could feel them arriving through the new covenant's open door: a softening at the edge of perception, a warmth, then a presence that chose to be there rather than had nowhere else to go. The child on the stairs came every evening to watch the carpenters' lamps and left before dawn, walking—actually *walking*—out through the front doors into whatever waited for her beyond. Maren was gone entirely. She had passed on the night the covenant sealed, and Felix had not been able to mourn her properly because he could not remember mourning anyone before her, and grief without comparison felt strangely weightless, like a coin from a country that no longer existed.

He set the ledger on the trestle table in what had been Odile's office and was now, by unanimous and slightly stunned agreement of the remaining guild members, his.

The room still bore her shape. Her heavy chair sat behind the desk like an accusation. Her seal-ring drawer hung half open, empty since she'd surrendered it. On the wall, the map of the guild's territories had a burn scar across the borderland, and nobody had suggested replacing it, because everyone agreed the map was now wrong in ways no cartographer could fix.

Felix opened the ledger to a fresh page and wrote the heading in his careful carver's hand:

**Accounts Reopened — Guild of Impossible Things**

Below it, the first entry:

*Item: One covenant, reforged. Paid in full by Vesper Voss, deceased, and F. Voss, living, jointly. Balance owed: none. Debt acknowledged: eternal.*

It wasn't a debt the ledger could hold. He wrote it anyway. Some things belonged in ink so that hands, years from now, would touch the page and know someone had tried.

* * *

Ilsa arrived at midmorning, as she did every day now, letting herself in through the front doors with the ease of someone who had decided the guildhall was half hers and dared anyone to dispute it. She carried a satchel of Compact documents—their surrender terms, their internal ledgers, the confessions of elders who had spent a century building an institution out of grief—and she dumped them on the trestle table with a thud that raised dust.

"More of them," she said. "Two more elders have come forward since yesterday. They want to know if the offer stands."

"It stands." Felix didn't look up from the ledger. "Anyone who served the Compact can come to the guild. Anyone who lost someone to the old covenant can come to the guild. The dead get passage; the living get honest trade. That's the whole document."

"You make it sound simple."

"It is simple. It's just never been tried."

Ilsa pulled up a stool and sat, elbows on knees, studying him with the flat gray gaze that had unnerved him their first week and now felt almost like shelter. She had cut her hair short since the shrine—a practical choice, she'd said, though Felix suspected it was also a funeral, a way of shedding the person who had crept through vaults and betrayed her own guild. She wore no Compact colors anymore. She wore nothing at all that announced her, which in her case was itself an announcement.

"Simple," she repeated. "Right. So explain to me again how we price an impossibility fairly. Because I've read your ledgers, Voss, all hundred years of them, and I'll tell you what I found. Every entry has a price. Almost none of them have a *reason*. A widow pays a bone token for her husband's last words—but who decides what the words cost? Who decides what a haunting costs? Your guild traded in longing for centuries and called the exchange rate tradition."

Felix closed the ledger gently. "I know."

"And?"

"And you're right. That's why we're rewriting it." He turned the book toward her. "New rule one. No price may be set by the party who profits from the buyer's desperation. Every valuation gets a second assessor. Rule two: nothing bought from the dead may be sold without the dead's consent—and consent means they can say no and mean it. Rule three—" he tapped the page where he'd written it out the night before, in ink still faintly shining wet in his memory, "—the guild takes no commission on passage. Crossing over is free. It was always supposed to be free. The old guild charged tolls on grief because grief can't haggle."

Ilsa read for a long moment. When she looked up, her eyes were bright in a way she would have denied under torture.

"The Compact will hate this."

"The Compact doesn't exist anymore. You told me so yourself. You said it died at the shrine, along with its reason for being." Felix smiled, small and crooked. "What exists is a few hundred people who spent their whole lives hating something, and now need somewhere to put their hands."

Ilsa laughed—a rough, startled sound, as if her own voice had surprised her. "Somewhere to put their hands," she echoed. "Fine. Fine. But I'm watching you, carver. Every account, every assessment. If this guild starts charging widows by the syllable again, I'll burn it down myself, and I'll enjoy it more the second time."

"That," said Felix, "is exactly why I wanted you as partner rather than prisoner."

* * *

Odile came at dusk.

Felix knew she was in the building before he saw her—the wards hummed differently around her, or perhaps that was only his own tension, strung through the guildhall's bones like wire. She climbed the stairs slowly. She knocked on the open door of the office that had been hers, which was a courtesy she had never once extended to anyone during her tenure, and Felix understood that the knocking itself was a resignation.

She looked smaller. That was the cruelest part. The woman who had forbidden him to act, who had sold his route to both the Compact and her own councilors to force a crisis she thought she could manage, who had arrived at the shrine expecting to sweep up the pieces and reassert a century of comfortable cowardice—that woman had been vast, institutional, immovable. The woman standing in the doorway was just tired, and graying, and carrying a wrapped bundle under one arm like a penitent carries candles.

"Guildmaster," Felix said, and let the word sit there, neither honorific nor insult.

"Not anymore. Not after tonight." She crossed the room without invitation and set the bundle on the desk. Inside the cloth, when she unwrapped it, lay the guildmaster's chain of office—heavy links of bronze, each stamped with a sigil that no longer burned but still, somehow, seemed watchful. "I've already told the council. They accepted. I think most of them are relieved. Cowardice hates company even more than guilt does."

"Why did you do it?" Felix asked. He had asked before, at the shrine, shouting over the fire. She had never answered. "You sold my route. You told the Compact where I'd be and told your own enforcers the same. You wanted me cornered there. Why?"

Odile looked at the window, where the evening light was going amber over the rooftops of a city that had nearly ended twice in one lifetime.

"Because every guildmaster for a hundred years chose to do nothing," she said. "And every one of us told ourselves the same story: that doing nothing *was* the choice, the wise one, the one that kept the balance. My predecessor taught it to me. Hers taught it to her. We inherited a prophecy we were afraid of and we called our fear prudence." Her jaw tightened. "When you started asking questions, I saw a century of my own choices about to be audited by a bone-carver with a stolen memory, and I could not bear it. So I tried to control the crisis. Force it early. Force it small. Force it somewhere I could stand between the realms and take credit for holding the line." She shook her head slowly. "I nearly unmade the world to avoid admitting I'd been wrong since the day I took office."

The ghosts, Felix noticed, had gathered. Not intruding—never intruding, that was the covenant's grace—but present along the edges of the room like guests at a hearing. He recognized some of them now. The child. A ferryman who had drowned two hundred years ago and crossed freely for the first time last week, yet still liked to visit. A woman in merchant's clothes who had been the Compact's first victim, long before it had a name. They had come to listen. Perhaps they had come to witness.

"What happens to you now?" Felix asked.

"Exile, probably. Or worse, obscurity." Odile almost smiled. "I've been offered a position advising the Council of Nine on covenant affairs. I intend to decline it. I've done enough damage from inside institutions." She pushed the chain of office across the desk toward him. "This is yours. The council ratified it this morning. Guildmaster Voss."

Felix stared at the bronze links. Under his fingers, the metal felt warm—not magically, he suspected, just warm from being carried against a guilty chest for a century's worth of successors.

"I don't want it."

Something flickered across Odile's face—hurt, then relief, then a kind of exhausted respect. "Then refuse it loudly. Make them find another way. That's the first honest thing any of us will have done in a hundred years."

"No. That's not what I mean." Felix picked up the chain anyway, feeling its weight, feeling the way it wanted to settle around his neck like a yoke. "I mean I won't wear it *like you did*. This isn't a crown. As far as I'm concerned it's a tool, like a chisel. It does a job. When it stops doing the job, someone else picks it up." He set it down beside the ledger, deliberately casual, deliberately unthroned. "And the first job it's going to do is preside over your testimony. Full record. Everything you knew, everything you did, entered into the guild accounts as a debt. Not punishment—debt. Debts can be paid. Punishments just rot."

Odile went very still. Then, quietly: "And how does a woman my age pay a debt like that?"

"Same way anyone does." Felix opened the ledger to a blank page and uncapped his ink. "One entry at a time. Starting tonight. Sit down, Guildmaster Emerita. Let's begin with the year you learned about the prophecy, and let's not stop until we reach the saltmarsh."

For a long moment he thought she would refuse. He thought he saw it gathering in her—the old institutional reflex, the urge to seal archives and forbid action. But then her shoulders came down, all the way down, for the first time since he'd known her, and she drew up the visitor's chair—the plain one, not the heavy carved throne behind the desk—and she sat.

"My predecessor told me about the prophecy in my second year," she began. Her voice cracked once, steadied. "There was a sealed annex beneath the archive. I was sworn to secrecy and sworn to inaction in the same breath..."

Felix wrote. The lamp burned low. Outside, the city settled into evening, and the dead drifted through the guildhall's open doors like moths who had finally found a flame worth trusting, and the ledger filled page after page with the truth, which was, in the end, the only impossible thing the guild had never traded honestly until now.

* * *

Near midnight, Ilsa appeared in the doorway, arms crossed, leaning against the frame. She listened to Odile's testimony for a while without expression, then caught Felix's eye and tilted her head toward the corridor.

He followed her out onto the landing. Below, the great hall lay half-rebuilt, moonlight falling through the uncovered roof beams onto scaffolding and stacked timber and the faint, patient shapes of ghosts wandering the floor they had haunted for a century and now merely visited.

"Well?" Ilsa asked. "Verdict. Is she paying?"

"She's paying. It'll take years."

"Good." Ilsa was quiet a moment. "You know the Compact survivors voted today. Formally. We're dissolving. What's left of us wants incorporation into the guild—as assessors. Second valuations. The people who hated you longest, checking your arithmetic."

Felix laughed softly, so as not to carry. "Rule one made flesh."

"Don't get sentimental. Half of them still think you're an abomination wearing a dead woman's life." She shrugged. "But they trust the ledger more than they trust their own memories now. Everyone does. There's talk—up north, in the Compact cities—of bringing disputes here. Not hauntings. *Disputes*. Living people, contract arguments, inheritance wars, wanting impossible things adjudicated by a guild that finally keeps honest books." She glanced sideways at him. "You'd be turning the Guild of Impossible Things into a court, Voss. A real one. First in history."

Felix leaned on the railing and looked down at the hall—at the child ghost chasing motes of dust in the moonlight, at the ferryman dozing in Maren's old chair, at the space where fear used to live in this building and no longer did.

"A court, a market, a door for the dead," he murmured. "Impossible things, traded fairly." He thought of tomorrow's date, uncircled, waiting in the ledger. "Write it up. All of it. We open accounts Monday."

Isla pushed off the railing, and paused. "One more thing. A courier came at sundown. No guild mark, no Compact seal. Just a parcel, addressed to you personally, in handwriting the courier swore was a woman's." She reached into her coat and produced a small package, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone brittle with age. "Whoever sent it paid for delivery a very long time ago. The postmark's over ninety years old."

Felix took it. The oilcloth was cold. Through it, faintly, he felt the unmistakable hum of carved bone—and within the hum, thin as a thread, a voice he did not recognize and somehow knew: *Vesper's* hand, reaching across a century to deliver one last thing.

He untied the cord as Ilsa watched, breath held, and the wrapping fell away from what lay inside.
