## 1. The Keeper's Routine

There were three certainties in Theo Delacroix's life, and he counted them each night the way other men counted sheep.

The first was that the Thornfell gate would hold. It had held for as long as anyone in Lowmark could remember—longer, probably, though nobody who remembered longer was talking—and it would hold tonight, and tomorrow night, because wards did not care about weather or war or whether the keeper had remembered to buy bread.

The second was that Wren Ashvale would be at the Drowned Lantern by the time his rounds were done, occupying the corner table nearest the window like a cat that had decided the spot belonged to her on the strength of having sat in it once.

The third was that no one, anywhere, would remember that Theo Delacroix existed.

He had made peace with the third one years ago. Invisibility, he'd found, was less a curse than a well-tailored coat: it fit him perfectly, kept out the rain, and asked nothing of him but that he wear it.

The gate stood at the end of the pass where the mountains pinched together like a fist closing. By day it was unremarkable—two leaning stones older than any kingdom, furred with moss, the kind of thing a traveler might piss against without a second thought. By night, if you knew how to look, you could see the wards laid over it like frost on a windowpane: thin silver traceries that hummed at the edge of hearing, the way a struck bell hums after the sound is gone.

Theo knew how to look. He had known for ten years, which was also, as far as he could reckon, the whole length of his life.

He walked the circuit now under a sky thick with stars—the Thornfell sky was always generous with stars, as if the mountains had bargained for them specially—and pressed his bare palm to the left-hand stone the way he did every seventh night.

"Evening," he said, because ten years of talking to a gate makes a man polite.

The stone said nothing back, which was correct. But beneath his hand he felt the ward's pulse, slow and steady as a sleeping heart, and beneath *that*—

Beneath that, something else.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. A flicker, deep down in the silver tracery, like a candle guttering in a draft that wasn't there. And through his palm—through the old burn scar he'd carried since before memory began—a single, sharp ache, bright as a needle.

Theo snatched his hand back and stood very still in the dark, listening.

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere downslope an owl complained about its evening. Nothing else. The wards glowed their usual silver, serene as Sunday.

"Hm," said Theo, to the gate, to the owl, to himself.

He pressed his palm to the stone again. Steady pulse. No flicker. No ache. Just the familiar hum, and the faint warmth of the scar, which was also normal—except that it wasn't, quite, because the scar hadn't warmed in six years, and he remembered exactly when it had stopped, because remembering things was rare enough in his life to be worth noting.

"Right," Theo told the gate. "You're drunk. We're both going to pretend this didn't happen."

The gate, wisely, declined to comment.

* * *

The Drowned Lantern sat at the crook of the border road, close enough to Lowmark that the magistrate could pretend she didn't know what went on there, and far enough that she didn't have to. Its sign depicted a lantern drowning in a barrel of ale, which told you most of what you needed to know about the establishment's priorities. Inside it smelled of woodsmoke, spilled cider, and the particular staleness of a room that has been argued in for two hundred years.

Wren Ashvale had the corner table, as foretold. She had her boots up on the bench opposite, her hat tipped over her eyes, and a half-finished plate of pie in front of her that she was guarding with the relaxed vigilance of a woman who would absolutely stab someone over the last slice.

"You look terrible," she said without lifting the hat.

"That's remarkable," Theo said, hanging his cloak on the peg. "I haven't said a word."

"I can hear your face doing it from here. Sitting down?"

"I live here."

"Sitting *down*, Delacroix. You're hovering. Men who hover want something, and I'm not sharing the pie."

"I don't want your pie." He sat across from her, nudging her boots aside to make room. "Your pie is a crime against pastry. Marta uses turnip."

"She does not."

"There are turnip chunks, Wren. I have seen them. I have *eaten* them, under protest, because you ordered it and then abandoned it halfway through like everything else you've ever started."

Wren lifted the hat brim just enough to expose one eye, brown and sharp as a hawk's. "One abandoned pie. One. And it was a strategic retreat."

"It was turnip."

"It was a *strategic retreat*." The eye narrowed. "You're deflecting. You came in here with a face like a man who's found a dead badger in his well. Talk."

Theo signaled Marta for a cider, mostly to buy time, and considered how to put it. This was the trouble with a life built on invisibility: when something finally happened, there was no graceful way to mention it.

"The gate flickered," he said.

Wren's boots came off the bench. That was the thing about smugglers—you learned to read them by what they stopped doing. Wren stopping being casual was worth more than any garrison report.

"Flickered how?"

"Like a candle in a draft. Half a heartbeat. Then fine."

"When?"

"An hour ago."

"And your hand?" Her gaze dropped to his right palm, folded loosely on the table. Everyone in the valley knew about Theo's scar; it was the sort of burn that drew the eye, a whorl of pale ridged tissue like a fingerprint made by someone with very strong opinions. Nobody knew where it came from, including, as far as anyone could tell, Theo himself. He'd arrived in Thornfell ten years ago with the scar already on his hand, a letter of appointment already in his pocket, and not so much as a surname history to account for either.

"It ached," he admitted.

Wren sat back. For a moment all the wry humor drained out of her face, and what remained underneath was older, and harder, and more tired. "In six years?"

"First time since the winter the mill burned."

"That was a bad winter."

"That was a very bad winter." He accepted the cider from Marta, who took his coin with the impersonal efficiency of a woman who had long since stopped wondering why the gatekeeper only ever drank alone with a smuggler. "It's probably nothing. Stones settle. Old magic hiccups. There's probably a perfectly boring explanation."

"There usually is," Wren agreed. Then: "You don't believe that."

"No," Theo said. "I don't."

The fire popped. Across the room, two drovers were arguing about horses with the escalating sincerity of men who would be best friends again by morning. Normal sounds. A normal night. Theo wrapped both hands around his mug and felt the scar throb once, faintly, like something turning over in its sleep.

"Ten years," Wren said.

"What about ten years?"

"You've kept that gate ten years. Rain, snow, the year the wolves came down, the year the tax men came down—worse than the wolves, that. Ten years, and it's never flickered."

"Thank you for the summary. Very thorough. Shall I fetch my own headstone?"

"Shall you *check* it?" She leaned forward. "Tonight. Properly. Not the seven-night pat-and-chat. The full walk. Every anchor stone, every thread."

"I checked it an hour ago."

"You checked it before it flickered. Check it after. That's the difference between a keeper and a man with a routine." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't, and Theo understood—as he always did, and always resented slightly—that she wasn't needling him. She was worried. Wren Ashvale expressed worry exclusively through insults and logistics, like most people express love.

"The full walk takes until dawn," he said.

"Yes."

"And I'm on duty again tomorrow."

"Yes."

"You're volunteering me for a night of freezing my fingers off pacing a mountain pass because of a hiccup."

"A flicker." She picked up her fork, speared a piece of pie, and pointed it at him like a tiny accusatory flag. "And yes. Because you came straight here instead of going home to bed, which means the hiccup scared you, which means it should scare me, which means—" the fork swung toward the window, toward the black shoulder of the mountain beyond, "—something down there is awake enough to hiccup."

*Down there.* People said it casually in Thornfell, the way they said *up north* or *across the water*. Beneath the mountain. Beneath the pass. Down there, where nobody went and nobody asked about, because asking questions about what lay under Thornfell was the fastest way to learn that the locals changed the subject with impressive coordination.

Theo looked at his palm. In the firelight the scar seemed almost to move, the whorls shifting like rings in water.

"If I do the full walk," he said, "you're coming with me."

That got the eyebrow. "I'm a respectable businesswoman."

"You're a smuggler."

"*Licensed* smuggler. Mostly. The license is pending."

"Your license has been pending for four years."

"Licenses are a journey, Theo." But she was already reaching for her coat, because of course she was; she'd been reaching for it since he walked in. "Fine. Dawn patrol. I'll bring the good flask, and if we find nothing, you owe me a real breakfast, with actual butter, from an actual cow."

"And if we find something?"

Wren paused, halfway into her sleeve, and for a heartbeat her face did something complicated—not fear exactly, but the shape fear leaves behind when it visits often enough to know the house.

"Then we'll want the breakfast anyway," she said. "Trust me."

* * *

They walked out under the stars at half past midnight, when even the Drowned Lantern's arguments had guttered low. Frost had come down off the peaks and silvered the road, and their breath hung around them like ghosts with nowhere better to be.

Theo led, because it was his gate. Wren followed a pace behind and to the left, because it was her habit never to walk directly behind anyone, and because, she claimed, the view was better.

"Anchor stone first," Theo said, peeling off toward the cairn below the track. "Then the standing pair. Then the arch itself. If the flicker was real, it'll have left a residue. Wards bruise, like fruit."

"Wards bruise," Wren repeated. "Lovely. And what bruises a ward?"

"Nothing, usually. They're woven tight. Whoever set these—" he patted the cairn's topmost stone, feeling the familiar hum rise to meet his palm, steady, steady, "—knew their craft. These aren't garrison work. Garrison wards keep out bandits. These are..."

He trailed off.

His palm was resting on the stone, and the ache had come back.

Not the needle-flick of earlier. Something deeper, slower—a pulling, like a tooth remembering it could hurt. The scar's whorls had gone warm, warmer than skin had any right to be in the frost, and beneath the ward's steady silver hum he heard—or thought he heard, or *felt*, down in the marrow of the sound—a second note. Faint. Rhythmic. Almost like breathing.

Almost like counting.

"Theo." Wren's voice had gone quiet and flat, the smuggler's voice, the one that meant her hands were near her knives. "Your hand's glowing."

"It's not glowing," Theo said. "It's—"

It was glowing. Faintly, from within, the scar lit along its ridgelines like veins of ember seen through smoke, and with every pulse of that buried light the ache deepened, and beneath it all, clear now, unmistakable, came the second sound. Not breathing. Not counting.

A knock.

Slow. Patient. Three soft blows, from somewhere far beneath the mountain, as if something down in the dark had knocked politely on a door and was waiting, with infinite courtesy, to see whether anyone would answer.

The light in his palm flared white.

Theo cried out and staggered back, clutching his wrist, and the world went sideways—the stars wheeling overhead, Wren shouting his name, the gate humming, humming, humming—and in the center of his vision, branded across everything, seared onto the inside of his eyelids the way a forge marks itself into a man's sight:

A word.

Written in fire. Written in the old script of the deep places, the script carved into boundary stones and coffin lids and the undersides of bridges where old things cross.

He could not read it.

But some part of him, some part ten years drowned and deeper than memory, heard the knock come a fourth time—and understood, with a certainty that turned his blood to frost water, that it was not knocking to be let in.

It was knocking to collect.
