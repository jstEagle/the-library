## 2. Night Without Stars

Theo Delacroix had a theory, developed over ten uneventful years and defended with the zeal of a man who had very little else to defend, that the wards of Thornfell could be read like weather.

A low hum in the gate-stones meant rain coming. A sour taste at the back of the throat meant someone had tried to chisel a loose sigil off the western pillar again — usually children, occasionally Wren Ashvale, and once, memorably, a tax collector from Lowmark who had mistaken the gate for an unregistered toll station and tried to dismantle it on principle. The wards spoke all night if you knew how to listen: a drowsy murmur in summer, a sharp chatter when frost crept up from the mountain, and on the rare nights when something wild moved in the high passes, a long low note like a finger run round the rim of a wet cup.

Tonight they were silent.

Not quiet — silent. There was a difference, and Theo felt it in his teeth the way you feel a missing stair. He stood at the top of the keeper's ladder with his lantern raised, listening to a silence so complete it seemed to lean against him, and told himself, in the reasonable tone he used for frightened travelers and for himself on bad nights, that stones were allowed to have moods.

"Say something," he murmured to the gate.

The gate said nothing.

He climbed down and pressed his palm flat against the eastern pillar, as he had done ten thousand times before, and for one merciful heartbeat nothing happened. Then the sigil burned.

He'd had aches before. Ten years of dull throb, a twinge when the frost came, a sting when someone touched the stones wrong. This was not that. This was white-hot wire drawn through his hand, and he tore free of the pillar and staggered back into the snow, cradling his palm, and the light was coming *from* it — a hard silver glare leaking between his fingers as though he'd caught a star and it objected.

"No," Theo said, conversationally, because terror made him polite. "No, no, we don't do this."

His hand did not listen. He pried his fingers open and looked.

The sigil had always been a small thing, a knot of fine lines no bigger than a coin, faded to the color of old ink. Now it blazed, and around it new lines were crawling — writing itself, letter by letter, seared into his skin in a script he did not know and yet somehow recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall. His stomach turned over slowly, like a boat coming loose from its mooring.

It was spelling a word.

It finished, and went dark, and left behind a single line of scarred characters across his palm that he could not read but which he understood, in the marrow-deep way one understands falling, meant *him*.

* * *

The tavern at Hollowbridge sat exactly where the border stopped pretending to be a border, and it was there, out of a decade of habit rather than any hope of sense, that Theo went.

Marda's hearth was doing its best against the cold. Six locals, two drovers, one dog, and Wren Ashvale occupying the corner table the way she occupied everything — thoroughly and without apology — boots up, a half-finished plate of pie in front of her, and a map she was pretending not to study spread under her mug. Smugglers, in Theo's experience, studied maps the way other people studied scripture, and lied about both.

He must have looked strange, because the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet around bad news. He hung his coat on a peg that wasn't his, which was as close to drama as he ever came, and stood dripping on the flagstones.

"Theo." Wren didn't move her boots. Her eyes did the moving for her, quick and gray and missing nothing. "You're pale. You're never pale. You're the color of oatmeal on principle."

"The stars are gone."

Someone's spoon hit a bowl. The dog lifted its head.

"Gone," Wren repeated.

"All of them. Every star over the fell, from the ridge to the tree line, in one moment. Not clouded. Not covered. *Gone.* I walked the whole boundary road looking up like an idiot and there is nothing above Thornfell tonight but black, and the wards—"

His voice betrayed him on the last word. He heard it crack and hated it, hated being seen, hated that ten years of careful invisibility had just come apart in a tavern doorway.

"The wards died," he said. "I felt it happen. Like glass. All of them at once, every stone from here to the pass, and my hand—" He held it up, palm out, and Marda dropped a ladle.

The scar-script glinted in the firelight, faintly silver, like a vein of ore under skin.

Wren's boots came off the table. That alone frightened him more than the sky had; in ten years he had never once seen Wren Ashvale move quickly unless money or danger was involved, and her face now had gone smooth and careful in a way he'd only seen on people deciding what not to say.

"Show me your hand," she said.

"You've seen it a hundred times."

"Not like this." She took it without waiting, turned it to the light, and went very still. Her thumb traced the letters — lightly, but even that ghost-touch sent a shiver up his arm — and whatever she read or didn't read, her jaw tightened.

"Do you know it?" he asked.

"No." Too fast. Then, softer, honest in spite of herself: "It's not smuggler's mark. It's older. Theo, sit down before you fall down, and tell me from the beginning."

So he did, in the low voice men use in taverns when the world has misbehaved: the silence in the stones, the burn, the letters. The locals drifted closer in the way of small communities, which is to say they pretended to sweep things and refill things while absorbing every word. Old Tam from the mill said the stars had flickered once in his grandfather's day, and was hushed. The drovers decided, wisely, to be elsewhere by dawn.

"And you feel it," Wren said. It wasn't quite a question. She hadn't let go of his hand. "The wards dying. In your palm."

"Like losing a tooth I didn't know I had."

"That's not normal keeper's ache. Keepers feel the wards. They don't *grieve* them." She looked up at him, and for the first time since he'd known her — through flooded fords, through a customs raid, through the year she'd talked him into hiding a wagonload of untaxed brandy inside the ward-line because, she'd argued, technically it was already inside his jurisdiction — she looked afraid. "What are you, Theo Delacroix?"

"I'm the gatekeeper."

"You're a man with a dead man's name and a brand that writes itself, sitting in a border tavern the night the sky went out. Don't give me the job description."

He opened his mouth to answer — with what, he had no idea — and the lamp above the bar guttered.

Not blew out. *Guttered*, all of them at once, hearth and lamps and candle-ends, flames bending sideways toward the door as though the night outside had inhaled. The dog whined and put itself under a bench. Frost bloomed across the windows in fern-patterns, fast, greedy, and through the glass Theo saw the frost on the *inside* arrange itself — briefly, horribly — into rows. Columns. Lines of writing, there and gone, like breath on a mirror.

Then the flames righted themselves, and the room was only a room, cold and smelling of smoke, everyone frozen mid-gesture.

"Right," said Marda, into the silence, in the voice of a woman who had buried two husbands and a mortgage and would not be done in by weather, "everyone finish your soup."

But Wren was already pulling her coat from the chair back, and Theo saw that her hands weren't quite steady, and that she was looking at the mountains through the window with an expression he'd never seen on her: not calculation. Recognition.

"You know something," he said quietly.

"I know routes," she said. "I know that everything I know runs through those passes, and I know that tonight every route I've ever run feels wrong, like walking a hallway and finding the doors moved. And I know—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "My grandmother had a saying. Never take coin from anyone who pays in remembering. I always thought it was about card sharps."

Outside, far off toward the fells, something howled. It was almost a wolf's howl, the way a drawing is almost a portrait — right lines, wrong everything else — and beneath it, so low Theo felt it in his boot-soles rather than heard it, the mountain groaned. A long, grinding exhalation, like a vault door testing its hinges after a century shut.

Every head in the tavern turned to Theo.

That was the worst of it, he thought later. Ten years invisible, ten years of being furniture with a broom, and now six strangers and a dog and one very dangerous smuggler were all looking at him as though he were the hinge himself. As though the word burned into his hand were a title and the night had come to collect.

He looked at his palm. The scar-script had begun, very faintly, to glow again — a pulse, slow and steady, like a second heartbeat. Like something under the mountain tapping its foot.

"I have to go back to the gate," he said.

"Alone?" Wren shouldered her pack, which she had apparently packed during the conversation, because of course she had. "You can't walk in a straight line on a good day, and tonight the straight lines are broken. Besides—" and here some of the old wicked humor surfaced, thin as ice over deep water, but surfacing, "—if the world's ending, I want to be standing next to the man it's ending *on*. Professional curiosity."

"Wren."

"Theo." She held the door open. Cold poured in, and with it a smell he'd never smelled on the border wind before: candle-wax and old paper, sweetish and wrong, drifting down from the high passes. "You'll notice I'm not asking permission."

They stepped out together into the starless dark.

The road was visible. That was the first wrongness — moonless, starless, and yet the snow ahead glowed with a faint grey light from everywhere and nowhere, enough to walk by, enough to see by, not enough to trust. The second wrongness was the silence: no wind, no owls, no creak of pines. The whole of Thornfell holding its breath.

The third wrongness he felt before he saw it. His palm had begun to pull, gently, steadily, northward and up — the way a compass needle pulls, if compasses ached. Toward the mountain. Toward whatever lay beneath it.

"It's tugging me," he said.

"Don't follow it yet." Wren matched his stride, eyes on the treeline. "Whatever's calling, you don't answer on the first knock."

"What if it's the wards? What if I can fix this?"

"Then we fix it." She said *we* without seeming to notice she'd done it, and something in Theo's chest, some clenched and ancient thing he hadn't known was clenched, loosened half a degree. "And if we can't fix it, we run, and I know ways out of these fells that don't officially exist. Either way, you're not doing it alone. Gatekeepers." She snorted softly at the sky that wasn't there. "Ten years I've drunk beside you and you never once mentioned your hand was tied to the sky."

"It wasn't. Until tonight."

"Everything was fine until tonight." She glanced at him sidelong. "Funny how the things we don't talk about wait so patiently, isn't it?"

He had no answer for that. The grey-lit road climbed ahead of them, and above the black mass of the fell, where the stars should have been wheeling in their ancient ordinary circles, there was only smooth darkness — and now, growing at its center, so faint he might have imagined it, a pinprick of silver. Not a star returning.

A point of light, descending. Slowly. Patiently.

Theo's palm flared in answer, the scar-script shining through his glove, and far below their feet — impossibly far, deeper than any mine, deeper than sense — he heard, or dreamed he heard, the sound of a great ledger opening, and a dry voice like turning pages say:

*Overdue.*
