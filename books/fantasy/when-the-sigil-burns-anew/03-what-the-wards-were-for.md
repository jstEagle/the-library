## 3. What the Wards Were For

Theo had ridden to Lowmark perhaps four hundred times in ten years, and in all that time he had never once managed to arrive before noon. It wasn't the distance—the garrison town sat a comfortable six hours from Thornfell gate at an easy trot. It was the horse. Biscuit was a chestnut mare of strong opinions and weak commitment, and she believed, with the certainty of a scholar, that every milestone was a suggestion rather than an instruction.

"Biscuit," Theo said, as she stopped for the third time to examine a fence post with what could only be described as professional interest, "there are no stars. The sky is a blank page. The wards are dead. Could we possibly treat this with the gravity it deserves?"

Biscuit flicked one ear backward, acknowledging his concerns, and then resumed her inspection of the fence post.

The ride itself had been the worst part—or Theo had thought so, until he remembered it would be followed by talking to people. The dark had not lifted with dawn. There *was* no dawn, properly speaking; there was only a gray seeping at the edges of things, like water through cloth, a light that came from everywhere and belonged to nowhere. No sun. He kept checking, riding with his head tipped back like a fool, and finding nothing but that flat, sourceless gray. Farmers stood in their fields staring upward. A shepherd boy near the mill road was crying without any apparent sense of embarrassment about it.

And his hand still hurt.

He'd wrapped it in linen against the cold, which was a lie he told himself, because the ache wasn't cold. It was a deep, rhythmic burning, like a second heartbeat conducted entirely in pain. When he unwrapped it at a stream crossing to let Biscuit drink, the sigil had changed. That was the word for it, though he hated the word. The old scar—the one shaped like a closed eye, or a seed, depending on how much wine he'd had—had opened somehow. Fine lines radiated out from its center now, thin as spider silk, forming shapes that almost resolved into letters and then refused. When he tried to read them directly, they slid away from his attention the way a fish slides away from a hand in dark water.

A word branded into his skin that he couldn't read. He had decided, somewhere around the fourth mile, not to think about it. This was a strategy that had served him reasonably well across ten years of deliberate ignorance regarding his own past, and he saw no reason to abandon it now just because the sky had stopped working.

* * *

Lowmark looked wrong.

Theo realized it as he crested the last hill: the town was there, all its pieces present—garrison wall, slate roofs, the crooked bell tower of the chapel of Saint Aldhelm-the-Unhurried—but the pieces were arranged differently. Everyone was outside. The market square was full to overflowing, and nobody was selling anything. Merchants stood beside their shuttered stalls with their arms crossed, looking up. Children who should have been underfoot were clustered on the steps of the well in perfect silence, which was possibly the most alarming detail of all.

He rode down the high street and people turned to look at him, and then, oddly, kept turning, heads tracking him the whole way, a slow wave of attention rippling ahead of him toward the magistrate's house. He had spent ten years cultivating invisibility. He was good at it. He was, in fact, professionally invisible—the keeper of a gate nobody visited, a man whose name most of Lowmark would have struggled to recall even with prompting. Now forty faces swung after him like sunflowers following a very anxious sun.

"Theo," someone said. "Gatekeeper Theo." Another voice: "It's the keeper. Someone's gone and got the keeper."

*Someone's gone and got the keeper.* As if the wards dying were something that happened *to* him, personally, like a theft or a fire. He supposed, in fairness, it was.

He left Biscuit with the farrier's boy—who accepted the reins with the wide-eyed solemnity of a child being handed a live grenade—and climbed the steps of the magistrate's house. The door was already open. They were expecting him. That, more than anything, sent a cold finger walking down his spine.

*They were expecting me.*

Magistrate Odile Vasseur met him in the hall. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a doorframe, with iron-gray hair pinned up in a knot that could probably stop arrows. She had been magistrate since before Theo's arrival, and in ten years she had exchanged perhaps two hundred words with him, nearly all of them about drainage easements. She was not a woman given to drama.

So when she took one look at him, went pale beneath her weather-brown, and said, "Close the door, Master Delacroix," in a voice like a drawn blade, Theo felt his stomach drop through the floorboards and into the cellar.

"Magistrate."

"Your hand," she said. "Show me your hand."

Now, here was the thing. Here was the thing he had been circling all morning like Biscuit circled fence posts. His hand was his business. The sigil was his business, insofar as anything about him was his business, and the entire architecture of his life rested on the principle that nobody asked about it and he never mentioned it, and this arrangement had held for a decade with the stability of a bridge.

"It's nothing," he said. "An old burn. I've had it since—"

He stopped. Since when? Since always. Since forever. Ten years ago he had woken at the foot of the Thornfell pass with a burned sigil in his palm, a purse of coin he did not remember earning, and a memory that began cleanly, conveniently, impossibly at the age of twenty-six. He had told himself a story about fever. He had never once examined the story closely, because examining it meant asking what came before, and what came before was—

Nothing. A white room. A locked door in his mind where thirty years of a man's life should have been.

"Since when?" Odile asked quietly.

"I don't know," Theo said, and heard his own voice crack on it.

She crossed the hall, took his wrist with hands like a blacksmith's, and peeled back the linen before he thought to resist. For a long moment she said nothing. The silence had texture to it. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged in wind that didn't seem to reach the street.

Then Magistrate Vasseur, terror of tax evaders, scourge of the Lowmark smuggling rings, sat down abruptly on the bottom step of her own staircase like a woman twice her age.

"A debtor's mark," she said. "Saints preserve us. A debtor's mark, and the wards dead above it."

"You know what this is."

"I know what the wards were *for*," she said, "if that's what you're asking. And I suspect you don't, which means somebody went to a great deal of trouble and expense to make sure you didn't." She looked up at him, and her eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse than if they'd been hard. "Sit down, Master Delacroix. What I'm about to tell you is going to make you wish you'd stayed invisible."

"I *am* invisible," Theo said. "That's rather my whole thing."

"Not anymore," said the magistrate. "I'm afraid you may be the most visible man in the Marches."

* * *

She poured him tea he didn't drink and brandy he absolutely did, and she talked while gray light pooled in the windows like dishwater.

"When the garrison was founded, two hundred years ago, the charter said we were here to guard the border passes. That's what everyone believes. Smugglers, raiders, the odd ambitious warlord—that's what the wards of Thornfell were raised against. Every schoolchild learns it." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "The charter is a lie. It has been a lie since the day it was written. The original charter—the true one—is in my keeping, as it has been in the keeping of every Lowmark magistrate since the founding, and it says plainly what the founders would not say aloud."

Theo waited. Outside, someone in the square was ringing the chapel bell, badly, in a rhythm that suggested panic rather than devotion.

"The wards were not built to keep things *out*," Odile said. "They were built to keep something *in*. Beneath the mountain. Beneath Thornfell itself, down in the roots of it, where nobody goes and nobody asks why nobody goes." She looked at him steadily. "There is a court down there, Master Delacroix. An old one. Older than the garrison, older than the Marches, maybe older than the kingdom. The founders found it—or it found them—and there was a war, of a kind, and the war ended not with a sword but with a bargain. The wards are the bargain's signature. The seal on a contract."

"What kind of contract?"

"The kind you don't want to be party to." She set down her cup. "Souls. Years. Memory. The court below trades in them—it always has. And the wards keep its ledgers balanced and its doors shut. Or they did." Her gaze dropped to his bandaged hand. "Until three nights ago. Until whatever happened at your gate broke the signature on two hundred years of paperwork."

"The wards died," Theo said. "I was there. It sounded like—" He hunted for it. "Glass. A window breaking, except the window was the size of the sky."

"And your mark flared."

"Yes."

"Because it's connected. Of course it's connected." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I hoped I'd die without ever having to explain this to anyone. I had it planned out and everything. Quiet funeral, a successor who'd inherit the charter and hate me for it, the whole arrangement continuing undisturbed until the sun burned out." She lowered her hands. "Do you know what a debtor's mark is?"

"No."

"It's what the court below brands into those who owe it. Not a punishment—a *record*. The debt lives in the mark. The mark knows what's owed, and when, and to whom. Most people who carry one don't survive the carrying. The ones who do..." She hesitated, and for the first time since he'd known her, Odile Vasseur looked uncertain. "The ones who do tend to have made arrangements. Payments. Trades."

"Trades," Theo repeated. The word sat in his mouth like a stone.

"Memory buys a great deal down there," the magistrate said gently. "It's the strongest currency they deal in. A man could purchase a great many things with thirty years of himself."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a boat tilts. Theo gripped the arm of his chair.

"I woke up ten years ago," he said. "At the foot of the pass. With money I couldn't account for and no past I could find. I told myself—I told myself it was fever. Loss. Some accident on the road." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin and glassy. "You're telling me I *bought* that. You're telling me I walked down there and sold thirty years of my own life, and came back up with—" He gestured helplessly at everything: the chair, the tea, the gray light, the whole careful small edifice of his days. "*This.*"

"I'm telling you that the mark on your hand is a debtor's mark," Odile said, "and that debts of that kind come due, Master Delacroix. Always. The court below is patient because it can afford to be. It owns time itself, or enough of it to be smug about the rest."

"And mine is due now. Is that it? The stars go out, the wards break, and suddenly the collectors come calling?"

"The stars going out isn't a symptom," she said. "It's an invoice."

The words landed in his chest like ice water. Theo set down the brandy because his hand had begun to shake, and the sigil beneath its wrapping pulsed once, slow and hot, as if in agreement—as if it had *heard*, and approved, and was already counting.

"There's more," Odile said. "There's always more; that's the nature of these things. The charter names a keeper. Not a warden of the surface gates—a keeper of the *seal*. Someone bound to the wards, someone the bargain recognizes. In two hundred years there have been five. Each one carried the mark." She looked at him, and her voice went quiet. "Each one made the same journey you're about to make."

"I'm not going anywhere," Theo said. "I'm going to go back to my gate, and I'm going to re-lay the ward-lines, and I am going to fix this with rope and chalk and stubbornness, which is how I fix everything."

"The wards aren't broken, Delacroix. They're *called in*. The contract's been invoked. You can't mend paper that's been presented for payment." She leaned forward. "The only way to settle a debt with the court below is to go down and stand before the ledger yourself. Face to face with whoever holds it. That's what the other keepers did. Four of them never came back."

Theo stared at her. "And the fifth?"

Odile Vasseur rose, crossed to the tall cabinet behind her desk, and unlocked it with a key she wore on a chain around her neck. From inside she drew a scroll, ancient and cracked, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. But she didn't give it to him. She held it against her chest and studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face seemed to decide something.

"You'll need this," she said. "But first you'll need to understand what you are. Because the fifth keeper came back, Master Delacroix. Came back changed, came back owing, came back with his history scrubbed clean as a ledger page." She set the scroll on the desk between them, and her hand did not quite tremble, but it wanted to. "Came back as *you*."

The bell in the square stopped ringing. In the sudden silence, Theo became aware of a sound he had been half-hearing for hours without naming it: a faint, rhythmic warmth in his palm, patient as tide, patient as interest accruing. The sigil, keeping its own count.

"The Broker holds your contract," Odile said. "Down beneath the mountain, in the candle city. And if you mean to steal back what you sold, you'll need a guide who knows roads that don't appear on any honest map."

"I don't know anyone like that," Theo said automatically.

"Yes, you do," said the magistrate. "You drank with her last night."
