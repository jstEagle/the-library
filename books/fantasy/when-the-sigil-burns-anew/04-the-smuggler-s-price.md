## 4. The Smuggler's Price

The magistrate's words followed Theo out of Lowmark like a dog that wouldn't be called home.

*Debtor's mark.*

He'd walked the whole way back to Thornfell without deciding to walk it. His boots knew the road; apparently they were more trustworthy than his head, which kept circling the same three facts like a mule in a small paddock. One: the wards were dead. Two: the stars were dark. Three: he had a brand on his palm that a garrison clerk had gone pale looking at.

The fourth fact—the one he kept trying not to look at directly—was that Wren Ashvale was waiting for him at the border tavern, and Wren Ashvale always wanted something when she waited.

The Broken Antler sat where it had sat for as long as Theo had known it, which was ten years, which was—apparently—all he had. Its sign creaked on its bracket, half the antlers broken off years ago so that it read, if you squinted, *The Broke*. Nobody had fixed it. Nobody ever would. It was that kind of establishment, and Wren loved it the way other people loved their mothers: uncritically and against all evidence.

She'd claimed her usual corner table, the one with the view of both doors and the window, because Wren believed in exits the way priests believed in prayer. A jug of cider stood untouched before her. That alone made Theo's stomach drop. Wren Ashvale did not leave good cider untouched unless her mind was elsewhere, and when Wren's mind went elsewhere, other people's weeks tended to go badly.

"You're back," she said, as he came through the door. "I had a bet running with Marta that you'd get arrested. I lost four coppers."

"Sorry to disappoint."

"Don't apologize. Just buy me a drink and tell me why the sky's broken." She kicked out the opposite chair with one boot. "Sit down, Delacroix. You've got the look of a man who's been told something enormous and is pretending it's a small thing so nobody makes a fuss."

Theo sat. He looked at his hands on the table. The right one wore the sigil, still faintly warm, like a stone left too near a fire.

"Marta can keep her coppers," he said. "Nobody arrested me. But Magistrate Corvane—" he stopped, started again. "Wren, do you remember anything about what the gate wards were actually *for*?"

"Keeping folk like me honest." She grinned, then lost the grin when he didn't answer. "That's the joke, isn't it? That's what everyone says. Keep the smugglers out of Thornfell, keep the pass tidy." She leaned forward. "But you're asking me like it's not a joke anymore."

"It was never a joke." He heard how it sounded even as he said it—like a madman's confession in an empty tavern—but there was no other way to say it. "Corvane says the wards weren't built to keep anyone out. They were built to keep something *in*. Under the mountain. And she took one look at my hand and called this—" he turned the palm up, reluctantly, the way a man shows a wound he's been hiding from a healer—"a debtor's mark."

Wren went very still. Wren going still was worse than most people shouting. She could hold a conversation through a customs inspection with contraband in her coat; stillness meant the ground had moved under her.

"Show me properly," she said.

He slid his hand across the table. She didn't touch it. She bent over it instead, close enough that her breath stirred across the sigil, and whatever she saw made her jaw tighten.

"That's not a smuggler's mark," she said quietly. "My family's been running these passes for three generations. We've got marks older than the king's coin, marks for every toll and every ward and every god who'll take a bribe. I know all of them." She looked up. "This one I know from stories my gran used to scare me with. Stories about the deep court."

"The deep court."

"The court beneath the mountain." Wren sat back, arms crossing. "Goblin-folk, or something like them. Old. Old enough that they stopped bothering to pretend to be human a long time ago. They don't trade in gold, Theo. They trade in memory. In souls. Years off your life, faces you loved, names your mother gave you. Gran said if you ever met someone with a mark like that, you should run and not stop running until you hit the sea."

Theo looked at his own palm. It looked so ordinary. Ten years of ordinary—of hauling water, trimming wicks, walking the ward-line, drinking terrible ale in this exact tavern while Wren cheated Marta at cards. Ten years of being invisible and liking it.

"I'm going down there," he said.

Wren laughed. It was a real laugh, short and sharp, and it died almost immediately when she saw his face. "You're joking."

"The wards are dead, Wren. The stars went out. Whatever's sealed under Thornfell is coming loose, and according to the magistrate, the seal is—" he flexed his fingers, hating the words, hating how much they sounded like fate and how much he needed them to sound like fate because the alternative was chaos— "*me*. Or my debt is. I sold something to that court. I don't remember selling it, but I signed for it, and the bill has come due."

"And your grand plan is to walk into a goblin court full of soul-brokers and ask them nicely for a refund."

"No," Theo said. "My plan is to steal it back."

For a long moment Wren just looked at him. Then she picked up the cider jug, poured two cups, and pushed one across. "Well," she said. "At least you're not boring. Drink. You're going to need your courage, and this stuff is nearly strong enough to count."

* * *

They talked until the candles burned low and the taproom emptied and old Marta went upstairs grumbling about the end of the world, which she announced nightly and therefore could not be blamed for noticing early.

It was Wren, in the end, who laid out the practical shape of the thing, because Wren thought in routes the way other people thought in sentences.

"There are three ways under the mountain," she said, counting them off on ink-stained fingers. "The high road, which the garrison watches and which leads nowhere near the deep court except through every checkpoint between here and the far side of the range. The miners' galleries, which are collapsed, flooded, or haunted—sometimes all three, depending which shaft you pick. And the smugglers' roads." She tapped the table. "Which aren't on any map, because maps get confiscated, and which don't exist officially, because officially we're just honest traders having a quiet drink."

"And those roads go down?"

"They go everywhere, love. Down especially. There's a whole world under Thornfell that the mountain doesn't advertise—old lava tubes, dry riverbeds from before the river learned better, passages the dwarves cut and abandoned and forgot. My grandfather mapped half of them. My father memorized the rest and burned the map, which is the sort of thing Ashvales do." Her voice had gone careful, the way it went when she talked about her family—fond and guarded at once, like a person describing a dog that bites. "I can take you down. Through the black trees, past the shrine, all the way to the gate below, if the magistrate's telling the truth and there is one."

"You believe me, then." It wasn't quite a question. It surprised him anyway. He'd spent the ride back rehearsing arguments, and here she was, already past them.

"I believe the stars went out. I believe you've got a mark my gran used to whisper about. And I believe you've got exactly the face of a man about to do something stupid and noble, and I've never been able to resist supervising that particular kind of disaster." She shrugged. "Besides, business is bad when the sky's dark. Smuggling runs on moonlight and starlight, mostly. You've cost me a season's income, Delacroix. Consider this me collecting damages."

"Wren." He hesitated. This was the part he'd been circling all evening, the thing he'd tried to raise twice already and swallowed both times. "There's a condition. Yours. You said—"

"I said I want the truth first." All the humor drained out of her voice, gently, like water out of a lock. She turned her cup slowly on the table, not looking at him. "I'm offering to walk into hell with you. Before I do that, I need to know who's dragging me there. Not the story you tell the magistrate. Not the keeper-of-the-gate story you tell travelers. The truth. What did you sell? Who were you before Thornfell? Because I've watched you for ten years, Theo, and here's what I've seen: a man with a soldier's habits and no soldier's history. A man who flinches at ledger-books and won't say why. A man who knows the exact weight of everything he owns because he counted it once and never stopped counting." She finally looked up. "Ten years, and I don't know a single true thing about where you came from. So. Tell me. Start anywhere. Start with your mother."

And that was where it all came apart.

Because Theo opened his mouth to answer—and found nothing.

Not nothing like reluctance. Nothing like a room he'd rather not enter. Nothing like grief or shame standing in front of a door. Just—nothing. He reached back through himself the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and the reaching went back and back and back, and then it stopped. Cleanly. Like a stairwell with the bottom flight missing.

A woman's kitchen. He had a kitchen—he had a cottage above the gatehouse, and there was a kitchen in it. Before that—

Before that was the gatehouse. Before the gatehouse was the road, and the road ended at the moment he'd arrived, ten years and some months ago, with a horse he couldn't remember buying and a purse he couldn't remember filling and a letter of appointment he couldn't remember requesting. Everything before that was fog. Not empty fog—worse. Fog shaped like memories. He knew he must have had a childhood; he knew he must have had a mother; but when he reached for her face, there was only the smooth blank place where a face should be, the way a glove holds the shape of a hand that isn't in it anymore.

He knew things he shouldn't have been able to know without remembering. He knew what bread smelled like rising. He knew that rain on a tin roof sounded like applause. He knew he had loved someone once—there was a hollow ache in his chest shaped like loving someone—but he didn't know her name, her face, her voice, whether she'd been mother or sister or wife or daughter.

"Theo?" Wren's voice, very carefully neutral. "You've gone grey as porridge."

"I can't," he said. His own voice sounded far away. "Wren, I can't remember my mother's face."

* * *

He told her all of it, after that. It came out in pieces, haltingly, the way water comes from a cracked jug, but it came.

Ten years. That was the length of it. Ten years and change since the road, and before that, nothing—not darkness, not suppression, just absence, smooth as the inside of an eggshell. He'd never noticed because the present kept arriving and filling the space, day after day, year after year, and a man doesn't miss what he doesn't know he had. He'd built a life on top of the gap the way a village builds on old mine workings, and now the ground was shifting and every floorboard was singing.

"I thought I was private," he said. He was staring at his cup. "I thought I was a man who kept his own counsel. Turns out I'm a man with nothing to keep. Do you understand how—" He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I've spent ten years being proud of my discretion. I was proud of an empty room."

Wren didn't laugh. He'd braced for her to—it would have been easier, somehow, if she had, because Wren laughing meant the world was still navigable. Instead she reached across the table and put her hand flat over his sigil-marked palm, which was the boldest thing anyone had done to him in ten years, possibly literally.

"Listen to me," she said. "You're not an empty room. I've shared campsites with you, Delacroix. I've seen you give your last dry socks to a stranger's kid and lie about having spares. I've seen you stand a ward-line in freezing rain because you decided the job mattered. Empty rooms don't do that. Whatever they took from you, they didn't take *you*." She squeezed his hand once, hard, and let go. "So stop apologizing for the hole and start figuring out what's in it."

"How can you sit there and offer to guide me," Theo said, "when I just told you I'm nobody? I don't know who I am. I might be anyone. I might be—" and the thought surfaced ugly and cold, "—I might be someone bad, Wren. Whoever walked into that court ten years ago and traded away their own past—they weren't desperate by accident. People don't sell their souls over nothing. Maybe I was a criminal. Maybe I was worse. You're about to trust your life to a man whose entire character is ten years old."

"Yes," Wren said. "I am."

"Just like that?"

"Not just like that." She leaned back, and for once she looked tired—tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "Do you think it's easy for me either? My family ran these roads for three generations, and my gran told stories about the deep court like they were bedtime warnings. Which means somewhere back there, an Ashvale dealt with that court closely enough to be scared of it. If you made a bargain under Thornfell, odds are decent my family carried something in or out for it. Odds are decent I'm not guiding you toward justice, Theo. I might be guiding you toward *family business*." She turned her cup again. Round and round. "So we're a fine pair. A man with no past and a woman with too much of one."

The candle guttered. Outside, the sky stayed starless, a black lid pressed down over the world, and somewhere beyond the shuttered window Theo felt the gate's silence like a missing tooth his tongue kept finding.

"So you'll come," he said.

"I'll come." She said it fast, before he could thank her, before either of them could make it a bigger thing than it could survive being. Then she held up one finger. "Conditions. One: we leave tomorrow at first light, before the garrison decides the gate-keeper's gone mad and locks us both up for our own good. Two: you do exactly what I say on the road, even when it's stupid, *especially* when it looks stupid—my roads work precisely because they don't look like roads. Three—" and here her voice dropped, and she turned her cup one final time and stilled it, "—when we find what you sold, whatever it is, however bad it turns out to be—you tell me. No protecting me. No managing me. I'm done being managed by men with secrets, and I include you in that, Delacroix, fondly, but firmly."

"All right," Theo said. "All three."

"Good." She stood, swept the last of the cider into her cup, and drank it like a woman sealing a contract—which, knowing Wren, she probably considered herself to have done. At the foot of the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "whoever you were before—I'd wager you weren't bad. Bad men don't worry about being bad. They're too busy being busy." A crooked half-smile over her shoulder. "Get some sleep, keeper. Tomorrow we find out what you're worth to the things under the mountain."

Theo sat alone in the dying candlelight a long while after her footsteps faded. He turned his hand over and studied the sigil: the burned word he couldn't read, written in a language he apparently once spoke fluently enough to sign his name in blood.

Somewhere beneath his feet, miles of cold stone down, something ancient turned a page in a great ledger and waited for its debtor to come home.

He blew out the candle and went up to pack.
