## 5. Roads That Should Not Exist

Wren Ashvale had a rule about mountains: never trust one that's gone quiet.

Mountains were supposed to talk. Wind through the passes, water under the ice, the slow grumble of stone settling its old bones—she'd grown up falling asleep to that noise and had spent half her life navigating by it. A quiet mountain was a mountain holding its breath. And in Wren's experience, things that held their breath were either dying or about to bite.

The Thornfell passes had been holding their breath for two days now.

"Say something," she said over her shoulder.

Theo Delacroix looked up from the scree he'd been picking his way across. "Something."

"That counts, barely. Keep going."

He obliged, which surprised her. Most people she guided got sulky by the second day of silence and started narrating their own misery like it was poetry. Theo just talked—about the gate, mostly, the little rituals of keeping it: how you fed the ward-stones lamp oil on the first of each month, how they hummed if you got the pitch right, how one of them had developed what he swore was a favorite time of day, dusk, when it would sing a low note for no reason anyone could chart.

"You're telling me," Wren said, "that a rock has moods."

"I'm telling you a rock has *preferences*. Moods implies it can be offended."

"Can it?"

"I've never tried. I liked it too much to risk it." He said it mildly, but his hand had drifted to his palm again—the sigil, wrapped in linen since Lowmark, since it had flared hot enough to leave a word branded into his skin that no scholar at the garrison could read. He kept touching it through the cloth, the way you'd touch a bruise or a letter you couldn't bring yourself to open.

Wren turned back to the trail before he caught her watching. She'd known the man ten years, in the way smugglers know the border folk—nodded hellos at the tavern, the occasional unasked favor, the particular trust of people who live on the same thin line between lawful and not. Ten years, and until three days ago she could have told you everything worth knowing about Theo Delacroix in a sentence: keeper of the gate, keeps to himself, tips well, laughs rarely but honestly when it happens.

Now the sentence was broken, and every new piece of him was stranger than the last.

The trail bent north around a shoulder of granite, and Wren stopped so fast Theo nearly walked into her.

"What—"

"Hush." She held up a hand. Ahead, where the pass narrowed between two walls of stone, stood a man.

That wasn't the problem. Travelers used these passes; Wren herself knew most of them by name and debt. The problem was the direction he was walking. He was coming out of the narrows, moving with purpose—and then, without turning around, without any change in his gait at all, he reversed and walked back into them. Ten steps. Stopped. Reversed again.

"He's been doing that?" Theo asked quietly.

"How would I know? We just got here." But even as she said it, Wren understood, and her stomach went cold in a way the mountain cold hadn't managed. "No. Look at his eyes."

They came closer, careful, and the man didn't react to them at all. His eyes were open and pointed forward and utterly empty—not dead empty, worse than that. *Busy* empty. Eyes like someone trying to remember something very important and getting nowhere, forever.

"Hello," Theo said gently. "Friend. Can you hear me?"

The man walked through him. Not physically—he stepped aside without seeming to see him—but he passed close enough that Wren saw his lips moving. Counting, maybe. Or reciting. She leaned in as he swung past on another of his endless reversals.

"—and the door is blue, and her name is Marta, and the door is blue, and her name is Marta—"

"Oh," said Wren softly. "*Oh.* That's Aldric Voss. Runs wool over the pass from Hollowmere. Has a wife. Marta." Her mouth had gone dry. "He's holding onto two facts and walking himself in circles to keep them."

Theo crouched into the man's path—not blocking him, just near enough—and held out his bandaged palm as though offering bread. "Aldric. The door is blue. Her name is Marta. You should go home to her."

Something flickered in the wool-trader's face. Just a flicker—a candle in a draft—but it was more than nothing. He blinked, focused for half a heartbeat on Theo's hand, and then his brow furrowed and he said, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge:

"You smell like the dark."

Then he turned and walked—really walked, downhill, southward, toward Hollowmere and blue doors and Marta—and did not look back.

Wren stared after him. Then at Theo. "What in all the hells was that?"

"I don't know." He was looking at his own wrapped palm like it had betrayed him twice. "I don't know."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow Wren had used a hundred times—a fold in the hillside with good sightlines and a fire-ring of stones she'd stacked herself years ago, back when her grandfather was still alive and still teaching her which smuggler roads were roads and which were rumors.

She built the fire small. You learned that early in the passes: a small fire warms you, a big fire invites things.

Theo sat across from it, unwrapping his palm for the first time since morning, and Wren watched the light do something wrong.

The sigil had been a scar, at Lowmark—a pale burn in the shape of a knotwork circle with a gap in it, like a ring with a missing link. The magistrates' scholars had cooed over it and called it a debtor's mark and generally been useless. But tonight, in the firelight, the gap in the ring was glowing. Faintly. Like a coal remembering it had once been fire.

"It's changing," she said.

"I noticed." He flexed his fingers. Didn't wince, which meant either it didn't hurt or he'd decided she'd seen enough of him wincing. "It started this afternoon. When we passed the shrine back at the fork."

"The shrine nobody maintains because everyone who remembers why it exists has forgotten why it exists."

"Yes, that one."

Wren poked the fire. "You want to say the thing we're both thinking, or shall I?"

"We're thinking that whatever's happening down there—" he nodded north, deeper into the black throat of the passes, "—isn't staying down there anymore. It's leaking up. The trees have it. That traveler had it."

"The trees," Wren said, and shivered, because the trees deserved the shiver. They'd passed a stand of old pines at midday, healthy green things except for one detail: each trunk wore a ring of pure black around it at chest height, smooth as lacquer, as though someone had painted a stripe of midnight around every tree at exactly the same height. Theo had touched one—of course he had, the man touched everything, it was his whole personality—and reported that the black was cold. Not cool. *Cold*, like the memory of winter rather than winter itself.

"My granddad had stories about the deep passes," Wren said, mostly to the fire. "Smuggler stories, so take them with salt. He said there were roads under Thornfell that predated the kingdom. Roads that went *down*, not through. He said the family routes—the real ones, the ones he only drew for me when he was drunk and crying—weren't smuggling roads at all originally. They were supply lines." She hesitated. This was the part she'd been chewing on since Lowmark, the part that tasted bad. "Supply for something underneath."

Theo was quiet a moment. "And you still offered to guide me."

"I'm asking myself that hourly." She tossed a twig into the flames and watched it catch. "Here's my answer so far: whoever made those roads, whatever deal my family had down there, it ended ten years ago. Granddad stopped drinking himself stupid around then, stopped drawing the maps, told me the deep routes were closed forever and to never, ever ask why. And ten years ago, some fool of a gatekeeper sold his own head to seal a mountain." She looked up at Theo. "So either your bargain worked and something's gone wrong with it, or it never fully worked and we've all been living downstream of a dam with cracks in it. Either way, I'd rather be walking toward the problem with you than waiting for it to walk to me."

"That's the most generous thing anyone's ever said about my life choices."

"Don't get used to it."

He smiled—that rare, honest smile she'd catalogued over ten years—and it occurred to Wren, watching firelight move on his face, that she was beginning to build a new sentence about Theo Delacroix, piece by piece, and that the pieces didn't fit together into anything simple. A man who talked to rocks. A man who could pull a forgetting stranger halfway back to himself with a word and an open hand. A man with a hole in his memory ten years wide who joked about it the way other men joked about old war wounds.

She respected competence the way other people respected saints. It was the smuggler's religion. And somewhere between the black-ringed trees and Aldric Voss walking home to a wife he could barely remember, Wren had realized she wasn't guiding a client anymore.

She was following a man who might actually manage the impossible thing he was walking toward.

It was deeply inconvenient. She intended to complain about it at length, internally, for the rest of the trip.

* * *

The third day, the darkness found a road.

Not a road Wren knew—which was itself alarming; she prided herself on knowing every goat-track and game-trail in the Thornfells better than the rangers did, mostly because the rangers paid taxes and she didn't. But there it was: an old paved way surfacing out of the scree, flagstones fitted so precisely that grass couldn't find a seam. It ran straight as a plumb line into the high country, into shadow, and it was *cold*. Not winter-cold. Memory-of-winter cold.

"Nope," Wren said immediately.

"Wren."

"No. I know what you're going to say, and no. My granddad had one rule about roads like this: if the stones are fitted too well, it isn't a human road, and human feet don't belong on it."

"And yet." Theo crouched and laid his bandaged palm flat against a flagstone. The glow through the linen pulsed—once, slow, like a heartbeat answering a knock. He exhaled. "It knows me. Or I know it. One of those." He stood, and there was something new in his face—not fear exactly, though fear was certainly present and doing its job. Recognition. "This is the way down, Wren. Whatever's leaking upward is leaking along this road. If we skirt it, we'll be circling blind while it gets ahead of us."

"If we take it, we'll be walking into the mouth of the thing that ate your memories."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "But with purpose."

She hated that. She hated how reasonable he made catastrophe sound, in that mild gatekeeper voice, like he was explaining ward maintenance schedules. She hated more that he was right, and that her own map-sense—the instinct she trusted above her mother, above money, above most gods—was pulling the same direction, telling her the deep routes ran under this ridge and this road was their front door.

"Fine," she said. "But rules. My road, my rules. Rule one: we touch nothing that looks like an offering, an altar, a toll-gate, or a hat left behind. Rule two: if I say run, you run and ask questions at altitude. Rule three—" she jabbed a finger at his palm, "—if that thing starts singing, glowing brighter, or developing opinions, you tell me *immediately*. No gatekeeper stoicism. I mean it. You go quiet on me and start sacrificing yourself in some noble dumb way, I will haunt you, and I won't even need to die first. I'll manage it through sheer spite."

Theo considered this gravely. "Those are fair terms."

"Rule four: I do all the complaining aloud. You've done your share."

"I wouldn't dream of competing."

They stepped onto the road together, and the temperature dropped so sharply their breath plumed white in what should have been mild autumn air. Behind them, Wren noted with the detached calm of a woman cataloguing her own doom, the sun seemed dimmer. Not hidden—dimmer. As though the road had opinions about daylight and was voicing them.

They walked. Flagstones slid beneath their boots, flawless, ancient, wrong.

An hour in, they met the others.

Travelers, scattered along the road like leaves caught in an eddy. A woman in a pilgrim's gray, standing perfectly still, whispering numbers. Two brothers seated against a milestone, holding hands, mouthing the same syllables over and over—Wren caught only fragments, a name, maybe, worn round with repetition. An old soldier pacing a precise twenty-step circuit, boots ringing on stone, eyes fixed on nothing.

Memory-blind. All of them. Wanderers who'd walked up the dark road chasing something they'd lost and forgotten they'd lost it, and now walked the road itself because it was the only fact remaining.

"They came down from the villages," Theo murmured. "Looking for the dark. Looking for whatever took—" His jaw tightened. "This is what happens when the wards fail. People feel the hole. And holes *pull*."

One of the wanderers, the pilgrim, broke her counting as they passed. Her head turned, tracking them—not them, Wren realized. *Him.* Tracking Theo the way a compass tracks north, or a wound tracks salt.

"*Keeper,*" the pilgrim whispered. Her voice was reverent and ruined. "Keeper, keeper, you came back, you came back, we knew you'd come back—"

Others stirred. Heads lifting along the road like a field of grim flowers turning toward a reluctant sun.

"Keeper. Keeper. He came back."

Wren's skin crawled. She put her hand on her knife without deciding to. "Theo."

"I see them."

"Do you know them? Do you remember—"

"No." His voice cracked slightly on it. "I don't. And that's the worst part, Wren—they know me, and I will never know them, and I think—" He stopped walking. The glow in his palm had brightened, spilling gold through the linen seams, and the nearest wanderers pressed toward it with their ruined faces full of terrible hope. "I think I'm the reason they're like this. Whatever I did down there, whatever I traded—I think they're part of the price."

For a long moment the only sounds were wind, and whispers, and the old soldier's boots marking his twenty steps.

Then Theo did the thing that finished rearranging Wren's sentence about him entirely. He knelt, right there on the cursed road, in the cold, in front of the kneeling-distance of the broken, and he spoke to them—not as a crowd, not as a symptom, but as people. "I'm going down," he said. "I'm going down to fix what I broke. Every soul the court holds, I'm going to try to bring home. Wait for me. Hold your names. Hold whatever you have left, and wait for me."

The pilgrim began, softly, to weep. The brothers fell silent, hands tightening. Even the soldier stopped pacing.

And the road—the road *hummed*, faint and resonant, like a struck bell far underground, and Theo's bandaged palm blazed through the linen, and every wanderer on that stretch of dark stone bowed their heads as one, the way a congregation bows.

Wren stood very still and thought: *Oh no.*

Not about the road. Not about the dark, or the court below, or the Broker, or any of the horrors she'd been bracing against since Lowmark.

About herself. Because she knew what respect looked like when it tipped over into something else, and she was looking at it, kneeling in the cold, promising the forgotten the impossible in a voice that made even the mountain listen.

"Up," she said roughly, hauling at his arm. "Get up, you absolute disaster of a man. You don't kneel to roads. Roads kneel to *you* or they don't get walked." She pulled him upright, glaring hard at the wanderers—at the hope on their faces, which was somehow harder to look at than the emptiness had been. "Walk. Both of us. Before I say something sentimental and ruin my reputation."

They walked. The road sloped downward now, unmistakably, bending into the mountain's roots where no sun had ever reached. The whispering faded behind them, but the last thing Wren heard, carried thin on the frozen air, made her blood run cold and hot at once:

The wanderers weren't whispering *keeper* anymore.

They were whispering a warning, all together, in voices worn round with repetition:

"*He counts the debts. He counts the debts. He counts—*"

Ahead, far down in the dark, something answered.

A single candleflame, impossibly distant, impossibly steady, burning where no flame should burn—right in the middle of the road, as if someone down there had lit it just for them.

Or had lit it just in time.
