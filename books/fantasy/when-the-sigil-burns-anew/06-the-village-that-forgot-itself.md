## 6. The Village That Forgot Itself

The village appeared out of the trees like something forgotten mid-sentence.

They had been walking since dawn, following a road Wren swore existed on no honest map—a smuggler's road, worn smooth by boots that had carried things better left unnamed. The darkness that had swallowed the stars had not stopped at the sky. It had come down to earth here, pooling in the valleys like ink in a woodcut's grooves. The pines wore rings of black beneath their bark, pale wood striped with shadow as if the forest itself had been counting something and lost track.

"There," Wren said, pointing her chin at the smoke rising between the hills. "Thin smoke. Hearth-smoke, not forge-smoke. Somebody's home."

"Somebody," Theo repeated. The word felt strange in his mouth. For two days now they had seen nobody at all—only empty waystations, an abandoned cart with its apples gone to mush, and once, horribly, a boot standing upright in the middle of the road with nothing in it. "That'll be a first."

"Don't sound so excited." Wren adjusted the strap of her pack and started down the slope. "Places that go quiet on the border usually have a reason. Reasons around here have teeth lately."

Theo followed, one hand curled loosely against his chest. The sigil in his palm had settled from its white-hot agony into a low, persistent burn—the ache of a bruise, or a tooth, or a debt. He had learned to ignore it the way he'd once learned to ignore his own reflection. That was, he was discovering, a skill he'd practiced far too well for ten years.

*You sold something,* the old woman in Lowmark had told him, gripping his wrist with surprising strength. *Nobody gets marked like that for free.*

He still didn't know what. But the mark was pulling him downhill now, toward the smoke, like a compass needle made of guilt.

* * *

The village of perhaps forty houses sat in a bowl of hills, tidy as a painted plate. Roofs mended. Gardens weeded—well, half-weeded. Laundry on lines. And everywhere, people. People carrying water, splitting kindling, sweeping steps that were already clean.

Nobody spoke.

Not to each other, not to the children—who played a game involving stacking stones in silence, their small faces grave as judges—and not to Theo and Wren when they walked up the main lane and said good morning three separate times.

"Hello?" Wren tried again, planting herself in front of a man hauling a yoke of buckets. He was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with the weathered look of someone who had spent his life outdoors. "Good morning to you. Fine village you've got. Terrible weather we're having. Or excellent. I honestly can't tell anymore."

The man looked at her. His eyes were the trouble. They were clear enough—brown, unclouded, sane—but there was nothing behind them looking back. Not emptiness exactly. More like a room where all the furniture had been covered in sheets.

"Water," the man said at last. His voice was flat and pleasant, a bell with no ring in it. "The water wants carrying."

"Yes," Theo said gently. "It does. And then?"

The man frowned, as though the question were in a language he'd studied long ago as a boy. "And then... it wants carrying."

"Right." Wren stepped back and lowered her voice. "Well. That's cheerful."

They moved deeper into the village. A woman kneading bread paused to watch them pass, hands still working, folding and pressing, folding and pressing. Two children stacked another stone. An old dog lay in the sun and thumped its tail twice—not at them, Theo thought, but at the ancient memory of being happy about something.

"It's them," he said quietly. "The darkness from the passes. The travelers going memory-blind. This is what happens when it finishes."

"Finishes?" Wren glanced at him sharply. "You say that like it's a recipe."

"Doesn't it look like one? Followed to the end." Theo watched the bread-woman work. Her motions were perfect. Practiced. Ten thousand mornings of dough lived in her hands even if nothing of those mornings lived anywhere else. "She doesn't remember making bread. But her body remembers. They're all just... running out the clock on habits."

"That's grim as a funeral, Delacroix."

"I'm told I'm a grim sort of person." He heard how flat it came out and tried to add, "Apparently I'm also a mystery. So there's variety."

Wren huffed—half laugh, half worry—and Theo understood something new about her in that moment: she joked when she was frightened, and she was frightened nearly always, and she carried it anyway. It made him like her more than he knew what to do with.

An old woman was watching them from a doorway halfway down the lane.

She was tiny, wrapped in shawls despite the season, and unlike the others her eyes were not sheet-covered rooms. They were open windows with someone home behind them—someone old and tired and terribly awake. She had been watching them, Theo realized, since they entered the square. Watching him specifically.

"Wren," he murmured. "Don't look—"

Too late. The woman crossed the lane with quick, decisive steps, seized Theo's hand in both of hers before he could react, and turned it over.

The sigil flared.

It didn't hurt this time—or rather, it hurt in a way that felt almost like recognition, like a handshake between old acquaintances. Light bled up between his fingers, gold shot through with black, and the old woman looked at the burning mark on his palm and began, very quietly, to weep.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, oh. You came back."

"I—" Theo's throat closed. "I don't think—"

"You're the one who sold himself." She said it the way other people said *you're bleeding*, matter-of-fact and full of pity. "We waited for you. We prayed somebody would come back down and make it right, and they sent us you. The one who sold himself."

The whole lane had stopped. The water-carrier stood with his buckets. The bread-woman stood with flour to her elbows. Every face in the village turned toward Theo, and none of them had anything to say, because whatever they might have said had been taken away and stored somewhere beneath a mountain.

"What did he sell?" Wren asked softly. She had a hand near her knife, Theo noticed, but her voice was careful, kind. "Ma'am. What did he sell, and what did it take from you?"

The old woman didn't answer at first. She kept holding Theo's hand, thumb tracing the edge of the sigil like she was memorizing it, or checking it for cracks.

"My name," she said finally. "I had a lovely name. My husband chose it. He used to sing it while he worked the forge, off-key, God rest him." A tear tracked through the deep lines of her face. "I know he sang it because I know there was singing. I don't know the song. I don't know him. I don't know me." She looked up, and her eyes were furious and heartbroken and, worst of all, lucid. "But I remember *this*. I remember the night the takers came through, ten years back, and a young man with a burned hand walked ahead of them like he was leading a procession. He was crying. Did you know that? Whatever you were, whoever you were—you cried the whole way through our square, and you said, *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, it's the only door that locks.*"

The world tilted.

Theo did not remember it. That was the horror—he stood there in the lane and felt absolutely nothing where the memory should have been, a smooth blank wall where this woman kept a scar. But his body remembered. His hand remembered. The sigil pulsed under her fingers in a rhythm he slowly recognized as his own heartbeat, racing.

"I don't understand," he said, and it came out cracked. "If I sealed something—if I paid—I don't understand why it took *yours*. The payment should've been mine."

"Debts spread," the old woman said simply. "Like fire in a dry field. Your bargain lit, and we burned slow." She released his hand at last, and the sigil dimmed, and Theo felt the loss of her grip like the loss of an anchor. "The court below took payment in memory because memory is the only coin worth anything. And they took ours as *interest*. Year by year. First the names went. Then the faces. Then everything except the doing." She gestured at the silent village, the clean swept steps, the bread. "We are very good at doing."

Wren made a sound low in her throat, and Theo glanced over to find her staring at the villagers with an expression he couldn't read—something between grief and recognition. He filed it away. There had been several such expressions in the past two days, each filed and unexamined, because there was never time and never safety and never enough light.

"Can it be undone?" Theo asked. "The taking. If the gate is sealed properly—if the debt is paid—"

"If the ledger is balanced," the old woman corrected, and something in the precision of it made the hair rise on his neck. "Yes. What is written can be unwritten. But you'd need to see the writing." She smiled without any joy in it at all. "And the writing lives below. With the Broker."

"The Broker," Theo repeated.

The name landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the splash—for some echo of memory, some flicker of recognition. Nothing came. Only the sigil, warm and steady, like a hand resting on his shoulder in the dark.

"He knows your name," the old woman said. "Even if you don't. Names are his trade, dear. He keeps them all in a book." She turned and, with terrible ceremony, pointed one crooked finger at the small stone chapel at the end of the lane. "One of our men was a runner, before. Before the forgetting. He kept accounts of his own—smuggler's habit, write everything down, trust nobody's mouth. What he knew of the court below, he wrote in his book, and he hid the book where he thought no one forgetful would ever look."

"Where?" Wren asked.

"In the chapel," the old woman said, and for the first time her mouth twitched toward something that had once been humor. "Under the altar. Because who among us could remember where that was?"

* * *

The chapel was cold and smelled of dust and old beeswax. Wren found the loose stone beneath the altar in less than a minute—of course she did; Theo was beginning to suspect there was no hiding place on the border she hadn't either used or robbed—and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, thick as a brick.

"A ledger," she said, weighing it in her hands. Her face had gone carefully blank in the way it did whenever anything touched her family's business. "A proper one, too. Good binding. Smugglers keep better books than bankers, my gran always said. Gran said a lot of things." She set it on the altar and didn't open it. "You want to do the honors? Feels like your book more than mine."

"Why would it be mine?"

"Because everyone in this village looks at you like you're a page torn out of it." She shrugged, but her eyes stayed serious. "Go on."

Theo opened the ledger.

The handwriting inside was cramped and practical, columns of goods and routes and prices, dates stretching back decades. It was the record of a life of small crimes, neatly kept. And then, ten years ago, the neatness broke. The entries changed. Pages of them, close-written and increasingly frantic, describing something else entirely:

*A court beneath the Thornfell massif. Not mines. Not bandits. A COURT. They trade in souls the way we trade in brandy, and they pay in wishes, and every soul who ever wished on a falling star over this border owes them something.*

*They are ruled by one called the Broker. Nobody has seen his face and remembers it after. He keeps the master ledger. Everything anyone has ever traded, promised, or lost—it is all written there. As long as the ledger stands, the debts stand.*

*The wards on the surface gate are not walls. They are a LOCK ON THE LEDGER ROOM DOOR. Whoever built them bound the lock with a living soul. One soul, freely given, freely spent, holds the whole book shut.*

*Someone means to sell themselves. Someone has been negotiating for months. I saw the marks on his palm.*

Theo stopped reading. His hands were shaking too badly to continue, and the sigil was burning again—not painfully now, but insistently, like a knock at a door.

"Wren," he said.

"I see it." She had been reading over his shoulder, and her voice had gone very quiet. "Ten years ago. Someone with marks on their palm negotiated with the Broker for months, and then sealed the ledger room with their own soul, and the surface wards were just the visible bit of it." She looked up at him. "The stars didn't go out last week because something got stronger, Delacroix. They went out because the lock is failing. Because *you're* failing. The seal was always temporary—it was you, and you were never meant to last forever without knowing what you were holding shut."

"And the Broker knows it." Theo closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child. "That's why the hunters. That's why the toll-wardens. The debt's coming due, and the Broker is collecting."

Below them, faintly, through stone and soil and the roots of the black-ringed trees, something shifted. Not an earthquake. More like a great book being opened. The candles in the chapel sconces, unlit for years, guttered alight all at once, flames standing perfectly straight in the motionless air.

In the doorway, the old woman stood watching them, shawls drawn tight.

"The chapel lights itself when the court stirs," she said. "It hasn't stirred like this in ten years." Her clouded eyes fixed on the ledger in Theo's hands, then rose to his face. "You're going down there, aren't you. To the book of books."

"I have to balance the ledger," Theo said. It sounded absurd said aloud, a madman's errand laid out plain. "Whatever I did ten years ago, it bought ten years for a border full of people. And it cost this village everything. Both of those things are true, and I don't remember which one I meant to be." He swallowed. "But I mean to find out. And I mean to put your names back."

The old woman studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, slow and solemn, and reached up to pat his burned hand once, lightly, like blessing it.

"Then listen to an old woman whose head leaks," she said. "Two things. First: the Broker will offer you exactly what you want most, and it will be a trap, and you will be tempted anyway, because that is what wanting most *means*."

"And second?"

Her smile returned, thin and fierce as a winter sun.

"Second: the man who wrote that ledger had a name, before. He hid it in the book, in case he forgot himself like the rest of us. Find it. A man going into the dark ought to carry his own name with him." She turned toward the door, then paused. "It's in the back. In the margin. Written small."

Theo turned to the last page with fingers that had gone numb.

There, in the bottom margin, in cramped, careful letters—the same hand that had recorded every route and toll and secret of thirty years of smuggling—one line, pressed hard into the paper as if the writer had needed it to survive:

*My name is Corvin Ashvale. Remember me.*

Behind him, Wren went utterly still.

"*Ashvale,*" she whispered.

The sigil in Theo's palm burned like a struck bell, and somewhere far beneath their feet, something began, very patiently, to laugh.
