## 7. Ledgers of the Deep Court

The chapel had no name anymore.

That was the first thing Theo understood about the place, standing in its doorway while rain came down outside like the sky meant it personally. The village below—Hollowmere, if you could call a place a village when its people answered to nothing but their chores—had forgotten everything else too. Its well. Its saint. Its reason for being here at all. But the chapel still stood on its hill above the black-ringed trees, and something in Theo's chest pulled toward it the way a compass needle pulls north, or a debt pulls a debtor.

His palm throbbed under the bandage Wren had wrapped around it that morning, brisk and businesslike, her fingers quick and surprisingly gentle.

"You keep touching it," she'd said. "That's half your problem."

"It's attached to my hand."

"Then stop thinking at it so loud."

Now she stood beside him in the doorway, wringing rain from her braid and eyeing the dark interior with the particular suspicion she reserved for anything that might contain either treasure or trouble. With Wren Ashvale, Theo was learning, those were usually the same thing.

"A church," she said flatly.

"Looks like one."

"In a village where nobody remembers their own names." She said it slowly, as though explaining to a child why you shouldn't lick a frozen fencepost. "And you want to go inside."

"The old woman pointed here."

"The old woman who called you 'the one who sold himself' and then cried on you."

"Yes."

"And that's *not* alarming to you."

Theo considered this. Rain drummed on the chapel's slate roof. Somewhere behind them, down in Hollowmere, a woman was sweeping a step that didn't need sweeping, because she had always swept it, because that was what hands did when they had forgotten everything else.

"Wren," he said, "everything since the stars went out is alarming. I've decided to be alarmed *efficiently*. One thing at a time."

She snorted—a short, unwilling sound—and pushed past him into the dark. "Fine. But if there's a skeleton in there holding a candlestick, I get the candlestick."

* * *

Inside smelled of dust and old tallow and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath. Candle smoke long burned out. The windows were leaded glass gone milky with grime, and the gray daylight that filtered through fell in pale rectangles across empty pews. Everything sat where it must have sat for years: hymnals squared neatly on the shelves, collection box on its stand, dust thick as felt over all of it.

No skeletons. No candlesticks. Wren looked genuinely disappointed.

But at the front of the chapel, where an altar should have been, there was instead a desk.

It was a good desk—dark wood, brass fittings, the kind of thing a merchant or a magistrate would own—and it did not belong in a chapel any more than a fish belonged in an orchard. It sat facing the pews, as if someone had once preached sermons from behind it. Or kept accounts.

Wren went very still beside him.

"That's a smuggler's desk," she said quietly.

"You can tell from here?"

"I grew up sleeping under one." She crossed the aisle, boots silent on stone despite her best efforts to scuff them, and ran her fingers along the desk's edge. "See the false bottom in the drawer? The depth of it? That's not for ink. That's for ledgers you don't want found. My grandfather had one just—" She stopped. Her hand stayed on the wood. "Just like it."

Theo watched her face do something complicated in the gray light. He was getting better at reading her—the ten days since the stars had died had been an education in Wren Ashvale, in her silences especially—and what he read now was guilt wearing the mask of concentration.

"Wren?"

"There'll be books in it." Her voice had gone flat. Professional. "Smuggler's desks always have books. And I'm suddenly very interested in what kind of books."

She found the catch on the second try—a small brass stud beneath the drawer's lip that depressed with a click like a knuckle cracking. The false bottom swung up.

Three ledgers lay inside, wrapped in oilcloth.

Two were ordinary-looking things, water-stained and swollen. The third was smaller, bound in green leather gone soft as skin, and when Theo lifted it the weight of it surprised him—heavy, dense, the way a book gets when every page has been written on and written over and written again.

The old woman's words rose up in him unbidden, her cracked voice and her tears: *the one who sold himself.*

He opened the green ledger on the altar-desk, and the past began to speak.

* * *

It was a smuggler's book, all right. Wren confirmed it page by page, leaning over his shoulder, her breath warm against his ear, translating as she went. Dates in a cipher that wasn't quite a cipher—she recognized it as the Ashvale shorthand, or something close enough to make her jaw tighten. Route notes. Toll amounts. Names of way-stations, some of which Theo realized with a chill he could name from his own patrols along the Thornfell passes.

But between the entries—crowded into margins, crammed onto flyleaves, sometimes taking whole pages in handwriting that slanted and hurried like a man writing against time—someone had recorded something else entirely.

*"Toll paid at the shrine of the Second Stair: one summer, whole. The warden counted it twice and smiled, which I did not care for."*

*"Do not trade with the court for years. They will sell you back your own childhood and call it generosity."*

*"The Broker does not lie. This is worse than lying."*

"Goblin work," Wren murmured. "This handwriting—it's got the downward hook on the ascenders. Old style. Court-trained scribes write like that; my grandfather said their letters lean the way their masters do." She traced one entry with a fingertip, careful as touching a wound. "The deep court. The one under Thornfell."

"You believe in it?" Theo asked. "Truly? A goblin court under the mountain that buys and sells—"

"Souls and memory, if these pages are honest." She tapped the line about the Broker. "My family sang songs about it. Lullabies, mostly. *Don't whistle on the high pass, don't answer voices that know your name, don't owe the mountain anything.* I thought they were songs to keep children from wandering." Her laugh was thin. "Turns out they were safety regulations."

Theo turned another page. The paper whispered. And there—halfway down, wedged between a note about salt prices and a sketch of what appeared to be a toll-shrine—he saw his own name.

**Delacroix, T.**

Ten years ago. Almost to the day.

The world went quiet. Not silent—the rain still hammered the roof, and Wren was saying something—but quiet the way the inside of a bell is quiet, all the sound gathered and waiting.

He read.

*"Received of T. Delacroix, in settlement of account: one soul, entire, together with all memories appurtenant thereto. In exchange rendered: the sealing of the gate at Thornfell, per contract, the wards to stand so long as the debt stands unpaid. Witnessed. Sealed. Entered in the master ledger."*

Below it, in different ink, added later:

*"Note: subject retains sigil. Standard practice would burn it out. Left intact by instruction of the Broker. Reason unstated. I do not like it."*

"Theo." Wren's hand was on his arm. "Theo, breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You've stopped blinking. Blinking's usually part of it."

He blinked. The chapel swam back into focus—the gray windows, the dust, Wren's face close and worried and sharp all at once. His palm burned under its bandage, and for once he let it burn, because the pain was proof. Proof that the ledger wasn't lying. Proof that the hollow where his childhood should have been had edges after all, and here they were, written down in a dead smuggler's hand.

"Ten years ago," he said. His voice sounded far away. "I sold my soul to seal the gate. That's what the wards were. That's why they're dying—they weren't built, they were *bought*, and the purchase is coming due."

"That matches what the magistrate said. Debtor's mark." Wren chewed her lip. "What I want to know is why the Broker left your sigil working. Every other note in this book says the court burns the mark out of a finished sale. Clean goods, clean hands. Yours they left live. Yours they left *aching* whenever the wards got touched."

"So it could call me back."

"Or so it could count down." She took the ledger gently from his hands and flipped backward through the pages, faster now, hunting. "Look—if this is a real account, there'll be terms. Courts like this run on terms. When's the debt due? What's the interest? What happens when—here."

She stopped. Turned the book back toward him.

The entry was older than the rest, the ink browned, the handwriting steadier—as if whoever wrote it had still believed the world made sense.

*"Of the master ledger of the deep court, and the office of its keeping:"*

Theo leaned closer. The candle-glow of memory, the sweetness under the dust-smell, the pull in his chest that had dragged him up this hill—all of it tightened like a fist.

*"The master ledger holds every debt the court has ever bought. Souls. Years. Names. While it sits in the Broker's hall, the Broker holds the border of the world in his two hands, and no gate sealed by contract may be broken save by balancing the book. Whoever keeps the master ledger keeps the terms. Remember this. The Broker's power is not the court. The Broker's power is the* ***book****."*

Below that, the final entry in the old hand, dated ten years ago, three days before Theo's name appeared:

*"I have given the master ledger to one who will carry it where the Broker cannot follow. If any reads this: the man who carried it out gave up everything to do it. Whatever you think of him, remember what he bought with the price. Remember what the gate holds shut. And pity him, if you can spare it, for he cannot spare a single hour of pity for himself—he hasn't any left to spend."*

Theo sat down. There was a pew directly behind him, and his legs simply declined to hold him up any longer, and so he sat, heavily, in a forgotten chapel, while the rain played percussion on the roof.

"Theo?" Wren crouched in front of him, searching his face. "Talk to me."

"He stole the ledger," Theo heard himself say. "Ten years ago. Someone stole the master ledger out of the deep court and used it to force a bargain—seal the gate, bind the Broker's hands, and the thief paid with—" He gestured vaguely at himself, at the whole improbable arrangement of his body and its ten-year hole. "Paid with me."

"That doesn't mean it was you."

"It's my name, Wren. In the book. Three days later. *Received of T. Delacroix.*" He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I spent ten years being nobody. Keeping a gate. Sweeping a step, like that poor woman down the hill. And it turns out I wasn't hiding from a past. I *sold* it. Deliberately. To do—" He stopped.

"To do something enormous," Wren finished softly.

"Something enormous," he agreed. "And now the bill's come due, and the stars are out, and I don't even know what I bought the world with my soul, except that everyone down here forgot their names because of it."

Wren was quiet a moment. Outside, the rain eased, settling into a steady whisper. Down in Hollowmere, faintly, the woman was still sweeping.

"Here's what I see," she said at last, in her practical smuggler's voice, the one that had gotten them through the passes alive. "You've got a map now. That's what this is. Ten years of somebody else's route notes through the deep country—shrine locations, toll-wardens, the works. Whoever wrote this knew the court better than the court knows itself." She tapped the green cover. "And the last thing he wrote says your ledger—the master ledger—is still out there. Still somewhere the Broker can't reach. Which means somewhere in this story there's a book with every debt in it, including yours, and books, Theo, can be *edited*."

He looked at her. Rain-light moved on her face. She was soaked to the skin, exhausted, wounded pridefully at having been right about the candlestick, and she was looking at him the way nobody had looked at him in ten years—not as the invisible keeper of the gate, not as a debtor, not as the one who sold himself, but as a person worth planning around.

"When did you decide we're going down there?" he asked.

"Oh, about the time you started talking to your own hand." She stood, brushing off her knees, and held the green ledger out to him. "You'll carry it. I navigate, you carry the doom-book. That's the division of labor."

He took it. It was heavier than a book should be. He suspected it had been heavier than a book should be for ten years, and he simply hadn't known what the weight was.

One question remained, sitting in him cold and small, and he asked it because Wren had earned honesty and he had so little of it left to give.

"Wren. Whoever wrote these notes—whatever I was, before—I walked into that court knowing exactly what I was doing. I bargained away my own soul like a man counting out coins. What kind of person does that? Because in three days I'm going to be standing in front of whatever's down there, and I'd like to know if I should be afraid of meeting myself."

Wren considered him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—not her sharp market smile, but something quieter, aimed more inward than outward.

"My grandfather ran contraband his whole life and told me once, 'Wren, the dangerous ones aren't the men who take risks. They're the men who take risks *on purpose*, with the bill in view, and pay it anyway.'" She shrugged, turning toward the door and the clearing sky beyond it. "Whoever you were, you didn't lose your soul, Theo. You spent it. There's a difference. Spendthrifts are common. Men who pay what they owe—" she glanced back over her shoulder, gray eyes bright, "—those are rarer than honest magistrates, and twice as useful in a tight spot."

She pushed open the chapel door. Evening light came through, gold and sudden, falling across the altar-desk and the open pews and the dust of ten forgotten years.

"C'mon, Delacroix," she said. "We've got a mountain to rob."

Behind them, on the desk where they had left it open, the green ledger lay in the light—and on the last page Theo had not yet reached, in fresh ink that had not been there an hour before, new writing was drying:

*"*Noted: the debtor approaches. The Broker is pleased. Bring the sigil.***"*
