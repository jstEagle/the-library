## 8. Ashvale's Inheritance

Wren had lied to better people than Theo Delacroix and slept fine afterward.

That was the thought she kept circling as they made camp in the hollow of a dead watchtower, three days past the village that had forgotten its own name, and it didn't help. She'd lied to magistrates who deserved it, to customs men who earned every word, to a customs captain in Lowmark once so thoroughly that he'd written her a letter of recommendation by way of apology for suspecting her. She'd slept like a stone afterward, too.

But she hadn't lied yet. That was the trouble. She was only deciding whether to.

Theo had the fire small — he was good with fires, the way he was good with everything quiet: gates, wards, wounded birds, her — and he was turning something on a stick over the coals with the grave concentration of a man defusing it rather than cooking it. A hare. Or half a hare. The other half belonged to a hawk earlier in the day, and there had been words about it.

"You're staring," Theo said without looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"You're staring at me like I owe you money."

"Everyone owes someone money," Wren said. "It's the natural order." And then, because the fire popped and because the dark beyond the tower mouth had teeth in it lately and because she was tired of carrying the thing around like a stone in her boot: "We should talk about my grandfather."

Theo looked up. The hare dripped fat into the coals and hissed.

* * *

She'd been building toward it since the chapel. She knew that now. In the chapel, when Theo had found his own name in the smuggler's ledger — *Delacroix, T., one soul, whole, delivered* — she had watched his face do something terrible and quiet, and she had felt the Ashvale name sitting right there beside it on the page, in her grandfather's handwriting, in the margin notes, in the route maps folded into the back cover. Routes she had learned before she could read. Routes she'd taught him.

He hadn't asked. That was the worst part. He'd closed the ledger and said, *We should move before light,* and he'd never once said *your name is in here too, Wren.* He was giving her time, or mercy, or both, and either one curdled in her stomach worse than an accusation would have.

So now, in a dead watchtower with a half-hare and a small fire, she gave herself up.

"The routes I know," she said. "The back roads through Thornfell. The passes that don't exist on any magistrate's map, the shrines where you pay tolls, the ways down under the mountain that aren't supposed to be there." She pulled her knees up. "My grandfather didn't invent them. He inherited them."

"Inherited from whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I always assumed from other smugglers. Smugglers inherit like everyone else — badly, and with grudges. But it wasn't smugglers. It was the court. The deep court. The Broker's people built those roads first, Theo. Before there was anything worth smuggling across this border except what came out of the mountain."

Theo set the stick down. He did that sometimes — went very still, the stillness of a man standing next to a ward and listening for whether it hums.

"My grandfather was a courier," she said. "Not for furs or brandy. For them. Whatever the deep court needed carried up, whatever they wanted brought down — his routes were their supply lines. He ran deliveries for the Broker for thirty years, and he was proud of it. Do you understand? Proud. He used to say the Ashvales kept the roads open when nobody else would go near them. Kept faith with the ones below." Her hands had made fists somewhere along the way without consulting her. "And when your contract was signed — ten years ago, that entry in the ledger — somebody had to deliver what you traded. Somebody had to carry it down."

The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the empty tower like a breath drawn through teeth.

"And it was Ashvale," Theo said quietly. Not a question.

"It was Ashvale." She looked at him straight, because if she was going to gut herself she might as well hold eye contact while doing it. "Whatever you sold — your memories, ten years of your life, your whole self — my family carried it. My grandfather's hands touched it. Maybe mine would have, if I'd been born twenty years earlier. The routes I've been walking with you, the shortcuts that've kept us alive — every one of them is paved with exactly this. With bargains like yours."

* * *

He didn't say anything for a long time. Wren counted his silences the way other people counted sheep; she'd learned his rhythms in the weeks since the stars went out. There was the silence where he was thinking, which had weight and direction. There was the silence where his sigil hurt, which had a particular flinch to it, a held breath. And there was this silence — long, level, careful — which was the one where he decided how to be kind.

She braced for it anyway.

"Say the rest," he said.

"There isn't any rest."

"There's always rest. You've got a face like a woman reading a bill she can't pay."

"Gods, you're cheerful for a man with no past."

"I'm told it keeps the wrinkles off." But he didn't smile, not quite, and he turned to face her fully, cross-legged, the firelight catching the scarred edge of his palm where the sigil sat. "You're wondering if I'm going to stop trusting you. Is that it?"

"I'm wondering why you haven't already. Which worries me more, frankly. Either you're slow, or you know something I don't, and historically when people know things I don't, it ends with me running."

"Wren."

"Don't use the reasonable voice at me, Theo Delacroix, I'll walk into the dark on purpose just to spite it—"

"*Wren.*" And then, gentler, the voice he used on frightened animals and locked gates: "Tell me about the debt."

She blinked. "What?"

"Family debt. You said loyalty between family debt and me, back in the chapel — no. You didn't say it. You wore it. It's been riding your shoulder since we found that ledger." He shrugged. "I can't remember my mother's face, but apparently I can read you fine, so let's spend that. What does your family owe the court?"

And that — that was the knife she'd been sitting on for three days, and he'd gone and named it plain, and now there was nothing to do but fall on it.

"When a courier serves the court," she said, "the service doesn't end. That's the trick of it. Grandfather took payment once — protection, early on, when the border wars were bad and the Ashvales were starving. The court kept us alive through three winters nobody else survived. And in exchange, the routes stayed open. Forever. Generation to generation." She heard her own voice go flat, reciting. "There's a page in the family book. Every Ashvale signs it when they come of age. I signed it at fifteen, thinking it was tradition. It's a contract, Theo. It says the roads stay open and the courier answers when called." Her throat tightened. "It says I answer when called."

"And have you been called?"

"No. Not yet. Not once in twelve years." She stared into the fire. "But the stars went out, and the wards died, and the court is waking up and collecting everything it's owed. So either the calling is coming, or—" She stopped.

"Or it already happened," Theo finished softly. "The night the gate fell."

The night the gate fell, Wren had been at the border tavern, drinking, trading wry words with the invisible keeper of Thornfell, feeling his palm-sigil ache under her fingers for the first time in years. And the next morning she'd known — somehow, in the marrow way smugglers know weather — that she had to take him down the mountain. Hadn't questioned it. Hadn't examined it. Just packed her kit and found him and said *I'll guide you* like it was her own idea.

Her skin went cold.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that absolute bastard."

"What?"

"He didn't call me. He *steered* me. The contract's in my blood, same as the routes are in my head — maybe the court just leaned on the road a little. Made sure the right guide met the right man at the right tavern." She was on her feet now, pacing the tower hollow, gesturing with a stick of firewood she did not remember picking up. "Ten years I've run free. Twelve years since I signed. And the first time the Broker needs something done, I find myself volunteering to walk his oldest debtor straight down his front road like a good little courier, and I thought it was *kindness*—"

"Maybe it was kindness," Theo said.

"Maybe it was both! That's what's vile about them. They never make you do anything, they just arrange things until doing it feels like your idea." She hurled the firewood back onto the coals, where it landed with a satisfying burst of sparks that did nothing whatsoever for her mood. "Every mile we've walked, I've been asking myself whose errand this is. And now I know. And I'm furious, and I'm ashamed, and I'm—" She stopped pacing. Looked at him. He was watching her with that steady keeper's patience, the hare forgotten, going cold and leathery on its stick. "And I haven't answered your real question yet, have I."

"Which is?"

"Whether I keep going." She sat back down across from him. Drew a breath. "Because here's the truth of it, Theo. Half of me wants to turn around. March back up, find some honest smuggling — is there such a thing, probably not, I'll invent it — and never look at this mountain again. Because my family built these roads for that thing below, and my signature is in its book, and every step I take downward is a step I was steered into taking, and a smarter woman would cut her losses."

"And the other half?"

"The other half watched you find your own name in that ledger and close it gently, like it was a sleeping animal. The other half has spent two weeks learning that you're the best man I've ever guided anywhere, and I've guided priests, fugitives, and one duchess who bit people." She managed a crooked smile. It cost her. "The other half says the Ashvales may have paved the road, but I get to choose what walks down it. And I choose you. Not the court's version. Mine."

* * *

Theo was quiet again — thinking-silence, weighted, directional — and then he reached across the fire and turned the hare stick, which had begun to smoke, and said:

"For what it's worth, I don't feel steered."

"You wouldn't. That's the whole art of it."

"No — I mean—" He frowned, working at it, the way he worked at everything, patiently, like picking a lock he respected. "The court arranges things so they feel like your idea. Fine. But I chose to come down here before I knew anything. I chose when all I knew was that my hand hurt and the stars were gone. If the Broker steered me, he steered a man with no memories and nothing to lose, and what he got was a man who intends to take it all back and burn the ledger doing it." His eyes came up, and there was something in them she hadn't seen before — not fear, not the keeper's calm. Something harder and brighter underneath. "Steering works on people going where they're told. Nobody knows where I'm going. Including me. It's very freeing."

Despite everything — despite the shame sitting in her chest like swallowed river stones — Wren laughed. A real one, startled out of her.

"You're deranged."

"Ten years of gatekeeping. It does things to a man."

"Ten years of *nothing happening*, you mean. The most exciting thing at Thornfell was the year the marmots got into the ward-stones."

"The marmots were a genuine crisis."

"They chewed through a containment rune and you wrote a formal report about it."

"A *thoroughly* worded report." And now he smiled, small and tired and real, and the firelight caught it, and Wren felt the stones in her chest shift, just slightly, not lighter exactly but settled. Bearable.

They ate the hare. It was, as usual, slightly burnt and completely delicious, because hunger is the finest chef alive. Wren cleaned the spit and wrapped the bones in cloth — you didn't leave bones lying around anymore, not with the dark leaking things upward that liked to wear what they found — and Theo banked the fire down to a red eye.

"Your grandfather," he said, as she rolled into her blanket. "Did he ever talk about what he carried? For them."

Wren lay looking at the broken ring of sky above the tower, where a few stars had come back — faint, wrong-angled, but back — and thought about it honestly, because he'd earned honesty and she was done rationing it.

"He talked about everything except that. He'd tell stories about the roads all night — the shrine tolls, the warden at the third pass, the time he outran a rockslide on the Switchback. But ask him what went down the mountain in his packs, and he'd go quiet as a locked door. Once — I was maybe ten — I asked him straight out. Was it treasure? Books?" She paused. "He said, 'It's heavier than gold, little bird, and it's always somebody's.' Then he taught me a new card game and never mentioned it again."

"Always somebody's," Theo repeated softly. His scarred hand lay open on his knee, palm up, sigil dim in the dying firelight. "Yes. That sounds like the Broker's arithmetic."

She wanted to say something comforting and couldn't find one that wasn't a lie, so instead she said, "Sleep. First watch is mine."

He went — trustingly, instantly, the way he did everything, and she sat alone with the red eye of the fire and the broken stars and the knowledge that her name was inked in a book beneath the mountain, and found that for the first time since the chapel, the knowledge didn't move. It just sat there. She could build around it. Smugglers built around worse.

* * *

Two hours into her watch, the cold changed.

She felt it before she understood it — the particular pressure-drop of the last few nights, the dark leaning close — and then the fire's red eye guttered flat though there was no wind, and Theo sat bolt upright out of a dead sleep with his sigil blazing through his bandages like a coal held under cloth.

"Wren." His voice was strange. Strained. "There's a road ahead of us that wasn't there yesterday."

She was on her feet, knife out, scanning the tower mouth. Beyond it, down the slope where the pass narrowed between two shoulders of black-ringed pine, the darkness had *organized*. That was the only word for it. It had gathered into a shape — a path, pale as old bone, winding down toward the lower passes, inviting as a lit doorway.

And standing at the head of it, small and still as a boundary stone, was a figure. Too far to see clearly. Near enough to be waiting.

"The shrine road," Wren breathed. Her mouth had gone dry. "Grandfather talked about this one. The toll-warden at the mountain shrine — you don't choose to reach it. It chooses when you arrive." She swallowed. "And Theo — whatever it wants, it wants it tonight, because that road doesn't open for anyone unless the court has business with them."

The sigil burned brighter. Theo looked at his own palm as though it had spoken without permission.

"Business," he said grimly, "seems to be the operative word."

Far down the bone-pale road, the figure raised one hand — courteous, patient, unmistakable — and beckoned.
