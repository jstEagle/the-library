## 9. The First Toll

The shrine appeared the way all the worst things in Thornfell seemed to: quietly, and exactly where Wren said it would be.

They had climbed for most of the day through a pass that Wren called "the Shortcut" in the tone other people reserved for words like *landslide* or *plague*, and by late afternoon the trees had thinned to a stubborn few pines bent sideways by wind, and the darkness that leaked up from below had thickened from a haze into something like weather. Not fog. Fog moved. This sat. It pooled in the hollows of the mountain like water finding its level, and when Theo waved his hand through it, it closed behind his fingers without a ripple.

"You're quiet," Wren said. She was three steps ahead, as she always was on descents—she claimed going downhill was where amateurs died—and she didn't turn around when she spoke. She never needed to. Ten days on the road together, and he'd learned she could hear a change in his breathing from thirty feet.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always quiet *comfortably*. This is quiet with corners in it." Now she turned, shading her eyes against the low sun, which hung behind the dark haze like a coin at the bottom of a well. "You've been rubbing your hand since noon. Out with it."

Theo looked down at his palm. The sigil sat there as it always did—a burn-scar in the shape of something between a knot and an eye, older than any memory he owned. For ten years it had been a dull companion, aching only when the wards were touched. Since the night the stars went out, it had been something else: a compass needle, a banked coal, a thing with opinions.

"It's warm," he admitted. "Not hurting. Just... aware. Like a dog that's heard something I haven't."

"Wonderful. I'm traveling with a man whose hand is a nervous hound." But she said it gently, and her eyes went past him, down the trail, to where the path narrowed between two shoulders of black stone. "Speaking of things with opinions. There it is."

* * *

The shrine was small enough that they nearly walked past it.

It stood in a fold of the mountainside—a ring of standing stones no taller than a man, each one carved over so completely with script that the rock beneath had stopped mattering. The writing wasn't in any language Theo knew, and yet his eyes kept snagging on it, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. In the center of the ring stood a basin of gray stone, dry as old bone, and beside the basin—

Beside the basin stood the toll-warden.

Theo's hand found Wren's arm before he'd decided to reach for it. "That's a person," he whispered. "Tell me that's a person."

"That's a person," Wren said. "Probably. They always are, at first." She lowered her voice further. "Toll-wardens. My grandfather's routes avoided them where they could. They sit at the crossings between what's above and what's below, and they collect. You want to pass deeper into the mountain, you pay. That's not smugglers' superstition—that's the oldest rule there is. Everything crossing gets weighed."

"What do they weigh?"

Wren didn't answer, because at that moment the warden raised its head.

It was an old man—or shaped like one—with a face like a riverbed, all channels and worn stone. His eyes were the problem. They were white, entirely, without iris or pupil, and they were fixed on the space just left of where Theo stood, which was somehow worse than being looked at directly. He wore robes that might once have been fine, now patched with scraps of cloth in a dozen mismatched colors, and around his neck hung a cord strung with small wooden tokens, each one carved with a single letter of no alphabet Theo could read.

"Travelers," the warden said. His voice was mild, courteous, terribly tired. "The crossing is open. The toll is owed."

"We're not crossing anything," Wren said smoothly, stepping forward with the easy slouch she put on for customs men and suspicious innkeepers. "Just stretching our legs. Lovely stones. Very atmospheric."

"The woman may pass free." The warden's blank eyes still hadn't moved. "She owes the mountain nothing but her footsteps. The man is another matter."

Theo felt the sigil flare—not painfully, not yet, but with the unmistakable heat of a brand pressed near skin. He stepped forward before he could think better of it. "What do you mean, another matter?"

The warden turned to him then, and Theo wished he hadn't. Being seen by those eyes was like being opened. "You carry a mark of the deep court, keeper. A debt-mark. And debts," the warden said, with something that in a kinder voice might have been sympathy, "accrue."

"I don't know anything about any debt."

"No," the warden agreed. "That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

Wren made a sound low in her throat—the sound of a woman deciding whether to laugh or draw a knife. "With respect, old father, we're passing through. What's the toll? Coin? We've got little enough, but—"

"The mountain doesn't eat coin." The warden gestured with one long finger toward the dry basin. "Memory. A memory, freely given, freely surrendered. One per traveler who bears the court's interest. That is the toll, and the toll is fair, and the toll is *owed*." Something shifted in his posture then—a straightening, a formality, like a clerk opening a ledger. "I should tell you what I am required to tell all payers. What you give cannot be returned by me. It goes below, into the account of the deep court. You will not miss it until you miss it. Choose carefully, if choosing is yours to do."

"And if we don't pay?" Theo asked.

The warden smiled, and it was the smile of a man at a funeral who has known the deceased a very long time. "Then the mountain keeps you here. Not unkindly. You'll simply find you can't remember why you were walking, or where you meant to go, and you'll sit down among the stones, and you'll stay. We've room." He nodded at the ring of standing stones, and Theo saw—for the first time, and would never afterward stop seeing—that the carvings on some of them were faces. Small, serene, worn smooth by weather. People-shaped.

"They're still in there?" Wren breathed. "The ones who wouldn't pay?"

"Some are resting," the warden said. "Some are waiting. It varies."

Theo looked at the basin. He looked at his palm. And he thought about the village they'd passed through two days ago—the one full of people who'd forgotten their own names, sweeping the same clean street over and over, smiling at strangers with the vacant courtesy of the hollowed-out. He thought about the old woman who had seized his hand, seen the sigil, and wept.

He thought, with sudden terrible clarity: *whatever I gave away ten years ago, I've been giving pieces ever since. Everyone around me pays for my bargain.*

"All right," he said.

"Theo." Wren caught his sleeve. Her voice had lost every ounce of smuggler's polish. "We don't know what he'll take. You don't get a menu. You don't get to pick—"

"I know."

"You *don't*. That's my whole point. You could walk out of this ring having forgotten how to read. Having forgotten me."

"He said choose carefully," Theo said. "So I'm choosing. If I refuse, the mountain keeps us both, and we're no use to anyone—including whatever's left of the border. Paying is the only way through, and through is the only way down, and down—" He flexed his scarred hand. The sigil pulsed, patient as a heartbeat. "Down is where my name is written in somebody's book. I intend to go and argue with whoever holds the pen."

Wren stared at him. Then she swore, comprehensively and with real artistry, in at least three dialects, and let go of his sleeve. "Fine. Fine. But I'm watching, and if you come out of this not knowing my name, I swear by every god I've ever cheated, I'll make you learn it again the hard way, with flashcards."

"That sounds genuinely horrifying."

"Good. Remember that feeling. Oh—" she stopped, winced. "*Sorry.*"

* * *

The warden guided him to the basin with the gentle efficiency of a barber.

"Sit, if you wish. Some stand. Kneeling is traditional but I won't insist; my last hundred years of insisting haven't improved compliance." Theo knelt anyway. The stone was cold through his trousers. Up close, the basin held nothing at all—it wasn't empty so much as *unfilled*, a shape waiting to be the opposite of a vessel.

"How does it work?" Theo asked. "Do I speak the memory? Think it?"

"You need do nothing so theatrical." The warden crouched beside him, robes pooling. Up close he smelled of dust and candle smoke and, faintly, of libraries. "The basin draws. Your part is only consent. Hold in your mind what you are willing to lose, and mean the losing. The rest is bookkeeping."

"And if I hold nothing? If I try to keep everything?"

"Then the basin chooses for you, and the basin is not sentimental." The warden tilted his head. "A word of counsel, keeper, since you've been polite and since politeness is scarce on this road. Do not offer what you treasure least. The court can taste reluctance. Offer something true—something that costs. Debts paid in counterfeit weigh light, and light payments invite... collection."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. Comfort is above my station." The warden rose. "Whenever you're ready. There's no hurry." A pause. "There is, however, eventually."

Theo bowed his head over the basin and searched himself.

This was the strange arithmetic of it: he had so little to begin with. Ten years of memories, no more. The tavern at the border, and the regulars, and the smell of the wards at dusk. Wren's laugh. Wren's terrible card cheating. The first time he'd stood at the gate alone at night and realized the silence felt less like exile than like home. All of it precious, all of it load-bearing. Take any beam out and the whole structure of the man he'd built might come down.

And beneath those ten years—nothing. Smooth wall. The basin couldn't draw from a locked room.

But there was one thing. One thing older than the wall, that had survived whatever surgery he'd done on himself, the way a splinter survives. He hadn't thought of it in years because thinking of it hurt, and he had become, in ten years, a great expert at not hurting.

His mother.

He didn't know her name anymore. He didn't know her voice, or her hands, or where they'd lived. All of that had gone below, or gone into whatever fire he'd signed with. But one image remained, lodged like a fossil: a kitchen, evening-gold, and a woman at the window with her back half-turned, laughing at something he couldn't recall saying, and the particular way the light came through her hair. That was all. One frame of a film with the reel burned away. He had carried it ten years like a coin sewn into a coat lining, checking sometimes that it was still there.

It was the only thing he owned from Before.

*Don't,* said some voice in him that sounded suspiciously like Wren. *That's the last of it. Find anything else. Find the tavern. Find the—*

But the warden had told him the truth, and Theo had spent ten years learning to recognize truth when it was spoken plainly, because he lived surrounded by his own lies of omission. Counterfeit payments invite collection. And if he was going down that mountain to take his soul back from a court that traded in them, he would not arrive owing them one more dishonest coin.

Besides—he was already missing it, and he hadn't even given it yet. That was the trick of memory. You missed it the whole time you had it.

"Keeper," the warden said softly. "Your face has changed. You've chosen?"

"Yes." Theo opened his hand, sigil up, over the basin. "I'm ready."

He held the kitchen in his mind. The gold light. The laughter he couldn't hear anymore, only *remember hearing*, a memory of a memory already fraying. The window. The hair. *I give this,* he thought, meaning it with everything he had. *Freely. Take it where it's owed.*

The basin drank.

There was no light show, no sound. The air simply leaned inward, the way a flame leans, and Theo felt the memory lift out of him the way a thorn leaves a wound—there, and then not there, and then a smoothness where it had been. He gasped. Not from pain. From the sheer *absence*, the new empty room inside him where a kitchen used to be.

"Mother—" he started, testing, reaching.

And found nothing.

Not the word itself—the word remained, a label on an empty drawer—but nothing behind it. No face. No window. No gold. He knew, intellectually, in the way you know a country you've never visited, that he must have had a mother, that everyone does. But the knowledge had all the warmth of an entry in a census. Somewhere in him a door had closed on a room he hadn't known he was standing in until the floor vanished.

He stayed kneeling a long moment. His eyes were wet and he let them be. Some things deserved the ceremony of tears even after the thing itself was gone.

"There," the warden said quietly. "It's done, and done cleanly, and I will not insult you by thanking you." He extended a hand and helped Theo rise—an unexpectedly strong grip. "You may pass. Both of you. The toll is—"

He stopped.

The warden's blank white eyes had dropped to Theo's outstretched palm, still open from the paying, sigil bare to the fading light. And the change that came over the old man's face was the change Theo had seen once before, years ago, on a garrison surgeon opening a soldier's bandages: the look of a professional discovering the wound is far worse than advertised.

"Oh," the warden said.

"What?" Wren was across the ring in four strides, hand on her knife. "What's wrong? He paid. He paid fair, he paid true, I watched him—"

"He paid true," the warden agreed. His voice had gone thin. "That's what frightens me. Look at the mark."

Theo looked. So did Wren. And Theo saw it—saw it clearly for the first time, because the sigil had begun to glow, faint and steady, a coal uncovered.

Around the old knot-and-eye shape, new lines had surfaced. Fine ones, fresh as ink on wet paper, spiraling outward from the central scar like frost growing across a window. And threaded among them, tiny as grains of rice, were letters.

"Do you read it?" the warden demanded.

"No," Theo said. "Should I?"

"I can't either, and I have read the court's script for six hundred years, which means it isn't script for reading. It's script for *accounting*." The warden took Theo's wrist—gently, the way one handles a document—and turned the hand toward the last gray light. "When you arrived, this was a debtor's mark. Dormant. A record of an old transaction, nothing more. Now—" his finger traced the air above the spreading lines without touching them— "now it's an *invoice*."

"Invoice," Wren repeated flatly.

"The payment you just made was received, logged, and applied," the warden said. "And the moment it was applied, the account updated. Which is how I can see what I was never meant to see, because the mark shows its balance to anyone who knows how to look." He released Theo's wrist and stepped back, and for the first time the ancient creature looked afraid. "Keeper, your account is not merely open. It is *in arrears*. Massively. The court has carried your debt for ten years without moving to collect—which no one who deals with the Broker will believe, because the Broker collects the way winter collects. Unless..."

"Unless what?" Theo's mouth had gone dry. The sigil pulsed again, warmer, and this time he could feel it in his teeth.

"Unless the delay was itself a term of the contract. Unless someone bought you *time*." The warden's white eyes lifted to Theo's face. "Someone with standing. Someone the Broker honors. And that arrangement, whatever it was, has just ended—because arrears this deep don't sit quietly. The account has been flagged. Marked overdue. And when the deep court marks a debtor overdue, keeper, it stops sending wardens."

Wren's voice came out very level, which Theo had learned was her most dangerous register. "What does it send instead?"

"From this direction," the warden said, "you will meet riders."

The word dropped into the silence of the ring of stones and lay there. Somewhere above the pass, the false darkness thickened, and the last of the sun gave up trying.

"They ride at night," the warden went on, already turning away, gathering his patched robes, suddenly in a hurry in a way that terrified Theo more than anything else he'd said. "They ride fast. And they wear the faces of everyone the court has ever collected—which, given the size of their ledgers, is a great many faces indeed. You paid fairly and I bear you no ill will, so take an old collector's advice and *run*, and don't stop running where the ground is soft, because they leave tracks that don't wash out, and neither do the people who follow them." He paused at the edge of the ring, silhouetted against the dying light. "One more thing, keeper. The letters on your mark. I couldn't read them. But I recognized the shape of the sum."

"Tell me," Theo said.

"They aren't numbers." The warden's tired voice drifted back through the stones. "Debts are always numbers. These are letters because they spell something. And the shape of the word, as best I could see..." He hesitated. "It spelled *overdue*. In your own handwriting."

Then he was gone—simply not there anymore, the way the darkness wasn't fog, the way the basin hadn't been empty—and Theo stood in the ring of sleeping stone-faces with his burning hand and his one fewer memory and the absolute certainty that the mountain had just changed its mind about letting them leave.

Wren grabbed his elbow. "Riders," she said. "Night's coming. Walk fast, ask questions later."

They ran.
