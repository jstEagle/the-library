## 10. Hunters of the Dark

Wren Ashvale had been in trouble before. She'd been chased off the Kettering estate with hounds behind her and a silver candlestick down her shirt. She'd spent a night in a Lowmark cell with a pickpocket who snored like a landslide. Once, at fourteen, she'd had to talk her way past a border sergeant while her grandfather's entire contraband shipment of smuggled cheese sweated in the cart behind her.

None of it — none of it — had prepared her for what came out of the dark on the third night below the tree-line.

They'd made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang of gray stone, a fire no bigger than two cupped hands, because Theo insisted that whatever was wrong with the world up here, fire still argued back. He sat across from her turning his bandaged palm over and over, as if the sigil might have changed its mind overnight and become something harmless. A birthmark. A bad tattoo. A love token from a woman whose name he'd sold.

"Stop staring at it," Wren said.

"I'm not staring at it."

"You've turned your hand over eleven times since we ate. I counted."

"That's not staring, that's inventory." He said it dryly, but his eyes didn't laugh, and that was the thing about Theo Delacroix that had kept her interested these ten years of border crossings: the humor sat on top of him like frost on a fence rail. Underneath, something was always burning.

She was banking the fire when the horses screamed.

Not whinnied. *Screamed* — the high, human-sounding terror that horses almost never gave voice to unless something had come among them that they could smell but not name. Wren was on her feet with a knife in her hand before she'd decided to stand, which was the Ashvale way: think afterward, survive meanwhile.

Theo's palm flared.

She'd seen it ache. She'd seen it glow faintly through his wrappings, a lantern behind linen. This was different. The light punched through the cloth like a brand through paper, white and gold and furious, throwing their shadows huge against the stone, and Theo made a sound like a man grabbing a hot kettle handle and refusing to let go.

"They're coming," he said.

"What's coming?"

"I don't know." His jaw clenched. "It does."

The horses broke loose from their picket line and bolted uphill into the black, and Wren heard — she would hear it the rest of her life — hoofbeats answering them. Coming *down*. Slow. Deliberate. Unhurried the way wolves are unhurried when the sheep are penned.

"The hollow," she said. "Back wall's climbable if we—"

The first rider came around the shoulder of the hill, and every plan she had died in her throat.

It sat a horse like any rider sits a horse, except that where its face should have been there was a face — that was the horror of it, that it *had* one, a real one, weathered and kind-eyed, a face like a miller or a shepherd or somebody's grandfather. It just wasn't attached to anything behind it. The skin lay over emptiness the way a glove lies over a hand that isn't there. And Wren knew the face.

"Master Ellory," she whispered. "That's — Theo, that's Master Ellory, he ran the ferry at Coldbrook, he drowned when I was twelve—"

A second rider followed. Then a third. Faces she half-knew, faces she didn't know at all, all of them worn like borrowed coats by things that were not people. Hollow riders. The soul-hunters of the deep court, riding down out of a darkness that leaked upward through the mountain like water through a roof.

*They wear the forgotten,* Theo had read aloud from the chapel ledger, three nights and a lifetime ago. *The court sends its unclaimed to collect its debts.*

"Wren." Theo's voice cracked on her name. "Run."

* * *

They ran.

Downhill, gods help them, downhill into the passes, because the only other direction was the cliff, and Wren had done enough stupid things in her life to know the difference between brave-stupid and dead-stupid. The ground tore at her boots. Pine branches raked her arms. Behind them the hoofbeats came on, patient as arithmetic, never gaining, never falling back, keeping pace the way debt keeps pace.

"They're herding us," she gasped. "This is — there'll be a cut ahead, a ravine, they want us boxed—"

"How do you know?"

"Because it's what I'd do!" She grabbed his sleeve and hauled him left, off the trail, into a fold of rock she remembered from a run two summers ago — a chimney of stone, narrow, climbing. "Smuggler's rule. When you're being chased, stop running their chase. Come on. Up."

Theo went up. That was another thing she'd learned about him in the last week: he complained, he doubted, he asked questions until she wanted to smother him with his own cloak — and then, when it mattered, he moved. No hesitation. As though some part of him older than his memory already trusted her.

She hated how much she liked that part.

The chimney spat them out onto a shelf above a stream-cut gorge, and Wren allowed herself exactly one breath of relief before the light found them.

It rose out of the gorge itself. Not the riders' torches — the riders carried none — but a pale, cold glow, and in it she saw the ravine floor below, and saw why the hollow riders hadn't hurried.

There were more of them. Dozens. Standing their horses in the shallows, faces tilted up toward the shelf, all those borrowed eyes reflecting nothing at all. And among the faces — she pressed her hand over her mouth — among the faces were villagers. The forgetful ones from the village that had lost its names. An old man who'd offered them bread. A child.

"No," Theo said beside her. Quietly. Terribly. "No, they can't have—"

"They haven't taken anyone new." Her own voice sounded far away. "Look at their hands, Theo. Look at their *edges*."

And once she made herself see it, she couldn't unsee it: the riders weren't solid. They frayed. Their outlines shimmered like heat-haze, like candle flames in a draft, like things held together by will and ledger-entry alone. The court hadn't harvested new souls. It had dressed its old stock in the faces it kept.

"It's a message," she said. "This is theater. This is the Broker showing you what happens to souls he holds."

"Then the message is received," Theo said, "and I'd very much like to decline the meeting."

The nearest rider lifted its head — Master Ellory's drowned face, mild and patient — and spoke. Not with a mouth moving. With a voice that arrived inside the ear, the way your own thoughts do.

*Delacroix. Overdue.*

"I know," Theo said through his teeth.

*Ten years of interest accrues.*

"I know."

*We are sent to carry you home gently.* The hollow eyes turned — slowly, deliberately — to Wren. *Or to carry what remains.*

Every hair on Wren's body stood up. But underneath the fear, something else was kindling, and it took her a moment to recognize it because it so rarely visited: fury. Cold, clean, banked-coal fury. Ten years her family had run routes for that thing under the mountain without knowing — her grandfather's maps, her father's silence, her whole inheritance of shortcuts and safehouses, all of it bent in service of a ledger-keeper who wore drowned ferrymen like gloves.

"You don't touch him," she said.

The riders' faces turned toward her. All of them at once. It was like being regarded by a field of portraits.

*Ashvale,* said the voice inside her ear, and it was almost warm now, almost kind, and that was worse. *Your family knows our courtesy. Your family has profited by our patience. Step aside, and the court remembers kindness.*

"They can remember it," Wren said. "I'm busy."

Below, the horses shifted. The cold light brightened.

Theo caught her arm. "Wren—"

"Don't." She didn't look at him. "Don't tell me to run. You did that already and I ignored you then too, you just weren't listening closely."

"That's fair," he admitted. "I was a bit preoccupied."

Above the gorge, on the shelf, with forty hollow riders between them and any road home, Wren Ashvale laughed — short and sharp and entirely genuine — and felt her fear turn over inside her like soil under a plow, and come up bearing something else.

* * *

The riders came up the gorge walls the way water comes up a beach. Not fast. Inevitable. Hooves finding purchase on bare stone, borrowed faces serene, and the cold light rising with them until the whole pass was lit like the inside of a lantern.

Wren fought the way her family had always fought: dirty, early, and sideways. She rolled stones onto the first climber. She cut the girth strap of a second saddle with a throw she'd practiced on chickens, God forgive her, and watched rider and empty saddle go down separately. None of it stopped anything. The fallen got up. The frayed edges knit. They were fighting smoke that had learned to ride.

"We need higher ground!" she shouted. "There's a shelf — a goat path — Theo, are you even *hearing* me?"

He wasn't. He stood at the lip of the shelf with his hand outstretched and his sigil blazing, and the light was doing something new — it pulsed now, slow and enormous, like a heartbeat the size of the valley, and each pulse made the nearest riders recoil. Their frayed edges streamed away from him like smoke from a wind.

*Delacroix,* the voice said, and for the first time it wasn't calm. *You were sold. You were paid for. You belong—*

"I don't." His voice came out low, and Wren felt it in her sternum. "I did belong to you. Ten years ago. Whatever I was, whoever signed, whatever I traded — it's *paid*, you said so yourself. Interest accrues. Well, look at the account, Broker. Look at what your collection has cost you tonight, and tell me again who owes whom."

The light flared.

Wren threw her arm across her eyes, and even through her sleeve the world went white-gold. She heard the riders scream — all of them, dozens, a chorus of borrowed voices crying out in borrowed throats — and heard hooves, and heard the cold guttering of that pale light collapsing like a snuffed candle, and when she could see again the gorge was empty.

Empty, except for Theo Delacroix, on his knees in the scree, breathing like a bellows, his bandaged hand smoking gently.

* * *

She got to him fast, knees hitting stone, and grabbed his shoulders and shook him once, hard, because she didn't trust her hands to do anything gentler.

"Never do that again."

"Do what?" He lifted his head. He looked gray as ash, wrung out, ten years older than he'd been at supper.

"The hero thing. The standing-in-the-light thing. You nearly—" Her voice did something embarrassing, so she stopped talking and started working instead: unwinding the bandage, checking the burn beneath. The sigil had changed again. The word branded into his palm had gained a second stroke, a fresh line of scar shining like it had been cut minutes ago, and when she touched it Theo hissed and she snatched her fingers back.

"Sorry. Sorry." She rewrapped it, quick and competent, because hands needed jobs or they started shaking. "Does it hurt?"

"Like I grabbed the sun." He flexed the fingers carefully. "But they're gone."

"For tonight." She tied off the knot. "They're gone for tonight. They'll be back, and angrier, and probably with paperwork."

He laughed. It startled both of them, that laugh — rough and surprised, like a door opening in a house thought long empty — and then he was laughing properly, doubled over in the scree, and after a moment Wren was laughing too, the wild post-fear laughter of two people alive against all sense, sitting in a ruined pass under a starless sky with smoke still rising off his hand.

When it faded, he looked at her. Really looked, the way he rarely let himself.

"You stepped in front of me," he said. "First rider up the wall. You put your body between me and it."

"You were busy."

"Wren."

"It's called tactics," she said. "I told you. Smuggler's rule. Protect the cargo."

Something crossed his face at that — something complicated and hurt and grateful all tangled together — and she realized, too late, what she'd said. *Cargo.* Him. The man she'd known for ten years of tavern corners and wry greetings, the invisible gatekeeper she'd teased about his dull little life, and here he was, revealed as a stolen soul with a burning brand, and she had just told him, out loud, that he was the thing worth protecting on this whole cursed mountain.

She should have taken it back. Made a joke. Deflected, the way Ashvales deflected, the way her father had taught her: never show the ledger of your heart to anyone who might audit it.

Instead she heard herself say, "And I don't mean the trade goods kind."

The silence between them stretched, thin as wire, holding weight.

"Get some sleep," she said gruffly, shoving his bedroll at him. "Whatever's next, we meet it rested."

He took the bedroll. Their fingers overlapped on it a moment longer than the transaction required.

"Wren. Why?" he asked quietly. "You've seen what I am. What I cost. What my bargain did to that village, to those people. Every reason to leave me to them. So why?"

She looked out at the dark pass where the riders had been, at the black sky where stars ought to be, and found the truth sitting in her chest where fear had been, having quietly evicted it sometime during the chase.

"Because they called you *overdue*," she said. "Like you were theirs. Like you'd ever belonged to anybody but yourself." She met his eyes. "Nobody gets collected on my watch. That's the rule now. My rule. Write it down."

He smiled — small, tired, real — and lay back against the stone, and within minutes his breathing slowed toward sleep, the sigil's glow dimming under its wrapping to a faint ember-pulse, steady as a hearth.

Wren sat awake, knife across her knees, watching the dark.

And that was why she saw it first: far up the pass, at the very limit of sight, a single point of light that was not a star — stars were still gone — a cold, patient, candle-colored gleam, bobbing gently, like a lantern carried by someone walking down out of the mountains toward them.

Behind it, faintly, came music. A fiddle, maybe. Or something that wanted her to think it was a fiddle.

"Wake up, Theo," she said softly. "Company's coming."
