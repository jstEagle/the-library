## 11. The Healer's Question

The healer's cottage sat in a fold of the mountain like a coin dropped in a pocket and forgotten—low-roofed, turf-covered, with smoke curling from a chimney that seemed to grow out of the hillside itself. Theo had walked past three false starts before Wren, grey-lipped and swaying in the saddle, had croaked, "Left at the split birch. The one that looks like it's praying."

The birch did look like it was praying. So did the cottage. Theo was beginning to feel that everything on this side of the passes was either praying or starving.

He carried Wren up the path because she refused to be carried any other way—"I can *walk*, Delacroix, I've been stabbed worse than this"—which was a lie so transparent that he simply kept walking while she made indignant noises against his shoulder. The wound in her side was long rather than deep, a parting gift from the hollow rider whose face had worn the features of a girl who might have been somebody's daughter, once, before something beneath the mountain wore her instead.

The door opened before he knocked.

The woman standing there was old the way river stones are old—not frail, but worn smooth by an enormous amount of time passing over her. Her hair was white as frost and cut bluntly at the jaw, and her eyes were the pale grey of weather about to make up its mind.

"Well," she said, looking at Wren draped across Theo's arms like an armful of contraband wool. "Ashvale blood. Third one of you I've stitched. You lot treat your bodies like ledgers—always carrying more than they should." She stepped aside. "Bed's by the fire. Mind the cat."

There was no cat visible. Theo decided not to ask.

* * *

The healer—who told them to call her Maren, "or nothing, names are optional out here"—worked in silence for the better part of an hour. She cleaned the wound with something that smelled like pine tar and regret, packed it with moss that glowed faintly green when it touched blood, and stitched with a bone needle that moved faster than Theo could comfortably watch. Wren hissed twice, swore once with real artistry, and otherwise bit down on a leather strap and endured.

Theo stood by the fire and tried to be useful, which mostly meant feeding sticks into it and trying not to look at the dark stain spreading through Wren's shirt.

It was his fault. He knew that with the flat certainty of a stone at the bottom of a well. The soul-hunters had come for him—for the sigil, for whatever the court beneath the mountain considered outstanding debt—and Wren had put herself between him and a rider wearing the face of someone forgotten. She'd taken a blade meant for a man who wasn't even sure he deserved to have people take blades for him.

"Stop doing that," Wren said, without opening her eyes.

"Doing what?"

"Brewing guilt. It smells like burnt barley. It's very distracting."

"You've lost blood. You're hallucinating."

"I'm *observant*." She cracked one eye open, found him, and managed half a smirk despite the strap-marks on her mouth. "You've been staring at your hand for an hour. The sigil's not going to apologize just because you glare at it."

She was right, which was annoying. The sigil had dimmed since the fight—a low ember-glow now rather than the white flare that had driven the riders screaming back into the dark—but it still pulsed in time with his heartbeat, and every pulse felt like a question being asked of him from underneath his own skin.

Maren tied off the last stitch, snipped the thread with a small knife, and sat back on her heels.

"She'll live," she said. "The moss will do the rest. Ashvales heal stubborn—it's how they survive their own decisions." She looked up at Theo, and her weather-grey eyes were suddenly much sharper than they had any right to be. "You'll want tea. Both of you. Sit."

It was not phrased as an offer.

* * *

The tea was bitter and dark and tasted faintly of the same pine tar as the wound-packing, which made Theo suspect Maren believed all medicine should announce itself. They drank around the fire—Wren propped on cushions looking furious about being propped on cushions, Theo perched on a stool, Maren in a chair that creaked like a ship.

For a while nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind combed through the praying birch.

"Black rings on the trees past Coldmere," Maren said at last, conversationally, into the silence. "Memory-blind travelers wandering the high road, calling their mothers by the wrong names. Stars gone out over Thornfell like snuffed candles." She sipped her tea. "And an Ashvale smuggler and a man with a debtor's mark walking toward the mountain. I'm an old woman. Humor me. Tell me what's happening, so I know whether to bar my door or bless it."

So Theo told her. Not everything—he couldn't; whole decades of himself were missing, gaps where a life should have been—but enough. The wards dying. The magistrate in Lowmark and the word *debtor*. The ledger in the forgotten village, and his own name written in it ten years ago. The descent. The toll-warden, and the memory of his mother's face that he had paid without hesitation and regretted with every step since. The hunters. Wren bleeding.

When he finished, Maren was quiet for a long moment, turning her cup in her hands.

"Ten years," she said. "You gave the court ten years of yourself, and now you mean to walk down and take them back."

"That's the plan," Theo said.

"Mm." She set the cup down. "And when you find them? When some clerk in that candlelit city hands you back everything you sold—your name, your history, whatever man you were before Thornfell?" She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight made her eyes gleam like coins at the bottom of a well. "What happens to the man sitting on my stool?"

The question went through Theo like the warden's needle.

"I—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't understand the question."

"Oh, I think you do." Maren's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Let me put it plainly, then. There's a woman on my bed with fresh stitches who took steel for you. Not for the border. Not for the wards. For *you*—the specific man who makes dry jokes about porridge and checks the gate locks twice because once isn't polite. Now imagine she gets exactly what she's risking her neck for. Imagine you come back up that mountain with ten years of Theo Delacroix poured back in. Is he the same man?" She spread her hands. "Or is he a stranger wearing a face she trusts?"

The fire crackled. Wind worried the birch. On the bed, Wren had gone very still, her cup halfway to her lips, watching him.

Theo looked down at his palm. The sigil pulsed—once, twice—patient as a creditor.

"I don't know," he said finally. It came out smaller than he wanted.

"Honest," Maren said. "That's rarer than you'd think, especially among men heading downhill."

"But I *have* to get them back." He heard his own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "Whatever I was, whoever—I sold pieces of myself to seal that gate. The stars are out because of what I did. The village below can't remember its own *names*, and that's the leak from my bargain. If I don't go down and fix it—"

"Then fix it," Maren agreed placidly. "I never said don't. I said: do you know who comes back up?"

"No." The word fell out of him, honest and heavy. "No. I don't. Maybe the man I was was better than I am. Maybe he was worse—the ledger said things I couldn't read. Maybe I get my memories back and discover I *chose* to forget for reasons that would make Wren walk away." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Maybe I get them back and discover I liked porridge jokes then too and none of this matters. I don't know. That's the truth. Ten years ago I handed over the only person who could answer your question, and I've been making it up ever since."

Silence again. Wren hadn't moved. Her eyes were fixed on him with an expression he couldn't read—or perhaps, he thought bleakly, an expression he could read perfectly well and didn't want to name.

Maren nodded slowly, as if he'd passed some examination he hadn't studied for.

"There it is," she said. "That's the wound under the wound. Everything else—you can carry. But a man walking down to reclaim himself had best decide first *which self* he's reclaiming, or the mountain will decide for him. Courts beneath the world aren't in the business of returning things as they were." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and gathered the teacups. "They return things as they're owed. There's a difference. It matters."

"What would you do?" Theo asked. "If it were you?"

The old woman paused at the hearth, her back to him, framed by the small fire.

"When I was young," she said, "I was somebody else entirely. Different name. Different sins. I came up here to disappear, and I did such a thorough job that when I finally got the chance to go back and be her again—" she shook her head, white hair catching the light, "—I found I didn't want her. I'd grown a new woman in the space she left. Better one, I think. Or at least one I chose." She glanced back at him, and for a moment the sharpness in her eyes softened into something almost kind. "The past isn't a coat you take back off a peg, keeper. Sometimes it fits. Sometimes you've changed shape. Know which before you dress."

She padded out with the cups, leaving Theo alone with the fire and the weight of it.

* * *

Later—much later, when Maren had banked the fire and vanished into some inner room, and the wind had settled into a low conversation with the eaves—Wren spoke from the bed in the dark.

"She's wrong, you know."

Theo turned. She was lying on her side, facing him, her eyes open and catching the ember-light.

"About which part?" he asked carefully.

"The part where you come back a stranger." She said it steadily, but her hand had found the edge of the blanket and gripped it. "People aren't ledgers. You can't just—pour the old entries back in and have the sum come out different. Whatever you were ten years ago, you've been *here* since. Ten years of cold mornings and bad porridge and checking the locks twice. That counts. That adds up." A pause. "You count."

"Do I?" His voice was rougher than intended. "Because I keep thinking—if I get it all back, if I remember why I really did it, what I really was—maybe the man who took a blade for me signed on to guard someone who doesn't exist anymore. And I can't—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I can't lose you to a version of me I've never met."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then, wryly, painfully:

"Delacroix. I grew up in a smuggling family. Half my relatives are strangers wearing faces I know. Believe me when I tell you I can tell the difference between a man and a mask." She shifted, winced, settled. "Whoever you were down there—you checked the gate locks twice because once wasn't polite. That's not a mask. Masks don't worry about politeness."

He laughed quietly despite everything, and the sound loosened something in his chest that had been clenched since the toll-warden's shrine.

"Sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we walk. And Theo—" her voice was already going drowsy, "—whatever you find down there... bring it up anyway. We'll sort out which parts fit. That's what I'm for. I'm very good with stolen goods."

He lay back and stared at the dark beams overhead, her breathing evening out across the room, Maren's question circling him like the wind circled the birch.

*Which self are you reclaiming?*

He pressed his palm flat against the floorboards and felt the sigil pulse its slow, patient pulse. Somewhere far below—deeper than roots, deeper than memory—candlelight burned in a city built from stolen years, and somewhere in it a clerk waited with ten years of Theo Delacroix in a drawer, ready to hand them back like change from a purchase.

He had no answer. All he had was the man he'd been these ten years, the woman sleeping behind him, and a road leading down.

In the morning, they would follow it.
