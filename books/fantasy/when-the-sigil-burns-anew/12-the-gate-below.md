## 12. The Gate Below

The mountain swallowed them by inches.

For three days they had climbed through passes that Wren insisted were roads, though Theo would have called them suggestions—scree fields that shifted like bad-tempered sheep, ledges barely wider than a boot, a frozen waterfall they crossed by pressing themselves flat against the rock behind it while the ice groaned overhead like an old man complaining about his knees. Theo's sigil had been quiet since the shrine, which frightened him more than the burning had. A wound that stops hurting, he thought, is either healing or dying.

"You're doing it again," Wren said.

"Doing what?"

"That face. The one where you're thinking so loudly I can hear you." She scrambled up ahead of him, sure-footed as a goat with better boots. "Whatever it is, say it out loud. You mutter less when you talk."

"I was thinking that the sigil's gone quiet."

Wren stopped. Turned. Her breath came out in white plumes that the wind stole immediately. "Quiet how?"

"Quiet like a held breath."

She looked at his palm for a long moment, though the bandages hid everything now. After the toll-shrine, after the hunters, he'd wrapped the hand himself, and she hadn't asked to see it. He suspected that was her way of being kind: pretending not to notice the thing that defined him.

"Held breaths end," she said finally. "Usually all at once. Keep moving."

* * *

On the fourth day the trees stopped.

Not thinned out—stopped, in a line so clean it looked drawn with a ruler. One moment they were climbing through black-ringed pines whose growth spirals curled inward like fingerprints; the next, bare stone, and cold that came up through their boots from below rather than down from above.

Theo knelt and put his good hand flat on the rock.

It was warm.

"Wren," he said. "The mountain's warm."

"That's not possible."

"No," he agreed. "And yet."

They followed the warmth. It led them along a shelf of stone that curved steadily downward and inward, into the mountain's root, until the shelf opened without warning into a cavern so vast that Theo's lantern couldn't find its walls or its ceiling. The light simply gave up, the way sound gives up in deep snow.

Except there *was* light. Far below them—farther than the eye had any right to travel—a glow. Not firelight. Not daylight. Something between: the color of candleflame seen through honey, of lamplight behind parchment, of every warm thing made small and distant.

"The surface wards," Theo said slowly, "were built at the top of this."

"What?"

"The gate I kept. Ten years I tended it, and I thought—" He laughed, and hated the sound of his own laugh, thin and echoing. "I thought I was keeping something out of Thornfell. But the wards at Thornfell are just... an echo. A shadow thrown up the mountainside. The real door is here. It always has been. I've been guarding the reflection my whole ten years and never knew there was a window."

Wren came to stand beside him at the shelf's edge. Below, half-hidden in the honeyed glow, he could see structure—the suggestion of towers, of streets, of a city laid out in the dark like a jewel box opened by someone who shouldn't have.

"How do we get down?" she asked.

"There's a stair." He didn't know how he knew. That was happening more often—knowledge arriving in him like weather, unbidden and unexplained, residue of whatever self he'd sold. "There. Along the left wall. See where the glow catches the edges? Steps. Thousands of them, probably."

"And you just *know* that."

"I just know that."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "Is any of the rest of you going to come back like that? Uninvited?"

"I don't know." He met her eyes. In the lantern light her face was sharp and tired and steady, and he found—as he had been finding, with increasing alarm since the shrine—that looking at her steadied him more than anything else did. "Does it frighten you? The idea that I might turn back into someone you didn't agree to walk beside?"

Wren considered this with the seriousness she gave to route-planning and haggling and almost nothing else.

"It frightens me that you keep asking," she said. "Ask me again when we're out. If the answer changes, I'll tell you then. Right now you're the man who paid a memory of his mother to get us past a toll-warden, so as far as I'm concerned, whoever you used to be has some large debts to answer for before he gets a vote." She shouldered her pack. "Come on. Stairs."

* * *

The stair went down for hours.

It was cut clean, each step fitted without mortar, and it wound along the cavern wall in long switchbacks that gave them view after view of the city below—and each view was worse than the last, or better, Theo couldn't decide which, and the not-deciding sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Because it was beautiful.

That was the revulsion of it. He had imagined the deep court as rot—he'd imagined goblin-warrens, bone ornaments, the squalor of things that live in the dark because they can't bear the light. What he saw instead was a city of candlelight. Thousands upon thousands of candles, no two the same height, arranged along streets that curved like script. Buildings of pale stone that seemed grown rather than built. Bridges arching over streets with no river beneath them. And everywhere the candles, each one burning with a steadiness no honest flame possesses, each one casting shadows that moved slightly wrong, slightly slow, like reflections in water that hasn't decided to obey.

"They're not candles," Theo said, somewhere around the thousandth step. His voice came out hoarse. "Look at the shadows."

Wren looked. Her jaw tightened. "What are they, then?"

He reached for the knowledge again, let it arrive like weather. "Years," he said. "Each flame is someone's years. Stolen time, burned slow. That's the light of this place—it's lit with what they've taken." His stomach turned. "That bridge there. Do you see how the stones fit? Those aren't stones. Look closer."

Wren looked, and made a small involuntary sound.

The bridge was built of pages. Thousands of them, pressed and fused into blocks, covered edge to edge in fine close writing. Every building in the city, when he truly looked, had the same texture beneath the stone-façade: paper, vellum, binding-leather. Ledgers. The entire city was constructed from records—contracts, accounts, entries—built from the bookkeeping of souls.

"It's a library," Wren whispered. "No. It's worse. It's a *counting house*."

"The court doesn't live in the dark," Theo said. "It lives in the arithmetic." And the words came out of him with a fluency that scared him, that felt borrowed, that felt like the voice of the man he'd been before. He pressed his bandaged hand against his chest and kept walking.

* * *

Near the bottom of the stair, they stopped in the shadow of an alcove, because the street below was not empty.

Goblins—Theo supposed that was still the word—moved through the candlelit ways with the unhurried purpose of clerks closing shop. They were smaller than he'd expected, neat rather than monstrous, dressed in gray livery with ink-stained fingers. Some carried candles. Some carried books. None of them spoke; the only sounds in the city were the soft scuff of feet and, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, the whisper of quills on paper.

One of them paused directly below their alcove, lifted its head as if listening, and stood motionless for the length of thirty heartbeats. Theo held his breath. The sigil stayed silent under its wrappings, which somehow felt like betrayal.

Then the clerk moved on, and the city's whispers closed over the silence it left.

"They didn't see us," Wren breathed.

"No."

"Why not?"

Theo unwound the bandage from his hand. The sigil lay there, dark and cool, a brand gone to ash. He stared at it and understood, with a lurch, why it had gone quiet.

"Because I'm expected," he said. "This mark isn't a warning to them. It's a *pass*. I sold myself to this place once. As far as the city knows, I'm not an intruder. I'm property coming home."

Wren was quiet. Then she did something he didn't expect: she took his marked hand in both of hers, careful of the brand, and held it the way you hold a bird—firmly enough that it won't fly off badly, gently enough that it won't panic.

"Then we use it," she said. "You walk in the front way, bold as brass, because that's the one door they won't think to bar. And I go the smuggler's way." She nodded toward a fissure in the cavern wall, half-hidden behind a fallen slab, breathing cold air like a sleeping thing. "Every great house has a servants' entrance, even houses made of sin. My family mapped these routes for generations. Whatever they were used for before—" her voice caught, steadied, "—they work for me now."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Her grip tightened. "We settled this in the chapel and again at the shrine and I'll settle it as many times as you need, but the answer doesn't change. We go in together or not at all, and 'together' means each of us takes the road the other can't. Find your contract. Steal yourself back. I'll find you." She released his hand, pulled her hood up, and managed, despite everything, the crooked half-smile that had gotten them through a hundred miles of dark. "Try to look like property coming home. You're terrible at looking harmless, but I believe in practice."

He laughed despite himself—quietly, so the city wouldn't hear—and for one moment, standing at the threshold of a city built from stolen years, he felt something absurdly like luck.

* * *

The gate itself stood at the head of the main street, and it was nothing like he'd imagined.

He'd imagined doors—iron, oak, banded, barred. Instead there was an archway of white stone taller than the Lowmark church tower, and set within it a pair of gates wrought from something dark and glassy, and both gates stood open. Wide open. Untended. As if the city had never once needed to lock its door, because everything it wanted walked in willingly.

Carved above the arch, in letters that predated any alphabet Theo knew but which he could read anyway—more weather arriving, more of the sold man leaking through—was a single line:

**ALL ACCOUNTS SETTLED IN FULL.**

Beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic:

**THE LEDGER IS ALWAYS OPEN.**

Theo stood before it a long time. Behind him: the stair, the dark, the world of stars (absent stars), the border he had guarded for ten years without knowing what he guarded. Ahead of him: candlelight, and the whisper of quills, and whatever remained of Theo Delacroix filed away in some clerk's careful hand.

His sigil stirred at last—not burning, not flaring, just warming, like a hand placed against his palm from the other side of his skin. Welcoming him. And that, more than anything, told him what kind of homecoming this would be.

"Well," he said softly, to no one, to himself, to the man he'd been. "I'm coming to collect."

He stepped through the gate, and the city of candles turned its attention toward him like a single eye.

Somewhere far off, in a tower at the city's heart, a bell that had not rung in ten years began, very quietly, to ring.
