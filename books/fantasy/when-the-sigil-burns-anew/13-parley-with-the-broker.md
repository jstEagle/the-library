## 13. Parley with the Broker

The candle city had a smell to it. That was the first thing Theo noticed as they were led down the avenue of white stone—wax and old paper and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun. Not unpleasant. That was the worst part. It smelled like a place you might *want* to stay.

The clerks walked ahead of them in perfect silence. There must have been two dozen of them—thin figures in grey robes, each carrying a ledger chained to its wrist, each with eyes like snuffed candles—and they moved through their own city without looking at it. They didn't need to. Theo understood that much instinctively. You don't look at your own home.

Wren walked close enough that her shoulder brushed his arm with every other step. She hadn't let more than an arm's length come between them since the gate below, and Theo found he was grateful for it in a way that embarrassed him.

"Don't sign anything," she murmured for the fourth time since they'd entered the city proper.

"I'm not going to sign anything."

"You said that about the toll-shrine too, and then you gave away your mother's face."

"That was different. The warden had a knife."

"The warden was a *stick*," Wren said. "A stick with opinions. And you handed over a memory like it was a round of drinks." Her voice stayed light, but her hand had found his sleeve again, gripping it through the fabric. "Whatever's at the end of this street, whatever it looks like, whatever it *says*—you look at me first. Promise me."

He looked down at her. Candlelight from a thousand windows turned her hair copper and her expression into something fierce and frightened and trying very hard to be neither.

"Wren Ashvale," Theo said, "if I make any bargain tonight, I promise you'll be the first person I cheat."

She snorted despite herself. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me, and I hate it."

* * *

They were brought at last to a building that could only be called a palace if palaces were things built by accountants. No banners, no guards, no ornamentation—just clean pale stone rising story after story, every window glowing warm, every door exactly alike. Inside, a hall stretched away longer than any building in Lowmark had a right to contain, and it was lined floor to ceiling with shelves. Ledgers. Tens of thousands of them, bound in leather of every color, some fat as loaves of bread and some thin as hymnals, all of them humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has technically ended.

Theo's sigil woke the moment he crossed the threshold. Not burning—the way it had burned against the soul-hunters—but *warm*, spreading up his wrist like sunlight through wine. His palm knew this place. His palm had worked here.

That thought arrived uninvited and sat down heavily in his chest.

At the far end of the hall stood a desk. A simple desk, oak, worn smooth at the edges by years of hands. Behind it sat a figure reading a ledger by the light of a single white candle, and he did not look up when they approached, because he already knew they were there. Of course he did.

"Master Delacroix." The voice was warm. Genuinely, terribly warm—the voice of a man greeting a guest he'd been hoping would come. "And Mistress Ashvale. Sit, please. Sit. You've had a long road, and I'll not conduct business with people who are standing on their dignity."

Chairs appeared—or perhaps they had always been there. Two of them, angled toward the desk, upholstered in deep green. Wren took hers the way a soldier takes a wall: back straight, weight forward, nothing given away.

Theo sat more slowly, because he was looking at the Broker's face.

He had expected a monster. He'd spent the whole climb down composing one—horns, maybe, or too many teeth, or the hollow-eyed blankness of the hunters. What he got instead was a middle-aged man with laugh lines and ink stains on his fingers, greying at the temples, wearing the kind of plain dark coat a schoolmaster might favor.

It was not quite Theo's face. But it was near enough that his own reflection, glimpsed in still water over ten years, had always seemed slightly wrong afterward, and now he knew why.

"Ah," the Broker said gently, setting down his ledger. "You've noticed."

"You have my face," Theo said. His voice came out steadier than he felt.

"I have the face you gave me." The Broker folded his ink-stained hands. "Or rather—you left it here, along with everything else, and what remained above ground was the shape of you without the substance. I kept the rest safe. I keep *everything* safe, Master Delacroix. That is my entire profession. Ask anyone." He smiled. "Ask almost anyone."

"What happened to the ones you can't ask?"

The Broker's smile widened by a degree that contained no humor whatsoever. "Now that is an excellent question, and we will come to it. First—" he turned the ledger around on the desk and slid it across the wood, "—your account."

Theo looked down. The page was written in a fine, even hand that made his stomach turn, because he recognized it. He'd seen it that morning in the chapel ledger in the forgetting village, and he'd seen it last night in the contract room, and he'd told himself both times that familiarity meant nothing.

It was his handwriting. Or it had been, once.

**DELACROIX, THEO. Principal sum: one (1) soul entire, self-surrendered. Interest accrued, ten years: compounding. Status: OVERDUE.**

Below that, in fresh ink still faintly wet:

**Balance due at present: one (1) soul entire, PLUS interest as assessed.**

"And what does interest come to?" Theo asked quietly. "In souls?"

"Oh, Master Delacroix." The Broker leaned back, and behind him the thousand humming ledgers seemed to lean with him. "You misunderstand the arrangement entirely. Interest is never paid *in* souls. Souls are principal. Interest is paid in—" he tapped the page, "—consequences. Time. Attention. Care. Every year you lived above ground as a free man, someone below carried a debt you shouldered for you. Every year your wards held, my court grew a little hungrier, a little smaller, a little more *creative*. Do you know what happens to a court that runs out of memory? It forgets why it exists. It begins eating itself." He said it lightly, the way a man describes weather. "I have lost three clerks that way this decade alone. Good clerks. Meticulous."

"So the stars going out—"

"Was arithmetic," the Broker said simply. "Nothing personal. The ledger demanded rebalancing, and the sky is merely the largest visible column." He laced his fingers. "But. But. I am not a cruel man, Master Delacroix. Cruelty is inefficient. What I am is *flexible*, and I have a proposal that I believe will interest you greatly—one might say it is the proposal of your lifetime, though technically it is the proposal of your *previous* lifetime."

Theo's heart was beating hard enough that he could hear it. "Say it."

The Broker reached beneath the desk and drew out a small box. Black lacquer, silver hinges, no larger than a book. When he set it on the oak and opened the lid, light spilled out—not candlelight, something older and cleaner—and Theo's breath stopped.

Inside lay memories. He couldn't have said how he knew, but he knew the way he knew his own name: a shimmering heap of moments, ten years' worth and more besides, folded like cloth. And threaded through them—

His mother, laughing, flour on her hands.

Rain on a roof he'd slept under for twenty years before Thornfell.

A name. His real name, the one before *Delacroix*, spoken by someone who loved him.

The word branded into his palm on the night the stars died—he suddenly understood he would be able to read it again.

"Everything," the Broker said softly. "Every moment you surrendered. Your childhood. Your trade. Your crimes—" a fractional pause, weighted like a coin on a scale, "—and your reasons for them. All of it, restored, whole, *yours*. You walk out of my mountain tomorrow morning a complete man. In exchange—"

He turned his head, unhurried, courteous, and looked directly at Wren for the first time since they'd entered the hall.

"—Mistress Ashvale settles the interest."

The warmth in the room did not change. The candle did not flicker. That was the horror of it—that the world went on being gentle while the sentence landed.

"Her family owes this court three generations of passage-tolls," the Broker continued, conversational, as though discussing grain prices. "Her grandfather hauled for us. Her mother signed. The debts are modest but *old*, and old debts gather interest the way old cheese gathers flavor. Her soul, freely tendered, clears your balance and the Ashvale account both. Everyone walks away solvent. Well." Another smile. "*Almost* everyone."

"No," Theo said.

"You haven't heard the terms fully—"

"*No.*"

The word came out of him with the force of the flare that had driven off the hunters, and for one instant the sigil lit his whole arm, throwing his shadow huge and jagged against the shelves. Several ledgers on the nearest shelf shivered in their places. Somewhere far down the hall, a clerk paused mid-step.

The Broker watched the light with an expression of frank, scholarly appreciation. Then he closed the lid of the box—not all the way, just enough to dim it—and folded his hands again, entirely unbothered.

"There he is," he said warmly. "There's the man who stole my ledger."

The silence that followed had weight. Wren had gone very still beside him; Theo could feel her staring at the side of his head.

"Theo," she said carefully. "*What* ledger?"

"A figure of speech," the Broker said smoothly, before Theo could open his mouth. "An old joke between colleagues." He rose, buttoned his coat, and came around the desk—unhurried, hands visible, the picture of harmlessness. Up close, the resemblance was worse. There was a small scar at his hairline that Theo knew, with sickening certainty, was mirrored under his own hair, from a fall he had no memory of taking.

"Let me tell you what I admire about you, Master Delacroix," the Broker said. "Ten years ago you sat where you are sitting now, and you made the coldest bargain ever struck in this hall, and you did it without flinching. Tonight you refuse to spend another man's soul to buy back your own—which, I note for the record, is precisely the same moral position you held the *first* time, applied in mirror image. Consistency. It's rare. It's *bankable*." He sighed, almost fondly. "No, keep your refusal. I confess I hoped for it."

"Hoped?" Theo's voice cracked on it. "You dangle my whole life in front of me and you *hoped* I'd say no?"

"I hoped you'd say no *quickly*, so we could proceed to the actual business." The Broker gestured, and a second ledger floated down from the shelves, drifting through the candlelight to land open on the desk between them. This one was old. Its binding had been mended many times. On its spine, stamped in tarnished silver: **MASTER LEDGER — SOULS OUTSTANDING.**

"Because here is the truth of your situation, and I will give it to you plainly, since plainly is what you've earned." The Broker's voice dropped, losing its warmth for the first time, becoming simply tired. "Your theft emptied this book's central column. My court is owed a soul of equal weight to the one you took, and the sky will stay black until the books balance. That is not cruelty. That is not spite. That is *bookkeeping*, and bookkeeping does not care whether you like it. Someone balances this ledger, or everything above and below goes dark together—every star, every ward, every village that has forgotten its own name until it forgets how to breathe."

He looked at Theo with those ink-stained hands resting flat on the ancient binding, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like a monster than like a man at the end of a very long shift.

"So take your memories, refuse your debts, storm out, do whatever makes you feel righteous," the Broker said. "The offer stands until dawn. But understand: I will get my balance. One way or another, the mathematics always win." He smiled again, and this time there was something almost kind in it, which was the cruelest thing yet. "You taught me that yourself. Though you don't remember teaching me. Which, I grant, is rather the point."

* * *

The clerks escorted them to a guest chamber—white walls, two beds, a window overlooking the candle city, and a door that locked itself behind them with a sound like a page turning.

For a long while neither of them spoke. Wren sat on the edge of her bed, boots still on, turning her belt-knife over and over in her hands. Theo stood at the window, watching the lights of the deep court burn below, and pressed his thumb against the sigil in his palm. It pulsed back, slow and steady. Patient.

"He wasn't lying," Wren said finally. "About the balance. I've heard the same arithmetic from shrine-wardens my whole life. Debts like this don't forgive. They just wait."

"Then someone pays."

"Yes. And it is *not* going to be me, and it is *not* going to be you walking out of here with a box full of you and leaving me hollowed out, so don't even start composing the speech, I can see you composing the speech—"

"I'm not composing a speech."

"You're absolutely composing a speech. You get a line between your eyebrows. It's very heroic. It's also very annoying."

Despite everything—a dead sky overhead, a stolen face downstairs, a box of his own life sitting on a broker's desk—Theo laughed. It came out ragged, but it was real, and Wren's mouth twitched in answer before she mastered herself.

"Theo." She set the knife down and looked at him squarely. "Whatever you're planning—and you're planning something, you've been planning something since he said *stole my ledger*—whatever it is, it better include me."

"It will."

"Promise."

"On my mother's face," Theo said, and immediately winced. "That was—I'm so sorry, that was the wrong—"

But Wren was laughing now, quiet and hopeless, shaking her head at him in the candlelight. "Gods help me," she said. "I'm starting to like you again. That's how I know we're both doomed."

Down in the city, the candles burned on, ten thousand small flames keeping a darkness at bay that no one below remembered starting. And in the master ledger, somewhere on a desk in a hall of shelves, a line waited in wet ink for a name.

Theo turned from the window.

"Dawn's in six hours," he said. "Tell me everything you know about where they keep the contracts."
