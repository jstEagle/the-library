## 15. What Theo Bought

The ledger room smelled of tallow and old paper, the way a church smells of incense—except this was a church where the prayers had been filed alphabetically.

Theo stood in the aisle between shelves that rose into darkness overhead, each one packed with ledgers bound in leather gone soft with handling. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Somewhere above, muffled by floors of stone, he could hear the court going about its evening: the dry rustle of clerks' quills, the distant clink of candle-snuffers, a voice raised in what sounded suspiciously like an argument over interest rates. Wren was up there somewhere, doing whatever it was she'd said she would do to keep the clerks busy. He'd chosen not to ask for details. Some things were kinder unknown.

His palm throbbed. The sigil had been burning steadily since they'd passed the last shrine, a low coal-heat that rose whenever he drew near anything that belonged to the deep court. And everything here belonged to the deep court.

*Find your own name,* Wren had told him before they parted. *Ledgers are organized by debt. You want the shelf where they keep the people who owe themselves.*

He'd found the section easily enough. It was marked with a small brass plaque, polished by centuries of fingers: **SELF-INSTRUMENTED CONTRACTS**. The word *instrumented* sat wrong in his mouth. As though a man were a violin. As though a soul were something you could tune.

He ran his finger along the spines, reading dates backward. Ten years ago. Nine. Eleven—

There.

The ledger was thinner than the others, its cover stamped not with a debtor's name but with a sigil he knew intimately, because it lived on his hand: a gate, crossed by a single burning line.

His heart was hammering now. He carried the book to the reading desk at the end of the aisle—a slab of black wood scarred with candle-wax—and set it down beneath the little lamp that burned without flame, giving light without warmth, the way everything in this place gave without giving.

He opened it.

The first page was a contract, written in two hands. The body of the agreement in a fine, patient script that made Theo's skin crawl with familiarity—he knew that handwriting, though he couldn't say from where; it felt like recognizing a face in a crowd and realizing you'd been dreaming about it for years. The signature at the bottom, though, was jagged, hurried, pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper:

***Theo Delacroix.***

Below it, in different ink entirely, dark red-brown, dried to the color of rust: a second line. Not a name. Just three words, small and deliberate:

***Paid in full.***

Theo stared at it. His own blood. He knew it the way you know your own reflection—the shape of the letters, the way the *x* slanted left. He had signed this. Ten years ago, or ten years from now, in some moment he had paid to forget, he had sat at a desk very like this one and bled onto paper.

He turned the page.

---

**CONTRACT OF EXCHANGE**

Let it be entered into the record of the Deep Court, in the reign of the Broker, Keeper of Accounts, that the party hereafter called *the Vendor*, Theo Delacroix, late clerk of the third desk, does sell unto the Court the whole of his memory, being every recollection of his life prior to this signing, including but not limited to: his parentage, his childhood, his name's origin, his service, his crimes, and his reasons.

In exchange, the Vendor receives:

One (1) warding of the Thornfell gate, to be maintained by the Vendor's own sigil, which shall ache when touched and burn when broken;

One (1) erasure, complete and irreversible, of all debts standing against the Vendor in the master ledger;

One (1) new life, ten years' term, renewable at the Court's discretion;

And silence.

*Silence,* Theo read again. The contract had purchased him silence. About what?

He turned another page, and the fine patient hand continued, in the manner of contracts everywhere, itemizing exactly what was being bought.

---

**SCHEDULE OF GOODS SURRENDERED**

Item the first: One (1) master ledger of souls, stolen from the counting-house of the Deep Court on the night of the Long Audit, value beyond reckoning.

Item the second: One (1) sealing of the Thornfell gate, effected by means of the aforementioned ledger, whose pages were burned in the gatefire to close the way between the world above and the world below.

Item the third: One (1) confession, herewith recorded verbatim, that the Court may know its loss.

And then the confession. In his own handwriting. In his own words.

Theo's hands were shaking so badly he had to press them flat against the desk to make them stop. He read.

---

*I, Theo Delacroix, clerk of the third desk, do confess as follows.*

*I served the Broker for nine years. I kept the accounts of the forgotten—the ones who traded their names for comfort, their griefs for peace, their years for a little more time. I told myself it was honest work. Debts must be kept somewhere. Someone must hold the pen.*

*But I read the ledger too closely. That is my crime. A clerk who reads too closely starts to notice things.*

*I noticed that we never collected equally. That the poor sold memories because they had nothing else to sell, and the rich bought them back when they grew bored. That grief, once purchased, did not leave the vault—it aged there, like wine, and the Broker drank it. I noticed that the stars above the mountain are not stars at all but the sum of every soul we held, and that the Broker counted them nightly, and smiled.*

*I noticed that hope was our most profitable commodity. We sold it back to people at interest.*

*So I stole the master ledger. All of it. Every soul the court held, every name, every grief in the vault—I carried it out through the Thornfell gate in a smuggler's pack, and I burned it in the gatefire, and the fire sealed the gate behind me.*

*The Broker cannot pursue what is destroyed. The dead souls are free. The stars went out because the count is wrong now—that is the price, and I pay it gladly, and the borderlands will be dark for a while, and I am sorry for that, truly, but better dark than farmed.*

*What remains is myself. The court is owed a soul of equal weight to what was lost, and mine is the only one I have authority to spend. So I sell my memory—not to free myself, but to seal the seal. A ward needs a keeper who has no reason to open the gate. Give me a life without the past, and I will keep the door shut and never know why.*

*This is not heroism. Do not record it as such. I was a thief for nine years before I was a thief for one night. I simply stole from the thief, once, at the end, and I have no intention of pretending that balances anything.*

*It doesn't balance. Nothing balances. But the gate holds, and the forgotten go free, and if someday the wards fail and someone comes down here reading this—*

*—then I am sorry. And I hope you hate me less than I hate myself. Though honestly, you won't know enough to.*

***T.D.***

---

Theo closed the ledger with great care, the way you close a door on a room where someone has died.

Then he stood very still in the aisle of shelved souls while the lamp burned cold beside him and his own heartbeat filled his ears like surf.

*Clerk of the third desk.* He had worked *here*. Those patient rows of shelves—he might have dusted them. The fine, crawling script of the contract's body—he had probably written a hundred contracts just like it, in that same hand, selling strangers out of their own pasts. He tried to remember and got nothing, only the words themselves, sitting in him like stones dropped into a well that had no bottom.

He hadn't been a victim. He hadn't even been an ordinary man caught in a bad bargain. He had been *staff*. The Broker's own pen. Nine years of holding the ledger open while people wept across the desk from him, and he had written the entries, and taken his wage, and gone home to whatever home a creature like that keeps.

And then—one night of conscience. One theft. One fire. As though a decade of complicity could be balanced by a single act of arson, as though burning the ledger washed the ink off his own hands.

*Better dark than farmed.* He thought of the village under the mountain's shadow, the old woman who had wept over his sigil and called him *the one who sold himself*. He had thought she meant the bargain at the gate. She hadn't. She'd meant all of it. The whole trade. The whole long harvest that he had helped administer, until the day he decided to stop—and even his stopping had cost other people the sky. Travelers wandering memory-blind in the passes. Rings of black in the trees. Ten years of a darkened border, and every soul in Thornfell paying the interest on *his* account.

He thought of Wren.

Her family's routes had been supply lines for the court. Her grandfather had hauled goods for the Broker—had perhaps hauled them *for him*, for the clerk of the third desk, carried the ledgers and the candles and the bottled years up the smugglers' roads. And Wren had grown up on those roads thinking they were just roads. She'd learned the mountain from the inside of a trade she never chose, and now she was bleeding for it, guiding a man she trusted toward the truth that he was the reason her family owed anything at all.

When she found out—and she would find out, because Wren missed nothing and because he would tell her himself, he owed her that much and infinitely more—what then? What did a man say? *I'm sorry I farmed your ancestors' neighbors for hope?*

A sound came from above—footsteps on stone, quick, uneven, followed by a voice calling his name in a sharp whisper that echoed down the stairwell. Wren's voice. And under it, further off, a bell beginning to ring: not the court's tidy accounting bells but something harsher, an alarm, tolling fast.

They had found the empty contract cradle. Or the missing clerk's ledger. Or her.

Theo snatched the ledger off the desk and tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay like a second heart. His palm flared white-hot, and for the first time since the night the stars died, the pain felt earned.

He took the stairs two at a time, into the ringing dark, to find Wren—and to tell her, before anyone else could, exactly what she had been guiding down the mountain.

Not a victim.

A thief of hope, coming home to confess.
