## 16. Wren's Choice

They took her knives first, which Wren considered almost professional courtesy, and then her boots, which she considered an act of war.

"Really," she said to the clerk who was cataloguing her belongings into a little ledger of his own—everything in the deep court went into a ledger, apparently, including bootlaces—"you'll want to write down that one squeaks. Left heel. It's a known defect. I'd hate for it to be resold under false pretenses."

The clerk did not look up. His pen scratched. Somewhere behind him, in the endless candlelit corridors of the court beneath the mountain, something laughed—a thin, papery sound, like pages turning in the wind.

They put her in a cell that was not a cell. That was the thing about this place that kept setting Wren's teeth on edge: nothing was ever simply what it was. The room had a bed, a washbasin, a window looking out on a street where candle-carrying figures drifted past at all hours, and every detail of it was wrong in ways she couldn't name. The bed was comfortable the way a trap is comfortable. The window showed a sky with no stars and no ceiling and no sense of distance, as though someone had painted *night* onto the world and forgotten the rest.

Three days, or what she judged to be three days by the rotation of the candles outside—the deep court kept time in wax, burning down its hours—and then they came for her.

Not clerks this time. A herald, tall and grey-robed, with a face like a coin worn smooth by too many thumbs. He carried a silver tray, and on the tray sat a book.

Wren knew that book before he set it down. She'd grown up with it under her mother's bed. She'd learned her letters tracing its columns by candlelight, learning which routes were family routes, which debts were family debts, which names in which villages owed the Ashvales a favor and which ones the Ashvales owed something darker.

The Ashvale ledger. Her grandfather's hand in the first third, her mother's in the second, her own cramped entries beginning ten years back, when she'd inherited the routes and the debts together, the way you inherit a house with a leaky roof and something living in the attic.

"You recognize it," the herald said. It wasn't a question.

"I recognize theft," Wren said. "You took it off me at the gate along with my spare socks."

"The court does not steal." The herald folded his hands. "The court *collects*. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. May I sit?"

"There's one chair."

"I know." He sat in it anyway, smooth as poured tallow, and turned the ledger so it faced her. "Wren Ashvale. Daughter of Maren Ashvale. Granddaughter of Odell Ashvale, who walked these roads before you were born and whose account, I regret to inform you, remains open."

"Grandfather's been dead eleven years."

"Death settles property," the herald said gently. "It does not settle debt."

* * *

He talked for a long while. That was the trick of the place, Wren decided—everything here talked, patiently, courteously, until you forgot you were the one being talked *at*. He laid out the family accounts like a merchant laying out cloth: the original arrangement, three generations back, when the Ashvale smuggling routes had been something else entirely. Supply lines. Deliveries running down into the mountain instead of up over the passes. And what the Ashvales had carried down, all those years, in boxes lined with wool and sealed with wax—

"Memories," Wren said flatly. "Souls. I know. I read chapter six."

Something flickered across the herald's smooth face—surprise, or its imitation. "You knew."

"I've known since the village that forgot itself. My grandfather helped deliver whatever Theo traded, didn't he? That's why your roads and mine run the same. We built them together, your court and my family." She leaned back against the wall that wasn't quite a wall. "So get to it. You didn't bring me my family's dirty laundry just to watch me blush."

The herald smiled. It was a good smile. It was designed to be a good smile, the way a fishhook is designed to be shiny.

"Theo Delacroix," he said, "stole from this court. He stole the master ledger itself—the record of every soul we hold, every year we've banked, every balance struck since before your grandmother's grandmother. Without it, our accounts cannot close. Our debts cannot be called. And the surface wards—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the dead stars, "—cannot stand, because the wards were drawn *from* the ledger's power. He has cost us everything, and he has cost *your* family too, Ashvale. While the master ledger sits in his keeping—or in whatever hiding place his stolen memories have buried it—every account is frozen. Including yours."

"Including mine," Wren repeated.

"Your grandfather's debt. Your mother's debt. Your own—smaller, but growing, as all debts do." The herald turned the ledger toward her again, to the last page, where her name sat in her own handwriting above a sum she'd never dared total. "Here is the offer, and it is the only offer this court will ever make you, so attend closely. Theo Delacroix will come for you. He is already coming; his sigil burns like a torch in the dark and half the city can see it. When he comes, you will lead him to the master ledger. You will help us recover what is ours. And in exchange—" he closed the Ashvale ledger with a soft, final sound, like a coffin lid, "—we burn this. Every entry. Three generations of debt, ash. Your family walks free of this mountain forever. No hunter follows you. No toll-warden calls your name. You could go to the coast, Ashvale. Buy a boat. Grow old under stars that shine."

The silence after was very long.

Wren looked at the ledger. Her grandfather's handwriting, looping and confident, the hand of a man who'd thought himself clever. Her mother's, tighter, more careful, a woman who'd begun to understand what cleverness cost. Her own entries at the bottom, and beside one of them—a delivery made eight years ago, a box bound in grey wool—a notation in ink she hadn't written. *Received in full.* In the Broker's hand, probably. She'd been carrying souls to the deep court for years before she'd ever known there was a deep court, and some clerk below had been tallying her innocence against her account like interest.

"He won't tell me where it is," she said. "Even if I asked. He doesn't *know* where it is. That's rather the point of him."

"No," the herald agreed. "But his memories do. And memories can be *read*, Ashvale, once the sigil is opened. All you must do is bring him to the reading room. What happens after is not your concern, and will weigh not at all upon your account."

"And what happens after?"

The herald's smile widened a fraction. "What always happens when a debt is called."

* * *

They left her alone with the ledger after that—which was, she understood perfectly, the whole design. Not torture. Not threats. Just a table, a candle, a book full of her family's sins, and time.

She read it cover to cover, which she'd never done. As a girl she'd used it as a route-book, checking tolls and safehouses. She'd never read the other columns—the payments received, the goods described, the names of the people her family had fed into the mountain's hungry accounts, most of whom had never known what they were carrying until it was too late to carry anything at all, even their own names.

Page after page. Year after year. Her thumb found the margin note in her mother's hand, dated the winter before Wren was born: *Odell says the road down is easier than the road up. Odell is a fool.* Her mother had known. Had kept walking the road anyway, because the alternative was the debt being called, and the debt being called meant hunters, and hunters meant—

Wren closed the book.

She thought about the coast. That was the honest truth, and she made herself sit with it: she thought about the coast, about a boat, about salt air instead of mountain dark, about never again hearing her own name spoken by something with a smooth face and a tray. The offer was real. She could feel its reality the way you feel a floor underfoot. The court wanted its ledger back more than it wanted anything, and for that it would genuinely, truly let her go.

And it would open Theo like a letter.

She thought of him sitting across from her in the Thornfell tavern a lifetime ago, nursing his ale and his invisibility, making dry jokes about ward-inspections while his palm ached under the table. She thought of him in the shrine, pressing his hand flat without hesitation to pay the memory-toll, giving away his mother's face like a man paying a bridge fee. She thought of him standing over her after the hunters came, sigil blazing, roaring at things made of hollow and hunger as if sheer stubbornness might burn them—and how it *had*, because with Theo, stubbornness and fire had always been the same substance.

He doesn't remember who he was. If they open the sigil, he never will. And whatever he was—it'll be theirs.

"Well," Wren said aloud, to the cell, to the candle, to three generations of dead Ashvales. "That decides that."

Because here was the thing she'd figured out somewhere on the black passes, watching trees grow rings of shadow and travelers wander memory-blind: the court ran on ledgers. Every cruelty it committed was an *entry*. Every bargain was balanced to the hair. The Broker couldn't lie—not outright, not ever—because a lie was an unbalanced column, and unbalanced columns burned this place down. Everything the herald had offered her was true, and everything he'd left unsaid was also true, and the space between those truths was exactly wide enough for a smuggler to drive a cart through.

The herald had said: *we burn this.*

He hadn't said who held the match.

* * *

They came for her at what the candles said was morning. Two clerks and the herald, and behind them a pair of hollow riders wearing borrowed faces, because the court trusted no one anymore—its city leaking light, its prisoners escaping, its whole immaculate accounting coming apart at the seams.

"Wren Ashvale," the herald said. "Have you reached your decision?"

"I have." She stood, barefoot on the floor that wasn't a floor, and picked up the Ashvale ledger from the table. "I've given it a great deal of thought. Family's family. Blood's blood. Debt's debt." She weighed the book in her hands. "My grandfather wrote this. My mother wrote this. Half of it's in my hand. It's the truest account of who we are that exists anywhere in the world."

"It is," the herald agreed, and there was something almost tender in his voice, which was how she knew she'd chosen right. "And it can all be forgiven."

"Oh, I know." Wren smiled at him, and it was not a good smile, but it was an honest one. "That's the problem. See, I've been thinking about what forgiveness costs in this place. Everything here runs on exchange. Nothing is forgiven free. So if you burn this book and call us clean, it means somewhere else in the ledger there's a new entry, bigger, waiting. You're not offering to erase our debt. You're offering to *consolidate* it. One family, freed—one soul, collected. Him." She jerked her chin toward the door, toward the corridors beyond, toward the man currently tearing through the candle city with a brand of white fire on his hand and her name in his mouth. "You don't want a guide. You want a Judas with clean books."

The herald's face did not change. But the candle flames between them all leaned slightly, the way flames lean when something cold passes through a room.

"The terms were plain," he said.

"The terms were *plain*," Wren agreed. "Plain as a straight road. And I'm a smuggler, friend. Straight roads are wasted on me." She raised the ledger high, and the nearest clerk actually flinched forward, hands rising—because they'd shown her, hadn't they, over three days of careful courtesy, exactly what this book meant to them, exactly how much of the court's grip on her family lived between its covers. "One last piece of professional advice, gratis. Next time you want to buy a woman's loyalty? Don't show her the price list first."

"*Stop*—"

"Family's family," Wren said. "So I'm settling *all* of it."

She hurled the ledger into the hearth.

Now—here was a thing about the deep court, the thing Theo had told her on the black passes, gasping and half-frozen, when she'd asked why a city of such power needed smugglers' roads at all: *it can't make anything. It can only trade. Even its fire is bought.* The hearth in her cell burned with purchased flame, flame traded up from some hearth in the surface world, and purchased things obeyed old laws whether the court liked it or not. And the oldest law of any hearth, in any world, was this:

Whatever is given to the fire belongs to the fire.

The ledger hit the coals and the flames rose up *glad*. Wren saw it—actually saw it—the fire reach up like hands, eager, hungry, *entitled*, and take the book entire. Three generations of Ashvale debt caught light at once, every entry, every name, every soul her family had delivered wrapped in grey wool, and the fire took them all, because the fire had been *given* them, and no ledger in the mountain could call that back.

The clerks screamed. The herald lunged. The hollow riders moved with terrible speed—but the room was filling with smoke, and the smoke was full of burning names, and names, freed, are the most disorderly substance in creation. They swirled up in the draft like sparks, like birds, like voices remembering themselves, and the riders stopped dead, heads turning, tracking sounds only they could hear—the faces they wore straining toward the voices of the people they'd been taken from.

In the chaos, nobody was watching the prisoner's feet.

Wren had spent three days learning her cage, the way she'd learned every lock on every border road since she was nine years old. The wall that wasn't a wall was a wall on the side facing the corridor, and walls exist to be load-bearing, and load-bearing means seams, and seams mean give. The panel behind the washbasin had shifted a quarter-inch on the second day, when the whole city had shuddered—she'd felt it, filed it away. Now she threw her shoulder into it, once, twice, and on the third blow it gave with a crack like a knuckle popping, and there was darkness beyond, and service tunnels, because every city ever built has service tunnels and the proud ones forget them fastest.

Behind her, the herald stood in the doorway with firelight painting his smooth face orange, and for the first time since she'd met him, he looked *old*.

"You understand what you've done," he said quietly. "The Ashvale accounts are destroyed. The court can never collect from your family again. And it can never forgive you either. There is no entry left in which your pardon could be written."

"Yeah," Wren said, backing into the dark. "I counted the columns. That was the point."

She pulled the panel shut behind her and ran.

* * *

The service tunnels smelled of dust and old wax, and they went everywhere, because servants carry cities on their backs and are remembered by no one—something, she thought wildly, Theo would appreciate. She ran by feel and memory, counting turns the way her grandfather had taught her, left-left-right-long-gallery-stairs-down, except reversed, stairs-*up*, because somewhere above her and several streets over, a sigil was burning like a second sun and the whole court was convulsing around it.

She heard him before she saw him. Of course she did. An alarm like shattering glass rolling through the upper corridors, shouts in voices that had forgotten how shouting worked, and beneath it all a low roar of flame that had nothing to do with candles.

She came up through a grate into a clerk's hall turned battlefield—desks overturned, ledgers scattered, paper snowing down through candlelight—and there he was, at the far end, backed against a shattered window with no sky behind it, ringed by clerks and riders and things she didn't have names for, one arm thrown up, his palm ablaze with white fire that made the shadows scream and shrink.

Theo Delacroix, invisible keeper of the Thornfell gate, was visible from one end of the mountain to the other.

"Wren!" His voice cracked with relief and fury in equal measure. "You were supposed to stay *put*!"

"You were supposed to have a *plan*!" She vaulted a fallen desk. "Where is it?"

"The ledger room! I think—I found the contract, I found out what I—" He staggered as a rider pressed closer, its borrowed face slack and longing. "There's no time, Wren, they're coming from everywhere—"

"So we go everywhere faster." She skidded to a stop beside him, grabbed his burning wrist—his fire didn't burn her, she'd known it wouldn't, some part of her had known since the shrine—and turned to face the ring of the court together, back to back, her knife-less hands raised and his fist full of daylight. "Listen up!" she shouted at the assembled ranks of the dead and the damned and the diligently employed. "The Ashvale ledger just burned. Every debt my family owed this court is *ash*. Which means, per your own precious rules—" she jabbed a finger at the herald, now pushing through the crowd, his composure gone, "—there is no longer any account in which my betrayal could be recorded. I'm not your agent. I'm not your debtor. I am, legally speaking, *nobody's problem*."

A ripple went through the clerks. Ledgers rustled. Somewhere, a pen scratched frantically and stopped.

"And him?" the herald demanded.

Wren grinned, wild and bright, and squeezed Theo's blazing hand. "Him? Him you'll have to take up with the management. Come on, keeper—we've got a ledger to steal and a star-sky to relight, and I've just become the freest woman under the mountain, so try to keep up."

She hauled him toward the corridor, into the dark, into the smoke and the screaming and the long climb toward the gate below—and behind them, gathering itself out of the deepest dark of the deep court, something began to laugh.
