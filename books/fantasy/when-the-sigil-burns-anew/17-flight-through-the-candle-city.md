## 17. Flight Through the Candle City

The fire alarm—if a court of goblins and stolen souls could be said to have such a thing—was a bell made of bone, and it sounded like the world's largest knuckle rapping the world's most impatient door.

Theo ran.

Behind him, Wren vaulted a ledger-cart, boots skidding on marble polished by ten thousand years of clerks' feet, and came up beside him with her knife already out and a grin on her face that he found, under the circumstances, deeply reassuring and mildly alarming.

"So," she panted, "just to confirm—when you said 'distract the clerks,' I did that, yes? Because I'm fairly sure I *over*-did that."

"You set three of them on fire."

"Two on fire. One I just pushed into his own inkwell. He'll be embarrassed, not dead." She ducked a thrown ledger—it whistled past her ear like a fat paper goose—and sprinted on. "Where's the gate?"

"Up. Always up."

The deep court was a city built downward, which Theo had thought impossible until he'd seen it: streets spiraling into dark, buildings stacked beneath buildings, every window lit by candles that burned without heat and never guttered. The candles were the worst part. He'd understood in the Broker's parlor what they were—each flame a stolen year, each wick someone's forgotten summer—and now, running through the great central avenue, he passed thousands upon thousands of them, a city illuminated entirely by grief.

His palm burned.

It had been burning since he'd torn his contract from its shelf, but here in the open it flared brighter with every step, light leaking between his fingers like he'd cupped a coal and forgotten to let go. Wren kept glancing at it.

"It's getting worse," she said.

"It's getting *better*. That's the problem."

* * *

They had come down into the deep court through the smugglers' ways—Wren's roads, narrow and secret, the ones her grandfather had mapped in a hand that shook with either palsy or guilt. Those roads had delivered them to the ledger room's back door, and for one glorious hour it had seemed possible they might simply steal a soul and leave.

Then Theo had read his own contract aloud. Then the master ledger, missing these ten years, had turned out to be sewn into his own skin. Then the bell had rung.

Now the avenues were filling.

Not soldiers—the court had clerks enough, and some of those could fight, but what poured into the streets now was something else. They came out of doorways and alleys and drains, out of places that had no business holding people: thin figures in clothes from a dozen different generations, faces slack as unwritten pages. The forgotten. Every soul the court had ever bought and shelved, called up from wherever the court kept them, and pointed toward the intruders like a finger.

"The Broker," Theo breathed, "has emptied his pantry."

"He's unleashing everyone." Wren had stopped smiling. That frightened Theo more than anything else so far. "Theo, there must be thousands."

"Then we'd better be fast."

They cut off the main avenue into a lane of candle-shops—a whole district given over to the trade, shelves of little flames behind glass, each labeled in clerk's script. *A girl's tenth birthday. A fisherman's last good haul. Forty years of a marriage nobody remembers anymore.* The forgotten souls flowed into the lane behind them, and ahead, more spilled from a side street, and Theo felt the trap close like a hand.

Wren put her back to his. "Options?"

He looked at his hand. The sigil blazed through his glove now—he'd lost the glove somewhere, or burned it, he wasn't sure—and the light fell across the nearest of the forgotten, an old man in a farmer's coat, and the old man *stopped*.

Not slowed. Stopped. His blank face tilted, following the light like a moth. And his mouth moved around a word that had no sound, but Theo felt it anyway, felt it in his teeth:

*Light.*

The other souls were stopping too. Dozens of them, then hundreds, crowding into the lane, all staring at his hand with expressions that were not quite hunger and not quite hope but some terrible mixture of both. Moths. All of them moths, and he was the flame.

"Oh no," Theo said quietly.

"What? What is it?"

"They're not hunting me." His voice came out strange. "Wren, they're *following* me."

* * *

They went up through the candle district because the crowd left them no choice—the souls pressed in from every direction except the direction of his hand, parting before his light like water before a prow, and so they climbed, stair after stair, through streets that grew narrower and stranger the higher they went.

Wren walked half-turned, knife out, watching the sea of faces trail them. "This is the strangest escort I've ever had, and I once guided a funeral procession through the Thornfell passes in fog."

"They want it." Theo flexed his fingers; the light pulsed. "The light. It's—I think it's *them*, Wren. Part of it. Years and memories and names, all mixed together in me, and they can tell. It's like—" he groped for it— "like walking past a bakery when you haven't eaten in a week. Except it's their own bread."

"Charming image."

"I'm being chased by an entire city of the dead-ish. My imagery standards have slipped."

That got a huff out of her—almost a laugh, quickly strangled—and he clung to it. Ten minutes ago she had watched him read his contract and learn what he'd been, and her face hadn't changed at all, and he loved her for it and didn't know what to do about loving her for it.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. The contract had told him. *Clerk, third rank, the Ledger Room, servant of the Broker.* He had spent ten years down here. He had written entries in ledgers like the one that held Wren's family debt. He had probably, at some point, recorded the exact transaction that bound the Ashvales to this place, in his own neat hand, and gone to lunch afterward.

And Wren knew it now, and she was still here, climbing stairs beside him, killing candlelight-goblins' lamps when they flared too near, calling him *Theo* like the name still fit.

"Left," she snapped, and yanked him sideways as two clerks with long silver pins—soul-pins, he realized, and his stomach turned—lunged from a doorway. Wren's elbow took one in the throat; the other grabbed Theo's wrist, and where its fingers touched skin near the sigil, the flesh sizzled and the clerk shrieked and let go, shaking its hand as though it had seized a stove.

"Handy," Wren gasped.

"It has its moments."

* * *

They broke out onto a terrace high above the city, and Theo made the mistake of looking back.

The candle city spread below like a galaxy seen from inside. Terraces and spires descending into dark, and everywhere the light of stolen years—except now the light was moving. The streets were rivers of pale figures, thousands strong, all flowing in one direction. Toward them. Toward the terrace. Toward the beacon in his hand.

And among them, moving fast, things that were not the forgotten at all: the hollow riders, mounted on shapes that had maybe been horses once, riding *through* crowds of souls without disturbing them, like sharks through minnows. Hunting the hunters. Hunting him.

"How far to the gate?" Wren asked.

"Through the upper courts. There's a processional way—my contract mentioned it. It leads straight up to the surface seal." He swallowed. "Half a night's climb. More, if we're fighting the whole way."

"Less, if we're not." She was watching the crowd below with narrowed eyes. "Theo. Your moth friends. How attached are you to keeping them exactly where they are?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean you're carrying a torch and half this city is cold and starving for it, and there are two of us and several thousand of them. If we can't outrun the riders, maybe we don't have to. Maybe we just—" she waved her knife vaguely at the seething streets— "point."

He understood, and the understanding sat in his chest like a stone. "You want me to lead them."

"I want you to *let* them follow. There's a difference, and it might matter later." Her voice softened. "I know what it looks like. But Theo—what else do they have? Down there, they're shelves. Candles in jars. Following your hand is the first thing any of them have chosen in years. Maybe ever."

He looked down at the old man in the farmer's coat, who had climbed farther than any of the others and stood at the base of the terrace stairs, face upturned, patient as weather. Waiting for the light to move.

"All right," Theo said. "But if this goes wrong, I want it noted that the plan involved the phrase 'point at the zombies.'"

"Noted. Now *run*."

* * *

The processional way was a grand stair cut into the living rock of the mountain's underside, wide enough for twenty abreast, lined with candles in niches—ten thousand years of tolls and trades glowing on either side like the aisle of some terrible wedding. The souls followed them onto it in numbers that made the stone hum, a slow pale tide climbing behind the beacon of Theo's hand, and for a while—for a beautiful, impossible while—it worked.

The hollow riders hit the tide and bogged down. Souls pressed around the horses-that-were-not-horses, not attacking, just *crowding*, drawn to Theo's light and indifferent to everything else, until the riders were swimming rather than charging, blades flashing, making no headway. One rider screamed—not a sound from its borrowed mouth but something that came out of the dark beneath, vast and furious—and Theo heard the Broker's voice in it, unmistakable, smooth even in rage:

*Mine. All of them are mine.*

"Come and collect," Theo muttered, and climbed faster.

It was somewhere past the ten-thousandth stair that he began talking to them.

He didn't decide to. It happened the way breathing happens. A woman near the front of the tide wore a shawl with embroidered roses, worn nearly to thread, and Theo found himself saying, "Roses. Somebody loved those once. Somebody planted them, probably argued about them. Whoever she was, she was loved, whoever forgot her." And the woman's blank face turned toward him, and her step steadied, and he kept going.

"That coat's good wool. Someone sheared the sheep. Someone carded it, spun it, cursed the loom—that's a whole village in a coat, friend."

"Those bells in the distance—can't be. Bells mean morning. There's no morning down here. Unless we're about to make one."

Wren, climbing beside him, gave him a look. "Are you *sermonizing* at the dead?"

"I'm—no. I'm just noticing things."

"You're leading them. You know that, yes? You're giving a sermon to several thousand souls on a staircase, and they're *listening*, and I would very much like it noted for posterity that I met you when you were hiding in a border tavern pretending to be boring."

"You met me cheating at dice."

"I met you *losing* at dice, which is a con all its own and I respected it enormously." She caught his arm, pulled him behind a niche as a shape wheeled overhead—one of the riders, having abandoned the crowded stair for the air itself, gliding on wings that were made of unlit candle-dark. It passed. It circled. It would be back. "Theo. The pace is wrong. We're slowing them down and they're slowing us down, and the riders only have to get lucky once."

"Then what—"

She was looking at the tide of souls, and then at his hand, and then at him, and he saw the idea arrive in her face and hated it instantly.

"No," he said.

"You could give it to them."

"*No.*"

"A little of it. The light's not yours—you stole it back, fair and square, but you don't need all of it to walk upstairs. And they're starving, Theo. Look at them." Her voice cracked, just slightly. "You've got a whole sun in your fist and you're hoarding it."

"And if I open my fist?" His own voice was shaking now. "Wren, I don't know what's in here. I don't know how it works. If I start handing out pieces of a soul I barely understand, I could snuff it out entirely. I could snuff *them* out. I could—" he stopped, because the rest was obvious: *I could end up empty again, and this time there'd be nothing left to bargain with.*

Wren held his eyes. Below them the tide waited, patient, dim, trusting a light it couldn't name.

"Then don't open your fist," she said quietly. "Just stop closing it so hard."

* * *

He did it on the twenty-thousandth stair, or thereabouts—he'd stopped counting.

He stopped clenching. That was all. He let the sigil breathe, let the light spill instead of blaze, and it fell over the front ranks of the forgotten like warm water, and he braced for hunger, for riot, for the stampede of the starving.

Instead, they *brightened*.

It started with the woman in the rose shawl. A faint glow kindled in her chest, small as a candle seen across a field, and her face—her blank, unwritten face—remembered, for one instant, how to smile. Then the old man in the farmer's coat. Then another, and another, the light passing through the crowd like laughter through a room, soul to soul, each one catching it and passing it on without diminishing it, and the whole grand stair filled with soft radiance, ten thousand small flames answering the one in his hand.

And the hollow rider diving out of the dark above them flew straight into that light and came apart like smoke in a gale.

Theo stood on the stairs with his hand open and his heart breaking, because he understood at last, completely, what the Broker had never understood: light divided isn't lessened. It never was. The whole rotten court was built on scarcity—souls hoarded, years banked, memory traded like coin—and here were the robbed and shelved remains of ten thousand lives, sharing a single spark, and growing brighter for it.

"Well," Wren said thickly. "That's going to make the paperwork awkward."

Theo laughed—a wild, cracked sound—and turned to climb.

And stopped.

At the top of the processional way, where the stair ended at the gate's threshold and the first thin gray hint of the true sky showed beyond, a figure sat waiting on a plain wooden chair, exactly as if he'd been there for hours. He wore clerk's robes. He had Theo's forgotten face. And he was applauding slowly, softly, each clap echoing down the ten thousand stairs like a dropping stone.

"Beautiful," the Broker said. "Genuinely. I shall enter it in the ledger." He rose, and behind him, out of the dark on either side of the gate, the hollow riders gathered like a tide coming in. "Though I'm afraid, Master Delacroix—or should I say *Clerk*—that you've led your congregation somewhere very unfortunate indeed. Because you see, you've finally brought the light to the door."

His smile was Theo's smile, and it was terrible.

"And now we're going to discuss what it weighs."
