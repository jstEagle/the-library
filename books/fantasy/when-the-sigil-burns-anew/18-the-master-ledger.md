## 18. The Master Ledger

They ran out of candlelight a hundred paces from the gate.

Theo felt it before he saw it—the way the deep court's glow simply stopped, as though someone had drawn a line across the world and forbidden warmth beyond it. Beyond that line stood the true gate of Thornfell: not the modest arch of black stone he had tended for ten years on the surface, but its ancestor, its original, vast as a cathedral door and carved with sigils so old they had worn themselves smooth, like words repeated until only their shapes remained.

It was open.

Not broken—open. Swung wide and patient, the way a door is left open for an expected guest.

"Is it supposed to be like that?" Wren whispered. She was walking again, mostly, one hand pressed to the bandage at her side where the hunters' blades had found her in the passes. Her color was bad. Her grin, mercifully, was intact. "Because I've smuggled past a lot of doors, Theo Delacroix, and every single one of them that was open meant somebody wanted me to walk through."

"No," Theo said. His palm burned. It had been burning since the candle city, since the lost souls began drifting after him like moths who'd mistaken him for a lamp. "No, it is very much not supposed to be like that."

Behind them, faint but persistent, came footsteps. Not hunters—not this time. The forgotten ones. The hollow-eyed villagers and nameless dead who had followed him out of the Broker's city, dozens of them now, walking in his wake because his sigil glowed and they were drawn to light the way drowning men are drawn to anything above water.

Wren glanced back at them, then at him. "You've collected quite the congregation."

"I didn't collect them. They just—" He gestured helplessly. "Followed."

"You realize how that sounds. From my end."

"Wren."

"A man walks into hell, lights up like a festival lantern, and acquires a crowd of adoring ghosts, and I'm supposed to pretend that's normal?"

"It's not adoring. It's—" But he didn't have a word for it either, and there wasn't time to find one, because that was when the Broker stepped out of the gate.

* * *

He did not arrive. That was the horror of it, or one of the horrors; there was a competitive field. One moment the gate's threshold held only darkness, and the next the Broker stood upon it, unhurried, hands folded behind his back, wearing—

Wearing Theo's face.

Not exactly. The Broker's version was older, finer, dressed in robes the color of ledger-paper, with eyes like ink wells. But the bones of it were Theo's bones. The set of the jaw, the crooked bridge of the nose. Ten years ago, apparently, Theo had looked at himself in the mirror and seen this man looking back, and had thought nothing of it.

"You're late," the Broker said pleasantly. "Though I suppose lateness is relative, beneath a sky with no stars."

"We're not staying," Theo said. "We're taking what's ours and leaving through your front door, and you can write that down in whatever book you people keep."

"Oh, I intend to." The Broker smiled, and it was Theo's smile, the wry one he used at the border tavern, and seeing it on another face made his stomach turn over. "That is rather the point of our meeting. You see, Mr. Delacroix—or shall I say, former clerk third-rank? No? Very well. You've come for the master ledger. Or for your memories. Or for your soul. The terminology gets slippery down here; forgive us our bookkeeping."

"The ledger," Theo said flatly. "You know why we came."

"Of course I do. I helped arrange for you to come." The Broker spread his hands. "Did you truly believe a smuggler's route would remain open all the way to my gate by accident? That two half-starved fugitives could outrun every hunter I own through roads your own family built, Miss Ashvale, if I wished them closed?"

Wren went very still beside him. Theo felt her hand find his sleeve—not for comfort, he suspected, but to hold him in place.

"You let us come," she said.

"I *needed* you to come." The Broker's voice remained courteous, almost kind, which somehow made it worse. "There is a matter of accounts outstanding. Come inside. It is cold on the threshold, and the explanation requires a table."

"We'll stand," Theo said.

The Broker sighed—the exact sigh Theo gave when travelers argued with him about gate fees—and turned, and led them through.

* * *

Inside the gate was a room, because of course it was. Beneath the mountain, everything was a room eventually. This one was small and round and lit by a single candle of white flame that cast no heat, and at its center stood a lectern, and on the lectern lay a book.

The master ledger of souls.

It was enormous—thick as a fallen log, bound in something dark that might have been leather and might have been regret—and even from across the room Theo could feel it. His sigil flared against his palm, and the book seemed to flare back, a low answering pulse, like two heartbeats finding each other across a distance.

"There it is," Wren breathed. "Ten years of smuggling routes, three sets of burned bridges, one stab wound, and a village full of people who forgot their own names. All for one ugly book."

"The ugliest," the Broker agreed fondly. He crossed to the lectern but did not touch it. "Do you know what this is, truly? Not a record. A *balance*. Every soul that enters the world owes a debt to whatever holds the dark back. Every soul that leaves pays it forward. For ten thousand years, this ledger has balanced itself—birth against death, memory given against memory kept, the great slow tide in and out. My court does not create the debt, Miss Ashvale. We merely keep the books. Someone must."

"And the gate?" Theo asked.

"Is the lock on the books." The Broker touched the lectern, gently, the way one touches a sleeping animal. "While the ledger balances, the gate stays shut, and the things behind the dark stay behind it. While it balances." He paused. "Ten years ago, it stopped balancing."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "My theft."

"*Your* theft." The Broker opened the ledger to a page near the end—a page Theo knew was there, because he had read it once already, in the chapel of the village that forgot itself, and had been sick afterward. His own name. His own blood-signature. *Received: one clerk, entire.* "You took the master ledger's most valuable entry—you—and walked out of my court with your memory as payment and your soul as seal. Do you understand what that did to the arithmetic? There is a weight assigned to every soul, Mr. Delacroix, and yours was considerable. Clever clerks are rare. Honest ones are rarer. A clerk honest enough to rob his own master for love of the world above?" The Broker shook his head, wearing Theo's rueful expression. "I searched ten years for your replacement. There isn't one."

"So balance it," Wren said sharply. "Take the memories back. That's what we came for. Give him his soul, close your book, done. Everyone goes home."

"That," said the Broker, "is where your arithmetic fails you. And mine failed me too, ten years ago, which is the only reason you are standing here instead of ash." He closed the ledger with a sound like a tomb. "A debt cannot be repaid with stolen goods returned. If I simply hand him back his soul, the ledger reads: *one soul removed, one soul restored.* Net change: zero. But the *theft* remains on the books. Ten years of interest has accrued against it. Ten years during which the dark behind the gate has pressed and pressed while the lock sat unbalanced." He looked up, and his ink-well eyes were, for one flickering moment, genuinely tired. "You saw the stars go out. You saw the villages forget. That is not malice. That is arithmetic. The ledger is owed a soul of equal weight to the one taken, and until it is paid, the gate cannot be sealed—only propped shut, badly, by whatever scraps of power my court can scrape together. We are running out of scraps."

The silence that followed was very long.

Theo heard it all settling into place, the way a trap settles, click by click. The open gate. The easy road. The hunters that drove them onward instead of stopping them. The congregation of the forgotten trailing him like ducklings. He had thought he was stealing back into the deep court.

He had been delivered.

"Someone must stay," he said. His voice sounded far away to him. "That's the payment. A soul, equal weight. Someone stays beneath the mountain, on the books, forever, and the ledger balances and the gate seals and the stars come back."

"Now you're doing my job for me," the Broker said warmly. "Yes."

"No." Wren stepped forward, bandage and all, and jabbed a finger at him—at both of them, at the whole rotten arrangement. "No. Absolutely not. Find another soul. There's a whole city of them out there, you've got a *congregation*, half of them followed us here—"

"Hollow souls weigh nothing," the Broker said gently. "They've already been spent. That is what hollow means, Miss Ashvale. And before you offer yourself—your family's debts are real but modest, and your soul, while spirited, weighs perhaps a tenth of what his does. The ledger is precise. Precision is the entire point." He turned back to Theo, and here his courtesy cracked, just slightly, and something older showed through—something that might, in a better light, have been grief. "I told you once, clerk, when you signed: *there will be a day of accounting, and you will hate me for how fair it is.* Do you remember?"

"No," Theo said. "That's rather the problem."

Something like a laugh escaped the Broker—brief, startled, and horribly human. "Yes," he said. "So it is."

* * *

Wren paced. Three steps one way, three steps back, the room being small, her fury having nowhere to go.

"So that's it," she said. "That's the grand scheme. He robs you, spends the proceeds sealing your gate, lives clean for ten years, and then you *invite him back* and tell him the bill's come due and the only legal tender is himself." She laughed, high and unhappy. "You know what we call that where I'm from? We call that a loan shark."

"We prefer *creditor*," the Broker said.

"I bet you do."

"Theo." She turned to him, and her face did the thing it did when she was frightened and refusing to admit it—the thing he'd learned to read somewhere on the long dark roads between Lowmark and here. "Don't listen to him. There's always another way. There's always a smuggler's road around the toll. We just haven't found it yet."

"The wards are dying, Wren." He said it quietly. "The trees grow black rings. An old woman forgot her granddaughter's name. And the stars—" He stopped. Started again. "I spent ten years keeping a little gate on a hill, checking the wards every night, telling myself I was guarding against smugglers and stray wolves. All that time I was the reason the big gate below was propped shut with twine. And now the twine's gone, and the only thing left on the shelf is me."

"Then we find something else on the shelf!"

"There is nothing else on the shelf. He's not lying about that part—I can feel it." He raised his palm, and the sigil burned there, steady and white, and the master ledger pulsed in answer, hungry, waiting. "It wants what I took. It has always wanted what I took."

Wren stared at him. Then at the ledger. Then at the Broker, who had retreated with perfect tact to the far side of the lectern, watching them with the patience of a man who had waited ten years and could wait ten more.

"Fine," she said at last, dangerously quiet. "Fine. Say it's true. Say the math is the math. Before anyone signs anything, I want to see the entry. The original theft. All of it."

The Broker raised an eyebrow—Theo's eyebrow; it was going to take years to scrub that man off his own face, supposing he lived to try. "Why?"

"Because I'm a smuggler, and we don't pay tolls on trust." She planted herself before the lectern. "Show me the page. Show me exactly what he took, exactly what he paid, and exactly what's owed. If someone's going to stay under this mountain, I want it in writing, in front of witnesses, no fine print. You people invented fine print."

For a long moment the Broker said nothing. The white candle guttered. Somewhere beyond the walls of the little room, the forgotten congregation stirred and murmured, drawn and restless, like tide against a shore.

Then the Broker inclined his head.

"Fair," he said. "Fairness is, after all, my one redeeming virtue, and I have been assured it is a terrible one." He reopened the ledger, turning pages with fingers that left no prints on the old paper, and stopped, and stepped aside.

"Read," he said. "Both of you. All of it. Because I warn you—" and here his borrowed face did something complicated, something that was not entirely the Broker's and not entirely Theo's, some expression stranded between the two men like a coin spun and not yet landed, "—the terms of the original contract contain a clause neither of you has considered. A way the debt may be discharged without a soul remaining. It was written ten years ago, by a desperate clerk, in his own blood. And I have spent ten years hoping he would never think of it."

Theo reached for the page. His sigil blazed so bright it lit the whole room, and the ledger's pulse quickened to meet it, eager, and Wren's hand found his shoulder and stayed there, warm and stubborn and refusing to move.

The ink began to swim into legibility.

The first word he read was his own name.

The second word was *Warden*.
