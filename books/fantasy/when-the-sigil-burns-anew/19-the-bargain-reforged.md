## 19. The Bargain Reforged

The gate beneath the mountain was not a gate at all.

That was the first thing Theo understood as he stood before it, Wren at his shoulder and the Broker's candlelit city spread out behind them like a wound in the dark. The surface wards at Thornfell had been ironwork and rune-stone, something you could touch and mend and curse when it froze over in winter. This was older than craft. It was a seam in the world itself, a place where the rock of the mountain simply *stopped agreeing* to be mountain, and beyond the seam there was nothing—not darkness, for darkness was something, but an absence that made his eyes water and his teeth ache.

Through that absence he could see them. The stars. All of them, hanging above Thornfell's sky like coins locked in a strongbox, dimmed and distant and *owed*.

"You're staring," Wren said quietly. She had her arm bound against her ribs where the hunters had caught her, and her face was gray with pain, but her voice was steady. It was always steady. It was the thing about her he trusted most, even now, even after everything.

"I'm thinking," Theo said.

"That's what worries me."

He almost laughed. It would have been easy to laugh—to let the moment stay small, the two of them trading wry words at the edge of an abyss the way they'd once traded them across a tavern table at the border. But the sigil on his palm was burning, white-gold light leaking between his fingers, and every lost soul in the deep court had followed its glow through the candle streets to stand here, a silent congregation of the forgotten, waiting to see what he would do.

And the Broker was smiling at him.

The Broker wore Theo's own face. That had stopped being shocking somewhere around the parley and become merely obscene—the way a mirror becomes obscene when you've decided you don't like who's in it. He stood beside the seam with his hands folded, patient as interest accruing, and his ledger floated open at his elbow, pages turning themselves in no wind at all.

"Ten years," the Broker said. "I have waited ten years, Keeper, and I will say this for you: you have never once been boring. You stole from me. You hid from me. And now you have walked into my court, past my hunters, through my tolls, carrying my mark in your hand like a lantern." His borrowed eyes—Theo's eyes—glinted. "So. What will you give me?"

"The question," Theo said, "is what you'll take."

* * *

He had thought about it on the flight down from the healer's hut—no, that wasn't right; they hadn't flown, they'd crawled and climbed and bled their way down—but he had thought about it in every quiet hour, and in most of the loud ones too. The healer's question had lodged in him like a splinter: *if you recover your soul, will you still be the man she trusts?*

He had no answer. But standing here, watching the Broker balance his books against the night sky, he realized the question had been pointing him somewhere all along.

"The ledger is unbalanced," the Broker said, tapping the floating book. "Ten years ago, someone removed from this ledger a debt of tremendous weight—a soul, or the equivalent—and left nothing in its place. The court does not forgive imbalance. The world does not forgive imbalance. Look up, Keeper. Those stars are collateral. They go out one by one until the account is settled. A soul of equal weight must be paid into my keeping, freely given, or the border fails and everything your little surface wards protected comes pouring through the seam."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we are all of us, eventually, memory-blind. Like your village. Like your travelers in the passes. The dark leaks upward, Keeper. It always has. The wards only slowed it. You knew that once—you built them knowing it."

"I know what I did ten years ago," Theo said. "I read my contract. I was your clerk. I stole your master ledger because the court was going to breach the gate, and I sealed it with myself as payment, and then I erased my own name so you couldn't collect." He flexed his burning hand. "The problem is that erasure *was* the payment, and the payment didn't hold. So here's my offer."

The Broker tilted his head, exactly the way Theo tilted his head when he was pretending not to be interested in gossip at the tavern. Seeing his own mannerisms worn by another man was like biting tinfoil.

"Not my soul," Theo said. "Not anyone else's either. Me. Not the part of me that can be weighed and shelved and traded—the whole of me. My memories as the ink. My body as the page. The sigil reborn as a living ward. I don't pay you a soul and walk away. I *stay*. I stand in the seam, awake, every hour, every year, holding the gate shut with everything I am. No balance required. No collateral. Just a keeper who can't be stolen from, because he's already given himself away."

Silence. Even the congregation of forgotten souls seemed to lean in.

Wren grabbed his sleeve. "No," she said. "No, absolutely not, that's not—we came here to get your soul *back*, Theo, that was the entire—that was the whole *point*—"

"The point was to save the border," Theo said gently. "Getting me back was the happy accident."

"It was not an accident, it was a *plan*, and the plan did not end with you becoming a piece of architecture—"

"Wren."

"Don't *Wren* me. I burned my family's ledger for you. I gave up the only thing my grandfather ever left us except debts. Do you know what I said when I lit it? I said *we're square now, all of us Ashvales, done with the deep court forever.* And now you want to sign yourself into it for eternity?" Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated that he could hear it, and he loved her for hating it. "You don't get to do this alone."

"I'm not doing it alone," Theo said. "You're going to witness."

* * *

The Broker had gone very still, which Theo suspected—for a creature whose whole existence was transactions—was the equivalent of a man being handed a contract he hadn't expected to find favorable.

"A living ward," the Broker repeated slowly. "Conscious. Perpetual. Bound to the seam." Something moved behind his stolen face, something that might have been hunger or might have been, incredibly, respect. "Do you understand what you're offering? Not rest. Not forgetting. You will feel every strain the gate takes. You felt the surface wards through that mark for ten years—from a hundred miles away, through stone and soil. Now imagine the seam itself. Imagine feeling the dark *press*, every day, forever."

"I've imagined it since Lowmark," Theo said. "It's why I'm offering."

"And your memories as ink. All of them. What remains of the clerk, what remains of the keeper, what remains of—" the Broker smiled thinly, "—of whatever grew in the space where your life used to be. Signed away. Mine to hold, though never again mine to spend. You understand that too? I keep the ink. I do not get to use it. A strange bargain. Almost a *charity*."

"It's not charity," Theo said. "It's a trade. You get permanence—no more imbalance, ever, no more hunts, no more collectors climbing my mountain. Your court keeps its ledgers and its candles and its long slow business. And I get the border safe." He looked at the congregation of forgotten souls, at the old woman from the village who had followed them weeping, at the hollow-eyed travelers. "And maybe, someday, some of these people get their names back. Because a balanced court has surplus. Doesn't it?"

The Broker's smile faltered—one degree, one heartbeat.

"You've been reading my ledgers."

"Someone had to. You weren't using them honestly."

* * *

They brought paper. Of course they brought paper; the deep court ran on paper the way the surface ran on sunlight. A clerk scurried forward—a small gray person made mostly of ink stains—and laid out a fresh contract on a lectern of black wood, and dipped a quill, and looked up expectantly.

"No," Theo said. "My terms. Written exactly as I say them."

The clerk glanced at the Broker. The Broker inclined his head with the graciousness of a man allowing the condemned to choose the rope.

Theo dictated. His voice was steadier than he felt:

*"Let it be known that Theo Delacroix, called Keeper of Thornfell, late clerk of the deep court, does hereby tender payment in full satisfaction of all outstanding balances, principal and interest, owed by any party touching the Thornfell gate: namely, his self entire. Let his memories serve as ink. Let his flesh serve as page. Let the sigil upon his hand be reborn as the living ward of the gate below, conscious and perpetual, so long as he endures. In exchange, the deep court shall hold the border sealed, shall make no claim upon any soul above or below the mountain, and shall restore, as its means allow, what was taken from those who forgot themselves. Witnessed and binding under the old law."*

The quill scratched it all down. The words appeared on the page glowing faintly gold, and Theo felt each clause settle into the air around him like stones dropping into still water.

"Sign," the Broker said softly, offering the quill. "Blood, if you please. Ink is for lesser bargains."

Theo reached for it—

—and Wren stepped in front of him.

She took the quill out of the Broker's hand. She did it casually, the way she did everything, like lifting a purse off a drunk, and the Broker actually blinked.

"Guarantor," she said. "There's such a thing in the old law. I've seen it in smuggler contracts my grandfather kept—he used it to bind routes to families, so if one Ashvale died the route passed to another and nobody could steal it. If a guarantor signs, the guarantor shares the burden of enforcement. If Theo breaks this contract, you can come for me. If *you* break it—" she jabbed the quill at his chest, at her own face reflected in his borrowed features, "—then I have standing. Me. A surface woman. An *Ashvale*. You think I don't know what that meant to your clerks a hundred years ago? My family carried your supplies. We were in your ledgers longer than he was."

The Broker went very still again.

"You would bind yourself to the gate's fate. Voluntarily. For a man who won't remember you tomorrow."

"He remembers me today," Wren said. "Today's the day that counts. Ask any smuggler."

She signed. Her blood rose bright red against the gold clauses—warmer-colored than anything else in the candle city, Theo thought, warmer than anything the court had produced in centuries—and where her signature settled, the contract's glow shifted, deepened, became something that pulsed rather than shone.

Then Theo took the quill.

His hand shook. He noticed, distantly, that it was the same shake he'd had signing the garrison's gate-inspection logs, ten thousand ordinary mornings ago. Some part of him wanted to laugh at that. Some part wanted to weep. He settled for both, briefly, privately, while the Broker watched and Wren watched and the forgotten souls watched and the stars hung in the seam like witnesses.

"Last chance to run," he murmured to Wren.

"Take your own advice."

"I never do. It's how you got stuck with me."

He pressed the quill to the page and wrote his name, and the world caught fire.

* * *

Not pain—or not only pain. It was fire the way a hearth is fire, the way a forge is fire, the way dawn over the Thornfell passes is fire: total and warm and utterly indifferent to what it burns. His memories lifted out of him one by one, and he felt each one go, and he held onto none of them, because holding on was the old bargain and the old bargain had failed.

The tavern. Gone. The taste of Wren's terrible stew. Gone. The morning he'd woken at the surface gate with no name and a burning hand, and been afraid, and chosen to stay anyway. Gone—except it wasn't, quite, because he had just written *chosen to stay anyway* into a binding contract, and some things survive their own erasure when you write them into law.

The sigil on his palm opened like a flower.

Light poured out of him—not into the seam but *along* it, filling it, stitching it, and Theo felt the shape of the gate the way he had once felt the surface wards: intimately, painfully, completely. The dark pressed against him, ancient and patient and vast, and he pressed back, and the seam closed by a hair's breadth, and the stars—

One star came back.

Just one, high over where Thornfell must be, a single point of silver in the absence. Then a second. Then a scatter of them, hesitant, like people lighting lamps after a long outage, unsure whether the power would hold.

In the candle city, the Broker closed his ledger. The sound was very soft, very final, like a door shutting on a room where someone had died peacefully.

"Well struck, Keeper," he said, and for one unguarded moment he wore an expression Theo recognized from his own mirror on good mornings: the look of a man who has lost a game and enjoyed losing it. "The old law binds me. The court withdraws. Your guarantor has standing, your clauses are iron, and your ink—" he touched the floating contract, now blazing gold and red, "—your ink is safe with me. Stranger things have been kept in vaults."

He began to turn away. Then he paused.

"For what it is worth," the Broker said, in Theo's own voice, wearing Theo's own face, "ten years ago, the clerk who stole my ledger was the finest mind I ever employed. I am not surprised he found a way to beat me twice." A thin smile. "Mind the third time, though. There is always a third time."

He walked back into his city of candles, and the candles dimmed behind him, and the deep court folded itself away like a letter being put aside unread.

Theo turned to Wren. He opened his mouth to say something—something important, something that mattered, something to carry across the dark between now and whenever she woke him at the surface gate.

What came out was: "Your stew really is terrible."

Wren laughed. It broke in the middle, the way laughter does when it's carrying too much grief to hold its shape, and she grabbed the front of his shirt with both hands and pressed her forehead against his, and around them the forgotten souls began, one by one, to remember their names.

Above the mountain, the stars came home.
