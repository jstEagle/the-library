## 20. When the Sigil Burns Anew

The gate beneath the mountain was not a gate at all.

That was the first thing Theo understood as he stood before it, Wren at his shoulder and the Broker's candlelit city burning quietly behind them. Gates had two sides — this side and that side, in and out, safe and not-safe. This was a wound dressed up as an architecture. A doorway of black stone that did not open onto anywhere, only onto *absence*, the place where the stars had gone when he stole them away ten years ago.

It stood open. It had been standing open for days, and every hour it stood open the dark above grew hungrier.

"Theo." Wren's voice came low, the way she spoke when she didn't want to frighten him and couldn't quite hide that she was frightened herself. "Say it again. The whole thing. I want to hear you say it like you mean it."

He turned to look at her. In the light of his own sigil — which burned in his palm now like a coal someone had cupped there and refused to drop — her face was all angles and soot and one fresh bandage across her ribs where the hunters had caught her. She looked terrible. She looked like the best thing he had ever seen.

"I offer myself," he said. "Not my soul. Not a piece of it. All of me — bone, breath, name, and memory — bound into the seal. I will be the ward. I will be the gate."

"And the interest?"

"And the interest," he said, "is paid in full, forever, by the keeping of it."

Wren nodded slowly, chewing on the inside of her cheek the way she did when she was doing sums she didn't like. "Right. Good. Terrible, but good." She pulled the stub of charcoal from her coat pocket and held it out. "You'll want something to write with. The Broker's rules don't bend for sentiment."

"No," said a voice behind them, rich and patient and horribly familiar, because it was *his* voice, worn smooth by centuries of use. "They do not."

* * *

The Broker came around the black stone of the gate the way fog comes around a headland — unhurried, inevitable, already everywhere before he arrived. He wore Theo's forgotten face the way a well-worn glove fits: comfortably, and with a certain smugness about it. His coat was the color of ledger paper. His smile was the smile Theo had seen in the chapel glass in the village that forgot itself, the smile of a man who has never once lost an argument because he wrote down all the arguments first.

"Ten years," the Broker said. He stopped perhaps ten paces away, close enough that the sigil's light reached him and no closer. Around him, drawn up in ranks among the candle-streets of the deep court, the forgotten souls waited — thousands of them, hollow-eyed and patient, the hunters and the hunted all wearing faces that somebody, somewhere, still grieved for. "Ten years I have kept your seat warm, clerk. Ten years I have balanced what books could be balanced and carried the rest as debt. And now you come back offering—" he tilted his head, savoring it— "*yourself*. Do you know what that is, Theo Delacroix? That is the finest entry anyone has ever proposed to write in my ledger."

"I'm glad you approve."

"Oh, I don't." The Broker's smile thinned. "I *mourn* it. Because here is the tragedy of you, thief: I would have taken your soul gladly. Your memories, even more gladly. But yourself — all of yourself, given freely, bound willingly into service without end?" He sighed, and it sounded almost genuine. "That is not payment. That is a *gift*. And gifts, by the oldest law of the deep court — older than me, though don't tell anyone I said so — cannot be collected. They can only be *accepted*. And once accepted..." His eyes glittered. "Once accepted, they bind the giver as surely as they bind the court. You understand what you're proposing. You become the lock. And a lock does not get to walk away."

"I know."

"You will ache. Every hour, every year, whenever the wards are touched, whenever the dark leans on the door. You felt a taste of it these ten years past, through a scar of a sigil. This will be the whole feast."

"*I know.*"

"You will forget everything but what you keep, and you will keep very little, and none of it will be yours by right — only by mercy of whoever stands witness."

"I said I *know*," Theo said, and his voice cracked on it, and he let it crack, because Wren was watching and she deserved to hear the fear in it plain. "I've known since the healer asked me the question I couldn't answer. I've known since I read my own signature in your ledger room. I spent ten years being nobody, Broker. Invisible keeper of the Thornfell gate. And you know what I learned?" He held up his burning hand between them. "Nobody is a *wonderful* job title. I intend to keep it. Permanently."

For a long moment the Broker said nothing. Somewhere in the candle city, a bell tolled — slow, deep, counting something. Debts, maybe. Or seconds.

Then the Broker laughed. It was a terrible sound and a beautiful one, because it was Theo's own laugh, the one he'd never gotten to hear, aged and polished and turned inside out.

"Well," the Broker said. "Witnessed, then?"

"Witnessed," said Wren Ashvale, stepping forward, and she planted herself between Theo and the gate like she was planting a flag.

* * *

"No," Theo said. "Absolutely not. Wren—"

"Save it." She'd already produced the Ashvale ledger from inside her coat — the family book, three generations of smuggling routes and secret tolls and debts owed to the deep court, the book her grandfather had used to carry whatever Theo traded down the mountain. She'd carried it the whole way here. She'd carried it through the flight through the candle streets with a hole in her side. She held it now in both hands, and Theo saw that its pages were already curling, scorched at the edges from some near thing in the dark. "You're not signing alone. I told you in the passes and I'm telling you under the mountain: nobody signs alone."

"That's not how contracts work here—"

"It's how *my* contracts work." Her chin came up, and there it was — the smuggler's grin, crooked and unkillable, the one that had talked them past three tolls, one warden, and an entire hunting party of the faceless dead. "My family helped build this bargain. My grandfather drove the cart. My mother kept the accounts. I burned the book an hour ago, by the way, so don't bother looking for it — the debt line died with it. Which means the court owes *me*, if anything, and I'm calling it in as guarantor. That's legal. Look it up."

"She's right," the Broker said mildly. "It's legal. I hate it enormously."

"Good," said Wren.

Theo looked at her. There were a hundred things he wanted to say and most of them were selfish — *go home, be safe, live a long life where your hands don't burn* — and every single one of them was also, he realized, an insult. She had not come this far to be protected out of the ending. Nobody who burns their family's ledger gets protected out of the ending.

So instead he said: "What do we write?"

"Names," the Broker said. "And terms. And then blood — yours freely given, hers freely witnessed. Ink is for ledgers. Blood is for *bindings*." He drew from his coat a book, and the sight of it stopped Theo's breath: the master ledger itself, vast and ancient, its cover stitched from something that had once been sky. "The old contract, if you please."

Theo took the pen the Broker offered. It was black quill, and it was warm, and he knew without asking that it had been cut from his own wing once upon a time, back when he'd had wings, or whatever he'd been before he was a man named Delacroix.

The open page showed his old contract. Ten years stale. He read it top to bottom this time, unhurried, because he would not sign anything blind ever again.

*I, the undersigned clerk of the deep court, having stolen the master ledger of souls to seal the gate above, do surrender my name, my history, and my self, as payment for the balance incurred...*

Below it, in the witness column, a name he had flinched from once: Magistrate Ilse Varrow of Lowmark. He'd deal with that tomorrow. If there was a tomorrow. There would be a tomorrow; that was the entire point.

He turned the page. Fresh paper. Blank, waiting, humming faintly like a struck bell that hadn't been struck yet.

* * *

He wrote carefully.

*I, Theo Delacroix, keeper of the Thornfell gate, offer my whole self — bone, breath, name, and memory — as the new seal of the gate below. Let my body be the wardstone. Let my sigil be the lock. Let my remaining years be the ink that keeps the words true. I ask nothing in return except that the border hold, and the stars come home.*

His hand shook only a little. The sigil in his palm pulsed with each word, and where the quill touched the page, the writing caught fire — not consuming, but *setting*, letters burning themselves permanently into the paper the way brands are set into living wood. The smell of it was strange and sweet, like a hearth lit after a long winter.

Then, below, in different handwriting — quick, slanted, unapologetic:

*And I, Wren Ashvale of the Thornfell routes, last of my name by my own choice, stand witness and guarantor. Whatever he pays, I pay attention. Whatever he loses, I remember. Whatever he becomes, I will know him. Signed in presence, if not in blood, until such time as blood is wanted.*

The Broker read both entries twice. His borrowed face did something complicated — grief and hunger and, buried deepest of all, relief, the relief of a jailer whose prison has finally found a lock worthy of it.

"Blood," he said softly, and offered his hand — and in his palm, mirrored, was a sigil of his own, faded to gray, the mark of a clerk who had once stood exactly where Theo stood and chosen differently. Chosen to keep the books instead of becoming one.

Theo pressed his burning palm to the page.

The world went white.

* * *

It did not hurt the way fire hurts. It hurt the way *truth* hurts — total, clarifying, unbearable for exactly as long as it takes and not one moment less.

Every memory he had left poured out of him and into the page. Not stolen — *spent*, willingly, each one a coin ringing down into the ledger's depths. He felt his mother's face go and grieved it in the half-second he still had it. He felt the taste of the border tavern's bad ale go. He felt the first morning at the gatehouse, the ordinary miracle of an unremarkable dawn, go — and he gave them all gladly, gladly, gladly, because giving was the whole point, because a seal made of stolen things would fail in ten years just as the last one had, and a seal made of *given* things—

A seal made of given things holds forever.

He heard Wren's voice somewhere above the whiteness, steady as a rope in the dark: "I've got you. I've got you. I'm remembering everything, so you don't have to. Go on, Theo. Burn."

And Theo burned.

The sigil spread from his palm up his arm like dawn coming over a ridge — not consuming him but *completing* him, writing itself along his bones, and where it passed he felt himself become less a man and more a *doorway held shut*, and it should have been horror, and it wasn't, it was the opposite of horror, it was the feeling of a joint finally seating correctly, a key turning true, a long dislocated thing clicking home.

Above them — far above, up through miles of black stone and frozen pass and the dead wards of Thornfell — the stars began to come back.

They felt it from underneath. Each returning star landed in the ledger with a small chime, and the chimes ran together into something that was almost music, almost laughter, and all around the candle city the forgotten souls lifted their hollow faces toward a light they couldn't see and began, one by one, to *remember themselves* — names rising in them like bubbles through dark water, breaking softly at the surface.

The Broker closed the ledger. The thunderclap of it echoed off the underside of the world.

"It balances," he said, and his voice had gone quiet, and for the first time it sounded only like a man's voice, tired beyond all telling. "Ten years of deficit, paid in full, with interest, by the strangest entry I have ever accepted." He looked at Theo — at what Theo now was, glowing faintly all over, human-shaped and gate-shaped at once — and he inclined his head, formal as a magistrate. "Keeper. The court withdraws. By our own law, while the seal holds freely given, we may not touch the surface, nor take toll, nor collect. You have made my kingdom poor, Theo Delacroix." A pause. The ghost of the smile. "Thank you."

He turned to Wren. For a moment something moved behind his borrowed eyes — something old and lonely, the thing that had once been a clerk too, before he chose the books over the binding.

"Ashvale," he said. "Your grandfather drove a cart for me thirty years. He always tipped his hat. It was the only kindness anyone showed me in that century." He reached into his coat and set something small on the black stone between them: a single page, torn from the master ledger, blank and clean. "A receipt. Blank pages are rare things, where I come from. Spend it wisely."

Then he walked back down into the candle city, and the candles went out behind him street by street, like a house closing its eyes for sleep, and the deep court folded itself away into the mountain's heart, and the gate —

The gate closed. Through Theo. As him. With a sound like a great book shutting gently, and then silence, and then, impossibly, distantly, the ordinary night-sounds of the Thornfell passes: wind, and snow, and somewhere a fox complaining about the weather.

* * *

He woke on cold stone with snow beginning to fall through the arch of the surface gatehouse, and the first thing he saw was stars — dozens of them, then hundreds, pouring back into the sky over Thornfell like a spill of silver coins someone was sweeping home. One by one. Brighter by ones and twos. The dark above the mountain unraveling stitch by stitch.

Wren was sitting beside him, her coat tucked under his head, her hand wrapped around his burning palm as though she could hold the light in.

"Hey," she said, when his eyes opened. "There you are." Her voice was rough. Her eyes were wet. Her grin was trying very hard to happen. "How many fingers am I holding up?"

"One. And it's holding my hand."

"Cheat." She laughed, and it broke in the middle, and she wiped her face with her free sleeve without any pretense of hiding it. "Do you — " She stopped. Started again, careful as someone crossing ice. "Do you know me?"

He searched. The sigil glowed steady in his chest, in his arm, in the stone of the gate, all one thing now. Most of him had gone into the page and stayed there. But some things had been written too deep to spend.

"You talk too much," he said. "You chew your cheek when you're doing sums. You burned your family's book for me and you'd do it again and you'll pretend for a week that you didn't cry just now, and you'll fail." His thumb moved against her fingers. "Wren Ashvale. Last of her name by her own choice."

Her breath went out of her all at once. "Oh," she said. "Oh, good. Oh — *good*."

"And you owe me a drink," Theo added. "At the border tavern. I remember that much. I remember it very specifically."

"You remember the *drink*."

"Some things are sacred, Wren."

She laughed again, properly this time, and pulled him upright, and together they stood in the falling snow under the returning stars while the old gatehouse creaked around them, settling, like a house whose owner has finally come home.

Neither of them noticed, yet, the second figure standing at the tree line at the bottom of the pass — travel-worn, hooded, with a magistrate's seals glinting dull silver at her collar — or the way Magistrate Ilse Varrow looked up at the mended sky, then at the two keepers silhouetted in the gate's glow, and began, slowly, to climb.

But the sigil felt her coming. And it ached.
