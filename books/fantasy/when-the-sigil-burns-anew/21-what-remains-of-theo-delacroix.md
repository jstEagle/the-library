## 21. What Remains of Theo Delacroix

Wren Ashvale had counted herself a hard woman more times than she could count, and most of those times she'd been lying to herself. But she had never once lied about this: watching a man burn was the worst thing she had ever done nothing about.

She'd held his hand through it. That was all the signing allowed—a witness's hand on the guarantor's line, and then Theo's grip crushing hers while fire crawled up his arm and into his eyes and he *smiled*, gods help him, smiled like a man setting down a load he'd carried ten years too far. The light had gone out of the ledger room and into him, and when it faded he was lying on the cold stone of the gate beneath the mountain, breathing, whole, and utterly, catastrophically empty.

The climb back up took three days. She did most of it carrying him on her back, which she told him—when he woke enough to hear—was excellent repayment for every favor he'd ever done her, and that the interest rate was ruinous.

He laughed at that. It was the first thing he'd done that felt like him.

* * *

He woke properly at dawn on the fourth day, in the keeper's cottage beside the surface gate, under the blanket Wren had stolen from the tavern and never returned. She was sitting in the chair by the fire pretending to mend a coat she'd been pretending to mend for two days.

His eyes opened and found her face, and something in them settled—the way a compass needle settles when it finally finds north again after being shaken.

"Wren," he said. Not a question. He said it the way you say the name of a thing you're sure of.

"That's me." She set down the coat. "How much of your head is still in there?"

He frowned, and she watched him go looking—she could see the looking happen, behind his eyes, a man walking through the rooms of his own house and finding the furniture gone. "You," he said slowly. "The mountain. The candle city. The Broker wearing my face. A toll-warden who took—" His hand flew to his chest, patting, searching. "My mother. I paid my mother's face. I can't—"

"It'll come back," Wren said, because the healer had said it might, and hope was cheaper than the truth. "Maybe."

"And before?" He sat up too fast and swayed; she caught his shoulder and pushed him gently back down, an old habit from tending hungover smugglers. "Before the mountain. Ten years of keeping this gate. I know there were ten years. I know I *lived* them. But they're..." He made a small, helpless gesture, a man describing a locked door in a house he no longer owned. "There's a shape where they were. Like footprints in snow."

"Do you remember the tavern?"

"No."

"The magistrate in Lowmark? Grella, with the eyebrows like storm fronts?"

"No." He paused. "Should I be embarrassed by how much I apparently disliked her?"

"Deeply. You called her 'the human tax collector' to her face. Twice."

"I stand by him," Theo said solemnly. "Whoever he was."

Wren laughed, and it came out wrong, wet around the edges, and she turned to poke the fire so he wouldn't see. The fire didn't need poking. She poked it anyway.

* * *

They rebuilt him the way you rebuild anything: badly, patiently, one piece at a time.

She walked him through the cottage. "This is yours. Was yours. The chipped mug is yours—you refused to drink from any other cup, said the chip fit your thumb. The boots by the door are yours. The garden's yours, though I'd call it more of a graveyard with ambitions."

He touched each thing like a man reading braille. The mug. The boots. A whetstone worn into a groove that matched his own thumb exactly. "I did this," he said wonderingly. "Ten years of mornings did this."

"Every morning. Rain or snow. You complained every single time and went out anyway."

"What kind of man complains and does it anyway?"

"A good one," Wren said, and then had to go see about the kettle, which also did not need seeing about.

* * *

On the fifth day she took him out to the gate itself.

It stood quiet in the spring light, and it was different now—she could see it even if he couldn't, or maybe especially because he couldn't. The stones that had been cracked for a century were sealed with seams of faint gold, like kintsugi worked by someone very patient. And beneath the old ward-marks, new ones: fine lines of light that moved when you weren't looking directly at them, slow as sap rising.

And on Theo's palm, the sigil glowed steady. No longer the angry brand that flared when the wards were touched. Just a low ember-warmth, constant, like a banked hearth.

Theo stared at it for a long time.

"Does it hurt?" Wren asked.

"No," he said, surprised. "That's the strange part. Ten years it ached whenever the wards were troubled. Now it just... hums. Like it's *content*."

"That's because you are the wards, you great idiot."

"I know what I am," he said quietly. "I'm just not sure I'm anyone."

The words landed between them and neither picked them up right away. Below the ridge, Thornfell stretched out green and ordinary—the border road, the tree line, the far hills where stars would come back tonight for the first time in a season. Somewhere down there, a village of nameless people was waiting for the list Wren had copied from the chapel ledger. Somewhere beyond it, Lowmark's garrison was still trying to decide whether Theo Delacroix was a hero, a criminal, or a liability. The magistrate's name had been in that contract. There would be reckoning for that. Later.

"You're the man who burned himself alive for a border full of strangers," Wren said finally. "Whatever else got sold off, that stayed. I watched it stay."

"You watched it stay," he repeated. "Then tell me—who was I? When I wasn't doing that?"

So she told him. Over supper, over the next week, over the slow repair of the cottage roof that neither of them needed to fix but both needed to do. She told him about the keeper who traded wry insults over the tavern table every market night but never once let her pay for her own ale, because "a smuggler's coin is tainted and I'm a public servant." About the time he'd spent three days nursing a wounded hawk back to flight and pretended the whole enterprise was a nuisance. About how he sang—badly, tunelessly, with tremendous commitment—only when he thought no one could hear, and how she'd heard him roughly four hundred times without ever telling him.

"I knew that," Theo said, mortified. "Somehow I *know* that. I can feel the embarrassment without the memory."

"That's called character. Congratulations. It survived."

* * *

The bad nights came anyway.

The third week, he woke screaming with a word in his mouth—not the branded word from the first collapse, but something else, something in a language that made the fire gutter—and Wren held him until it passed, and afterward he sat shaking and said, "I don't know what I lost tonight. That's the worst part. I can't even mourn it properly. It's grief with no grave."

"Then mourn the not-knowing," Wren said. "Mourn that. It counts."

"Does it?"

"It's the only kind I've got left of my grandfather," she said, "and I've gotten decent practice." She said it lightly. He saw through it, the way he always seemed to see through things, memory or no memory. Some skills live deeper than recollection.

"He helped make the bargain," Theo said. "Your family delivered for the Broker. And you burned the Ashvale ledger for me anyway."

"For *you*," she corrected. "For the specific, insufferable, self-immolating you. Don't let it go to your head."

Too late. It was already written all over his face, plain as morning, and Wren looked away first, which was becoming a dangerous habit.

* * *

Spring deepened. The stars came back—one the first night, then three, then a scatter, then a sky. Travelers returned to the border road and reported, with the vague unease of people who couldn't explain their own relief, that the passes felt *safe* again. The black rings in the trees began to fade. In the forgotten village, an old woman wept over her own name written on a slate and spent the rest of the day saying it aloud to everyone she met.

And Theo learned his life like lessons. How to check the gate-seams (though they checked themselves now, really, glowing brighter when anything brushed too close, dimming when satisfied). How the garden wanted planting. How Wren took her tea—too strong, half honey, drunk standing up because sitting down meant staying.

One evening he caught her wrist as she headed for the door after supper, some smuggler errand or other, and said, "Stay," and it wasn't a request about tea.

Wren Ashvale, who had stared down soul-hunters and the Broker of the Deep Court and her own family's ghost-debts, found she had absolutely nothing clever to say. So she stayed.

* * *

But it was on a night in late spring, the two of them on the cottage step with the gate humming its low golden note behind them, that the hook came.

Theo turned his palm toward the starlight. The sigil glowed steady, content, banked. Then—faintly, at the edge—it flickered. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat skipping.

Wren saw it. So did he.

"Probably nothing," Theo said.

"The gate's been quiet for two months."

"Probably the mountain settling."

"Mountains don't settle, Theo. They're mountains."

He closed his hand slowly, and for just a moment—before his fingers curled shut—Wren saw that the sigil hadn't merely flickered. It had pulsed *inward*, once, like something on the other side knocking politely.

Far below them, beneath a hundred fathoms of stone, in a city of candles and ledgers bound by rules older than kings, something had remembered it was owed a courtesy visit.

Or perhaps—Wren thought, watching Theo watch his own hand with an expression she was learning to read as well as any map—perhaps the living ward had simply begun, at last, to dream.

Either way, come morning, they would need to talk about the letter that had arrived at dusk: no courier, no seal, only a single line in handwriting Theo did not recognize and Wren, with a cold twist in her gut, did:

*The clerk remembers. Come collect the rest.*
