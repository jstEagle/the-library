## 22. Keepers, Two

The first thaw came to Thornfell on a Tuesday, which Wren Ashvale considered a personal insult. Thaws ought to arrive on Sundays, or at the very least on feast days, with trumpets and honey-cakes and a general air of occasion. Instead it arrived while she was hauling timber up the switchback road, one plank at a time, sweating through her shirt in March like a fool who'd forgotten every winter she'd ever survived.

The snow went soft all at once. The pines shook themselves loose of white. Somewhere down in the valley, a stream woke up mid-sentence, chattering over stones it had spent four months forgetting.

And Theo Delacroix, standing at the gate with his sleeves rolled and his palm pressed flat against the wardstone, laughed.

Wren stopped mid-slope with her plank balanced on her shoulder just to look at him. She'd known the man for eleven years—known him as the quiet keeper who drank exactly two cups of cider at the border tavern and never three, who mended his own coat rather than buy thread, who spoke to travelers with a courtesy so even it might have been painted on. In all those years she had heard him laugh perhaps four times, and she could have counted each one.

Since the mountain, since the candle city, since the night he burned himself into the gate and came back to her with most of himself left behind—he laughed at thaw. He laughed at burnt porridge. He had once laughed for a full minute at a mule that refused to cross a bridge, and Wren had sat there on the bridge rail thinking: *I would burn down a court of goblins for that sound.* She had, more or less. It had gone very well, all things considered.

"You're staring," Theo called up the slope.

"I'm assessing your posture," she said. "You're slouching against a load-bearing piece of ancient mystery. Show some respect."

"This *ancient mystery* has a crack in it shaped like a duck."

"It does not."

He stepped aside so she could see. It did. Right through the middle of the wardstone, where the old carved sigils spiraled inward toward the center, a pale fissure ran in a curve with a smaller curve beside it, and above them two weathered hollows that could, if you squinted and were prepared to be generous about beaks, be eyes.

"Huh," said Wren, arriving at last and setting her plank down. "Well. The wards held back the Broker's whole court for a thousand years. I don't see why they can't hold back a duck."

"The wards aren't holding anything back anymore." He said it lightly, but Wren caught the thing underneath it, the way you catch a fish under ice—a shadow moving where you can't quite reach. "I am."

"Yes," she said. "I was there. I signed the paperwork." She knocked her knuckles against the stone. "Which makes me co-owner of this duck, and I say we keep it."

* * *

The cottage took shape over six weeks, the way cottages do when built by two people who are good at many things but not, specifically, building.

Wren had smuggled goods through the Thornfell passes since she was nine years old. She knew every goat track, every deadfall, every cave with a floor that lied about being solid. What she did not know was how to make a wall stand plumb. Theo, who had spent ten years keeping a lonely gate in perfect repair, turned out to know carpentry the way he knew everything else—thoroughly, patiently, and with a competence that made Wren want to throw her hammer into the ravine out of pure envy.

"Left," he'd say. "Your other left."

"I have a map of these mountains in my head that took twenty years to memorize."

"And yet."

"And yet what?"

"You've nailed your sleeve to the rafter."

The cottage stood thirty paces from the gate—close enough that either of them could reach the wardstone barefoot in under a minute, which was the entire point, though neither of them said so aloud. It had one large room, a hearth Theo built twice because the first chimney smoked like a signal fire announcing an invasion, and a porch facing east, because Wren insisted, and when Wren insisted in that particular tone, mountains had been known to reconsider their positions.

They hung the door latch on the inside, and then, after some discussion, added a second latch on the outside of the gate-house itself, so that anyone coming up the border road would have to knock. Travelers still passed sometimes. Fewer than before—the stars going dark for a season had a way of rerouting caravans—but enough. A tinker woman in April. Two pilgrims in May, heading nowhere in particular, which Wren privately suspected meant fleeing something specific, and fed generously on principle.

Every traveler got the same greeting from Theo: tea, bread, directions, and a cheerful warning not to stray off the road into the high passes until midsummer. Every traveler asked why, and every traveler got the same answer, delivered with a shrug: "Landslides."

There had been no landslides. There had been a goblin court beneath the mountain full of stolen souls, and its Broker bound by contract to stay below, and a ledger now balanced by the weight of a living man's name written in fire. But "landslides" saved an hour of explanation, and besides—Theo had developed a theory about truth that Wren found both wise and slightly infuriating: that you told people the truth they could carry, and no more. Anything heavier, you carried for them, or you taught them to carry it slowly.

The villagers of Hollowmere were learning to carry it slowly.

* * *

Hollowmere—that was the name they'd chosen for themselves, in the end. The village whose people had forgotten their own names, who had lived all winter by rote and routine while the darkness leaked up from below, had spent the spring doing something Wren had never seen before: remembering on purpose.

It went like this. Every seventh day, the whole village gathered in the chapel—the same chapel where Theo had found the smuggler's ledger that started them down the mountain—and someone read names aloud from a list pinned to the wall. Old Marta, who had been the first to grab Theo's hand and call him "the one who sold himself," kept the list. When a villager remembered something—a mother's face, a wedding day, the words to a lullaby—they told it to Marta, and she wrote it down, so that if the memory slipped away again it wouldn't slip away alone.

Wren and Theo went down every seventh day. It had started as penance, near as Wren could tell—or Theo's version of it, which was quieter and more stubborn than most. He had learned, in the deep court, that his bargain ten years ago was part of what let the darkness spread; that the seal he'd bought with his own soul had been imperfect, patched, leaking at the seams. The village's forgetting had been a symptom of that leak. So he went down and he sat with them, and he helped them remember, and he never once said *this is my fault*, because he also never once missed a week.

By midsummer, the list had grown fat. The baker remembered she had a daughter married off to a family in Lowmark, and cried into her dough, and made the best honey-bread any of them had tasted. The miller remembered he'd once been a soldier and, better, remembered he'd hated it, and laughed until he wheezed. Children who had spent the winter silent began naming things—chickens, clouds, the crooked fence post—and demanding the names be written down too, because they'd seen the adults do it and wanted in.

One evening in June, a girl of maybe seven tugged Theo's sleeve as they were leaving the chapel.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Delacroix," he said. "Theo Delacroix."

She squinted at him with the ferocious skepticism only small children and customs officers can manage. "That's not what I mean. What's your *real* name? Before?"

The lane went very quiet. Wren felt the question land in Theo like a stone dropped down a well—she saw it in the set of his shoulders, watched him wait for the splash that didn't come. Because that was the arithmetic of it, the bargain's strange mercy and cruelty braided together: he'd paid ten years of memories to seal the gate, then paid more at the toll-shrine, then given nearly everything else to the new signing. What remained was fragments. Wren's face, yes. The feel of a wardstone under his palm. How to build a chimney that didn't smoke. But the man he had been before—the clerk of the deep court, the thief who stole hope itself to save a border he couldn't remember choosing—was ink now, burned into the gate, holding it shut.

"I don't know yet," Theo said to the girl, crouching to her height, entirely unhurried. "But I'm working on it. I've got a good teacher." He nodded at Wren. "She remembers things for a living."

"That's not a real job," said the girl.

"It's the oldest job there is," Wren said. "Now run along before I teach you smuggling."

Marta wrote it all down anyway. Wren saw her do it through the chapel window as they walked away: *Keeper Theo. Doesn't know his first name yet. Working on it.*

*Yet*. Wren liked that word more than she could say.

* * *

The sigil ached, sometimes.

Not often. Not the way it had before—not the white-hot brand of the night the stars died, not the slow poison throb of the years when the wards were failing and nobody knew. Now it was smaller than that. A twinge on cold nights, when the wind came down off the passes carrying something older than winter. A pull at odd moments, like a tooth that remembers it was once sore.

The first time it happened in the new cottage—three weeks after they'd moved in, a night of hard rain—Theo sat bolt upright in bed with his hand clenched and his breath going fast, and Wren was awake before he was, because she had been half-awake every night since the mountain, listening for exactly this.

"Hey," she said. "Hey. Look at me. Where are we?"

"Cottage," he managed. "East porch. Crooked door. Your hammer is still in the—"

"In the woodpile, yes, where you told me twice to move it and I ignored you both times." She took his hand and unfolded the fingers one by one. The sigil glowed faintly under the skin, blue-white as starlight, calm as a banked coal. "There it is. Just checking in. Nothing's wrong. The gate's fine. You're fine."

"How do you know?"

"Because if something were wrong, it would hurt worse. And because I checked the wardstone before bed, like I check it every night, like a person with a completely normal and proportionate relationship with an ancient magical boundary." She pressed his palm flat between both of hers. "Also because it's raining, and rain always makes it ache a little. We've been over this. Weather report, courtesy of your hand."

A breath went out of him. He lay back down. But he didn't let go of her hands, and she didn't pull hers away, and after a while, in the dark, he said:

"When it aches—I used to think that was the worst feeling in the world. Ten years of it. Like something under the mountain knocking on my skin." A pause. "It doesn't feel like that anymore."

"No?"

"It feels like—" He was quiet long enough that she thought he'd fallen asleep. Then: "Like a rope. Someone holding the other end."

"Well," Wren said, to the ceiling, gruffly, because her throat had decided to be unreliable. "Someone is."

* * *

In August, the magistrate came up from Lowmark.

Wren had opinions about Magistrate Corvane that could fill a book, and most of the pages would be profanity. This was the woman who had recognized Theo's debtor's mark, sent them down the mountain with a pat and a blessing, and whose signature—Wren had learned, in the ledger room, in the worst hour of her life—sat witness on Theo's original contract. Whatever game Corvane had played ten years ago, whatever debt or deal had put her name on that blood-signed line, she had never explained it. Theo had never demanded she. Some accounts, he said, you leave open on purpose, so you remember they exist.

She arrived on a mule, of all things, wearing travel clothes instead of magistrate's grey, and she stood at the foot of the gate steps looking up at the wardstone for a long time before either of them spoke.

"It's different," she said finally.

"It's me," Theo said.

"Yes." Something moved behind her face—regret, or fear, or the particular exhaustion of a person who has been waiting a decade for a bill to come due. "I've come to bring you the rest of the Lowmark archive. The original survey of the wards. Records from before the founding." She unslung a leather satchel from the mule's back and held it out. "You're the ward now. You should have them."

Wren took the satchel before Theo could move, mostly because she wanted something to do with her hands that wasn't strangling a magistrate. "And that's all? You rode three days on a *mule* to deliver paperwork?"

Corvane looked at her, and for a moment the magistrate's composure thinned, and underneath it Wren saw something almost young. "No," Corvane said. "I came to ask if it hurts."

Theo was quiet a moment. Rain-wind stirred the pines. Then he raised his right hand, palm out, and let the sigil show—faintly luminous even in daylight now, a spiral of fine silver lines with the old carved signs threaded through them, the mark of a man who had become a door's answer.

"Sometimes," he said. "On cold nights. When storms come down off the passes. And once, in July, when a peddler tried to sneak past the gate at midnight with a wagon full of untaxed brandy."

"That was *taxes*, not magic," Wren muttered.

"Everything's connected," Theo said serenely. To Corvane: "It hurts less than it used to. And it doesn't hurt alone. That was the trick of the new contract—my name isn't the only one on it."

Corvane looked from him to Wren and back, and whatever she was weighing, she seemed to set it down. "Then I'll say what I should have said ten years ago, before I let a stranger sign away a decade of his life without telling him what he was buying." She bowed—shallow, but real. "I'm sorry. And thank you."

"Marta would tell you," Theo said, "that sorry works better with names attached. All of them. Even the ugly ones."

Something flickered across the magistrate's face—there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Perhaps someday," she said, and mounted her mule, and rode back down toward Lowmark, leaving the satchel and the apology and one question Wren intended to keep warm for a very long time.

* * *

Autumn came, and with it the first hard frost, and with *that* the first anniversary of the night the stars went out.

They marked it without meaning to. Wren realized halfway through the evening that the date had arrived, and braced herself for it—for grief, or dread, or whatever the proper feeling was supposed to be. But the evening refused to cooperate. They ate stew. Theo read aloud from one of Corvane's archives, an account so dryly bureaucratic it circled back around to comedy ("Item: forty pounds of tallow for the ward-lamps, requisitioned by Underwarden Pell, *who is reminded that lamps require wicks*"). Outside, the frost made the whole valley glitter, and overhead the stars burned in their courses, steady and thick as spilled salt, every single one of them accounted for.

"They came back slowly," Theo said later, on the porch, wrapped in one blanket apiece because Wren maintained that sharing blankets was inefficient, and Wren's definitions of efficiency had never once survived contact with Theo Delacroix. "Do you remember? One every few minutes. Like someone relighting lamps down a long hall."

"I remember counting them," Wren said. "Two hundred and eleven. I counted because if I was counting I wasn't crying."

"Were you crying?"

"Absolutely not."

"Mm." He tilted his head back against the chair. His hand lay open on the armrest, palm up, sigil dim and easy. "Sometimes I try to remember what it was like. Being hollow. Having a hole where ten years should be." He was quiet. "Mostly what I remember is the tavern. You, cheating at cards. Me pretending not to notice."

"I have never cheated at cards in my life."

"You marked the ace of bells with a thumbnail. I watched you do it."

"That was *quality inspection*."

"That," said Theo, "is why I married you."

Wren went still. They had been married in May—Lowmark, a borrowed hall, the whole of Hollowmere in attendance and Marta writing every word of it down, because that was Marta's way now. It had been a good day, a loud day, a day when Theo had laughed so much his face had looked unfamiliar to himself, he'd said afterward, in the good way, in the way of a man finding his own expression again. Eleven months of marriage. He still announced it sometimes, out of nowhere, with the delight of a man reading good news off a fresh page.

"Why," said Wren, when she trusted her voice, "does it still surprise you? Saying it."

"Because it's true every time." He shrugged, boyish, unbothered. "True things are allowed to surprise you. Otherwise what's the point."

Below them, the valley slept. Above them, the stars kept their watch, and the gate kept its keeper, and somewhere far beneath the mountain—so far that it was less a place than a direction—a courteous figure with a borrowed face sat in a city of candles, honoring terms he could not break, and knew it.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and with it, on the first real snow, a knock at the cottage door.

Wren answered it with a spoon in one hand, expecting the tinker woman, or a lost traveler, or Hollowmere children come to demand stories and honey-bread. Instead she found a boy of fifteen, half-frozen, leading a mule loaded with crates, and wearing the grey sash of a Lowmark courier—and behind him, up the border road through the falling snow, came a second figure. Small. Bent. Walking with a stick and a bundle tied in cloth, moving with the deliberate courage of someone very old who has decided, at last, to make a journey everyone told her was impossible.

Marta.

Wren looked at the boy, at the crates, at the old woman laboring up through the snowfall toward the gate where the sigil's light had already begun, faintly, to rise—and understood, in the marrow-deep way you understand weather coming, that something had changed down in the world. That the story they had closed in the dark beneath the mountain had opened a door somewhere else.

Theo appeared at her shoulder, tea towel over one arm, and followed her gaze up the road. The sigil on his palm brightened another degree, steady as a heartbeat, and he said, softly, with wonder:

"Wren. Look at her hand."

And Wren looked—and saw, clutched around Marta's walking stick, glowing faint and silver through aged skin, a mark that had no business existing outside this family of two.

"*Well,*" said Wren Ashvale, and reached for her boots.
