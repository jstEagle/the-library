## 2. The Pilgrim Who Knew My Name

The bell for Vespers of the Quiet Hours rang thin and late that evening, the way it always did when Brother Osmund drew the rope with his bad shoulder, and I let its unevenness wash over me like rain on a roof I did not own.

I was in my alcove by then, third from the end on the eastern cloister wall, copying the temple's grain accounts into a hand so careful it erased me. That was the trick of scribe work no one told you when you took the indenture: if your letters were perfect enough, no one looked at the person making them. I had spent six years learning to be a well-made line. Straight. Unremarkable. Exactly where the ledger expected it to be.

*Four years, seven months,* said the tally in my head, the one I kept behind my eyes where even an abbot could not audit it. *Four years, seven months, and eleven days.* Mother's debt, compounded at the temple's gentle rate. The number moved slowly now, slower than it used to, because of the other ledger—the one that lived folded inside my left boot.

Three routes through Thornfell's southern passes, sold piecemeal to a smuggler factor named Corvo Hale who paid in temple scrip and never asked how a scribe came to know which goat trails held frost in summer and which shrine bells were rung only to warn of soldiers. The answer was simple and I kept it simple: my mother had walked those mountains her whole life, hauling tin and secrets, and before she died she had poured all of it into me the way you pour the last of a cask into a smaller cup. I sold what she gave me to keep her debt from drowning me. It felt less like betrayal than inheritance, badly spent.

The evening bell finished its crooked song. Somewhere below, the gate brothers would be barring the great doors against the dark, and the mountain—Thornfell, black and enormous beyond the temple walls, close enough some nights that its shadow seemed to lean against the windows—would settle into the silence we were named for.

I heard the commotion instead.

It began as voices at the gate, muffled, then sharpening. Not the ordinary quarrel of a beggar turned away; there was a wrongness in the pitch. I set down my pen because my hand made the decision before I could stop it, and I disliked my body already for its curiosity.

Through the cloister arch I saw them: two gate brothers half-carrying a figure between them, and even across the courtyard I could tell the figure was ruined. A pilgrim, from the dust-gray cloak and the staff they'd dropped somewhere behind them. But pilgrims arrived at the Temple of Quiet Hours worn, hungry, footsore. This one had been *unmade*. Their skin showed a lattice of fine red lines, like cracks in overfired clay, and the lines were bright. Moving, almost. As though something beneath the flesh still glowed on a slow banked fire.

"Get the infirmarer," one of the brothers called. "No—don't touch the—" He stopped himself, which told me everything. You don't touch what you're afraid of.

I should have gone back to my alcove. Invisibility is safety; I had written that law into myself line by line for six years. Instead I crossed the courtyard with my ink-stained hands hidden in my sleeves, the way I hid everything, and stood near enough to see without being seen.

Up close the pilgrim was worse. Old, or aged suddenly past their years—a woman, I thought, her face seamed and gray as ash. Her lips were cracked, and when she breathed there was a faint smell off her, not of rot but of a forge: hot iron and quench-water. The red lines pulsed once, twice, keeping time with nothing.

Her eyes opened. They found me instantly, in a courtyard full of brothers, the way a compass finds north, and I understood with cold certainty that whatever she was looking for, she had been looking since long before this gate.

"You," she said. Her voice was a whisper dragged over coals. "Emrys."

My name. My actual name, spoken plainly, in a temple where I had worked six years to be a straight unremarkable line. Brothers turned to look at me. I felt each gaze land like a dropped stone.

"I don't—" I started, the reflex of smallness, deny deny deny.

She reached out. The brothers flinched away from her arm, and no one stopped her, because fear makes room the way nothing else does. Her hand closed around mine and pulled it from my sleeve, and her grip was furnace-hot, unbearable, and I did not pull away—I have asked myself every day since why I did not pull away—and she pressed something into my palm.

Iron. Small, round-edged, warm as a stone left too long by a fire. And burning into it, into *me*, a mark: a branching shape, thorned, like a tree drawn by someone who had only ever seen lightning.

"The sigil finds its bearer," she said, and the red lines under her skin flared so bright the brothers cried out and shielded their eyes. "It waited. Gods are patient. Men broke the pact, but the god kept the door." Her breath rattled. "When the mountain speaks, child, listen. It has been waiting longer than your silence."

"What is it? What's happening to you?" My voice cracked on the last word, and I hated that too.

She smiled, and her teeth were gray. "I carried it forty years and it never burned me. Because I wasn't the one." Her eyes went past me, up, to the black shoulder of Thornfell rising over the wall, and something in her face was terror and relief in equal measure, the way a drowning man looks at shore. "It knew your name before I did."

Then the light went out of the lines all at once, like a lamp pinched, and her weight came forward onto my arm, and she was gone between one breath and the next, quietly, in a temple built for exactly that.

They pried the sigil out of my hand eventually—or rather, Abbot Corvane's steward tried, and found that while the iron could be lifted from my palm, the mark remained beneath it, printed into the skin in a fine silver-white scar, cool to every eye but mine. To me it still hummed, faintly, like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

They laid the pilgrim out in the mortuary chapel. No one knew her name. No one knew how she had come through passes that were closed to honest travelers this season. No one asked me anything, which was somehow worse than being questioned—the way the brothers' eyes slid off me, off the mark, off the whole impossible hour, as if looking too long might catch fire.

I went back to my alcove and sat before my unfinished page until the candle guttered, and the grain accounts swam, and the sigil in my palm beat its small slow heat against my fingers like a second pulse that was not mine.

I did not sleep. Of course I did not sleep.

Somewhere past midnight I gave up on the pretense and climbed the stair to the scriptorium gallery, the high walk where the temple kept its oldest maps, because I wanted—needed—to look at Thornfell. From the gallery window the mountain filled the world: black rock, snow-filled couloirs, the old watchtower ruins on its knee that appeared on no chart the temple owned, though my mother's voice in my memory could have traced every ledge of them. The smuggler's mountain. The silent mountain. For six years it had been scenery, a wall behind my small careful life.

Tonight it looked like it was listening.

I pressed my marked palm flat against the cold stone sill without meaning to. The sigil warmed. And because I was alone, and terrified, and so tired of being a straight unremarkable line, I whispered the thing I had never let myself say aloud in any temple:

"If anyone is listening—if anything up there remembers us—tell me what she meant. Tell me what you want. Because I am owed nothing, I know it, I know what I'm worth, but she put your mark in my hand and I didn't ask for it, so just this once—"

I stopped. It was a half-prayer at best, the kind you make to no one, the kind that costs nothing because no one hears it. I had made a thousand like it over my mother's sickbed, into the dark of the dormitory, into the space between heartbeats. Prayers shaped specifically to go unanswered.

The mountain answered.

Not in words at first—in pressure. The air in the gallery thickened, went taut as a drumhead, and every candle in the scriptorium below bent sideways though there was no wind. Frost bloomed across the window glass in a pattern I recognized before I understood it: branching, thorned, the exact shape printed into my palm, drawn in ice across the whole pane, growing outward like the mountain itself was reaching through it.

And then, inside my skull, inside the scar in my hand, a voice—not sound, *weight*, vast and slow and terribly awake, like bedrock deciding to speak:

**YOU PRAYED.**

I staggered back from the window. The frost-thorn spread another finger's width. Below me, in the courtyard, a dog began to howl, and then every dog in the temple yard, and beyond the walls, faintly, dogs in the town, a rising chorus of animal alarm rolling down from the heights as if the whole slope had been kicked awake.

**SIX YEARS YOU KEPT YOUR HEAD BENT. SIX YEARS I LISTENED TO THE LEDGER IN YOUR HEART AND COULD NOT ANSWER. A HALF-PRAYER IS STILL A KEY, EMBRYS ISHIKAWA.**

It knew. It knew my name, my mother's name braided into mine, the tally behind my eyes, maybe the ledger in my boot, maybe every route my mother ever poured into me—and I felt the terrible thrill of it, the thing I will spend the rest of this story trying to confess honestly: underneath the dread, underneath the animal wish to be small again, some starved part of me *rejoiced*. Six years unseen. Six years of being a line in someone else's account. And here was a voice older than the temple, older than the usurper's laws, saying my full name into the center of my chest like it was setting a seal.

Being seen is a wound. Being seen after so long is a wound and a feast both, and I did not know yet which one would kill me.

"What do you want?" I whispered. The frost-thorn filled the window now, beautiful and awful, silver branches against black sky. "What am I supposed to do?"

The voice gathered itself, and I felt its size then, truly felt it—the patience of a thing that had watched empires grow over its grave and say nothing—

**CARRY IT TO THORNFELL'S ROOT. CARRY IT TO THE GATES OF ASH. THE PACT IS BROKEN, LITTLE SCRIBE, AND THE INK OF THE WORLD IS DRYING. YOU WHO KEEP ACCOUNTS WILL WRITE THE LAST ONE.**

"And if I refuse?"

The pause was not cruel. That was the horror of it. It was the pause of something that already knew my answer better than I did.

**THE MARK CHOSE YOU BEFORE THE PILGRIM DID. REFUSAL HAS NEVER BEEN AMONG ITS DOORS. ONLY DELAY.**

The pressure lifted. The candles straightened. The frost on the window held its shape a moment longer, a thorned tree hanging in the night, and then, from the topmost branch downward, it melted—as if weeping—and ran in thin bright lines down the glass like tears, or like the red seams that had laced the dead pilgrim's skin.

Below, the dogs fell silent all at once, which was worse than the howling.

I stood in the dark gallery with my marked hand pressed to my chest, shaking, and I made myself think the scribe's thoughts, the safe thoughts: *Inquisitors burn god-touched things. Vaelric's men pay temple informants in forgiveness of debts. By morning the abbot will know, if he doesn't already; the gate brothers talk; the dead woman talked; my name is spoken and names travel.*

Six years of invisibility, undone in one evening by a dying stranger and a mountain that had apparently been reading my mail.

Far below, a light moved through the abbot's quarters. Then a second light. Then the soft, urgent knock of a brother waking Corvane before dawn, which no one did for anything small.

The sigil in my palm pulsed once, patient as the pilgrim had said. Patient as mountains.

I watched the lights in the abbot's window, and I understood that whatever kindness or cruelty came with morning, the small careful life I had built letter by letter was already ash—and that the voice in the mountain had known it would be, had perhaps arranged it, the way a factor arranges a route.

*You who keep accounts will write the last one.*

I had sold three roads through Thornfell to keep my mother's debt alive.

Now the mountain itself was calling in a debt of its own.
