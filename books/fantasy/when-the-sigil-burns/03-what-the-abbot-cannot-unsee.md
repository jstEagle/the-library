## 3. What the Abbot Cannot Unsee

The sigil did not cool.

Emrys had wrapped it in linen by the second bell of the morning, and by the third the cloth had browned, then blackened, then crumbled away like a leaf left too long in a fire. They tried cold water from the laundry basin. The water boiled in the bowl—actually boiled, a thin skin of steam rising off it while the brothers splashed through the cloister outside, oblivious—and the mark beneath their skin glowed faintly through the flesh, a lattice of light like veins seen through a candle flame.

So Emrys did what they had always done with unbearable things. They went to work.

The scriptorium smelled of oak galls and tallow and the particular dustiness of vellum that had waited centuries to be useful. Emrys sat at their usual place at the end of the long table—the worst seat, nearest the draft from the door, farthest from the warm light of the high windows—and copied figures from one ledger into another. Grain shipments. Candle wax. The interest accrued against a dead woman's name for eleven years running.

*Fourteen years left,* they thought, pen moving in small, disciplined strokes. *If I sell three more routes this season, twelve.*

Their hand shook. The line wavered.

They looked down at their palm, half expecting the ink to have caught fire where it touched the skin. It hadn't. But the sigil had spread—or no, not spread; it had *settled*, the way sediment settles in water, as if it had always been there and was only now done arriving. It was smaller than it had been last night, when the pilgrim had pressed it into them with fingers hot enough to brand. Smaller, deeper, neater. A thorned circle the size of a thumbnail, sunk below the surface of the skin, glowing when Emrys's pulse quickened and dimming—never quite dying—when they forced themselves calm.

*Calm,* they told themselves. *You have survived worse than being marked.*

But they hadn't, actually. They had survived debt, hunger, the slow humiliation of indenture, the particular loneliness of being an orphan raised by an institution that measured them in currency. All of those were weights. This was a door, and something on the other side of it was knocking.

"Brother Ishikawa." Brother Anselm paused beside the table, ink-stained and incurious, holding a folio. "The abbot asks for you. His study. After terce."

Emrys's blood went very quiet. "Did he say why?"

"He never says why." Anselm moved on, already forgetting.

Emrys looked at the ledger. The numbers swam. In the margin of the page, unbidden, their pen had drawn a small curling shape—a thorn. They scratched it out so hard the nib tore the vellum.

*He knows,* said a voice in them that was older than hope. *Someone saw the light under your cell door. Someone heard you talking to the mountain. He knows, and he has sent for the inquisitors already, and you will die in a cart on the road to Vaelric's capital with your hands bound and your mouth full of straw—*

"No," Emrys whispered, to the voice, to the ledger, to the sigil burning its patient ring in their palm.

Then they bent their head over the page and copied figures until the bell rang for terce, because discipline was the only prayer they had ever been permitted.

* * *

Abbot Corvane's study occupied the tower's south corner, where three windows met and the light came in from every direction at once, so that nothing could hide in shadow. Emrys had always assumed this was for reading. Standing in it now, they understood it was for *inspection*. Corvane liked to see things clearly. It was his one cruelty and his one virtue, and he had never learned where one ended.

He was an old man made almost entirely of angles, tall once and now stooped into something resembling a question mark, as if he had spent sixty years asking the world a question and had finally begun to bend beneath the silence of the answer. His hands were enormous, a scribe's hands gone knuckled with age, and they lay flat on the desk before him like two dead birds.

On the desk, between the hands, lay Emrys's mother's contract of indenture.

"Sit," Corvane said.

Emrys sat. The chair was hard and honest. Through the window behind the abbot, the Thornfell mountains stood in their eternal gray vigil, and Emrys could not stop their eyes from flicking to them—to the peak called the Old Man's Tooth, where last night the storm had gathered out of a clear sky, where the thunder had spoken in a voice that knew their name, where they had whispered *help me* to no one and been answered by *everything*.

"You slept badly," Corvane observed.

"Yes, Father."

"Your cell smells of smoke."

"The lamp guttered."

Corvane regarded him. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, the eyes of a man who had read so much by candlelight that the color had burned out of them. "Emrys," he said, and it was the first time in eleven years he had used their given name instead of their title, and the sound of it frightened Emrys more than shouting would have. "Show me your hand."

The sigil flared. Emrys felt it happen—a bloom of heat up the wrist, into the forearm, a warmth behind the breastbone like swallowed sunlight—and they saw the abbot see it: the glow rising through the skin of their folded hand where it rested on their knee, gold-white, unmistakable, the light of something that had never belonged in a temple dedicated to quiet hours.

There was no point denying it. Corvane had already seen. That was written across his face—not anger, not disgust, but a grief so old and so practiced that it had worn smooth, like a stair trodden by ten thousand feet.

"How long?" the abbot asked.

"Since the night before last. A pilgrim at the gate. He was burning from inside—he pressed it into my palm and—" Emrys stopped. Their throat had closed.

"And it spoke to you." Not a question.

"The mountain spoke." Emrys heard their own voice go thin and defensive, the voice of a child caught with stolen bread. "I prayed. I didn't mean to pray. It was half a prayer, barely words, and something answered me. By name."

Corvane nodded slowly. He turned the contract on the desk around, so it faced Emrys, and slid it across the wood.

"Eleven years you have served this house," he said. "You copy faster than any brother here. You ask fewer questions than any brother here. In eleven years I have never once had cause to correct you." He paused. "Do you know why I never asked why you were so quiet?"

Emrys said nothing.

"Because I was afraid of the answer," Corvane said. "A young person who makes themselves invisible in a house of God is either very holy or very frightened, and I did not want to learn which, because whichever it was, I would have been obligated to do something about it. And I am a coward, Emrys. I have been a coward since before you were born. It is the reason I am still alive, and the reason this temple still stands, and I confess it to you now as a sin I intend to keep committing."

He drew a breath. Outside, the bells finished ringing terce, and the sound rolled away down the mountain and was lost.

"There is a man in the village," he said. "Came yesterday evening, while you were... whatever you were doing. Gray coat, no insignia, very polite. He asked whether we had received any pilgrims recently. He described one." Corvane's huge hands turned over, palms up, empty. "He described the pilgrim who died at our gate. He knew what was burning in him. And he asked—I want you to understand how politely he asked—whether anyone had touched the body."

The study was very bright. Emrys felt the sigil pulsing with each heartbeat, and knew, with the terrible clarity of prey, that somewhere down in the village a man in a gray coat was feeling exactly the same pulse, in a relic held in a gloved hand, growing warmer with every step he took toward the mountain.

"Maren," Emrys said. They didn't know where the name came from. Perhaps the sigil. "Her name is Maren."

Corvane went still. "You know this."

"I know what the god knows." Emrys laughed—a broken, awful sound. "I don't *want* to know what the god knows, Father. I want to copy ledgers. I want fourteen years to be twelve. I want to be nobody. Being nobody kept me alive for twenty-three years."

"I know," Corvane said softly. "It is the finest art I ever taught you, and I am ashamed of it."

He rose. He crossed to the window, and stood looking at Thornfell for a long moment, and Emrys watched the old man's shoulders and thought: *he is deciding how to dispose of me. A locked cell. A boat. A grave.* The temple of Quiet Hours had served Vaelric's regime for eleven years precisely because it asked no questions and buried its small secrets efficiently.

Then Corvane opened a drawer, took out a key, and unlocked the iron box that sat chained to the leg of his desk—the box every novice speculated about and no brother had ever seen opened. From it he withdrew a purse, heavy with coin, and a folded paper sealed with plain wax, and laid them on the desk beside the contract.

Then he took up a knife—a letter opener, bone-handled, ancient—and cut the contract of indenture nearly in two. One clean stroke, through the debt column, severing eleven years of accumulated interest from the fourteen years of servitude remaining.

"One year," Corvane said. "I forgive one year. It is a fraction, and it is insulting, and it is all I can make it worth without drawing attention." He pushed the pieces toward Emrys. "The coin is four months' wages. The paper is a letter of passage to the free cities under a name that is not yours. There is a carter leaving before dawn. You will be under the sacking in his cart."

Emrys stared at the ruined contract. "Why?"

"Because I cannot protect you." The abbot's voice did not waver, but his hands, picking up the pen, trembled like leaves. "And because if the inquisitor finds you here, he does not take only you, Emrys. He takes the house. Every brother. The ledgers, the relics, the children in the almonry. Vaelric's law requires that any house which sheltered a bearer be purged." He wrote as he spoke, fast, in the cramped cipher of temple accountants. "So you see, sending you away is both mercy and arithmetic."

"And if I refuse to go? If I stay and face him?"

"Then I will denounce you myself," Corvane said quietly, "and weep at your trial, and watch you burn, and pray afterward for forty days. That is what my cowardice costs, Emrys. It always pays in other people." He set down the pen. "One night. You have one night, and then the gates are barred, and anything left inside these walls belongs to the fire that follows you. Do you understand?"

Emrys understood. It was exile dressed in kindness. It was the only kind of kindness the age allowed. They picked up the severed contract, the purse, the letter—objects that weighed almost nothing and everything—and stood.

"Father," they said at the door. "The pilgrim knew my name. Before I spoke. Before he saw my face. He said it like he'd been sent to find me specifically. What did he think I was?"

Old Corvane did not turn from the window. When he answered, his voice was so low Emrys almost missed it.

"He thought you were the covenant," the abbot said. "And after last night, little scribe, so do I."

* * *

*(Here the record shifts, briefly and without apology, into the mind of another—for the truth of that night has two witnesses, and only one of them fled.)*

Abbot Corvane stood at his window long after the boy had gone, watching the dark bulk of Thornfell against the stars, and admitted to himself the thing he had admitted to no one in thirty-six years.

He had seen a sigil before.

He had been a young monk, newly professed, when the last purge reached the northern temples—the great burning, the years when Vaelric's men carried braziers of salt-fire from shrine to shrine and gave the old gods' servants the choice of the stake or the ledger of recantation. Corvane had chosen the ledger. He had written, in his beautiful hand, the required sentence—*the gods of the old covenant are dead, and their marks are superstition*—and he had believed it right up until the night they brought in the girl from the shepherd villages, sixteen years old, marked from brow to wrist in thorned silver, who would not recant because, she said, simply and terribly, *he still answers me.*

They burned her in the temple yard. Corvane had been ordered to attend, and he had attended, and at the moment the fire took her she had turned her head—not in pain, in *recognition*—and looked directly at him, at the coward in the crowd with ink on his fingers, and smiled.

He had dreamed of that smile every night for thirty-six years. Tonight, watching a golden glow fade down the tower stair, he dreamed it waking.

"Forgive me," he said to the mountains, to the girl in the fire, to the boy whose hand he had just failed. "Forgive me. It is all I know how to ask."

Below, in the village, a lantern moved along the road toward the temple gate. Gray coat. Unhurried. Patient as arithmetic.

Corvane snuffed his candle and began, methodically, to burn his own correspondence.

* * *

Emrys did not sleep.

They spent the dark hours in their cell doing the accounting of a whole life, because that was the only ritual they trusted. Eleven years of service: one year forgiven, the rest erased by fire if they stayed, by flight if they went. Four months' wages. A false name—*Marren of the Lowfields*, an irony so bitter they almost laughed. Their mother's ring, kept in a knot of cloth at the bottom of their trunk. Their pen. Their inkhorn. Three smugglers' routes committed to memory and nowhere else, because paper could be searched and memory, until this week, could not.

*Until this week.* They looked at their palm. In the darkness the sigil glowed steady as a banked coal, and when they held it near their heart they could feel it keeping time.

"What do you want from me?" they whispered.

The warmth deepened. Not words. Not yet. Something more like a direction—the way a compass needle is not a word but is impossible to ignore.

North. Toward the pass. Toward Thornfell's high roads, which appeared on no lawful map, which Emrys knew anyway, because knowing unlawful things was the one talent they had ever been paid for.

"No," Emrys told it. "I'm going to the free cities. The coast. Somewhere with a harbor and a hundred thousand strangers to disappear into. That's the plan. Plans have kept me alive."

The sigil pulsed once, gently, like a parent humoring a child, and Emrys hated it with a hatred so close to terror that they could not tell the difference, and packed their trunk anyway.

In the gray hour before dawn they went down to the scriptorium one last time—not for anything, just to look—and found the ledgers waiting in their neat stacks, the day's work assigned in Brother Anselm's tidy hand, the world of small countable things continuing without them as though they had never occupied the worst seat by the door. They stood a long moment among the smell of oak galls and tallow.

Then they wrote, on a scrap of waste vellum, a single line, and left it weighted on the abbot's desk:

*The debt is paid in the only coin I have. Pray for me the way cowards pray—quietly, and too late.—E.*

The carter's cart stood in the courtyard, sacking heaped and stinking of turnips. Emrys climbed under it with their trunk and their mother's ring and their four months of wages, and the cart lurched out the gate as the first bell rang, and the walls of the Temple of Quiet Hours sank behind them like a ship going down.

They had gotten perhaps a mile along the valley road, dawn barely graying the sky, when the sigil flared so hot they gasped aloud—and not northward this time, but backward, down the road, toward the temple. Warning or summons or fear; they couldn't tell.

The carter reined in. "Someone on the road," he muttered. "Odd hour for walking."

Emrys parted the sacking a finger's width.

A man stood in the middle of the track where the road bent beneath the cliffs. He wore armor under a travel-stained cloak—Emrys could see the shape of it, the sword, the way he stood—and he was watching the cart come toward him without moving out of the way, and there was blood dried on his gauntlet, and his face, when the growing light found it, was the face of someone who had stopped expecting to live a long time ago and was merely curious about the details.

"Hold," the knight said to the carter, and put his hand on the wagon's rail, and turned his eyes—gray, exhausted, terribly awake—on the heap of sacking where Emrys lay hidden.

"I'm looking for the Thornfell road," he said. "And I think you know the way."

Behind them all, far down the valley, a second rider had come onto the road, wearing a gray coat, and was not hurrying at all.
