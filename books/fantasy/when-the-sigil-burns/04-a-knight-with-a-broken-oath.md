## 4. A Knight with a Broken Oath

Ser Aldric Vane had been following the temple scribe for two days before he finally decided to stop.

Not because the scribe was difficult to follow. Quite the opposite. Emrys Ishikawa moved through the foothill country like smoke through a keyhole—slipping off roads at the first distant hoofbeat, walking in drainage ditches, doubling back twice through a pine stand until their own trail was a knot no honest tracker would bother untying. Aldric had watched smugglers work the Thornfell approaches for six years of soldiering, and he had never seen anyone better at being unnoticed. That was precisely the problem. He had been noticed anyway, or rather he had failed to notice that he *couldn't* be noticed, and on the third morning, crouched behind a fallen fir with frost melting through the knees of his stolen wool trousers, he understood that the scribe had known about him since the river crossing and had simply been letting him walk behind like a patient, useless dog.

The realization should have stung. Instead it made him laugh—a short, rusty sound, the first in weeks—and the scribe's head came up across the hollow like a deer catching wind of him.

There was no point running after that. They both knew it. So instead of fleeing, Emrys did something Aldric found far more unsettling: they sat down on a rock, pulled a heel of bread from their pack, and waited for him to approach, chewing with the weary deliberation of someone adding one more inconvenience to a very long ledger.

"You're not very good at this," the scribe said when he was close enough. Their voice was flat, unhurried. Up close they were younger than he'd expected, mid-twenties perhaps, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of wet slate that catalogued him in a single pass: sword, empty scabbard wear where a dagger should be, the missing tabard, the army boots worn down at the outer edge from a rider's stance. "Following people, I mean."

"I'm good enough," Aldric said. "You just let me catch you."

"Did I?" A flicker of something—not quite a smile. "Then we're both lying to each other already, and we haven't even exchanged names. This is going well."

He stopped three paces away, out of grabbing range, and let his hands hang loose at his sides. Old habit. The scribe tracked the movement too, and filed it away, and Aldric felt the strange vertigo of being read by someone who had clearly spent a lifetime reading dangerous people and surviving it.

"Aldric Vane," he said.

"I know." Emrys finished the bread and dusted their palms. "Ser Aldric Vane, formerly of the Third Lance, Vaelric's own. Deserted at Candlemere four months ago. There's a price on you—eight silver crowns, which is insulting, honestly, given what you cost to train. Half the border towns have your face nailed to a post." They tilted their head. "You're wondering how I know."

"The question occurred to me."

"I map routes for a living. Routes need safe houses, safe houses need gossip, gossip is cheaper than coin and spends better." The scribe stood, brushing frost from their coat with brisk economy. "So here's my offer, knight. You're going to ask me to guide you over Thornfell, because every pass you know is watched and both of us know it. And I'm going to say no, because you are a walking catastrophe with a bounty and a story, and I have eleven years left on an indenture that only stays survivable if nobody important ever learns I exist. Then you're going to threaten me, and then bribe me, and eventually one of us will do something regrettable. Or—" they shouldered their pack, "—we skip all that and you tell me why a man who burned his whole life down is hiking toward the mountains instead of away from them."

Aldric said nothing for a long moment. The wind moved through the pines, carrying the smell of resin and old snow.

"You knew about the bounty," he said slowly, "but you didn't sell me at the crossing. There was a garrison post at Miller's Ford. One shout and you'd be eleven years richer in time."

Something crossed Emrys's face then, quickly, gone again—the look of someone stepping around a hole in the floor they knew was there. "I don't collect for Vaelric," they said. "That's not arithmetic. It's principle." They turned and started walking. "Keep up or don't. But if you're coming, stop looming in trees like a guilty ghost. It's embarrassing for both of us."

* * *

They walked, and against every instinct Aldric had cultivated in four months of running, he kept up.

He told himself it was practical. The scribe was right about the passes; the two main routes over Thornfell had been sealed by Vaelric's order since midwinter, chained and garrisoned, and the third had collapsed into a gorge two springs back. A man alone could die very thoroughly in those mountains without ever meeting a soldier. He needed a guide. That was the transaction, clean as a blade: protection for passage. He had coin sewn in his belt, half of it stolen from his former comrades' pay chests on the night he ran, and he offered it at the first campsite, counting it out on a flat stone between them like a man laying down cards.

Emrys looked at the money the way a starving person looks at food they distrust.

"That buys passage," they said. "It doesn't buy questions. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"It doesn't buy obedience. If I say drop, you drop. If I say we go dark for three days and eat pine bark, you chew and complain quietly."

"Agreed."

"And if the sigil on your hand starts glowing, we're renegotiating everything."

Aldric looked down at his own hand, half-smiling at what he took for a joke, and saw Emrys wasn't smiling at all. They were staring past him, at the firelight, at nothing—or at some memory wearing nothing's shape—and their jaw was set in a way he would come to recognize as the scribe's entire philosophy of survival made visible: *I do not want this. I will survive it anyway.*

"No glowing," Aldric agreed gravely. "On my honor."

"Your honor deserted at Candlemere."

He laughed before he could stop himself—a real laugh this time, startled out of him—and Emrys almost smiled, caught it, killed it, and threw another stick on the fire as if the fire had asked for it personally.

They traveled north by paths that were not paths: sheep tracks that ended at cliff faces yet somehow continued along ledges Aldric would have sworn didn't exist; a dry riverbed under the stones of which someone, generations ago, had cut steps; a smuggler's stair hidden in the roots of a lightning-split oak, its rungs numbered in an old thief's cant that Emrys read aloud like scripture. Twice they hid from patrols in culverts and crevices so tight Aldric could feel the scribe's heartbeat through their pack straps, quick and disciplined, never panicked. The second time, the patrol passed so close he could hear the soldiers complaining about their boots, about the cold, about a captain who docked pay for unauthorized fires. Children of the empire, bored and frozen and utterly certain of the rightness of their cause. Four months ago he had been one of them. He wondered which of them would be ordered to do what he'd done, and which would do it.

When the boots had faded, Emrys crawled out of the culvert and did not look at him, but said, quietly, "You held your breath the whole time. I could feel it."

"Habit."

"From hiding?"

"From waiting," Aldric said, and did not explain, and Emrys did not ask, because they were both fluent in the grammar of things not said, and it was the first language either of them had learned.

* * *

It was on the fifth night, in the shadow of a ruined watchtower that predated the empire, that the honesty finally came—as honesty always did, bleeding out of a wound neither of them had planned to open.

They had argued. That was the surface of it. Emrys wanted to swing west around the high shoulder of Thornfell, adding two days to avoid a valley they called the Shepherd's Throat, and Aldric had insisted the direct route was worth the risk because every day added was a day for pursuit to close. Voices had risen. The scribe had called him a man who thought courage was a substitute for planning, and he had called them a coward who mistook hiding for wisdom, and the words had landed harder than either intended, because both were true, and both knew it.

Silence followed. Emrys sat with their back to the fire, arms around their knees, and Aldric sharpened a knife he did not need to sharpen, and somewhere on the mountain a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Candlemere," Emrys said at last, without turning around. "Your story says you burned it."

"Yes."

"But you're alive, and there's no smoke on your conscience that I can see, or you wouldn't sleep at all. So tell me what actually happened, because if I'm going to spend a week in the high passes trusting you with my life, I'd rather trust the truth than the rumor."

Aldric set the knife down. He looked at the fire for a long time. When he spoke, his voice came out level, which surprised him; he had expected it to break.

"There were forty-one children in that village," he said. "God-touched. Marked, like—" he flexed his right hand reflexively, though his palm bore nothing but callus, "—like stories say the old covenant children were marked. Born with signs. The priests of Quiet Hours had sheltered them; the temple sent records, petitions, proof that the marks were benign. Vaelric's chancery returned one order. Not expulsion. Not quarantine." He heard his own breath go out of him. "*Cleansing*, with witnesses. They wanted the Third Lance because we were decorated, loyal, photographed in the histories. Our fire would sanctify the policy."

"And the rest of your company obeyed."

"Nineteen of twenty lances obeyed." The number never softened. He had carried it four months and it had never once worn smooth. "Captain Sorvane gave the address first. Spoke about necessity, about the greater peace, about how mercy now was cruelty later. It was beautiful, the way it was beautiful. That's the thing no one tells you. It doesn't sound like madness. It sounds like arithmetic." He met Emrys's eyes across the fire. "I stood in formation and listened to arithmetic about burning children, and I felt the exact moment my oath turned to ash in my chest, and I drew my sword anyway—on my own captain, on my own brothers—and I got eleven of the forty-one out through the orchard before the flames took the rest. Eleven. Three died in the mountains after, of cold. Eight are scattered somewhere I'll never find them. And I have spent every night since doing the sums I despised Sorvane for doing, except mine come out the other way, and I don't know if that makes me better or just a different kind of monster with different numbers."

The fire cracked. Emrys hadn't moved, but their whole stillness had changed quality—from wariness to attention, the attention of someone who recognized what they were hearing.

"Why tell me the ugly parts?" they asked. "Eleven of forty-one. Most men would round up."

"Because you already know I'm a liar by trade—everyone in Vaelric's service is—and you need one thing in me that isn't negotiable. That's the number. I won't pretend it's forty-one. I won't pretend I'm clean." He paused. "And because you didn't sell me at Miller's Ford, and I've been trying to work out why ever since, and I think—I think you know what it costs to keep your hands clean inside a machine built to dirty them. I think you've paid that price every day of your life. Small prices. Invisible ones. But the same currency."

For a long moment Emrys said nothing. Then, softly, almost to themselves: "My mother borrowed from the temple to buy medicine. Twelve gold. She died anyway. The debt transferred to me, per the contract, per the law, and the interest compounds monthly, and I have spent nine years copying other people's accounts in a ledger that lists me as an asset." Their voice stayed perfectly even, which was its own kind of terrible. "So yes, Ser Aldric. I know the machine. Mine just wears kinder robes than yours."

*Kinder robes*, Aldric thought, remembering Sorvane's beautiful voice, and said nothing, because there was nothing to add, and because silence between two people who understood each other was not emptiness but a shared room.

Emrys unfolded at last and came around the fire, sitting closer than before—not close, but nearer—and looked at him with the appraising slate eyes.

"Here's my amended offer," they said. "Passage over Thornfell, full price, as agreed. But I'm changing the terms of what protection means. You don't guard my body. Anyone can guard a body." A pause, and something raw flickered under the flatness. "You guard my right to choose my own road. Whatever we meet—soldiers, gods, whatever's been making my hand burn since the pilgrim died at the temple gate—if I say we turn east, we turn east, and you don't ask the ledger why. In exchange I'll get us over mountains that have swallowed armies. Deal?"

"Your hand has been *what*?" Aldric said.

Emrys closed their eyes, as if they'd sworn an oath and broken it in the same breath. "Deal first," they said. "Confessions after. That's the smuggler's way."

"Deal," Aldric said, and meant it, and reached out to clasp forearms like a soldier because he didn't know any other way to seal a thing.

Their sleeve slid back as their arm turned. And there, in the firelight, on the scribe's palm: a mark like a brand of blackthorn, fine-lined and intricate, and as Aldric's skin touched theirs it flared—not with heat but with light, a slow red-gold pulse, once, twice, like a heart remembering its purpose—and along the ridge above them, faint but unmistakable, the mountain itself seemed to exhale.

Emrys snatched their hand back and stared at it, and then at him, stricken.

"That," they said hoarsely, "is going to be a problem."

Far below them in the valley dark, small and distant as a dropped ring, a light answered—one white flame among the pines, then another, moving, searching, patient as winter. Aldric knew that discipline of movement. He had trained it into men himself.

"Inquisitors," he breathed. "They're not following our trail." His eyes went to the burning sigil in the scribe's palm. "They're following *you*."
