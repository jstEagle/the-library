## 5. Roads Only Smugglers Remember

The map I kept in my head had no paper twin, and that was the point.

For six years I had sold fragments of it—never whole, never the same fragment twice—to men who paid in temple coin and never asked why a scribe knew where the salt caravans slipped past the toll-stones at Greywater. They assumed corruption. Corruption was a comfortable explanation; it asked nothing of them. The truth was stranger and quieter: I had learned the mountains the way my mother had learned debts, line by line, interest compounding in the margins, until the whole shape of Thornfell lived behind my eyes like a second skeleton.

Now that skeleton was walking, and it was walking with a knight who thought maps were things you could burn.

"We should cut east here." Ser Aldric Vane stood at the fork below Raven's Tooth, his broken sword slung across his back like an apology he'd stopped making. East was the pilgrim road. Wide, graded, marked with shrine-cairns every league. Also watched—the way roads are watched when a kingdom wants its travelers counted. "There's a garrison at Stonebridge, but we can pass as pilgrims."

"We can," I said, "if we enjoy being described to inquisitors by every toll-keeper between here and the coast." I pointed north, to a seam in the rockface that most eyes would read as shadow and cliff-fall. "We go through the Hollow Pass."

Aldric looked where I pointed for a long moment. "There is no pass there."

"There is no *road* there," I corrected. "The pass has always been there. Roads are just what people agree to remember."

He turned to study me then, and I felt the look like weather. He had the habit of listening with his whole body—a soldier's discipline or a penitent's, I hadn't decided which—and when he finally nodded, he nodded the way men nod at funerals: accepting something they don't understand and won't argue with yet.

"Lead, then."

I put my hand against the cold stone to find the first hold, and the sigil flared.

Not painfully—not anymore, not since the third night, when I'd learned the difference between the burn of warning and this other heat, low and certain, like a hand closing around mine from inside my own palm. It warmed when I chose right. That was the word for it, though I hated the word. When my fingers found holds no one else would have found, when my eye picked the true seam out of a hundred false ones, the mark on my skin glowed faint gold beneath the wrappings, and some vast attention leaned closer, pleased.

*Good,* said the warmth, without words. *This way. This way.*

I snatched my hand back from the rock.

"Emrys?" Aldric had half-turned, one hand drifting toward his sword out of pure reflex. Six days on the road together and he still flinched at my silences like they were ambushes.

"The stone's loose," I lied, and climbed.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being chosen: it feels exactly like being used, except warmer.

I had spent my whole life being small on purpose. In the Temple of Quiet Hours, invisibility was not a misfortune but a craft, and I had practiced it the way monks practice chanting—daily, devoutly, until it became breath. Keep your ledgers immaculate. Keep your questions few. Sell your secrets in portions too small to be worth following. A person no one remembers owes nothing, fears nothing, loses nothing but everything they never let themselves want.

And then a dying pilgrim had pressed a burning god into my palm, and now every old path I took lit me up like a lamp in a dark house, and something enormous on the other side of the world said *yes, yes, this way* as if I were a hound doing cleverly what it was bred to do.

By midday we were deep in the Hollow Pass, and the walls had narrowed to a slot of sky. This was smuggler country. No cairns, no shrines, no cut steps—just the routes my mother's debt-collectors' couriers had used, which she had memorized and passed to me in fragments disguised as bedtime stories, because even dying women in debt-towns know the value of an unwatched road. There was a shelf of stone above the second gorge where you could stand and watch the pilgrim road wind along the valley floor, tiny travelers crawling like ants down a blade, each one countable, taxable, stoppable.

I stood on that shelf and did not look down at them. I looked instead at my wrapped palm, where the warmth pulsed in time with my pulse, patient as interest accruing.

"You hate it," Aldric said.

I hadn't heard him climb up. For a big man he moved quietly, another thing that didn't fit his story, and I was starting to collect a list of those things. "Hate what?"

"That." He nodded at my hand. "You keep checking it like a wound and then hiding it like evidence. You hate it."

"It steers me." The words came out harder than I meant them to. Good. Hard was honest. "Every time I take an old path, it warms. Every time I even think about the pilgrim road, it goes cold as riverbed stone. It has opinions about my route, Ser Aldric. It is very free with them."

"And that frightens you."

"Frightens me?" I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "No. Frightened is for people who might lose something. I'm a debtor, ser. Everything I have is already owed. What it does—" I pressed my fist against my chest, against the mark, feeling the heat answer even through the cloth, even through the bone. "What it does is make me a puppet and call it a calling. It puts my own knowledge in my hands and then applauds, as if the hands weren't mine first. I learned these passes before any god bothered to notice I existed. I earned them route by route, year by year, selling pieces of myself to keep my mother's grave uncluttered by creditors. And now something ancient and enormous wants to walk me through them like I'm its favorite horse on a road it built."

The echo came back off the gorge walls and made my anger sound larger than I was. Below us, an ant-traveler on the pilgrim road paused, looked up at the empty cliff, and moved on.

Aldric sat down on the shelf, slowly, armor clinking, and was quiet for long enough that I almost left him there.

"When I was a boy," he said at last, "on my father's farm, there was an ox we plowed with. Big grey beast, gentle as bread. And every spring, when we put him in the yoke the first time, he'd plant all four feet and refuse. Just refuse. Half a day, sometimes. My father swore he wasn't stubborn—he was *reminding* us. Reminding us he wasn't born to the yoke." Aldric turned a stone over in his fingers. "Then he'd lean in, and pull, and do the work better than any ox in the valley. But never once did he forget to refuse first."

"Are you calling me your father's ox?"

"I'm saying the refusing matters." He set the stone down carefully, as if placing it on a grave. "Whatever that mark thinks you are, whatever it wants you to walk toward—you're allowed to plant your feet first. Maybe you're required to. Maybe that's the part it's actually testing." He stood, wincing, and looked back down the way we'd come, where the day's backtrack lay folded into shadow. "Come on. The light won't hold."

I followed him down from the shelf, and I noticed—I made myself notice—that I chose the next path myself. Not the warmest one. Mine.

The sigil went cool as a coin in winter, and I walked on it gladly.

* * *

We camped that night in a smuggler's cache I'd known since I was nine years old: a hollow behind a waterfall curtain, dry-floored, ringed with soot-black stones where generations of men who dealt in untaxed goods had cooked their meals. Someone had left an iron pot wedged under a ledge, decades ago, and it was still there, and finding it still there had been the closest thing to religion I'd known before the pilgrim died at the temple gate.

Aldric cooked barley and dried mutton in the old pot, competently, silently. He had a way of doing camp work like a man serving penance—as if each chore were a small installment on a debt I was only beginning to guess the size of. I sat with my back to the rock and unwrapped my palm to look at the mark by firelight.

It had cooled. It stayed cool while I watched it, which somehow felt like watching a liar hold still.

"Tell me about the routes," Aldric said suddenly. "The ones you sold. Who bought them?"

"Why? Planning to report me to someone?"

"Planning to understand you." He didn't look up from the fire. "You've sold smuggler routes to pay a temple debt. You know the mountains better than any army scout I ever marched with. And you hate the god who's asking you to use that knowledge for something bigger than staying alive." He glanced up, firelight moving on the ruined side of his face—the scar he never explained. "That's not a coward's profile, Emrys Ishikawa. Cowards don't resent puppets. Puppets don't resent anything. Only someone who knows exactly how much their freedom costs resents having it spent for them."

I stared at him. The waterfall filled the silence with its white noise, and somewhere in it, distantly, I heard the mountain breathing—or told myself I did.

"My mother borrowed from the temple granary the year the blight took the barley," I said, because he had earned a piece of it, and because talking was easier than thinking. "Forty crowns. By the time she died it was two hundred and eleven. Interest, fees, the scribe's surcharge for maintaining her ledger—which was me, incidentally, charging myself for the privilege of recording my own family's ruin. She taught me the passes because she grew up running wool over the border before she married, and because she said a woman with a secret road can't be entirely owned." My thumb traced the edge of the sigil's scar tissue. "She was wrong about a lot of things, but I've been trying hard to prove her wrong about that one."

"And the buyers?"

"Factors. Caravan-masters. Once, a man whose rings cost more than my mother's entire debt—he wanted a way to move something out of Vaelric's new mint without it being weighed." I shrugged. "I sold them each one road, once. Never the same stretch twice. A road you sell twice is a road you lose forever, and a guide who sells the same road twice ends up in a gorge. So I rationed myself. Smallness, portioned out. Enough to slow the interest. Never enough to finish it."

"Never enough to be free."

"There's no amount that makes you free." I said it flatly, factually, the way I'd say *water runs downhill*. "That's the whole architecture of debt. It isn't designed to be paid. It's designed to be *carried*. The temple doesn't want my forty crowns back, ser. It wants me copying ledgers until my hands give out, grateful every day for the privilege of owing." I flexed my burned hand, watching firelight slide along the old scar-lines of ink stains that no pumice had ever fully lifted. "So yes. When your god warms my hand every time I choose correctly—when it rewards me like a dog for walking the paths my mother bled to teach me—I resent it. Because it's the same architecture. Bigger temple."

Aldric was quiet. Then he said, "Your mother sounds like she'd have liked Sefa."

"I don't know anyone named Sefa."

"No," he said, in a tone I couldn't read at all. "Not yet."

* * *

The trouble started the next morning, at the Weepstone Narrows.

There were two ways through. The low route ran along the streambed—faster, sheltered, invisible from above. The high route climbed the eastern wall through a crack called the Shepherd's Needle, exposed to the sky for half a mile, miserable in wind, but ending at a ridge from which you could see three valleys. Any smuggler in Thornfell would tell you: take the low road unless you need to see far, and never need to see far, because needing to see far means someone's looking for you.

My hand warmed for the high route.

I stood at the fork with water to my ankles and fire in my palm, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what the mark was telling me. Not *this way because it's safer.* This way because from the Needle you can see the pilgrim road for miles—and the mark wanted me to see what was coming up it.

"No," I said aloud.

Aldric, ten paces back, went still. "Emrys?"

"It wants the high road. It's steering again." I pulled the wrapping off my hand and held it up, and there in the grey morning light the sigil glowed through the burn-scar like a coal seen through ash—gold, insistent, patient. "Well, I won't. Low road. Streambed. Like any sensible smuggler."

The heat climbed.

It didn't hurt—that was the horror of it, it *still* didn't hurt—it simply became impossible to ignore, a brightness spreading up my wrist, and with it came something that was not a voice, was never a voice, was more like a memory of being spoken to by a mountain: an impression of vast, unhurried attention, and beneath it, unmistakably, something like sorrow. Like a parent watching a child reach for a stove.

"They're coming," I breathed. Not a deduction. A delivery. Knowledge arriving in me the way rain arrives in a field. "On the pilgrim road. Behind us. Soldiers—" I gripped my own wrist hard enough to bruise, fighting the knowing the way you fight a current. "I'm not a window. I'm not your—"

"*Emrys.*" Aldric had crossed the stream and taken my shoulders, and his voice had the flat calm men learn in battles. "Look at me. If soldiers are behind us, the low road is a trap—we'd be in a slot canyon with one exit and no sightlines. The Needle is the only way we see them before they see us. Is that what it's showing you?"

I wanted to say no. I wanted, with a purity of wanting that surprised me, to say that the god was lying, that the warmth was manipulation, that my refusal was my own and sacred and correct. But the knowledge sat in me like swallowed stone, and it agreed with the tactics, and worse—it agreed with the *smuggler's logic*, the cold arithmetic my mother had poured into my bones. Needing to see far means someone's looking for you.

"Yes," I said. "That's what it's showing me."

"Then we climb." His grip tightened once, briefly. "And when we're over, you and the god are going to have a conversation about the difference between steering and shoving. But we climb *now*."

We climbed.

The Shepherd's Needle was exactly as miserable as memory promised—wind that came off the high snowfields with intent, handholds glazed with frost, a half-mile of sky on our left shoulder. Twice I nearly lost my footing and twice the sigil flared hot at the exact moment of slip, steadying me with heat like a hand at my back, and I hated it even as it saved me, hated it *specifically*, the way you can only hate something that keeps being kind to you.

From the ridge top, breathless, we looked back down at the pilgrim road threading the valley floor—and there they were.

A column, perhaps two hundred strong, moving north. Not a patrol. A hunt. At its head rode riders in black-and-crimson, and even at this distance I could see the thing one of them carried: a staff crowned with a lens of smoked glass, angled continuously backward along their own trail, sweeping the mountainsides, the way a man sweeps a hearth for embers.

Searching. For heat.

The sigil in my palm pulsed once—slow, deliberate—and went cool, satisfied, quiet, like a cat settling after waking its household.

"They'd have caught us in the Narrows," Aldric said quietly. He was squinting at the column with a soldier's assessment, and something in his face told me he recognized the livery, though he said nothing about it. "Another hour, maybe less."

"I know." My voice sounded far away to me. I wrapped my hand, tight, three turns of cloth, as if bandaging could be a border. "That's what I can't forgive it, ser. It was right. It saved us. It knew the whole time, and it let me stand in that stream and refuse until refusing would have killed us both—and then it just *waited* for me to figure it out." I watched the distant lens of glass sweep the slopes below us and miss us by a thousand feet of sheer cliff. "It doesn't shove. That's the worst part. It arranges the world so that my own knowledge, my own mother's roads, my own survival—all of it agrees with where it wants me to go. And then it lets me call it choice."

"Maybe," Aldric said, "that's the only kind of choice there is. The world arranges itself, and you pick your road anyway." He turned from the view and shouldered his pack. "My father's ox again. The refusing is the prayer. The pulling is the answer."

"Your father sounds like a philosopher."

"He sounded like a man tired of being kicked."

* * *

We came down off the ridge at dusk into a country I knew only by reputation: the southern skirts of Thornfell, where the mountains softened toward foothills and the streams began to remember they were headed for rivers. Two days south and west lay Hollowbridge—a river town, a ferry town, a town where the Temple of Quiet Hours had no chapter-house and therefore no claim on me, and where, if my fragments of caravan-gossip could be trusted, excommunicated priests went to drink and argue and not be arrested for it.

I hadn't planned to say this aloud. But as we made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, Aldric looked at me across the small fire and said, "Where now? Your god's gone quiet. This one's yours to choose."

Mine to choose. He said it deliberately, I realized—handed it to me like something breakable.

I looked at my wrapped hand. Cool. Silent. Waiting, the way creditors wait, which is to say: not waiting at all, merely patient about what is already theirs.

"Hollowbridge," I said, and chose it myself, out loud, on purpose. "There's a priestess there. Or something like one. If anyone south of the mountains knows what this mark is—if anyone's ever seen one survive its bearer—she'll be the one."

Aldric fed a stick to the fire. "Sefa," he said, so softly the wind almost took it.

"You *do* know her."

"I knew of her." His face did the closed thing it did, the scarred side going shadowed. "Years ago. Before. Everyone in the army knew of the priestess who screamed at the purges until they took her orders away." He looked into the flames. "Let's hope exile's made her kinder than her reputation."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, a nightbird called, and the mountain listened to itself, and my hand lay cool and quiet in my lap like a debt deferred.

But as I drifted toward sleep, in that borderland where thought loosens its knots, I felt it one last time—not the heat, not the steering, but something smaller and stranger. An impression, faint as scent: of a road ahead bending gently toward a river town, of a door I had not yet seen already standing open, of three lives converging on a bridge with the terrible patience of water finding the sea.

*You may choose,* the mountain seemed to murmur, without malice, without apology. *Choose well. Choose fast. The man with the glass eye is learning your name.*

I slept badly, and dreamed of ledgers, and woke before dawn with my hand warm as a banked coal, pointing south.
