## 6. The Heretic of Hollowbridge

The river came into Hollowbridge the way sin comes into a congregation—slowly, then all at once, carrying everything downstream with it.

Sefa watched it from the window of her attic room above the dye-house, where the water ran green and violet and occasionally the bruised orange of a bad mordant batch, staining the pilings like a sermon nobody had asked for. Ten years ago she had preached in a cathedral of white stone with acoustics so perfect that a whisper at the altar reached the last pew. Now her parish was a room that smelled of alum and wet wool, and her congregation was the river itself, which listened to nothing and forgave less.

She was grinding ochre for the evening lamps when she heard the bells—not the temple bells of Meridian, which she still heard sometimes in dreams, but the small cracked bell of Hollowbridge's ferry shrine, rung only when someone needed a rite performed that no licensed cleric would touch.

A birth out of wedlock. A suicide's burial. A prayer to something old.

Sefa set down the pestle. Her hands had not trembled in ten years, and they did not tremble now, though something in her chest turned over like a boat taking water.

*Ten years,* she thought. *And still you come down those stairs like a girl.*

* * *

The ferry shrine stood on the near bank under a roof of river-smoothed stones, its walls hung with votive ribbons gone grey from damp. Sefa had tended it in secret since the year the Synod struck her name from every ledger in the free cities—excommunicata, anathema, silenced—and the town had pretended not to notice, because Hollowbridge had always understood that certain debts were paid off the books.

Three figures waited for her in the shrine's dimness.

Two she took in at once, the way you read weather. A big man, knight-built even in road-worn leathers, standing like someone who had learned to make himself smaller and hadn't quite managed it—a sword at his hip with the peace-knot still tied, which told her more than his face did. And beside him, slighter, hooded, with ink stains worked so deep into their fingers that no amount of scrubbing would ever lift them: a scribe. Temple-bred. The kind of person who flinched when spoken to kindly, because kindness always came with a bill.

The third figure made her stop in the doorway.

It was a corpse—or nearly one. An old woman laid out on the shrine's stone shelf, wrapped in a pilgrim's cloak gone stiff with dried sweat, her face the color of candle tallow. But it was not death that had marked her. Death leaves a body alone. This woman had been burned from the inside out, the way a wick burns after the wax is gone—scorched along the lines of her veins, faint traceries of char visible through the skin of her throat and wrists, as if something had caught fire in her blood and consumed her from within.

Sefa had seen this once before, eleven years ago, on the day before the Synod declared the old gods dead and herself a liar.

"Who is she?" Sefa asked.

"A pilgrim," said the scribe. Their voice was low, careful, the voice of someone who measured every word against a price list. "She died three days ago. On the mountain road." A pause. "Before she died, she gave me something."

"Show me."

The scribe hesitated. The knight shifted his weight, and Sefa saw him glance at the scribe—not possessively, not protectively exactly, but with the particular attention of a man watching a door he could not close.

Then the scribe pulled back the glove.

The light in the shrine changed.

Sefa would swear afterward that the votive flames all leaned toward the scribe's hand at once, like grass bending to wind. In the center of their palm sat a sigil, burned into the flesh itself, and it was not black or red or any color fire has a right to be. It was gold—the deep, patient gold of lamplight behind horn windows, of honey held up to the sun, of the first hour of the world. It pulsed. Not quickly, not frantically, but with the slow rhythm of something breathing, or keeping time, or waiting.

Sefa knelt.

She did not mean to. Her knees simply decided, ten years of exile notwithstanding, that this was what they were for.

"Don't—" the scribe started, backing up a step.

"Hold still." Sefa's voice came out rougher than she intended. She reached into her sleeve and drew out the thing she had sworn never to carry again: a lens of polished river-glass, banded in lead, that the Synod had issued to every ordained reader of signs before they had burned the rest of them. She had kept hers the way you keep a knife from a life you've left—buried, forgotten, impossible to throw away.

She held the lens over the sigil.

Through the leaded glass, the mark resolved into structure, and the structure resolved into language, and the language broke her heart all over again, because she recognized it. Every priestess had once been taught to recognize it, in the years before the teaching was forbidden. It was the Old Concordance—the script in which the covenant between gods and mortals had been written, back when the realms still remembered why the mountains stood where they stood. Not a spell. Not a brand. A *clause*. A single clause of a contract older than any kingdom, signed in living flesh.

"This," Sefa said quietly, "is not possible."

"I know," said the scribe. "Believe me. I've spent three days telling myself the same thing."

"No." Sefa looked up at them, and saw the exhaustion in their face, the sleeplessness, the terror worn smooth by repetition into something almost like calm. "You don't understand. I don't mean it shouldn't exist. I mean the last confirmed Covenant Mark burned out of its bearer four hundred years ago, and the bearer with it. The Synod teaches—I *taught*, before—that the marks ended because the gods ended. That Thorn and the Quiet Ones went silent because there was no one left to answer." She lowered the lens. "This mark isn't fading. It's *strengthening*. Which means either everything my whole church believes is true—"

"—or it isn't," said the knight.

They both looked at him. He had a voice like gravel rolled in wool, and eyes that had done arithmetic with terrible things.

"The old gods didn't die," he said. "They went quiet. Isn't that your heresy, priestess? I've heard it quoted in interrogation cells. 'The gods are not dead; they are silent; silence is not absence.'" His mouth twisted. "I carried a man's confession out of a burning village once. He'd been ordered to kill god-touched children, and he couldn't, so he prayed while the smoke came in, and he swore afterward that something answered. Not in words. In *weight*. Like a hand on the scale." He looked at the scribe's hand. "Is that what happened to you?"

The scribe's jaw tightened. "I prayed without meaning to. Half a prayer. Less than half." Their fingers curled closed over the sigil, and even from across the shrine Sefa saw the light leak out between them, gold as dawn through shutters. "Something answered anyway. Out loud. From the mountain."

"And it hasn't stopped talking since," Sefa said. It was not a question. She had seen the way the scribe flinched at silence—the specific flinch of someone who can no longer trust silence to be empty.

"How do you know that?" The scribe's eyes narrowed. Suspicion sat well on them, Sefa thought; it fit like a garment they'd worn so long it had shaped itself to their bones.

"Because you haven't slept. Because you keep your hand away from your body, the way you'd keep a wound away from cloth you didn't want stained. Because you agreed to bring her here—" a nod toward the dead pilgrim "—to a town whose only unlicensed cleric is a woman who was excommunicated for saying exactly what I'm about to say." Sefa rose, knees cracking, and met the scribe's gaze. "You didn't come to Hollowbridge for shelter. You came because you're losing an argument with yourself, and you wanted to lose it in front of a witness."

For a long moment the scribe said nothing. The river filled the silence, slapping the pilings, patient as a creditor.

"My name is Emrys Ishikawa," they said finally. "I copy ledgers for the Temple of Quiet Hours. I copied ledgers for nine years, and I mapped smuggler routes on the side, and I kept my head down, and I paid my mother's debts at six percent interest, and none of it—none of it—prepared me for a god using my mouth to say its own name." They unfolded their hand again, deliberately, like a merchant opening a case. The gold light breathed. "So tell me, priestess. What am I carrying?"

Sefa looked at the sigil for a long time.

Then she looked at the dead pilgrim, and did the thing she had trained ten years to stop doing: she let herself hope.

"You're carrying the terms," she said. "Of the oldest agreement there is. Before Vaelric. Before the Synod. Before the temples learned to sell prayers by weight." She touched the shelf beside the pilgrim's body, gently, the way you touch a grave. "The gods and the mortal realms made a pact at the beginning of things. We would remember them; they would hold the world together—the mountains up, the rivers in their banks, the oaths of men binding because the gods guaranteed them. The payment was memory. A piece of the bearer, given freely, each time the covenant was renewed. That's what the marks were. Receipts. And when the last bearer refused to pay—" she shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than the words "—the gods fell silent. Not dead. Silent. Waiting for someone to reopen the account."

"That's a story," Emrys said. But their voice had changed. It had gone thin, the way voices go when the speaker is holding something heavy and pretending it's light.

"It's a *ledger entry*," Sefa said. "The oldest one. Your church keeps fragments of it in a vault under the Temple of Quiet Hours, and calls them apocrypha, and pays two scribes a year to keep the dust off them. I know because I was one of them." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "You've probably copied them, Emrys Ishikawa. You may have copied them without knowing what they were. That's how it always works. The truth sits in the archive wearing a plain cover, and no one opens it until it walks out on its own legs and puts its hand in yours."

Emrys looked down at their palm. The gold light lay on their face from below, and Sefa watched them take inventory of themselves—the way they had clearly learned to take inventory of everything, counting costs, weighing interest—and watched the arithmetic fail.

"There's a price," Emrys said at last. Flatly. Not asking.

"There's always a price."

"I want to know it. All of it. Before I agree to anything."

"Then you already understand the covenant better than the last bearer did," Sefa said softly. "He wasn't offered the choice. Or he believed he wasn't. Refusal felt like freedom to him, and it damned four hundred years of silence onto the world." She turned to the knight. "And you? You've heard enough heresy for one night. Do you draw steel now, or do you sit down?"

The knight considered her with those arithmetic eyes. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter.

"I deserted Vaelric's army rather than burn children," he said. "One more excommunicate in this company hardly tips the scale. Ser Aldric Vane." He did not offer his hand, and Sefa understood why: some men's names arrived pre-stained. "We need passage east. Through Thornfell. And we need to move quietly."

"Then you've come to the wrong town and the right woman," Sefa said. "Hollowbridge sees everything the river brings, and the river brings everything eventually. Including, lately—" she paused, and chose her next words with the care of a woman setting down loaded cargo "—riders in grey. Two days ago. Asking after a scribe who fled the Temple of Quiet Hours, and a knight who broke his oath at Cinderfen. Vaelric's inquisitors don't travel in pairs unless they're very sure of their scent."

She watched the word land. *Inquisitors.* Watched Emrys go still in the particular way of prey animals—not frozen, but listening with the whole body. Watched Aldric's hand drift, casually, to the peace-knot on his sword.

"They have something," Emrys said quietly. "Don't they. A way to track the sigil."

"They have a relic," Sefa admitted. "Stolen from the vaults during the purges, they say. A shard of the last Covenant Mark, preserved in glass. It warms when it comes near a living bearer." She met Emrys's eyes. "Which means, child, that you are not hiding anymore. You are a lit candle in a dark house, and somewhere on this river there is a woman walking toward you with her hand cupped around a flame of her own."

The votive lights leaned. The river ran on, green and violet and indifferent, carrying everything downstream.

Above the dye-house, in Sefa's attic, an old travel-case waited under the floorboards with a lead-lined lens, a forbidden book, and ten years of a faith she had refused to bury. She thought about the weight of it. She thought about the Synod's sentence, still legally binding: return to the fold or be counted among the godless.

"Well," Sefa said aloud, to the corpse, the candle, the god she had spent a decade insisting was only sleeping. "It seems I'm counted either way. I might as well be counted on the right side of the river."

She blew out the lamps—except one, which she left burning before the pilgrim's body, because some debts of light should be paid—and picked up her staff.

"We leave before the morning tide," she said. "And Emrys Ishikawa? When the god speaks to you tonight—and it will—you tell me *everything* it says. Word for word. Because I have waited ten years to hear that voice again, and I will not have it paraphrased."

Emrys opened their mouth. Closed it. And in the space between, Sefa saw the exact moment the argument they had been losing for three days finally, mercifully, collapsed—

—which was when the ferry bell began to ring again. Frantically. Three strokes, pause, three strokes: the signal every child on the riverbank knew.

*Grey riders on the water.*
