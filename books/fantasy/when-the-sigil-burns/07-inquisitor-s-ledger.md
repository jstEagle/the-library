## 7. Inquisitor's Ledger

The relic sat on the desk like a dying coal.

Maren did not touch it. She had learned in her first year of service that the relics of the old faith rewarded curiosity with burns—small ones, precise, placed where the flesh was thinnest: the inside of the wrist, the soft hollow beneath the ear, the webbing between the fingers. Vaelric's inquisitors were taught to read pain as text. The relic had been teaching her for three days now, and its lesson was simple and getting louder.

It was a lump of glass no larger than a sparrow's egg, fused around something that might once have been bone, dredged from a riverbed outside Hollowbridge by a fisherman who had thought it pretty and sold it to a pawnbroker who had thought it worthless. It was neither pretty nor worthless. It was warm, and it was warming, and warmth in a dead thing meant one thing only in the Nine Realms.

A god was awake.

Maren dipped her pen and wrote in the ledger that would eventually reach the Usurper's own desk—or would not, depending on what she decided the Usurper needed to know. There was an art to that decision. Vaelric's empire ran on paper the way a body runs on blood, and every inquisitor was both vein and valve.

*Day forty-one since first anomaly,* she wrote. *Relic thermal drift continues. Rate of increase: steady, not exponential. Subject has not fled south toward the sea or east toward the free cities' walls. Subject is moving into Thornfell.*

She paused over the last line and considered it again, because it was the line that mattered, and because precision was the only form of devotion left to her.

*Subject is moving into Thornfell. Deliberately.*

* * *

Inquisitors of the Third Rank—the rank Maren had held for six years, and would hold until she died or until Vaelric found a use for her corpse—were not soldiers and were forbidden to behave like them. Soldiers broke things quickly and called it victory. Inquisitors understood that everything broken could be mended by whoever held the pieces, and so their work was acquisition: of documents, of debts, of names, of the small shames people carried like coins they were too embarrassed to spend.

Maren had acquired the temple records of Quiet Hours in four hours without drawing her blade or raising her voice. She had arrived at the gate in plain gray wool, presented letters of customs audit signed by a name she had invented that morning, and stood in the scriptorium while the under-abbot sweated through his robes. By the time the sun set she knew three things worth knowing.

First: a scribe named Emrys Ishikawa, indentured against a mother's debt of two hundred and eleven marks, had for six years supplemented their wages by selling maps of smuggler routes to a broker in the low quarter—a tidy little crime, forgivable, useful, exactly the kind of quiet corruption that kept temples running and kingdoms fed. Emrys Ishikawa had survived by being small. Maren wrote down the word *small* in the margin and underlined it twice.

Second: on the night the pilgrim died at the gate, someone had burned the ledger page recording Emrys's debt. Not torn out—burned, carefully, with a taper, by a hand that wanted no fragment legible. Someone in the temple hierarchy had tried to erase this particular scribe from the books. That was not mercy. Mercy would have forgiven the debt openly. Burning was fear wearing kindness as a disguise, and fear was the most reliable informant in any institution.

Third: the abbot himself, Corvane, gaunt and trembling, had given the scribe one night's head start and a purse of eleven marks. Eleven. Maren had turned the coin over in her palm in the abbot's presence and watched him flinch at the sound it made against her rings. Eleven marks was what you gave when you loved someone and were also a coward, which described most of the clergy in the Nine Realms and, if she were honest, most of everyone.

She had left Corvane alive and unpunished, and told him he owed the throne nothing further. He had wept. They always wept, and it always meant less than nothing, and she always noted it anyway, because a ledger that skipped entries was not a ledger.

* * *

The relic warmed another degree while she ate.

Maren ate alone, in the way she did everything, at a table in the back room of a coaching inn outside Hollowbridge, where the innkeeper had been paid enough not to wonder why a woman traveling with two silent attendants needed a room with a lock on the inside. Her attendants were not inquisitors; they were worse. Brother Hesk had once been a monk of the Quiet Hours before his vows curdled, and he could read a temple's silences the way sailors read weather. Sister Ondine had spent nine years extracting confessions in the cells below Vaelric's keep, and had come out of it with soft hands and a voice like warm milk, which was all the tool she needed.

They waited for her to finish eating. They always waited. She had never asked them to and had never needed to, and this, too, went into the private ledger she kept behind her eyes: obedience freely given was worth ten times obedience compelled, and cost nothing to maintain if you simply never gave anyone a reason to reconsider.

"The fisherman," Hesk said, when her bowl was empty. "The one who found the relic. His wife says he dreams of a mountain that breathes."

"Everyone within thirty miles is dreaming of a mountain that breathes," Maren said. "I dreamed it myself last night. That is not evidence of anything except proximity."

"You dreamed it?"

"I dream what the region dreams. It is a professional hazard." She wiped her mouth. "What did the wife say about the sigil?"

Ondine answered without looking up from her needlework—a habit she maintained even on the road, stitching patterns that were, Maren suspected, a private code of her own devising, though she had never bothered to break it. Some subordinates performed better believing themselves unreadable. "That the pilgrim came up the river road from the west, from the passes, walking out of Thornfell rather than into it. That he was already burned from the inside when he arrived. That he said—" she paused, delicately, the way she always paused before the words that mattered, "—that he said he was carrying it home."

"Home," Maren repeated.

"His word. The wife swears by it. He said he'd found the end of the road and it was a person."

Hesk shifted. "A person. Meaning the scribe."

"Meaning whoever he met at the gate," Ondine said. "Who happened to be the scribe."

Maren took out the relic and set it in the center of the table between them, and let them look at it, because letting your instruments see the evidence kept them honest. The glass pulsed very faintly now, like a heart heard through a wall.

"When I was a girl," she said, and stopped, because she had not intended to say anything of the kind.

Her attendants waited. Patience, she had long ago concluded, was the whole of the inquisitorial art; cruelty merely accelerated results, and acceleration introduced error.

"When I was a girl," she began again, evenly, "in the salt villages, we still prayed. Before the edicts. My grandmother taught me the old words for the mountain god, the one the maps call Thorn now because no one remembers its true name. I prayed every night for my father to come home from the herring boats. Every night for two years." She turned the relic a quarter-turn with one finger, watching the light move inside the glass. "The mountain never answered. I assumed the gods were deaf, or dead, or had never existed, which are the three conclusions available to a child, and I chose the third because it was the least humiliating. When the edicts came I reported our village priest myself. I was fourteen. It was the first entry in my first ledger."

Neither attendant spoke. This was correct.

"Now a dead thing on my table is growing warm," Maren said, "because somewhere north of here, an insignificant debtor with a talent for cartography touched a burning man and got answered. And the question that should occupy us is not whether the old gods live. I find I no longer care whether they live. The question is what the Usurper will do when he learns that they do—and whether it serves the realm for him to learn it from me, or from some panicking provincial bishop with half the facts and all of the fear."

"You intend to delay the report," Hesk said.

"I intend to *complete* it," Maren said. "There is a difference, and the difference is the entire discipline. Vaelric does not pay me to bring him smoke. He pays me to bring him fire, contained, with the wind direction noted." She rose, and took the relic back, and wrapped it in wool. "Pack. We ride at dawn. The subject is in the foothills, moving slow, moving deliberate, following paths that appear on no map I have ever bought, stolen, or tortured out of anyone. Which means the subject is being guided. Which means the god is choosing the route."

"And we follow the same route?"

"No." She smiled, briefly, without warmth. It was an expression she practiced in mirrors the way other women practiced dances. "We follow the subject. A god chooses mountains. People choose inns."

* * *

The tracking was almost embarrassingly easy, which was itself information.

Emrys Ishikawa traveled with a knight—Ser Aldric Vane, formerly of the Fourth Company, deserter, the man who had refused the order at Ashenmill and walked away from a hundred sworn brothers rather than burn a village of children. Maren had read the incident report twice. The official version called it cowardice. The unofficial version, assembled from the testimony of three soldiers who had been drunk enough to speak honestly and then sober enough to regret it, was that Ser Aldric had put his sword to Captain Drev's throat, given him one hour to countermand, and left when the hour expired without killing anyone—which was either cowardice or conscience, and the distinction mattered enormously to Maren, though she could not yet have said why.

Deserters made poor companions for fugitives. They drew patrols. But a knight knew how to read country, how to pick ground, how to move a small party fast, and the fact that the scribe had acquired one within a week of fleeing suggested either remarkable luck or a god with opinions about personnel. Maren noted both possibilities in the margin and assigned them equal weight, because certainty was a luxury the patient could not afford.

They picked up the trail at a shrine ruin in the foothills—an old waystone, pre-Imperial, its carvings defaced by edict-men two generations back. Someone had recently knelt there. The frost was pressed flat in the shape of knees, and beside the knees, a small circle drawn in spilled tea or wine, deliberate, careful, the gesture of a person who prayed the way they wrote: precisely, apologetically, expecting nothing.

Maren crouched over the mark for a long time.

The relic in her pocket had gone hot enough to feel through wool. At the shrine it did not pulse anymore. It hummed, a fine continuous vibration, like a struck bell past hearing.

"Rank Three protocol," Hesk said quietly behind her, "would be to report the resonance locus immediately."

"Protocol also says burn the waystone," Maren said, standing, brushing frost from her knee. "And protocol has never once explained to me why a stone that no one worships anymore frightens anyone." She looked north, up the white shoulder of the foothills, toward Thornfell, where the clouds moved wrong—not with the wind but against it, slowly, the way a chest moves when something inside it breathes. "Report the resonance. Date it accurately. Let the bureaucracy chew on it for a week. Bureaucracy is the finest ally an inquisitor has; it devours urgency and returns patience."

"And when the Usurper's answer comes back?"

"It won't," Maren said. "Not for a week. And by then I will know what the scribe knows, and my report will arrive complete, and complete reports are the only kind that make careers." She turned back down the trail, following the knee-shaped frost and the little circle of spilled devotion, feeling the relic hum against her ribs like a second heart that had opinions about her life. "Besides. I want to meet them first."

"The scribe?"

"The key." She said it without drama, the way she entered figures in a column. "Hesk, in twenty years of service I have hunted heretics, prophets, pretenders, and one genuine miracle, and I will tell you what I have learned. The dangerous ones are never the loud ones. The loud ones want to be keys; they turn themselves in every lock they find. The dangerous ones are the ones who never wanted to open anything. The ones who were small, and careful, and invisible, and got chosen anyway." She started walking. "Those are the locks the realm cannot survive losing. Or opening. I genuinely do not know which yet. That is what makes this one worth doing properly."

* * *

They nearly had them at the burnt orchard.

Maren felt it before Hesk's scout came galloping back—felt it as heat, sudden and specific, the relic going from hum to blaze against her breastbone hard enough that she gasped, actually gasped, the first undignified sound she had made in eleven years. The prayer in the orchard. Whatever the scribe had said into the black branches, the mountain had answered, and the answering had rolled down the valleys like weather, and every relic, every defaced waystone, every sleeping piece of the old faith within fifty miles had turned toward it like flowers toward a sun that had been dark for two generations.

By the time her horses reached the orchard's edge, the party was gone—but not gone cleanly. Gone *recently*. The ash still held bootprints, and among the bootprints, at the center of the scorched circle where the prayer had been made, the snow had melted in a ring five paces wide, refrozen at the edges into a lace of ice that glittered like the inside of the relic.

Ondine crouched at the ring's edge and did not touch it. "Warm ground," she said. "Still warm. Under the frost."

Hesk had gone pale. Maren noticed that she herself had not gone pale, and examined this, and filed it: *your hands are steady, Maren. Ask yourself later why.*

"They prayed," she said aloud, mostly to the orchard. "Deliberately. Knowing what it cost them the first time. Knowing what it marked them as." She walked the ring's circumference, counting, cataloguing, the inquisitor's catechism running under her breath: *who, what, when, whither.* "A frightened person runs from the god that answers. This one walked back to it and asked a question." She stopped at the ring's northern edge, where the melted-and-refrozen snow formed a shape almost like a doorway. "Do you understand what we are following? Not a fugitive. Not a heretic. A person conducting negotiations."

"With a god," Hesk said.

"With a god that broke its side of a bargain two hundred years ago and knows it." Maren took out her ledger and, standing in the ruined orchard with the ice-ring glittering around her boots, wrote the entry she had been building toward for forty-one days:

*Subject demonstrates agency. Subject is not fleeing the covenant; subject is pursuing it. Assessment revised: the scribe is not a threat to the realm. The scribe is a key—and keys, unlike threats, can be seized, copied, or turned by whoever arrives first.*

She blotted the page, and looked north one more time, at the breathing mountain.

Then, because the orchard was empty and the relic was humming and no one living could hear her, Maren said—quietly, in the salt-village words her grandmother had taught her, words she had not spoken since she was fourteen years old and reporting priests:

*I'm coming. Answer me this time, or don't. But know that I am asking.*

The relic flared once, brief and bright as a struck match.

And far up the white passes, something that had been silent for two hundred years adjusted its attention, the way a great eye adjusts toward a candle, and Maren—who had built her whole self out of patience—felt, for the first time in her life, the vertiginous understanding that patience was not the same as control, and that whatever waited in Thornfell had been waiting longer, and better, and with far more at stake than she had.

"Ride," she said. "We ride tonight."
