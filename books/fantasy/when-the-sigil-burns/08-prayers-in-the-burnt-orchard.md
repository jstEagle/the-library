## 8. Prayers in the Burnt Orchard

The orchard announced itself a mile before we reached it, by smell.

Not the green smell of apples, not even the sweet rot of fallen fruit left too long in grass. This was char and old smoke, the smell of a thing burned so thoroughly that even its burning had finished years ago and moved on to something quieter—decay without moisture, rot without softness. The wind coming down off the Thornfell foothills carried it to us like a warning nailed to the air.

Ser Aldric smelled it first, or noticed it first, which for a knight amounted to the same thing. He raised a fist and we stopped on the road, three travelers in a land that had stopped pretending it had laws.

"Smoke," Sefa said.

"Old smoke," Aldric said. "A season old at least. But roads run through orchards, and orchards have walls, and walls keep out things with more legs than two." He glanced at me. "Your mark agree?"

I had learned, in the weeks since Hollowbridge, to read the sigil the way I'd once read ledgers: not for meaning but for movement. It sat in my palm now as a dull warmth, banked coals under skin, and it stirred when we turned toward Thornfell's high passes and cooled when Vaelric's patrols crossed our intended path. A compass made of heat. A god steering me through the dark like a hand at the small of my back, gentle and utterly uninterested in whether I wanted to be steered.

"It's quiet," I said. "Quieter than the road ahead. If that means anything."

"It always means something," Sefa murmured. "With him, everything means something."

*Him.* She spoke of Thorn the way other people spoke of an estranged brother—affection braided tight with grievance—and she was the only one of us who did. Aldric spoke of the god not at all. And I—I had spent every night since the pilgrim pressed his burning iron into my palm telling myself that whatever answered my half-whispered prayer in the temple dormitory had been thunder, or fever, or the mind's ordinary trick of hearing voices in wind. I was a scribe. I knew how texts lied, and I knew no lie was more fluent than the one a frightened person tells themselves.

We went into the orchard anyway. Smallness had been my art once; I was trying to learn its opposite.

* * *

Vaelric's purge had come here in the second year of his reign, Sefa said, when the free villages along the mountain's southern skirt refused the tithe of names—the census by which the usurper catalogued every soul who might be god-touched. The trees told the story better than any chronicle. They still stood, hundreds of them, apple and pear and quince, but they stood black and split, their crowns broken off at the height a fire ladder reaches, their trunks scarred with vertical burns where pitch had caught and climbed. Someone had fired the orchard from below and let the flame eat upward, row by row, patient as an auditor.

Between the rows lay the stones of a press-house, its roof tiles fused into a single gray crust. A well mouth gaped under a collapsed lintel. And everywhere, in the black earth between the dead trees, the small pale geometry of bones—not human, mostly. The villagers' animals had been penned among the trees. Whatever else the purge had done, it had been thorough about livestock.

"We shouldn't sleep in the open," Aldric said. "Press-house wall will break the wind."

"And the roof?"

"Gone." He looked up at the fused tiles, then away. "Roofs go first."

I didn't ask what he meant by the way he said it. There were a number of sentences Aldric began and never finished, and I had learned to let them lie where they fell, like the bones.

We made camp against the press-house wall with the economy of people who had done it too many times. Sefa laid out her rations with a priestess's precision—three portions, measured, no argument possible. Aldric checked the road twice, then a third time, standing among the dead trees with his sword loose in its scabbard and his face turned toward the pass we'd come down. I sat with my back to the warm stone and my ruined palm open in my lap, watching the last light go copper on the mountains, and understood that I was waiting.

That was the truth I hadn't said aloud to either of them: since the night the mountain answered me, I had been waiting. Not dreading, exactly, though dread lived in it. Waiting the way you wait outside a door you've knocked on and heard footsteps behind.

The sigil warmed, very slightly, as the sun went down.

"All right," I said, to no one, to the palm of my own hand. "All right."

Sefa looked over. "Emrys?"

"I'm going to pray." I heard how strange the sentence sounded in my own mouth, how foreign, like a line in a language I could read but not speak. "Deliberately, this time. Not half-whispered into a pillow. I want—" My voice did something humiliating, cracked and steadied itself. "I want to know what I'm carrying. Before someone makes me find out the hard way."

Aldric came back from the treeline. He didn't sit, but he didn't leave either; he stood at the edge of our little triangle of bedrolls and firewood, a man planting himself between me and whatever might come through the dark. Sefa knelt across from me, and her face, in the last light, held something I hadn't seen on it before. Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, a kind of terrible hope, like a woman watching someone else open a letter she'd written years ago and never dared send.

"You don't have to do this tonight," she said.

"Yes, I do." I flexed my fingers. The sigil pulsed under them, patient as a heartbeat that wasn't mine. "Every day we walk, it gets warmer. Every day it points somewhere I haven't chosen to go. I'm tired of being a route on someone else's map." I almost laughed. "Do you know what I did, before all this? I sold smuggler routes. Little maps of little paths, drawn small enough to hide in a ledger margin. That was my whole life—knowing the ways through, and charging for them, and keeping my name off every page." I looked up at the black trees, the copper sky, the fused roof that had once covered someone's whole harvest. "And now I *am* the page."

Sefa bowed her head. When she looked up again, the fear had settled into something workmanlike. "Then pray," she said. "And I'll do what I was trained for, which is listen. But Emrys—gods answer honestly, and honesty is heavier than most people expect. Whatever he tells you, don't take it as a verdict. Take it as a door."

"What if I don't like what's behind it?"

"Then you'll be in excellent company." Her mouth bent, not quite a smile. "Everyone who ever prayed is behind it."

I closed my eyes.

* * *

I had prayed before. Let me be honest, since honesty was apparently the currency now: I had prayed the way poor people pray, which is to say constantly and without faith, the way you scratch at a locked door knowing there's no one home. Prayers for my mother at the end, when the temple physicians charged by the candle. Prayers to no particular hearer that the debt collectors would take the ledgers instead of my hands. Prayers shaped less like requests than like arithmetic—if you exist, and if you are anything like fair, then this equation should balance differently.

Nothing had ever answered. I had taken that as data. I was a scribe; silence was simply what the record showed.

So now, deliberately, in a dead orchard under a dying sky, I did what I knew how to do. I kept records.

"I don't know the forms," I said aloud. My voice sounded enormous among the burnt trees. "I never learned the old rites. So I'll do it the way I do everything. I'll account."

The sigil warmed another degree. Encouragement, or amusement. With gods, I suspected, the two were hard to distinguish.

"Item," I said. "One scribe, indentured, debt of nine years, four months remaining at last count. Item: one mother, deceased, whose debts I inherited because the Temple of Quiet Hours holds that a child's hands are collateral for a parent's word. Item: one pilgrim, unnamed, who died at my gate holding my name in his mouth like a coin he couldn't spend. Item:" —my hand closed, opened— "one mark, burned into me without my consent, which has steered me through three passes and past two patrols toward a destination no one has deigned to explain."

I took a breath. The smoke smell filled it, years-old and patient.

"So I'm asking. Not praying—asking, the way you'd ask anyone who owes you an explanation. What am I carrying? What do you want? And why—" and here the ledger ran dry, and the arithmetic became just a voice, smaller than I meant it to be, "—why me? I kept my head down. I made myself small. I was *careful*. There are ten thousand people in that city who pray with real words, real rites, real belief, and you walked past all of them and put your fire in the palm of a woman who sells smuggler routes out of a ledger margin. That's not grace. That's not even sense. So either you're cruel, or you're desperate, or—"

"*Or I remember you.*"

The voice did not come through my ears.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last craft I have left. The voice came through the sigil—through the burn in my palm, up the bones of my arm, into the cage of my ribs, and it spoke there the way a bell speaks, not in a place but in a resonance, so that my whole body was briefly the shape of the words. The burnt trees did not move. Aldric did not reach for his sword, though I saw later that his hand had frozen halfway. Sefa's lips were moving, silently, around words of her own.

The voice was not vast. That was the horror of it, if horror is the right word—it wasn't the avalanche I'd braced for. It was level, and worn, and *tired*, a voice like a road much traveled, and it said:

"*You counted yourself in the inventory. Do you know how long it has been since anyone did that? They list their sins to me. Their fears. Their wants. You gave me an accounting. You stood in a dead field and balanced your own books aloud, to a god, as if I were owed an honest ledger—and child, I have not been owed an honest ledger since the world had different borders.*"

My eyes were still closed. Behind them, the orchard had changed—or rather, I was seeing two orchards at once, the black one and beneath it, translucent as ink washed thin, another: green rows, heavy boughs, a press-house with a sound roof and a woman singing at the well. Harvest day. I could smell apples that had not existed in years.

"What are you showing me?" I whispered.

"*What was here. What Vaelric burned when they refused the tithe of names. Look longer. There is a stone by the well—you can see it even now, if you know how to look. Go and read it.*"

I opened my eyes. The double image faded like breath off glass, but it left a residue, a direction, the way the sigil left a direction when it warmed. I got up. My legs were unsteady. Aldric moved to steady me and I shook my head, and he let me walk alone between the dead rows, which was perhaps the kindest thing anyone had done for me in years.

The stone was there. Of course it was there; it had been there the whole time, a knee-high marker at the well's edge, so furred with moss and scorch that it read as nothing—a rock, an accident. I crouched and scraped it clean with my thumbnail, the way I'd cleaned ten thousand ledger margins, and found letters cut deep and old, older than Vaelric, older than the temple, cut in the script the priests called First Hand and taught only as ornament.

But I was a scribe. Ornament was just writing wearing nice clothes.

I read it aloud, slowly, translating as I went, and Sefa came and knelt beside me and her breathing changed at the third line, went ragged, because she knew the script better than I did and was reading faster.

It said: *Here the covenant was paid. Here the bearer refused. We gave memory for memory, and the last of us would not, and the gates closed, and the gods fell silent, and the silence has a price that accrues.*

Below that, smaller, nearly worn away: *Forgive us. We were afraid to forget.*

* * *

For a long time nobody spoke. The wind moved through the burnt trees and made a sound like a congregation clearing its throat.

"The last bearer," Sefa said at last. Her voice had gone very careful, the way mine went careful around figures that would ruin someone. "There was a bearer before you. At these gates. And the covenant broke because—"

"Because the payment is memory." Thorn's voice again, quieter now, speaking through the mark and through the stone and through the dead air equally, a god leaning close. "*You asked what I want. I will tell you what the covenant is, and you may decide what I want afterward, and hate me for it, and be right to.*

"*Before your temples, before your kingdoms with their borrowed thrones, the realms were held together by a pact, and the pact was sealed in the only coin that cannot be counterfeited: what a soul remembers. Each bearer of the mark gave a memory, freely, wholly—and what was given did not vanish. It became law. The love you remembered became the love the borders enforced. The grief you surrendered became the mercy the roads required. We did not take your memories, little scribe. We *spent* them. Spent them on the world, the way a householder spends grain—so that everyone ate.*

"*And the last bearer stood where you stand, at a gate of glass and bone, and was offered the same terms, and looked at the memory in her hands—the one the covenant asked for—and would not let it go. And I could not blame her. I have never blamed her. She loved something too much to spend it, and so she kept it, and kept it, and the gate stayed shut, and we went silent, and the silence spread through the world like cold through an unheated house. Every war since, every burned orchard, every tithe of names—that is the interest on her refusal. That is what unpaid debts do. You of all people should understand what accrues.*"

The sigil was hot now. Not painfully—not yet—but hot the way a coin is hot when it's been held too long, and I realized my fist was closed around my own palm, around the mark, the way a person closes a fist around something they've already decided not to give up.

"You're asking me for a memory," I said. My voice sounded far away, a stranger's voice. "That's what's ahead. That's what you've been steering me toward."

"*I am asking nothing yet,*" Thorn said. "*The gate asks. I only carry you to the question. But hear this, and carry it with the rest of your accounts:* the covenant will not tell you which memory. It will open your hands and accept what you choose to place in them—and you will not know, until it is spent, whether you chose rightly. The last bearer chose to keep hers. Perhaps she chose wrongly. Perhaps there is no wrongly. That is the terror of a free gift, child: no one can save you from your own choice. Not even me.*

"*Especially not me.*"

Silence came back into the orchard, the ordinary kind, wind-and-ash silence. Sefa had her head bowed. Aldric had come up behind me sometime during the speaking, close enough that I could hear him breathe, and when I turned to look at him his face was doing something complicated—pity and recognition and a third thing he was working very hard not to name.

"You knew," I said to Sefa. "Some of this. In Hollowbridge. When you read the mark."

"I knew it was a Covenant Mark." Her jaw tightened. "I did not know the payment. No rite I was taught says what the coin is. The church erased it, or the bearers kept it secret, or—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—or the last bearer's refusal broke something so thoroughly that even the story of the price went out of the world with the gods' voices. Emrys. Listen to me." She gripped my wrist, above the mark, and her hand was shaking. "Whatever the gate asks—you can refuse. Refusal is written in the rock. Refusal has precedent. You are allowed to be afraid, and you are allowed to say no."

"And then?" I looked out at the burnt rows, at the bones in the black earth, at the fused roof of a house where children had once stolen fruit. "Then the silence keeps accruing. Then Vaelric keeps tithing names. Then some other pilgrim dies at some other gate and puts his fire in some other scribe's hand." I uncurled my fist. The sigil glowed faintly now, visible even in the dusk, lines of gold like script on skin. Like a ledger line.

Like a debt.

"I've spent my whole life keeping my name off the page," I said quietly. "Maybe that was always the coward's version of the story. Maybe the point of a name is to spend it."

Neither of them answered. Above the dead orchard the first stars came out, indifferent and ancient, and the mountains to the north stood black against the last light—the direction the sigil had pointed every single day since Hollowbridge, warmer each time, patient as interest.

We slept in shifts. Somewhere past midnight, in the gray hour before dawn, I woke to a sound from the road we'd left behind: hoofbeats, several horses, moving at a pace too disciplined for bandits and too slow for messengers. Aldric was already awake, already at the treeline, and when I joined him I saw the torchlight strung out along the southern road like beads on a wire.

He didn't look at the torches. He looked at the banners beneath them, barely visible, and I watched his face do something I had never seen it do—watched the soldier's calm crack straight through to the boy underneath.

"That's my company," he said. "That's *my* company. They're not tracking the sigil." His hand found my shoulder, grip hard enough to hurt. "They're tracking *me*."
