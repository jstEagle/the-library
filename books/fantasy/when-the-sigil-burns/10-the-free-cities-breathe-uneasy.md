## 10. The Free Cities Breathe Uneasy

Sefa smelled Meridian before she saw it—tallow, tar, and fear, the particular perfume of a city that had begun to believe its own death was possible. The river carried it downstream to meet them, and she stood at the bow of the ferry barge with her hands folded in her sleeves, reading that stink like scripture.

Below the walls, the fields were stripped. Not harvested—stripped. Grain cut green and hauled in, orchards felled for timber, even the hedgerows torn up where they might shelter an advancing company. The great windmills on the eastern ridge had stopped turning; their sails had been taken down and lashed into barricades. A city preparing for siege did not merely gather. It amputated.

"They've been at it a month," Aldric said beside her. He stood the way he always stood now, half-turned toward Emrys, as though the world might reach for them when he wasn't looking. "Maybe two."

"Vaelric will not besiege this city," Sefa said. "He'll buy it."

"Then why the walls?"

"Because the council must be seen to resist before they sell." She watched the gates grow larger, the watchtowers, the new-cut palisades bristling along the riverbank like the spines of a frightened animal. "Fear is currency here too. They'll spend it carefully."

Emrys said nothing. They had said very little since Hollowbridge, since the night Sefa had laid her thumb against the Covenant Mark burning in their palm and named it aloud. But she felt their attention shift toward her, the way a candle flame leans when a door opens elsewhere in the room. She had learned to feel that attention over three weeks of shared road, and she had learned, too, what it cost them to be looked at. The scribe who had made an art of smallness now could not cross a village without children staring at the light leaking between their fingers.

The barge grounded. The harbor guards met them with leveled spears until a harbor-clerk squinted at Sefa's ruined vestments—at the scorch-marks where her order's sigils had been cut away, the holes where a council of men had once burned her name out of the rolls—and decided, with the exquisite pragmatism of free cities, that a heretic priestess was still a priestess, and priests were useful in a siege.

They let her bless the gate. She did it properly, palms open, speaking no god's name, because every god's name had been struck from the liturgy by decree of the Usurper's Concord—and beneath her ribs, old and stubborn, Thorn stirred like a sleeper turning over.

*Soon,* she told him silently. *We are nearly home.*

She did not know yet how much the word *home* would cost.

* * *

Meridian's Council Hall had been a granary once, then a temple, then a courthouse, and now wore all three skins at once: grain sacks stacked behind iron grilles, a dais where the altars had been, benches where the magistrates' clerks scratched at ledgers under hanging lamps. Nine councillors sat in a crescent. Sefa counted the factions within a minute—the merchant seats leaning together like conspirators, the guild seats with their ink-stained fingers, the two military seats wearing borrowed armor over soft hands.

And one seat apart from the rest, occupied by a woman in grey with a courier's satchel and eyes like a ledger's margin: neat, empty, waiting to be filled with other people's worths.

An envoy of Vaelric, seated openly at council. Sefa's stomach went cold.

"You bring us a tale," said Councillor Hessa Marrow, whose family had held the grain concessions for four generations and whose voice had the oiled patience of someone accustomed to being owed things. "A temple scribe carrying a mark of the old covenant. A knight who deserted. A priestess without an order." Her gaze settled on Emrys like a hand closing around a bird. "And you bring this to us in the season of our greatest need. Forgive me if I ask what you want."

"Nothing," Sefa said. "That is what I came to tell you. The covenant asks nothing of Meridian. It asks of the bearer."

"The bearer," said the man in the second military seat—a general by his bearing, or a man playing one. "You're asking this council to stake the city's survival on a folk-tale. On a god nobody has heard speak in two hundred years."

"I'm asking you to remember what the silence bought," Sefa said. She stepped forward onto the floor of the hall, and thirty years of preaching rose in her throat and steadied it. "When the gods went quiet, the Usurper told you they had died. He told you their silence was an ending. It was not an ending. It was a wound. The covenant broke, and everything that bound realm to realm, oath to oath, army to conscience—it broke with it. You have lived your whole lives inside that brokenness and mistaken it for the shape of the world. Vaelric's soldiers burn villages and feel nothing, because nothing binds them. His inquisitors hunt a scribe across half the mountains, because they know what you refuse to know: that the binding can be reforged. And if it is reforged by *him*—" she pointed at the grey woman, and did not apologize for the pointing— "it will bind you instead. Chains are covenants too. That is the only kind he knows how to make."

Murmuring moved through the benches. Above, in the gallery, common folk had come to watch—siege crowds did that, hungry for anything that wasn't ration counts—and Sefa saw among them faces that had wept at her words before she finished saying them. Faith was still there. Starving, but there.

Councillor Marrow tapped one finger against the arm of her chair. "The envoy has offered terms. Full provisioning, withdrawal of Vaelric's forces beyond the Thornfell passes, guaranteed autonomy—in exchange for the bearer and the mark."

"Guaranteed," Sefa repeated. "By whom?"

"By the Usurper's word."

"His word broke the last covenant." Sefa turned to face the crescent of chairs fully. "I have read the old rites. I have spent my life being laughed at for it, so hear me once more while you still find me amusing. The Gates of Ash stand three weeks from this hall. The rite can be completed there. If it is, Vaelric's armies stop—not defeated, not bought, *stopped*, turned by law older than his throne. If he takes the bearer first, the mark becomes his instrument, and no wall in the free cities will matter again, because he will hold the thing that makes oaths optional. There is no bargain here. There is only the question of who holds the key when the door is locked."

"And the bearer?" Marrow's voice dropped, gentle in the way of people about to do something terrible kindly. "What does the bearer wish?"

Every head turned. Sefa turned too, and her heart clenched.

Emrys stood near the doors where the guards had put them, hood back, and the sigil's glow showed faintly through the wrapping on their palm. They looked—Sefa searched for the word and hated every candidate—*small*. They had made themselves small again, the way they must have done for years in that temple, shrinking to survive. When they spoke, their voice was quiet and level.

"I want the debt paid," Emrys said. "However it's paid. I've been property since my mother died. This council wants to trade me; the Usurper wants to spend me; the god wants to use me. At least the council is offering grain in return."

The hall laughed—nervous, ugly laughter—and Sefa wanted to weep. That was the cruelest thing she had witnessed on the whole road: not Maren's hunters, not the burnt orchards, but watching a person learn to price themselves before others could.

Aldric moved. Just shifted his weight, one hand brushing Emrys's shoulder—barely a touch, gone in a breath—but the whole hall saw it, and Sefa saw Marrow see it, and file it away.

"The council will deliberate," Marrow announced. "The bearers of the petition will be lodged in the Hall precinct. Under courtesy." The pause before *courtesy* was a door locking.

* * *

They were given a room with good beds and barred shutters, which told Sefa everything about the quality of the courtesy. Night fell, and the siege-city noises seeped through the walls: hammering on the palisades, militia drilling in the yard, somewhere a mother singing to a child in a voice worn thin.

Sefa sat by the shutter-bar and prayed the way she'd prayed since her excommunication—not asking, just keeping the line open, like tending a fire you refused to let go out.

*He's afraid,* said the presence she had learned to call Thorn, faint as heat off stone.

"Of course they're afraid," she murmured. "Cities are afraid. It's what they're for."

*Not the city. Mine.*

She went still. In the bed by the far wall, Emrys lay awake—she could tell by the breathing—and Aldric slept sitting up against the door, out of habit, a man who no longer trusted rooms.

"Then say it to them," Sefa whispered. "You answered them once. Answer now."

Silence, long enough that she thought the moment had passed. Then, so softly she felt it more than heard it, a warmth moved through the room, and Emrys sat up in the dark.

"It's louder here," Emrys said. Their voice had a strange, unmoored quality. "Inside the walls. Like standing next to a bell."

"What does it say?"

"That it's sorry." Emrys laughed, a small cracked sound. "A god apologizing. For what?"

Sefa knew. She had known since Hollowbridge, when she read the mark and understood what the old rites demanded, and had said nothing, because the road had seemed long enough already without that weight. But the weight was here now, in a locked room in a city about to be sold, and she found she could not carry it alone anymore.

"Come sit," she said. "Both of you. There's something I should have told you at the river, and I was a coward, and I'm done being one."

Aldric woke fully at her tone. Emrys crossed the floor and sat, and the sigil lit their wrapped hand like a coal under cloth.

"When the covenant was forged," Sefa said quietly, "the gods asked a price of the bearer. Not blood. Not service. A memory. Freely given—one that matters, one the bearer would grieve. That was the law of it: that the pact must cost something real, or it holds nothing real. The last bearer refused at the Gates of Ash, and the refusal broke the covenant, and the silence began." She met Emrys's eyes. "If you complete the rite, it will ask the same of you. And it will not let you choose cheaply."

The room was very quiet. Down in the yard, the drill sergeant called the turn, boots stamping, a city rehearsing its own defense.

"A memory," Emrys said at last. "Which memory?"

"I don't know. The rite doesn't say. Perhaps the bearer doesn't get to choose." Sefa reached out and took their unwrapped hand, sigil and all, and the warmth of it hurt pleasantly, like tears. "I have argued before councils and before kings' stewards and before my own order, and I have never once lied to win an argument. So hear me plainly. I believe you are the only chance any of us have. And I believe the price is monstrous. Both things are true, and I hate the god who made them both true, and I love him anyway, which is what faith is."

Emrys looked down at their burning palm for a long time. Then, in a voice almost too soft:

"On the wall tops tonight—I mean, before the council—there was a night. On the walls." A glance at Aldric, quick and helpless. "There's a memory I think I know which one it would take."

Aldric had gone white. He said nothing. He put his hand over theirs, over the sigil, over whatever the night on the walls had been, and Sefa looked away from both of them, suddenly a trespasser in her own sacrament.

* * *

The verdict came at dawn, and it came dressed as generosity.

Councillor Marrow received them in a side chamber, without the full crescent, with wine poured and honey-cakes set out—the hospitality of a knife going politely into its sheath. The grey envoy stood by the window, hands folded, patient as arithmetic.

"The council has voted," Marrow said. "Nine to nothing. We accept the Usurper's terms. The bearer will be conveyed into the envoy's custody at noon, with full honors. In exchange, the siege is lifted, the passes opened, and Meridian stands." She spread her hands. "Child, understand what we're doing. We are saving forty thousand lives with one. No priestess's ghost-story weighs against that."

"No," Sefa said. "It doesn't weigh. It *binds*. That's rather the point."

"The vote is taken."

"Votes are taken," Sefa agreed. "Covenants are kept. Your city survives this bargain exactly as long as it takes Vaelric to no longer need your grain routes. You are not buying peace. You are scheduling your own surrender and calling it mercy." She turned to the envoy. "And you. Does he know the mark cannot be commanded? Has anyone told him what happened to the last man who tried to hold the covenant like a deed of ownership?"

The envoy smiled for the first time. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "The Usurper believes all things can be held, priestess. It's why he wins."

"It's why he'll lose," said Emrys.

Everyone turned. They stood by the door in their temple-grey clothes, and something had changed in them overnight—or perhaps in the last hour, in some room where they had decided a thing. The smallness was still there, the scribe's careful posture, but underneath it now was a load-bearing wall.

"But thank you for the honey-cakes," Emrys added, with the ghost of their dry, tired humor, and picked one up, and ate it, slowly, while nine councillors' proxy stared and did not know what to make of them at all.

* * *

Noon came, and with it twelve of Vaelric's own soldiers to escort the custody—grey cloaks, white sigils, faces scrubbed of expression. The council meant it as efficiency. Sefa understood it as a message: *we are already his.*

The handover was staged in the Hall courtyard, before witnesses, with documents. A clerk read terms aloud. Marrow signed. The envoy signed. A soldier stepped forward with iron manacles wrapped in velvet—velvet, Sefa thought, the age's whole theology in a single detail—and reached for Emrys's wrists.

Aldric broke the soldier's nose before the manacles closed.

It happened fast after that, and Sefa would remember it always as fragments: the courtyard erupting, Marrow shrieking about treason, the envoy backing toward the gate with her ledger-eyes finally wide; Aldric fighting like a man settling accounts, using a practice-yard's worth of restraint and not one ounce more; Sefa herself seizing the moment the way she had seized pulpits for thirty years, arms flung wide, voice pitched to fill a nave, shouting at the crowd packed above the colonnade—"*Is this what freedom looks like? Watch! Watch what your council sells!*"—and the crowd, God love them, crowd that they were, answering with a roar and a rain of thrown fruit and a sudden impassable press of bodies between the grey soldiers and the gate.

And Emrys, in the middle of it, calm as a clerk closing a ledger, pulling Sefa and Aldric through a postern she'd noticed the night before and mentioned to no one, out into the smell of the river and the open ground beyond the walls, running south toward the mountains with the whole city's fury and hope howling behind them.

They did not stop until dusk, in a fold of hills where Meridian's fires showed small and stubborn against the coming dark. Aldric doubled over, hands on knees, laughing breathlessly. Sefa leaned against a boulder and shook. Emrys stood looking north, at the black shoulder of the Thornfells rising against the stars, and the sigil's light spilled between their fingers like something eager.

"Three weeks to the Gates of Ash," Sefa said. "And Maren somewhere behind us, and Vaelric's army somewhere ahead."

"Yes," Emrys said.

They turned, and in the starlight their face was the face of someone who had stopped being small, and it frightened Sefa more than any inquisitor, because she knew now exactly what such courage would be asked to pay.

"Then we'd better walk," said Emrys Ishikawa, and walked.
