## 11. A Night That Owes Nothing

The bells of Meridian did not toll for the hour. They tolled for fear.

Emrys had learned their language in three days: the short triple stroke for a watch change, the long rolling peal when a supply caravan made it through the eastern passes, and now, at dusk, the slow mournful count that meant the council had finished arguing and had decided nothing. Nine strokes tonight. The same as last night. The city was running out of nights.

They climbed the wall stairs alone, or told themselves they were alone, with a stolen loaf under one arm and a flask of Sefa's bitter herb wine under the other. The guards on the wall walk let them pass. That was the strangest part of Meridian—everyone seemed to know who they were, and no one could say how. A baker had pressed a honey roll into Emrys's hands that morning and called them *the covenant-bearer* before hurrying away. A child had touched the hem of their sleeve for luck. In the Temple of Quiet Hours, Emrys had spent six years learning to be invisible; here they were visible everywhere, and it fit them like a coat cut for someone else.

The sigil pulsed faintly against their palm, warm as a held coin. It had been warm all day. Warmer since the council session, where twelve merchants and one archon had looked at Emrys like an entry in a ledger—an asset, a liability, a number to be moved—and Emrys had sat very still and thought: *I know this arithmetic. I have been on both sides of it my whole life.*

Aldric was already at the wall's shoulder, where the stone bulged out over the river gate and no lantern reached. He stood the way he always stood, feet planted, hands loose, a man who had unlearned how to rest but not how to wait. The wind off the Thornfells pulled at his grey cloak, at the stubble he'd stopped bothering to shave, at the scar that ran from his ear down into his collar like a seam someone had picked open and never closed.

"You're late," he said, without turning.

"I'm never late. I'm precisely as late as I decide to be." Emrys set the loaf and the flask on the merlon between them. "Bread. Wine that tastes like punishment. I bribed the kitchen boy with your name."

"Did you."

"He thinks you're a famous knight. I didn't correct him."

"Good." Aldric broke the loaf in half and handed the larger piece over without being asked about it, which was its own kind of conversation. "Famous is safer than deserter."

Below them, Meridian burned with a hundred small fires—cookfires, forge-fires, the great braziers on the harbor towers—and beyond the firelight the world went black to the foothills, where Vaelric's outriders were said to camp. Somewhere out there was Maren. Emrys felt her sometimes, or imagined they did: a cold point of attention moving through the dark, patient as ink spreading through water. They had not spoken of her in two days. Not speaking of her was becoming a habit, and habits were how people lied to themselves politely.

They ate. The wine was terrible. The city murmured beneath them like a hive in winter.

"I watched them vote today," Emrys said at last. "Or try to. Archon Vess kept calling for order and the tallow merchant kept shouting about grain stores, and every time someone mentioned me—" they turned the flask in their fingers, watching lamplight swim in it, "—the whole room went quiet in a particular way. You've heard it. The quiet of people pricing something."

"I've heard it." Aldric's voice was flat. "I heard it the day before Kellam's Ford."

"The day you were ordered to burn the village."

"Yes." He said it the way he always said it now—not flinching, not performing his penance either, just setting the fact on the table like a stone. Six days ago he hadn't been able to say the word *village*. Sefa had done that, or the orchard had, or the slow erosion of traveling with two people who knew what he'd done and had not turned him in. "Vess wants to send you to Vaelric with terms. Your mark, your cooperation, in exchange for lifting the siege before winter. She thinks she's being merciful. She thinks she's saving ten thousand lives with one."

"She might be right."

"She might be." He looked at Emrys then, fully, and the look was worse than anger. It was the look of a man checking a wound he already knew the depth of. "You believe that. Don't you. Some part of you has already priced yourself the way they price you."

Emrys opened their mouth to deny it and found the denial wasn't there. That was the trouble with a life spent keeping ledgers: you learned to see value everywhere, including in yourself, especially in yourself, because value was the only thing anyone had ever agreed you had. Their mother's debts had been paid in installments of invisibility. The temple had bought their years at a rate so fair no one could call it cruelty. And now a free city wanted to spend them, and the horrible thing—the thing that sat in Emrys's chest like swallowed river water—was that the math was not wrong. Ten thousand lives. One scribe who had spent twenty-three years practicing smallness.

"The sigil's been hot since the council," they said instead. "It knows something I don't."

"It knows what you're thinking."

"Then it knows I'm thinking about running. Just me, out the north postern, into the mountains, gone before dawn. No trade, no siege lifted, no covenant either. Just—" they gestured vaguely at the black wall of the Thornfells, "—smallness again. My old art."

"And the god?"

"Thorn doesn't chase. That's what frightens me. It just... waits, the way the mountain waits. I could run for thirty years and every road would still be a road it remembered first." Emrys laughed, short and without humor. "I used to sell smuggler routes. Do you understand? I sold the secret paths of the world to people who wanted to move through it unseen. Now the whole world is one route and I'm the cargo."

Aldric was quiet for a while. Below them a patrol went by with a lantern, saw them, and passed on; even the guards had begun to treat Emrys with a strange deference, bowing their heads slightly, as if to a shrine rather than a person. Emrys hated it. Emrys also, in some shameful corner, leaned into it. Both of those things were true, and both of those things were going to be taken away soon—they could feel that too, the way you feel weather coming in a healed bone.

"Aldric." Their own voice surprised them, low and steady. "Ask me why I came up here."

"Why did you come up here?"

"Not for the view." They turned to face him. The brazier-light caught half his face, left the rest to the mountain dark, and Emrys realized they had memorized both halves without meaning to—the way you memorize a route, the way you memorize anything you cannot afford to lose. "I came up here because I think I'm about to lose something, and I don't know what yet, only that the sigil gets warmer every time I think of you, and I am not a fool. I have read enough temple contracts to know what a price looks like from the outside. And whatever this is—" their hand hovered between them, the scarred palm with its iron-bright mark, "—this is going to be in the ledger. Whatever the covenant takes, it will take this night, or these weeks, or you. Something. The gods don't give receipts but they keep accounts. Sefa says so. Thorn said so, in the orchard, in its voice like roots breaking stone."

"*Every payment is a memory,*" Aldric recited quietly. "*And the bearer chooses nothing.*"

"And the last bearer refused, and the realms burned for a century." Emrys breathed out. "So here's what I want, and then you can refuse, and we'll go back to bread and bad wine and pretending we're employer and guide. I want one night that owes nothing. No debt. No interest accruing. No covenant waiting at the end of it with its hand out. One hour, one wall, one man, where I am not a key or a bargain or a bearer. Just Emrys, who copies ledgers badly on purpose so the abbot won't notice how good they are. Who steals routes. Whose mother hummed when she baked and went silent the year the debt collectors came. Can you give me that? Because after the verdict tomorrow, I don't think anyone can give me anything anymore. I think everything will be transactions from here until the gate."

The wind moved along the battlements. Far below, the river gate creaked, and a night fishing boat put out with a single lamp, absurdly small against all that black water.

Aldric reached over and took the flask. He drank, grimaced—it truly was terrible—and then he did something he had never done in all the weeks Emrys had known him. He took off his sword belt, wrapped the strap around the scabbard three times, and laid it on the stones between them like a man laying down more than steel.

"No oath tonight," he said. "No debt. You asked. I'm answering." His voice roughened. "Do you know what I was going to do tomorrow, if Vess's vote goes the way it looks like it will?"

"Fight the whole council?"

"Half of one. Maybe less. I counted the guards." A ghost of a smile. "But yes. I was going to stand up in front of those merchants and declare myself your sworn shield, formally, on the record, which would make trading you an act of war against a knight of the realm—or a former one—and buy us maybe a day, maybe two. A stupid plan. A dead man's plan." He shook his head. "And then I thought: that's just another transaction, isn't it. Me buying you time with my life, you owing me a grave. Everything we do is ledgers. So no. Tonight I'm not your shield and you're not my guide. Tonight I'm a man sitting on a wall next to a person whose name I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Whose name I would like to keep saying for a long time, and I don't get to know if I'll be allowed."

Emrys's throat tightened. "Say it anyway. Practice. So it survives."

"Emrys Ishikawa." He said it carefully, like a phrase in a language he was learning. Then again, looser. "Emrys. There. That's the short form. That's the one I use when I'm—" he huffed a breath, almost a laugh, "—when I'm talking to you instead of at you."

"When are you ever at me?"

"Daily. Hourly. *Emrys, get behind me. Emrys, that bridge is rotten. Emrys, stop negotiating with the mule.*"

"The mule respects me."

"The mule fears you, which is different, and I respect you, which is also different, and neither of us is going to survive this conversation if I keep—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and looked out at the mountains, and Emrys watched a man fight his own reticence and lose on purpose, which was braver than any charge he'd ever described. "I carried a mark once. I told you. Gave it up, buried it, told myself the silence afterward was peace. It wasn't. It was just quiet. And then I saw your palm in that ditch outside Hollowbridge and every buried thing stood up at once, and I told myself it was duty. The covenant. The realms. All the big words men use when the small true word is too frightening." He turned back to Emrys, and his eyes in the brazier light were the color of wet slate. "The small true word is *you*. It has been *you* since the pass, since you argued with the god about which trail to take and won. Nobody argues with their god and wins, Emrys. Nobody. And you did it because you'd rather be lost than steered, and I stood there in the snow thinking: *there it is. There's the bravest thing I've ever seen, and it's a scribe refusing directions.*"

Emrys laughed—a real laugh, cracked and startled out of them, and found their eyes stinging. "That's not bravery. That's just being contrary."

"It's the same thing, done well."

They sat close then, shoulders touching, the cold stone leaching up through them and the warmth where they met refusing, stubbornly, to leave. Emrys talked. That was the gift of the night, they realized later—that they talked, really talked, for the first time since the temple. About their mother's humming and the exact smell of the temple scriptorium, tallow and dust and iron-gall ink. About the first route they'd ever sold, and the sick shine of the coins, and how they'd told themselves it was survival and it *was* survival and it had still changed the shape of them. About the years of counting: four thousand days of indenture, minus good behavior, minus the abbot's mercy, a number they had kept in their head the way other people keep prayers.

Aldric gave back what he could. The village—he named it now, Marrow's Bend, eleven houses and a shrine, and he said the names of the children he'd failed to save, all seven, in order, like a soldier reporting strength. Emrys listened without flinching, because flinching would have been another form of lying, and tonight owed nothing, least of all comfort.

At some point the moon rose over the Thornfells, thin and white, and lit the fused-glass scars on the far peaks where, Sefa said, the old covenant had been forged and broken. Emrys looked at those pale wounds in the mountainside and felt the sigil turn over in their palm like a sleeping animal.

And the knowing came, the foreknowledge they had been carrying all evening like a stone in the belly, and they finally spoke it aloud, because a night that owes nothing owes the truth most of all.

"It's going to be this," they said. "When we reach the gate. When Thorn shows me the price. It's going to be this—tonight, this wall, your voice saying my name. Or my mother's voice. Or all of it together, everything I love, everything that makes me *me*. That's the payment. That's what the last bearer couldn't give." Their voice stayed steady, which felt like a miracle performed by someone else using their mouth. "And I can feel myself already deciding, Aldric. Down underneath the fear. I'm already deciding to pay it. Because Vaelric chose refusal and became that—became a man who owns everything and is owned by nothing and loves nothing—and I would rather be emptied than be him."

Aldric's hand found theirs in the dark. Found the scarred palm, the burning sigil, and covered it, as if a hand could shield a person from a god's arithmetic. It couldn't. They both knew it couldn't. He did it anyway.

"Then take tonight twice," he said roughly. "Once now, and once as a memory you'll carry into the fire. Let it burn last. Make it fight for every other one first."

Emrys leaned into him, forehead against his jaw, and above them the thin moon crossed toward morning, indifferent and beautiful, and below them the city slept the sleep of the condemned, and the sigil glowed softly through their joined hands like a coal deciding.

"Forget the verdict," Emrys whispered. "Forget the vote. For one more hour, owe me nothing and I'll owe you nothing, and we'll be the richest two people in Meridian."

They had almost managed it—almost reached that weightless place past grief where the night was only night—when the bells began.

Not nine strokes. Not the weary count of a stalled council.

All of them. Every tower at once, hammering, urgent, a sound Meridian had not made in living memory, and along the eastern horizon, where the black hills met the blacker sky, watchfires bloomed one after another like a string of lit fuses—too many, far too many, an army lighting its camps for the morning march.

Aldric was on his feet with his sword belt in his hand before the third bell fell.

"They're coming," he said. "Emrys—they're coming *now*."

And in Emrys's palm, the sigil flared white-hot with something that was not fear at all, but recognition—as if the mountain itself had smelled smoke and remembered the last time.
