## 13. Maren's Mercy

Inquisitor Maren had interrogated forty-one heretics in her eleven years of service to the Crown of Vaelric, and she had learned one truth that the manuals never wrote down: everyone breaks at the thing they love, not the thing they fear.

The woman called Sefa was proving no exception, which was a small mercy, because Maren was tired.

She sat across the cell from the priestess and did nothing at all. That was the technique the manuals *did* write down, though they buried it in an appendix between notes on proper chain-gauge and the disposal of relic-ash: sit. Wait. Let the prisoner fill the silence with their own dread, because a person's imagination is always crueler than any instrument you could bring into the room. The instruments were in the next chamber anyway, laid out on oiled cloth in their neat rows — the hooks, the pincers, the little copper funnel whose purpose Maren had never once needed to explain aloud. Their presence did the talking. They always did.

Sefa sat chained to the wall with her grey braid undone and hanging over one shoulder like a rope cut loose from an anchor. She was older than Maren had expected. Heretic priests usually were; conviction took decades to calcify into something worth burning. There was blood at her temple where the Hollowbridge constables had been enthusiastic, and her left hand was swollen where a soldier had stamped on it, but her eyes — her eyes were clear as a winter morning, and they watched Maren with something that looked horribly like *recognition*.

"You're not what I pictured," Sefa said. It was the first thing either of them had spoken in two hours.

"No?"

"I pictured a man. Big. Loud. The kind who needs the screaming to feel alive." Sefa shifted against the wall, wincing. "You're a woman with ink stains on her fingers and dust in the seams of her coat. You look like a scribe."

"I was trained as one." Maren turned her hands over, considered the stains. "Before the Inquisition found better uses for someone who can read four languages and lie in all of them."

"And now you hunt people like me."

"People like you are my work," Maren agreed. "Not my hatred. I want you to understand the difference before we begin."

"Begin." Sefa laughed, and it cost her something — Maren saw the breath hitch around cracked ribs. "We've been sitting here half the night. If this is the beginning, your god of efficiency must weep."

Maren set the stolen relic on the floor between them.

It was a small thing, unremarkable to any eye but a trained one: a disc of black glass the size of a coin, rimmed in silver gone brown with age. Officially it did not exist. Unofficially it was the reason Vaelric's Inquisition had caught nine sigil-bearers in three years while the old regime had caught none in thirty. When a Covenant Mark came within a mile of the disc, it warmed. Within a hundred yards, it burned. Maren carried it in a lead-lined case against her sternum, and for eleven years it had been cold as riverbed stone.

Three days ago, outside Hollowbridge, it had begun to sing heat into her ribs like a swallowed coal.

"This is how I found you," Maren said. "Not the constables. Not the reward notices. This." She let Sefa look at it, then closed her fist around it — gently, the way you'd close a fist around a bird. "Your priestess-friends spent thirty years saying the old gods went silent. Your order printed pamphlets about it. And all that time, the Crown had proof the silence was a *symptom*. Something woke up, priestess. Something is answering prayers again, and it chose a temple scribe with debts and smuggler contacts to carry its mark. I intend to know why."

"Then ask your questions," Sefa said. "I've been told I'm stubborn, but I'm also fifty-three years old and my ribs are broken. I'll tell you true things. Whether they're the things you want—" she shrugged, and paid for it, "—that's the god's business, not mine."

Maren studied her for a long moment.

Here was the problem with Sefa of Hollowbridge, the problem Maren had identified within the first hour and had been turning over ever since like a stone in the palm: the woman was not afraid. Fear made prisoners lie cleverly or confess stupidly, and Sefa was doing neither. She was simply *waiting*, patient as a stone saint, as if torture were a weather she had already decided to stand in.

There was only one kind of person who waited like that.

Someone who had already given everything away once, and found it did not kill them.

* * *

They moved the interrogation to the upper room of the tollhouse at dusk, because Emrys Ishikawa was coming, and Maren wanted the meeting staged precisely.

She knew the scribe would come. That was not arrogance; it was arithmetic. The knight — Aldric Vane, deserter, oath-breaker, a name Maren had carried in her ledger for two years without ever managing to close the entry — would have fled south with the priestess if he'd had any sense, and instead he had doubled back toward the toll road the moment word reached him of her capture. Men who abandoned their oaths still kept the smaller ones. It was one of the few things Maren found bearable about men.

And the scribe — the scribe would follow the knight. The sigil-bearers always traveled in threes: bearer, blade, believer. It was in the old accounts, the ones the Inquisition kept locked below the Chancery, the ones Maren had read by candlelight in her second year of service until her eyes ran and her hands shook. *Bearer, blade, believer.* Thirty-one times through history the pattern repeated, and thirty-one times it ended the same way, at the same place, and the last account ended mid-sentence because the archivist who wrote it had been the last bearer's—

No. She did not think about the archivist. She had spent eleven years not thinking about the archivist, and she was very good at it.

The tollhouse upper room had one window facing the mountain road, and Maren had the table turned so its occupant would face the window, and the light arranged behind her own chair so that when Emrys entered they would see her silhouette first, faceless, the way authority prefers to be seen. She put the relic on the table. She put a single lamp beside it. She put nothing else, because the room itself was the message: *I know exactly what you are, and I have known longer than you have.*

They came an hour past full dark. She heard the horse first — the knight's, lame-shod, trying to be quiet and failing — and then the long silence of people deciding whether to knock or break down the door. They knocked. Of course they knocked. The scribe would insist on knocking; everything Maren had learned about Emrys Ishikawa in six weeks of ledger-tracing and informant-work said *knock*, said *small steps, soft voice, pay your debts, be no one*.

"Inquisitor Maren," she said, when the door opened on the knight's drawn sword and the scribe's white face. "Come in, both of you. Close the door. Your priestess is warm, fed, and unmarked, and she will remain so exactly as long as this conversation stays civil."

Aldric Vane was bigger than his file suggested and moved like a man carrying his body carefully, the way you carry something already dropped once. He put himself between Maren and Emrys without seeming to decide to, which told her more about the geometry of their little company than six informants had.

"The priestess first," he said. "Then talk."

"She's downstairs. Alive." Maren didn't rise. "Ser Aldric. Or do you prefer just Aldric now? Files are unclear on what one calls a man who burned his own oath."

Something crossed his face — quick, old, familiar — and Maren felt the relic warm against the tabletop and realized with a small cold start that it wasn't warming for the scribe alone.

Later, she thought. Later, that.

Emrys stepped out from behind the knight, and Maren got her first true look at the most dangerous person in the Three Realms.

It was almost funny. Almost. A slight figure in a road-dusted coat too big for them, ink-stained fingers curled at their sides, dark circles under eyes that kept moving — cataloguing exits, counting her weapons, measuring the window drop. A smuggler's mapper's habits worn like skin. The sigil was visible on their right palm when the lamplight caught it: a branching burn, root and crown together, still faintly luminous at its edges weeks after any ordinary brand should have scabbed and faded.

"You've been reading my ledgers," Emrys said. Their voice was quiet and level, and Maren noted the tell anyway: the smallest flattening, the way a person sounds when they are spending enormous effort on sounding like nothing.

"I've been reading everyone's ledgers. It's my trade, as it was yours." Maren gestured to the chair. "Sit. Both of you, if you like, though the knight will have to take the trunk. I have an offer, and I have a confession, and I have very little patience left tonight, so let us waste none of it on posturing."

Emrys didn't sit. Neither did the knight. Fine. Standing it was.

"The offer first," Maren said, "because the confession will make it harder to hear." She turned the lamp slightly, so its light lay across the black glass disc. "This is a reliquary key. It predates Vaelric's reign by centuries. It does what you've no doubt guessed: it finds the Mark. It found your priestess through me, and me through it, and I followed its warmth up the toll road for six days like a man following a wound. I have used it to catch nine bearers. Eight of them are dead. One recanted and now sweeps the Chancery floors and won't meet anyone's eyes."

"And the ninth is sitting in front of me," Emrys said.

"The ninth is sitting in front of me," Maren agreed, "and here is the offer. The sigil, freely surrendered — the rite of severance performed properly, at the chapel in Fort Meridian, under witnesses, so that the Mark passes out of you cleanly and cannot simply rekindle — and the priestess walks free. Full pardon. Papers. An escort to whatever border she likes. The knight too, if he wants it, though I suspect he doesn't; deserters rarely trust pardons, and he's right not to."

The silence that followed had weight. Maren let it stand.

She watched Emrys's hand — the marked one — close slowly, fingers folding over the sigil as if shielding it, an unconscious gesture, a child hiding a candle flame from wind. And she watched something else, something that made her chest ache in a place she had boarded shut years ago: the scribe glanced at the floor, toward the room below where Sefa was held, and Maren watched the exact moment the arithmetic finished. Watched them price a life against a self and find the exchange obvious.

"Don't answer yet," Maren said quietly.

"It's a good offer," Emrys said. Their voice had gone smaller. "That's what makes it filthy."

"Yes," Maren said. "It is. Both things are true."

* * *

"Now the confession," Maren said, "so you understand that I am not mocking you."

She rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the Thornfell foothills, black against a sky salted with stars. Somewhere up there was a gate of fused glass and bone that appeared in none of her files except the sealed ones, and beyond it whatever remained of a pact that had once held the world's teeth apart. Behind her she heard the knight shift, and the scribe breathe, and neither of them speak. Good. This part she had rehearsed for eleven years and had never once said aloud, and it came out of her the way splinters leave flesh: slowly, and leaving a hole.

"When I was nine years old," Maren said, "in the river country, before the Chancery took me, there was a fever in our village. My mother prayed at the hearth to every licensed god in the calendar and got nothing, because the licensed gods were already dead and only the priests hadn't admitted it. But my grandmother — my grandmother did something different. She took me out to the orchard behind the house, the burnt one, the one that had burned the year I was born, and she knelt down in the ash, and she taught me a prayer. Not words. A *shape*. She said you don't pray to Thorn with language, because language belongs to the temples and the temples sold their gods' names years ago. You pray with a wound. You open the worst thing in you and you hold it out, and if the old god is listening, it answers."

The relic on the table had gone warm enough now that Maren could feel it from across the room, a faint pressure against her back like a hand between the shoulder blades. She ignored it.

"I was nine, and my mother was dying, and I had a great deal of wound available. So I knelt in the ash and I opened myself the way she showed me, and I prayed harder than I have ever done anything in my life." Maren turned from the window. "Do you know what answered me, scribe? Nothing. Silence. The same silence that answered my grandmother, and her mother, and every faithful soul in the river country for thirty years. My mother died in the spring. I stopped believing by summer. By winter the Chancery recruiters came through testing children for memory and literacy, and I went with them gladly, because the Chancery at least told the truth about the world: that it is empty, that power is real and gods are stories, and that the person who keeps the best ledger wins."

Emrys had gone very still. The knight was watching Maren with an expression she refused to interpret.

"Twenty-two years later," she continued, "the Chancery gives me a black glass disc and tells me the gods are waking up after all, and sends me to hunt the people they choose. And I do it. I'm good at it. I caught eight of you before you, and I never lost a night's sleep, because I told myself the story the Chancery taught me: that the bearers were deceived, that the thing answering prayers is not a god but a predator wearing a god's face, that severance is mercy." Her voice did not change; that was the discipline of it, the terrible evenness. "And then I tracked the ninth bearer up a mountain road, and I watched the disc burn against my ribs every time they prayed, and somewhere outside Hollowbridge, in the dark, alone, I did the thing I swore I never would again."

"You prayed," Sefa said from the doorway.

They all turned. The priestess stood in the door with a soldier's hand on her arm — unnecessary; she wasn't fleeing anywhere — and blood dried at her temple, and her clear winter-morning eyes full of something that was not triumph, which was somehow worse. Triumph Maren could have handled.

"I knelt in the ash of a burnt orchard, actually," Maren said. "Near the toll road. There's an irony there I refuse to enjoy. And I opened the worst thing in me and held it out, twenty-two years late, and asked the god I serve against my will whether it remembered a nine-year-old girl in the river country."

"And?" Emrys asked. Softly. As if the answer might be a living thing that loud noises would frighten.

"It answered," Maren said. "That's the confession, scribe. It answered. It didn't speak — I'm told it doesn't, not to me, not the way it speaks to you — but the disc in my pocket went cold for the first time since I took custody of it. Cold as riverbed stone. And I lay in the ash of that orchard and I understood what I had been doing for eleven years, and who sent me, and why a usurper who wants a world with no gods in it would arm his inquisitors with relics that *find the gods' chosen*."

She came back to the table and stood over the lamp, over the relic, over the two people she had hunted across half a realm.

"He broke it," Maren said. "Vaelric. Or his masters did, before him — the sealed accounts say the last bearer refused the price at the Gates of Ash, and the covenant shattered, and the gods went silent, and the Chancery was built on the silence like a city on a graveyard. And now something is reforging itself, and it chose you, and the Crown will spend any number of lives to stop it, because a god that answers prayers is a god that can *demand things*, and Vaelric's entire claim to the throne rests on the proposition that nothing demands anything of kings."

"So you're offering me a way out," Emrys said slowly, "because—"

"Because I need to know what you'll choose," Maren said. "And yes. Also because I meant it. The priestess goes free. I keep my bargains; it's the one virtue the Chancery couldn't train out of me." She paused, and then, because it was true and because the truth was the only weapon she had left that didn't disgust her: "Because forty-one heretics, scribe, and every one of them broke at the thing they loved. And you're sitting there doing the arithmetic already — her life against your self — and I need to watch you finish it, because I finished mine in an orchard two nights ago, and I have not slept since, and I am so tired of being the Chancery's answer to a prayer nobody heard."

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off in the hills, thunder rolled where no storm cloud hung — a low, deep sound, less like weather than like a voice clearing its throat beneath the world.

Emrys looked down at their marked palm. Opened the fingers slowly, the way you open a hand around something that has grown into it.

"The sigil doesn't come off," they said. "You must know that. Whatever rite you're offering — I've felt what this thing is. It's not a brand. It's a *root*."

"I know," Maren said. "Severance has killed two of the eight."

"Then your bargain is a knife with a handle on your side."

"Yes," Maren said. "It is. I told you I'd tell you true things."

Emrys looked up, and for the first time the inquisitor saw the thing the files could never hold: not fear, not cunning, but a weary, furious clarity — the look of someone who has spent their whole life keeping their head down and has finally, finally run out of ground to press their face against.

"Take me to the priestess," Emrys said. "All three of us, in one room. And then, Inquisitor Maren, I'm going to offer *you* a bargain, and you are going to hate how much it sounds like one Thorn would write."

Far above the tollhouse, beneath the sleeping mountains, the thunder rolled again — and this time, unmistakably, it was laughter.
