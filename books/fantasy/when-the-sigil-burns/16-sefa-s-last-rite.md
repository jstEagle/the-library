## 16. Sefa's Last Rite

Pain was an old teacher, and Sefa had always been a diligent student.

She had learned its first lesson in Hollowbridge, at nineteen, when the excommunication decree was read aloud in the market square and the crowd that had sung with her the week before stood silent as her vestments were burned. Pain taught her that faith which costs nothing is decoration. It had taught her again on the roads of Thornfell, in frostbitten toes and hunger that made the stars swim. But Maren's instruments had been a different school entirely—graduate studies in the anatomy of belief—and Sefa had failed to graduate cleanly.

Now she lay in the dark of a shepherd's hut high above the tree line, wrapped in every blanket Aldric could find, and took inventory of herself the way she once inventoried relics: what remained, what was broken, what still held its consecration.

Her left hand did not answer when she asked it to close. Three fingers on her right had gone strange and distant, as if they belonged to someone she'd met briefly at a festival. Her ribs were a nest of cracked branches. When she breathed too deeply, something inside her shifted with a wet reluctance that she recognized, professionally, as very bad indeed.

But her mind. Her mind was still hers. That was the miracle Maren would never understand—you could break a body like kindling and the soul stayed whole, curled at the center like a flame in cupped hands.

*You prayed to him too,* Sefa thought, drifting. *He answered you with silence, and you decided the silence was his voice.*

The door scraped open, letting in a blade of gray mountain light and the smell of snow. Emrys came through it sideways, carrying firewood, moving with the careful economy of someone who had learned not to jostle the world. Behind them, Aldric filled the doorway, ducking under the lintel with a hunted look he probably believed was hidden.

"You're awake," Emrys said. Relief and fear fought openly across their face; they had never learned to mask it, bless them. Smallness was an art, but they were learning larger canvases.

"I've been awake for some time," Sefa said. Her voice came out like paper tearing. "I've been composing a lecture."

"Don't," Aldric said hoarsely. "Please don't."

"Sit down, both of you. I have perhaps—" she considered, honestly, because a lie here would be a desecration—"two days. Perhaps three. Less if I keep talking, so listen quickly."

* * *

They had carried her out of Maren's camp on the second night, after the inquisitor had made the mistake Sefa had predicted she would make: leaving her alive because dead witnesses convince no one, and because some part of Maren—the part that had once knelt before a shrine and heard nothing—could not quite bring herself to extinguish the last living priestess of the old rite.

Sefa had used those hours well. Pain clarifies. With her hands ruined and her ribs stove in, strapped upright to a chair while Maren asked her questions in that terrible patient voice, Sefa had done the only work that mattered. She had spoken the first half of the Rite of the Reforged Pact aloud—not to Maren, who thought it was delirium, but into the room itself, into the walls, into the iron sigil burning beneath her own skin where she'd pressed her palm to Emrys's mark weeks ago and felt it answer.

Half a rite is worse than none, traditionally speaking. It opens a door without building a threshold. But doors, once opened, stay opened.

"The first half binds the bearer to the gate," she told them now, propped against the hut's one wall, watching their faces in the firelight. "It establishes the covenant's terms in the bearer's flesh. Emrys, your sigil already carries it—I spoke it over you the night we read the Mark in Hollowbridge, though neither of us knew what I was doing. The gods are efficient that way. They use whatever vessel is nearest."

"And the second half?" Emrys asked. They always asked directly. It was one of the things about them that had undone Sefa's cynicism, piece by piece, over the miles—a person who had spent years being invisible and still chose clarity over comfort.

"The second half must be spoken at the Gates of Ash, by the bearer, freely given. No one can say it for you. That's the whole point—the covenant broke because payment taken by force isn't payment, it's theft, and theft poisons the pact." She paused to breathe, and the wet thing inside her shifted, and she rode the wave of pain down and back up like a boat in weather. "But there is a third element no text names plainly, because the texts were written by priests who wanted the job for themselves. The rite requires a bridge. A memory of the gods, freely surrendered, to carry the bearer across the threshold intact enough to speak. Without a bridge, the price takes everything indiscriminately. With one—" she smiled, and knew it must look ghastly, and didn't care—"the bearer chooses the shape of their own loss."

Emrys had gone very still. Even in the firelight Sefa could see the calculation behind their eyes, the smuggler's instinct for hidden costs. "What kind of memory?"

"The oldest kind." Sefa closed her eyes. "I was six years old. My grandmother took me up the hill behind Hollowbridge to a stone older than any temple, and she put my hand on it, and I heard—" Her breath caught, not from the ribs this time. "I heard singing. Not with my ears. With the stone. The god whose name the mountains forgot, singing to itself in the dark before the world, and it let me hear because I was small and asked nothing. Do you understand? I have built forty years of faith on four seconds of song. And I give it to you now, gladly, because a candle does not diminish the flame it lights."

"No," Emrys said. Just that. Flat and immediate and terrified. "No. Keep it. You'll—"

"I will die either way, child. That was decided in Maren's tent, and no rite unbreaks ribs." She said it gently, and watched the words land like stones in water. "The only question is whether what I know dies with me or lives past me. Faith passed hand to hand is the only kind that has ever survived anything. Hand it along."

* * *

Aldric went outside. He was a man who processed grief the way other men processed firewood—by chopping it into manageable pieces at a distance from the house. Sefa heard him out there in the dark, breathing hard through his nose, doing violence to a log that had done nothing to deserve it.

Emrys sat beside her and took her ruined right hand in both of theirs, careful of the broken fingers, and did not speak for a long time. The sigil beneath their skin threw faint heat into her palm, like holding a stone that remembered the sun.

"When I was indentured," Emrys said finally, "there was an old copyist in the temple archive. Brother Anselm. His eyes were going, and the abbot wouldn't buy him spectacles, so I used to read to him after hours. He told me once that the worst part of dying wasn't fear. It was that all the things you alone remembered would stop being true when you stopped remembering them. That memory only exists while someone carries it." Their thumb moved across her knuckles, feather-light. "I thought he was being morbid. He was being practical, wasn't he."

"He was being a priest," Sefa said. "We're all just librarians of the invisible."

"Then teach me the catalogue."

So she did.

Through the night, while the wind worried the thatch and Aldric kept the fire fed, Sefa gave away her life in ordered portions. She taught Emrys the cadences of the old tongue—not its grammar, which would take years, but its music, the rise and fall that turned words into keys. She gave them the names of the nine aspects of Thorn, from the Root-that-Remembers to the Crown-that-Forgot, and which aspect answered which need. She described the proper posture for receiving a covenant: kneeling, palms open, head bowed but eyes lifted—"you kneel as a servant but you look up as a peer; that tension is the entire theology, don't lose it."

And between the lessons, in the pauses where the fire popped and the wind sang counterpoint, she gave them the smaller inheritances. How to tell when bread was done by smell alone. The name of the innkeeper in Meridian who watered his wine and how to shame him with courtesy. What her grandmother's hands looked like, flour to the wrist, shaping loaves on a holy day. The particular green of the light through temple windows at dawn, which she had loved more than any doctrine.

"Why the small things?" Emrys asked at one point, voice rough. They had not slept. Neither had she; sleep was a country whose border she could no longer cross. "Why waste your last strength on bread?"

"Because the great memories are what get taken, child. The small ones are what remain to prove a person existed." She smiled. "When you cross the gate and it burns everything out of you—and it will, don't make that face, I've read the refusal written in the rock and I know what the price becomes—someone should have planted ordinary seeds in you beforehand. Things too small for a god to bother harvesting. A taste. A smell. So that when you wake up empty on the far side of your own sacrifice, there's something growing already. Something that was never yours to lose."

Emrys stared at her. "You're planning for my survival."

"I'm planning for your *after*," Sefa corrected. "Survival is Aldric's department. He's very good at keeping bodies alive. It's souls he fumbles."

* * *

Near dawn, she called Aldric back inside.

He came in smelling of pine and cold and the sweat of a man who had been armoring himself against this moment all night. He knelt by her pallet with the awkward grace of a large man making himself small, and she saw that he had brought her something—a sprig of rowan, berries still red, snapped from a tree at the treeline. Mountain ash. Thorn's tree, in the oldest iconography. He hadn't known that. He'd simply seen it and thought of her, which was better than knowing.

"For the pain," he said gruffly, offering it like an apology.

"It's beautiful." She took it in her working hand. "Put it where I can see it."

He did. Then, because he was Aldric, and honesty was the wound he never stopped reopening: "The village. The children. If I had—if I'd been faster—"

"Aldric." She waited until his eyes came up. "You have confessed this to me three times now. I absolve you each time. Either you don't believe me, or you believe forgiveness should feel different than it does. Let me tell you what it feels like. It feels like this. Like a tired woman telling a good man that guilt is not penance, that penance is *action*, and his action has been walking toward the Gates of Ash with two strangers to fix what an empire broke. You are doing your penance. It is happening right now. Stop auditing it."

His jaw worked. His eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "And you? Who absolves you?"

"Ah." She leaned her head back against the wall. Outside, the sky was going from black to the deep bruised blue that precedes dawn on high passes. "No one. That's the secret of priests, Ser Aldric. We are not forgiven. We are *spent*. There's a difference. A coin can be absolved of nothing—it can only be given away until it's gone, and trust that somewhere in the giving, it mattered." She held up the rowan sprig. "This mattered. Go wake the fire."

* * *

She waited for full light. Some things require it.

When the sun cleared the eastern ridge and poured through the hut's single window, laying a bar of gold across the floorboards, Sefa had them help her sit upright—Aldric's arm bracing her spine, Emrys's shoulder under hers—and the movement cost her something she did not get back, and she paid it without comment because haggling over the price of a gift insults the recipient.

"Now," she said. "The bridge. Take my hands."

Emrys took them. The sigil flared at the contact—not painfully, not yet, but with a deep structural heat, like a forge deciding whether to be a hearth or a pyre.

"Sefa," Emrys whispered. "I can feel it pulling. Whatever you're about to do, it's already starting."

"Good. Don't fight it. Fighting makes it ragged." She fixed her eyes on theirs—grey-green eyes, ink-stained fingers, a face that had learned invisibility and was now, day by day, learning the opposite. *You will forget me,* she thought. *All of this, gone. The lectures, the songs, the woman who argued with you about theology over bad stew. And you will walk into the world after, trusting everyone and known by everyone, and you will build yourself anew out of whatever seeds I've managed to plant.* "Repeat after me. In the old tongue. Sound is more important than meaning—meaning will arrive on its own."

"*Vael tor enna,*" she began. *I carry what was given.*

Emrys repeated it. Their accent was atrocious. It was perfect.

"*Vael tor enna, sel maro dun.*" *I carry what was given, and pass it burning.*

Verse by verse, line by line, she poured the first half of the rite into the morning—completion and confirmation, the door she'd opened in Maren's tent now framed and hung and fitted to Emrys's flesh specifically. The sigil brightened until it showed through the backs of Emrys's hands like veins of lava. The hut grew warm. Frost retreated up the window glass. Outside, she sensed rather than saw, the pines stood very straight, the way trees do when something enormous pays attention.

And at the rite's heart, at the moment the liturgy demands its bridge, Sefa reached for the four seconds of song.

It rose in her easily—six years old, her grandmother's flour-dusted hand over hers, the ancient stone humming under her palm, the god singing to itself in the dark before the world. Four seconds. Forty years of fuel. She gathered it up the way you gather the last of a candle's wax, and she looked at Emrys one final time—

"—listen," she whispered, and gave it.

Not told. Given. The distinction is the whole of theology, and she had forty years to be sure of it.

The song crossed between them like a spark crossing a gap. She felt it leave—felt the place where it had lived go quiet, a room in her suddenly vacant, and found that the vacancy did not hurt. It rang. It rang like a bell after the strike, all resonance and no source, and she understood with the last of her scholar's clarity that this was what inheritance felt like from the giving side: not loss. Relay.

Emrys gasped. Tears ran down their face, though she doubted they knew why. Somewhere behind their eyes, a six-year-old girl was hearing singing in a stone, and they would carry her now, and never know her name, and that was fine. That was the whole point of candles.

"Sefa?" Emrys's voice cracked. "*Sefa?*"

"I'm here." Her own voice was very far away now. The ringing was filling everything. She found she could see the fire perfectly, every color in it, as if colors had recently been invented. "I'm here, and then I'm there, and both of those are fine. Listen—the rest. Quickly. The gate will show you the price and it will offer you a bargain, a small one, a gentle one. It offered the last bearer the same. *Refuse the gentle thing.* Gentle bargains are how gods become tyrants and men become Vaelric. Pay the whole price. Pay it laughing if you can manage it, for my sake, since I won't be there to appreciate—"

Her breath hitched. The wet thing inside her made its final argument.

Aldric's arms came around her. She registered that distantly, comfortably—pine smell, cold wool, a heartbeat slower than hers and steadier.

"Tell me it mattered," she said. "Lie if necessary."

"It mattered," Aldric said, and he was not lying, and the bell rang louder.

*It mattered,* Sefa agreed, and let the candle go out so the flame could move on.

* * *

They buried her at noon above the tree line, in ground too stony for digging, under a cairn they built together in silence. Aldric set the rowan sprig among the stones, berries red as votive flames. The wind off the peak took the smoke of the tiny fire they lit and carried it east, toward Hollowbridge, toward the hills where a stone older than any temple still hummed its four seconds to anyone small enough to ask nothing.

Emrys stood before the cairn a long time. When they finally spoke, their voice was steady in the way that costs the most.

"She gave me her song. I can't remember it exactly. I remember that I almost remember it." Their fists clenched. "Thorn took even the shape of the gift. Is that how gods say thank you?"

Aldric put a hand on their shoulder. "Maybe the thank-you comes later."

"Then let's go collect it." Emrys turned from the cairn, and the sigil burned visible through their sleeves now, day and night, a brand the world could read. Southward, beyond the ridgelines, the air above the Gates of Ash had begun to shimmer with a heat that had nothing to do with the sun—and far behind them, down the valley, dust rose in a long deliberate column, patient as a ledger line: Vaelric's army, coming to collect its own debts.

"We have three days," Emrys said. "Maybe less. Teach me the road."

Aldric looked at the shimmer, at the dust, at the stranger Sefa had died to finish building. "Walk fast," he said, "and pray loud."
