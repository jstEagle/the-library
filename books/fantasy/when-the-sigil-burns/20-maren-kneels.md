## 20. Maren Kneels

The relic had been silent for three days, and Inquisitor Maren Vess could not stop listening to the silence.

She held it now in her cupped palm as she climbed the last ridge before the Gates of Ash—a sliver of black glass on a chain of woven hair, older than the empire it served, older than Vaelric's throne, older, if the archivists were honest, than anything they dared write down. For eleven years it had been her instrument. It hummed when god-touched flesh was near. It sang when the Covenant Mark burned. It had sung so loudly in Hollowbridge that her teeth ached for a week, and she had followed that song across the Thornfells like a hound on blood.

Now it was quiet. Not dormant—she knew dormancy; this was not that. Dormant was a banked fire, patient, waiting to be fed. This silence was *emptied*. As though something had passed through the glass on its way somewhere else and taken everything with it.

As though the relic had never been an instrument at all, only a straw stuck into a river, and the river had turned its course.

Maren closed her fist around it and kept climbing.

Behind her, the army came up the pass in columns of gray and iron—forty thousand men under the black sun banner of Vaelric, forty thousand oaths sharpened over eleven years of conquest. She heard them before she saw them: the creak of harness, the cough of men breathing thin mountain air, the endless metronome of boots. An army sounds like patience. That was the first thing they taught you in the Inquisition's school at Corvale. Anyone can be cruel for an afternoon. Cruelty is cheap. What wins realms is the willingness to wait—to read a ledger until the numbers confess, to sit outside a city until hunger does your work, to follow one small scribe through half a kingdom because the sigil in their palm is worth more than any fortress.

Maren had been patient for eleven years. She had burned what needed burning, or told herself so at the requisite hour each night. She had learned the doctrine until she could recite it asleep: *the gods are gone, and what answers prayer now wears their names like stolen coats.* Vaelric's truth. The truth she'd built her life inside like a house with no windows.

And then the river turned, and the house stood open to the sky, and she did not know where to stand.

* * *

She crested the ridge alone, as she preferred, and looked down upon the Gates of Ash for the first time.

The histories called it a gate. It was more like a wound that the world had healed wrong. Two cliffs of fused glass and pale bone leaned toward each other across a defile, melted together at their crowns by some heat that had come and gone before the first kingdoms rose. The rock itself bore writing—not carved, but *burned*, letters seared into stone a hand's width deep, in a script no living scholar could fully read. Sefa had read fragments aloud once, in another life, in a river town with lantern light on the water. *We gave. We were given. Ask again.*

Below the gate, the valley floor was scoured white, and in the middle of that whiteness lay three figures small as spilled ink.

Maren knelt behind a shelf of rock and put the relic's lens to her eye.

One figure stood apart from the others—tall, gray-cloaked, a sword planted point-down in the ash like a grave marker. Aldric Vane. She had hunted his ghost for two years since his desertion; it was almost a relief to see him solid, alive, standing vigil over—

Over Emrys Ishikawa, who lay on their back in the whiteness with their hands open at their sides, and who was either dead or transformed, and Maren found she could not tell the difference from this distance, and suspected there was no longer a difference to tell.

The third figure knelt beside them. Priestess Sefa, whom Maren's own interrogators had broken in a cellar beneath the Meridian garrison. Sefa should have been dead. Maren had seen the ledgers; she had signed the orders; she knew precisely how much of a body could be taken before it stopped. Yet here the priestess knelt, upright, her hands moving over Emrys's brow in the old rite—the rite Maren had catalogued as superstition, as residue, as ash—and even at this distance Maren could see that the air around her hands bent slightly, the way it bends above summer roadstone.

The relic in her fist shivered.

Not sang. Shivered—once, like a man coming out of a long faint—and then went quiet again, and Maren understood with a certainty that arrived in her chest rather than her mind:

It wasn't empty. It had been *answered*. Everything the relic had ever carried, every scrap of borrowed divinity Vaelric's artificers had sealed into the glass eleven years ago, had drained away through the Gates of Ash and gone home.

*Ask again*, said the burned words in the cliff face.

Maren Vess, who had not wept since she was nine years old, sat down hard in the ash.

* * *

She had been nine years old in a fishing village called Brackenmere, which no longer existed, which appeared on no map anymore except in the archives as a line item: *purged, year four of the interregnum, cause: god-touched congregation.* Her mother had prayed. Everyone's mother prayed, in Brackenmere; it was a village built on herring and on the old rites, the kind the new church called residue. And when the fever came through the winter Maren turned eight and took her little brother Tomas, her mother had gone down to the sea wall at night, as her own mother had done, and prayed to whatever still listened under the old names.

Maren had gone with her once. Just once. She remembered the cold of the stones through her shoes, the smell of salt and tallow, her mother's voice low and steady and utterly certain, saying the old words wrong—she must have gotten them wrong, everyone said the words had been lost—and asking. Asking for Tomas. Asking the dark water and the dark sky beyond it: *if anyone hears me, if anyone at all—*

Tomas died on the ninth day. And the village priest, a kind man named Ollen who would burn two years later with the rest of them, had held Maren's mother while she screamed at the sea that there was nothing out there, nothing, nothing, and Maren had stood in the doorway of the sickroom and made herself a promise in the ashes of that scream: *I will never ask again. I will never kneel. If the gods are real and let children die, I will serve whoever puts a leash on them.*

Eleven years later, a recruiter from the Corvale school had watched her break a smuggling ring's cipher in four hours and asked if she believed in anything. She said, "I believe in ledgers." He laughed and wrote *yes* on her papers.

That was the whole of it. That was the wound dressed in doctrine, in discipline, in the cold clean grammar of interrogation reports. Every god-touched child she had sent to the pyres, every village, every ledger entry ending in ash—all of it had been a nine-year-old girl standing in a doorway refusing to ever be that helpless again. You cannot be abandoned by a god you refuse to acknowledge. You cannot pray if you never open your mouth.

Vaelric understood that. That was why she had served him. The usurper had built his whole bright terrible architecture on a single insight: that a god who owes you nothing can never demand anything of you. Break the covenant, he had told his inner circle—Maren among them, the night before the Thornfell campaign—and the gods lose their claim on mortal memory forever. No more payments. No more bargains struck over the heads of children. A realm with no covenant is a realm where no one owes the sky a thing.

He had said it like liberation. Standing on this ridge now, looking down at the small ink-stain of Emrys Ishakawa's body, Maren finally heard it as what it was: the scream of a man who had asked once, been refused, and decided that refusal was the shape of the universe.

She recognized it. Gods forgive her—no. *Thorn*, she thought, testing the name in her mouth like a coin bitten to see if it was true. *Forgive me,* she thought, and then caught herself, appalled, kneeling in the ash of a ridge with her doctrine in ruins and the word *forgive* already halfway out of her heart before she could stop it.

Eleven years without asking. And the asking had been waiting in her the whole time, patient as an army.

* * *

She went down into the valley alone.

She told herself it was reconnaissance. She drafted, in her head, the report she would deliver to Vaelric's forward command when they reached the defile at dusk: bearer expended, covenant reforged, relic inert, threat status—what? She found she could not finish the sentence. *Threat status:* the pen hovering over the line, in the report of her soul, with nothing to write.

The army would come anyway. Forty thousand men do not stop for an inquisitor's crisis of faith. Vaelric himself was less than a day behind, riding with his vanguard, and he had not crossed half a kingdom to be turned back by a miracle he did not believe in. Whatever happened next at the Gates of Ash would happen with her in it or without her, and she found, walking down through the scoured white valley with her relic silent against her breastbone, that she could not make herself turn back. Could not make herself climb to the ridge and wait with the engines of war for the order she had spent eleven years making herself ready to give.

Her boots crunched on glass. The whole valley floor was fused sand, smooth as a frozen lake, and the burned words in the cliffs grew taller as she approached, until she was walking beneath letters the size of doors. *WE GAVE. WE WERE GIVEN.* And lower, half-eroded, in a different, desperate hand: *ASK AGAIN. ASK AGAIN. ASK AGAIN.*

Aldric Vane saw her first. She watched him take up the sword from the ash and level it at her, and she noted—he would note such things even now, even with her hands empty—that he placed himself between her and the bodies. Between her and Emrys. Between her and Sefa, who rose slowly, unsteadily, a woman held together by rags and will, and looked at Maren with eyes that had every right to be full of hatred and were instead full of something worse: recognition.

"Inquisitor," Aldric said. His voice was wrecked. She had heard men speak like that after burying brothers. "You're late. There's nothing left to burn."

"I'm not burning," Maren said. And then, because the lie of the report had died somewhere on the ridge: "I don't know what I am."

Sefa limped forward, past Aldric's guard, close enough that Maren could see the ruin the cellar had made of her—burns mapped along her jaw, fingers bandaged with strips of cloak. The priestess studied her face the way she had once studied the sigil in Emrys's palm. Reading.

"You prayed," Sefa said. "Just now. On the ridge."

Maren's breath went out of her. "You can't know that."

"The valley knows." Sefa touched the fused ground with her ruined foot, gently, as if the earth were a sleeping animal. "This place is the covenant now. It listens the way stone used to listen, before we all agreed to pretend it was deaf." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "What did you ask?"

And Maren Vess, who had extracted confessions from cardinals and smuggler-kings and once, famously, from a man who had sworn his tongue was blessed against truth, stood at the foot of the Gates of Ash and could not get the sentence out for a long moment. When it came, it came in her mother's cadence, in the low steady voice of a woman at a sea wall, because some prayers are inherited like eye color:

"If anyone hears me. If anyone at all."

The relic against her breastbone warmed.

Not sang—warmed, once, like a hand laid flat over her sternum from the inside, and the warmth spread, and the burned words overhead seemed to lean closer, and the wind that came down through the gate smelled impossibly of woodsmoke and hawthorn, of a spring morning in a village that no longer existed, and Maren heard—

Not thunder. Not a voice out of the sky. Nothing so theatrical. She heard, quite distinctly, the way one hears a knock at a door in a house where no one has visited in years: a presence arriving at the threshold of her attention, courteous, enormous, and *waiting*—waiting for permission, because the covenant was reforged and the old law lived again in the mountains and the law said no god crosses uninvited.

It waited for her to ask.

Nine years old at a sea wall. Eight days watching her brother die. Eleven years of ledgers and ash and the promise never to kneel.

Maren Vess got down on her knees in the fused glass, in front of the knight whose village she had ordered burned, in front of the priestess she had broken, in front of the body of the scribe who had paid the price she had spent her whole life refusing to pay—and she opened her mouth, and she asked.

Not for absolution. She had earned none and knew the difference. Not for Tomas back; even now, even granted everything, she would not insult the dead with arithmetic. She asked the question under all her questions, the one her mother had really been asking at the sea wall, the one that had curdled into Vaelric's whole empire:

*"Did you hear me? Back then? Was anyone—was anyone there?"*

The warmth in her chest became a voice that was not a sound. It said, in the manner of mountains speaking, which is to say wordlessly and yet unmistakably:

*Yes. We heard. We were dying, child, and the covenant was breaking, and we could not answer. Your brother was not refused. He was unanswered, which is the cruelest thing a universe can do, and we have carried it with yours among our debts. We remember him. We remember Brackenmere. We remember you, Maren, who promised never to kneel, kneeling now. Ask again. Anything. The gate is open.*

And the devastating thing—the thing that broke her open like ice off a bough in thaw—was not forgiveness. It was being *heard*. Eleven years of perfect competence, of reports filed and pyres lit and the leash held tight on heaven, and it had all been a letter written to no address, and now, at the end, someone had signed for it.

Maren wept into the ash, both hands pressed to the warm glass of the ground, and the god of thorns and small mercies stayed with her while she did, patient as an army, gentle as spring.

* * *

When she could see again, dusk had come down the valley, and with it the sound of the vanguard.

Horns, far up the pass. Torchlight strung along the ridgeline like a burning rosary. The black sun banner cresting the ridge where she had knelt an hour ago, and beneath it, she knew without seeing, the litter of the usurper, coming down to look upon the miracle he had built his life against.

Aldric had drawn her up from the glass. Sefa stood between them, swaying, holding the rite's last knot of incense like a candle against the wind. And on the white ground between them, Emrys Ishakawa lay unchanged—hands open, face peaceful, the sigil burned away leaving only smooth unbitten skin, a body with no mark on it at all, which frightened Maren more than any wound would have.

"They'll reach the defile within the hour," Aldric said. His sword was drawn, pointless, magnificent. "Forty thousand."

"Twenty-two hundred reach the defile first," Maren said. The old machinery woke in her, the ledgers, the logistics, and she was ashamed of the relief of it and used it anyway. "Vanguard only. The rest camp at the ridge—there's no water below the treeline and Vaelric's quartermasters know it. You have an hour before his first column tests the gate."

"And what turns them back?" Aldric asked bitterly. "Prayer?"

"Yes," Maren said. "But not ours."

She looked down at Emrys's body—at the covenant wearing a dead scribe like a garment, or a sleeping one, she could not tell—and felt the new law humming in the stone under her knees, in the air, in her own pulse, a law that had just answered her after eleven years and might, if the stories Sefa had gasped out in the cellar were true, be able to answer armies too.

Oaths dissolving. Soldiers finding their vows gone soft as rope rotted through. She had read the fragment in Sefa's recovered effects and dismissed it as heresy. Now she stood on the covenant's doorstep and understood it was a weather report.

"Get them clear of the defile," she said, turning to face up-valley, toward the torches, toward the black sun, toward the man she had served and the doctrine she had buried an hour ago on her knees in the glass. "Both of them. Whatever wakes here—or doesn't—it shouldn't wake under Vaelric's shadow."

"And you?" Sefa asked quietly.

Maren Vess, inquisitor of the Corvale school, keeper of eleven years of ledgers written in ash, drew herself up in her gray coat and began, deliberately, to strip it off—to pull the black sun badge from her collar, pin by pin, each one a small cold click like a lock opening.

"I'm going to go brief the usurper," she said. "Every report he receives tonight, I write. And tonight—" she glanced once more at the small still figure on the white ground, at the gate leaning overhead with its ancient burned plea, *"ask again"*—"tonight I'm going to tell him the truth. All of it. Slowly. In the language of ledgers, so he can't look away."

Up the valley, the horns sounded again, closer now, and the first torchlight spilled down over the rim of the defile like blood over a threshold—and in the silence between the horn calls, so faint she would spend the rest of her life unsure whether she had imagined it, Maren heard the ground beneath Emrys Ishakawa's open hands begin, very softly, to glow.
