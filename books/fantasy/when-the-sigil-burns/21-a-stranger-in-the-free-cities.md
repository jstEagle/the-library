## 21. A Stranger in the Free Cities

The first thing Emrys knew was the sound of water.

Not rain, not a river—something gentler. A fountain, perhaps, or a cistern fed by slow pipes, its surface broken and mended over and over by some small deliberate motion. A hand trailing through it. Their own hand, they realized. Their own fingers, pale in early light, moving through green water that smelled of stone and salt.

They were lying on a bench in a walled garden. Ivy had gone amber along the bricks. Above them, laundry lines crossed a strip of sky like rigging on a patient ship, and somewhere beyond the wall a city was waking: cart wheels, a bell with a cracked voice, gulls arguing over something thrown from a window.

Emrys opened their eyes fully and waited for the world to arrive.

It did not.

There was no world behind their eyes. No corridor of memory leading back to a childhood, a mother's kitchen, a debt ledger with neat columns of ink. They reached inward the way one reaches for a stair in the dark—and found nothing, only smooth emptiness, vast and clean as fresh paper.

They did not scream. That surprised them later, when they could think about it at all. Instead they lay very still on the bench, breathing, listening to the water, and felt for the shape of what was missing the way a tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth. Something enormous had been taken. They could feel its absence the way a room feels an absence of furniture: not by knowing what stood there, but by all the space where nothing stood.

*I am Emrys.*

The name arrived without permission, floating up out of nowhere, and they clung to it. *I am Emrys Ishikawa.* But even that felt less like a possession than a label someone else had sewn into their collar. They said it aloud, testing.

"Emrys," they said, to the garden, to the gulls. "My name is Emrys."

A woman hanging sheets two walls away called back, warmly, without looking, "Good morning to you too, Emrys."

They sat up so fast the bench scraped stone.

*She knows my name.* The thought landed strangely—not with fear but with a kind of vertigo, as if the whole city were a book they'd been dropped into at the final page. Everyone knew them. That was the second thing they learned, in the hours that followed, and it was worse than knowing no one, because it meant there was a story about them already written, and they had been erased from its authorship.

* * *

Meridian took them in the way a tide takes in driftwood: completely, unquestioningly, as though the sea had always intended this.

They walked out of the garden into streets of white stone and colored awnings, and people turned. Not the furtive turns of the hunted recognizing danger—Emrys's body knew that flinch even if their mind didn't; something in their shoulders braced for it—but open turns, faces lifting, hands rising half-formed into gestures of greeting or blessing. An old fishmonger stopped gutting his catch mid-stroke to watch them pass. A child ran alongside for half a street before her mother caught her hand, laughing, apologizing.

"You're awake," said a baker, flour to his elbows, blocking his own doorway to look at them. "Praise the Quiet Hours, you're actually awake."

"I'm sorry," Emrys said. "Do I know you?"

The baker's face did something complicated—joy folding down into grief and back again. "No," he said gently. "None of us do. And all of us do." He pressed a warm roll into their hands, refused payment twice, and let them go.

By noon Emrys understood the shape of it, or the shape of the hole where understanding should have been. There had been a war. There had been an army coming down out of the Thornfell mountains, forty thousand strong under the usurper Vaelric, and then—one morning, weeks ago—the army had simply stopped. Turned around. Soldiers weeping at their campfires, deserting in droves, oaths dissolving off their tongues like sugar in rain. Vaelric himself had withdrawn east, his banners folded, his ambition apparently amputated at the wrist.

And at the center of every account, spoken of with the hushed certainty usually reserved for miracles, was a name: *the Covenant-Bearer*. Someone who had gone up to the Gates of Ash and done something. Given something. Become something.

"The covenant walks among us now," a street preacher was telling a knot of listeners near the harbor, arms wide. "Written not in stone but in a living pulse! You can hear it, friends—stand close enough and you'll feel your own heart remember what it swore!"

The crowd parted for Emrys without being asked.

They stood at the edge of that circle of faces—all of them turned toward Emrys, none of them demanding anything—and felt the strangest sensation of their borrowed life so far. It was not fame. It was closer to being a wellspring in a dry country: everyone drew near, everyone drank, no one asked the water how it felt about being drunk.

*What did I give?* They searched the emptiness inside themselves and found, at the very bottom of it, faint as heat off stone after sundown, a pulse that was not entirely their own. When they held still and listened, it kept a rhythm—a law, almost, a sentence repeating:

*What is freely given cannot be taken. What is taken must be returned.*

Over and over, gentle as the fountain that morning. The covenant, living in their blood. They put a hand over their heart and the preacher saw the gesture and fell to his knees, and the crowd followed him down like wheat bending, and Emrys stood alone upright in a harvest of strangers and felt nothing but the terrible lightness of having no floor beneath their history.

* * *

They learned the practical things quickly, because their hands remembered what their head did not.

Their fingers found letters and loved them. In a chandler's shop they picked up a stub of charcoal and, without thinking, began writing on the slate wall—clean scribe's script, ledger-perfect—and stopped only when the chandler made a sound like a man witnessing a minor resurrection. "That's how they wrote it," she whispered. "The new law. On the gate itself. They say the letters are still warm."

Their feet knew mountain paths. Crossing the stepped streets toward the upper terraces, Emrys chose routes by instinct—shaded ways, cut-throughs, the smuggler's logic of a body that had once needed to be invisible—and each time they arrived faster than they should have, and each time they paused, heart hammering with a familiarity that had no face attached to it. Whoever they had been, they had spent years learning to move unseen through hostile country. That much survived. The reasons why did not.

At dusk they climbed to the seawall, because the crowd thinned there and because something in them wanted height, wanted to see edges. Meridian spread below in its bowl of lamplight, harbor water black and star-pricked. Beyond it, far north and east, the Thornfells stood against the last light like a jawline of old bone.

Emrys looked at those mountains and their chest hurt.

Not grief exactly—they had checked, carefully, methodically, and found no grief anywhere in themselves, which was its own horror. This was different. It was the ache of a room they'd lived in, seen now from outside. They knew those mountains the way you know a house you grew up in even after the house has burned: by absence, by the negative space where everything used to stand.

*I gave everything,* they thought, testing the idea against the emptiness. *I gave it freely. And I don't know what 'everything' contained.*

A roll of bread appeared at their elbow. Warm. The same baker's work, wrapped in cloth, set down silently on the seawall stones beside them by hands they hadn't heard approach.

"You'll want to eat," said a voice. Low. Careful. A voice that was trying very hard to be a stranger's voice, and failing at it beautifully. "You didn't eat anything today except that morning roll. I watched."

Emrys turned.

He was tall, broad through the shoulders, with gray coming into a soldier's close-cropped hair and a scar splitting one eyebrow. He wore no colors and carried no visible blade, but he stood like a blade stands in a scabbard—straight, sheathed, aware. His eyes were the problem. They were kind, and they were wrecked, and they looked at Emrys the way men look at harbors from ships that have been too long at sea.

"I know you," Emrys said slowly. "Don't I? Everyone seems to know me. But you—" They searched the stranger's face for anything, a splinter, a shadow. Nothing came. "You look at me differently."

"Do I?" He almost smiled. It cost him something. "I'm Aldric. Aldric Vane." He said his own name like he was checking whether it still fit.

"Aldric." Emrys tried it. The syllables sat in their mouth with a weight that had no explanation. "Why does your name feel like a door?"

His breath caught—small, quickly mastered, but Emrys's body noticed it, catalogued it, filed it in a drawer whose key was also missing.

"There was a knight by that name," Aldric said, after a moment. "In the stories they tell here. He went up the mountain with the Covenant-Bearer. Carried them partway, when they could no longer carry themselves." He looked out at the darkening water. "I've met him. Once or twice. He's not in the stories anymore. He's just a man who lost—" The sentence stopped. He started another one, further down the same road. "He's just a man who owes a debt he can't repay, because the person he owed it to doesn't exist."

"That's grim company to keep."

"He's gotten used to grim company." Now the smile came, crooked and tired and real. "You have no idea."

Emrys broke the bread and offered him half. It was an impulse, unconsidered—and Aldric looked at the offered piece for a long three seconds before taking it, and when he did, his hand shook slightly, and Emrys pretended not to see, because pretending seemed like a kindness their body remembered how to give.

They ate in silence while Meridian's lamps multiplied below.

* * *

"You should know what people expect of you," Aldric said eventually. He spoke like a man laying out maps, careful to keep his own borders marked. "The council wants a ceremony. A temple, officially—they're building you one, did you know? Started before you woke. The Temple of the Living Law." His mouth twisted. "Priests are already applying. Sefa would have hated it."

"Sefa?"

Another hitch in his breathing. He was getting better at hiding them, but not good enough, and Emrys began to suspect the gaps between his sentences were where most of him actually lived. "A friend," he said. "Of yours. Of ours. She isn't—" He stopped again, longer this time. "You gave more than memories, up there. Some things can't come back from a place like the Gates of Ash. Sefa was one of them."

"I'm sorry," Emrys said, and meant it in the way you mean sympathy for a character in a play—true, and weightless, and ashamed of its own weightlessness.

"Don't be." Gently. "Grief is a thing you carry for people you remember. You're not obligated to carry mine." He turned to face them fully, and whatever he'd been holding in check all evening he let show for just a moment—an ocean of it, dammed behind ordinary eyes—and then, deliberately, folded it away again. "I didn't come here to make you sad. I came to tell you one thing, and then I'll go, and you never have to see me again if you don't want."

"Tell me."

"When you wake up tomorrow, you won't know who you are. Nobody will ever hand it back to you—it's gone, truly gone, and no god or council or grieving knight is going to return it." His voice stayed level, but his knuckles whitened on the seawall stone. "So everyone in this city is going to try to build you a self out of their stories. The temple, the council, the preachers, the poets. They'll tell you what you did until you start believing their version instead of finding your own." He leaned closer, and his voice dropped. "Don't let them. Whoever you were—you didn't become that person by accepting other people's versions of you. I know that much. It's the only thing I know that matters, and I know it about you specifically."

Emrys stared at him. Inside their hollow chest, the covenant pulsed its patient law, and beneath it—very faint, like a coal refusing frost—something stirred that was not the covenant at all. Not memory. Just orientation. The way a compass needle moves without knowing why.

"Why are you telling me this?" they asked. "You said you're nobody to me."

"I am," Aldric agreed. "Completely." He rose, brushed bread crumbs from his knees, and looked north at the mountains one last time. "But I'm going to be here anyway. Tomorrow evening, and the evening after. Down at the chandler's steps, where the boats come in. If you want to learn who you might be—I could teach you. One evening at a time. Your story, as I knew it. The true version, as best I can tell it, and you can argue with every word." A pause. "You liked to argue."

"I don't remember liking anything."

"No," he said softly. "But remembering isn't the same as being. That's the whole lesson, Emrys. That's the first one, free of charge." He stepped back, and the distance opened between them like water. "Sleep. Eat. Be angry at the god who did this to you—that's allowed too, and honestly, you earned it. And if tomorrow you decide a stranger's company is worth an hour of your evening, I'll be on the steps."

He turned to go.

"Aldric."

He stopped. Didn't turn. Stood in the lamplight like a man waiting for a verdict.

"Why did you hesitate, before? When I offered you the bread."

A long silence. The harbor lapped the stones. Somewhere below, the cracked bell rang the hour, and its imperfection made it somehow more honest than a perfect bell would have been.

"Because once," Aldric said finally, still facing away, "someone shared food with me on a wall just like this one. And it was the last night before everything changed, and neither of us knew it, and I would give—" His voice frayed. He mastered it. "I would give a great deal to have that night back. So when you offered me bread, for a moment I thought—" He shook his head. "It doesn't matter what I thought. Goodnight, Emrys."

He walked away down the seawall stairs, unhurried, a big man making himself smaller with every step, and Emrys stood alone above the black water with the covenant ticking in their chest and a half-eaten roll in their hand and one clear, cold, astonishing thought:

*I don't remember him. But my body leaned after him when he left. My whole hollow self leaned, like grass after wind.*

They looked down at their palm—the left one—and found, ghosted across it in skin finer than scar tissue, a pattern like a branching thorn. It wasn't a mark anymore. It was healed over, incorporated, part of them. But when they pressed it lightly with their thumb, warmth answered.

And in that warmth, for half a heartbeat, they heard—not words, not memory, just tone, just timbre—a voice like low sun on stone, saying something they couldn't hold onto, in a register they somehow recognized as *affection*.

Then it was gone, and the emptiness closed over it, and Emrys stood trembling on the seawall of a city that worshipped them, holding a stranger's bread.

Tomorrow, they decided, they would go down to the chandler's steps. Not because they remembered. Because they chose. And if choice was all that remained—if identity was not a treasure to recover but a building to raise, plank by argued-over plank, evening by evening—then let it begin with a knight-shaped stranger and a story they were allowed to dispute.

They finished the bread. Below, the tide came in, patient, certain, erasing and rewriting the sand.

On the chandler's steps at dusk the next day, Aldric Vane waited with a lantern lit, and when he saw Emrys coming down through the crowd—saw them choose him, walk toward him, sit beside him—he exhaled like a man surfacing, and said, in a voice that shook only a little:

"All right. Lesson one. Your name is Emrys Ishikawa, and the first thing you ever taught me was that debts are just love wearing armor." He hesitated, lantern-light trembling in his ruined, hopeful eyes. "Shall I tell you how you learned that?"
