## 1. The Courier's Last Breath

The trouble with selling luck in a city that had outlawed magic was that you had to be very, very good at pretending it was nothing.

Marguerite Almeida had gotten good at nothing. Three years of it. She kept her shop on Candlewick Row with its crooked shutters and its smell of dried lavender and beeswax, and she sold charms the way bakers sold day-old bread—cheaply, apologetically, and with no promises. Knotted red thread for safe travel. Iron nails wrapped in cloth against nightmares. Dried four-leaf clovers pressed between glass, which were just clovers, and everyone knew it, and bought them anyway because hope was legal even when wonder wasn't.

"Nothing in here works," she told every customer who lingered too long at the shelf of knotwork. "That's the point. If anything worked, we'd both be hanged."

They laughed. They always laughed, the way people laugh at jokes that aren't quite jokes, and they paid their coppers, and Marguerite counted them into a tin box that had once held a very different kind of treasure, and she went home to her narrow room above the shop and did not dream. She'd trained herself out of dreaming years ago. Dreams were doors, and she had nailed all hers shut.

The stranger came in an hour before close, on a Tuesday gray as dishwater, when the fog off the Vessen River was thick enough to chew.

She heard him before she saw him—the wrongness of his footsteps on her stairs, uneven, dragging, each one costing him something. She was already reaching under the counter for the little knife she wasn't supposed to need when the door banged open and he fell through it.

He was young. That was her first stupid thought. He was so *young*, maybe twenty, with river water freezing in his hair and a dark stain spreading down his left side that she recognized instantly, professionally, as the kind you don't come back from. His coat had been fine once. Magistrate's courier blue.

"Shop's closing," Marguerite said, because what else did anyone say to a dying man?

He laughed—a wet, terrible sound—and pushed himself up from the floorboards by way of her counter, leaving a red smear on the wood that she would later think about far too much. "Almeida." Her name came out cracked. "You're Almeida."

"No one by that name here."

"Marguerite." He gripped her wrist with fingers cold as river stones. "I ran the length of the city for this. Don't you dare make it worthless."

Every instinct she'd honed over three years said: *deny, deflect, get him out before someone sees.* Instead she heard herself say, "Who sent you?"

"The map." He fumbled inside his coat, and his hand came out shaking, holding a roll of oilcloth no bigger than her forearm, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. He pressed it into her hands and folded her fingers over it, hard, like he could transfer his urgency through skin. "The sigil is falling. Do you understand? It's been failing since the night it broke, and Corvane knows, and he's been *feeding* it—no—" he coughed, grimaced, "—starving it. Starving it so it falls faster. Thirteenth moon. He means to claim the gate whole."

The word *gate* went through her like a blade finding an old scar.

"Wrong man," she whispered. "Wrong shop. I sell thread and clover."

"You sell *nothing*," he said, "and you're the best liar in Vessengard, and I know what you are because I carried messages for the man who unmade your covenant, and I couldn't—I couldn't carry one more." Blood flecked his lips. He didn't seem to notice or care. "Corvane. Say the name. Say it and remember."

"I don't remember anything."

"Liar," he said gently, and died.

Not dramatically. Not all at once. He simply leaned harder against the counter until leaning became sliding, and then he was on her floor among the lavender dust, and his eyes stayed open on the ceiling beams, and the shop was very quiet except for her own breathing and the rain starting against the windows.

Marguerite stood there for a long moment with the map burning in her hands.

Then she did the thing she had sworn never to do again, the thing three years of careful nothing had been built to prevent: she looked.

The wax seal parted under her thumbnail too easily, warm, almost eager. Inside, a sheet of good paper covered in inked lines—not roads, not rivers, but a sigil diagram, the great sealing mark rendered as a city plan, waypoints marked where its anchor-points stood disguised as landmarks across Vessengard. The first waypoint was annotated in a cramped hand: *the ferryman's shrine below Old Bridge. Ask for Sef. Trust nothing else.* And at the center of it all, drawn in faded gold leaf that shimmered when she moved, the gate itself—and around the gate, unmistakably, a hairline fracture through the sigil's heart. Growing. Someone had redrawn that fracture line eleven times, each pass longer than the last.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere underneath the fear, something old and furious had woken up and taken over the steering.

*Corvane.* The name she'd spent three years not thinking. Aldous Corvane, magistrate, reformer, the beloved face of the new lawful Vessengard—the man whose signature was on the Edict of Severance, whose speeches about safety she'd heard through tavern windows while she ran. The man who had broken the covenant and let the reprisal fall on every hedge-witch and charm-seller in the city while he rose on the ashes.

And her sister—

No. She put that thought down like setting fire to a letter. Later. Maybe never.

The bells started then. Not the hour bells—the watch bells, two streets over, then one, then closer. Enforcers didn't ring bells for a drowned courier found in the river. They rang bells when they'd followed a blood trail.

Marguerite looked down at the dead boy on her floor and felt the guilt arrive like weather, total and inescapable: *he came to me because I was the keeper, and I am not the keeper anymore, and now he is dead on my floor.*

"Damn you," she told him softly. "Damn you for finding me."

Outside, boots on the stairs.

Three years ago she had walked away from the gate with nothing but the clothes she wore, and in those three years she had built exactly one thing worth keeping: a way out. Every charm-shop has a back room. Hers had a wall, and behind the wall a space between buildings barely wider than her shoulders, and woven into the bricks of that space—thread by patient thread, knot by knot, over two hundred nights—was a working she'd never named aloud. Not a spell. Spells left residue. Just craft. Old craft. The kind that looked like nothing, smelled like nothing, read as nothing even to enforcers with detection charms strung round their necks like festival garlands.

She took the map, took her coat, took the tin box of coppers because sentiment apparently extended to money, and paused at the door of the back room long enough to look at the dead courier one more time.

"I'll finish it," she promised him, not knowing yet if it was a lie.

Then she slipped through the hidden door, drew the threads closed behind her, and stood in the dark crack between buildings as the shop door burst open above and boots thundered across the floorboards she'd just left.

Through the gap in the wall she saw them: three enforcers in Corvane's gray-and-gold, moving through her shop with the bored efficiency of men doing this nightly. One knelt by the body. One tipped over the shelf of knotwork charms. The third picked up the fallen wax fragments of the seal, sniffed them, frowned, and turned slowly toward the back room.

Toward her.

Marguerite did the hardest thing she had done in three years: she waited. Craft held. Threads hummed against her palms like sleeping wasps. The enforcer stared at the wall for five seconds, ten, fifteen—then shook his head and called out, "Back room's clean. Courier came in off the street. Trail ends at the river."

Boots receded. Bells faded toward the waterfront, chasing a ghost.

Marguerite breathed, and the breath shook, and she slid the map from her coat and held it up to the thin light of the alley. Gold leaf glimmered. The fracture in the sigil's heart seemed, in this light, to have grown again since she'd unfolded it.

Below Old Bridge, a ferryman's shrine. A name: *Sef.*

A fae name, if she'd ever heard one. And fae, in Vessengard, were either dead, fled, or hiding worse than she was.

She pulled her hood up and stepped out into the rain, toward the river, carrying the past she'd buried in her own two hands.
