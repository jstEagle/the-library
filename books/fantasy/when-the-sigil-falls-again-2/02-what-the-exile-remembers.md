## 2. What the Exile Remembers

The waypoint was a well.

Of course it was. The map's first mark sat at the center of the Saltmarket, ringed by fishmongers and fortune-sellers who'd never once looked down and wondered why their coins came up damp. Marguerite had walked over it for three years. Everyone had. The city had a talent for walking over things.

She waited until dusk, when the market stalls folded up and the lamplighters made their slow circuit, then knelt at the well's lip and held the courier's map against the old iron grate. The wax seal, broken in her shaking hands that morning, had left red crescents on her thumbs. Under her touch, ink that had been smudged and ordinary began to move—lines crawling like veins toward the second waypoint, out past the tanneries, into the hills where the gate itself slept under stone.

"Three years," said a voice above her, "and you still hold it like an apology."

She came up fast, charm-flare already lit between her fingers—a trick worth two coppers on market days, lethal when you meant it. A man stood at the well's far side, half in shadow. Tall. Wrong. Not wrong the way enforcers were wrong, all armor and appetite. Wrong the way winter is wrong: patient, inevitable, dressed in a beggar's gray coat that fit him badly because no coat ever fits what you actually are.

His eyes caught the lamplight and gave none of it back.

"You're fae." She kept the flare burning. "In Vessengard. You're either dead or you're mine to report."

"I'm Sef." He stepped closer, unbothered by the light in her hand, and she saw now how thin he'd become—the exile's leanness, magic starved down to bone. "And you're the keeper of Vessengard's gate. Or you were."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Yes," he said softly, "you do. That's the whole tragedy of you."

* * *

Sef

He had watched her for three years without meaning to.

Exiles don't get to choose their obsessions; obsession is what fills the space where home used to be. Sef had been cast across the threshold in the same night the covenant shattered—he could still feel the door slamming behind him, a bruise on the world—and he had landed in this city of wet stone and outlawed wonder with nothing but his memory, which was both his punishment and his only wealth.

He remembered the gate whole. He remembered the sigil singing its low note through every root and river, the two realms breathing against each other like sleeping sisters. And he remembered the keeper's daughter, Marguerite Almeida, standing at the threshold with blood on her hands and her sister's name in her mouth, doing nothing.

That was the version he'd carried. He fed it nightly, the way other exiles fed grudges. So when he saw her kneel at the well with the courier's map—the map he'd spent two years arranging to have delivered, courier by expendable courier, until one finally survived long enough to reach her—he felt something worse than anger. He felt cheated. She wasn't magnificent in her ruin. She was small, and tired, and holding the flare like a woman defending a market stall.

"The map has four waypoints," he said. "You've found one. Corvane's people will find you before you find the second unless you come with me."

"Why would I trust you?"

"Because I hate him more than I hate you." He let that sit. "And that took effort. You should be flattered."

Her flare guttered. Not extinguished—he noticed she banked it rather than lost it, a keeper's instinct even after three years of pretending otherwise—but lowered.

"My sister," Marguerite said, and stopped, as if the sentence were a door she'd welded shut.

"Say her name."

"Don't."

"You'll have to. Before the end, you'll have to say all of it." Sef crouched at the well's edge, and beneath them, faint as a heartbeat, something answered: the old ward-stone at the well's bottom waking at the nearness of keeper-blood. "Because the sigil isn't just falling, Marguerite. It's being unwound. Thread by thread, by hands that know the knot. And those hands learned the knot from someone who stood at your sister's side that night."

* * *

Marguerite

She had buried the memory so deep she'd convinced herself it was mercy.

Now, in the dark beside a well that hummed with everything she'd abandoned, Sef pried it up like a coffin lid.

"There was a price," he said. "You know there was a price. Covenants are not free. The original binding cost the gatekeepers a life for every generation—a keeper gives a daughter, the gate stays quiet. Your grandmother paid. Your mother paid. And when your turn came—"

"I ran," Marguerite said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "I stood at the threshold with my hand on the sigil and I couldn't—I couldn't say her name into the stone. I couldn't give Lira. So I broke the circle instead. I thought—if the covenant fails, no one pays. I thought I was saving her."

"And?"

"And Corvane was there." The words came faster now, three years of dam breaking. "He was the covenant's under-clerk then, nobody, taking minutes. But he wanted what the gate held. When the binding cracked, the power didn't disperse—it *pooled*, and he drank from the pool. He took enough to make himself a magistrate. Enough to buy the law that outlawed magic everywhere except inside his own skin." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "They love him. They put his face on the coin."

"Your sister didn't die because you failed," Sef said quietly. "Look at me. Look. Lira saw the crack you made. She understood what would pour through it. And she went into the gap herself—not as sacrifice demanded, but as sacrifice chosen. To hold the breach until it could be sealed. It bought the city three years. It bought *you* three years."

"That's worse."

"It usually is." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it unbearable. "Corvane needed Lira's death to look like yours. Guilt keeps a keeper running. Running keeps her from the gate. Three years, Marguerite. How many couriers did he kill getting that map away from you? And now the thirteenth moon rises and the seal Lira bought with her life is nearly threadbare, and he means to complete his claim while everyone who remembers is dead or scattered or drowning in shame."

Marguerite pressed her palms flat against the cold well-iron. Beneath it, the ward-stone pulsed against her skin like a small stubborn heart. Lira's heart, some treacherous part of her whispered.

"What does the rest of the map want?" she asked.

* * *

Sef

He spread the map between them on the well's lip, weighting the corners with coins, and watched the ink crawl.

Four waypoints. The well, which recognized her blood. Then the Tannery Stair, where the city's oldest grief was poured into the river nightly. Then the Widow's Hill, where the first covenant had been signed in a language no human throat could make anymore—except hers, because keepers learned it at the breast. And then the gate.

But the map showed more than roads. As Marguerite's fingers traced the route, secondary lines surfaced, thin as spider silk, and Sef went cold seeing them. Patrol routes. Enforcer rotations. Corvane's own hand annotated in copper-bright script: *the thirteenth moon, full and doubled, claim completed.*

"He's mapped it too," Sef said. "Same waypoints. He's known the sequence longer than we have."

"Then we're racing a man who owns the roads."

"We're racing a man who needs you alive," Sef corrected. "Think. Why send couriers instead of soldiers? Why leave you three years of borrowed time? A claim on the gate isn't complete without the keeper's line. He doesn't want you dead, Marguerite. He wants you *willing*. There's a difference, and it's the only weapon we have."

She looked at him then—really looked, the flare-light catching the exile's hunger in her face and finding, he suspected, its twin in his.

"You remember the gate whole," she said. "What it sounded like."

"Like a held breath. Like the moment before a name is spoken."

"Then help me speak it again." She gathered the map, and her hands had stopped shaking. "Not for forgiveness. Not for forgetting. I want my sister's death to have *bought* something. I want it witnessed."

Sef rose, joints cracking like green wood, three years of exile aching in every one. An alliance built from shared shame was a rotten foundation, he knew. Rotten things grow the fastest, though.

"The Tannery Stair," he said. "Before midnight. We move tonight."

Above them, past the chimney-smoke and the lamplighters' fading circles, the sky was clearing—and high and cold and wrong, the moon hung already fat with its impossible thirteenth fullness, rising weeks early, as if the night itself had been bribed.
