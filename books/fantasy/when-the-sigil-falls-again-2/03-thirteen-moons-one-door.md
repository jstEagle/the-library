## 3. Thirteen Moons, One Door

They took me in the fish market, of all places, between a stall of silver-skinned catch and a boy selling candied ginger. No drama. No chains rattling. Just four enforcers in grey livery closing around me like weather, and the crowd parting with the practiced indifference of people who had learned that noticing things was dangerous.

The tall one didn't even draw his blade. He simply held out an open hand.

"Magistrate's compliments," he said. "He'd like a word about maps."

I looked at that hand for a long moment. I could have run. Sef had taught me three ways out of the market by now—through the smokehouse, down the old culvert, over the tanner's roof where the tiles were loose and the drop forgiving. But the boy with the ginger was watching me with wide eyes, and one of the enforcers had drifted toward him without seeming to move at all.

So I put my wrist in the tall man's grip, and I let them walk me out into the grey afternoon like a lady being escorted to tea.

* * *

Corvane received me in a room with no guards and no windows, which told me everything: he wasn't afraid of me, and he didn't want witnesses.

He looked exactly as the city loved him. Silver at the temples, arranged carelessly, as though it had arrived there through honest work. A magistrate's coat in dove wool. Hands folded on the table before him like a patient man, a reasonable man, a man you could trust with your grievances.

"Marguerite Almeida." He said my name the way you'd set down a fragile thing. "Three years. You've been three streets from my office this whole time. Selling luck-charms."

"They work," I said. "Mostly placebo. The rest is good thread."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger. Recognition. The look of a man finding a coin he'd dropped years ago and assumed lost forever.

"I'm not going to hurt you," he said. "I want you to understand something first. Sit."

I stayed standing. He shrugged, as if my defiance were a charming local custom.

"The covenant had a price," he said. "You know it better than anyone alive. Blood given freely, every generation, by the keeper's line. Your mother paid it. Her mother before her. And then it came to you and your sister, and the ledger demanded—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. "You were children of that arithmetic your whole life. You never got to choose anything. Neither did she."

My hands had gone cold. I kept them still.

"So when I broke the sigil," Corvane went on, softly, "I know what you think you saw. Tyranny. Theft. I've read what the old keepers wrote about men like me. But consider what I actually did, Marguerite. I ended the tithe. No more blood. No more daughters carved up by an ancient contract no one signed. Magic outlawed, yes—and look around you. Nobody bleeds for the gate anymore. Nobody remembers the price. Isn't that a mercy?"

"It's amnesia," I said.

"It's peace." He rose, unhurried, and came around the table. Up close he smelled of ink and cedar, a respectable smell, an honest man's smell, and that was the most frightening thing about him. "Here is my offer, and I make it only once. The thirteenth moon rises in two nights. The sigil is failing—I need a keeper's hand to complete the claim, or the gate tears open on its own and both realms bleed into each other like a wound gone septic. Stand with me. Take your place beside the door. And I will give you this: no one will ever have to remember what it cost. Not the city. Not the fae. Not you."

He paused, and then he played the card he'd been holding since I walked in.

"Your sister died so the covenant could hold. If I hold the gate, her sacrifice becomes unnecessary. Unneeded. You could stop carrying it. All of it—the night, the blood, the sound she made. Gone. People forget, Marguerite. It's the one magic left that isn't illegal. Let me do the forgetting for you."

And there it was. The thing I had spent three years pretending I didn't want, laid out on polished wood like merchandise, priced fairly.

Because he was right about the worst part. There were mornings I woke reaching for her name and felt it slip. There were nights I'd have traded my hands to stop hearing that sound. He wasn't offering me power. He was offering me anesthesia, dressed as partnership, and he'd measured the dose perfectly.

"You've done this before," I said quietly. "Offered people their own wounds back, minus the pain. That's how you got the city to love you. That's how you broke the covenant with half of Vessengard thanking you."

"The city is happy."

"The city doesn't remember why it should be angry. That's not happiness. That's a debt being collected slowly."

His smile thinned by a degree. "Then refuse. But understand what refusal costs. In two nights the gate opens unclaimed, and everything your sister bought with her life burns anyway."

"Everything she bought," I said, "was the truth that the price existed. That someone always pays. You want a gate with no ledger. I want the ledger read aloud. Those aren't the same door, Corvane."

I turned for the door I'd come in by, knowing it wouldn't be there.

It wasn't. Just smooth stone where wood had been.

"I hoped you'd be difficult," he said behind me, and there was real regret in it, which made it worse. "The cell is comfortable. Two nights passes quickly, if you let it."

* * *

The cell was comfortable. That was the trap of it—a bed, a lamp, water, bread. A room designed to make waiting feel like your own idea. I sat against the wall and counted hours by the lamp's oil and tried not to think about my sister's name, because thinking it felt like spending it.

Sometime past midnight, the lamp guttered blue.

Not wind. Not draft. The flame bent sideways, toward the wall, and the wall exhaled cold air that smelled of river stones and green places, of a country I had closed my eyes to for three years.

A seam opened in the stone like a page turning.

Sef slid through sideways, all elbows and irritation, his exile-pale face lit by the dying lamp. "You're sitting," he whispered. "She's captured by the most dangerous man in Vessengard, and she's sitting."

"You found me."

"I followed the enforcers' route for six days while you were being politely kidnapped. Yes. I found you." He pressed something into my hand: my own charm-knife, the one with the harmless-looking stitching. "Riot in the Lantern Quarter. His own doing—he's been squeezing the grain tariffs, and tonight it boiled over. Half his enforcers are holding barricades. The other half are wondering whose side they're on."

"And him?"

"With them. A magistrate can't be seen to hide while his city burns. He'll want to be adored in the smoke." Sef's mouth twisted. "Men who sell forgetting always underestimate what people remember when they're hungry."

We went out through the seam he'd made, through walls that remembered older shapes, and the city took us in like a fever takes blood. Fire in the east. Bells rung ragged and wrong. Crowds moving through the streets carrying torches and loaves and broken fence-posts, shouting words I couldn't parse but understood completely. And above it all, rising over the rooftops, low and swollen and wrong-colored—

The moon. One night early, or my counting had failed, or the thirteenth moon simply refused to obey calendars built by men who wanted to forget it.

"Tonight," I said.

"Tonight," Sef said. "Run."

We ran. Through the Lantern Quarter with its burning stalls, across the canal on a bridge crowded with rioters who didn't spare us a glance, up through the terraces where the cobbles gave way to root-broken stone and the old road began—the one that led to the hollow hill, to the door, to everything I had buried.

Behind us, somewhere below the smoke, I heard a voice lifted over a crowd, calm and resonant, turning rage into order the way only Corvane could. He would figure it out soon. He would come.

Ahead, the hill opened its throat, and the last light of the failing sigil flickered along its arch like a heartbeat missing beats.

Two moons ago I had run from this door. Tonight I ran toward it, with my sister's name held whole and unanesthetized in my chest, every syllable of it costing me, every syllable of it mine.

The gate stood before us, and it was already open a crack.
