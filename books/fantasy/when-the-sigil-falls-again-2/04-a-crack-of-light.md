## 4. A Crack of Light

The gate was smaller than memory made it.

That was the first cruelty of returning. Three years of nightmares had built the threshold into something vast and cathedral-dark, a wound in the world wide enough to swallow a city. What stood before her now, in the hollow beneath Vessengard's oldest hill, was a door. Grey stone, taller than a man, older than any language she knew. Across its face, the sigil guttered like a candle in wind—half its lines already dark, the rest flickering with a light that hurt to look at directly.

Marguerite pressed her palm flat against the stone.

It was warm. That was worse than anything. The gate was dying warm, like a fevered child, like her sister's hand in hers on the last night she'd allowed herself to remember until tonight.

"It's thinner than paper," Sef said behind her. He stood at the mouth of the hollow, where the tunnel climbed toward the city's forgotten cisterns, and his whole body was angled outward, listening. "The veil. I can feel it tearing every time someone in the city tells a lie about what they saw three years ago. Every denial feeds it. Corvane built himself a throne out of forgetting."

"Then he'll feel this." She pulled the courier's map from her coat—the wax seal broken, the ink that moved when no one watched—and smoothed it against the stone beside the sigil. The map's final lines were not lines at all. They were instructions, written in the old covenant script, and she read them aloud so they would be real: *The keeper remakes the seal with what cannot be taken. Ink of blood is cheap. Ink of memory is binding.*

"You don't have to do all of it," Sef said quietly. "I remember too. Some of it. I could carry—"

"No." She said it before pride could dress itself up as mercy. "If I take shortcuts, it will show. The gate deserves an honest seam." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Three years running, and it turns out my whole job was bookkeeping."

Sef crossed to her and took her hands—his skin cool as riverbed stones—and held them a moment longer than practicality required. "Whatever you see in there," he said, "you come back out. That's the price you set for yourself, not the covenant. Promise me."

"I promise."

She opened her memories the way she'd once opened locks: deliberately, painfully, one tumbler at a time.

* * *

The first thing the stone took was small. A kitchen. Flour on her sister Wrenna's cheekbone where she'd pushed her hair back with dirty hands. Marguerite gave it up and felt the sigil drink it, one dead line flickering half-alive.

Then the harder ones. The night the covenant broke, unspooling now in full color because she refused to blur it. Corvane—not magistrate then, just an ambitious man with a scholar's smile—standing at this very gate with a blade forged from a broken oath, cutting the sigil's spine while she ran, actually *ran*, because Wrenna had shoved her through the closing door and shouted words Marguerite had spent three years pretending she hadn't heard: *Keep it alive somewhere else. Someone has to survive being the keeper.*

And the worst one. The moment after. Wrenna standing alone between Corvane and the collapsing threshold, choosing to pour herself into the breach so the gate wouldn't tear both worlds open at once. Not a sacrifice anyone asked of her. A choice she made while her sister fled.

Marguerite wept into the stone, and the stone drank that too, and the sigil burned brighter line by line. This was the difference between her blood and Corvane's power: he wanted magic without cost. The covenant had only ever been made of paid-for things.

*Outside*, she heard it dimly—shouts, boots, Sef's voice raised in a language that predated cities. Corvane had found them.

* * *

Sef held the tunnel mouth with nothing but a knife and the truth.

Corvane's enforcers came down the cistern steps in their grey lacquered armor, and the exile met them with the one weapon their master couldn't permit them to carry: memory. He spoke the names of their mothers. He spoke the smell of bread from before the outlawing, the sound of fiddle music in the Lantern Quarter, the exact blue of a fae lantern burning in a shop window that officially had never sold charms at all. Each word hit them like weather. Men who had been weapons wavered and became men again, and men who remembered did not easily raise blades against the person reminding them.

Corvane came last, descending alone, and he had stopped pretending. Power rolled off him in visible waves—the stolen covenant-force he'd hoarded for three years, enough to level the hill, and his face above it was almost gentle.

"Keeper," he said. "You're doing it wrong."

"Am I."

"The gate doesn't need your grief. It needs management. Order. I've kept this city peaceful for three years—that counts for something. Give me the threshold, and I'll hold it better than your order ever did. No more sacrifices. No more sisters. No one has to remember what it cost." His voice dropped, sincere as a priest's. "I'm offering you peace, Marguerite. You could finally sleep."

From inside the sigil-work, eyes closed, hands bleeding lightly against warm stone, Marguerite answered without opening them.

"That's exactly why I can't give it to you. You're offering everyone the thing I bought with flight and guilt. Forgetting isn't peace. It's just debt with the invoices burned." She drew a breath that shook. "My sister paid. So I remember. That's the whole covenant, Corvane. It always was."

His power broke against the reforging sigil like surf against a seawall, and for the first time in three years, Corvane looked small. The stolen force drained out of him in a long silver exhalation, drawn home through the crack beneath the door, returned to the covenant it had been skimmed from. When it was gone, he was only a tired man in expensive wool, standing in the dark with the people whose grief he'd been spending.

Above, faintly, the city was waking. Bells. Voices. Not a mob—a reckoning. Vessengard had remembered everything at once, and it was coming down the hill to find its magistrate.

Sef stepped aside and let them pass.

* * *

Dawn found Marguerite sitting against the sealed gate, spent, her hands bandaged with strips of her own shirt.

The sigil was whole. Whole, and different—because at the final stroke, with the covenant complete and the door ready to shut forever, she had stopped. Held the last line open. A crack. A hand's width of light along the gate's edge, thin as the courier's map, through which the two realms could see each other and never again pretend otherwise.

Sef lowered himself down beside her, exhausted, half-smiling. "Your order never specified fully closed."

"My order never specified anything," she said. "That was the problem. Closed doors breed magistrates." She flexed her bandaged fingers. "So. Keeper of the crack. It doesn't have the same ring."

"It has better ones." He looked at the seam of light, and something in his face eased for the first time since she'd known him. "Through there, someone will be waiting to know if I made it home. Not home. But near it."

"Then knock," Marguerite said, and reached up, and tapped the stone twice—polite as a neighbor.

Beyond the crack, faint but unmistakable, something knocked back.
