## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The fence post had been split by frost, the way most things in the Hollow Valley were eventually split — slowly, patiently, by forces too quiet to argue with. Silas Nystrom knelt in the wet grass at the eastern edge of his holding, drove a wedge into the crack with his palm, and began to bind it straight again with wire and willow-withy.

He had mended this same fence four times since he'd come to the valley. He expected to mend it four times more before he died here, and he found that thought not grim but comforting. Things that broke in the Hollow Valley stayed broken only if you let them. There was no sorcery here to knit bone overnight or summon timber out of thin air. There was only work, and weather, and the long stubborn mercy of doing the same small task until your hands knew it better than your head did.

That was why he'd chosen it, though he'd never told anyone so. The valley hummed.

Most people who came here took weeks to notice it — a low, sourceless vibration beneath everything, like a struck bell heard through a hillside. Travelers called it wind in the stone, or their own blood in their ears. But Silas knew what it was. It was silence itself, made solid: an old ward laid down by hands far older than his, a place where magic simply could not follow. Spells unraveled at the ridgeline like smoke meeting rain. Curses thinned to nothing. And swordspeech — the word-magic he carried in his scars like a disease carried in the blood — went mute on his tongue the moment he crossed the western pass.

He had tested it once, eleven years ago, standing knee-deep in the cold river at the valley's mouth, and tried to speak a single syllable of power. Nothing had come. His voice had been only a man's voice, hoarse and ordinary, saying nothing that could unmake anything.

He'd wept into the river then, and had never spoken of it since.

* * *

Wren found him at the fence line a little past midmorning, as she always did — arriving without announcement the way she arrived everywhere, all elbows and appetite, her brown hair escaping its braid in wisps that made her look perpetually mid-storm.

"You're doing it wrong," she announced.

"I'm doing it the way it's been done for two hundred years."

"Then it's been done wrong for two hundred years." She crouched beside him and studied the split post with the fierce attention she gave everything, as if the world were an argument she intended to win. She was sixteen, maybe seventeen; she didn't know herself, and had told him once, in one of her rare unguarded moments, that she'd run from a household she wouldn't name, carrying nothing but a bread knife and a grudge. He hadn't asked. In the valley, you didn't ask.

"Hold the wire lower," he said instead. "Wrap from the bottom up. A fence fails where it's weakest, not where it looks worst."

"That's either fencing advice or life advice."

"It's fence advice." He passed her the pliers. "Life advice is the same, though."

She snorted but did as he said, tongue between her teeth, and for a while they worked in the easy rhythm they'd fallen into over the past season. Wren had appeared at his gate in late autumn, soaked through and lying badly about being fine, and he had put her in the loft above the woodshed because turning her out would have meant becoming the kind of man who turned children out in the rain, and he had already been enough kinds of men.

After the fence came the knives. This was the other thing they did together, three mornings a week, in the flat clearing behind the woodshed. He taught her the way his own teacher had taught him, back when his forearms were smooth and his name was different — grip first, then balance, then the short brutal geometry of a blade used close to the body. Never flourish. Flourish was for men who expected to survive long enough to be admired.

"Again," he said, after she'd fumbled the inside disarm for the fourth time.

"My hands are cold."

"They'll be colder when someone's breaking your wrist. Again."

She came at him, quick and furious, and this time he let her get almost to the turn before he caught her blade against his forearm guard and rolled it away. She stumbled, caught herself, glared.

"You moved."

"I always move. Expect it." He reset her stance with two fingers at her elbow. "You're fast. Faster than you need to be. What you lack is patience, and patience can't be taught, only survived into."

"You sound like Brannoc when he's talking about marching."

"I sound like everyone who's ever buried someone impatient." He said it lightly, and watched the lightness land wrong — saw her eyes flick, just briefly, to his sleeves. To the forearms beneath them, which he kept covered even in high summer, even washing at the river, even alone.

The scars ran from wrist to elbow on both arms, old and pale and precise: rows of bound words, inked into flesh with needles and salt in some other lifetime. Oath-scars. Every soul in the valley knew there were people who had once done such things, in stories, in songs. No one in the valley knew that one of them lived behind the crooked fence at the eastern edge, growing beans.

"What are they?" Wren asked. She asked maybe once a month. It was practically liturgical.

"Old debts," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I give free."

She held his gaze a moment longer — she was getting bolder about that, testing the edges of him the way you test ice — and then let it go, because that was the covenant of the valley, unwritten and absolute: *you don't ask, and I don't lie to you more than I must.* He fed her lunch. She complained about the lentils. Peace, of the kind that has to be rebuilt every single morning.

* * *

Supper that evening was at Brannoc and Ivetta's cottage, because it was Duskday, and Duskday supper was law. The walk over was short and beautiful, the sky going copper behind the western crags, the valley's hum steady beneath his boots like the purr of some enormous sleeping animal.

Brannoc answered the door already complaining. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, gray at the temples, with a soldier's posture he couldn't slouch out of no matter how many years he spent keeping bees. Twenty years in the Vaelmoor border regiments, he'd admitted once, deep into his cups — twenty years, and then something at a place called Redford Bridge that he never named, after which he'd walked south until the hills stopped having roads. Silas had recognized the shape of that story. He had one much like it folded away himself.

"He's late," Brannoc said, jerking his thumb at the table, where Ivetta was ladling barley stew. "Your girl's late."

"She's not my girl. And she's coming."

"Wren's always late. Wren thinks time is a rumor."

Ivetta looked up from the pot and smiled at Silas — the particular smile she saved for the two of them squabbling, fond and weary, a mother's smile worn by a woman who had never had children but had patched up half the valley's. She was older than Brannoc by a decade, silver-braided, with healer's hands that seemed to carry warmth in them the way coals carry heat. She had pulled splinters from Wren's palms, set Brannoc's finger when he crushed it under a hive, and once, without asking, stitched a gash along Silas's ribs that he swore he'd gotten from falling on his own sickle.

She hadn't believed him. She'd stitched anyway. That was also the covenant.

They ate. Brannoc told a long, embellished story about a bear and a stolen ham that Silas had heard four times and would happily hear forty more. Ivetta scolded them both about salt. Through the little window, the first stars came out over the crags, and the valley hummed on, low and constant, the sound Silas had learned to sleep inside the way other men slept inside silence.

This, he thought, is the whole of it now. This is what I bought with everything else. Fences. Lentils. A runaway girl learning knife-work in the cold. An old soldier's complaints. A healer's warm hands. Not forgiveness — he wasn't fool enough to call it that — but a truce. A life built deliberately small, deliberately quiet, on ground where the past could not follow, because the past, for him, spoke a language this valley refused to hear.

He was reaching for the bread when the hum stuttered.

It was small. Anyone else might have missed it — a hitch in the constant tone beneath the world, like a singer drawing breath a half-beat early. But Silas had lived inside that sound for eleven years the way a sailor lives inside the sea's motion, and he felt it the way a sailor feels the sea go suddenly, wrongly still.

He set down the bread. Brannoc was frowning at the ceiling. Ivetta's hands had gone still around her cup.

"Did you—" Brannoc started.

"Yes."

The lamp flame stood perfectly upright, as if listening. Outside, the evening had gone strange — not dark exactly, but *attentive*, the way air feels before lightning. Somewhere far off, a dog began to bark, then stopped mid-cry, and the silence where its voice should have continued was worse than any sound.

Eleven years, Silas thought. Eleven years, and the valley had never once faltered. The ward was older than the chapel ruins on the ridge, older than any settlement here; the oldest gravestones spoke of it as a thing already ancient. It did not stutter. It did not *breathe*. Magic could not follow into the Hollow Valley, and the valley's own silence was the one fixed point left in his universe.

And just now, for the length of half a heartbeat, it had flickered — like a candle guttering, like an eye blinking open.

Then it steadied. The dog resumed barking, aggrieved. The lamp flame leaned back into its ordinary shape. Ivetta laughed, a little shakily, and said something about the cold making the stones sing, and Brannoc grunted agreement, and the evening repaired itself the way evenings do, papering over the wrongness with talk and dishes and the clatter of chairs.

But Silas sat very still, and did not touch the bread again.

Because he had felt the direction of it. The stutter had come rolling down from the ridge — from the north side, from the chapel ruins where Wren had taken to wandering lately, poking through the collapsed cloister with that restless curiosity of hers, hunting for whatever old things the valley kept and nobody wanted.

He told himself it was nothing. Stones settling. Frost in the foundations. The mind of an old man hearing ghosts in ordinary noise.

Then the door banged open, and there stood Wren — mud to the knees, cheeks flushed, eyes blazing with a light he recognized with a sinking, decades-deep dread, because he had seen that exact light in mirrors, long ago, in young faces gathered around forbidden pages. She was clutching something to her chest wrapped in her scarf. A book. Old, square, swollen with damp.

"Silas," she said, breathless, triumphant, terrible. "You have to see what I found."
