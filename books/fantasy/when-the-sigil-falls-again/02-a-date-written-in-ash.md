## 2. A Date Written in Ash

Wren had stolen three things in her life: a heel of bread from a baker in Marrow's Cross, a copper button she'd liked the shine of, and now — balanced against her ribs inside her coat — a book that felt like a sleeping animal.

She hadn't meant to take it. That was the lie she kept handing herself, and it kept refusing the delivery. She had meant to. She'd been coming out to the chapel ruins every evening for a week, once Silas dismissed them from knife-work, once Brannoc's snoring started up through the floorboards of the longhouse, once the valley settled into that particular hush that made even the creek sound ashamed of itself. She'd pried at the loose stone beneath the fallen altar until her fingernails split. And tonight the stone had given, grudging as an old man standing up from church, and there it was: a book wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, tied with cord that crumbled when she breathed on it.

The Hollow Valley didn't allow things like this. That was its whole personality, if you asked Wren. The valley hummed — a low, bone-deep note you stopped hearing after your first month but always felt, like a hand pressed flat between your shoulder blades. Magic couldn't live here. Ivetta said it made wounds heal slower and fevers break honest. Silas never said anything about it at all, which was how Wren knew he thought about it constantly.

So a book hidden under an altar in a magicless valley was either a contradiction or a confession.

She carried it back toward the ruins' east wall, where the last of the daylight came through the broken rose window in slanted amber bars. She sat on a slab of fallen masonry, hugged her knees up, and unwrapped the oilcloth with the concentration of someone defusing something.

The binding was leather gone dark as chestnuts, cracked along the spine. No title. Just a sigil stamped into the cover — a circle crossed by seven lines, like a wheel with too many spokes, and one spoke deliberately snapped short. Wren ran her thumb over the broken line and felt something in her sternum answer, a plucked-string sensation, faint and wrong, like hearing her own name spoken in a crowd by a stranger.

"Okay," she whispered. "Okay, okay."

She opened it.

The pages were heavy stock, foxed at the edges, handwritten in ink faded to the color of weak tea. The script was cramped and beautiful, the kind of hand that belonged to someone who'd been taught penmanship by a person they feared. It opened with a genealogy of kings she didn't recognize — *Aldric the Third of the Vael Line* circled twice in later ink — and then descended into prophecy, or what passed for it: verses about crowns and hollows, about a sword that would forget its own edge, about a door in a valley that would open on a night written in ash.

Wren read fast. Reading fast was her one unambiguous talent; she'd taught herself off grain sacks and wanted posters before anyone caught her at it. And so she was halfway through the third verse before the thing happened that made her stop breathing.

The dates changed.

Not all of them. Most stayed fixed, brown and ancient, minding their own business across the century the book claimed to span. But scattered through the margins, beside certain verses, were small inscriptions — day, month, year — and one by one, as her eyes moved down the page, those inscriptions began to darken. The tea-brown ink deepened. Bled. Turned the gray of wet slate, then the flat black of char, then blacker than black, a black that seemed to sit slightly above the page, wet-looking, fresh.

Tonight's date.

Wren knew the date. She'd known it since the thaw, since Ivetta had marked it on the longhouse beam in charcoal because Silas refused to keep any calendar more official than his own stubborn memory. *Frostwane, the ninth.* She looked at it now, inked in ash-black letters a hundred years old and still drying, beside a verse she read once, twice, three times without the words going in:

*When the sigil falls again, the door shall be unmade by the mouth of the young and unknowing. What sleeps beneath the crown shall reach for what sealed it, and the valley that forgets shall forget no longer.*

Her mouth had gone dry as flour dust. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing. And underneath all the fear — she would remember this later, hate herself for it later, be forgiven for it eventually — underneath everything there was a bright, terrible lift in her chest, the feeling she got at the top of a climb with the drop yawning below. Curiosity, Ivetta called it, like a diagnosis. *That curiosity is going to hang you someday, little bird.*

"I'm not young and unknowing," Wren told the empty chapel. Her voice came out thin. "I know plenty. I know eleven ways to pick a pocket and four to talk my way out of a hanging."

The book did not care. The next date had already begun to darken.

Here was the thing nobody understood about Wren — not Ivetta with her gentle lectures, not Brannoc with his patient sighs, certainly not Silas, who looked at her sometimes like she was a fire hazard in a hay barn. They thought she was reckless. She wasn't. Reckless people didn't weigh their risks; they just leapt. Wren weighed everything. She simply always concluded, in the end, that knowing was worth the price of knowing.

She needed to tell them. That was the responsible move. Run to the longhouse, put the book in front of Silas, watch his face do the thing it did — close like a shop shutter — and wait while he decided whether this was allowed to exist.

But the words were right there. And the verse had been written to be read aloud — she could feel it in the meter, in the way the stresses fell, old liturgical cadence, call-and-response, the kind of text that wanted a voice the way a lock wanted a key. Half a prophecy sitting silent on her tongue like a held breath.

"Just the first stanza," she reasoned aloud, because reasoning aloud was a habit and the chapel was empty and no one would ever know. "Just to see."

She cleared her throat. The valley hummed beneath her, steady, indifferent.

"*When the sigil falls again,*" Wren read, "*the door shall be unmade—*"

The word *unmade* left her mouth and the hum stopped.

It didn't fade. It didn't stutter. It simply ceased, mid-note, like a hand lifted from a drumhead, and the silence that replaced it was so total and so wrong that Wren's ears rang with the shape of the missing sound. Fourteen months she'd lived in the Hollow Valley. She had not once in fourteen months heard true silence. She'd forgotten it sounded like this — like standing in a room where someone had just died.

"Oh," she said. "No. No, no—"

The rose window's glass shivered. Not broke — shivered, all forty shards at once, like a dog shaking off water. Dust rose from the fallen masonry in slow spirals, defying every law of fall and weight she'd ever trusted, and hung in the amber light turning like the innards of a clock. Under her palms the stone grew warm. Then warmer. Then hot enough that she snatched her hands back with a yelp.

The book lay open in her lap, serene, and every marginal date on the page was black now, all of them, a century of appointments kept at once, and fresh letters were writing themselves across the bottom margin in a hand that was somehow, horribly, familiar — cramped, beautiful, the hand of someone taught by fear:

*Spoken. Heard. Opened.*

Wren slammed the book shut and threw it away from her. It hit the flagstones and fell open again, patiently, to the same page.

Somewhere beyond the chapel walls, the creek stopped mid-gurgle. The evening birds went up from the pines in one ragged scream. And from the direction of the longhouse — half a mile down the valley — Wren heard Brannoc bellow her name in a register she'd only ever heard him use once before, the night the slavers found their camp in the pass.

She ran.

She burst through the chapel door into a world rearranged. The air above the valley floor was *visible*, rippling like heat over a forge, except the light was wrong — silver, then violet, then a bruised nothing-color that hurt to look at directly. The hum was gone but its absence had weight, pressing down on the valley like a change in weather. And at the center of the meadow, where Silas had mended the fence that very morning, where nothing had ever stood in living memory, a gate was opening.

It wasn't built. It *happened* — a tall arch of that same bruised light assembling itself out of the air, stone and not-stone, edges bleeding smoke. Through it Wren could see somewhere else: cobbles slick with lamplight, spires, a skyline crowned by an enormous white tower wrapped in scaffolding of gold. Somewhere vast and loud and utterly unlike home.

Figures were running up the lane. Brannoc first, huge and fast despite the limp the winters gave him, axe in hand because Brannoc believed in being embarrassing rather than sorry. Behind him Ivetta, skirts hiked to her knees, satchel banging, and behind her —

Silas.

Silas was not running like a man frightened of the gate. He was running like a man recognizing it. His face — Wren had catalogued every expression that face owned in fourteen months, she'd thought — wore one she had never seen. Not fear. Recognition wearing fear's clothes, grief arriving ahead of understanding, and beneath it all, buried so deep she almost missed it: relief. Terrible, resigned relief, like a man who'd spent years listening for hoofbeats finally hearing them.

He saw her and his stride hitched.

"What," he called, and even at distance, even shouting, his voice was flat and controlled, "did you touch?"

Everything in her wanted to lie. Lying was easy; lying was her first language. But the gate was widening and the sky was the wrong color and the book was burning a hole through her coat like a coal, and so Wren did the second bravest thing she'd ever done.

She pulled it out and held it up, and shouted across the breaking valley: "It was already open! It was already written! I just — I read it!"

Ivetta reached her first, grabbed her arm, checked her over with healer's hands — pulse, pupils, the burn on her palms — all while the world unraveled. "You read it *aloud?*"

"It wanted to be read aloud."

"Nothing wants anything, Wren, that's the point of this valley, nothing here is *allowed* to want—"

"The ward's dead." Silas had arrived. He wasn't looking at the gate. He was looking at the trees, at the creek, at the ordinary miracle of a place with nothing magical in it, and his face said he was watching a friend die. He picked a pine needle off the ground, held it a moment, let it go. When he turned to Wren his voice was terribly gentle. "What did it say? Exactly. Word for word."

"*When the sigil falls again,*" she recited, hating how her voice shook, "*the door shall be unmade by the mouth of the young and unknowing.*"

Silas closed his eyes. Exhaled through his nose, long and slow, the way he did before sparring matches, before bad news, before everything. "Young," he repeated. "And unknowing." Something that might have been a laugh escaped him, brief and bitter as a spark. "It means you, Wren."

"I know it means me!"

Behind them the gate groaned — an actual groan, stone-deep, and the arch lurched wider, and through it they could all hear it now: bells, hundreds of them, ringing from that distant gold-wrapped tower, and beneath the bells a sound like a city cheering, sustained and rhythmic and wrong, the cheer of people who had been told to cheer.

Brannoc planted his axe head-down in the turf like a man planting a flag. "Right. Nobody's dragging me anywhere I don't walk." He glared at the gate with the loathing of a veteran meeting an old enemy. "We go around. There's a pass over the ridge, we take the high trail, we—"

The gate exhaled.

There was no other word for it. The bruised light swelled inward, drew breath, and pulled. Wren felt it take hold of her the way current takes hold of a wader — not violent, just absolute, patient as gravity. Her boots slid an inch through the grass. Then another. The book in her hands flung itself open, pages riffling backward to the ash-black date, and the pull centered on it, on her, threading through her ribs like a fishline.

"Drop it!" Silas shouted. "Wren, drop the book!"

She tried. Her fingers wouldn't obey. The pull wasn't on the book — she understood that in one clarifying, horrifying instant — the pull was on *her*. *Unmade by the mouth of the young and unknowing.* She was the hinge. She was the key turned.

Brannoc caught her wrist. Ivetta caught Brannoc. Silas grabbed Ivetta's belt and dug his heels into the turf and for one absurd, suspended moment the five of them made a chain against the end of the world, and Wren thought — wildly, giddily — *we might actually win this.*

Then Silas's boots tore free of the grass, and the valley let go of all of them at once.

They went through together. Wren had expected screaming — her own, certainly — but the crossing was silent, a held breath, the whole world pinched down to a needle's eye of bruised light. She felt the book's pages fluttering against her knuckles. She felt Brannoc's grip crush her wrist. She felt, absurdly, Silas's hand find the back of her coat, steadying her, the way he did when she slipped on the scree during knife-work, the oldest reflex of their strange family.

Then cobblestones slammed up to meet her boots, cold and slick with evening rain.

Bells. Hundreds of bells overhead, deafening, joyous, mandatory. Lamplight on wet stone. Spires stacked against a bruise-colored sky, and above them all the white tower wound in gold, and rising from its crown a column of violet light that pulsed like a slow, vast heartbeat.

Around them, citizens in festival dress filled the square, faces tilted toward the tower, smiling. All of them smiling. None of them blinking.

Wren stood shaking in a stranger's city with her family arrayed around her — Brannoc already reaching for the axe, Ivetta's hand hard on her shoulder, Silas staring up at the tower with the face of a man come home to his own execution. The book hung open in her hands, quiet now, satisfied, its ash-black date finally dry.

Above the bells, above the cheering, she heard it — soft, intimate, spoken directly into her ear from nowhere at all, in a voice like her own:

*Welcome, little bird. He's been waiting so long to be remembered.*
