## 3. The City Under the Crown

Brannoc had marched into eleven cities in his soldiering years, and he had learned to read them the way other men read weather. A city about to be sacked smelled of smoke and sour panic. A garrison city moved in rhythms — boots, bells, the lazy arrogance of men who knew they were untouchable. A starving city went quiet at dusk.

Vaelmoor did none of these things. Vaelmoor smiled.

They came through the last standing arch of the shattered gate at midmorning, five strangers blinking in a light that seemed too clean, and the city received them like a host receiving honored guests. The streets of the capital were wide and pale as bone, cobbled in white stone that caught the sun and threw it back softened, kind. Fountains played at every crossing. Flower boxes spilled crimson over balconies. And everywhere, everywhere, the people smiled.

A baker drew loaves from her oven and smiled at them as she set them in the window. Two old men playing tiles under an awning looked up and smiled. A mother scolded her boy for running through the fountain spray, and even mid-scold, she was smiling, her mouth curved in a pleasant fixed arc while her eyes said nothing at all.

"Someone tell me why everyone here looks like they've just been handed good news," Wren muttered, pressed close to Silas's side.

"Because they've been told to," Brannoc said, before he could stop himself.

He kept his voice down after that. But it was true, and his body knew it before his mind had finished arranging the words. He had seen this once before — not this, exactly, but its cousin. In the war of his twenties, in a river province whose name he'd spent fifteen years not saying aloud, an occupying commander had ordered every household to hang white cloth from their windows as a sign of loyalty. Anyone whose window stayed bare was fined, then beaten, then gone by morning. Within a week, the whole city had been white. It had looked, from the ridge above, like a wedding. Like celebration. It had been neither.

This was that, but written on faces instead of cloth.

* * *

The guards were worse than the smiles. That was what turned his stomach, in the end — not the wrongness itself but how well it was done. Brannoc had drilled men for two decades. He knew discipline when he walked through it, and Vaelmoor's crown-sworn soldiers were disciplined down to the marrow. They patrolled in perfect intervals, four-man files, spacing so exact you could have measured it with a rope. They turned corners on the beat of some internal drum. Their armor was immaculate, black-lacquered plate over grey surcoats, and not one strap hung loose, not one greave sat crooked.

But no one fidgeted. No one shifted his weight. No man glanced sideways at a pretty girl or scowled at the noon heat or scratched under his collar the way real soldiers did, the way men did who were wearing forty pounds of steel and bored out of their skulls. These stood as still as fence posts between patrols, and when they moved, they all moved together, like water finding its level, like puppets strung from a single crossbar somewhere above the rooftops.

"Ivetta," Brannoc murmured, falling in beside the healer as they crossed the Sighing Bridge over the canal. "Look at their hands."

She did, sidelong, careful. "Gloves."

"Now look at how they hold their spears." Every grip identical. Thumb along the flat, three fingers wrapped, little finger hooked loose — a textbook hold taught in exactly one way, held in exactly one posture, forever. Real soldiers developed habits. Habits were how you picked your friends out of a shield wall. These men had no habits. Only instruction.

"You're shaking," Ivetta said quietly.

"I'm angry."

"Yes. Those look the same on you."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. But there was a stone sitting in his chest where his oath used to sit, and every step deeper into the city ground against it. When he'd mustered out — when they'd finally let him muster out, after the third inquiry and the second mass grave — he had sworn on the only thing he had left, which was his own name, that he would never again take orders whose logic ended in *because someone told me to*. Never again would he be the spear in another man's hand. He'd come to the Hollow Valley because nothing happened there. Because fences needed mending and firewood needed splitting and none of it required him to point his anger at anyone.

And now here he was, walking through a city where ten thousand people had been made into instruments, and every instinct he owned was already mapping sight lines, patrol rotations, choke points, staging grounds. His mind was doing what it was built for. That was the betrayal hiding under all the rest: some part of Brannoc had woken up the moment they crossed that gate, stretched, and said *finally*, and he hated that part with his whole heart.

* * *

It went wrong at the fish market, near the base of the palace hill, where the streets climbed in switchbacks toward the spire that dominated everything — a needle of pale stone crowned with gold, visible from every quarter, always, so that no citizen could ever forget what watched over them.

They had been trying to look inconspicuous, which in Brannoc's professional assessment they were failing at badly. Silas moved like a man expecting ambush. Ivetta's healer's eyes catalogued suffering she couldn't stop herself from seeing. Wren simply didn't know how to walk without looking at everything, and in a city where nobody looked at anything, curiosity was a beacon.

The file of crown-sworn came around the corner at the exact moment Wren stopped dead to stare up at the spire.

Brannoc saw it happen the way he'd seen a thousand ambushes begin: the small wrongness, the hitch in rhythm. The lead guard's head turned — not smoothly, but with a mechanical snap, like a door swinging on a dry hinge — and fixed on Wren. The file's spacing collapsed and reformed into a pursuit wedge without a single word spoken. No command. No shout. Four men moving as one thought that wasn't theirs.

"Walk," Brannoc said. Then, louder, with the parade-ground voice he'd sworn off, "*Walk.* Don't run. Runners get chased."

They walked. The guards followed. Not hurrying — that was the horror of it. They simply advanced at regulation pace, closing the gap with the patience of tide, and Brannoc understood that these men did not tire, did not lose interest, did not decide that five travelers weren't worth the paperwork. Whatever moved them would follow until it had them or the world ended.

He steered them left at the fountain, right past the tile players — still smiling, still smiling — down a lane narrowing between dye-shops, and the gap refused to widen. Behind them, boots struck stone in perfect unison. Ahead, at the mouth of the lane, another file appeared, drawn from somewhere by the same silent summons, sealing the exit.

Silas's hand went to the knife at his belt. Wren's breath had gone quick and shallow. Brannoc felt the old arithmetic start up in his skull — *four behind, three ahead, narrow frontage favors defenders, Ivetta can't run fast, I can put myself between* — and he despised how fluent he still was in it, the way a drunkard despises how quickly his hand finds the bottle.

"No fighting," Ivetta whispered. "Not yet. Look at their eyes."

He looked. The nearest guard's eyes were the worst thing in the city. Open, aware, present — and behind them, far back, something small and trapped, like a man watching his own hands do murder through a window he couldn't reach.

"There are people in there," Brannoc said grimly. "Good. Bad. Gods." He put his body between the guards and the others anyway, because some vows bent and some didn't.

Then a hand shot from a doorway, seized his sleeve, and hauled him sideways into darkness.

* * *

The space beyond the door was a cellar, low-ceilinged, smelling of brine and tallow, stacked with empty crates. A lantern burned behind a shutter of pierced tin, throwing coin-spots of light across a face that resolved out of the gloom: a woman, sixty-odd, wrapped in layers of patched wool, with a beggar's crutch propped against the wall and eyes like awls — sharp, quick, entirely her own.

"Down," she hissed, pointing at the floor. "All of you. Breathe quiet."

Above them, through the floorboards, came the sound of boots. Perfect unison. Passing the doorway. Stopping. A long silence in which Brannoc counted his own heartbeats and got to thirty-one before the boots resumed, receding, obedient to whatever called them elsewhere.

The woman waited until even Wren's breathing had slowed. Then she lifted the lantern and studied each of them in turn, lingering longest on Silas — on his forearms, where his sleeves had ridden up and the scarred words showed pale against skin. Something moved through her face. Recognition? Fear? It was gone too fast to name.

"So," she said. "Outsiders. From outside *outside*." She spat neatly into a corner. "Gate brought you, didn't it. Saw the light from my grate two nights running. Thought, either the world's ending or the valley's done waiting."

"You know about the valley?" Ivetta asked.

"Lady, I know about everything that happens under that spire. Osla's my name. I sell information, shelter, and occasionally fish. Mostly I sit in doorways being invisible, which is the finest military training there is." She grinned, and hers was the first real smile Brannoc had seen since the gate — crooked, sly, alive. It nearly undid him.

"Why does everyone smile like that?" Wren asked, hugging her arms. "What's wrong with them?"

"Nothing's wrong with them, girl. That's the trouble. They're *content*. All of them, all the time, whether they're burying their mothers or selling their bread. Contentment from the crown down, poured in like rain." Osla's grin faded. "You want to know about the king, since that's eventually where all roads here go. Official story: Aldric keeps to his chambers in mourning for his father, issues edicts through his ministers. True story—" she tapped the floorboards with her crutch, twice, as if knocking on heaven's door "—nobody's seen him truly awake in months. The thing that walks and wears him has gotten better at the wearing. There was a while, early, when you'd catch him. Windows, mostly, at night. A boy's face looking out of his own eyes like someone drowning behind glass. Begging, if you knew how to read a face." Her awl-eyes settled on Silas again, and her voice dropped. "These days, not even that. But the palace servants whisper there's writing that shows up on the walls of the royal wing sometimes. Light, they say, not paint. A child's hand. Same words, over and over."

"What words?" Ivetta asked softly.

Osla looked at her a long moment.

"*Somebody, please.*"

The cellar was very quiet. Above them the city hummed on, contented, smiling, ten thousand throats singing whatever song had been poured into them.

Brannoc found his fists had closed without permission. Here it all was, laid out plain: a puppet army, an occupied boy, a machine that needed breaking — and every soldier reflex he owned leaning forward like a dog at a hare. His vow had never said he couldn't fight. It had said he wouldn't *serve*, wouldn't be a tool, wouldn't let another man's will move his hands. But standing in this cellar, listening to a child-king write pleas on walls, Brannoc understood with sick clarity that the distinction was thinner than he'd told himself for fifteen years. Sometimes refusing to choose was just choosing on a delay.

"The smuggler routes," Silas said at last, his voice rough. "You know ways into the palace."

Osla's eyebrows climbed. "I know ways in and twelve ways out, swordspeaker." She said the title like a test, watching his face flinch. "Question is what you're planning to do when you're inside. Because I'll guide thieves, runaways, and fools. But I don't guide dead men, and I don't guide men who've already decided to die. Which are you?"

Brannoc answered before Silas could, before he even knew he meant to.

"He's neither. We're going in to bring the king out alive — all of us, back out, or we don't go." He heard himself say it and marveled: the voice was his drill-yard voice, but the words belonged to the valley, to supper and mended fences and the family sitting around him in this brine-smelling dark. Maybe serving wasn't the problem, he thought. Maybe it had only ever mattered *who*.

Osla studied them all again, then nodded once, slow.

"All right. But not tonight, and not through any door with hinges." She reached beneath a crate and drew out a rolled hide, spreading it across the floor: the palace hill in fine ink, corridors and crawlspaces threaded through the stone like veins. "There's an old way under the coronation spire. Dry cistern, dead since the last dynasty. It lets out below the throne room itself."

Her finger stopped on a chamber drawn directly beneath the dais, and Brannoc saw her hesitate for the first time.

"One thing more," she said quietly. "The cistern's not marked on any living map. I found it because the rats won't go near it. And the last three people I sent down that passage to scout it—" she looked up, and her awl-eyes were flat as slate "—came back smiling."
