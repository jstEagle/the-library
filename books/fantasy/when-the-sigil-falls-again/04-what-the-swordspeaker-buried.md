## 4. What the Swordspeaker Buried

Ivetta had stitched enough men back together to know that bodies kept secrets better than mouths did. A soldier could swear he'd never been wounded and still flinch when she reached for his left side. A widow could smile through a funeral and carry grief in the way her shoulders hunched, as if bracing for a blow that had already landed. The body told the truth in its own language — old scar tissue, badly healed breaks, the tremor of a hand that had done something it couldn't undo.

Silas Nystrom's body had been shouting at her since the day she met him.

He sat on the edge of the cot in the back room of Osla's safehouse, shirtless, while she worked on the gash along his ribs. The skirmish in the archive district had been brief and ugly — three crown-sworn soldiers who moved like marionettes with their strings pulled taut, and Silas moving between them like something older than war itself. He'd taken a blade meant for Wren. He hadn't made a sound.

"Hold still," she said, though he already was. He was always still. That was the thing about Silas that unsettled people who didn't know him: the absolute discipline of him, the way he occupied space like a man who had learned that movement invited attention.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere beyond the door, Brannoc's low voice rumbled through the boards as he argued strategy with Osla, and Wren's lighter voice wove through it, insistent, unafraid. The sounds of a family arguing about supper and survival in the same breath. Ivetta found it steadying. She wondered if Silas did, or if he simply endured it.

The gash was clean work — she closed it in seven stitches, small and even, the way her mother had taught her in the marsh villages where a crooked scar could mean a slow death from infection. Silas didn't flinch. He watched her hands with a kind of detached interest, as though the wound belonged to someone else entirely.

"You should have let me see to this an hour ago," she said.

"There were other things."

"There are always other things." She tied off the final knot and reached for the cloth to clean away the blood — and stopped.

His forearms were bare. She had seen them before, of course; in the valley, summers were hot and sleeves were rolled, and everyone had noticed the scars eventually. They ran from his wrists to his elbows in parallel lines, precise as plough furrows, and everyone had assumed — she had assumed — whip scars, or the marks of some old captivity. People fled to Hollow Valley carrying every kind of history. You didn't ask. That was the covenant of the place, unwritten and sacred: the valley forgot so you didn't have to remember alone.

But now, in the lamplight, with blood still wet on his skin, she saw what she had never let herself look at closely.

The scars were words.

Not cut carelessly. Cut deliberately, in rows, in a script she almost recognized — the flowing formal hand of old Vaelmoor legal documents, the kind pressed into wax seals and tomb markers. Dozens of lines. Hundreds. Some had healed silver-white and ancient; others were newer, rawer pink, though none fresh. And they weren't random phrases. The spacing was too deliberate, the rhythm too structured. It was a text. He had written something onto himself, line by line, over years.

Or *bound* something, whispered a voice in her that knew old stories from her grandmother's knee, stories about swordspeakers whose words could split stone and stop hearts, and who paid for that power in the only coin that mattered.

"Silas," she said quietly. "These aren't scars from someone else's hand."

His breathing didn't change. But something behind his eyes did — a door closing, or trying to.

"They're mine," he said.

"Say what they are."

"Ivetta—"

"I've been a healer for thirty years. I've seen penitents flay themselves in the chapel at Drossen Vale. I've seen soldiers carve their dead comrades' names into their arms so they wouldn't forget to grieve." She set down the cloth and folded her hands in her lap, deliberately, so he would see she wasn't reaching for him anymore. "This isn't grief. This is grammar. These are sentences. You bound yourself, Silas. Didn't you?"

For a long moment the only sound was the lamp's small hiss and the muffled argument beyond the door. Then Silas looked down at his forearms the way other men looked at graves.

"You know what a swordspeaker is," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Stories. Grandmother stories. Words of power spoken through a blade — a word to fell a wall, a word to boil a river. Everyone says the last one died generations ago."

"The last one is sitting on a healer's cot getting stitched up like a fool because he stepped in front of a girl instead of letting her dodge." His mouth curved, but there was no humor in it. "Yes. Swordspeech. Every word I speak in power does exactly what I intend and takes something from me in exchange. Not blood. Not years." He paused, and Ivetta saw his throat work. "Memory. A word to shatter a shield costs me my seventh summer. A word to kill costs more. The greater the working, the deeper the taking."

Ivetta felt cold settle into her chest. "And these?" She touched the air just above his forearm, not touching skin. "You wrote your oath in your own flesh."

"An oath of silence. Every line is a word I swore never to speak again, cut in so the vow lived somewhere outside my own unreliable head." He laughed softly, terribly. "It was supposed to be permanent. You can't unsay a thing you've carved out of yourself."

"Why?" she asked, though part of her already knew, the way healers always know before the patient confesses. "What did you do that made you want to stop being what you are?"

He was silent for so long she thought he might refuse. Beyond the door, Brannoc's argument had gone quiet; either they'd finished or someone was listening.

"I told you I came to the valley twenty-nine years ago," Silas said finally. "That I was born elsewhere, ordinary, and fled here after a war I won't discuss."

"Yes."

"That's not true. None of it." He flexed his hands once, then stilled them. "Ivetta, how old do you think I am?"

"Twenty-nine when we met. Thirty-one now. Though you carry yourself like a man of sixty."

"I carry myself," he said, "like a man of a hundred and thirty, because that is closer to the truth. I don't fully remember the count. Memory is the coin, remember. I've spent so much of myself that whole decades are missing — gaps with edges worn smooth, like stones in a riverbed. I know I helped seal something, long ago. Something broke beneath the city of Vaelmoor, in the age of Aldric's great-grandmother's great-grandmother, and it wore a king the way a fire wears a house. Six of us put it down. Five died. I spoke words that night I can't recall speaking, and I woke in the rubble with silver hair and empty years and a sigil-thing buried but not destroyed."

Ivetta's hands had gone still in her lap. "The Vessel," she whispered. "The thing wearing the boy-king. That's what you sealed."

"What I *helped* seal. Sealing is not killing. We bound it, and binding has seams, and seams tear." He met her eyes at last, and she saw the thing she had suspected all along and never wanted confirmed: shame, deep as wellwater. "When I heard rumors, years ago, of a young king in Vaelmoor who smiled wrong, I ran harder for the valley. Hollow Valley eats magic. I thought if I buried my swordspeech under an oath and hid where power couldn't follow, whatever I'd helped loose would simply... forget me. Move on. Find some other seam."

"But it didn't."

"It's been looking for its last unbroken seal. Me. And I led four innocent people straight to it, because I wanted supper tables and fence-mending and a girl calling me teacher." His voice cracked on the last word, barely, a fracture line in stone. "Ivetta. What I did before — before the oath — you should know what I am capable of. When I say I caused a massacre, I don't mean I failed to prevent one. I mean I stood in a field with a blade full of words and I *spoke*, and when the speaking was done there were no enemies left standing and no village left standing and I couldn't remember, afterward, whether the ones dying had deserved it, because I had spent the memory along with everything else. I paid for power with the ability to know if I'd used it rightly. That is what a swordspeaker is. That is what sleeps in your safehouse and eats at your table."

The lamp flame bent. Ivetta became aware that her eyes were wet and did nothing about it.

She had patched broken men her whole life. Men who had done terrible things under orders, men who had done terrible things freely, men who mistook numbness for peace. She knew the taxonomy of guilt the way she knew the taxonomy of wounds — which kind festered, which kind killed, which kind scarred over hard and useful. Silas's guilt was none of those. His was the oldest kind: the guilt of a man who had decided he was a weapon, and had spent a century trying to make sure he could never be fired again.

"You gave up your memories," she said slowly, "to make sure you'd never hurt anyone again. You cut the words into your own arms so you couldn't be tempted."

"And it bought a hundred years of quiet fences and honest bread."

"And it cost you knowing why." She reached out — slowly, giving him time to pull away — and laid two fingers on the silvered lines of his forearm. He didn't flinch. "Silas. You don't even remember if you were guilty. You've been executing yourself for a crime you can't recall committing."

"That doesn't make me innocent."

"No. But it makes you a man, not a verdict." Her fingers stayed where they were. "Wren adores you. Brannoc would die for you and tells me so when he thinks I'm asleep. I have watched you mend fences for three years and I have never once seen your hands shake — until tonight, holding a needle's worth of fear that the girl got cut instead of you. Weapons don't tremble, Silas. Men do."

He looked at her hand on his arm. Something moved behind his face — the first true expression she'd ever caught there that wasn't discipline or weariness. It looked almost like relief, and relief terrified him more than any soldier had.

"If the Vessel finds me," he said hoarsely, "everything I buried comes due. All of it. There won't be a choice this time. To unbind that thing from Aldric, I'd have to break my oath and speak the full swordspeech again, and I don't have enough memories left to survive the price. It would take—" He stopped.

"It would take everything," Ivetta finished. "Everyone you—"

The door burst open.

Wren stood in the doorway, white-faced, the prophecy book clutched against her chest with both arms, ash-black ink smudged across her fingertips.

"Silas," she said. "There's a page about you. It's in the book. Your name — it's written in the *old* section, and the ink is still wet."

* * *

Outside the safehouse window, far across the sleeping city, the crown of the palace spire began to glow — soft at first, then brighter, a sick golden pulse, like a heart remembering how to beat.
