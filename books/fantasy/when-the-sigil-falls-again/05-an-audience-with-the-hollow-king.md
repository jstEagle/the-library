## 5. An Audience with the Hollow King

Osla's routes ran beneath the city like veins beneath skin, and Silas learned quickly that in Vaelmoor, the skin was the lie.

They moved through the underdark of the capital for the better part of a day — through cisterns where the water sang too sweetly, through grain-cellars stacked with sacks that whispered when no one touched them, through a seam of old smuggler's stairs so narrow that Brannoc had to exhale fully and turn sideways to pass. Osla went first, lamp held low, her hunched silhouette flickering on walls carved with sigils that had been chiseled out long ago. Someone had tried to unmake them. Someone had failed.

Silas kept his eyes off the carvings. He had spent a hundred years learning not to look at things that might remember him.

"Warm," Wren murmured behind him, and he felt her small hand close on the back of his belt like she'd done a hundred times crossing the valley market. "It gets warmer the deeper we go."

"That'll be the crown," Osla said without turning. "The Vessel breathes heat. Whole palace sits on its lung."

Ivetta, at the rear with her healer's satchel clutched to her chest, said nothing, but Silas heard her breathing change — the deliberate steadying she did before triage, before bad news, before death walked into her tent. He knew all her kinds of quiet now. That was the trouble with letting people close: they became legible. Every one of them was a book he'd read so often he could recite it blind, and every word he spent today would be a page torn out.

*Don't think about the cost yet,* he told himself. *Costs are paid at the counter, not on the road.*

The stairs ended at a door made of silver that had gone black as old blood. Osla pressed her palm flat against it, muttered something in thieves' cant that was older than thieves' cant, and the door sighed open onto warmth and light.

"Service corridor runs along the throne room's spine," she said. "There's a gap behind the dais — masons' access, three hundred years dead. You come up through the floor like ghosts. After that, I got nothing for you. I don't go where the king is." She looked up at Silas with one eye milky and one eye sharp, and the sharp one held him. "Whatever you're planning to say in there — swordspeaker or no — count your words first. Words are the only coin that thing respects."

Then she was gone, swallowed back down into her dark, and the four of them stood in the light of the hollow king's house.

* * *

The service corridor was hot enough that Ivetta made them drink. Water passed hand to hand in silence. Wren's was shaking; Brannoc wrapped his big scarred fist around hers until the tremor stilled. Silas watched them do it and felt the ache of a thing he could not name — the same ache he felt watching fence-posts hold against the valley wind, watching the kettle pour, watching ordinary peace perform its ordinary miracles.

Behind the dais, the masons' gap was exactly where Osla said: a crawl of stone beneath the great carved throne, opening upward through a grate furred with dust. Silas went first because he was always first, because being first was the last shape his guilt still fit into.

He lifted the grate an inch and looked out into the throne room of Vaelmoor.

It was beautiful the way a drowned woman is beautiful — wrongness floating just beneath the surface. The hall stretched away in columns of white stone shot through with veins of gold, except the gold moved. Slowly. Like something circulating. The famous stained-glass windows, which should have thrown saints and kings across the floor in colored light, threw instead a single repeating figure: a crowned silhouette, over and over, hundreds of times, so that whoever stood in the room stood always inside the shadow of the same king.

And the court — the court was worse than empty. Hundreds of nobles knelt in perfect rows upon cushions, motionless, their foreheads bowed, their smiles fixed. Silas could see the smiles even from below, curved and white and patient, the smiles of people who had been smiling for months. Between two of them, a lady's fan had slipped from fingers that had not moved to catch it. It lay on the marble like a fallen bird.

"Silas." Ivetta's voice, barely air. "Their hearts. Look."

He looked. Beneath the translucent skin of the nearest kneeling lord, a pulse beat in the throat — but all of them, every one, beat together. One rhythm. The whole room breathed as a single body, rising and falling in unison, and above them on the throne sat its heart.

Aldric — the boy Silas had seen on wanted-leaflets and remembered from a century of rumors — sat slumped in his own throne like a marionette hung up between performances. He was seventeen, maybe. Thin. His crown sat crooked, and the Vessel sat straight: a lattice of light wound through him like roots through soil, gold-white and branching, threading out of his chest and down through the throne, down through the floor, down, Silas understood with a lurch, through everything. The moving veins in the columns. The rhythm in the throats. The warmth in the deep roads.

The sigil he had sealed a lifetime ago had grown a body, and worn a kingdom over it.

"We can't fight all of them," Brannoc breathed against his ear. "If those guards—"

"Guards are for trespassers," Silas said quietly. "We're not trespassing. We're answering a summons a hundred years overdue."

He raised the grate.

* * *

Crossing the throne room was like wading upstream through sleep. The heat thickened with every step, and the single vast heartbeat slowed as they approached the dais — not alarmed, Silas thought. Interested. The smiling courtiers did not turn their heads, but their eyes rolled, wet and tracking, following the four of them across the marble while their bodies stayed kneeling in their perfect rows.

Wren pressed close to his left side. Ivetta to his right. Brannoc walked behind them all, facing backward, one hand on his axe, a wall built of refusal.

At the foot of the dais, the king stirred.

The Vessel's light flared, and when Aldric raised his head, his eyes were not eyes. They were apertures — round holes of gold-white through which something ancient regarded them with the mild appetite of a man regarding bread.

"*The last seal walks into my house,*" it said, and the voice was layered, a chorus speaking through one boy's throat. "*I have waited so patiently, swordspeaker. I have eaten a kingdom by inches.*"

"You've eaten a boy," Silas said.

"*He is the dish it came served on.*" The light rippled; the court laughed, all of them, in perfect unison, a sound like a bell full of bees. "*You sealed me once and paid in years. Now you have so little left worth taking. Come. Kneel. Be forgotten gently.*"

And that was when the boy surfaced.

Silas saw it happen — saw the aperture-eyes stutter, saw the golden lattice dim like a candle cupped by wind, saw the small body strain upright against its own strings. Aldric's mouth opened, and what came out was not words. Light spilled from his lips in thin luminous threads, and the threads wrote themselves in the air between him and Silas: wavering, crooked, enormous letters, the handwriting of a child who had learned letters young and never been allowed to grow.

**PLEASE**

The letter *P* trembled.

**IT IS SO LOUD INSIDE**

**I CAN HEAR MYSELF FORGETTING**

The threads of light shook. Somewhere beneath the throne, the vast heartbeat stumbled, and for one moment the whole court's breathing caught ragged and human, a thousand throats remembering how.

**NOBODY HAS COME IN MONTHS**

**PLEASE SOMEBODY UNBIND ME**

**I DON'T WANT TO BE THE CROWN ANYMORE**

**I WANT MY MOTHER'S FACE BACK**

Silas stopped breathing.

Because he knew. He knew that grief the way you know your own hands. Somewhere in him there had been a mother's face, warm and specific, gone now — spent decades ago on words of power spoken over a broken sigil in a burning pass — and here was a boy begging for the very thing Silas had already paid away, unable even to remember what mourning was for it.

*Ivetta,* he thought, absurdly. *She has her mother's chin. Wren will grow into her own face someday and someone should be there to tell her she looks like someone.*

The light-letters hung in the air, fading at their edges like breath on glass.

Then the Vessel took the boy back.

The apertures reopened. The lattice blazed. Aldric's small body snapped straight, and the voice that came out was only the chorus now, cold and amused:

"*Sentiment. How the seals always crack.*"

It gestured — a child's wrist, an emperor's authority — and the court rose.

All of them. At once. The smile-smooth faces turned forward, the eyes emptied of everything but obedience, and three hundred nobles of Vaelmoor drew steel they had not chosen to carry and came up the dais steps in perfect lockstep, a single creature wearing silk.

"BACK!" Brannoc roared, and met them.

What followed was not a battle; battles have lines and noise and moments to think. This was a flood. Brannoc was an anvil of a man, wide and immovable at the stair's throat, his axe singing in short brutal arcs, shouting — *"Ivetta! Down! WREN, MOVE—"*

Wren did not move fast enough.

Silas saw it the way you see lightning: already happened. A courtier-lord, some fat magistrate's face slack as unset butter, broke past Brannoc's guard with a hunting knife and drove it at the girl's back, and Wren turned — turning far too slowly, sixteen years old and mortal and turning — and there was no time. There was no plan. There was no cleverness left in Silas Nystrom, only the oldest word in him, the first word, the one bound tightest to the deepest root—

He spoke it.

One syllable. It left his mouth like a blade leaving a scabbard for the first time in a century, and the world *rang*. The air itself turned edge-on. The knife stopped. Not the man — the knife, arrested a handspan from Wren's shoulder-blade, shivering, then tearing free of the puppet-hand holding it and hanging in the air, point outward, humming.

The word took its price before the echo died.

Silas reached for his mother — not deliberately; the mind goes to its wounds — and found the place where her face had been swept smooth as river ice. He knew he had loved her. He knew she had hummed while she worked, that her hands smelled of bread, that she had called him by some name no one else used. He knew all these facts the way he knew the capital's history.

He could not see her.

Not her eyes. Not the line of her mouth. There had been a laugh-line near her left eye — he was certain there had been, the certainty itself the only survivor — and it was gone, and the space where it lived was clean and white and silent, and somewhere in Vaelmoor, inside the lattice, inside the thing on the throne, a new thread of gold light bloomed like a vein of ore, and he understood, sick and total, exactly what had just been fed to it.

His mother's face. Eaten. In exchange for a knife stopping midair.

The courtiers collapsed as one, puppets cut loose, sprawling across the marble in heaps of silk and clattering steel. The suspended knife fell and rang on the floor. Brannoc stood panting at the stair-top, blood sheeting down one arm, staring at Silas with dawning horror — he'd heard. They'd all heard the word, felt it in their teeth.

But Silas was looking at the throne, where Aldric had surfaced again, straining against the light, and where new letters were spilling desperately from the boy's mouth, faster now, urgent, the handwriting shaking:

**YOU PAID SOMETHING JUST NOW**

**I FELT IT GO PAST ME INTO THE CROWN**

**THAT IS WHAT IT EATS — THAT IS ALL IT EVER EATS**

**FORGOTTEN THINGS**

**THERE IS A BOOK — THEY KEEP A BOOK OF WHAT IT TAKES**

**FIND THE ARCHIVE BEFORE YOU SPEAK AGAIN**

The letters began to burn gold at their edges, consumed from below as the Vessel rose through the boy like water filling a boot, and Aldric's real voice — his true voice, thin and cracked and seventeen — tore loose for one final second:

*"Please hurry. I'm almost—"*

And then the hollow king smiled, and it was not the boy's smile, and the lattice blazed bright enough to throw all their shadows up the throne room walls like fleeing men.

Silas stood in the heat of it, one memory lighter, his mother's face gone wherever faces go, and heard Wren say beside him, in a small hard voice he would remember longer than she could know:

"The archive. Then we talk about what you just spent."
