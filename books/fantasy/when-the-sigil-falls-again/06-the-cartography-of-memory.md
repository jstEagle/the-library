## 6. The Cartography of Memory

Wren had not slept. She sat cross-legged on the floor of Osla's back room with the prophecy book open across her knees, and she did not sleep because every time she closed her eyes she saw the ash-black letters rearranging themselves, patient as spiders, into dates that hadn't happened yet.

The candle had burned down to a stub. Somewhere beyond the door, Brannoc's snore rose and fell like a tide, and beneath it the city of Vaelmoor hummed its wrong little hum — that too-wide, too-sweet sound of ten thousand smiling people who were not entirely people anymore.

Wren turned a page with the tip of her knife.

The book was old. Older than old; the leather cover had gone soft as skin, and the pages smelled of smoke and something sweeter underneath, like flowers pressed in a coffin. The early chapters were prophecy proper — wars, floods, a comet shaped like a weeping eye — but the further she read, the stranger it became. The entries stopped describing *events*. They described *absences*.

*In the year of the double harvest, the city shall forget the name of its founding queen.*

*When the third bell cracks, no child will remember learning to walk.*

*On the eve of the coronation, one man shall lose his mother's face.*

Wren's breath caught. She read that last line again. Then again. Her finger trembled against the vellum.

She had been there. In the throne room, three nights ago, when the guards came down like wolves and Wren had frozen, stupid with terror, and Silas had spoken — just one word, two syllables that made the air ring like a struck bell and threw four armored men across the marble as if they were toys. And afterward he'd stood very still in the wreckage, touching his own cheek, saying, quietly, *I can't remember what color her eyes were.*

He had lost his mother's face. The book had known. Not predicted — *known*, the way a ledger knows what's been spent.

Wren flipped backward through the pages, faster now, her heart climbing her throat. There. An entry dated a hundred years past: *The swordspeaker seals the wound with words, and pays in years.* Another: *What is forgotten is not destroyed. It is kept.* And another, in a tighter, more desperate hand: *It feeds where memory fails. It nests in the hollows left behind.*

The Vessel. It didn't feed on blood or fear or any of the things the ballads claimed. It fed on forgotten things. Every memory surrendered, every name lost, every face worn away — none of it vanished. It went *somewhere*. It was *kept*, the way a miser keeps coin, and the thing wearing King Aldric's crown had been growing fat on a century of other people's losses.

And if that was true — if forgotten memories weren't destroyed but stored —

Then everything Silas had ever paid away might still exist. Somewhere. In some vault of the sigil's making.

Wren sat back on her heels, dizzy with it. This wasn't just prophecy anymore. This was a map. A cartography of everything taken.

She needed to show them. Now, tonight, before the fear could talk her out of it.

* * *

She woke them badly, which was to say honestly — shaking the book under Brannoc's nose until he came up off his bedroll with a knife already in his fist, then thrusting it at Ivetta, then hammering on the thin wall between them and the room where Silas slept.

He answered the door looking like a man who had never once slept well in his life. Twenty-nine years old, he claimed, though the gray threading his temples said otherwise, though the lines around his mouth said otherwise, though the way he carried himself — weight settled low, like a man who had been standing guard over something for a very long time — said otherwise, and Wren had always filed those contradictions away in the place where you keep the things you love about someone that don't quite add up.

"Show me," he said, when she held up the book.

They gathered around the single candle, all four of them, heads bowed over the pages like conspirators, which Wren supposed they were. She read the entries aloud. *What is forgotten is not destroyed. It is kept. It feeds where memory fails.*

"It's not a prophecy," she said, when she finished. "Or it's both. It maps what the Vessel takes. Look —" she flipped pages, jabbing her finger at dates and absences, matching them to things they'd seen: the guards who moved like puppets, the citizens whose smiles never reached their eyes, the beggar Osla who could remember her childhood but not her mother's voice. "Everyone in this city has holes in them. Little ones. The Vessel's been grazing here for months. Years, maybe. And every scrap it takes goes into the sigil."

Brannoc rubbed his jaw. "So it's a hoard."

"A hoard," Wren agreed. "A treasury. And treasuries—" she looked up, met Silas's eyes, and felt her pulse jump, "—can be raided."

Silence. Ivetta's hands had gone still in her lap, healer-still, the stillness she wore when she was afraid for someone. Brannoc was staring at the ceiling as if doing sums. And Silas —

Silas had gone the color of old paper.

"The archive," he said at last, and his voice was rough, like a hinge unused for years. "Under the palace. If the crown keeps records — census rolls, tithe books, anything a hundred years deep —"

"Why would you want dusty old tax ledgers?" Brannoc asked.

"Because," Wren said slowly, watching Silas's face, "if the book maps memories, maybe other records map time. Maybe we can find out what Silas paid. What he lost. Where it's kept." She hesitated, then, gentler: "Maybe we can find your mother's face."

Something moved behind Silas's eyes — hope, or its corpse — and he nodded once, sharp, and said, "Tomorrow night. Osla knows the ways in."

Later, much later, Wren would remember that moment and wonder how she hadn't seen it — how none of them had. How he had looked at the idea of finding his own history the way a man looks at a trap he already knows is sprung.

* * *

Osla's smuggler route into the royal archive ran through a drainage culvert that smelled like the death of a hundred rats and emerged, miraculously, into a wine cellar that smelled like the death of a thousand more. From there, a stair. From there, silence — the heavy, curated silence of a place built to keep things.

The royal archive of Vaelmoor was a cathedral of shelves. Racks of scrolls rose three stories into darkness, and the air was cold and dry and tasted of dust and ink, and somewhere overhead something skittered — mouse, or worse. Lantern-light made the shadows lean.

"Split up," Silas murmured. "Records from a century back. Census, guild rolls, military — anything with names."

They spread through the stacks. Wren took the western wall, running her fingers along shelf labels, pulling scrolls at random, unrolling them just far enough to catch a date. Forty years back. Sixty. Seventy-five. The paper grew older under her hands, yellower, more fragile, until —

*Census of the City of Vaelmoor and Surrounding Holdings, in the Year of the Double Harvest.*

A hundred years ago, to the season. Her heart slammed. She carried it to the lantern on the central table, and Brannoc drifted over, and Ivetta, and finally Silas, drawn despite himself.

"Names beginning with N are in the second register," Wren said, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, too loud in the hush.

She found him on the fourth page.

*Nystrom, Silas. Swordspeaker, First Rank. Age recorded at conscription: thirty-one. Household: none. Notes: decorated twice for valor at the siege of Karth Hollow.*

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above them, the skittering thing went silent, as if it too were reading.

"That's not possible," Brannoc said flatly. "That's — he told us he was twenty-nine. He told us —" Brannoc stopped. Wren watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes, the same arithmetic happening in hers, the same sick downward slide. Thirty-one at conscription. Decorated twice. A siege. A hundred years ago.

"He cannot be twenty-nine," Ivetta said softly. "He cannot be *thirty*-nine."

They turned together.

Silas stood at the end of the table, half in shadow, holding a scroll he had not yet opened. He had heard. Of course he had heard. His face was perfectly still — the stillness of deep water, of a man who has practiced being unreadable for longer than any twenty-nine-year-old could practice anything.

"How old are you?" Wren asked. Her voice cracked on the last word. "Silas. How old are you *actually*?"

He set the scroll down with exaggerated care, as if it might detonate. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and level and completely wrong, a stranger's calm laid over something screaming.

"Older than that."

"How much older?" Brannoc demanded. His soldier's voice, the one that made raw recruits stand straighter. "How much older, Silas?"

"I don't know." And that was the worst part, Wren thought wildly — he meant it. "I've paid away so many years' worth of remembering. I don't know how many times. I don't know what else I've lost. I stopped counting because counting required remembering that I'd counted before."

Ivetta made a small sound, hand pressed to her mouth.

"You lied to us," Wren said. The hurt came out before she could stop it, hot and childish and true. "You lived with us. You ate supper with us. You taught me knife-work. You let me think — you let us think we knew you, and you were — you're a *hundred years old*, and you sealed the sigil, didn't you? You're in the prophecy book. You're the reason the Vessel exists, or the reason it's loose, or — what else haven't you told us?"

"Wren." Ivetta's warning tone.

"No. No, I want to hear it. I want to hear him say it." Wren's eyes were burning and she hated it, hated the tears, hated that her voice shook. "Because I found your mother's face in that book, Silas. I thought I could give it back to you. I thought I was carrying good news. And instead I find out you're a century-old liar who —"

"I swore an oath," Silas said.

And the words rang. Actually rang, faintly, the way his swordspeech rang, and Wren realized with a lurch that the oath-scars on his forearms must be burning under his sleeves, that even speaking of it cost him something.

"Not to deceive you," he went on, softer. "To never speak of it. To bury it so deep that nothing wearing a crown could ever dig it up. Every year I stayed in that valley, every fence I mended, every supper — I told myself it was enough. That I could be someone new." He looked down at his scarred forearms, at the sleeves covering them. "But the Vessel remembers me, even if I don't. It's been hunting its last unbroken seal. And that seal is me."

The archive breathed around them, three stories of dead paper holding dead names, and among them, in faded ink, his.

Wren wiped her face angrily with her sleeve. Above them, the skittering started again — closer now, faster, moving along the tops of the shelves with a purpose no mouse had ever had.

"Silas," Brannoc said quietly, hand drifting to his knife, eyes tracking the dark. "What exactly did you seal a hundred years ago?"

Silas didn't answer. He was staring past them all, at the far end of the aisle, where the shadows between the shelves had begun — very slightly, very patiently — to smile.
