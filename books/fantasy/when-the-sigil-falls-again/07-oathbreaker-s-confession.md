## 7. Oathbreaker's Confession

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and old stone, and for a long time nobody spoke.

They had come back from the archive in silence, the way soldiers come back from a field where the dead outnumber the living. Brannoc carried the stolen ledger under his arm like something that might bite. Ivetta hadn't looked at Silas once since the archivist's lamp had fallen across the page — *Silas Nystrom, Seal-Bearer, Year 112 of the Long Peace* — and her hands had gone still the way they went still over a wound too deep to close.

Wren sat on the floor with the prophecy book open in her lap, though she wasn't reading it. She was watching him. She had been watching him since the archive, and Silas understood, with the terrible clarity of a man standing at the edge of a drop he'd been walking toward all his life, that the time for silence was over.

He set down the cup of water Ivetta had pressed into his hands and hadn't drunk.

"You want to know how old I am," he said.

Nobody answered. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, crown-sworn boots rang across flagstones in their too-perfect unison, and the sound seemed to underline everything.

"A hundred and nineteen," Silas said. "Give or take a winter. I stopped counting after the first thirty years in the valley, because counting felt like a lie I was choosing to live inside."

Wren's breath caught — a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"I was twenty-two when I helped seal it," Silas went on, because now that the dam had cracked he could not stop the water. "The sigil. The Vessel's mother-thing, the original binding. There were six of us then. Swordspeakers, wardsingers, one woman who could walk through walls of light. We found it feeding on a city called Carreth — every forgotten thing in Carreth, every name on every grave no one visited anymore. It wore a bishop's body. We cut it out of him and we bound what remained, and the binding required payment." His thumb moved to the scarred words on his forearm, tracing them without looking. "Years from some of us. Memories from others. From me, both."

"Both," Brannoc repeated flatly. He'd planted himself by the door, arms crossed, in the stance of a man guarding against the person inside the room. Silas couldn't blame him.

"The sealing took forty years off me at once," Silas said. "And took—" he searched for it and found only the shape of the hole, the way you find a missing tooth with your tongue, "—my brother. My wife. The road we walked together. I know I loved someone. I know it the way you know rain fell last week, from the evidence. Not from the feeling."

Ivetta finally looked up. Her eyes were wet but her voice was steady, healer-steady, the voice she used when the wound was mortal and everyone knew it. "And the oath-scars. You bound your swordspeech."

"After. When I understood what I'd become." He laughed, and it came out wrong, rusted. "You have to understand what swordspeech is. Every word spoken in power spends a memory to fuel it. A word buys a strike, an unbinding, a wall of force. And I had just learned exactly what those memories were worth. So I swore myself silent. Carved the oath into my own arms so I'd see the price every morning. And I went to the one place in the world where nothing could make me speak."

"The Hollow Valley," Wren whispered.

"The Hollow Valley," he agreed. "Where magic cannot follow. Where a swordspeaker is just a man who mends fences badly." He tried a smile; it didn't hold. "I told myself I was hiding from the Vessel. That was half true. Mostly I was hiding from myself. From the temptation. Because the Vessel has been hunting me for a century, Wren — not this king, not this body, the thing underneath. The fragment we failed to kill. It eats forgotten things, and I am the largest store of forgetting left in the world. Every memory I paid. Everything I sealed away. Its last unbroken seal is sitting in my skull, and if it cracks me open—"

"—it gets everything," Brannoc finished. "A hundred years of a seal-bearer's mind. The binding itself." His jaw worked. "That's why it took the king. Why it built an army. It wasn't conquering. It was digging."

"It needed a gate," Silas said. "And a prophecy book that a curious girl would happen to read aloud on exactly the right night."

The words landed before he could soften them. He watched them land.

Wren closed the prophecy book slowly, her fingers flat on the cover as if holding something shut. "Say that again."

"Wren—"

"No." Her voice cracked upward. "Say it again. Say that you think this is my fault."

"I don't think—"

"You *said* it." She was on her feet now, small and blazing, and Silas thought, absurdly, of the girl he'd taught to throw a knife, who had once cried for two days over a rabbit she'd snared and eaten anyway. "A hundred years you've known this thing exists. A hundred years you've been running, hiding in our valley, eating our food, teaching me knife-work, letting us love you — and you never said a word. And the first time anything goes wrong, you look at me. At *me*."

"That wasn't blame. That was arithmetic." Silas rose too, and his knees ached, and he realized distantly that his body had never lied about its age even when his mouth did. "The book chose tonight because tonight the seal was weakest, and the seal is weak because I am here, and I am here because I chose cowardice—"

"Don't you dare call it cowardice like it's noble!" Brannoc's voice filled the room like a slammed gate. He'd uncrossed his arms; his fists were white-knuckled at his sides. "I served men who hid things from their soldiers. Officers who knew the bridge was mined and sent us across anyway, because the truth was *inconvenient*. I swore I'd never follow another one." He stepped closer, and up close Silas could see the old campaign scars pulling at the corner of his eye. "You want to know what I keep hearing? Every lesson you ever gave me. *Watch a man's hands, not his mouth.* Your hands have been shaking since the archive, Silas. Your mouth has been smooth as a chapel floor for three years. What else did we cross that ward and nearly die for?"

Silas had no answer that would survive the air between them. He stood in it and let it hurt.

"Brannoc." Ivetta's voice, quiet, cutting. "Sit down."

"He lied to us—"

"He lied to *himself* for a hundred years, and it nearly worked." Ivetta stood, and something in her bearing made even Brannoc still. She crossed to Silas and took his scarred forearm in both her hands — gently, the way she handled the dying — and turned it to the firelight. "Do you see these?" she said to the room. "This isn't a soldier's concealment. This is a man who punished himself every day for a century. I've bandaged enough guilt in my life to know it when it's written on skin." Her thumb traced the oath-words. Then, softer, only to Silas: "Why didn't you tell us?"

"Because telling you would cost you," Silas said. "Everything I love gets spent eventually. That's the whole grammar of my life. I sealed a sigil and lost my family. I saved Wren in the throne room and lost my mother's face. If I told you what I was, I'd lose you too — not to the Vessel. To this." He gestured at the room, at the anger, at the fracture spreading through the only thing he had left. "And I couldn't bear it. So yes. I was a coward. I chose the valley because there, at least, I could pretend the price would never come due."

"But it came due," Wren said.

"It always comes due."

She looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes that Silas recognized, and hated himself for recognizing: the look of a person doing arithmetic of their own. Weighing what he had been to them against what he had hidden. The scales moving in a way he couldn't control and wouldn't beg about.

Then she picked up the prophecy book, tucked it under her arm like Brannoc's ledger, and walked to the door.

"Wren." Ivetta moved toward her. "It's curfew. The streets are full of crown-sworn—"

"I need air." Wren's voice was frighteningly level. "I need to not be in a room where the floor is a lie. I'll be careful. I've been careful my whole life; it's what got me to the valley, remember?" She pulled her hood up, and for one heartbeat her eyes met Silas's over the wool, and they were not cruel, which was somehow worse than cruelty. They were deciding. "Don't follow me. Please. Just — give me until dawn."

The door opened on cold air and the distant, perfect rhythm of marching boots. Then it closed, and she was gone into the occupied dark.

* * *

They did not sleep.

Brannoc took the door, which meant taking the door away from Silas — he posted himself against it with the finality of furniture. Ivetta laid out her kit on the table, not because anyone was wounded, but because her hands needed work and sorting bandages was honest. Silas sat by the fire and wrote in the journal Ivetta had given him weeks ago, back when the worst secret in the house was the taste of grief.

*Her name is Wren,* he wrote. *She likes honey on everything, even things that should not have honey. She laughs at her own jokes before she finishes them. She reads aloud when she thinks no one is listening.*

His pen stopped. He read it back and understood what he was doing — building the record before the record was needed. Because tomorrow he might speak again. Tomorrow the Vessel might corner them somewhere with no fence to mend and no valley to hide in, and a word might be the difference between Wren breathing and Wren bleeding, and each word would take a piece of this room, this fire, these people, this night.

*Brannoc hums when he sharpens knives. Badly. On purpose, I think, to annoy me.*

*Ivetta puts willowbark in the tea when she thinks we won't notice. We always notice.*

The scratching of the nib was the only sound. Once, near midnight, Brannoc spoke without turning around.

"She'll come back."

"I know."

"And if she doesn't?"

Silas looked at the fire. "Then I go get her. And I pay whatever the road asks."

Brannoc was quiet a long time. "That's the first true thing you've said all night."

Near dawn, boots sounded in the street below — not the crown-sworn metronome, but lighter, faster, stumbling. Silas was at the window before he decided to move.

Below, hood thrown back, face pale as paper, Wren stood in the middle of the empty street looking up at their window. In her hand she held something that caught the gray light and held it wrong — a sliver of black glass, veined with faint gold, like a splinter cut from something enormous.

She pressed one finger to her lips.

Behind her, at the far end of the street, a crown-sworn patrol turned the corner and began, in perfect unison, to march.
