## 8. The Crown Remembers

They did not bind her hands. That was the first insult, and Wren understood it for exactly what it was: a courtesy extended to something already caught. The guards walked her through the palace corridors in loose formation, not gripping her arms but *guiding* them, the way one guides a chair toward a table. Their smiles were the city's smiles — wide, white, and empty as painted masks.

She counted doors instead of panicking. It was something Silas had taught her during knife-work, back when the valley still hummed and the fences still needed mending and she had still been only a runaway with quick fingers and quicker questions. *When fear takes your breath, give it a task.* Count the doors. Name their hinges. Watch how they open.

Seventeen doors between the courtyard and whatever waited at the end of this corridor. Fourteen swung inward, three outward, and all of them were oak older than any tree left standing in Vaelmoor's poisoned outskirts. The palace drank light through windows of colored glass and gave nothing back. Even her footsteps sounded borrowed.

The guards stopped at a door taller than the rest, banded in silver that had gone black at the edges, as though the metal itself had been bruised. One of them knocked — a strange, tender gesture — and the door opened without anyone answering from within.

*Come in,* said no one, in a voice that was everywhere at once, like the hum the valley used to make before it broke.

Wren stepped inside because the alternative was being stepped inside by someone else, and pride was the only thing she had left that they hadn't confiscated.

* * *

The throne room had been rearranged since Brannoc described it to her — or perhaps he had described it as it used to be, and rooms changed when their owners changed. The dais remained, but the throne itself had been turned to face the wall. Aldric sat upon it sideways, knees drawn up like a boy hiding from a tutor, his small crowned head resting against the cold stone of the seat's back.

He was watching the wall. On it, written in lines of pale gold fire that flickered like candlelight, hung thousands of names. Some glowed steady. Some guttered. A few were fading even as Wren watched, letters thinning to whispers of themselves, and where each faded name vanished, the sigil-thing wearing the king seemed to breathe a little deeper.

"Those are mine," the boy on the throne said, without turning. "Or they were. I kept them as long as I could."

His voice was wrong. Not the wrongness of a puppet — Brannoc had taught her what puppetry looked like, and this was subtler, crueler. The voice was layered, a child's voice laid over something vast and patient, and beneath both, threaded through them like wire through wax —

Silas's voice.

Wren's stomach dropped through the floor. She knew that cadence. She knew the dry half-smile buried in it, the way he let vowels soften at the ends of sentences when he thought no one was listening. She had heard it across a hundred suppers, a hundred knife-lessons, a hundred nights when he stood watch and murmured old valley songs under his breath so the dark wouldn't feel empty.

"You hear it," the Vessel said, and now Aldric turned, and his eyes were not a child's eyes. They were old the way deep water is old. "You hear him in me. How could you not? He is the loudest thing I have ever eaten."

"You haven't eaten him," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her, and then didn't. Fear had given her a task after all. "He's sitting in a cellar two streets from here, eating stolen bread and pretending he isn't worried about me."

"He is very worried about you." The Vessel unfolded Aldric's body from the throne and came down the steps with the careful gait of someone learning limbs that weren't quite the right size. Up close, the king was smaller than she'd imagined — thirteen, maybe fourteen, his wrists sharp as bird bones beneath his ceremonial cuffs. Only the eyes belonged to something else entirely. "That is why you are here, Wren of nowhere, Wren of the broken gate, Wren who read aloud what should have stayed shut."

"I have a lot of names, apparently."

"I have more." The Vessel smiled, and the smile was Aldric's smile stretched over an abyss. "Would you like to know yours? The ones you've forgotten? You've lost so few — children lose almost nothing, it's why you're such poor eating — but there are some. A dog. A winter. A promise made on a bridge."

Something in Wren's chest clenched, hot and involuntary. She shoved it down. *It lies,* she told herself. But the words *a promise made on a bridge* rang against something in her like a bell struck in an empty room, and she couldn't un-ring it.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"To make you an offer." The Vessel stopped an arm's length away. This close, she could see the sigil moving beneath the boy's skin — faint lines of script swimming up his throat like fish rising to feed, sinking again before they could be read. "Your swordspeaker bound himself twice, did you know? Once to seal me, and once to forget the sealing. Every word he has ever spoken in power, every year he paid, every memory he surrendered — none of it is destroyed."

The golden names on the wall guttered. The Vessel glanced at them with something like tenderness.

"Nothing is destroyed," it repeated. "Everything he gave up lives in me. His mother's face. His mother's name. The taste of the bread she baked. The sound of his own laughter before the massacre, before the oath, before the valley. I keep them the way a miser keeps coins — and unlike him, I am willing to spend."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "Spend them on what?"

"On him." The Vessel spread Aldric's small hands, and in the left palm a shape surfaced — a shard of black glass veined with gold script, no bigger than a knife blade. Wren's whole body went still, because she recognized binding-script when she saw it; she'd spent three days in the royal archive with Brannoc's hand heavy on her shoulder, teaching her the difference between wards and chains. "Deliver him to me. Walk him through my door willingly — willingness matters, oh, it matters enormously — and I will return everything. Every memory, every year, every word. He will be whole. Twenty-nine again, truly twenty-nine, with a century returned to him and a mother's face he can finally mourn properly."

"And the king?"

"Aldric?" The Vessel tilted the boy's head, and for one vertiginous moment the layers shifted and Wren saw, underneath, a child looking out through his own eyes from somewhere very far down, drowning and reaching. "Aldric will be released. Unharmed. Free to be a boy again in a kingdom that remembers him fondly. Everyone wins, Wren. Isn't that what families want? For everyone to win?"

*Everyone wins* was a liar's arithmetic. She knew that too — had learned it young, in the years before the valley, when adults with soft voices offered her warm beds and took things in exchange that she only understood she'd lost much later. But the offer wasn't the trap. The trap was that part of the offer was true. The memories *were* in there. She could see them — the wall full of names, the hoard of a man's entire self, glittering behind the monster's ribs.

And Silas would never ask for them back. She knew that too. He'd sooner cut out his tongue than speak one syllable that bought back his past at anyone's cost but his own. Which meant if those memories were ever going home, someone would have to steal them.

Someone with quick fingers.

"Let me think," Wren said, and was proud of how wounded she made herself sound, how tempted.

"Think," the Vessel agreed pleasantly. "I am very old. I can wait. I have waited a hundred years in a crack beneath the world, and thirty years in the dreams of a crown prince, and eleven months in a boy who cries at night when he thinks I cannot feel it." The golden names flared briefly, all of them at once, and the room filled with the smell of hot iron. "But do not mistake patience for blindness. I know what you're doing with your hands."

Wren froze. Her hands, she realized, had drifted toward her sleeves — toward the little knife, the real one, the one the guards hadn't found because it wasn't a weapon, exactly, but a tool, and tools hid differently.

"No," the Vessel said softly. "Not the blade. Your fingers. They move when you lie. Did you know that? Tap-tap-tap against your thigh, like a bird learning a song. He notices it too, at supper. He finds it charming." Something moved through Aldric's face — hunger, or its imitation. "I find it useful."

Wren forced her hands flat and still against her sides. *Give the fear a task.* She counted the exits. Two guards at the door, motionless. Three windows, high and narrow, glass thick as her thumb. The Vessel, one arm's length away, holding out the shard of binding like a merchant offering fruit.

"Say I believe you," she said slowly. "Say I deliver him. What proof do I have you'll pay?"

"Proof." The Vessel considered this with the boy's brow furrowed, a grotesque echo of homework. Then it reached into the air beside it, into the space where the golden names burned, and plucked one out.

Wren saw it happen. Saw the Vessel's fingers close around a line of light — a short name, guttering, half-faded — and draw it out of the constellation like a splinter from skin. And in the moment it hung there between them, Wren felt it. Not saw. *Felt.*

Bread. Woodsmoke. A kitchen with a crooked door. A woman singing low and off-key, flour on her knuckles, saying *say it back to me, love, say it back so I know you'll keep it* — and a small boy's voice answering, promising something, and the woman laughing —

Then the memory folded itself closed, and the Vessel held out not light but a sliver of black-gold glass, humming with warmth, and Wren understood with sick clarity that she had just been shown a sample. A taste. Silas's mother, portioned out like cheese at market.

"That one is small," the Vessel said. "One of hundreds. Bring me the swordspeaker, and you may carry them home yourself, one by one, like seeds."

Wren looked at the shard. At the boy behind the monster's eyes, still reaching, still drowning. At the wall of names, where fresh gaps had opened, thinning the constellation further.

And she did the bravest and most cowardly thing she had ever done in her short, thieving life.

"Yes," she said. "I'll bring him."

* * *

They walked her out through seventeen doors, and this time she counted them backward, and this time her hands were perfectly, deliberately still.

At the last door, the Vessel caught her shoulder — Aldric's small fingers closing with strength no child should have — and leaned close, and spoke directly into her ear in Silas's voice alone, stripped of all its other layers, gentle as a lullaby:

"Tap-tap, little thief."

Her blood went to ice.

But it let her go. It pushed her out into the grey evening street, into the smell of rain and distant bread ovens, and the great door boomed shut behind her, and Wren stood alone on the palace steps with her heart hammering and her sleeve heavy.

Because while the monster had watched her fingers, it had not watched her sleeve — or rather, it had watched for the *knife*, and missed the moment in the throne room when the Vessel had pressed the binding-shard into her palm as earnest money, proof of good faith, a seed of trust.

It believed she would come back. That was its blind spot: it was a miser, and misers always believed everyone could be bought.

Wren opened her hand. The shard sat in her palm, black glass veined with crawling gold script, warm as a living thing, whispering in a voice like the valley's hum before it broke.

She had lied to a god. She had a piece of its chain in her fist. And two streets away, a man with a century of stolen years was waiting, unaware that his family had just begun the work of stealing him back.

She ran.
