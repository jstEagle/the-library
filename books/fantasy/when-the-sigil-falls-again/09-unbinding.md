## 9. Unbinding

**Silas**

The coronation spire rose out of Vaelmoor like a needle of white stone, its crown-shaped summit lit from within by a light that had no warmth in it. Silas climbed the last spiral stair with his hand on the wall, because his legs wanted to betray him, and because he wanted to touch something solid while he still knew what solid was.

The scars on his forearms burned. They had been burning since the valley — since Wren's voice cracked open the ward with a dead woman's prophecy — but here, at the heart of the thing, they felt less like scars and more like seams. Like skin stretched over words that were trying to get out.

*Don't*, he told them. *Not yet.*

Behind him, Brannoc's boots rang on the marble, steady as a drumbeat. Ivetta's breathing was controlled, the way it got when she was about to do something that frightened her. And ahead, small and quick and terrified and brave, Wren.

He could hear the Vessel before he saw it. It sang through the spire itself, through the stones, a sound like a hundred voices trying to remember one name. The light at the summit pulsed in time with it.

At the top of the stairs, the chamber opened wide, a crown of glass and moonstone under the naked sky, and there stood King Aldric — or the shape of him. The boy stood at the center of a sigil vast enough to cover the whole floor, drawn in lines of slow silver fire. His eyes were open. They were not his own. Something ancient looked out through them, patient as erosion, and when it saw Silas it smiled with a mouth that had learned smiling from watching humans do it.

*The last seal walks to me freely,* it said, in a voice like pages turning. *I have waited a century for you, swordspeaker.*

"I know," Silas said. "I'm sorry I kept you waiting."

He stepped onto the sigil. Somewhere behind him, Wren made a small sound — half protest, half prayer — and he did not turn around, because if he turned around he would look at her face, and he needed her face. He needed all their faces. He was going to spend them like coin, every last one, and he refused to do it blind.

The unbinding was old swordspeech, older than the massacre, older than the oath. A binding sealed with words could only be unsealed with words, and the words would be paid for from the speaker's own well. One memory per syllable. He counted them once, in the dark of the chapel ruins, and then made himself stop counting, because counting was a coward's arithmetic.

*Ivetta,* he said inside himself. *Brannoc. Wren. Supper. The fence I never finished mending. Her laugh. His grumbling. Her hands. The way the valley hums at dusk—*

"Begin," he said aloud, and began.

* * *

**Brannoc**

They came up the stairwell three minutes after Silas started speaking, and Brannoc heard the first word of power like a bell rung inside his own skull.

Then the guards came, and there was no more time for listening.

There were twelve of them on the landing, crown-sworn, moving with that terrible puppet-smoothness he'd seen in the streets below, halberds leveled, faces slack and smiling. Brannoc planted himself at the top of the narrow stair where only two could come at him at once, and he did the thing he had sworn on a mass grave never to do again.

He took command.

"Left side holds," he barked, soldier-voice, the old voice, the voice that had once moved three hundred men across a burning field. "Wren — shard, now, don't watch us, watch *him*. Ivetta — the king, go."

And they went. That was the miracle of it, the thing that would keep him upright for years afterward: they went. No argument, no fear he could see. They trusted him. He had spent nine years believing trust was a debt he could never repay, and here it was, being spent all at once, and he found he could carry it.

The first guard came on and Brannoc broke his halberd shaft with a shield-blow and put him down with the pommel, not the edge. The second came, and the third. He did not kill them. That was the vow he kept tonight: these were boys, most of them, hollowed out and ridden by something older than their grandfathers, and they were not the enemy. The enemy was on the throne of light above him, and the man paying for its death was doing it one memory at a time, and Brannoc's job was simply this — *hold the line so no one interrupts the bill.*

A blade opened his cheek. Another caught his shoulder. He gave ground, gave ground, held the stair. Blood ran into his eye and he spat and laughed, actually laughed, because some young part of him that had died in the war had crawled back up out of its grave to stand here, in a doorway, protecting people who loved him.

"Come on, then," he told the twelfth guard, as the spire began to scream. "You're not taking one more thing from my family."

* * *

**Ivetta**

Ivetta knelt inside the sigil's outer ring, where the silver fire parted around her like water around a stone — it wanted the king, not her — and she took Aldric's face in her two hands.

Up close, the possession was worse than she'd imagined. The boy was still in there; she could feel him the way she felt fever under skin, a small heat guttering. The Vessel had wrapped itself around his mind like ivy around a sapling, and the sapling was bending, bending, nearly broken.

"Aldric," she said. "My name is Ivetta. I'm a healer. I need you to hear me."

The ancient eyes rolled toward her. The mouth smiled. *"He cannot hear you. He is very far away. He has been far away for a long time."*

"He can hear me." She poured everything she had into her voice — the tone she used on dying men, on mothers holding children who wouldn't survive the night. Calm. Certain. A rope thrown into dark water. "Aldric. Your mother used to sing you to sleep. I don't know the song, but you do. Find it. Hold on to it. Climb toward it."

Something flickered in the boy's eyes. A stutter in the ancient patience.

Above her, Silas's voice went on, syllable after syllable, and each one landed on Ivetta's skin like cold rain, because she alone understood what each cost. She had seen the oath-scars. She had read them the night she tended his wounds — *I bind my speech, I bind my years, let what I was stay buried* — and she had understood then what she understood now: that Silas Nystrom had been spending himself in installments his whole life, and tonight he meant to spend the account entire.

"Don't you dare leave anything for regret," she whispered to him, not knowing if he could hear. "Pay it all. We'll remember for you."

The king shuddered. Beneath her palms, faint as a pulse in a wrist, she felt a child's mind stir — a boy humming a lullaby in the dark, climbing toward her voice hand over hand.

"That's it," she breathed. "That's it. Keep coming."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren crossed the sigil with the stolen shard clutched against her chest, and the silver fire did not burn her, because the shard was part of the fire, and the fire recognized its own.

The Vessel saw her coming. Of course it did.

*Little thief,* it said, in Silas's old voice — the voice from the vision it had shown her in the palace cell, the voice of a younger Silas standing over a broken sigil, weeping. *I offered you everything he lost. Every supper, every laugh, every kindness he has already forgotten and will forget tonight. You could have had him whole.*

"You offered me pieces," Wren said. Her voice shook. She let it shake. "I want the man who's still standing."

She reached Aldric at the center. Up close, the king looked sixteen and exhausted, a lantern with something enormous coiled inside it, and the binding-sigil blazed beneath his feet — the same geometry, Wren realized, as the prophecy book's final page. The book hadn't mapped events. It had mapped *this*. It had always been an instruction manual wearing a prophecy's clothes.

*You don't know what you hold,* the Vessel said, and its attention came off Silas for the first time, and fastened on her, vast and cold and curious. *Give me the swordspeaker and I will give you back his mother's face. His valley. Every supper. He will know you, little runaway. Isn't that what you want? To be remembered?*

Wren thought of the chapel ruins. Of ash-black ink. Of the five of them crammed into a collapsing gate, screaming, falling, arriving — her curiosity, her consequence, her fault. She thought of Silas teaching her knife-work in the yard, correcting her grip with infinite patience, saying *again* forty times without once making her feel small.

"No," she said. "What I want is for this to end."

And she drove the shard home into the heart of the binding.

* * *

**Silas**

He was deep in the words now, deeper than he had ever been, and he was losing things faster than he could name them.

He had already lost the valley's hum at dusk. He had lost the taste of Ivetta's stew, though he could see the bowl in his mind, steaming, flavorless as a painting. He had lost the sound of Brannoc's grumbling, and then Brannoc's face, and then the fact that Brannoc had a face — only the knowledge remained, a name attached to nothing, a coat hook with no coat.

He kept speaking. The swordspeech poured out of him like blood from a opened vein, and the sigil beneath the king began to unravel, silver fire turning to silver thread turning to silver dust, and the Vessel screamed through the boy's mouth, a sound that cracked the moonstone overhead.

*STOP,* it said. *SWORDSPEAKER. STOP.*

"There is no stopping," Silas said, and heard his own voice come out strange and thin, a stranger's voice. "I bound you once with words. I unbind you with words. Take them. Take everything."

He reached for the last of it — the family, the whole shining weight of them — and found, to his surprise, that his hand did not shake.

*Wren's laugh.* Gone.

*A name: Ivetta.* Gone.

*Someone mended fences with me. Someone taught someone knives. Someone held a line. Someone—*

Gone. Gone. Gone.

The last word left him and the world went white and silent, and somewhere in the silence something ancient unraveled screaming into a thousand years of scattered threads, and a boy fell forward into waiting arms, weeping, free, alive.

* * *

**Silas**

Silas woke on cold marble with dawn coming through broken glass.

His arms ached. His throat was raw. There was blood on him — not his, he thought, though he couldn't have said whose — and a big scarred man sat nearby with a bandaged cheek, watching him with an expression Silas couldn't read, grief and joy fighting openly on the man's face.

"You're awake," the man said roughly.

"I— yes." Silas pushed himself up. His head was strangely light. Empty rooms everywhere, doors standing open. "I'm sorry. Do I know you?"

The man flinched as if struck. Then, visibly, deliberately, he smiled. "No," he said. "But you're going to. My name's Brannoc. Come on — there are people who've been waiting a long time to meet you."

Across the shattered spire, a girl with ink-stained fingers was opening an old book.
