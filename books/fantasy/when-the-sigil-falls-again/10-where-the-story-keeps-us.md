## 10. Where the Story Keeps Us

Ivetta had stitched closed a hundred wounds in her life, and she had learned long ago that the sewing was the easy part. The hard part came after, when the bleeding stopped and the body remembered it was alive, and the patient opened their eyes and asked *what happened to me* — and she had to decide how much truth a person could hold without tearing.

She had never imagined she would be the one asking that question about Silas Nystrom, or that he would look at her — when his eyes finally opened in the grey hush of dawn, three days after the spire — with the flat, courteous blankness of a stranger.

"You're hurt," he said. His voice was rough as an unsharpened blade. "Your hands."

Ivetta looked down at them. Burns, mostly, from the shard of the binding — the sigil-light had run through her palms like water through cupped fingers, and she'd held on anyway, because letting go meant losing Aldric's mind to the dark. The skin was still angry and pink. She'd bandaged them herself, badly, because there had been no one else to do it and no time to care.

"They'll heal," she said.

"Let me see them properly." He sat up — too fast; she saw the wave of dizziness cross his face and watched him refuse it, the way she'd seen him refuse pain a hundred times before. Some things, apparently, lived deeper than memory. He took her wrist with careful fingers, turned her hand over in the light, frowned at her work with the particular disapproval of a man who has been field-stitched by soldiers and knows exactly what bad bandaging looks like. "You wrapped this too tight. It will cut off the blood."

"Old habit," Ivetta said. "Wrapping tight. Holding on."

Something flickered across his face — not recognition, not quite, but the shadow of its absence. The shape of a hole. He let go of her hand and looked at his own forearms instead, at the scarred words written there, old and new, the oath-scars and the unbinding-scars laid over each other like two languages arguing on the same page.

"I don't know who wrote these," he said quietly.

"You did," Ivetta said. "Both times."

He was silent for a long moment. Outside the infirmary window, Vaelmoor was waking — the real city, the one beneath the crown's occupation, the one that had smiled too widely for months and was now learning, painfully, to smile like people again. She could hear hammering somewhere, and a child laughing, and the bells of the coronation spire ringing wrong because half of them had cracked when the Vessel screamed itself apart. The sound was imperfect and human and it made her throat ache.

"I don't remember any of you," Silas said. "Do I."

It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway. "No."

"The girl — Wren. She's been reading to me from a book. A prophecy." He shook his head slowly. "She cries when she thinks I'm asleep. She thinks I don't notice."

"She's fifteen," Ivetta said. "Crying is most of what she does. The rest is stealing things and being right about everything."

"And the big one. Brannoc. He sits outside my door all night like a dog guarding a grave." Silas's mouth twisted. "He told me I taught him a sword-form once. He showed me the stance. My body knew it." His voice dropped. "My body knows all of you. It's only me that doesn't."

Ivetta pulled a stool to the bedside and sat, because her knees were not steady and she refused to show it. She was sixty-one years old and she had buried a husband and a daughter and every patient she couldn't save, and she had thought herself past the point where grief could find anything new in her to hold. She had been wrong. This was new. This was watching a man be born out of the ruin of another man, and mourning the first one while loving the second, both at once, in the same breath.

"Do you want to know?" she asked. "What you were to us?"

Silas looked at her. His eyes were the same eyes. That was the cruelest part — nothing about him had changed except everything underneath, the way a house is the same house after a fire takes the inside of it.

"Yes," he said. "But not today. Today my head is full of a hundred years I can't account for, and a king who keeps coming to sit with me and call me by name like I'm someone he owes his life to." He exhaled. "Tell me slowly. All of you. Tell me so I can keep it."

Ivetta reached into her apron pocket and brought out a small book — cheap paper, leather cover gone soft with handling, the kind sold in market stalls for a few coppers. She set it on his blanket.

"Then write it down," she said. "Everything we tell you. Everything you feel. Because we've learned something in this valley and this city, Silas Nystrom, all of us together: memories kept only in the head can be taken. Words on a page are harder to steal."

He touched the book like it might burn him. "Whose idea was this?"

"Mine," said Wren, from the doorway.

Ivetta hadn't heard her come in — the girl moved quiet as a cat when she wanted to, though lately she'd been trying to move less quiet, trying to be *present*, Ivetta thought, in a way that cost her visibly. She stood there now with the prophecy book hugged against her chest, ash-black letters glinting faintly on its open page, her eyes red-rimmed and her chin set in the stubborn angle Ivetta had come to think of as the whole of Wren's soul.

"It's my fault you're like this," Wren said. "I read the date out loud. I broke the ward. So it's mine to fix." She crossed the room and put a second book beside Ivetta's — a fat, blank ledger she'd clearly stolen from the palace archive, still bearing the royal stamp. "This is yours now. And I'm going to read you the prophecy book, all of it, even the parts about you, even the parts that are horrible. And Brannoc is going to tell you about the fights, and Ivetta is going to nag you about your bandages, and you're going to write it ALL down, because—" Her voice cracked and she pushed through it, furious with the crack. "Because the sigil ate what you forgot. But it's dead now. Dead things can't eat words on paper."

Silas looked between the two of them — the healer with her burned hands and the thief with her stolen books — and something happened in his face that Ivetta would remember writing about later, in her own journal, by candlelight: not recognition, but the *reaching* for it. A hand extended across a chasm toward a bridge that wasn't there yet.

"All right," he said simply. "Start wherever you like."

Wren sat down on the foot of the bed like she owned it, which in a way she did. She opened the prophecy book to its first page.

* * *

The days found a shape, the way days do when people refuse to let them stay broken.

In the mornings, Aldric came. The young king looked older than his nineteen years now — the Vessel had worn him thin as parchment — but he was awake in a way the city whispered about with wonder, awake and grieving and ferociously determined. He would sit with Silas for an hour and talk, and Silas would listen with the careful attention of a man assembling a puzzle whose picture he couldn't see.

"You sealed the original sigil," Aldric told him, on the fourth day. "A hundred years ago. You paid with your years and your memories, and then you hid somewhere magic couldn't reach, and you stayed hidden until my crown found you anyway." He paused. "I'm told I begged you to save me. In light, on the air. Like a child writing on a window."

"You did," Silas said. "They tell me. I don't remember it either."

"No," Aldric said, and smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked and entirely his own. "But I remember *you*. Someone has to, if you can't. That's what a kingdom is, maybe. Everyone remembering everyone else."

On the fifth day, Brannoc carried Silas down to the training yard against every instruction Ivetta gave, and put a wooden practice sword in his hand, and said, "Show me the seventh form," and Silas's body flowed into it like water finding its old channel while his face went white with something that wasn't fear exactly and wasn't joy exactly but was some marriage of the two. Afterward they sat on the steps and Brannoc retold the fights — the collapsing gate, the puppet guards, the line he'd held at the coronation spire with a broken shield and a borrowed knife while the world screamed above him.

"Held the line for you," Brannoc said. "Don't suppose that means anything to you."

"No," Silas said honestly. Then: "Tell me again anyway."

Brannoc grinned — the wide, gap-toothed grin of a man who had sworn never to serve again and had discovered that serving a family was not the same thing. "Oh, I'll tell it again. I'll tell it so many times you'll wish you'd forgotten it twice."

And in the evenings, Wren read. She read the prophecy book cover to cover, twice, doing voices, correcting herself, stopping to argue with the dead cartographer who'd written it. She read him the records from the archive — *Silas Nystrom, Swordspeaker, Year One Hundred Before the Crown* — and watched him grapple with the arithmetic of his own impossible age. She read him the journal entries he himself was writing, back to him, so he could hear his new voice describing his old scars.

"It says here," she announced one night, tapping the page, "that you were supposed to forget us. That was the price. But it also says—" she hesitated, and Ivetta, mending by the fire, saw her gather herself— "it also says the story isn't finished. There's a last page. It's blank. It just says *where the story keeps us*."

"Then we'll have to write it," Silas said.

"Yeah," Wren said, blinking hard. "That's the general idea, swordspeaker."

* * *

On the ninth evening, Aldric came to the infirmary with snow on his shoulders — the first snow of winter, early and clean, falling on a city that no longer flinched from the sky — and he did not sit in his usual chair. He stood, formally, the way kings stand when they are about to make something official.

"The Vessel didn't just take memories," he said. "It took names. Half of Vaelmoor has gaps in it — streets no one remembers building, families no one remembers burying. Rebuilding means remembering. All of it. Every forgotten thing." He looked at Silas. "You've forgotten more than anyone alive, and you're learning it back faster than anyone alive. I want you here. Not as a soldier. As a keeper of the record. Help me build the archive of everything the crown tried to erase. Help me make sure no one in Vaelmoor is ever forgotten again."

The room went very quiet. Snow ticked softly against the glass.

Silas looked down at his forearms, at the scarred words, at the small new journal open on his knee filled with his own careful handwriting — *Brannoc: loyal, loud, afraid of small spaces. Wren: reads aloud when frightened. Ivetta: wraps bandages too tight, holds on.* A stranger's observations about people who loved him. People who, day by day, were becoming familiar the way a road becomes familiar — not remembered, but walked so often it wears itself into knowing.

"I spent a hundred years hiding from the world," he said at last, "so I would never have to pay for anything again. And it found me anyway. And it cost me everything I loved." He looked up, and his eyes — the same eyes — found each of them in turn. "So yes. I'll stay. Not because I remember why any of you matter to me. But because you clearly do, and I intend to find out."

Ivetta laughed — a wet, cracked laugh she didn't bother to disguise — and Wren threw her arms around him hard enough to knock the journal off his knee, and Brannoc said something gruff about *finally* that fooled no one.

Later, much later, when the others had gone to bed and the snow fell thick and silent over the mending city, Ivetta sat by the fire with her own journal and wrote the day down, because that was the covenant now, the new oath none of them had spoken aloud but all of them kept: *if it matters, write it down.*

At the bottom of the page, she paused, pen hovering.

Then she added one final line:

*He chose us twice — once without knowing why, and once knowing exactly what it cost. Whatever comes next, the story has us now, and we have it, and no power under any crown can take what's written down.*

Outside, the snow kept falling, gentle as forgiveness, on the valley far away where a fence still needed mending — and on the city where five people, bound by ink instead of memory, began the long work of remembering the world.
