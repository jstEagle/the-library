## 1. The Charms of Low Market

The rain came to Vellmark the way debt came to the poor — slowly, then all at once, and never where you wanted it. Edmund Dunmore watched it silver the canvas awning of his stall and thought, not for the first time, that he had built his entire life out of things that could be taken down in under a minute.

That was the point of a stall. That was the beauty of it.

He had timed himself once, years ago, alone in this back corner of Low Market before the vendors arrived, when the cobbles still smelled of last night's fish and the gulls were arguing over something in the gutters. Forty seconds. The awning folded, the trays of charms went into their padded case, the little brass scales tucked under one arm, the signboard with its painted sun and moon slid flat against his chest. Forty seconds, and Edmund the charm-seller ceased to exist, leaving behind nothing but a patch of wet stones.

He had never needed those forty seconds. Not once in seven years. But he counted them anyway, some mornings, like a man counting the steps between himself and a cliff edge. It was a comfort, knowing the way down.

"Morning, Dunmore." Old Sef the fishwife paused by his stall, her cart rattling behind her, her breath visible in the gray air. "You've got your good face on."

"I only have the one face, Sef."

"That's what I said." She squinted at him the way she always did, as though he were a herring she was deciding whether to buy. Seven years, and she had never bought anything from him but luck-charms for her nets, and she had never quite stopped looking at him as if she were waiting for the mask to slip. Perhaps that was just her face. Or perhaps — and this was the thought Edmund kept in the locked room at the back of his mind, the room he visited as rarely as possible — perhaps seven years was simply not long enough. Perhaps it would never be long enough.

"Rain's coming in off the harbor," Sef said. "Bad day for selling trinkets."

"Good day for selling umbrellas."

"You don't sell umbrellas."

"No," Edmund agreed. "I don't." What he sold were charms: small discs of tin and copper stamped with spirals and knots, braided cords, knotted ribbons, bits of river-glass wrapped in wire. He sold them for two coppers apiece, three for five, and every single one of them was exactly as magical as a button.

This was the joke of his life, the joke nobody else could ever be allowed to understand. The Purge had come through Vellmark like a fever seven years ago — the edicts nailed to the cathedral doors, the pyres in Founder's Square, the inquisitors in their bone-white coats going door to door with their cold-iron compasses and their lists. And Edmund Dunmore, third-ranked gatekeeper of the Vellmark ward, keeper of the seal beneath the city, heir to an office older than the kingdom itself, had survived it all by becoming a fraud.

He made his charms with a jeweler's patience and no magic whatsoever. He hammered the tin flat, stamped the spirals with a punch he'd bought from a smith who asked no questions, aged the copper in vinegar so it looked old and wise. Customers bought them for safe childbirth and fair winds and protection from the evil eye, and they believed, because believing was free, and Edmund took their coppers with a shopkeeper's smile and a small ache behind his breastbone that never entirely went away.

Because he *could* have made them work.

He could have stood in his stall on any morning of the last seven years and breathed a word over the river-glass and set it glowing like a captured star. He could have tied a knot into a cord that would hold true through storm and sickness both. There had been a woman once, early on, whose daughter was dying of the lung-fever, who had come to him weeping and offering everything she had, and Edmund had sold her a two-copper charm and told her — as the law required him to imply, as survival required him to insist — that it was only a token, madam, only a blessing, no more than hope made solid.

The girl had lived anyway. Edmund had gone home that night and been quietly, furiously sick, and had never told anyone why.

*You keep the gate,* his master had told him, back when there had been a master, back when there had been words worth saying aloud. *The gate does not need you to be brave. It needs you to be boring. Be so dull that even the gods overlook you.*

He had gotten very good at dull.

* * *

The morning picked up around mid-bell, as it always did, when the market filled with bodies and noise and the smell of frying onions from the cook-stall two rows over. Edmund sold a luck-knot to a young mason with mortar on his hands, a birthing charm to a grandmother buying for her daughter, and a river-glass pendant to a girl of maybe ten who counted out her coppers one at a time, biting each one first, with the gravity of a treasury official.

"For my mam," the girl explained. "She works nights at the ropewalk and she says the dark follows her home."

"The dark can't follow you past a good strong light," Edmund said, which was the sort of thing charm-sellers said, and also happened, in this case, to be almost true. The glass caught the gray daylight and threw a small warm spark across the girl's palm, and her whole face opened like a window.

Edmund watched her go and felt the ache behind his breastbone shift, as it always did, into something harder to name.

He was weighing a handful of tin discs when the nervous man came to his stall.

He came the way nervous people always came to Edmund's stall — browsing without buying, touching things without asking, circling twice before landing. He was thin, gray-faced, dressed in the decent-but-not-too-decent clothes of a clerk or a scrivener, with ink stains on his right cuff and sweat on his upper lip despite the cold. His eyes kept moving. Market, crowd, alley mouth. Market, crowd, alley mouth.

Edmund had spent seven years learning to read that pattern the way sailors read weather.

"Looking for anything particular?" he asked pleasantly.

The man flinched. Actually flinched, shoulders jumping like a startled bird, and Edmund revised his assessment upward from *nervous customer* to *nervous customer with a reason*. "Charms," the man said. "Yes. Charms. For — luck."

"We have a fine selection of luck."

The man laughed, high and wrong, and picked up a tin disc at random, turning it over in fingers that didn't quite steady. Up close Edmund could see the sleeplessness written on him, the hollows under his eyes, the way his collar sat slightly askew as though he'd dressed in the dark or in a hurry or both. "My wife says I'm being foolish," he said. "She says if I just stop reading things, stop — asking questions, everything will go back to—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and set the disc down. "How much is this?"

"Two coppers. Three for five."

The man paid for one. His hand shook doing it, and when their fingers touched briefly over the coin, Edmund felt it — faint as a draft under a door, unmistakable as a shout. A residue. The man had been near magic recently. Near it, or near someone who carried it, and recently enough that it clung to him like smoke to wool.

Edmund smiled his shopkeeper's smile and felt the smile and the fear arrive together, twins, inseparable after seven years.

"Thank you," the man whispered, tucking the charm away inside his coat, against his chest, the way people did when they meant it. Then he leaned forward, just slightly, just enough, and his voice dropped to almost nothing beneath the market's noise. "Master Dunmore. Someone has been asking about gatekeepers."

The word went through Edmund like a nail of ice, straight down, pinning him to the spot behind his stall. He did not move. He did not let his face move. Somewhere in the middle distance a gull screamed and the onion-fryer cursed a customer and the world went on being ordinary, and Edmund stood very still inside it, the way he had trained himself to stand for seven years.

"Gatekeepers?" he said mildly. "Like the men on the city gates? I don't know much about—"

"Not the walls." The man's eyes were wet now, and terrified, and fixed on Edmund's with the terrible directness of a drowning man. "The *old* kind. Under-kind. Someone's been asking. In the records offices, in the parish registries. Pulling names. Old names. Names from before." He swallowed. "I copy documents, Master Dunmore. That's all I do. But I read what I copy, and what I copied last week had your street in it, and yesterday it had your *name*, and today I couldn't stand it anymore, so I came to tell you, because—" His voice cracked. "Because whoever's asking isn't stopping at the names. They're asking what became of them. Where they went. Who they became."

The market roared on, oblivious. A child ran past chasing a hoop. Somewhere a bell marked the hour, bright and indifferent.

"How many names?" Edmund heard himself ask. His voice was perfectly steady. He was distantly proud of that, and distantly horrified.

"On the list I saw? Nine." The clerk pressed the purchased charm against his chest as though it might actually protect him, and Edmund thought with a strange, floating detachment: *he thinks I sold him a working charm. He came to warn me because he thinks I'm the real thing.* "Eight are crossed out already. Dead, or found, or — I don't know what the marks mean. Yours is the ninth. It isn't crossed out yet."

"And the person asking?"

"Wears white." The clerk's throat moved. "Bone-white coat. Cold-iron compass on a chain. She doesn't write the answers down. She *remembers* them." He stepped back suddenly, as if remembering himself, as if the enormity of what he'd done had finally landed on him. "I have to go. My wife—"

"Wait." Edmund's hand was already moving before his mind authorized it, selecting a cord from the tray, pressing it into the man's palm along with his two coppers back. "For your trouble. A gift. Go home the long way. Through the fishmarket. Don't take your usual route today, or tomorrow. Understand me?"

The clerk stared at the cord, at the coins, at Edmund's face. Whatever he saw there made him nod, quick and jerky, and then he was gone, folding into the market crowd like water into water, and Edmund stood behind his stall with his heart hammering against his ribs and his hands — his traitor, honest hands — beginning, very slightly, to shake.

* * *

He closed early. It was the first time in seven years he had closed early, and he did it badly, fumbling the awning ties, dropping the coin-box, laughing at himself in a thin unsteady thread because the alternative was standing very still in the empty market and screaming.

Forty seconds, he thought. Forty seconds and gone. But gone *where*, that was the question, wasn't it, that was always the question, and for seven years he had refused to look at it directly, the way you refuse to look at the sun.

His rooms above the stall were two chambers and a ladder, spare as a monk's cell, because a man planning to run someday does not accumulate. But he had accumulated anyway — everyone accumulates, that was the bitter lesson of it — a chipped blue cup he liked, a blanket worn soft, a chair by the window where he sat in the evenings and watched the lamplighters work. Seven years of careful, deliberate, forgettable life, and every piece of it suddenly weighed itself in his hands and asked: *can you carry me? Can you leave me?*

He sat in the chair by the window and did not light a lamp, because lamplight drew the eye, and made himself think.

Nine names. Eight struck through. A woman in a bone-white coat who remembered instead of wrote. Gatekeepers, under-kind, the old offices, the ones the Purge had supposedly erased root and branch — and here was the part that made the cold spread outward from his spine into his limbs, the part the clerk hadn't understood and couldn't have: the gatekeepers weren't merely mages. They were *wardens*. Every gatekeeper in the realm held a piece of something, a duty, a binding older than the edicts and the pyres and the inquisitors' compasses. If someone was hunting them, striking them from a list like a merchant striking debts from a ledger—

Then they weren't hunting sorcerers. They were dismantling something. Piece by piece, name by name, eight times already, and Edmund's name sat ninth on the list, uncrossed, waiting.

And beneath his feet, beneath all of Vellmark, behind seven years of sealed stone and older seals, the ward he had tended in secret — checking its pulse through the floorboards on certain nights when the moon was dark, feeding it whispers of maintenance when the city slept — that ward had been *straining*. He had told himself it was age. He had told himself it was the damp of the undercity working into old mortar. He had told himself stories the way his customers told themselves stories about two-copper charms.

It had been straining for months now. Like something leaning on a door from the other side.

Edmund got up, lit the lamp after all — damn the eye that might be drawn, he needed to see his own hands — and pulled loose the floorboard at the foot of his bed. Beneath it lay a box, and in the box lay the things of his other life: his father's gatekeeper's ring, warm to the touch even now, even after seven years in the dark; a stub of chalk mixed with crushed silver; and a folded scrap of vellum bearing half a sigil, drawn by a dead man's hand, that he had not dared look at since the night he'd hidden it.

He did not take them out. He knelt there with the box open and the lamp guttering and listened — really listened, the way he had not permitted himself in years — down through the floorboards, down through the foundations, down through the clay and the older stone beneath, to the place where the ward pulsed in its long slow sleep.

The rhythm was wrong.

Not strained. Not aging. *Wrong* — skipping, stuttering, like a heartbeat with something lodged against the valve. And threaded through it, faint but growing, a second pulse answering it from somewhere far away and getting closer, the way thunder gets closer.

Someone was knocking. From outside the seal. Or from inside it.

Edmund closed the box. He put it back beneath the floorboard, and then he took it out again, and put the ring on his finger for the first time in seven years, and the metal settled against his skin with a familiarity that hurt worse than any wound.

Down in the market square, faintly, a whistle blew — the watch, probably, or a drunk, or nothing. Above the rooftops the clouds were tearing apart to show a sliver of hard white stars, and the wind coming off the harbor smelled of salt and, underneath, very faintly, of ash.

Somewhere out there, a woman in a bone-white coat was working her way down a list, and his name was next.

Edmund looked at the ring on his hand, at the door of his two small rooms, at the forty seconds of escape he'd practiced for seven years and never used.

He was still looking when the knock came at his door — three slow blows, patient as a headsman — and a voice through the wood, low and courteous, said:

"Master Dunmore. We should talk about the gate."
