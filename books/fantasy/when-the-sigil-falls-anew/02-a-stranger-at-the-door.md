## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The knock came at the hour when Vellmark's bells had gone hoarse and the Low Market lamps burned low in their oil, and Edmund Dunmore was counting coppers with the particular attention of a man who has learned that small sums kept honest are the best armor a hunted thing can wear.

He almost didn't answer it.

Seven years of practice lived in that hesitation — seven years of measuring every knock against the ones he knew. The fat brewer's boy who came Thursdays for a luck-charm his mother swore by. Old Mistress Halloway, who bought love-powders she never used and paid in buttons. The inquisitors, who never knocked, because knocking was for men who expected doors to open honestly.

This knock was wrong in three ways. It was too soft, as if the knocker were holding himself upright by the doorframe. It came in a rhythm that stumbled rather than rapped. And it stopped halfway through, as though the hand behind it had simply forgotten what it was doing.

Edmund set down his ledger. He looked at the little brass bell above his door — a genuine warding piece, one of the last true workings he owned, tuned to taste iron and ill intent — and saw its faint inner glow gutter from white to amber and back again, uncertain. Not an inquisitor's touch, then. Inquisitors rang wards like cracked bells. This was something else. Something bleeding.

"Closed," he called through the wood, and hated how steady his voice sounded, how practiced. "Come back at dawn."

The answer was a sound like a sack of wet grain sliding down a wall, and then silence.

* * *

He told himself he would not open the door. He stood there in the narrow space between counter and shelf, among the hanging bundles of dried rowan and the trays of pewter talismans he cast himself — all of them dross, all of them harmless, all of them deliberately, painstakingly empty of anything the Purge could hang him for — and he told himself the story he had told himself a thousand times: *You are a shopkeeper. You sell charms that do nothing. Whatever is dying on your step belongs to someone else.*

Then he opened the door, because seven years of careful living had not managed to kill the man he'd been before them, and that man had never once left a stranger bleeding on stone.

The courier was young — younger than Edmund, anyway, which put him somewhere south of thirty — with the lean, rope-muscled look of a man who earned his bread by outrunning other people's trouble. Trouble had caught him tonight. His left side was soaked black to the hip, his riding cloak stiff with it, and when Edmund hauled him inside by the collar and got him onto the floor of the back room among the packing crates, the man's eyes were already going glassy, tracking things that weren't there.

"Wardstone alley," the courier rasped. "They cut me off at the— doesn't matter. Doesn't matter now."

"You need a surgeon. There's one on Tallow Street who doesn't ask—"

"No surgeons." A hand shot out and clamped around Edmund's wrist with shocking strength, and Edmund felt it — felt the cold crawl up his arm, the specific cold of a wound made by a blade quenched in something worse than water. An inquisitor's steel. His stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept falling. "No surgeons. Listen to me, gatekeeper. I don't have time for you to pretend."

The word landed like a hammer through glass.

"I don't know what you—" Edmund began, and the courier laughed, a horrible wet sound, and pulled something from inside his coat.

It was a map. Folded into eighths, swollen with damp, and stained across half its surface with blood that was still, impossibly, moving — creeping along the ink lines like frost growing backward, filling in roads and rivers that hadn't been drawn yet. The courier pressed it into Edmund's hands and folded Edmund's fingers over it with both of his own, and his grip was fever-hot now, shaking.

"You're Dunmore," he said. "Gatekeeper of the underseal. Don't insult us both. I carried this from the coast on the strength of your name, so be worth it."

"Who gave it to you? Who sent—"

"The seal is *feeding.*" The courier's voice cracked on the word, and his eyes found Edmund's and held them with the terrible clarity of a man spending his last coins. "Do you understand me? Seven years they've told us it sleeps, that the Purge keeps everyone safe, that the mad king is locked away and quiet. It isn't sleeping. It's *eating.* Every mage they burn, every witch they drown, every gift they cut out of some frightened child — it goes somewhere, Dunmore. It goes *down.* And whatever it's feeding down there is wearing a crown by now."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"I'm just a charm-seller," Edmund said, but the words came out thin and gray, and the courier smiled at him — pityingly, almost kindly.

"Seven years," the courier said. "I know. My sister does the same thing. We all do the same thing." His grip was loosening now, strength draining out between Edmund's fingers like sand. "The map knows the way. Blood remembers. Get to the gate before the new moon, or there won't be anything left to save. Promise me you'll—"

Boots. Outside. Many of them, unhurried, the measured tread of men who owned every street they walked and wanted everyone to know it. And beneath the boots, faint as a struck tuning fork, the sound Edmund had spent seven years training himself to hear in his sleep: the high, thin whine of warded steel being drawn.

The courier heard it too. Something shifted in his face — fear burning away, and underneath it, exposed, a kind of grim relief, like a runner finally allowed to stop.

"They followed me," he said. "Good. That's good. Listen — back room, cellar grate, it opens on the drain-cut under Tallow Street. I saw it when you dragged me past. Go *now*, while they count my body and argue about whose kill it was. Inquisitors are vain. Vanity buys minutes."

"I'm not leaving you to die on my floor."

"You're leaving me to die buying you a head start. There's a difference, and it matters." He fumbled at his belt, produced a short knife, and set it beside him on the boards with the deliberate care of a man arranging his own altar. "Go. And Dunmore — the name on the map. When you find it. Don't be angry at me. I only carried it."

"What name? What are you talking about?"

But the courier's eyes had gone to the door, and whatever answer he might have given was already receding behind a wall of pain and purpose. "Cellar," he whispered. "Now. Please. Let me be worth something tonight."

Edmund stood in his own back room, in his own careful life, and understood with perfect clarity that whichever way he moved next, the life he'd built was over. Stay, and be taken with a dead courier and a bleeding map in his hands. Flee, and become exactly what the Purge said he was.

There was no choice in it at all. That was the worst part. There had never been any choice; the whole seven years had been a corridor with painted doors.

He blew out the lamp.

* * *

The cellar grate was where the courier said, rusted at one hinge, and it took Edmund three tries and skin off both palms to work it open wide enough for a grown man. Below it ran the drain-cut, a brick throat carrying the market's gray water down toward the river, stinking and echoing and utterly dark. He lowered himself into it feet-first, the map pressed flat against his chest beneath his shirt, and hung there for one last moment listening to the world he was abandoning.

The front door of his shop did not burst open. That was the courier's gift, and it was working: Edmund heard the inquisitors outside conducting their theater — the polite, carrying announcements, *Inquisition of the Crown, in the name of the king's peace, open in the Crown's name* — the ritual words that existed not to gain entry but to establish, for the record, that everything after them was lawful. Then the crack of the door frame. Then voices, sharp with surprise, and the courier's voice rising over them, loud and defiant and full of words Edmund couldn't make out but could guess — insults, accusations, maybe a false confession naming accomplices in districts far away, spending his dying breath to scatter the hunt.

Then light bloomed orange through the grate seams, and heat rolled down the stairwell — fire, thrown carelessly, because inquisitors did not investigate charm shops, they erased them — and Edmund let go of the ledge and dropped into the water.

It came to his waist, freezing, reeking. He caught himself against the brick and turned once to look back up at the rectangle of gray light where his cellar grate hung open, and beyond it, muffled by stone, the sounds of his shop burning and a young man shouting defiance at men who would not even remember his face.

*I'll finish it,* Edmund thought, not knowing yet what *it* was, promising anyway. *Whatever you carried, whoever you died for — I'll see it through.*

Then he turned downstream and walked into the dark, and the city closed over him like water.

* * *

He went deep, the way he had gone deep once before, in another life. Down through the drain-cuts and the old storm-works, past the bricked mouths of cellars whose owners had never come back from the Purge's first year, into the undercity's oldest veins where the brick changed to older brick, and the older brick to dressed stone, and the dressed stone bore marks that no mason alive would admit to knowing. The warding bell rode in his pocket, silent now, its glow banked to a coal-dim ember. Twice he heard movement ahead and diverted through junctions he remembered with his body if not his mind, and twice he waited in standing water with his heart slamming until the sounds resolved into rats, or pipes, or nothing.

The map, he did not look at. Not for hours. Some instinct older than sense told him that its light, if it gave light, should not be spent in a place with eyes.

He surfaced near midnight in a disused cistern beneath a collapsed tannery, a dry-walled vault the size of a chapel, and only then, sitting against the stones with his soaked clothes steaming faintly in the warmth of his own skin — a small magic, a survival trick, the first honest working he had done in seven years and it shamed him how good it felt — only then did he unfold the courier's map.

It was vellum, old, soft as cloth. By the light of a candle stub he'd carried since the shop — the last object he owned that had crossed his threshold twice — he spread it out across his knees and read it.

The western provinces. The mountains. And there, at the center of the sheet, rendered in ink so fine it must have been a needle-worked line, a mountain with its roots opened like a dissected flower, and beneath the mountain a chamber, and within the chamber a shape he knew better than his own heartbeat.

The sigil. The great gate-seal. His sigil — the pattern he had been taught at nineteen, sworn over at twenty, tended for three years with his master before the Purge came and his master went to the pyre and Edmund ran and buried his name under a shopkeeper's smile. Someone had drawn it here, perfectly, every interlocking line of it, from memory or from record. And threaded through those lines, following them like a hand tracing a scar, ran fresh ink. Fresh, and red, and moving even now, slow as honey, brightening as it traveled — and where it brightened, the sigil lines beneath it had begun to fray, the fine strokes dissolving like sugar in rain.

Feeding. The courier had been right. The seal was being eaten from the outside, or worn away from within, and the map itself was dying in sympathy with it.

His hands were shaking. He steadied them against his knees and made himself keep reading. Routes were marked — safe houses, a chain of them, running west through country he'd only ever seen on other maps. Marginal notes in a cramped, urgent hand. And near the bottom corner, in letters that seemed to have been written last, pressed hard enough nearly to tear the vellum, two words that stopped his breath:

*KING LIVES.*

Not *the king lives* in the way of loyalists' graffiti, the desperate slogan daubed on walls and scrubbed off by morning. The handwriting matched the route notes. The courier's hand, or his patron's. A statement of intelligence. A fact, recorded.

And below it, smaller still, almost apologetic, in ink that was already fading as he watched:

*Find the name. It was always meant for you, Edmund Dunmore. Forgive me for what it costs.*

He read it four times. The words did not change. The blood-red thread crawling through the sigil did not pause. Somewhere above him, Vellmark slept under a crown that ate its magicians, and somewhere in the ashes of Low Market, inquisitors were sifting his shop for evidence and finding nothing — because he had kept it clean, God, how he had kept it clean, seven years of nothing — and concluding instead that the gatekeeper had fled, which meant the gatekeeper was real, which meant the hunt had changed shape entirely. They would no longer be looking for a charm-seller who bent the law. They would be looking for the last keeper of the underseal.

Edmund sat in the dark of the cistern with the dying map across his knees and let the terror move through him, wave after wave, until it had washed everything loose. His stock. His ledgers. His name. Mistress Halloway's buttons. The Thursday boy. The small, warm, half-lived life he had built out of cowardice and called it wisdom. All of it gone in the space between one knock and the next, and the strange thing — the thing he would remember afterward — was that beneath the grief and the fear there was a note of something else, something he despised himself for feeling.

Relief. Terrible, guilty relief. As if a splint he'd forgotten he was wearing had finally been cut away.

He folded the map along its old creases. He pinched out the candle. And in the darkness, speaking aloud for the first time in days, in a voice roughened by smoke and river water, Edmund Dunmore made his second oath.

"I'm coming to the gate."

The words echoed in the cistern and died. But the map, tucked warm against his chest, pulsed once against his skin — faint, patient, and red as an open vein — as if it had heard, and approved, and was already counting down the nights to the new moon.

He needed passage. He needed someone who knew the western roads, and the border wards, and how to move unseen through a country gone mad. And there was exactly one person in Vellmark who had ever guarded royalty and lived to be disgraced for it.

Edmund rose, wrung out his sleeves, and started walking toward the docks, where the drinking dens opened early and closed never, and where a knight without a banner was rumored to drown her shame in anything stronger than river water.

Behind him, far beneath the sleeping city, something fed.
