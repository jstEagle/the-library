## 3. The Map That Bleeds

The undercity of Vellmark smelled of wet stone and old secrets.

Edmund had gone down through the coal chute behind the tannery, the one he'd found his second year in hiding, when a charm-buyer with too many questions had made him nervous enough to learn every back way out of Low Market. Seven years of careful living, and it came down to this: a man in his forties crawling through soot-black brickwork with blood drying on his sleeves, carrying nothing but the clothes he'd fled in and a dead man's map.

He'd left the lamps behind too. Inquisitors watched for light where light shouldn't be. So he sat in the dark beneath the Tanner's Reach cisterns, listening to water drip somewhere in the blackness, feeling for the map's edges with his fingertips because his eyes were useless here.

*Feeding,* the courier had said. *It's feeding.*

He turned those words over again as he had a hundred times since. The seal was not supposed to feed. The seal was supposed to be inert — a lock, a weight, a thing that simply *was*, like the mountain above it or the bedrock below. He had tended it for three years before the Purge, walking its perimeter each month with his instruments, recording the slow, geological patience of its decay. Nothing fed on a binding. A binding only ever gave.

Unless someone had changed what it bound.

His hands were shaking. He noticed it distantly, the way one notices rain through a window — it was happening to someone else's body. He pressed them flat against his knees until the tremor slowed.

Think. Think like a gatekeeper, not a shopkeeper.

The courier had died twenty feet from Edmund's back room door, had spent his last breath and the last of his running strength getting to *that door specifically*. And the inquisitors had come minutes behind, which meant they hadn't been following the courier at all. They'd been watching Edmund. For how long? Since the courier entered the city? Since the market? Since—

Since someone had been asking about gatekeepers.

The nervous customer's words from two days ago came back to him now, stripped of their small anxiousness and wearing armor instead. Someone asking about gatekeepers. Not sorcerers. Gatekeepers. There were perhaps four people alive who knew Edmund Dunmore had ever held that title, and he'd have sworn to any of them that the knowledge had died with the old order.

Apparently it hadn't. Apparently it had been waiting underground all this time, patient as the seal itself.

He needed light. He needed to see the map properly, and that meant risking a flame, and that meant — he laughed once, a dry sound with no humor in it — that meant being exactly the kind of man the Purge said he was. Hiding in a hole with forbidden things, whispering over candleflame. Let them find him like that. At least the caricature would be accurate.

There was a stub of tallow candle in the third cache along the cistern wall, wrapped in oilcloth where he'd stored it years ago against a night like this, though he'd told himself then there would never be such a night. He found it by touch. His flint sparked twice before it caught, and the small flame threw his shadow huge and wavering across the dripping stone, a giant hunched over a scrap of paper.

The map was smaller than he'd remembered from that terrible glimpse in the back room. A single sheet, folded eight times, soft as cloth from handling. The blood on it had dried to a brown so dark it was nearly black, spattered across one corner and streaked along a fold as if someone had gripped the paper hard while bleeding. The courier's blood. It had soaked through in places, and where it had soaked through, something strange had happened: the stains weren't just stains. Under the candlelight they moved. Slowly. The way ink moves when water touches it, except there was no water, and the movement had purpose — creeping always toward the center of the sheet, toward whatever lay written there.

Edmund's skin prickled. This wasn't a map that had been bled on. This was a map made *of* blood — blood-ink, the old scriptorium craft, writing that carried its author in it. Blood-ink messages could be sealed, warded, timed to reveal themselves. They could also be traced. Whoever read this page was reading the courier himself, what remained of him, and Edmund understood suddenly that he was holding more than instructions.

He was holding a corpse's last testament.

"Right," he said aloud, and his voice sounded obscene in the dark. "Let's hear it, then."

The surface of the map showed Vellmark's eastern provinces rendered in fine cartographer's hand — roads, rivers, the mountain spine called the Old Crown, and there, in a fold of granite north of the Ashfall province, a mark no ordinary map would carry: a circle bisected by a descending line. The sigil of the deep ward. The gate.

Edmund stared at it until his eyes burned. He knew that mark better than his own face. He had inked it a thousand times in practice exercises as an apprentice, had walked its full carved length in the mountain's roots, had run his fingers along grooves cut by masons working under direction of gatekeepers dead five hundred years. The last gate. After the others had been closed and collapsed on the orders of frightened kings, this one alone remained, and Edmund had been its final keeper — young, proud, certain that his vigilance mattered.

Then the Purge had come, and he'd learned how little his title weighed against a kingdom's fear.

But the map was doing more than showing geography. As he tilted it toward the flame, lines emerged between the printed roads — thin lines, drawn in something paler than the base ink, visible only at an angle. A route. Starting from a point in Vellmark itself — he traced it with a fingertip and felt his stomach drop, because the starting point was the Low Market district, was very nearly his own street — and winding east through the burned provinces, skirting Harrowgate, climbing into the Old Crown along ways that were marked not with road-symbols but with small dwarven runes he recognized from the gate's own maintenance records. Service tunnels. The deep ways. Someone had known about them. Someone had known enough to draw them from memory.

And along the route, at intervals, tiny annotations in that same pale script. He brought the candle close, close enough that the heat stung his knuckles.

*Ward-stone at the third milestone — cracked, do not touch.*
*Harrowgate council will bargain. Do not trust the blue door.*
*The salt cells hold what you need.*

Each line landed like a stone dropped into still water. Whoever wrote this hadn't just known the route. They'd known the obstacles. Known the players. This was intelligence gathered over years, distilled onto one sheet, and carried by a man who had run himself to death delivering it.

To me, Edmund thought. Delivered to me.

Why?

He almost didn't look at the center of the map. Some part of him, some animal and ancient part, understood that the center was where the moving blood was creeping, and that whatever waited there had been waiting for his eyes specifically. But dread was a luxury now. Dread was for men with something left to lose, and as of tonight he had lost the shop, the stall, the false name of his life, everything except the truth he'd been avoiding for seven years.

He flattened the map against his knee and looked.

At first it was only the sigil again, larger here, drawn with obsessive precision — every line of the deep ward reproduced in miniature, the great circle and the descending line and, around them, the interlocking runes of the binding itself. Edmund's breath went shallow. He had seen the true sigil only once, on the day of his investiture, when the old gatekeeper Aldric had uncovered the heart-chamber floor and let him look upon the full design. No apprentice was permitted to copy it. No record of it existed outside the mountain. Yet here it was, complete, accurate down to the smallest flourishes, and rendered in blood that still breathed.

Beneath it, in a hand that shook — from injury, or fear, or the effort of pushing the words out through dying blood-ink — a single line of text:

*The king still lives. The seal feeds on him. Only a gatekeeper can unbind what gatekeepers made. Find Edmund Dunmore.*

His name. His actual name, written in a stranger's blood, in the dark under Vellmark, and around it the blood-marks crept and pooled like something listening for his reaction.

Edmund sat very still for a long time.

The king still lives. Three words that unmade seven years of carefully constructed grief. Because everyone knew the mad king was dead — had died in the night of fire that began the Purge, or so the proclamations said, and the realm had passed to the regent and then quietly, inevitably, to the crown that never left the regent's head. Everyone knew. Everyone mourned or didn't and got on with surviving.

But if the king lived — if he lived *beneath the seal*, if the binding Edmund had guarded was not a cage built around a monster but a shroud wrapped around a drowning man — then the seal was not protecting the realm from the king. It was protecting *it* from being discovered. Feeding on him, the courier had said. Growing stronger as it consumed. Consuming what?

And then the answer arrived, cold and complete, the way the worst answers do: consuming the mages. The Purge hadn't been fear. The Purge had been harvest. Every sorcerer dragged into the Crown's service, every hedge-witch burned or bound or drained — they hadn't been destroyed. They'd been *used*. Something wearing the throne had spent seven years eating the magic of a nation, one confiscated soul at a time, and the seal — his seal, the gate he had abandoned — was the throat down which it all went.

Edmund put the candle down because his hands were no longer steady enough to hold both it and the truth.

He thought of the charms he'd sold that morning. Love-lures woven from nothing, luck-knots with no luck in them, the harmless theater of a man pretending his gift had died with his old life. Seven years of half-life justified by one sentence repeated like a prayer: *the seal holds without me; my duty is to stay hidden; survival is vigilance.* And all the while, underneath the mountain, under his very city, the thing he should have been watching had been awake and hungry and growing fat.

Aldric would have spat at him. The old man had died within a year of the Purge — of illness, people said, though Edmund had always suspected a gentler word for what the inquisitors did to captured gatekeepers — and he had died still believing his student would keep faith with the stone. Keep faith with the stone. Edmund had kept faith with a market stall instead.

"I didn't know," he said to the dark, and heard how thin it sounded. The blood on the map seemed to pulse once, gently, like a heartbeat forgiving him, which was somehow worse than accusation.

Because here was the thing that terrified him more than the inquisitors, more than the thing in the crown: some part of him *had* known. Had felt it the way a dog feels weather coming — the wrongness in the wards' resonance during his last inspection, the faint hunger in the sigil's hum that he'd filed away as fatigue, as imagination, as anything rather than the truth. Gatekeepers were taught to trust the stone over their own senses, and he had trusted neither, and so he had hidden, and the hiding had been comfortable, and comfort had made him blind.

The candle guttered. Edmund realized he'd been sitting motionless long enough for the wax to pool, and that somewhere above him dawn must be approaching, and that every hour he spent grieving his old life was an hour the enemy spent consolidating.

Resolve did not arrive like lightning. It arrived like the tide — slowly, in increments, each thought pulling him a little further from the shore of the man he'd been. He catalogued what he had: a route east. Knowledge of the deep ways, at least the ones near the gate. A title that apparently still meant something to someone. And a name he needed — several names, actually, if the map's annotations were honest. A knight who had guarded the royal family before the fall; Rowenna Vale, disgraced, drinking herself blind somewhere near the docks, if the gossip that reached even a charm-seller's ears was true. The annotation *Harrowgate council will bargain* told him institutions were dead ends; he would need people instead, people with skills and grievances, which in the realm as it now stood described nearly everyone.

He catalogued what he lacked, and stopped cataloguing quickly, because the list was long enough to be funny.

First, though — first there was a debt. The courier had died on his floor buying him minutes, and Edmund had fled without even learning his name. He smoothed the map flat and studied the bloodstains again, really studying them now, and saw what grief had kept him from seeing: initials worked into the corner sigil in the blood-script, deliberate, a signature. M.K. Mara Kess. And beside it, smaller, the courier's own mark — J.K. — and a final line of text that surfaced only when he angled the flame just so:

*My brother believed in this. Finish it, and tell my sister why.*

So there had been two of them. Brother and sister, couriers both, carrying the truth of the age in their veins and their family's stubbornness besides. One dead in a charm-seller's back room. One out there somewhere, not yet knowing she was an only child, and Edmund would have to be the one to tell her. Would have to stand in front of a stranger's grief and say *your brother chose me, and I ran, and I'm sorry* — and then ask her to help him.

Dread settled over him like a coat of lead, familiar now, almost companionable. But beneath it, in the place where fear and duty had always fought for control of him, something new had taken root: a hard, quiet kernel that felt less like courage than like arithmetic. Seven years of life bought with silence, and the bill had finally come due. The interest was a kingdom.

He folded the map along its worn creases and tucked it inside his shirt, against his skin, where it lay warm and faintly pulsing, a second heart he hadn't asked for. Then he pinched out the candle, took up his flint, and stood in the absolute dark, letting his eyes adjust, letting the undercity's silence press against him like water.

Somewhere above, Vellmark was waking. Somewhere in it, inquisitors were kicking in doors and asking after gatekeepers. Somewhere east, past the ash provinces, a king who did not know he was a king lay dreaming under a seal that ate him by inches.

And somewhere near the docks, a disgraced knight was drinking away a shame Edmund was about to make her relive.

He turned toward the tunnel that climbed toward the river district and began to walk, one hand trailing the damp wall, the map's warmth beating against his chest like a countdown.

* * *

The climb out took him past the cistern galleries and up through a disused well-shaft whose bricks he'd mortared loose himself, years ago, against precisely this kind of night. He emerged before dawn in the shell of a burned granary near Tanner's Reach, and paused there in the rafters' black skeleton, looking out across the sleeping city he had loved in the way a man loves a mask that fits.

Vellmark under its last stars. Spires and slate roofs, the distant glint of the river, and above it all — invisible, deniable — the mountain where the gate slept and the king dreamed and the hunger fed. He had walked these streets for seven years as nobody. The nobody had died last night on his back room floor alongside a courier whose name he'd finally read in blood.

What remained was a gatekeeper with no gate, a mage with no license to exist, and a debt payable only in a currency he'd sworn off spending: the truth, spoken aloud, in daylight, to strangers.

Rowenna Vale first. Then a forger, if the map's cipher demanded one. Then a sister who would hate him on sight, and rightly.

Edmund pulled his collar up, became a gray man in a gray city, and descended into the streets as the bells rang matins — walking, for the first time in seven years, toward the thing he feared instead of away from it.
