## 4. The Knight Without a Banner

The Salt and Anchor did not so much serve ale as surrender it, sullenly, to anyone stubborn enough to keep asking. It sat at the crooked end of Harborside Row where the cobblestones gave up on being level and the air tasted permanently of tar, old rope, and fish left too long in the sun. The kind of place, Rowenna Vale had learned, where a person could drink herself blind without anyone troubling to remember her name — or worse, remembering it.

She kept her back to the wall out of seven years of habit and her hood up out of five years of shame, though by now the two had grown together like roots of the same tree. Her sword hung behind the bar, wrapped in oilcloth. The barkeep held it as surety against her tab, which was a joke between them that had stopped being funny some years ago: no one would steal Ser Rowenna Vale's blade. Who would want it? A knight's sword without a knight, a banner without a house, an oath without an oathkeeper. She had been all three for long enough that the shapes felt like her own body.

"Vale." The barkeep set down a cup of something brown and honest. "You've got a shadow."

"I know," she said. "Been watching him nurse that same cider for an hour. Either he's waiting for me to pass out or he's the world's politest cutpurse."

"He came in asking after you. By name."

Rowenna's hand paused on her cup.

By name. Not *the big woman who drinks at the Anchor*, not *the one who used to be somebody*. By name. There were perhaps six people left alive who knew the name Rowenna Vale meant anything, and she could account for four of them with certainty — two dead, one exiled, one wearing a crown that had gone wrong in the head somehow, if the rumors that drifted into port were worth anything, which they usually weren't.

She turned slowly and looked at the man in the corner booth.

He was trying very hard not to look like he was hiding, and failing in the way that only honest people fail. Shopkeeper's hands, ink-stained at the cuffs. A peddler's coat of decent wool worn soft at the elbows, buttoned wrong — one button off from the bottom up, the small error of a man who'd dressed in a hurry and in the dark. He was maybe thirty-five, lean in the way of someone who'd forgotten to eat well for years rather than someone who couldn't afford to. And beneath the hood he kept tugging forward, there was something about his eyes —

No. Not recognition. But something adjacent to it, a shape she almost remembered, like a word in a language she'd stopped speaking.

Rowenna picked up her cup, crossed the room, and sat down across from him without being invited. Up close he smelled faintly of dried lavender and copper — charm-seller's stock and something else underneath, something that made the fine hairs on her arms lift the way they did before a storm.

"You've got nerve," she said, "walking into a dockside den and asking for me by name. Half the men in here would sell you to the harbor watch for the price of a meal, and the other half would sell you to worse."

"I know." His voice was quiet but it didn't waver. "I didn't have anywhere else to go. I've been told — I've worked out — that you're the last person in Vellmark who might help me, and the only one who might be able to read this."

He set something on the table between them.

It was a map. Folded small, stained dark at one corner in a way that made her stomach clench before her mind caught up — blood, old blood, blood soaked through paper. She should have looked away. She had looked away from enough blood these past seven years, trained herself to it like a man training himself not to flinch at loud sounds.

She didn't look away.

Instead she looked at the corner of the map itself, at the seal pressed into the vellum in faded wax that wasn't wax at all — she saw that now, up close, saw the fine grain of it, the way it caught the tavern's lamplight with a depth no tallow ever had. A circle broken by seven points. A tower within the circle, and beneath the tower, a gate.

The room went very quiet, or perhaps that was just inside her skull.

"Where," Rowenna said, and her voice came out in a register she hadn't used since she'd worn armor every day, low and flat and meant to carry across a battlefield, "did you get this?"

"A dying man put it in my hands. He'd been running from the Crown's inquisitors and didn't make it far enough." The man — charm-seller, she reminded herself, whatever else he was — spread his hands flat on the table, deliberately, showing they were empty, showing they were steady. "My name is Edmund Dunmore. I keep — kept — a stall in Low Market. And until last night I believed I was nobody worth hunting."

"And you thought I'd help. A disgraced knight. You know what they say about me."

"I know what they say about everyone," Edmund said. "I've spent seven years listening to what people say about people. Most of it is wrong, and nearly all of it is designed to make the speaker feel safer."

That startled a breath of laughter out of her, short and rough as a cough. Seven years. Everyone she met seemed to live inside those same seven years, that same single night, and none of them had the decency to say it plainly. This shopkeeper with his misbuttoned coat said it like arithmetic.

*Seven years.* The number sat between them, and she understood suddenly that he hadn't chosen it as a figure of speech.

"The seal on this map," she said, tapping the edge of the broken circle with one finger, careful not to touch the center, some instinct older than memory telling her not to. "Do you know what it is?"

"No. That's why I'm here."

"It's the royal guard's seal. The inner company. The one that answered to the king himself and no one else." She heard her own voice do something strange on the word *king*, bend around it like water around a stone. "There were forty-one of us who carried it. I could draw all forty-one faces for you right now, awake or drunk, doesn't matter. Twenty-nine are dead. The rest scattered when — "

She stopped. The tavern noise rushed back in around the silence she'd made, the clatter of cups, a dice game arguing with itself, the sea against the pilings below the window.

"When the mad king was bound," Edmund finished quietly. "When the Purge began."

"That's the story."

"You don't believe the story."

Rowenna leaned back. Her cup sat untouched; she'd stopped drinking the moment the map touched wood, some part of her insisting on a clear head whether she liked it or not. Outside, a gull screamed and was answered by others, the harbor's endless quarrel.

"I stood guard outside the king's chambers for three years," she said. "I watched him walk those halls. Mad? He was tired. Tired and sad and carrying more than any one man should, and he talked sometimes to people who weren't there — or so we thought. But mad the way they said? Raving, dangerous, burning counselors in their beds?" She shook her head slowly. "The night they came for him, I was on rotation at the river gate. Someone arranged that. I've spent seven years being glad I wasn't in the hall that night, and seven years knowing that being glad was the most shameful thing about me."

Edmund said nothing. He let the silence sit there, open-handed, the way good listeners do, and she found herself talking more than she had in years precisely because of it.

"They say I abandoned my post. That's the official record — Ser Rowenna Vale, absent without leave on the night of the king's binding, stripped of banner and title, name struck from the rolls. What actually happened is that I ran. When the inquisitors started hunting anyone who'd been close to the crown, anyone who might have known the king's secrets, I took off my armor and put on this coat and came down here to rot where nobody looks." She turned her cup in a slow circle on the scarred wood. "So if you're looking for a hero, Dunmore, you've wasted a trip. What you've found is a coward with a sword."

"I'm not looking for a hero." He slid the map across the table toward her, and the lamplight moved in the strange deep grain of that ancient seal. "I'm looking for someone who can read this. And I think — I think you already know you can."

She could. That was the terrible thing. Even before she pulled the map fully open, even before she saw the route inked in careful lines running east from Vellmark through the ash country toward the mountains, she could feel the seal's geometry settling into her mind like a key finding its lock. The inner company had drilled on that sigil for hours a day. Draw it blindfolded. Draw it with your off hand. Know every line of it, because you are the door it stands behind.

She had believed, all these years, that she'd forgotten.

She hadn't forgotten anything. That was her curse: she forgot nothing, and so she remembered exactly what she'd run from.

"This route," she said, tracing it without touching it. "It goes to the old gate. Under Mount Cael." She looked up at him sharply. "You know what's under Mount Cael."

"I know what I guard," Edmund said. "Or what I thought I was guarding. A ward. One of many, I assumed. A gatekeeper's post, inherited from my mother, hers from hers. We keep the seals maintained. We don't ask what they hold." His jaw tightened. "The man who died giving me this map told me the seal was feeding. Those were his words. Feeding. And everything I've learned since says the thing the Purge built its whole rotten law upon — the mad king they bound — is still alive under that mountain, and the binding isn't protecting the realm from him. It never was."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly. Or perhaps that was the ale she hadn't drunk, catching up with her out of pure spite.

"If that's true," Rowenna said slowly, "then the Purge —"

"Wasn't justice and wasn't madness. It was maintenance. Hunting mages, burning texts, striking names from the rolls —" he nodded at her, gentle and merciless at once, "— it was pruning. Clearing away anyone who might understand the seal well enough to question it. Anyone who might undo it."

*Name struck from the rolls.* She heard her own disgrace reframed in a single sentence, and it was not a comfort; it was a horror. She had spent seven years believing her cowardice had saved her life at the cost of her honor. Now it appeared her cowardice had merely delayed her, and the honor had never been lost — only misplaced, waiting in a dockside den with an oilcloth over it.

"Why me?" she asked. Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "There are other swords in this city. Mercenaries who'd take your coin and ask nothing."

"Because you recognized the seal before I finished setting it down. Because your name is on the same list mine is, whether you know it or not — the list of people who were close to the truth when the lie went up. And because—" Edmund hesitated, and for the first time he looked less than certain, young in some way his face didn't match. "Because the courier chose me, a charm-seller, to carry this. Someone decided that hiding was no longer the point. I think the same must be true of you."

Long silence. The dice game ended in shouting and a chair scraping. Somewhere below, a ship's bell rang the hour, muffled by fog coming off the water.

Rowenna thought about her sword behind the bar. Seven years in oilcloth. She had told herself she kept it there because she had nowhere else to put it, but that was a lie, and she had known it was a lie, and tonight a stranger with bloodstained maps and wrong-seeming eyes had laid the truth out on the table next to it: she had kept it there the way a man keeps a letter unopened. Because opening it would mean the old life was real. Because as long as it stayed wrapped, she could go on being nobody, and nobody couldn't fail again.

"What's your plan?" she asked finally. "Walk to Mount Cael? The ash provinces are crawling with Crown patrols, the free cities are one by one folding, and every inquisitor in the realm would trade their own mothers for whatever that map leads to."

"My plan was to find you," Edmund said. "It was the only part of the plan I had."

A laugh escaped her before she could stop it — a real one this time, startling her with its sound, rusty as an unused hinge. Then she stood, walked to the bar, and held out her hand to the barkeep, who looked at the hand, then at her face, then shrugged and brought the sword out from under the counter, oilcloth and all.

She unwrapped it right there. The blade came out grey and true, oiled well enough, edges holding. Forty-one swords had been forged for the inner company, each marked along the ricasso with the broken-circle seal, and hers was still hers, still marked, the sigil small and sharp as the day the smith struck it.

"Well," she said, more to the sword than to Edmund, "you're heavier than I remember. Or I'm weaker. Probably both."

She bound it across her back with the strap she'd kept, God help her, all these years — cleaned monthly, worn daily under her coat, a weight she'd pretended wasn't there. Some things you cannot stop carrying no matter how thoroughly you abandon them.

"Terms," she said, sitting back down. "I'll guide you to the mountain. I know approaches to Cael that aren't on any Crown map, because I helped survey them for the king's engineers, back when the king had engineers instead of inquisitors. But I don't take coin."

Edmund frowned. "Then what —"

"Truth." She laid her finger on the seal, and this time she did touch it, and it felt warm under her skin, like something sleeping but not dead. "Every step of the way. Whatever you learn, whatever that map shows, whatever's really under the mountain — I hear it first, complete, no protecting me from the ugly parts. I spent seven years not knowing, and I'm done with it. If I'm going to die on a mountain for a dead man's map, I'll die knowing exactly what for. That's my price."

Edmund studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw seemed to satisfy him.

"For truth," he agreed, and then, quieter: "And for what it's worth, Ser Vale — I don't think you're a coward. I think you're the only kind of survivor there is: the kind that's ashamed of it. Cowards sleep fine."

"Don't," she said roughly. "Don't make me into something. I've been nothing for seven years and it's the only peace I've had."

But her hand, she noticed, was still resting near the sword's hilt, and had been since she'd unwrapped it, and showed no intention of moving.

They settled the practicalities in whispers — leaving before dawn, avoiding the East Gate where the inquisitors kept their heaviest watch, buying passage on a coal barge as far as Graymouth. Rowenna paid their fare with coins counted out one by one from a purse lighter than it had been in years, and found she minded the emptiness far less than she'd expected. Coin was for the life she'd been living. This was the other one.

She was tightening the sword strap across her shoulders when Edmund went still beside her, his gaze fixed on the tavern's front window, on the lamplit street beyond it.

"Rowenna," he said, very softly. "The man in the grey cloak. Across the street. He's been there since before I came in."

She looked without turning her head, using the smoky reflection in the window glass. Grey cloak, hood, standing in the mouth of the alley opposite with the unnatural patience of someone being paid by the hour. And as she watched, the man raised one hand slightly, and down the row another figure peeled away from a doorway and began walking, unhurried, toward the Salt and Anchor's door.

"Inquisitors?" Edmund breathed.

"Worse or better, no way to tell from here." Rowenna was already moving, years of drill waking in her muscles like dogs hearing a bell. She flipped the table onto its side with one heave, shoved Edmund toward the kitchen passage she'd watched a hundred smugglers use to dodge the harbor watch. "Out the back, down the fish stairs, don't stop for anything. Move *now*."

"And you?"

She drew the sword. Seven years of rust, and the sound it made coming free of the scabbard was still the cleanest thing she'd ever heard.

"I'm the one with the banner," said Ser Rowenna Vale, and went to meet them.
