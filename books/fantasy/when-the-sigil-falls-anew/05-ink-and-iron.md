## 5. Ink and Iron

Tam Weller learned to read backwards before he learned to read forwards.

That was the first thing Master Corvin had taught him, six years ago, when he was a skinny orphan with ink-stained fingers and no name anyone remembered: *a forger's eye sees the world in reverse, boy. The letter is nothing. The mirror of the letter is everything.* So Tam had spent his childhood hunched over a copying desk in the back of Corvin's print shop, tracing letters from right to left until his hand could produce a creditable royal warrant, a merchant's bill of lading, or — if the coin was good enough and the risk high enough — pages that had never been licensed by the Crown at all.

The forbidden texts were where the real money lived. And the real danger.

He was working on one now. It was nearly midnight, and the print shop smelled of tallow smoke and iron-gall ink, and Tam was three-quarters through a fair copy of something called *The Ledger of Sealed Names* when the bell above the street door gave its single soft chime.

Not the knock. The bell.

Corvin's rules were simple, and Tam knew them the way he knew his own heartbeat: after dark, customers used the knock — two short, one long. The bell meant one thing only. Someone who didn't care about rules.

"Blow out the candle," Corvin said quietly, already moving, his heavy frame surprisingly quick as he slid the *Ledger* beneath a loose floorboard and kicked a stack of legitimate psalters across the desk. "Back room. Now."

Tam pinched the wick and slipped into the darkness behind the press, heart hammering. Through the crack between the door and its frame he watched Corvin unbar the front door with the unhurried ease of a man who had done nothing wrong in his life, and let in the cold, and let in them.

Three figures in grey. Inquisitors of the Purge. Their lanterns threw long shadows up the walls, and the lead one — a woman with a voice like dry paper — held up a folded paper.

"Master Aldous Corvin. Printer of Vellmark, Girdler's Row. You are licensed for scriptural and mercantile work only." She unfolded the paper. Even upside down, even in bad light, Tam's mirror-trained eye read it in an instant: a warrant. "We have information that you have been producing heretical material. We are authorized to search your premises."

"Of course," Corvin said, and his voice was smooth as fresh vellum, but Tam had worked beside the man for six years and he heard the hairline fracture underneath it. "Search whatever you like, Reverend Mother. I print psalms and price lists. If there's anything else here, someone put it here to ruin me."

The search took two hours. They pulled every book from every shelf. They ran their fingers along the underside of the worktables. One of them — young, jumpy, his hands too big for his gloves — came within a handspan of the loose floorboard, and Tam, pressed against the cold wall of the back room with his knees cramping, stopped breathing entirely.

In the end, they found nothing. But as they left, the woman paused in the doorway and looked back at the shop with an expression Tam couldn't name — not suspicion exactly. Patience.

"We'll return, Master Corvin," she said. "Someone always talks, eventually."

The door closed. The bar slid back into place. And then Corvin's voice came through the dark, low and shaking: "Boy. Out here."

Tam emerged. His master stood in the middle of the wrecked shop, lantern relit, and his face was grey as the inquisitors' coats.

"They were looking for something specific," Corvin said. "Not heresy generally. A document." He looked at Tam, really looked, the way he did when he was deciding whether to take a job. "There's a man coming tomorrow. Before dawn. He needs a cipher broken — a map. He'll pay enough to buy us both passage out of this city if it comes to that. And you're the one who'll do the work."

"Why me?"

"Because my eyes are fifty-four years old and yours are sixteen." Corvin sat down heavily on the copying stool. "And because if this goes badly, they'll be looking for an old printer and a young apprentice. An old printer can hide easier than you'd think." He held Tam's gaze. "I'm not going to lie to you, Tam. This is the kind of work men hang for. Say no, and we go back to psalms, and we hope tonight was the worst of it."

Tam thought about the floorboard. About the *Ledger of Sealed Names*, which he had copied line by line without understanding a word of it, names in columns, names crossed out, names circled in red. He thought about the bread money. About the way the city had changed these seven years, the way people lowered their voices around words like *mage* and *gate*, the way the grey coats walked the streets in pairs now, always in pairs.

He thought about how tired he was of being afraid in a way that had become so normal he'd stopped noticing it.

"I'll do the work," Tam said.

* * *

The stranger arrived half an hour before dawn, and he was nothing like what Tam had expected.

He was maybe thirty, with the worn, careful look of a man who had spent years making himself invisible, and he carried himself like a shopkeeper — apologetic, hunched, hands tucked into his sleeves. But his eyes moved over the print shop the way Tam's own eyes moved over a forged signature: reading it, checking its weight, finding the places where it might fail.

"This is Edmund," Corvin said. "Edmund, the boy."

"Tam," Tam said, and hated how young his voice sounded.

Edmund Dunmore — Corvin had given him the name the night before, whispered over the last of the cooking fire — laid the map on the worktable, and Tam forgot to breathe again.

It was beautiful. It was wrong. It was both at once, and every instinct he had stood up and shouted.

The map itself showed the eastern reaches of the kingdom in old survey style, mountains rendered as writhing serpents' backs, the rivers silvered with some kind of mineral wash. But over the geography lay lines — a second map drawn inside the first, a route marked in a cipher that wasn't written so much as *woven*, each symbol looping through the next like thread through cloth. And the whole thing was stained with blood, dried to a brown so dark it was nearly black, and where the blood touched the cipher-lines, the lines seemed to move. Faintly. Like something under ice.

"What is it?" Tam asked, and then felt stupid, because you weren't supposed to ask questions about the work.

"It's a map to a place that isn't supposed to exist," Edmund said quietly. "And the writing on it is older than the kingdom. Can you read it?"

Tam bent close. He traced the loops with his eyes, left to right, right to left, mirrored and reversed, and slowly the structure began to open to him the way all ciphers eventually did — not as language but as pattern, and patterns were just letters that hadn't confessed yet.

"It's substitutional," he murmured, mostly to himself. "But it's keyed to the geography. See — this character changes shape depending on which river it sits next to. It's not just a code, it's... anchored. To the land itself." He looked up. "I can break it. Not tonight. Maybe not in a week. But I can break it."

Something shifted in Edmund's face — relief and fear tangled together, like a man who has been told the surgery will work and that it will hurt beyond bearing.

"Then we don't have much time," Edmund said. "Can you work here?"

"He works here," Corvin said sharply. "Nobody else knows he exists—"

The knock came. Two short, one long.

Everyone froze. Corvin crossed the room in three strides and put his eye to the peephole, and Tam saw the blood leave his master's face by degrees, like water draining.

"It's Jory," Corvin breathed. "My night man. He's early—" Then, louder, through the wood: "Why aren't you at the market stall, you useless—"

The door exploded inward.

Later, Tam would try to remember the sequence of it and never quite manage. There was the splintering of the door, and Corvin staggering backward, and grey coats flooding in — four of them, five — and the dry-paper voice of the woman from the night before saying, *I told you someone always talks,* and Edmund's hand closing hard around Tam's wrist, dragging him toward the back, toward the cellar hatch that Tam had never been allowed to use because *that's for emergencies, boy, and emergencies mean I'm dead.*

"Master—" Tam twisted around. Corvin was on his knees, an inquisitor's boot on his shoulder, and the old man wasn't struggling. That was the worst part. He wasn't struggling at all. He was looking straight at Tam across the chaos of his ruined shop, and his expression was calm, almost gentle, and he shook his head once. *Go.*

"Your master's been producing heresy for years," the woman was saying, conversational, as her men began pulling books from shelves again. "We found a witness. Funny thing about witnesses — everyone has a price, and most prices are lower than you'd think. Where is the map, Master Corvin? Where is the gatekeeper?"

*Gatekeeper.* The word hit Tam like cold water, and he understood, in one vertiginous instant, that this had never been about psalms or warrants or Corvin's sins. They had come for Edmund. Corvin was just the door they were breaking to reach him.

Edmund pried up the hatch. Below was blackness and the smell of wet stone. "Down," he whispered. "Now. Don't make a sound. Don't stop until the tunnel ends at the culvert grate."

"But Master Corvin—"

"Is buying us the only chance either of us has," Edmund said, and his voice cracked on it, and Tam would remember that crack for the rest of his life. "I am sorry. Down."

Tam went down.

The hatch closed above him, and the world became darkness and the sound of his own pulse. Above, muffled through the boards, he heard the shop being torn apart, heard Corvin's voice — steady, unhurried, lying magnificently: *"Map? Boy? Reverend Mother, the boy copies psalms and steals my wine, I promise you he's hiding neither"* — and Tam pressed his hands over his ears and crawled.

The tunnel was narrow, brick-lined, running with cold water at the bottom. He crawled through it in absolute blackness, elbows and knees screaming, following the faint draft of moving air, and the whole time the guilt walked beside him like a third person. *You left him. You crawled away and left him on his knees. Six years. He taught you everything. He shook his head and you obeyed him and you'll never know if that was loyalty or cowardice.*

He reached the culvert grate at last, shouldered it open, and came out into the pre-dawn grey of the drainage flats east of Girdler's Row, filthy, bleeding at both elbows, shaking so hard he could barely stand.

Edmund was already there, waiting by the culvert mouth, and when Tam looked at him, the man's face was terrible — not cold, but full of a grief so controlled it was worse than sobbing.

"We can't go back," Edmund said simply. "If we go back, we're caught, and the map dies with us, and whatever's coming keeps coming. Corvin knew that. That's why he told you to run." He held out a hand. "I'm sorry, Tam. I am so sorry. But right now you and I are the only ones carrying this, and we have to keep walking."

Tam looked back at the city. Girdler's Row was somewhere beyond those rooftops, and somewhere on Girdler's Row an old man was being questioned by people who always got answers eventually, and there was nothing in the world Tam could do about it except the one thing Corvin had bought with his calm, his shop, and probably his life: finish the work.

He took Edmund's hand. He stood. His legs barely held him.

"Then teach me everything," he said, and his voice broke too, and he didn't care. "Everything about the map. Everything about why they're hunting you. Because I'm not doing this just to run anymore. I'm doing it because he chose this, and I'm choosing it too. Understand?"

For the first time, Edmund Dunmore smiled — small, exhausted, real.

"I understand," he said. "Come on, then. There's a knight I need you to meet, and she drinks somewhere worse than a sewer."

They walked east along the drainage flats as the sun came up over Vellmark — a thin, watery sunrise the color of diluted ink — and Tam Weller, forger's apprentice, copier of psalms and thief of his master's wine, walked out of the only life he had ever known and into the one that had been waiting for him all along. Behind him the city slept, and somewhere in it, in a ruined shop on Girdler's Row, the inquisitors were asking their patient questions.

He would break the cipher. He would learn what the map said. And someday — he swore it to the sunrise, to the black water, to the old man with the gentle shake of his head — someday he would come back down Girdler's Row, and someone would answer for this.

Behind them, faint as a whisper, a church bell tolled the hour. Tam counted the strokes without meaning to.

Six. The same hour his master had always said the day's honest work began.

He wiped his face with his sleeve, and kept walking.
