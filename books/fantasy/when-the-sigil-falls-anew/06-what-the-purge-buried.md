## 6. What the Purge Buried

The fire had burned down to embers by the time Rowenna finally spoke, and she was glad of it. Embers gave a man leave to look at anything but the person he was talking to. She stared into them as though they might hold the words she'd been swallowing for seven years, and Edmund sat across from her with the bloodstained map spread between them on the floor of the ruined mill, and Tam sat curled against a fallen beam with his knees drawn up like a boy pretending not to listen, and neither of them rushed her.

She had been drinking since sundown — not from the dockside den's rotgut but from the honest bottle she'd carried out of Vellmark in her saddlebag, saved for an occasion she'd told herself would never come. The occasion had come. It wore a gatekeeper's face and asked questions she couldn't answer without telling him everything.

"You knew my father," Edmund said. Not for the first time. "You said so at the docks. You said you recognized the seal on this map because you'd seen it on royal correspondence." His voice was careful, the way a man's voice is careful when he's carrying something breakable. "So tell me what I'm carrying, Ser Vale. Tell me why a courier bled out in my back room to put this in my hands."

Rowenna turned the bottle in her hands. The glass was warm where her palm had been.

"I was sworn to the royal household when I was nineteen," she said. "Shield of the Inner Court. Do you know what that means, gatekeeper? It means I stood three paces behind the king's left shoulder while he held audience. Three paces, every day, for six years. Close enough to see the way his eyes moved when he read petitions. Close enough to know his moods better than his queen did." She took a breath. "Aldric Vane was not a madman."

Edmund went very still.

"They called him that afterward. *The Mad King.* They needed a word for it, and madness was the one that stuck, because madness explains everything and forgives everyone. Madness meant no one had to ask harder questions." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only an old bitterness worn smooth as river stone. "Aldric Vane was the sharpest mind I ever served. He read four languages. He settled a grain dispute between two duchies in an afternoon that should have taken a season of lawyers. And he was kind — that's the part no one tells you. Kind to servants, kind to petitioners, kind to a green knight who'd been given armor too big for her because her father's name bought it and nothing else."

"What happened?" Tam asked quietly. He wasn't pretending anymore. His dark eyes were wide in the ember-light, and Rowenna thought: *he's sixteen, and I'm about to make him older.*

"The winter before the binding," she said, "the king changed."

* * *

She made herself say it plainly, because plainness was all that kept it from being a story, and if it became a story then it could be dismissed, and she had spent seven years watching people dismiss it.

"It started small. He began forgetting things — not like an old man forgets. A king can hide that. No. He forgot *wrong*. He'd finish a sentence and look startled, as if someone else had said the words through his mouth. He stopped eating at table. He said the food tasted of ash. Then one night I was on the door of his study, and I heard him talking to himself, except —" Her jaw tightened. "Except the voice answering him was *his own voice*, pitched slightly wrong. Too even. Like a man reading words off a page he'd memorized but never understood. I looked through the keyhole, and Aldric Vane was standing in the middle of his own study, arms at his sides, and he was crying, and his mouth was smiling."

Tam made a small sound and pulled his cloak tighter.

"I reported it. To the Lord Protector, to the Queen's circle, to anyone with rank enough to matter. Most of them didn't believe me. The ones who believed me were more frightened than I was, because some of them had seen it too — the wrong smiles, the hours the king went missing from his own schedule and came back with dust on his boots he couldn't account for. There was a court mage then. Maren Solis. She did the working none of us understood — she looked into the king's shadow. That's the phrase she used. *Looked into his shadow.* What she found, she wouldn't say aloud. She wrote it down once, showed it to the Lord Protector and the High Justiciar and the captain of my order, and then she burned the page in front of us and said, 'It has eaten his shadow. It wears him from inside. We cannot kill it without killing him, and we cannot cut it out, because it has grown roots into everything he is.'"

Edmund's hand had drifted, without his seeming to notice, to rest flat against the map. Against his own name written in a dead courier's blood.

"So they bound him," Rowenna said. "That's what the binding was. Not a cage for a monster under a mountain. A harness. A seal laid over the thing wearing the king, to hold it still while they searched for a way to cut it free without killing him. The old gate — your gate, Dunmore — wasn't built to keep something *in*. It was built to keep the binding *anchored*. Every ward-stone, every line of the sigil, tied back to the crown itself. As long as the seal held, the thing in the king could not act. It could only wait, and wear its coat, and smile at nothing."

"And the king himself?" Edmund's voice came out rough. "Where was Aldric Vane, underneath?"

"Drowning," Rowenna said simply. "Maren Solis believed he was still there. Buried deep, but alive — she said the binding worked both ways. It held the shadow-thing still, and it held a thread of the true king safe beneath it, like a coal banked under ash. That was the whole hope of it. One day the sigil could be unwound carefully, by the right hands, and the king would wake up seven years or seventy years later, and the thing wearing him would be unmade." She looked up, met Edmund's eyes across the embers. "The right hands. That's what the contract says. That's why the courier died getting that map to you."

The mill creaked around them. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the dead orchard, dragging branches across the ground like men crawling.

"Then the Purge," Edmund said slowly, "wasn't about sorcery at all."

"The Purge was about *you*," Rowenna said. "About everyone like you. Think, gatekeeper. If the binding can be unbound — if the sigil can be broken from outside, or corrupted, or unwound by the wrong hands — the first thing the shadow-thing needs is for the binding to fail. So it needed the binding's protectors gone. The gatekeepers. The ward-mages. Anyone born with enough craft to read the sigil, let alone reforge it. The inquisitors weren't created to hunt sorcery. They were created to hunt *gatekeepers*. Seven years of burnings, and every pyre was lit to keep one man's prison locked."

"But the usurper sits the throne now," Tam said. "If the binding held the thing still—"

"It held it still," Rowenna agreed. "It did not hold it *powerless*. A bound thing can still whisper. Can still reach through the cracks, through dreams, through desperate men who think they hear a god. The Lord Protector who signed the Purge edicts — who dissolved the old orders, who put the crown aside 'for safekeeping' until the king recovered — do you know what happened to him? He woke one morning with a new certainty in his heart, and within a season the certainty had a title, and the title had an army." She spat toward the fire. "The thing doesn't need to break the binding anymore, Dunmore. It found a better trick. It's *eating* the realm instead. Every mage it burns or drains, every ward it unmakes, the binding gets thinner. Your seal hasn't been failing for seven years because of neglect. It's been failing because it's being *fed on* from the far end."

"That's what the courier meant," Edmund whispered. "The seal is feeding."

"He knew. Someone in the old circles survived long enough to work it out, and they sent their best runner to the last gatekeeper they could find, with a map that bleeds because the binding itself is bleeding." Rowenna drained the last of the bottle and set it down with a click that sounded final as a coffin nail. "And here is the part that keeps me up at night, and I'll say it plain because you've earned it plain: I helped build the Purge's machinery before I understood what it was. My order was folded into the inquisition's structure. My commander signed the integration papers. When I objected — when I asked one question too many about why we were burning hedge-witches instead of hunting the thing in the palace — they stripped my banner and called me a drunk and a liar. Which was convenient for them, because half of it was even true by then." Her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it. "Seven years I've hated myself for a disgrace I didn't earn. And the truth is worse. The truth is I was *right*, and being right got me exiled, and the lie got a kingdom."

Silence. Tam had tears running silently down his face, and he wiped them with the heel of his hand like a child annoyed at the weather.

"So the mad king," Edmund said at last, "is the true king. And he's still alive. Still buried under the thing wearing him."

"Banked coal," Rowenna said. "Under ash. Waiting for the right hands."

* * *

It was Tam who broke the silence after, because Tam was the sort who couldn't leave a sum unsolved.

He crawled forward to the map and knelt over it, pulling out his stub of charcoal, and began, almost absently, to count. "The cipher — the layer under the route — it lists names. I thought they were waypoints. Stations along the old ward-line." His charcoal stopped moving. "They're not waypoints. Look at the spacing. Look at how the names are grouped. Master Ferren taught me that registers of the guilds always cluster by trade and by district, and this clusters the same way, but by—" He looked up, and his face had gone pale. "By *craft*. These are names of mages. Wardens, charm-wrights, gatekeepers, hedge-healers. It's a register. A list of everyone whose magic was anchored to the old wards."

"Everyone who could have reforged the sigil," Edmund said.

"Everyone the binding was counting on," Tam corrected, and his voice shook. "And look — most of these names are struck through. Crossed out in the same ink as the rest, but crossed. Struck names are dead ones, or drained ones. I've copied enough debt-ledgers to know what a struck entry looks like." He traced the columns with a finger that trembled. "There are maybe thirty names left unstuck. Thirty. In the whole register."

Edmund leaned over it beside him, and Rowenna watched the two of them — the old gatekeeper and the forger's boy — reading the dead like a ledger, and she felt the fury rise in her again, hot and clean, the only clean thing the last seven years had given her.

"Thirty," Edmund said. "Out of how many?"

"The register goes back centuries," Tam said. "Hundreds of entries. Maybe thousands, counting the older pages." He swallowed. "Whoever wrote this updated it recently. The ink of the crossings-out is fresher than the ink of the list. Someone has been striking names as they fell. Keeping count."

"Keeping count," Rowenna repeated softly. "Of course they are. Because whoever survives this purge will be the ones who rebuild. Or the ones who finish it." She pushed herself to her feet, and her knees protested, and she ignored them. "Do you understand what you're holding, either of you? That list isn't a map of the past. It's a census of the future. Every struck name is a ward that will never be rebuilt, a craft that dies with its holder, a piece of the old realm that the Crown has chewed up and swallowed. And every name *not* struck—"

"—is a target," Edmund finished quietly.

"—is the last of us," Rowenna said. "The last unbound mages in the nine provinces. The binding was anchored to hundreds of hands once, and now it hangs from thirty threads, and the Crown is cutting them one by one. You're not a fugitive, Dunmore. You're a *remainder*."

The word landed in the ruin of the mill and sat there among the embers. Edmund looked down at the map, at his own name in dried blood, and Rowenna saw something settle in his face — not courage, not yet, but the hard ground courage gets built on later. The look of a man who has stopped asking whether the world owes him an explanation and started asking what the world will cost to fix.

"Seven years," he said. "I hid for seven years. Sold luck-charms and love-knots to farmers while they burned people who could have done what I can do. I told myself it was wisdom. Survival." His hands closed slowly into fists on his knees. "It was cowardice with good posture."

"No," Rowenna said, and her voice surprised her with its gentleness. "It was arithmetic. You stayed alive, and the binding held another year because of it. Cowardice doesn't anchor seals." She looked at Tam, hunched and tear-tracked and gripping his charcoal like a knife. "And you, boy. You copied forbidden texts for bread. You kept the words moving when the Crown was burning books. That's not crime. That's *garrison duty*." She let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "Gods. A gatekeeper, a disgraced shield, and a printer's brat. The remainder of the nine provinces. We're what's left of the old realm's promise, and we're sitting in a burnt mill arguing about it."

"Then we stop arguing," Edmund said. He rose, folding the map with the care of a man handling relics. "The register has unstuck names besides mine. If any of them live — if any of them can be reached before the inquisitors reach them—"

"Vellmark first," Rowenna said. "Your stall, your rooms, whatever you left behind. The inquisitors will have torn it apart, but torn things tell you what they were looking for." She was already moving, shrugging into her cloak, and the old discipline of it steadied her more than the wine ever had. "And then north. The next unstuck name on that list is a woman in Harrowgate — I remember the name from court, before the fall. If she lives, we need her. If she doesn't—"

"If she doesn't," Edmund said grimly, "then we'll know how fast the Crown is cutting threads."

Tam scrambled up, scrubbing his face, and paused with one foot in his boot. "What was her name?" he asked. "The woman in Harrowgate."

"Mara Kess," Rowenna said, and bent to kill the embers. "Courier's sister. Map-runner. If the brother bled that map to Vellmark, the sister learned the routes from the same source — which makes her either our fastest road north or the first blade the Crown sends after us."

She stamped the last ember dead, and the mill went black around them, and somewhere far to the south, in a city none of them could see, a thing wearing a king's face smiled at nothing and waited for the threads to snap.
