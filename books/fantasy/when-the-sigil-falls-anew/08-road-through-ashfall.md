## 8. Road Through Ashfall

The province had been called the Greenreach once. Edmund knew it from maps he had sold at his stall — not real maps, nothing that would draw an inquisitor's eye, but pretty things with painted borders and gilded compass roses — and on those maps the Greenreach had been a wash of green running from the hills down to the river country, stitched through with orchards and hop-fields and little market towns whose names sounded like songs.

What lay before them now was the color of old bone.

They crested the ridge at dawn, the four of them strung out in a loose line with their hoods up against the wind, and Edmund stopped walking without quite deciding to. Below them the land stretched away for miles, blackened and gray, the burned skeletons of orchards standing in rows like mourners at a funeral no one had dared to hold. Ash lay over everything in drifts, softening the ruins, giving the whole province the look of something seen through frosted glass. The wind picked it up in long pale ribbons and carried it across the road ahead of them, and when Edmund breathed, he tasted it — bitter, faintly sweet, like the memory of smoke that would not quite die.

"Gods," Tam said quietly beside him. The boy had gone pale under the freckles. "It just keeps going."

"The Crown's engineers fire the grain provinces first," Rowenna said. Her voice was flat, the voice she used when flatness was all that kept her voice from breaking. "Starve the free cities into negotiating. Then they burn the mage-towns after, so no one can hide." She pulled her cloak tighter and started down the slope. "Keep to the road. Walk like pilgrims. Pilgrims are still allowed to be pitiful; travelers with secrets are not."

Mara passed Edmund without a word, but her eyes lingered on the horizon, on the thin dark line where the road bent north toward Harrowgate. Her brother had run this road, Edmund thought. Or one like it. Carrying a map soaked in his own blood toward a man she now walked beside and did not yet forgive.

Edmund followed, and the ash closed over their footprints behind them like water.

* * *

They found the first of the conscription camps on the second day.

Rowenna heard it before any of them — a rhythm of boots on packed earth, the creak of wagon axles — and got them off the road and into the ribs of a burned tithe-barn with time to spare. Through the gap where the barn door had been, Edmund watched the column pass: soldiers in Crown gray, wagons under guard, and in the wagons —

He had prepared himself for prisoners. He had told himself, in the careful way he told himself things, that he knew what the Purge's armies did, that he had spent seven years listening to rumors through his stall's counter and calling that knowledge understanding.

He had not prepared himself for the stillness.

The people in the wagons sat upright, hands in their laps, eyes open. They were not bound, because they did not need binding. They were not weeping, because there was nothing left in them that knew how. A woman in the third wagon wore the faded apron of a hedge-healer, the kind who set bones and brewed febrifuges and had never in her life thrown so much as a spark, and she was looking straight through the barn wall as if the world beyond it were a story someone had once told her. Beside her, a boy of perhaps twenty rocked very slightly, back and forth, back and forth, his mouth moving around words that never came.

"They're drained," Mara whispered. She had gone gray. "I thought that was — I thought people exaggerated."

"It's the conscription writs," Rowenna said. "Every registered mage in the province, every unregistered one they catch, marched to the Crown's field-camps. There's a ritual. It doesn't kill you." Her jaw worked. "That's the cruelty of it. It takes everything you are and leaves the rest standing. The usurper needs living vessels. Magic flows out of a living body slower, steadier. Like tapping a tree."

"And the child mages?" Edmund asked, though some part of him already knew, and hated knowing.

"There's a separate wagon," Rowenna said, "for those too small to walk the march," and she said it in the voice of a woman who had once sworn an oath to protect a royal family and had learned precisely what crowns did with protection.

The column passed. The wagons rolled on toward the horizon, and the ash drifted down over the road, gentle as snowfall, covering everything.

Tam was shaking. Edmund put a hand on the boy's shoulder and felt him flinch, then steady. "They can't take what isn't awake," Edmund said quietly. "You've never trained it. Your gift is asleep, Tam. To them you read as ink-stained and frightened, and both of those are true enough to pass."

"And you?" Tam asked, and it was an innocent question, the kind only the young could ask without cruelty. "Do you read as empty?"

Edmund looked at his own hand, at the faint silver scarring across the palm where the gatekeeper's brand had been laid twenty years ago, and lied, because lying was the shape his life had worn for seven years. "I read as a charm-seller," he said. "Nothing more."

* * *

They found the child on the fourth day, in the ruin of a village that had no name anymore.

It had been small even by Greenreach standards — a cluster of cottages around a well, a shrine to some river god whose carved face the fire had half-erased. Now it was a field of chimneys and collapsed roofs, and in the middle of it, in the doorway of what might once have been a miller's house, sat a girl of six or seven years, perfectly still, watching the road with open eyes.

Rowenna saw her first and called the halt with a hand signal, and for a long moment none of them moved, because they had all learned by now what stillness meant in this province. Drained folk wandered sometimes, when their handlers lost them or the camps overflowed. Drained folk sat in doorways and waited for nothing.

But drained folk did not shiver. And this child was shivering — small violent tremors running through her thin frame, her lips cracked, her breath coming in shallow hitches — and her eyes, when Edmund knelt in front of her, were not empty. They were terrified.

"She's not drained," he heard himself say. His voice came out strange. "Or — not finished. They started. Something interrupted them." He looked closer and saw it: the faint tracery of ritual lines drawn in ash-and-oil on her skin, the Crown's extraction sigils, smudged but legible, winding down her small arms like the roots of a poisonous tree. "Raiders hit the camp. Or deserters. She ran during the fighting." He looked up at the others and heard his own heart hammering. "She's full, and blocked, and the block is killing her. The power has nowhere to go. It's eating her from inside."

"What do we do?" Mara demanded. "Is there a healer? The next town—"

"The next town is three days away and she has hours." Edmund's mouth was dry. He was aware, distantly, of the others' faces turning toward him, of Tam's breath catching, of Rowenna's hand drifting toward her sword hilt not in threat but in the old reflex of a guard sensing danger. "There's another way."

"Edmund." Rowenna's voice was very quiet. "Say the next sentence carefully."

Seven years. Seven years of smiling at farmers over love-charms that were nothing but braided thread and suggestion. Seven years of feeling his own magic coiled in him like a held breath, gatekeeper's magic, deep and slow and strong, fed by the ward beneath Vellmark even across all that distance, and never once using it, because using it was a candle flame and the Purge was a continent of tinder. Seven years of being half a man and calling it survival.

And now a child was dying in a doorway, shivering, looking at him with eyes that had already decided adults would not save her.

"I'm a mage," Edmund said.

The word came out of him like something exorcised. He had not said it aloud since the night the Purge began. It hung in the ash-fall between them, absurdly small, four letters and a lifetime.

"A *what*—" Mara started.

"A gatekeeper. Trained at the Vellmark ward. I can draw the extraction sigils out of her, ground them through myself, unmake them. It will hurt her for a moment and then it will stop hurting forever. If I don't, she dies tonight." He was already pulling off his gloves, and his hands were shaking, and to his own astonishment the shaking felt less like fear than like a man flexing muscles locked too long in one position. "I need everyone to look at the road and not at me, because if an inquisitor feels this much magic released within fifty miles, they will come, and we will run, and that is tomorrow's problem."

"You've been hiding this," Mara said. Not a question. Her voice had an edge he had never heard from her, grief sharpened into accusation. "This whole road. Every checkpoint, every patrol. You let us think—"

"Yes," Edmund said simply. "Because hiding is how I've stayed alive, and staying alive was how I stayed useful, and I am done pretending those reasons weigh more than she does." He turned back to the child. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and fever. "Hello," he said softly. "My name is Edmund. I'm going to touch your arms, and it's going to feel like cold water, and then like bees, and I am so sorry about the bees. Can you be brave for one minute?"

The child looked at him for a long moment. Then, in a voice scraped nearly to nothing: "Everyone else went to sleep and didn't wake up."

"I know," Edmund said, and his chest cracked cleanly down the middle. "Not you. Not today. One minute. That's all I'm asking."

* * *

Later, he would remember it in fragments.

The sigils rising off her skin at his summons, glowing sullen orange like coals raked from a fire, writhing in the air as they fought the hand that pulled them. The sensation of taking them into himself — cold first, then the bees, exactly as he'd promised, a swarm of burning needles threading down his own arms, the Crown's hungry geometry trying to find purchase in his channels and finding instead twenty years of disciplined gates slamming shut along its path. The child screaming once, short and sharp, and then going rigid, and then — going soft. Melting against Tam, who had knelt and gathered her in without being asked, murmuring nonsense about bread and print-shops and the correct way to ink a capital letter.

And beneath all of it, underneath the pain and the concentration and the terror of exposure, something else. Something he had not let himself feel in seven years.

Relief.

It rose in him like water into a dry field. His magic moved through him whole, unfettered, magnificent — the deep patient strength of the wards, the old grammar of thresholds and boundaries that he had studied until it lived in his bones — and every inch of it sang *here, here, this is what you are*. He had spent seven years telling himself that suppression was discipline, that his small life was a virtuous life, and now, kneeling in the ash with stolen fire crawling out of a child's flesh and into his hands, he understood that he had been drowning and calling it prudence. Using his gift felt like breathing after a long immersion. Felt like stepping through a door he had nailed shut from the inside.

The last sigil came apart in his grip like wet paper. The ash-lines on the girl's arms faded to nothing. She sighed — a child's sigh, petulant and enormous — and burrowed into Tam's shoulder, and was asleep between one breath and the next.

Edmund swayed and caught himself on the doorframe. His arms ached to the bone. His eyes were wet, which he decided was the smoke.

When he turned, the other three were arranged around him in a rough crescent, and none of them had looked at the road.

"I said watch the road," he managed.

"We decided," Rowenna said, "that the road could manage itself for one minute." Her face was unreadable in the way of career soldiers delivering verdicts. Then, slowly, she unbuckled her sword belt's outer strap — a knightly habit, a gesture of respect Edmund had only read about — and inclined her head. "Gatekeeper Dunmore. Seven years I drank myself stupid wondering if anyone with real power was still left in this kingdom. You were selling charms two streets from my garrison the entire time."

"I make excellent charms," Edmund said hoarsely.

"You're an idiot," Mara said.

"Mara—"

"An idiot." Her voice shook, and he saw now that she was crying, furiously, silently, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand as though the tears were a personal failing. "Dovan died getting that map to you. My brother bled out in your back room because he believed the gatekeeper of Vellmark was worth finding. And you were going to walk us all to Harrowgate and maybe to the gate itself, and let us die protecting a man who wouldn't spend one honest spark to save a *child*—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was low and terrible and, somehow, not unkind. "Don't you ever hide again. Not from patrols. Not from us. Whatever's coming for you, whatever it costs — we carry it together or none of us carries anything. Do you understand me?"

Edmund looked at her — at the dead courier's sister, at the fury that was love wearing armor — and then at Tam holding the sleeping girl, at Rowenna with her head still slightly bowed, and felt the last wall of seven years come down not with a crash but with a sigh, like snow sliding from a roof.

"I understand you," he said. "No more hiding."

"Good." Mara scrubbed her face one final time and turned briskly toward the horses. "Then we move. If you lit up the sky like I suspect you did, we have perhaps a day before someone comes to investigate. Harrowgate is two days' hard ride. We'll ride three days hard and sleep in the free city."

They made ready in the blue light of late afternoon, and it was Tam — settling the girl into the pannier sling they rigged from blankets, humming to her as she slept — who looked up suddenly and went still.

"Edmund," he said. "Her eyes."

The child had woken, just barely, one eye cracked open, watching Edmund with drowsy unconcern. But it wasn't her gaze that stopped his heart. It was what flickered in it, deep in the pupil, for only an instant before it sank away: a reflection of light that had no source. A thin ring of silver, precise as an engraver's line, turning slowly around each iris like the rim of a coin.

Edmund had seen that ring before. Once. In the contract they had taken from the Salt Prison, in the wax seal pressed at its foot — the mark of a binding witnessed and notarized by the Crown itself.

"She was in one of their field-camps," Rowenna said quietly, following his stare. "The extraction ritual leaves a trace in the oldest subjects. I've seen it in the veterans who escaped the first year." She paused, and Edmund heard the next words arrive before she spoke them. "But the ring fades within weeks. Hers looks new. Fresh-marked. Which means the camp that held her—"

"—is close," Edmund finished. "Days. Maybe hours."

Far off across the ash-plain, so distant it might have been imagination, a horn sounded. Once. Low. Patient.

Mara was already swinging into the saddle.

"Harrowgate," she said. "Now."
