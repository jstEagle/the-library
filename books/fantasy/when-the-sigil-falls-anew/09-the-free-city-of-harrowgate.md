## 9. The Free City of Harrowgate

Mara had imagined the last free city as a fortress. That was what the songs called it — Harrowgate the Unbowed, Harrowgate the Door That Would Not Open — and in her brother's letters, written in his slanting hand between courier runs, it had been a place of white towers and open gates where no inquisitor's writ could follow. *If everything goes wrong, Kess,* he'd written once, three years ago, *go to Harrowgate. The walls are old but they hold.*

The walls did hold, she would grant them that. They rose from the plain like the spine of some buried titan, gray stone veined with older masonry beneath, and the banners of the Council of Nine snapped along the battlements in the cold morning wind — nine quarters, nine cities that had once stood against the Crown, eight of them now fallen or bought. But what she saw inside those walls was not a fortress.

It was a market.

The gate road funneled into a boulevard thick with stalls and shouting vendors, fish-sellers and spice-merchants and money-changers weighing Crownsilver against free-minted copper, and everywhere the sound of people who did not yet believe the war was coming for them. A woman argued over eels. Children chased a dog through the press of bodies. Somewhere a fiddler played, badly, and nobody minded.

"They don't know," Tam said quietly at her shoulder. He had his hood up despite the mild day; the bruise-yellow marks on his throat from the Salt Prison had faded, but he still flinched at uniforms, and Harrowgate wore its city guard everywhere — blue cloaks, honest faces, nothing like the gray-and-red of the inquisitors, but cloth all the same.

"They know," Mara said. "They're choosing not to." She had carried messages through two burning provinces. She knew the difference between ignorance and denial, and this boulevard stank of denial, thick and sweet as rot under fruit.

Rowenna pushed ahead of them through the crowd, and the crowd let her — people moved aside for her without seeming to know why, some animal memory in the spine recognizing a woman built like a siege door. Edmund walked close behind her, his charm-seller's stoop finally gone from his shoulders, though he still glanced at every stall of trinkets with something between longing and grief. The child, Pell, rode asleep against his chest in a sling they'd bought in Ashfall, her drained magic slowly refilling like a well after drought, and even sleeping she clung to his shirt with both fists.

They had come forty miles in two days since Rowenna found the ward-stones torn up. Nobody had said *we need the Council* out loud. It had simply become the only door left, and doors left unchosen have a way of opening anyway.

The Council Hall stood at the city's heart, older than everything around it, a squat fortress of black granite with the nine banners hanging dead in the windless shadow of its portico. Petitioners clustered at the iron gates — farmers with a boundary dispute, a guild delegation with a tax grievance, a one-eyed woman holding up a petition about grain tariffs. Ordinary grievances. Mara listened to them for a moment and felt something twist in her chest: these people were arguing about tariffs while the Crown's legions burned the world down around their walls.

A clerk with ink-stained fingers took Rowenna's name, looked at it, looked at her face, and went very still.

"Wait here," he said, and disappeared inside faster than a clerk should move.

"That's either very good or very bad," Edmund murmured.

"It's Aldous Vey," Rowenna said. "He was a supply clerk when I held Vellmark. He remembers me." Her jaw worked. "Whether that's good depends on what he's become."

They waited an hour in the cold. The tariff woman gave up and left. The guild delegation went in and came out looking satisfied, which told Mara the Council was still functioning, still processing papers, still performing the great theater of governance. Then the clerk returned with two guards, not inquisitors, just tired men in blue, and led them through corridors that smelled of lamp oil and old decisions, into a chamber where a heavyset man with a councilor's chain sat behind a table too big for him.

"Ser Rowenna Vale." He said the title carefully, tasting whether it was poison. "They struck you from the rolls seven years ago."

"They did."

"And now you come to my hall asking—" He looked down at a paper in front of him, and Mara watched his face change as he read whatever the clerk had written there. "Asking us to declare for the true king. On the word of four travelers and a blood map."

"On the word of the binding contract," Rowenna said, "stolen from the Salt Prison at the cost of four of our number captured, tortured, or nearly killed. On the word of the gatekeeper of Vellmark." She gestured, and Edmund stepped forward, and Mara saw the councilor look at him and see exactly what everyone saw — a shopkeeper, a tired man of no consequence — and dismiss him. She saw Edmund notice being dismissed, and she saw him decide, again, to let it happen.

"The Council is aware of the situation in the east," the councilor said at last, each word laid down like a coin counted out. "We are also aware that the Crown's armies are sixty thousand strong and our walls garrison nine thousand. We are aware that Vellmark fell in a day. That Corvassy opened its gates and was spared. That Ashfall resisted and is ash." He folded his hands. "There are discussions underway. Diplomatic channels. The Crown has indicated that a free city which... accommodates itself... may retain its charter, its faiths, its markets."

"Accommodates itself how?" Tam asked, before anyone could stop him.

The councilor didn't look at him. "By rendering up persons of interest. By accepting royal overseers in matters of security. By discontinuing practices inconsistent with the Accord of Crowns." He meant mages. He meant handing over every hedge-witch and charm-seller and child born with light in their palms, and he wrapped it in words like *charter* because he could not bear to say the word *surrender* in his own hall. Mara watched Edmund's hand drift, without apparent thought, to rest on the sleeping child's back, shielding her from a sentence spoken across a table.

"You're negotiating," Rowenna said flatly.

"I am *preserving*," the councilor said, and for one moment something human crossed his face, exhaustion and fear and the terrible weight of nine thousand lives weighed against strangers'. "You were a soldier, Ser Vale. You know arithmetic. Sixty thousand. Come back when you have an army, and we will declare for your king. Until then, the Council's position is unchanged, and your presence in Harrowgate is... noted."

*Noted.* Mara turned the word over as they were escorted out through corridors that suddenly seemed full of listening ears. Noted by whom? Filed where? Handed upward to whom?

Outside, the fiddler was still playing. Someone laughed.

* * *

They took a room above a dockside tavern called the Drowned Bell, because Rowenna said she needed to see someone and Mara guessed — correctly, as it turned out — that the someone drank within walking distance of water. They left Pell with Tam and a deck of marked cards, and followed Rowenna through streets that grew narrower and saltier as the harbor came near, until she stopped before a low door with no sign and pushed it open into air thick with pipe smoke and lamplight.

Twenty men and women drank in silence at scattered tables. This was not a laughing tavern. At the far end, alone, a gray-haired woman sat facing the wall, a cup of something dark before her and a sword propped against the wall beside her — not hung, not stored, *propped*, the way a man props up a crutch.

Rowenna crossed the room. The drinkers watched. Nobody spoke.

"Captain-General," Rowenna said. "Or should I say—"

"Don't." The woman didn't turn. Her voice was gravel poured slow. "That name retired when I did. Sit down, Vale, or stand, but stop looming."

Rowenna sat. After a moment, so did Mara, close enough to hear, far enough to grab a knife if it went wrong. Up close the old woman's face was a map of old campaigns, and her eyes, when they finally came around, were pale and steady and terribly sad.

"They told me you died at the Purge," the woman said. "Danelle Marsh. My aide. She swore you cut down six of ours before the wall guns took you."

"Marsh lied to you."

"Marsh writes the Crown's interrogations now." The old woman lifted her cup, considered it, set it down untouched. "She also writes to me. Every month. Asking if I've heard rumors of gatekeepers." Her eyes flicked to Rowenna's pack, where the corner of a leather case showed. "I wondered when one of you would wash up here. The tide brings everything to Harrowgate eventually. That's the problem with last cities. Everything ends up in them, including the end."

"We came to ask for the Council's aid," Rowenna said. "We've had the Council's answer."

"The Council's answer is the answer of frightened old merchants, and it will change nothing, because it isn't meant to change anything. It's meant to make them feel like statesmen while they measure the depth of the water." The old woman turned her cup in a slow circle. "You want to know if I'll raise the garrison for you. I can't. Half my officers have Crown kin. The other half drink with councilors. And I'm old, Vale, and tired, and the last time I believed in a cause I lost a king, a student, and my commission in the same fortnight."

"I'm not asking you to fight," Rowenna said. "I'm asking you what you know."

Something shifted in the old face. Respect, maybe, for a question that cost nothing and asked everything. She leaned forward, and her voice dropped until Mara had to lean in too, past the smell of smoke into the smell of old steel and lampblack.

"I know the inquisitors' route ledgers pass through this city — sealed copies, for the archive I keep for a Council that thinks I'm a drunk. I read them because reading is all I'm good for now." She tapped the table once. "Three weeks ago, a copy came through describing a search pattern in the eastern passes. Search pattern for five travelers. Descriptions. Your descriptions, Vale — yours is twenty years old and still right. And the girl's brother, dead a year before the search order was drafted."

Mara's blood went to ice water. *Kess.* Dead a year. They knew about Kess before Kess had ever pressed a map into Edmund's hands. Which meant—

"They didn't chase the courier," Mara heard herself say. "They *sent* him."

"Now she's doing sums," the old woman said, not unkindly. "Yes. The dying-courier gambit is an old inquisitor play. Find the person the fugitives will trust, break him or buy him or replace him, and let him lead you home to the thing you can't find. Except—" she raised a finger, "—the ledger notes an anomaly. The courier was found to be carrying nothing. Whatever he passed, he passed before he died, and the search pattern assumes it's still moving. They've been following *you*. Through Ashfall. Through the passes. And the pattern's next projected node—" she looked at Rowenna, and her pale eyes were almost gentle, "—is Harrowgate. It may already be here. There's a gray ship due in tomorrow's tide that flies merchant colors and draws too deep for cargo. Inquisitor transports sit high and empty, girl. I've unloaded enough of them to know."

The room seemed to tilt. Mara thought of the boulevard, the fiddler, the children chasing the dog. She thought of Pell asleep against Edmund's chest, her small magic barely rekindled, defenseless as a candle in a gale.

"Why tell us?" Rowenna asked quietly. "Why now?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. "Because I trained you, and they took you from me, and I have spent seven years drinking instead of being angry about it." She slid something across the table — a small brass key, harbor-stamped. "Boat shed fourteen, the north quay. Skiff, oars, sail. It belongs to a smuggler who owes me and won't miss it for a week." Then, gruffly, as if embarrassed by her own generosity: "And because you asked me what I know instead of what I'd give. That was always your best quality, Vale. Don't lose it in kings' courts."

She picked up her cup at last and drank, and turned her face back to the wall, and the audience was over.

* * *

Mara didn't remember crossing the city. She remembered fragments — the fiddler still playing, damn him, the fish-stalls, a guard's blue cloak that made her heart slam — and then she was in the room at the Drowned Bell with the door barred, telling them all of it, watching the words land on each of them like stones.

Tam went pale first. "Sent him. They *sent* Kess's brother—" He stopped, hearing himself, and shot Mara a stricken look, but she was past flinching. She had done the grieving already, on the road, in the night watches. What she felt now wasn't grief. It was colder and cleaner than that.

"No," Edmund said slowly. He had set Pell down on the bed, and the child slept on, oblivious, her breath a small tide. "No, wait. Think. If the courier were theirs, why kill him? Why let him die buying me time at the back door? Their own tool, spent on a diversion for a charm-seller?" He shook his head. "The old woman's ledger says he carried *nothing* when they found him. Because he'd already given the map away. To me." His voice gathered certainty as it went. "They tried to use him and he beat them. Or he was never wholly theirs. Either way — they know the map moves, and they know roughly where we are, and they know it before we knew where we were going ourselves."

"So we run tonight," Rowenna said. "Shed fourteen. North quay. Out on the tide before their gray ship docks."

"Run *where*?" Mara demanded, and heard her own voice crack like green wood. "Where is there left? Eight cities gone. The ninth selling us with the market rates. There's nowhere behind us and the gate is ahead of us and the armies are between—" She stopped. Pressed her fists against her eyes until the dark sparked. When she lowered them, all three of them were looking at her, and none of them looked away, and that undid her more than anything the Council had said all day.

She had spent her whole life believing in things with names and charters. The courier service — its oaths, its routes, the sacred inviolability of the satchel. Her brother, sworn to the same. The free cities, the pacts, the walls with their songs. And here was the sum of it all: a councilor counting arithmetic, a general drinking at a wall, a boy's death filed in a ledger as an anomaly. Institutions were just walls, and walls were just stones, and stones did not love you.

But the boy by the window was already pulling out his pens, saying something about forging harbor passes, his hands steadier than they'd been a month ago. The disgraced knight was already planning tides and sightlines, her shame burned off her like morning fog. The shopkeeper was rocking a stolen-back child and looking at Mara as if her fury were a resource, something precious to be spent carefully.

Nobody had saved any of them. No wall, no charter, no crown worth the name. They had saved each other, badly, over and over, and would again.

"All right," Mara said, and her voice came out level. "Then we go to the gate. Tonight, on the tide, ahead of their ship. And if the inquisitors want the map—" she touched the leather case at her hip, where her brother's last gift rode against her body like a second heartbeat, "—they can dig it out of the wreck of everything they send against us."

Edmund smiled at her, small and tired and grateful, and began gathering their few things.

They were at the door, hooded, ready, when the knocking started below — heavy, rhythmic, official, the sound of a fist that represented something larger than a fist. From the street rose voices, many of them, and among them a voice like a file drawn across iron, calling up:

"Gatekeeper. We know you're keeping watch over more than charms. Come down, and the city keeps its charter."

Tam looked at Edmund. Rowenna put her hand to the empty scabbard she hadn't yet refilled.

The gray ship, Mara thought, sick with the arithmetic of it. The tide had turned early.
