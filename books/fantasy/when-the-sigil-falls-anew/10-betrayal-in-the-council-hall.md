## 10. Betrayal in the Council Hall

Tam had copied a thousand documents in Master Ferren's print shop, and he had learned early that the most dangerous lies were the ones set in beautiful type. Council proclamations were the worst of them — all flourished capitals and wax seals, promising protection in language so polished you could see your own hopeful face reflected in it.

He was seeing that face now, reflected in the polished table of the Harrowgate council chamber, and it looked like a fool's face.

The chamber itself was meant to awe. Seven tiers of seats curved around a floor of pale marble, and banners of the free cities hung from iron rings along the walls — Ashfall's black and copper, the Salt Marches' grey, Vellmark's blue, gone dark now behind the Crown. Light fell through high windows of colored glass, painting the councilors in jewel-toned patches that made them look, Tam thought, like saints in a chapel window. Saints who had not yet decided whether to be saints.

The company stood on the chamber floor like defendants. Rowenna had insisted on clean cloaks and combed hair; Mara wore her brother's courier sash buckled over her chest like an accusation; Edmund carried the map wrapped in oilcloth against his heart, and Tam could tell by the set of his shoulders that he hadn't slept. Tam himself clutched his satchel of pens and inks the way another boy might clutch a mother's hand. He was sixteen years old and standing in the last free council of the free cities, and he kept waiting for someone to notice he didn't belong and throw him out.

Councilor-First Aldous Vane presided from the center tier, a broad man with a voice built for festivals and funerals. He listened to Rowenna's report — the map, the gate, the drained mages of Ashfall, the legions massing past the border stones — with his chin propped on his knuckles, wearing the expression of a man hearing about weather in a distant country.

"You ask," Vane said at last, when she finished, "for soldiers. For ships. For the Council to declare war on the Crown of Vellmark."

"I ask for the Council to remember what it is," Rowenna said. Her voice filled the chamber without effort; Tam had heard her use it in dockside taverns and dwarven tunnels alike, and it always sounded like someone who had once been obeyed and never quite forgiven herself for it. "Harrowgate was founded by people who fled a king. The whole reason any of this exists—" she gestured at the banners, the marble, the jeweled councilors, "—is that we were supposed to be the place the Crown couldn't reach."

"The Crown reached Ashfall," said a thin woman on the third tier. Councilor Brede, Tam remembered from the pamphlets he'd copied. "The Crown reached the Salt Marches. And where were our armies? Where were anyone's?" She spread her hands. "We are one city against a realm."

"Then we are one city that gets to choose how it dies," Mara said.

The word *dies* moved through the chamber like a draft under a door. Several councilors shifted. One older man closed his eyes as if praying.

Vane raised a hand for calm, and got it instantly, which told Tam more than anything else about how this room worked. "No one is talking about dying, Mistress Kess. We are talking about survival, and survival sometimes requires… patience." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm as bread. "The Crown's envoy has already approached us. Terms are being discussed. Trade guarantees. Autonomy in municipal matters. The Purge would not extend to Harrowgate's registered citizens."

"Registered," Rowenna repeated flatly.

"Listed. Documented." Vane's smile did not waver, but something behind it went very still. "The alternative is fire. I have seen what happened to Callowmere. Have you? There is nothing left of Callowmere but a lake of glass and the smell. I will not trade my city's beauty for its principles when principles cannot be buried under gravestones."

"Your city's beauty," Rowenna said, "is standing right here asking you to protect it."

For a moment — just a moment — Tam saw something flicker across Vane's face. Not guilt. Something worse: calculation. The look of a man doing arithmetic with lives, and finding the sum acceptable.

"The Council will deliberate," Vane announced. "In private. You have our thanks, and Harrowgate's hospitality tonight. The Silver Quill inn will accommodate you. Rest. Tomorrow we will have an answer."

Tam watched Rowenna's jaw tighten. He watched Mara's hand drift, unconsciously, toward her belt knife. And he watched Edmund — quiet Edmund, charm-seller Edmund, whose magic sat folded up inside him like a moth in winter — go very still, staring at Councilor Vane with an expression Tam had only seen once before, on the night the courier died in the back room of a shop that no longer existed.

It was the look of a man watching a door close.

* * *

They took rooms at the Silver Quill because there was nowhere else, but none of them pretended to sleep well. Tam lay on a narrow bed in a room he shared with Edmund and listened to the inn settle around them — creaking beams, a dog barking two streets over, the distant clang of the harbor bells counting the hours.

"Edmund." His own voice sounded too loud in the dark. "You think they'll help us?"

A long pause. Then, from the other bed: "I think Master Vane counted heads very carefully while we talked."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean he looked at us the way a man looks at goods he intends to sell."

Tam stared at the ceiling. Somewhere below, the taproom crowd was thinning; he could hear chairs scraping, a woman laughing at some joke he'd never hear. It all seemed unbearably ordinary. In Ashfall, children with hollow eyes were being drained like wineskins, and here people laughed over ale, and a councilor smiled, and tomorrow the answer would come down like a verdict.

"Maybe he's just careful," Tam said. "Being careful isn't a crime."

"No," Edmund agreed softly. "But selling us would be."

Tam turned that over. He thought about the raid on the print shop — the boots on the stairs, the smell of lamp-oil, Master Ferren's face going white as the inquisitors kicked the door in. He thought about how he'd run, out the window and over the rooftops, leaving his master shouting his name behind him. He'd told himself it was what Ferren wanted. He still told himself that, most nights. Some nights the telling didn't take.

"Edmund," he said. "If it comes to it. If they come for us here. You'd get out, wouldn't you? All of you?"

"We'd all get out together or not at all," Edmund said. "That's rather the point of us now."

Tam didn't answer. But he lay awake long after Edmund's breathing slowed, and somewhere past midnight he rose, dressed quietly, and went down to the common room to sit by the banked fire with his satchel, copying cipher notes into a fresh notebook by candle-stub. It was the only thing he knew how to do with fear: turn it into work. Ink and iron, Rowenna called it. He was starting to understand that iron was the part nobody wanted to be.

He was three pages deep when the first light appeared in the eastern sky — not dawn. Dawn came slow and gold. This came fast and orange, and it was followed by a sound he knew from nightmares: the crackle of burning timber, and beneath it, thin and rising, the tolling of the alarm bells.

Then the door of the inn burst open and Rowenna filled it, already armed, her hair loose and her eyes like chips of slate.

"Back door," she said. "Now. They're burning the quarter."

* * *

The streets of Harrowgate's river quarter had become an oven. Flames climbed the timber-framed houses on both sides of the lane, and the smoke hung low and thick, tasting of pitch and something sweeter underneath — Tam didn't want to know what. Figures ran through the murk carrying children and bundles and nothing at all, and above it all, impossibly calm, came the measured footfalls of inquisitors in their ash-grey coats, moving through the burning street with lanterns that cast no warmth.

"White masks," Rowenna breathed. "They brought the white masks. That means a Lord Inquisitor commands this."

"How do you know?" Tam gasped as they ran.

"Because white masks don't come for smugglers." She shoved a stumbling family into an alley doorway ahead of the flames. "They come for *names*. Someone gave them names."

Mara fell in beside Tam, blade drawn, using it less as a weapon than as a tool — cutting a rope here, prying a shutter there, opening paths through the chaos. Edmund ran with the map pressed to his chest, and every time an inquisitor's lantern swept near, Tam felt the air around Edmund change pressure, like the moment before a storm. Holding it back. Holding himself back.

"Sewers," Edmund panted. "There's a grate on Chandler's Lane — the old tanner's outflow—"

"I know it," Rowenna snapped. "Move."

Chandler's Lane was already half-lost to smoke. They came at it crouched, single file along a wall of burning warehouses, heat pressing against Tam's face like a living thing. Ahead, iron grate, rust-eaten, exactly where memory said it should be. Rowenna seized it and hauled, tendons standing out in her neck—

And the lanterns converged.

Tam saw it happen the way you see things in dreams, each detail lit and separate. Four figures in ash-grey, white masks blank as eggs, spreading across the mouth of the lane with the unhurried confidence of people who know exactly where their prey must run. Behind them, torchlight. Behind *that*, the fire, closing like a hand.

"Grate," Rowenna snarled, wrenching again. The rust held.

"They know the route," Mara said, voice dead calm in the way Tam had learned meant the opposite. "Someone drew it for them."

"Later," Edmund said. "Deal with later later."

But the later was already arriving. The inquisitors advanced, and Tam felt the strange cold pressure around Edmund sharpen — felt, rather than saw, the air between Edmund and the masked men thicken like glass. One inquisitor stumbled, clawing at his mask. Another's lantern guttered and died.

"Edmund's buying seconds," Rowenna grunted, bracing her boot against the grate frame. "Not minutes. Somebody make minutes."

Tam looked at the inquisitors. He looked at the grate. He looked at the notebook in his satchel — three pages of cipher readings, false readings, the ones he'd invented to feed the enemy if it ever came to this, because he had known since the print shop that someday it would come to this, and lying in ink was the only weapon he owned.

"I can make minutes," he heard himself say.

"No," Edmund said, without turning.

"Somebody has to hold them at the corner while you get the grate up, and I'm the fastest runner and I'm the smallest target and I'm the only one who can lie to them in their own cipher." The words came out steady, which surprised him, because inside he was screaming. "Master Ferren taught me the Crown's document codes. Every inquisitor carries a field-book keyed to them. If they catch me, they'll think they caught the forger — the *cipher-bearer* — and they'll want me alive, and wanting me alive means questions, and questions take time." He was backing away as he talked, toward the alley fork, toward the smoke. "Rowenna. Get them to the tunnels. Tell them—"

"Don't you dare finish that sentence," Mara said.

"—tell them I'll re-ink the sigil lines better than anyone when we're at the gate." He grinned at her, and it was the grin he used with customers when he shorted them honest weights, cheeky and unafraid, and it cost him everything he had left. "Tell them Tam said the binding needs a better hand than theirs anyway."

"Tam—" Edmund started forward, and the pressure around him flickered, and the nearest inquisitor recovered his balance and began to run.

Tam ran the other way.

He ran into the burning quarter with his satchel bouncing against his hip and his heart hammering a rhythm he'd carry for the rest of his life, however long that proved to be. Behind him he heard the scream of iron giving way — the grate, surrendering at last — and Rowenna's voice, raw, barking orders, and Mara's voice, higher, cut off by distance and fire.

He led them four streets. He made himself loud, made himself visible, hurled his satchel's spare ink bottles into lantern-light so the glass flashed, played the fleeing apprentice to perfection because that was the role he'd been born to and, tonight, chose. At the mouth of Fishmonger's Stair he let the smoke take his breath and his footing together, and went down hard on the cobbles as the white masks closed around him like water.

Hands seized his arms. A gloved finger hooked his satchel strap and lifted the notebook into lantern light. Through the eye-holes of the nearest mask, Tam saw eyes — human eyes, tired, curious, and behind them something that hungered.

"Well," said the inquisitor, in a voice like dry paper. "The little cipher-man. The Lord Inquisitor will be pleased."

Tam spat blood onto the cobbles and smiled his shopkeeper's smile. "Took you long enough," he said. "I've been running for days."

Above the burning roofs, the harbor bells tolled three, and far to the south, beneath a mountain he had never seen, a broken sigil waited for a hand steady enough to re-ink it. Tam hoped his hands would be steady. He hoped there would be hands at all. And as they dragged him up off his knees, he fixed his eyes on the glow of the fire and memorized the direction of it — north, always north, the way they'd gone — so that whatever they did to him, whatever he was forced to say, some part of him would stay pointing home.
