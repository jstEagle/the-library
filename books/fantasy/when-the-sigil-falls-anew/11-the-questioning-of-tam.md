## 11. The Questioning of Tam

The first thing Tam learned about the Salt Prison was that it was never silent.

Not the screaming — though there was screaming, sometimes, muffled by walls so thick the sound arrived more as a pressure than a noise, like weather trapped in stone. It was smaller than that. The drip of brine somewhere behind the eastern wall, patient as a heartbeat. The rasp of an iron gate two floors up, opening and closing on no schedule Tam could ever determine. The breathing of the man in the next cell, who had stopped calling out on the second day and now only breathed, slow and deliberate, as if to prove to someone that he still could.

Tam counted the drips when he couldn't sleep, which was most of the time. He counted them now, curled on the pallet that smelled of mildew and old salt, his wrists raw beneath cuffs that had been forged with something more than iron in them. Ward-cuffs. He'd copied engravings of their like once, in Master Corvin's shop, back when forbidden texts were just lines to be reproduced and the worst thing in his life was hunger.

*Four hundred and six. Four hundred and seven.*

The little lamp of defiance he kept lit inside himself — that was what he counted for. Every drip the cell took from him, he took one back.

They had not asked him questions yet.

That was the part no one warned you about, Tam thought. In Harrowgate's print quarter, the apprentices told stories about the inquisitors' cells: hooks and irons and the Questioner's Voice, the charm that peeled truth out of you like skin from fruit. The stories were all teeth. But three days into his captivity, no one had laid a finger on him. They had fed him — thin soup, black bread, but food. They had left him alone in the dark except for the dripping and the breathing man, and they had let him wait.

The waiting was the question. He understood that now. They wanted to know what broke first: his body or his certainty about his friends.

*They won't break either,* he told the dark. *Rowenna could hold this whole prison with a broom handle. Mara would already be through the wall if she knew where it was. And Edmund—*

He stopped. He had been stopping at Edmund's name for three days, because thinking of Edmund meant thinking of the moment in the sewer when he'd turned around — turned *back*, which was the stupidest thing he had ever done, which he would do again — to see gray-robed figures flooding the tunnel mouth and realize that if he ran, they would follow the others' footprints in the muck straight to them.

So he had stood in the filth of Harrowgate's undercity with his heart trying to claw out of his chest, and he had shouted words he didn't have a plan behind: *"This way! I've got the cipher! You want the cipher, it's me you want!"*

And gods help him, it had worked. They wanted the cipher. They had knocked him half-senseless, bound his hands, and carried him up into burning air while Harrowgate's council quarter went up in flames behind him, and every step of the way he had repeated the only sentence that mattered, the sentence he'd built out of Tam-as-a-child logic, out of years of hiding contraband pages in his shirt and lying to customs men with a straight face:

*They can't take something from you if they don't know how much of it you have.*

He knew things. That was his whole life, knowing things — where Master Corvin kept the real ledger, which guards took bribes, how to read a text upside down across a table. And the thing he knew that mattered most was this: the map's cipher was *his*. Edmund needed him to read it. Without Tam, the blood-inked route was just elegant nonsense. So as long as the inquisitors believed that — believed the cipher lived in his head and nowhere else — he was worth more alive and unmarked than broken.

Which meant his job, here in the dripping dark, was to be valuable.

And to lie.

*Four hundred and twenty.*

* * *

They came for him on the fifth morning, or what he judged was morning, by a slot of paler gray high in the wall.

Two guards, faces wrapped against the salt air, and between them a woman in dove-gray robes with silver thread at the collar — not the dramatic black-and-white of the street inquisitors Tam had feared all his life, but something quieter, worse. She had the look of a person who did her work carefully. Her hair was pinned with mathematical precision. Her eyes were the pale brown of watered tea, and they looked at Tam the way Master Corvin looked at a page with a suspected forgery: not with anger. With interest.

"I am Inquisitor Halvene," she said, when the guards had chained him to the chair in a room above his cell — a real room, with a table and a lamp and a window, and the sudden wealth of light made his eyes stream so that his first tears in front of her were only physics, and he hated being grateful for that. "You are Tam of Corvin's print shop, Harrowgate. Sixteen. Apprentice forger. Copier of restricted texts since you were eleven." She sat down across from him and folded her hands. "You've been busy for a child."

"I copy what people pay for," Tam said. His voice came out steadier than he'd expected, and he clung to that.

"Yes. And four days ago you were pulled out of a sewer clutching—" she unfolded one hand, and a guard set a leather case on the table, "-this."

Inside, when she opened it, lay papers. Tam's stomach dropped through the floor of the world, because he recognized them: pages from Edmund's satchel, notes in Edmund's careful hand, and — worst — Tam's own transcription worksheet, the practice key he'd made in the inn at Ashfall, the one he'd thought he'd burned.

"We know the courier's map is a cipher," Halvene said mildly. "We know you can read it. Your worksheet tells us you'd begun." She turned the page toward him. "So let us not waste each other's time. The Crown wants the location of the old gate. Read it for us, and you will eat well tonight, and sleep in a bed, and in a year you'll be copying in the Palace scriptorium with a pension and a title. Refuse—" and here her voice didn't harden; it simply continued, level as a ruled line, "-and we will find other ways to arrive at the same answer. Ways that leave less of you to enjoy the result."

Tam looked at the worksheet. His own handwriting stared back at him, sixteen-year-old loops and all, and he made himself think, slowly and clearly, about everything it contained.

Because here was the beautiful thing, the thing he'd realized somewhere in the third day of counting drips: the worksheet was true. It really was his working notes. The substitution scheme, the blood-reagent notation, the way the courier had layered a merchant's ledger over a pilgrim's road-song — all of it was accurate, all of it genuine, the honest skeleton of the cipher.

What the worksheet did not contain — could not contain — was the final layer. The map's last turn keyed to blood. Not ink, not reagent: *blood*, and specifically the courier's blood, and through some sympathy Tam only partially understood, to the name written in it. To *Edmund Dunmore*. Tam had watched Edmund solve that final turn with the map spread on the tavern floor at Ashfall, watched the letters crawl and rearrange, and Tam had copied down the method afterward from memory — but the memory-key was written in the margins of a page sewn into the lining of his coat.

A coat the inquisitors had taken from him and searched, judging by the state of it when they gave it back. A lining they had surely cut open.

Except Tam had resewn it himself in Rowenna's camp, after Mara joined them, after the night around the fire when they'd all agreed — without ever quite saying it aloud — that anything one of them carried, any of them might lose. So the memory-key wasn't in the coat anymore. It was folded small and swallowed in the only place no searcher would cut: a hollow heel in his left boot, packed with tallow.

All of which meant that Halvene was holding four-fifths of the truth, and the missing fifth was the whole of it.

"Read it for us," she said again.

Tam wet his lips. Made his hands shake a little — that part wasn't acting; fear sat in his bones like cold marrow — and let his eyes go wide and young.

"It's a road cipher," he said. "Ledger-over-song, see? But it's corrupted. The courier bled on it, and the blood ate the key columns." He pointed, tracing lines he knew perfectly well how to truly read. "I can get maybe a third of it. Places. Distances. But the destination — the gate itself — that section's destroyed. Genuinely destroyed. Whoever made this map didn't want it read by anyone the courier didn't choose."

Halvene watched him for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the iron gate opened and closed.

"You're lying," she said, without heat.

"A bit," Tam admitted, and surprised himself with his own recklessness. "But mostly not. Check the worksheet. Run my readings against your own cryptographers. I'll show you exactly how much of it I can do." *Show you a third of a map pointing where I want you to look,* he added silently, because that was the other half of his plan, the half he'd built from the moment they'd put the worksheet in front of him: if they demanded answers, he would give them answers. False ones. A route through the Glass Wastes, east and north, weeks out of their way, toward a dead dwarvish waystation he'd once copied into a margin and never forgotten. Let the usurper's legions march east chasing a ghost while the real road ran south.

Inquisitor Halene studied him the way water studies stone.

"Very well," she said at last, rising. "You'll work here. Under supervision. You will produce everything the damaged sections allow, and nothing else." At the door she paused, and for the first time her level voice bent, just slightly, toward something almost human. "You're good at this, Tam of Corvin's shop. Lying with structure. Most liars lie loose, and they come apart. Yours has load-bearing walls." She shook her head slowly. "That's what makes your kind dangerous. And that's why the Purge was necessary. Good day."

* * *

They gave him a table in a long gallery room, lamplight and guards, and the worksheets, and he worked — genuinely worked, because false readings built on sloppy method would collapse under Halvene's cryptographers the way a bad forgery collapsed under Corvin's loupe. He gave them the Glass Wastes route with immaculate craft, every distance plausible, every waypoint drawn from real geography, and he buried his false turns inside the true ones so deeply that even he sometimes had to check which was which.

It was exhausting in a way he had no name for. Every hour at that table was a performance with his own mind as the stage, a constant doubled awareness: reading truly enough to seem honest, lying thoroughly enough to misdirect, watching Halvene's face in the corner of his eye for the flicker that meant belief. On the sixth day she brought another cryptographer, a soft round man who chewed his lip and found nothing. On the seventh, Halvene said, in front of the guards, "East through the Glass Wastes. It's consistent," and wrote something in her book, and Tam went back to his cell that night feeling lighter than the soup deserved, floating on the smallest, meanest, most magnificent victory of his life.

On the eighth day, they took him to court.

* * *

He hadn't known what to expect of the usurper's palace — something grand and terrible, he supposed, all obsidian and banners. What he got was a barge upriver and then carriages, and a palace of white stone and gilded emptiness, and crowds of courtiers dressed beautifully and speaking softly, and everywhere, everywhere, the sense of a held breath. The servants moved like people walking on ice. The guards' eyes never rested on anything longer than a heartbeat. Even the music, when a string quartet played in an antechamber, was pitched low, apologetic, as though sound itself had been instructed not to presume.

They processed him through in clean clothes — his own, brushed, the boot heels replaced with solid leather, which he noticed with a cold drop in his stomach until he remembered the tallow packet had already traveled elsewhere, sewn days ago into the waistband they'd presumably also missed, because Tam had learned sewing before he'd learned letters and he could hide things in seams the way spiders hid in mortar. Then into a vast hall where the court assembled itself around a throne dais with the fluid, fearful choreography of fish avoiding a shadow.

And there, at the far end, beneath banners bearing a sigil Tam now knew too well — the broken circle, the severed sigil — sat the king.

The mad king. The usurper. Whatever he was.

Tam had imagined monsters. Scales, or smoke, or too many joints. What sat on the throne was a handsome man of perhaps fifty, silver-haired, robed in deep blue, his posture relaxed, one hand resting openly on the arm of the throne. He looked tired, Tam thought first. He looked like a king at the end of a long reign, a grandfather at a wedding.

Then Tam saw his eyes.

They were wrong the way the space behind a painted backdrop is wrong — flat, depthless, the color of a photograph of eyes rather than eyes. They did not track. They did not blink in rhythm. And the smile — the famous gentle smile, the mercy-of-the-Crown smile that was stamped on proclamations all over the realm — sat on his face like something hung there, a mask on a hook, smiling slightly to the left of where anyone was standing. Smiling, Tam realized with a lurch, at *nothing*. At the middle distance. At the empty air beside the throne, where perhaps something only he could see was standing.

*There's nobody home,* Tam thought, and the thought arrived with absolute, sickening clarity. *Or rather — somebody else is home, and he's wearing the house.*

"He's beautiful, isn't he," murmured a courtier near Tam, and Tam turned and found a young woman in silk, wineglass in hand, gazing at the throne with an expression of rapt adoration — and beneath the adoration, in the tight muscles of her jaw, something that was not adoration at all. "He ended the mage-plague. He ended the wars. Seven years of peace."

Seven years of silence, Tam thought. Seven years of Purge.

The king rose, and the whole hall rose with him, a rustle of silk and held breath, and he descended the dais at a slow, ceremonial pace, and Tam's heart slammed against his ribs because the flat eyes were turning, sweeping the crowd, coming closer, closer—

—and stopped on him.

For one long moment, the thing wearing the king looked at Tam. The smile did not change. The eyes did not focus. But Tam felt *seen* anyway, felt it physically, like a thumb pressed to the back of his neck, like standing in a doorway when something vast passes by outside — a pressure displacement, a wrongness in the air. The courtiers nearest Tam stepped subtly away from him, sensing weather.

And then the king passed on, and paused before an old lord, and said in a voice like warm honey poured over gravel: "Lord Aldery. Your daughter's wedding. We are delighted." The smile widened precisely one degree. "Everyone is delighted."

The court exhaled in ragged unison, laughing softly at the royal pleasantry, and the laughter had terror in it, and Tam understood in that moment more about the last seven years than any banned text had taught him. This was why they obeyed. Not armies. Not inquisitors alone. This — the smile at nothing, the delight commanded like a tax, a whole court performing love for a hollow place.

That night, back in his cell in the Salt Prison, Tam did not count drips. He sat cross-legged in the dark with his back against the wall and he thought about the woman in silk with the terror under her adoration, and the guards whose eyes flicked away, and the breathing man in the next cell proving with each slow breath that he still existed. All these people, afraid alone, each certain they were the only one.

*They're not,* he thought. *That's the secret. Nobody's the only one.*

He smiled in the dark — a small, private, sixteen-year-old smile, load-bearing.

* * *

On the eleventh day, Halvene came to his workroom with the leather case, and her face was composed as always, but she set the case down more heavily than usual, and Tam's stomach dropped before she even spoke, because eleven days was fast for legions but not impossibly fast, and her voice, when it came, was still level as a ruled line and aimed like one too.

"The Glass Wastes were empty," she said. "You sent my Crown's army chasing dust, Tam of Corvin's shop. Clever. Load-bearing walls indeed." She opened the case, and inside, instead of worksheets, lay a single sheet of paper, and on it, drawn in a fine precise hand, was the sigil — the true sigil, the unbroken circle — with annotations in Old Tongue, and beneath it, a name.

Edmund Dunmore. Gatekeeper.

"They found this on the body of a knight's squire taken on the south road," Halvene said quietly. "Your friends are three days from the gate, and they are carrying you with them — in here." She tapped the paper. "So we are done pretending you cannot read the whole cipher. And we are done pretending you don't matter." For the first time, her level voice showed him what was underneath it, and underneath it was hunger. "His Radiance wishes to meet the boy who lies for love. He has questions about gates."

Far above, through the high window, Tam heard the horns of the palace sounding noon, bright and clear over the white city — and beneath them, so faint he might have imagined it, the deep grinding note of the legion roads filling with boots, marching south.
