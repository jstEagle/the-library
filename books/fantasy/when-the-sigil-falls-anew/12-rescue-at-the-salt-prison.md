## 12. Rescue at the Salt Prison

The Salt Prison had earned its name honestly. It stood on a spit of gray rock in the harbor of Vellmark's outer bay, a squat fortress of white-streaked stone that wept brine from every crack, so that in summer heat it seemed to sweat and in winter frost bloomed on its walls like some pale, hungry lichen. Rowenna Vale had passed it a thousand times in her years of service — had once, in another life, delivered prisoners to its gates with proper warrants and a clear conscience.

Now she crouched among the fishing nets drying on the shingle beach across the channel, watching gulls wheel over its battlements, and understood with cold clarity that she had never truly known what happened inside those walls.

"They move him at dawn," Mara said beside her. She lay flat against a net post, spyglass to her eye, her brother's old courier coat buttoned to her chin despite the warmth. "Laundry barge. Every third day. Two guards on the deck, six more at the dock gate."

"Tam's not doing laundry."

"No," Mara agreed. "But he's small enough to pass for laundry-adjacent, if the alternative is staying where he is."

Rowenna took the glass and studied the prison herself. The outer walls were nothing — she'd scaled worse drunk on a dare at nineteen. The walls were stone and men, and stone and men could be beaten. It was the light that worried her. Along the ramparts, at even intervals, burned lamps of a color no flame should burn: a deep, bruised violet, steady as stars, casting no smoke. Ward-lamps. She'd seen them only once before, seven years ago, the night the Purge began, when they'd lit them along the palace walls like festival candles gone wrong.

"Those are new," she said. "Someone's spent real coin warding this place."

"Edmund says—"

"I know what Edmund says." She lowered the glass. "I'm telling you what I see."

Edmund stood apart from them, at the tideline where the water chewed at the shingle, and he was not looking at the prison at all. He was looking at his own hands, turning them over as though they belonged to a stranger, and Rowenna felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name. Three days since the sewers of Harrowgate. Three days since Tam had turned back down that flooded corridor with an inquisitor's bootsteps echoing behind him, buying their lives with his freedom. Three days of Edmund growing quieter and quieter, until now he barely spoke above a whisper, as though he were afraid the sea itself might carry his voice to the wrong ears.

"He'll do it," Mara said quietly, following her gaze. "When it comes to it. He knows what it costs."

"That's what frightens me." Rowenna handed back the glass. "A man who's spent seven years hiding doesn't come out of hiding gently. He comes out all at once, like a dam breaking, and dams don't break carefully."

* * *

They crossed the channel in a fisher's skiff borrowed through means Rowenna chose not to examine too closely — Mara had a talent for borrowing things that did not wish to be lent, a talent Rowenna suspected ran in the Kess family alongside stubbornness. The fog came off the water thick and salt-sweet, swallowing the skiff whole, and by the time the hull ground against the prison's dock pilings, the violet lamps were smears of color overhead like bruises through cloth.

Rowenna went first. Old habits: two guards at the dock gate, one bored, one cold, the bored one facing the water because nothing ever came out of the water. Her knife handle found the base of the bored one's skull before he finished turning, and she caught him as he folded, lowering him to the boards with a gentleness that surprised her. Seven years ago she'd have let him drop. The disgraced knight had learned something in disgrace, though she'd be hard-pressed to say what.

The cold one got a knee to the gut and Mara's elbow to the temple, and then they were inside the outer yard, pressed against a wall of sweating stone while a patrol tramped past close enough to touch.

"Cell block's below," Mara breathed. "Water level. That's where they keep the mages. Edmund said the wards would be thickest there."

"Then that's where we go last." Rowenna counted doors in the flickering torchlight. "Records first. Every prison keeps records, and every record tells you which cell holds a boy who can read cipher. We don't kick open forty doors hoping."

"And if the records are warded?"

"Then Edmund earns his keep early."

The records room was exactly where a man who had served in Vellmark's garrison for twelve years knew it would be: behind the armory, where clerks liked to be near soldiers and soldiers liked to be near wine. Rowenna picked the lock with two pins and a patience she did not feel, and inside they found a fat sergeant asleep over a ledger, snores rattling the ink pots. Mara gagged and bound him before Rowenna had finished drawing her knife, and they exchanged a look over his body — *we are getting efficient at this, and someday we will have to answer for what efficiency costs* — and then Rowenna pulled the ledger toward her and ran her finger down the columns.

*Prisoners, special holding.* There it was, in a clerk's careful hand: **BOY, ~16, CIPHER WORK SUSPECTED. WARD-BLOCK, CELL 9. NOTE: SUBJECT COOPERATIVE UNDER QUESTIONING. INQUISITOR HALE REQUESTS CONTINUED ACCESS.**

"Cooperative under questioning," Mara repeated, and her voice had gone very quiet, the way it went quiet right before things broke. "He's sixteen."

"He's alive." Rowenna closed the ledger. "That's the whole of the good news tonight. Let's go collect it."

* * *

The stair down to the ward-block was cut into the living rock beneath the prison, and the air changed halfway down — grew heavy, tasted of copper and lightning, the way air tastes just before a storm breaks. Rowenna had felt that weight once before and never forgotten it. Magic left a residue on the world the way blood left a residue on skin, and this stair was soaked in it.

At the bottom, a corridor of iron-banded doors stretched away into gloom, each door marked with a sigil painted in white salt-paste, each sigil faintly glowing. And between the doors walked a figure in the gray-and-violet robes of the Inquisition, lantern held high, making a slow circuit.

Rowenna pressed Mara flat into an alcove and did the arithmetic she hated: one inquisitor, awake and armed; a rescue that needed minutes they didn't have; a boy behind the ninth door.

"We need a distraction," she whispered.

"We have a distraction." Mara's grin in the dark was all teeth. "We have a whole gatekeeper."

They retreated up three steps and Rowenna found Edmund in the fog-damp dark of the stairwell, and what she saw stopped her words for a moment. He had changed. Not in body — same lean frame, same tired eyes — but something in the way he stood, as though he'd finally set down a weight he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his spine.

"The ward-seals on the cells," he said, without preamble. "You want them open."

"All of them," Rowenna said. "Every door on that block. Let the whole floor empty at once and no one will know which cell we came for."

Edmund looked past her, down toward the glow of the sigils, and Rowenna watched something move behind his eyes — fear, yes, but beneath the fear a kind of terrible homecoming, a man looking at the work he was born for after seven years of pretending to sell love-charms and luck-knots.

"It will announce me," he said. "Gatekeeper's craft isn't quiet. When I break those seals, every inquisitor within a league will feel it, and they'll know my name before I've spoken it. Everything after tonight, I do in the open."

"Good," Mara said fiercely. "Let them know. Let them choke on it."

Edmund smiled — a small, exhausted, genuine smile — and stepped past them both.

What happened next, Rowenna would spend the rest of her life failing to describe properly. She had seen magic in the Purge years, seen mages burn and blast and bleed light, but Edmund did none of that. He simply walked down the corridor with his hands loose at his sides, and the world *rearranged* itself around him. The salt-sigils flared white as he passed each door — recognition, Rowenna thought later, like locks recognizing a master key — and then guttered and died, and the iron doors swung wide on screaming hinges, and prisoners stumbled blinking into the corridor: hollow-cheeked men and women, drained and swaying, but *alive*, alive and free, and the sound they made was not a cheer but something older and stranger, a low collective moan like wind through a canyon.

The inquisitor spun, lantern rising, and Edmund looked at him — just looked — and the violet flame bent sideways, toward Edmund, like a plant toward sun, and went out.

"Gatekeeper," the inquisitor whispered, and there was awe in it, and horror, and Rowenna drove her pommel into the side of his head before either sentiment could become an alarm bell.

Cell nine. Tam sat against the far wall, wrists raw, one eye swollen shut, and when the door swung wide he looked up with his good eye and saw Edmund wreathed in dying ward-light, and his cracked lips split into a grin.

"Took you long enough," he rasped. "I've been *bored*, Ser Rowenna. They kept asking me about the cipher and I kept giving them rubbish. You should've heard me. I invented an entire false reading. There's a fake gate under a mill outside Duncarrie. Very convincing."

"You gave them false readings?" Mara hauled him upright, and her hands shook as she checked him for wounds, rougher than tenderness allowed.

"Mm. Which reminds me." Tam fumbled at his shirt and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, bound against his chest with strips torn from his sleeve. "When they brought me here, they made me copy documents. Evidence. One of them—" he winced as Mara probed a rib "—one of them was old. Older than anything in the archive. A contract. I recognized the seal because I'd spent three nights staring at it on a certain map. So when they weren't looking, I copied it. Every line. Every word. Then I stole the original, because I'm a professional."

Silence in the cell. Even the freed prisoners in the corridor had gone still, listening.

"You stole—" Rowenna stared at him. "The original binding contract?"

"The Inquisition's been carrying it around as evidence for seven years like it was a grocery list." Tam's grin widened, bloody and bright. "Seemed rude to leave it."

Mara made a sound half-laugh, half-sob, and crushed him against her, and Tam endured it with the martyred grace of sixteen-year-olds everywhere, but his hand came up and gripped her sleeve and did not let go.

* * *

They almost made the dock. That was the thing Rowenna would remember afterward — how close it came. Up the rock-cut stair, through the records room where the bound sergeant still snored, across the outer yard under fog-thick dark, Tam limping between Mara and a freed mage who'd found his feet somewhere along the way. The skiff was thirty feet from the pilings when the violet lamps along the ramparts flared all together, blazing now instead of glowing, and a bell began to toll with a voice like cracking ice.

"They know," Edmund said. "Run. Don't fight. *Run.*"

Crossbow bolts hissed out of the fog. Rowenna caught one on her shoulder pauldron and another buried itself in the dock planking at Tam's heel, and then they were in the skiff and pushing off into black water, and the prison's gates vomited gray-robed figures onto the dock, lanterns swinging, shouts ripping through the fog.

And then the water *rose*.

Not waves — a swell, a single smooth lifting of the harbor beneath the skiff, as though the sea had cupped them in a palm, and the boat shot forward, out of crossbow range, riding a current that had no business existing in a sheltered bay. Rowenna looked at Edmund. Edmund stared at his own hands again, but differently now, and his face was wet, and it might have been spray.

"Habit," he said quietly. "Gates open both ways. Water wants to flow."

No one answered. Behind them the Salt Prison burned violet against the fog, and ahead the free water waited, and in the bottom of the skiff Tam clutched his oilcloth bundle like a newborn and grinned at all of them through broken lips.

"So," he said. "Anyone want to hear what the contract actually says? Because I read it twice, in the dark, by feel. And there's a part in the middle there — about what unbinding costs — that I think you're all going to want to sit down for."

The fog swallowed the prison behind them. Rowenna took the tiller and did not look back, and around her, battered and bleeding and hunted from this night forward, her strange little family held on tight as the skiff flew toward the dark shore.
