## 13. The Contract of the Mad King

They did not open the contract until they were three days clear of Harrowgate, holed up in the shell of a shepherd's cot that clung to the hillside like a barnacle to a drowned ship. The roof was half-collapsed, the hearth cold for years, but the walls still stood against the wind, and the road below was empty in both directions — empty the way roads had been empty since the legions marched, which was not the same as safe.

Edmund sat apart from the others, on a broken stool near the eastern window, and watched Rowenna turn the binding contract over in her scarred hands.

She held it the way she would hold a wounded bird. The scroll was old — older than any of them had guessed — its vellum gone the color of weak tea, its edges soft as cloth from centuries of handling. Wax seals hung from it like withered fruit: seven of them, each pressed with a sigil Edmund knew better than his own heartbeat. The gate-seal. His seal. Or rather, the seal he had inherited without ever being told what it truly cost.

"Seven seals," Tam said. He sat cross-legged by the dead hearth, ink-stained fingers hovering an inch above the parchment, afraid to touch. "Seven wardens signed it. That's... that's not how contracts work anymore. Nobody signs seven."

"Nobody signs anything anymore," Rowenna said. "That's rather the problem."

Mara stood at the window, her back to the room, watching the road. She had barely spoken since the Salt Prison. She had carried the contract out through fire and iron, wrapped against her chest beneath her coat, and now that it was here — now that it might hold the answer to everything — she could not make herself look at it. Edmund understood. He had spent seven years not looking at things.

"Read it," she said quietly, still facing the window. "Whatever it says. Read all of it."

Rowenna broke the first seal.

* * *

The language was archaic — high court script, the kind Tam read for bread and Rowenna remembered from a childhood spent in palace corridors — but the contract's opening was plain enough that even Edmund, who had spent his life among charms and small deceptions, felt the words land like stones dropped into still water.

*Let it be known, in this one hundred and ninth year of the Concord, that the crown of Vellmark sits upon no head, but upon a hunger.*

Tam read aloud, because he was the fastest, and because reading gave his voice something to do besides shake. *The king is not mad. The king is devoured. That which wears his face ate first his shadow, and would have eaten the rest of him, and through him the realm entire.*

Edmund's hands went still in his lap.

He thought of the stories. Everyone knew the stories — the Mad King Aldric, whose wits had curdled in his final year, who laughed at nothing, who spoke to empty corners, who was bound away "for his own protection" by seven loyal wardens before the Purge swept the world clean of sorcery. Edmund had grown up on those stories. Every child in Vellmark had. They were the moral at the end of the tale: *this is what magic does to a man. This is why we burned the books.*

But the contract said otherwise. The contract said the madness was never the king's at all. It was a symptom. A feeding.

"The shadow," Edmund heard himself say. His voice sounded far away, like someone else's. "It took his shadow first. That's how these things work. That's—" He stopped. Swallowed. "That's what the ward beneath Vellmark was built to watch. Not the king. The thing that came *out* of him."

"You've known about the ward your whole life," Mara said, turning from the window at last. Her eyes were red-rimmed and hard. "You kept its gate. Why didn't you know *this*?"

"Because gatekeepers keep gates." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and did not try to soften it. "We're given the key and the duty and a list of what happens if we fail. We are not given the *why*. The whys get locked in rooms like this one, in documents like this one, and the people who sign those documents decide that a shopkeeper with a stall full of luck-charms doesn't need to carry the truth around with him." He looked down at his hands. They were trembling. "I guarded a door for seven years and I never once asked what was behind it. I told myself that was discipline."

"It was survival," Rowenna said flatly. "Keep reading, boy. It gets worse."

It got worse.

*The binding cannot be broken from without,* Tam read, and his voice cracked on the words like ice underfoot. *No army may breach it, no mage unravel it, no king command it undone. The seal was made of seven wills joined into one, and only a will freely given can unmake what wills have made.*

"That means nothing," Mara said sharply. "That's lawyer's fog. Freely given — given *what*?"

Tam didn't answer. He was reading ahead. Edmund watched the color drain out of the boy's face the way dye drains out of cloth in rain — gradually, then all at once.

*Tam,* Edmund started, but the boy was already reading, because Tam had never in his life been able to leave a text unfinished, even now, even when every instinct in his body was screaming at him to stop.

*A gatekeeper holds the seal because a gatekeeper is willing to die for it. This is the whole of the office, and always has been. The sigil is anchored in a life offered willingly — not taken, not spent in battle, but laid down at the seal's heart by the keeper's own hand, in knowledge and consent. To unbind the thing below, the gatekeeper must give the anchor back. There is no other key. There is no other way.*

The silence after was absolute. The wind worried the loose shutter. Somewhere down the valley a crow called twice and went quiet.

"No," Mara said. Just that. Once. Quietly. And then again, louder, as if volume could repeal the words: "*No.*"

"There'll be another way," Rowenna said, but her voice was the voice of a woman reading a battlefield before the charge, counting the ground she already knew was lost. She took the scroll from Tam's shaking hands and read the closing clauses herself, slowly, her lips moving. Then she set it down very carefully on the table between them, as if it might go off.

"The original seven," she said. "When they made the binding, they each intended—"

"They each intended to be the sacrifice," Edmund finished. "And none of them were. Because a binding made of seven willing deaths needs only one willing death to hold, and the first of them stepped forward before the others could stop him." He had known this. He realized, with a strange floating calm, that some part of him had always known it — that the reason gatekeepers were chosen solitary, sworn silent, forbidden families, was that you could not ask a man with a daughter to be kindling. "Warden Corvane went first. My predecessor's predecessor's predecessor. And every gatekeeper since has been trained to do the same, if the day ever came. We just weren't told the day had a name."

"Then we find another way," Mara said. She crossed the room in three strides and put her hand flat on the contract, over the words, as if she could smother them. "There's always another way. Tam, tell me there's another way. You've read more forbidden texts than anyone alive. Tell me."

Tam looked at the floor. "The cipher in the map," he said miserably. "The courier's map. I worked it out in the prison — I never told anyone because I thought I'd misread it. The last line isn't coordinates. It's..." He swallowed. "It's a warning. *The key is a keeper.* I thought it meant Edmund needed to be there to open the gate. I didn't—"

"You didn't know," Edmund said gently.

"I didn't *want* to know." The boy's voice broke. "There's a difference."

Edmund rose from the stool. His legs felt distant, borrowed, like the legs of a man in someone else's dream. He walked to the window where Mara stood and looked out at the gray road, and he found that his mind — his careful, shopkeeper's mind, the mind that had spent seven years pricing charms and smiling at inquisitors — had already begun doing what it always did. Organizing. Preparing. Making lists.

*Tell no one at the stall. Settle accounts. Leave the good scissors to the cobbler's boy.*

He had written a will in his head a dozen times over seven years, always as a joke, a tic, a superstition. Now it assembled itself, unbidden, clause by clause, and he understood that it had never been a joke at all.

"Listen to me," he said, and turned to face them — Mara with her fury, Tam with his guilt, Rowenna with her terrible soldier's clarity. "Nothing is decided tonight. We have a contract, we have a route, and we have perhaps two weeks before the legions reach the old gate. What matters now is getting there before they do, and seeing what state the sigil is truly in. The contract is seven hundred years old. Bindings fail. Wards rot. Maybe the thing below has already eaten through so much of the seal that the terms don't matter anymore." He smiled, and was privately appalled at how easily it came — the same smile he'd sold ten thousand charms behind. "Maybe the lawyers' fog works both ways."

Mara searched his face. She was the sister of a courier; she had grown up reading people the way Tam read scripts, and Edmund saw her catch something — a flicker, a hesitation, the ghost of a tell — before he smoothed it away.

"Edmund," she said slowly.

"We should sleep," he said. "Rowenna takes first watch. Tomorrow we climb."

* * *

He did not sleep.

He sat by the cold hearth while the others settled into their bedrolls — Rowenna with her sword across her knees even in sleep, Tam curled tight as a comma, Mara on her side facing the door, always the door — and he fed the fire a little at a time, and he thought.

The horror, he discovered, was not sharp. He had expected sharp. He had expected the terror of the inquisitor's knock, the white panic of the night Wren the courier died in his back room with blood on his hands and a map against his chest. But this was not that. This was quiet. This was the horror of a ledger balancing itself — of a debt he had, in truth, been carrying since the day he'd accepted the gatekeeper's oath at nineteen years old, kneeling in the dark beneath Vellmark with an old man's hand on his head and a candle burning in a place where candles should not burn.

*Do you accept the keeping?*

*I accept the keeping.*

*All of it?*

*All of it.*

He had thought *all* meant long nights and loneliness. He had been nineteen. Nineteen-year-olds think *all* means loneliness.

He looked at the others sleeping. Tam, who had left his master to the raiders and still woke some mornings apologizing to a print shop. Mara, who had every right to hate him and had instead chosen to walk beside him toward whatever end. Rowenna, who had traded a knight's honor for a bottle and was trading the bottle, sip by sip, for a second chance she didn't believe she deserved.

*Their lives,* the thing in the crown had promised him once, in a vision he'd never confessed. *Give me your magic and they live.* And he had refused, because a bargain with that thing was just another binding, and bindings were how the world had gotten into this mess. But the contract offered something cleaner. Something honest. His death, freely given, bought the true king back and unmade the usurper entire — and no bargain, no corruption, no shadow in the fine print. Seven hundred years of wardens had agreed on that if nothing else.

And after — well. After, there would be no Edmund Dunmore to stand in Low Market selling luck to people who needed more than luck. But there would be a king awake, and free cities unbowed, and Tam's press running, and Rowenna teaching children to hold a sword line, and Mara — Mara going wherever couriers went when the roads were finally safe, carrying letters instead of maps that bled.

It was a fair trade. He turned the thought over and over like a coin, checking for the false ring, and could not find it. That was the horror. Not the dying. How *reasonable* the dying was.

Near midnight, Mara stirred. She sat up without a sound — couriers' sisters learned to move silently — and looked across the embers at him, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"You're doing it," she whispered. "The thing where you decide something and arrange everyone else's feelings around it like furniture."

"I'm resting," he said.

"You're arranging." Her voice stayed low, but it had an edge that could open letters. "I've watched you do it since Harrowgate. Since the prison. You smile differently when you've already decided. You smiled like that when you told us maybe the terms don't matter." She leaned forward. The firelight caught the grief in her face and the fury underneath it, and both of them were for him. "My brother died carrying that map to *you*. He chose you, Edmund. Out of every gatekeeper, every mage, every soul in this rotten realm — he bled his way across three provinces because he believed you were the man who would fix this *and live*. If you take that belief away from me too, I swear by every god the Purge didn't burn—"

"Mara." His voice was gentle. Gentle was the cruelest thing he had available. "Sleep. Tomorrow we climb."

She held his eyes a moment longer — hunting the lie, finding the truth, hating it — and lay back down. But she did not close her eyes, and neither did he, and when dawn came gray and thin through the broken shutters, they were all four awake, and none of them said so.

* * *

They climbed.

The old gate lay in the shoulder of a mountain two days north, and the road to it was a wound: villages burned, fields salted, waystones toppled by legion engineers so that travelers would lose their way and their hope in equal measure. Rowenna led them off the road entirely, following a drover's track that switchbacked up through pine and scree, and on the second morning they came around a granite spur and saw it.

The gate.

Even from a mile distant, Edmund felt it in his teeth. Every gatekeeper did; it was the one inheritance that never needed explaining. A doorway cut into the mountain's living rock, framed in stone older than the kingdom, and across its face — the sigil. Seven interlocking rings, each carved a fathom deep, each once blazing with ward-light visible from the valleys below. Pilgrims had come for generations just to see it glow.

Six of the rings were dark. The seventh guttered like a lamp out of oil.

And around the mountain's base, tiny with distance, the tents of an army had already begun to bloom — black and gold, patient as mold.

"They're early," Rowenna breathed. "Gods' teeth, they're *early*. The legions aren't due here for days."

"They were never marching to a battle," Edmund said quietly. His hand had risen, without his permission, to the hollow of his own throat, where the gatekeeper's mark slept beneath his skin. It was warm. It had never been warm before. "They were marching to a funeral."

Behind him, Tam unrolled the map with hands that shook, and Mara drew her knife, and the seventh ring of the sigil flickered once, twice —

— and Edmund felt it, faint and unmistakable, like a voice through a wall:

*Come in, keeper. You're expected.*
