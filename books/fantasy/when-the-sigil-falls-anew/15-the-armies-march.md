## 15. The Armies March

Rowenna Vale had spent twenty years learning to read armies the way other people read weather, and what she saw from the ridge above the Ashfall road made her stomach drop like a stone into a well.

Fires. Not campfires — those scattered and flickered, casual as stars. These burned in lines. Long, disciplined rows of them stretching east along the river valley until they vanished into the haze where the land fell away toward Vellmark, each one a beacon in the dark like a rosary of embers laid out across the throat of the world. The Crown's legions did not make campfires. They made signals.

"They're not stopping," she said to herself, though her voice was swallowed by the wind off the ridge before it could even reach her own ears properly. She crouched lower behind the broken wall of a shepherd's cot, one that had stood through three wars and had finally been put down by this one, its stones tumbled like teeth knocked loose. Below, the column moved without pause. Wagons. Pikes. The glint of lamplight on helms, thousands upon thousands of them, flowing past the burning fields of Ashfall Province like blood through a vein.

The pact was dead, then.

She'd known it would come — had felt it coming for days, the way an old hound feels weather in its bones — but knowing and seeing were different animals entirely. The Free Cities' Pact had been signed in Harrowgate's council hall with wax and ceremony and the particular solemnity of men who intended to break it the moment it became inconvenient. Rowenna had watched the councilors sign it. She had watched their eyes while they signed. She had told Edmund then: *Don't trust men who look at the door when they swear an oath.* But hope is a stubborn thing in people who have nothing else left to carry, and so they had hoped, and now the legions were marching, and every city east of the mountains would learn what the Crown's gratitude looked like.

She counted by habit. Old discipline, drilled into her by commanders whose names she no longer let herself speak. Four columns visible, maybe five, moving in relay so the road never emptied. Supply trains heavy with siege equipment — she could tell by the way the wagons sat deep-rutted in the mud, by the teams of eight oxen hauling them. Ballistae. Siege ladders. And worse, if she knew the inquisitors' craft at all, and she did: ward-breakers. Machines designed not to breach walls but to unmake the magic in them. She had seen one used once, years ago, on a rebel holdfast in the Marches. The stones hadn't fallen. They had simply *forgotten* how to be a fortress, crumbling into gravel at a touch.

They weren't marching to besiege the gate. They were marching to erase it.

"Right," she muttered, and pulled back from the ridge.

* * *

The ride back took most of the night. She went the long way, doubling across a ford and again through a stand of blackened pines where the fire from two summers past had never been cleared, because a knight learns early that the direct route is the one your enemies watch. Her horse — a rangy gray mare she'd bought in Harrowgate with coin she still half-felt guilty about taking from the council's cowardly purse — picked her way through the ash with the sure-footed patience Rowenna had come to rely on more than she liked to admit.

She thought about the others as she rode. She found herself doing that more and more lately, thinking of them as *the others*, as if she were a scout reporting to some garrison instead of one quarter of a ragged band of fugitives hiding in dwarven ruins. Edmund, who carried the weight of the world in his hands and had stopped pretending he didn't. Mara, all edges and grief sharpened into purpose, who watched Rowenna now with something that was almost trust and was certainly not forgiveness — not yet. Tam, sixteen years old and already carrying scars from a Salt Prison cell, who flinched at raised voices and re-inked sigil lines with a steadiness that shamed grown soldiers. And Tam was still healing. That was the thing Rowenna couldn't stop circling. The boy had given them three days in that prison, three days of the inquisitors' questions, feeding them false cipher readings with a liar's calm, and he had come out thinner and quieter and with a cough that wouldn't leave him, and every time Rowenna saw him wrap his hands against the cold she felt the old shame rise in her throat like bile.

She had failed kings before. It was, apparently, her particular talent. But she would be damned — thoroughly, inventively damned — before she failed these ones.

The ruin appeared out of the darkness around midnight: a dwarven waystation buried in the mountainside, its arched entrance half-swallowed by rockfall, older than any kingdom currently claiming to matter. A thin thread of smoke rose from within, banked low. She gave the signal — two short whistles, a pause, one long — and heard Tam's answering whistle crack slightly in the middle. Nervous. He'd be watching the entrance. He always watched the entrance now.

Inside, the waystation opened into a vaulted hall where centuries of dust lay undisturbed except along the paths they had worn themselves. Edmund sat near the fire with the binding contract spread open on a flat stone before him, reading it for perhaps the hundredth time, his lips moving slightly over passages he already knew by heart. He didn't look up when she came in. He had stopped looking up at entrances too, since the prison. Since he had torn open the Salt Prison's wards with the full force of what he was and shown every inquisitor in three provinces exactly what a gatekeeper looked like when he stopped hiding.

Mara was awake, of course. Mara slept the way cats do, in shallow intervals, one ear always cocked. She was sitting against the far wall with her brother's map across her knees, sharpening a knife that had not needed sharpening for days. She looked up. Read Rowenna's face in one glance, the way she read everything — fast, hungry, braced for the worst.

"How many?" she asked.

"That many," Rowenna said, and shrugged off her cloak, and told them.

* * *

She told it plainly, because plainly was the only honest way to tell it. The legions were three days out from the mountain passes, two if they pushed, and they were pushing — she'd seen the supply wagons running light, the pace of a column that had somewhere to be and permission to break itself getting there. The pact cities hadn't fallen yet; they'd simply stopped existing as anything the Crown needed to fear. Negotiating parties had gone out from Harrowgate under white banners. None had come back. The Council that had promised them aid, that had sworn the pact with wax and seals and solemn faces, had folded like wet paper the moment the first legion crossed the border stone.

"So it's just us," Tam said. He was sitting cross-legged near the fire, a stub of charcoal pencil behind his ear and ink stains up both forearms to the elbow, permanent now, part of him. His voice was steady. His hands, Rowenna noticed, were not quite.

"It was always going to be just us," Mara said. "Institutions don't save people. I learned that in Harrowgate." There was no bitterness in it anymore, or rather the bitterness had been worked into her like dye into cloth, part of the fabric now, no longer bleeding. "People save people."

Edmund closed the contract carefully, aligning its edges, a small precise gesture that Rowenna had learned meant his thoughts were churning. "Three days," he said. "The sigil's failing — we've all seen it. Every day the ward-lines bleed a little more. If they reach the gate before the unbinding is done—"

"Then the thing wearing the king gets what it wants," Rowenna finished. "A dead binding and a realm with no mages left to resist it." She crouched by the fire and held her palms toward the coals, though her hands weren't cold. "There's more. I scouted the approach roads before I came back. All three passes are picketed — light screens, scouts, signal fires. We can't go around. But that's not the worst of it."

She made herself say it evenly. "The inquisitors have dug up the outer ward-stones."

The silence that followed had weight to it, like the pressure change before a storm.

"The standing stones," Edmund said slowly. "On the ridge above the gate. The outer ring." He was very pale suddenly; even the firelight couldn't warm him. "Those aren't markers. Those are the first line of the seal itself. My predecessors set them four hundred years ago. They bleed power into the sigil — slow, constant. They're why the binding has lasted this long after—" He stopped. Swallowed. "After everything else was allowed to rot."

"I know," Rowenna said quietly. "I grew up in sight of those stones. Every child in those valleys did. We thought they were just old rocks." She laughed, short and joyless. "Turns out my whole childhood was spent playing at the feet of a prison wall and nobody told me."

"Where are they?" Mara asked. Sharp. Always sharp. "The stones. Did they move them, destroy them—"

"Hauled them out with oxen teams. I found the socket-holes, six of them, empty. Drag-marks heading down the valley toward the army's baggage train." Rowenna met Edmund's eyes. "They know what they are. They're not destroying the seal, Edmund. They're *dismantling* it. Piece by piece, careful as surgeons. They want it open — controlled, timed, on their terms, when the army's in position. Whatever comes out of that gate, they mean to meet it with ballistae and ward-breakers and whatever the thing in the crown actually is."

Tam had gone white. "Then we have less than three days. If they keep pulling stones—"

"We have less than three days," Rowenna agreed. "Maybe less than two."

Edmund stood. He walked to the mouth of the passage where the night showed as a ragged grey seam between fallen stones, and he stood there a long moment with his back to them, and Rowenna watched the set of his shoulders and recognized it, because she had worn that same set once, the night she'd decided to ride alone into a burning palace to warn a king everyone else had given up on. It was the posture of a man doing arithmetic with his own life.

"No," she said.

He turned. "I haven't said anything."

"You're about to. You're about to say you'll go ahead tonight, alone, start the ritual early, take the burden off everyone else. I can see it forming in your face like weather." She rose to her full height, which put her nearly eye to eye with him, and pointed one finger at his chest. "And I'm telling you now, gatekeeper, that if you try it, Mara will hunt you down, Tam will follow Mara, and I will follow Tam, and we'll arrive mid-ritual and ruin everything, and then we'll all die together in a hole in a mountain, which is a stupid ending and I refuse it."

For a heartbeat, something crossed Edmund's face that might have been a laugh trying to happen. Then it settled into exhaustion, and honesty. "Every door keeps closing, Rowenna."

"Aye," she said. "But not all of them. One's still open. One has to be." She turned to the others. "So here's how it is. We move tonight. Through the tunnels — Tam, can you read the dwarf-marks well enough to keep us off the collapsed galleries?"

"Better than the inquisitors can," Tam said. There was a flicker of the old spark there, the print-shop apprentice's pride. "Their maps are copies of copies. Mine are original work."

"Mara — I need you on rear guard. Anything follows us into those tunnels, I want to know before it knows us." Mara nodded, already rolling the map. "And Edmund—" She hesitated, because the next part was hard, because the next part was the truth she'd been chewing on the whole ride home. "When we get to the gate, you begin the unbinding. That's your work, and nobody else can do it. But you do it with us there. You do it with all of us. Whatever the contract says about sacrifice—" She saw him flinch and pressed on before he could speak. "Whatever it says, we'll find the truth of it together. That's not sentiment. That's tactics. A gatekeeper alone is one point of failure. A family isn't."

The word hung in the smoky air of the ruined hall. *Family.* She'd said it without meaning to, without deciding to, and now it stood there among them like a lit candle, impossible to take back, and none of them tried.

Edmund looked at each of them in turn. At last he nodded, once, the smallest motion, and began folding the contract away with careful hands.

"Then we march," he said.

* * *

They broke camp within the hour, moving by the grey pre-dawn light that made the mountain slopes look like the surface of a drowned moon. Rowenna took point, as she always did, and paused once at the tunnel mouth to look back the way she'd come — south and east, toward the river valley where the fires still burned in their disciplined, patient lines, and beyond them, faint as a memory, the direction of a throne room where something hollow wore a crown and smiled at nothing.

Let it smile, she thought. Let it march. It had eaten a king's shadow and called itself eternal. But it had never sat in a dockside den buying drinks for strangers, never stood in a print shop's doorway at dawn, never buried a brother and chosen to walk on anyway. It understood thrones and seals and the arithmetic of power.

It did not understand them. And Rowenna Vale, disgraced, bannerless, twenty years late for every battle that mattered, had finally found the one fight worth being on time for.

She ducked into the dark of the dwarven tunnels, and behind her the mountain swallowed the last of the sky.

Two days. Maybe less.

They would have to be enough.
