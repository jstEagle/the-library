## 18. The Battle of the Broken Ward

Mara had held a sword exactly twice in her life before this one.

The first time had been in her brother's back garden, when they were children and Dain had stolen their father's practice blade and let her swing it at a straw target until her arms shook. The second time had been three days ago, in the dark of the dwarven tunnels, when Rowenna pressed a short blade into her hands and said, *You won't be fighting. You'll be running. But if anything stops you running, put this in it and keep moving.*

Now she crouched behind a fallen ward-stone the size of an ox, the third time's weight heavy in her grip, and watched an army come down the mountainside like a landslide given orders.

They came in ranks of gray and black, torchlight rippling along spear-points, the Crown's sunburst stamped on every shield — but the sigil on those shields was wrong, she could see it even from here, even through the haze of dust and fear. The rays of the sun bent inward instead of outward, curling like fingers closing around something. Around everything. Tam had seen it first, hours ago, when he'd studied the captured contract by candlelight and gone pale as parchment. *That's not the king's seal,* he'd said. *That's what ate him.*

"Positions!" Rowenna's voice cut across the cavern mouth like a whip-crack, and Mara flinched, then hated herself for flinching.

The tunnel mouth opened onto a shelf of broken stone above the gate-chamber — the old gate beneath the mountain, half-corrupted, its sigil-lines crawling with a darkness that moved like oil on water. Edmund was down there already, kneeling at the heart of the pattern, his hands bare against the stone, his lips moving in words too old for any tongue Mara knew. The light that gathered around him was faint and gold and trembling, a candle-flame in a wind that wanted very much to snuff it.

And between that flame and the army, there were only four people.

*Three,* Mara corrected herself grimly, tightening her grip on the blade. She counted as family now. That was the whole cursed point.

Rowenna stood at the narrowest part of the approach — a choke-point where the dwarven corridor spilled out onto the shelf, wide enough for three soldiers abreast and nothing more. The disgraced knight had stripped off her tabard, bound her hair back, and planted herself in that gap like she'd been grown there. Her shield was old, her armor older, and her expression was the one Mara had learned to read over these weeks of travel: the flat, patient stillness of a woman who had decided exactly how much of herself she was willing to spend, and found the number sufficient.

Tam was further down, along the outer ring of the gate-sigil, where the corrupted lines had burned themselves away to bare grooves. He knelt with an ink-horn clamped between his teeth, a bundle of reed pens thrust through his belt, and the original binding contract spread beside him weighted with stones. Every few moments he'd lean forward, dip a pen, and lay a line of restored ink into the channel of the sigil — and where the ink touched, the darkness recoiled with a hiss like water on hot iron, and gold light would run along the pattern a hand-span, a foot, before guttering out again.

It wasn't enough. Everyone knew it wasn't enough. It only had to be enough until Edmund finished.

"They're forming up," Rowenna called without turning her head. She had eyes like a hawk and ears like a cat and nerves like bedrock, and Mara had stopped wondering how she did it. "Two hundred, maybe more. They'll send skirmishers first to test the gap, then the column. When the column comes—"

"When the column comes, we'll be dead," Tam said around his pen, not looking up. "Yes, you've mentioned."

"—then someone will need to buy the others time," Rowenna finished, in the same tone she might have used to discuss the weather. "Mara. The contract."

Mara's stomach dropped. She'd known this was coming since the plan had been made around a dwarf-forged lamp two nights ago, but knowing and hearing were different animals. "I know my part."

"The thing wearing the king wants that paper more than it wants our blood," Rowenna said. "Edmund needs it to complete the unbinding — the contract is the binding's root, and you can't pull a root without the plant. If we fall, if I fall, *especially* if I fall, you take the contract and you get down that shelf and you put it in his hands, and you don't stop for anything. Not for me. Not for Tam. Not for—" She paused, and for just a moment the hawk-gaze flicked toward the glowing figure at the sigil's heart, and something old and unguarded crossed her face before the stillness closed over it again. "Not for anyone."

"Not for anyone," Mara repeated. It tasted like ash.

She thought of Dain, bleeding out in a Vellmark alleyway with a map pressed into a stranger's hands, buying a life he'd never meet with the last of his own. She had come all this way hating Edmund Dunmore for surviving when her brother hadn't, and somewhere between Ashfall and Harrowgate and the Salt Prison, the hatred had transmuted into something she didn't have a name for yet — something that looked suspiciously like the reason Dain had died. He'd believed the realm was worth saving. He'd believed a stranger with careful eyes and hidden magic was worth saving. Mara had spent seven years learning to hate, and these last months had taught her, against her every objection, to hope.

She was damned if she'd waste either.

The first horns sounded.

* * *

They came out of the dark like beetles from under a rotten log — twenty skirmishers in the vanguard, moving low and fast, no torches now, only moonlight off drawn steel. Rowenna met them alone.

What happened in the next ninety seconds, Mara would remember for the rest of her life in fragments: the ring of steel, the grunt of impact, the terrible economy of Rowenna Vale's movements — no flourish, no wasted motion, each strike placed like a signature on a confession. Two men went down clutching their legs. A third took a shield-boss to the teeth and folded. The gap was narrow enough that they could only come at her two at a time, and she used it ruthlessly, stepping inside their reach before their spears could find their rhythm, turning their numbers into a crowd that got in each other's way.

But twenty was twenty, and even two at a time adds up. By the time the skirmishers broke — six dead or crippled in the gap, the rest scrambling back up the slope — Rowenna was breathing hard, and there was a red line seeping down her left forearm where a spear-tip had kissed her.

"You're hurt," Mara called.

"I'm *annoyed*." Rowenna rolled her shoulder, reset her feet. "Save your nursing for after."

Down at the sigil's heart, the gold light had strengthened. Edmund's voice had risen from a murmur to something audible, rhythmic, the cadence of a litany — and where his words fell, the corrupted lines writhed. Mara could see the shadow in the stone fighting back now, could see the way the darkness pooled and thickened wherever the gold passed, like a bruise defending itself. And beneath the litany, fainter, almost lost under the wind and the distant army-noise, she heard another sound.

Singing. No — chanting. From the enemy lines.

"They're doing something," Tam shouted. He'd worked his way three stations down the sigil-ring, laying ink as fast as his frozen fingers would allow. His hands were shaking — from cold or fear or both — but his lines were perfect. Mara didn't know how they were perfect. She suspected it was because they had to be. "Every time they chant, the corruption spreads faster. Look — look at the seventh station, it's *moving*—"

He was right. The darkness at the seventh station of the outer ring had begun to crawl, eating backward along the groove Tam had just inked, swallowing his gold light like a tide coming in. And past the shelf, past the army, Mara saw them: a circle of figures in white-and-gray robes, standing apart from the soldiery, arms raised, voices rising in unison. Inquisitors. Dozens of them, and behind them, at the center of the formation, something larger — a palanquin or a throne carried on the shoulders of eight men, curtained in black silk that moved without wind.

The usurper had come to watch its binding defended in person.

"More ink," Tam gasped. "I need more ink, the corruption's drinking it faster than I can—" His ink-horn scraped empty against the stone. He stared into it, tipped it upside-down. One dark drop fell. "No. No, no, *no*—"

"Contract," Mara said, and her voice surprised her by being steady. "Can you draw from it? The blood-ink?"

Tam's head snapped up. For a heartbeat he looked at her like she'd suggested setting fire to the mountain. Then his expression changed — the forger's apprentice calculating, the boy who'd copied forbidden texts for bread money seeing the shape of the forbidden text itself. "It's the *original*. The binding's written in the usurper's own stolen power — if I dilute it, extend it, use it as pigment—"

"Will it work?"

"It might destroy the contract. It might destroy *me*. It might—"

"TAM." Rowenna's voice, cracking across the shelf like a thunderclap. "The column. *Column!*"

Mara turned and her heart seized. They'd stopped testing. Two hundred men in tight formation, shields locked, spears bristling like a hedgehog's spine, advancing on the gap with the slow inevitability of a glacier. Behind them, the chant of the inquisitors rose to a fever pitch, and the darkness in the sigil-lines surged in answer, gouting up out of the stone like black smoke given weight.

"Do it," Mara said to Tam. "Whatever it costs. *Do it.*"

* * *

Later, Mara could never say how long the battle lasted. Time went strange in the melee, stretching and snapping like taffy, measured not in minutes but in discrete and terrible images.

Rowenna in the gap, a wall of scarred steel and stubbornness, taking hits that would have felled an ox and giving back worse. She fought differently now than she had against the skirmishers — slower, heavier, spending herself like a miser forced at last to open the vault. Mara saw her take a spear through the meat of her shoulder and *keep fighting*, saw her hook a man's ankle and drop him onto his comrades' blades, saw her roar — actually roar, wordless, animal — as the column pressed her step by step back toward the shelf's edge.

Tam at the fifth station, the contract open before him, a brush made from his own cut hair working diluted blood-ink into the sigil-lines. The gold light that followed his strokes was different from Edmund's — thinner, stranger, shot through with threads of red — but it *held*, and where it held, the corruption screamed. She could hear it screaming, a sound below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, and she could see what it cost him: each line he laid seemed to take something out of him, color draining from his face stroke by stroke, until he looked less like a living boy than like one of his own unfinished sketches.

And Edmund at the center, rising slowly to his feet, arms spreading, the litany climbing toward some final crescendo — and the whole sigil blazing now, half gold and half black, a coin mid-toss between dawn and midnight, waiting to see which way it would land.

Which left the middle distance. Which left fifty yards of open, burning, screaming ground between the contract's resting place and the gatekeeper's hands.

*Mara. The contract.*

She ran.

It should have been suicide. She knew that even as her boots hit the stone. Fifty yards of open ground in the middle of a battle, carrying the single most valuable object on the continent — every soldier on that slope had a spear, and every inquisitor had a reason to want her dead. But the melee had a shape, and grief had taught Mara Kess to read shapes. The column had funneled into the gap after Rowenna; the flank was thin, held by a handful of skirmishers who'd circled wide and were watching the inquisitors' ritual more than the battlefield, waiting for orders that never came because the thing in the palanquin was watching *Edmund*, and everything else was scenery.

She went through them like a rumor. One man grabbed for her and got a boot to the knee. Another raised a spear and hesitated — a heartbeat of confusion, a boy's face under a borrowed helmet, *don't think about it, don't you dare think about it* — and that heartbeat was all she needed. The contract was in its satchel against her chest, and the satchel was soaked with Tam's blood where he'd gripped it, and she ran with her brother's ghost at her shoulder and the taste of iron in her throat.

An arrow took her in the side at forty yards.

She didn't even break stride — that came later, the pain arriving like late news from a distant province. At thirty yards the chanting reached a crescendo and the corruption in the sigil erupted, a wave of black rolling outward from the seventh station, and Tam — brave, foolish, magnificent Tam — threw himself bodily across the ninth station with his arms spread, shielding the freshly inked lines with his own body, and the black smoke broke around him like surf around a rock, and he *screamed*, high and young, and stayed.

At twenty yards, Rowenna fell.

Mara saw it happen in the corner of her eye and her whole body tried to turn, every instinct screaming to go back, to help, to do *something* — but Dain's voice was in her ear, steady as ever: *Finish what you started, little sister. Don't you dare stop for anyone.* Rowenna had gone down under three spears, her shield split, her back against the shelf's edge — but even falling, even dying, the knight had chosen her ground well. The column poured past her into the open shelf, streaming toward the sigil, toward Edmund, toward victory—

—and Mara Kess, bleeding, limping, seventeen yards out, did the single most reckless thing she had ever done in a life that had lately been composed entirely of reckless things.

She screamed the usurper's name — the false name, the crown-name, the name the heralds used — at the top of her lungs, and she held the satchel high overhead so that every eye on that battlefield could see the shape of it, could understand what was running toward the gate unopposed.

It worked better than she dreamed. Heads turned. Spears wavered. The inquisitors' chant stuttered as a dozen robed figures spun toward her — and in that stutter, that single dropped beat, the corruption faltered, and Tam's ninth station blazed, and Edmund Dunmore opened his eyes at the heart of the gate and saw her coming.

"MARA!" His voice cracked across the chamber like the world splitting. "THROW IT!"

She didn't throw it. Seventeen yards was too far, and the contract was too precious, and she was a courier's sister and couriers did not throw the mail. She ran the last of it through a hail of shouting and grasping hands, took a spear-graze across her hip that would leave a scar to match the arrow-wound, and slid the last five yards on her knees through dust and blood, crashing into the sigil's inner ring hard enough to knock the breath from her body.

Edmund caught her before she hit the stone. His hands were burning-hot and trembling, and his face — gods, his face, streaked with sweat and gold-light and something that looked terribly like tears.

"You came back," he said. "You were supposed to run."

"We're done running," Mara gasped, and pressed the contract into his hands. "All of us. Finish it."

And Edmund Dunmore, gatekeeper, charm-seller, the last man who remembered why the gate was built — looked down at the contract, and up at the battlefield where his family was bleeding for him, and made whatever decision he had been carrying alone since the night he'd read the binding's price.

"No," he said softly. "We finish it together."

Above them, the black silk curtains of the palanquin parted, and the thing wearing the king's face stepped down onto the battlefield, smiling at nothing, and began to walk toward the gate.
