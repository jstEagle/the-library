## 22. A New Stall in Low Market

The morning light came down into Low Market the way it always had — slantwise, through the gap between the chandler's roof and the old bell tower, catching the dust above the cobbles and turning it briefly to gold before the crowds trampled it into something ordinary again. Edmund Dunmore had watched that light from this same corner for seven years of his life, and then lost it for one year of exile and war, and now stood in it a third time with a crate of charms on his hip and no reason at all to hide.

It still felt strange. He suspected it would feel strange for a long while yet.

He set the crate down on the new stall — Tam had built it for him, which meant it was slightly crooked and utterly indestructible — and began laying out his wares. That was the difference, he reminded himself, arranging a row of small glass vials that caught the sun and threw tiny moving lights across the worn wood. This was the difference. Every one of these was real.

A warming stone that would keep a cellar from freezing until spring. A salve of comfrey and quiet enchantment that drew splinters and soothed burns. A luck-charm that was honest about being only a small nudge against the world's indifference, priced accordingly. And in a locked case at the back, because he was not a fool even in peacetime: three warding sigils of his own design, each one keyed to a single household, each one strong enough to turn a knife or a stray curse. The Crown's new Office of Chartered Magic had inspected them in the spring, and a nervous young clerk with ink-stained fingers had stamped them *licensed* with hands that shook until Edmund had made him tea and shown him how the trick of it worked.

*Licensed.* Edmund turned the word over sometimes at night, the way a man turns a coin from a country he never expected to visit.

"Master Dunmore!" called Maren the fishwife from two stalls down, where she'd held ground since before the Purge and intended to hold it until the sun burned out. "You've moved my morning fog again. I can smell it from here."

"That's the rosemary charm," Edmund called back. "It's supposed to do that."

"It's supposed to stay on *your* table."

He grinned and leaned over to nudge the little clay dish back toward his own stall, and the rosemary-and-rain smell of it drifted obediently home, and Maren pretended not to smile, and the market went on around them all — carts and cries and gulls wheeling up from the harbor, a city that had learned to be a city again. Somewhere down the row a child was laughing about nothing. Somewhere above, the great cracked bell of Vellmark rang the half-hour with its familiar broken voice, repaired by decree of the king but never re-tuned, because the king had said — and this had made the whole court stare — *some things should be allowed to keep their scars.*

Edmund had liked him immediately for that.

The first customer of the day was a boy of perhaps nine, who approached the way children approach anything interesting: sideways, pretending interest in something else, orbiting slowly closer until pretense gave out entirely.

"Are you really a mage?" the boy demanded, arriving at last at the front of the stall with the bluntness of his age.

"I really am."

"My gran says mages burned the harvest wards in Ashfall Province and that's why there was a war."

"Your gran is half right, which is more than most grans manage." Edmund crouched to be at eye level and picked up a simple charm — a wooden bead strung on leather, threaded through with the faintest suggestion of warmth, enough to keep hands comfortable on a cold night. "There were bad people who used magic badly, yes. There were also bad people who used swords badly, and nobody banned swords. Here." He pressed the bead into the boy's palm and closed the small fingers over it. "For your gran. Tell her it's an apology for the half she got wrong."

The boy stared at the bead, then at Edmund, then fled with the treasure clutched to his chest as though the whole street might try to take it. Edmund watched him go and felt the old ache move through him — the ache that had lived in him since Ashfall, since the drained child whose fever had broken under his hands in a burned farmhouse — but it was a different ache now. It had cooled into something he could carry. Grief does that, if you let it, instead of hiding it behind a shopkeeper's smile for seven years until it goes septic.

He straightened and looked down the length of Low Market, to where the crooked sign of the Marchetti Street print shop swung gently in the sea wind — *TAM & WREN, PRINTERS AND BOOKBINDERS*, and beneath it in smaller letters, *ciphers decoded, documents fair-copied, pamphlets printed honestly.* Tam had insisted on the last part. Wren, his partner in the enterprise and a girl with a printer's ink stain permanently on one cheekbone, had insisted on the ampersand, saying every good business needed one, and Tam had gone scarlet to the ears and agreed.

And beyond the print shop, loud even at this hour with the crack of wooden practice swords and Rowenna Vale's unmistakable bellow — *"Elbow DOWN, you disaster, were you raised in a barn?"* — stood the training hall. The Crown had offered her a commission. The Crown had offered her a generalship, in fact, and a place at court, and Rowenna had considered all of it for precisely the length of time it took her to finish her ale, and then she had asked instead for a building near the harbor with a dirt floor and room for anyone who wanted to learn to hold a line. She taught soldiers, and she taught dockworkers, and she taught merchant daughters whose fathers wanted them able to say no convincingly. On the sign above the door she had painted, in her own terrible hand: *VALE'S HALL — NO BANNERS REQUIRED.*

Edmund had wept when he first read it, quietly, standing in the street like a fool. He'd told Tam it was the sea wind.

* * *

Tam arrived mid-morning with ink to his elbows and news to his teeth, which was his usual condition.

"She said yes," he announced, vaulting over the stall's back rail with a printer's disregard for other people's merchandise. He was sixteen going on seventeen now, taller by an inch since winter, and he wore his apprenticeship papers framed on the print shop wall like a battle standard. "Mara. She said yes, she'll take the northern route. Harrowgate, then the lake towns, then over the pass if it's clear."

"When does she leave?"

"Tomorrow. Dawn. She's already packed; she packs like the inquisitors are still after her." Tam caught himself, and his grin dimmed a degree. "Old habits. Anyway. Wren's printing the proclamations — two hundred copies, the king's writ on open trials for the provinces, plus Mara's letters to the mage families who went to ground. She says some of them won't believe it unless the paper smells right, so we're printing on the same stock the Purge edicts used." His mouth twisted. "She's got a sense of humor about it. You have to, she says."

"You could go with her," Edmund said, and made it light, though it wasn't entirely. "The north road suits young legs."

"And leave Wren running the shop alone during the autumn fair? And leave—" Tam gestured vaguely at the market, the harbor, the training hall, the whole small geography of their rebuilt lives, "—leave all this? No. She wouldn't want me anyway. She says I'm a liability north of Harrowgate because I can't hold my tongue around anyone wearing a uniform, which is—" he sighed, "—fair."

They worked side by side a while, restocking the stall, and Edmund let the silence be the kind of silence that families are allowed without explanation. It struck him, not for the first time, that this was what he had never had in seven years at the old stall: someone beside him whose presence didn't require performance. In those days every customer had been a possible informer, every friendly question a hook. Now Tam knocked over a jar of salve, swore creatively, and mopped it up with his own sleeve, and Edmund just laughed, and none of it cost anything at all.

"Rowenna wants everyone at the hall tonight," Tam said, wringing out his sleeve. "Last supper, she called it. For Mara. She's cooking."

"Gods help us all."

"She found a recipe. That's somehow worse."

* * *

He closed the stall early, as he'd promised himself he would. Seven years he had kept that corner until dark, hawking his harmless charms to the last possible customer because idle hours invited questions. Now he folded the awning at noon, left Maren minding the till against his return tomorrow, and walked out of Low Market through streets that no longer required him to memorize the patrol routes.

He climbed instead. Up through the middle city, past the fountains running clean, past the Council Hall with its doors propped open now — actually open, in the ordinary way of buildings that have stopped being afraid of their own citizens — and up the long stair to the high wall where Vellmark ended and the sky began.

The gate was gone. That was the thing almost no one understood who hadn't been there, in the dark under the mountain, when the sigil came apart in light. People asked him about it constantly — pilgrims, scholars, the occasional overwrought poet — *where did it go, the great seal, the ancient binding? Show us the ruins.* And Edmund would tell them the truth, which was that a gate built to hold a shadow had never been a ruin waiting to happen. It had been a promise waiting to be kept differently. When they had all laid their hands on it together — Rowenna's oath, Tam's ink-stained fingers, Mara's grief finally given somewhere to go, and Edmund's own willing surrender of the office and the ward and the whole sealed architecture of his old life — the gate had not shattered. It had *opened*. And everything it had been hoarding — all that power, all that patience, all those centuries of a kingdom's stolen magic — had gone out into the world like water finding its level.

The mages waking in their hiding places across nine provinces, blinking in sunlight, feeling their gifts come back like blood into a numb limb — that was the gate now. That was the sigil, rewritten in living hands.

Edmund reached the wall and stood looking east, toward the mountains, where a true king who still woke some mornings not knowing what year it was was learning — slowly, humb, painfully — to rule a realm that had nearly forgotten him. Grieving, too. That was the part the ballads left out. The king had come back to find his wife dead and his sons scattered and grown, and a country that remembered him mostly as a madman in a story mothers told. He was rebuilding anyway. He insisted on open trials for the Purge's architects, over the screams of half his council, because — Edmund had been in the room — he had said, very quietly, *I know what it is to be judged by fear instead of law. No one else will suffer that in my name.* Mara had cried, though she denied it. Rowenna had saluted so hard she nearly dislocated something.

And below him, at the foot of the wall, half-sunk in green summer grass, stood the old outer ward-stones the inquisitors had dug up in the war's last desperate weeks. The city had voted to leave them lying where they fell. A memorial, the plaque said. *So that we remember what fear builds.* Children climbed on them now. Edmund found that he minded far less than he'd expected.

"Thought I'd find you here."

Rowenna came up the stair with the rolling gait of a woman whose knees had opinions, carrying two cups and the smug expression of someone who has located a person who wanted to be alone.

"How's the elbow disaster?"

"Getting better. I only yelled twice this morning." She handed him a cup. It was tea, which from Rowenna was practically sentiment. They stood shoulder to shoulder, watching the harbor glitter, and for a while neither of them performed any conversation at all.

"He'd have hated this view," Rowenna said at last. She said it every time they came here. It had become a ritual, and rituals don't need novelty. "Your courier. Kess. All that running, all those roads, and he ends up under a plain stone in the veterans' yard while we stand around drinking tea on a wall."

"He'd have hated the stone," Edmund agreed. "He'd have loved the wall. He struck me as a man who appreciated height he hadn't paid for."

Rowenna snorted into her tea. Below them, faintly, the training hall's afternoon class could be heard failing to keep formation, and Rowenna's second-in-command yelling at them in an accent suspiciously like hers.

"Mara leaves at dawn," Rowenna said.

"I know."

"Somebody should tell her the door stays open. Somebody who isn't me, because when I say things like that I get emotional and then I make everyone do push-ups."

Edmund smiled and looked at his tea. "I'll tell her."

* * *

He found her at the print shop, of all places, sitting on the floor between stacks of drying proclamations, helping Wren collate pages with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had once organized supply lines under bombardment. Her travel pack sat by the door like a patient animal. She looked up when he came in, and something passed over her face — the particular expression she reserved for him, complicated and fond and still carrying, at its bottom layer, the ghost of a knife she'd once held at his throat.

"Come to see me off early?" she said. "Dawn's the proper time. It's dramatic."

"I come bearing supper intelligence. Rowenna's cooking."

Mara closed her eyes briefly. "Then tonight we eat before, or not at all."

"There's also this." He took the small parcel from his coat and set it in her lap. Inside was a charm — his best work of the year, though he'd never say so. A compass, of a sort, but it pointed not north. It pointed to whichever of them needed it most. He had made four others. Rowenna carried one, and Tam, and the king kept his on the arm of the throne itself, which Edmund suspected was the loneliest use a charm had ever been put to.

Mara unwrapped it and was quiet for a long moment. Her thumb moved over the casing, once, twice.

"This is a leash," she said finally, roughly.

"It's a door," Edmund said. "Doors work both ways."

She looked up at him, and for a heartbeat he saw her brother in her face — the courier in the back room, bleeding, pressing a map into his hands, choosing him. *Feeding,* the man had gasped, and died so Edmund could run, and everything since — the knight and the apprentice, the sister with the knife, the mountain and the gate and the light — everything had grown from that one desperate seed of trust planted by a dying stranger.

"The door stays open," Edmund said. "All of them. Whatever road you're on, however far. You come back, or you call, and we come to you. That's the whole spell. That's the only one that matters."

Mara Kess, who had crossed a burning province to kill him and stayed to save the world with him, blinked hard and blamed the printer's dust, and Wren tactfully developed an urgent need to check the press.

"Dawn, then," Mara said, when she trusted her voice. "Don't be late. It's dramatic."

* * *

That evening, Rowenna's stew turned out to be — to universal astonishment — genuinely good, which she attributed to the recipe and everyone else attributed to Wren, who had quietly supervised. They ate in the training hall among the practice racks, because it held the most chairs, and Tam stood on a bench and proposed a toast so rambling and sincere that Rowenna threatened push-ups halfway through, and Mara laughed the real laugh, the one she rationed, and Edmund sat in the noise of it and thought: *this is the sigil now, too.*

In the morning they gathered at the north road as the sky went grey to rose. Mara embraced them all in order of increasing resistance — Wren quickly, Tam fiercely, Rowenna longest, and Edmund last, with her forehead pressed briefly to his shoulder, the wordless salute of someone who had once meant to kill him and had decided on family instead.

Then she walked north with two hundred proclamations on her back and her brother's compass in her pocket, and did not look back, because she was Mara, and looking back was for people with less road ahead.

They watched until the road bent. Then Rowenna clapped Edmund on the shoulder hard enough to register as assault, said something gruff about drills, and strode off toward the sound of clashing wooden swords. Tam lingered, hands in pockets, looking up the empty road a moment longer.

"She'll write," he said. "Wren showed her how to send letters through the harbor post now. It's legal and everything."

"Imagine that."

Tam grinned, and sprinted off toward the smell of fresh ink and the day's printing, and Edmund Dunmore walked back down into Low Market as the morning bells rang — cracked and scarred and keeping their time.

The stall waited, crooked and indestructible. Maren had sold three salves and a warming stone in his absence and left the coin in a jar, trusting, unguarded, unremarkable. He unlocked the little case at the back and checked the warding sigils out of pure habit, and then, out of something newer than habit, he took out a blank slip of paper and set it by the till, and weighted it with the smooth river stone he'd carried down from the mountain a year ago.

Above him the sun cleared the bell tower and lay along the stalls like a blessing, and the first customers of the day were already drifting near — a woman with a sick horse, an old soldier with a tremor in his sword hand, a child with a question. Real magic, legally sold, in the open air of a city learning its own strength.

Edmund smiled, rolled up his sleeves, and opened the stall.

By noon the blank paper had a message on it, in a careful hand he knew well, sent ahead from the first relay station on the north road, dated that same dawn:

*First town believes me. Second one will need proof. Bring the good ink when you come — I have a feeling we're not done. — M.*

He read it twice, laughing softly, and reached for his coat.
