## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The merchant's name was Corvus Hale, and he was dying the way rich men die — slowly, expensively, and in denial.

Astrid Kavanagh knelt beside his bed in the amber light of a dozen candles, her bone stylus resting against his temple like a moth that had landed there by accident. The room above his counting-house smelled of beeswax, dried lavender, and the particular sweetness of a body beginning to forget how to live. Rain ticked against the leaded windows of Greyhollow's Merchant Quarter, a patient gray percussion, and somewhere below them the city went on arguing about grain prices as though the world were a permanent thing.

"Tell me again," she said softly, "what you want to keep."

Corvus Hale's eyes were the color of watered wine. He had been handsome once; she could see it under the jaundice and the fear, the way you could see a fresco under whitewash. His hands lay on the coverlet like two dead birds, but they twitched when he spoke, reaching for ledgers that no longer mattered.

"The summer house," he whispered. "The one on Lake Verre. My wife before she — before. And my daughter learning to swim. I want those. I'll pay for those."

"You're not paying me to keep them," Astrid said. "You're paying me to take them. There's a difference, and it matters."

He frowned at her, and for a moment she saw the merchant surface through the dying man — the instinct to haggle, to clarify terms, to win. "I don't understand. If I'm paying—"

"Because everyone thinks buying is keeping." She adjusted her grip on the stylus. Its shaft was pale yellowed bone, worn smooth at the grip where ten years of her fingers had polished it, and its tip was a sliver of something that had never been part of any living animal. "But memories aren't coin, Master Hale. They don't sit in a vault being yours. They're only real while someone remembers them. I can carve out the summer house and your first wife and your daughter's small brown arms kicking in the lake water, and I can seal them in glass so well that fifty years from now some grieving clerk in Vaelmoor will weep over a life he never lived. But *you* will have nothing. You'll lie here at the end with clean hands and an empty house behind your eyes, and you won't even know what you sold, because the knowing goes with it."

The candle flames leaned in the draft from the window. Down in the street, a cart driver cursed his mule with the inventive fluency of the trade.

"Then why," Hale said, and his voice cracked like river ice, "why does anyone buy?"

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. She had answered this question a hundred ways in ten years — with lies, with salesmanship, with the flat truth delivered like a blade between ribs. Tonight, with this man, she found herself giving the honest one.

"Because the desperate would rather own a borrowed joy than carry their own grief," she said. "And because the dying would rather leave something beautiful behind than rot entire. And because I am very good at what I do, Master Hale, and the world has never once rewarded anyone for being good at mercy."

She touched the stylus to his temple.

* * *

The extraction was not painless, but it was gentle, and gentleness was the whole of her craft's secret. The charlatans who worked the river districts used iron needles and left their clients babbling, holes blown through their minds like shot-through sails. Astrid had learned — from whom, she could no longer quite say; that was one of the gaps in herself she'd learned not to look at directly — that memory was not stored in the flesh at all. It was written in something finer, a lattice laid over the flesh the way frost is laid over glass, and if you found the right thread and drew it out slowly, slowly, the mind closed behind it like water closing over a lifted stone.

She breathed with him. That was the other secret. You breathed with the client until your rhythm and theirs were one thing, and then you were no longer a stranger rummaging in their soul; you were them, briefly, and what you took you took from yourself.

The summer house came up the stylus in a slow golden coil, visible to her inner eye as it traveled: white walls baking in the sun, the particular squeak of the veranda boards, a woman laughing with her head tipped back and a straw hat held on with one hand, a child shrieking with delight as cold water swallowed her to the waist. A dog, brown and idiotic, bounding through the shallows. The taste of peaches. The specific weight of an afternoon that believed itself infinite.

Astrid felt all of it as it passed through her — she always did; there was no way to handle a memory without wearing it for a moment — and she felt, beneath it, the thing Hale was really buying relief from: the winter after, the fever, the small grave, the wife who stopped laughing and started drinking and finally stopped altogether. He wasn't selling her the summer. He was selling her the summer *because* of what came after, and both halves would go into the vial together, because a memory cut cleanly cannot be cut twice.

That was the cruelty of the trade that no one warned you about. You couldn't extract just the sweet part. Joy came fused to its context like amber to its insect. So the clerks and lords who bought her vials got the peach trees along with the funerals, the wedding nights along with the widowing, and most of them came back for more, chasing the sweetness and swallowing the sorrow with it, and Astrid had long ago stopped pretending she didn't know what she was feeding them.

The coil of light reached the stylus's tip and slid down into the reservoir in its haft, a vial within a vial, and Astrid sealed it with a breath and a word in no language currently spoken by the living, and set it gently into a padded case lined with dark wool.

"There," she said, when Hale's breathing had steadied. "It's done."

He blinked at the ceiling. Already his eyes were different — not emptier, exactly, but *tidier*, the way a room looks after thieves have taken only the things you loved best. "Done," he repeated. "What was — there was something. Wasn't there something?"

"A summer," Astrid said. "A good one. It's safe."

"Safe," he murmured, and smiled at nothing, and she knew that by morning he would not remember her face or her name or the fact that he had ever owned a daughter who laughed in lake water. The payment sat in a lockbox by the door — forty crowns, more than a dockworker earned in a season — and it would go unclaimed by any heir's grief, because the will specified it: *to the cutter, for services rendered.* Rich men planned everything, even the hollowing of themselves.

Astrid packed her case. She blew out the candles one by one, leaving him to the dark and the rain, and paused at the door.

"Master Hale," she said quietly, though she knew he was already drifting. "I hope wherever it goes, the summer keeps."

He didn't answer. They never did.

* * *

Her attic rooms above the chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane were cold, and she did not light a fire. Fires invited conversation with neighbors about fuel costs, and conversations invited questions, and questions were the one commodity Astrid Kavanagh refused to trade in.

She lit a single lamp instead, and set her case on the table, and did the night's accounting with the mechanical precision of long habit. Forty crowns from Hale. Twelve owed from the widow Quill, who paid in installments for memories of her son, gone to sea. Three vials ready for the broker's run to Brackwater next week — a first kiss, a mother's lullaby, the smell of a bakery that had burned down twenty years ago. Small goods. The inventory of ordinary hearts.

And beneath the false bottom of the case, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in silk, kept the way other women kept letters from the dead:

One vial that had never been for sale.

Astrid unwrapped it the way she did every night, with hands that knew the ritual so well they performed it while her mind stood apart and watched with something like contempt. *Again,* the watching part said. *Every night, like a drunkard to the bottle.* And the hands went on unwrapping, because the hands had learned what the watching part refused to admit: that a person could survive almost anything except the absence of a single fixed point.

The vial was older than her trade. The glass was green-tinted, hand-blown, slightly crooked, and sealed with wax stamped by a sigil she had never been able to read — a spiral broken by three straight lines, like a path interrupted. Inside swirled a memory in colors warmer than any she had ever extracted herself: lamplight on a kitchen table, flour dust hanging in the air like slow snow, and a woman's hands — strong hands, scarred at the knuckle, dusted white — kneading dough while a voice hummed a tune that had no words, or words in a language Astrid had never identified and had stopped asking about.

In the memory, the woman turned and looked directly out — at whoever had carried this memory once, at whoever now held the vial — and said, in a voice roughened by laughter: *"Come here, little bird. Your hands are never too dirty for bread."*

Astrid had replayed it every night for eleven years, and every night it did the same thing to her: it opened a door in her chest that had no business being open, and let the warmth out, and let the cold in after it, and closed again leaving her standing in the wreckage of a life she had never lived.

Because it was not her memory. She had checked — early on, desperately, with techniques she was ashamed of now. The memory's lattice bore another signature, another mind's grain. Some dying woman, some client's mother or grandmother, some stranger whose kitchen and flour and crooked smile Astrid had stolen in her first fumbling years of the trade, back when she took whatever paid and asked nothing about provenance.

She had stolen a mother. And the theft had taken root so deep that she could no longer tell whether she loved the woman in the vial or merely the idea of being loved by her, and she had decided, somewhere around the fifth year, that the distinction did not matter, because love that works is indistinguishable from love that's real, and hers worked. Every night it worked. Every night the humming filled the cold attic and the flour hung in the lamplight and a woman who had never seen her face said *come here, little bird*, and Astrid Kavanagh, the best memory-cutter in Greyhollow, the woman who could hollow a man to the walls and leave him smiling, sat alone at her table and let a dead stranger's kindness hold her together until morning.

She pressed the vial to her sternum, the glass cold through her shirt, and closed her eyes, and let it play.

Flour. Lamplight. Humming. *Your hands are never too dirty for bread.*

Outside, the rain thickened. It had been raining for three days without conviction, the gray kind of autumn rain that Greyhollow wore like a second skin, but tonight it gathered intent, drumming harder on the roof slates, shouldering against the shutters. Astrid noticed it only distantly, the way you notice your own heartbeat. The storm was building toward something. The lamp guttered once in a draft that shouldn't have reached this far inside.

She put the vial away. Silk, oilcloth, false bottom. She banked the lamp low and lay down on her narrow bed in her clothes, as she always did, boots within reach, stylus under the pillow — habits from years she didn't talk about, in cities whose names she sometimes had to think to recall.

Sleep came slowly, and when it came it was thin.

She woke once, near midnight, to a sound she almost mistook for the wind: a knocking. Faint, arrhythmic, from the direction of the attic stair — the outside stair, the rusted iron one that climbed the chandler's rear wall and ended at her door, a door no honest visitor had ever used.

Astrid lay still in the dark, listening, her hand finding the stylus beneath the pillow without her deciding anything at all.

The knocking came again. Small. Stubborn. The knock of someone with very little strength and nowhere else in the world to spend it.

And beneath the rain, beneath the wind worrying the eaves, she heard — or thought she heard, though the thought made no sense and she would examine that failure of sense later, much later, when sense had been thoroughly renegotiated — a voice, high and young, speaking words in a language that had been dead since before the walls of Greyhollow were laid.

Words that made the vial in its hiding place hum, soft as a struck bell, against the bottom of her memory-case.

Astrid Kavanagh sat up in the dark, and did not move, and listened to the storm decide her future one knock at a time.
