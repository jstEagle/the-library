## 2. Knocking at the Wrong Door

The storm came up the Spine like something with a grudge.

Astrid felt it before she heard it — a pressure in the old bones of the Greyhollow attic, a creaking in the rafters that had nothing to do with wind and everything to do with the building remembering what it was to be a ship. The tenement had been a chandler's warehouse once, back when Greyhollow still bothered to pretend it was a port, and its attic rooms still leaned like decks in bad weather. Hers leaned worst of all, because she rented the worst room in any building on principle: cheap, private, one door, no windows worth breaking through unless you were very stupid or very desperate.

She was cataloguing vials by lamplight when the first thunder cracked over the rooftops. Forty-one memories in amber glass, each stoppered with wax and labeled in her own cramped hand. *Merchant's wedding night. Drowned boy's last summer. A woman's memory of forgiving her sister.* None of them hers. None of them ever going to be hers, except the one.

She took that one down from the shelf above her cot without deciding to, the way she did every night, because the body keeps its own ledgers even when the mind refuses to sign them. Small vial. Faded label. *Mother — kitchen, bread, rain on glass.* She'd lifted it off a dying laundress six years ago, paid for the funeral out of guilt or greed, she'd never decided which, and she had replayed it every single night since.

The stylus went into the groove at her temple. The world folded.

*Steam. Flour to the wrists. A woman humming something tuneless, broad-backed, sleeves rolled, saying — not words, just the warm vowel-sound of someone content in their own kitchen. Rain ticking at a window that Astrid had never looked out of, in a house she had never stood in, belonging to a childhood that belonged to somebody else.*

It lasted four minutes. It always lasted four minutes. She could have stretched it, thinned it like broth over more evenings, but she refused. Some things you didn't water down. You took them whole or you put them away.

When she surfaced, the lamp had guttered low and the rain had arrived in earnest, hammering the slate roof like a mob demanding entry. She sat in the dark afterward, as she always did, hands empty in her lap, letting the ache come. This was the part nobody who bought her vials understood — that the ache was the product. That's what they were really paying for, those grey-faced rich men and hollow-eyed widows: not the memory, but the proof that they could still hurt about something. Feeling, bottled. Grief as luxury goods.

Her door had three locks. She checked them anyway, out of habit, then banked the lamp and lay down on the cot in her coat, because Greyhollow winters found every gap in a building and she'd learned not to trust blankets alone.

She was nearly asleep when the knocking started.

* * *

Not the door. The wall.

Three small knocks, low down, at knee height, right beside where her head rested. Astrid's eyes opened. Her hand found the bone stylus under her pillow — not to use, just to hold, the way other people held knives.

"Wrong wall," she said aloud, to no one. "There's nothing behind that wall but chimney."

The knocks came again. Same height. Same spot. Then a voice, muffled through brick and plaster and forty years of soot, said something in a language Astrid did not know and yet, horribly, almost understood — the way you almost understand a song from a country you've never visited. It had a structure to it. A grammar of rising and falling tones, like water finding its level, and underneath the strangeness there was a word-shape she recognized from nowhere, from nothing, from the inside of her own skull: *Thornfell*. No — not Thornfell itself, but something older wearing Thornfell's shape, the way a mountain wears the shape of the fire that made it.

Nobody spoke that language. The passes were sealed. Whatever had lived behind them had lived there before the binding, and the binding was older than the kingdom, older than the crown, older than anything with a name anyone used twice.

The knocking stopped. In its place: silence, and then, faintly, a sound like a wet cough. Small. Child-sized.

Astrid lay very still and conducted the arithmetic she'd been conducting her entire adult life. Every knock was a cost. Every opened door was a ledger entry. She had built herself a closed system — buy nothing, sell everything, owe no one, love nothing that could be repossessed — and it worked. It had kept her alive through nine years of Greyhollow winters, through the purge of unlicensed cutters two springs back, through all of it, because she wanted nothing and therefore could be threatened with nothing.

Except the one thing in the vial above her cot, which was already not hers, and which she would trade every other memory she possessed to keep.

The child knocked again. Weaker now.

"Go away," Astrid said, and heard her own voice come out wrong — too rough, too loud, aimed at the child like a weapon at something soft, which is exactly how cruelty works when you're doing it to protect yourself. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying. Whoever sent you, tell them the shop's closed."

Silence. Then, through the wall, in accented, halting, terrible common tongue — each word picked up and set down like unfamiliar stones:

"Cold."

Astrid stared at the ceiling. Rain hammered the slates. Somewhere below, in the tenement's guts, a pipe groaned.

"You're at the wrong door," she called back. "There's a shelter on Chandler's Row. Saint Verity's. They'll—"

"Gone," the voice said. "Burned." And then, after a pause in which Astrid distinctly heard the child deciding whether to say the next part: "They ran. When I talked. They ran."

*They ran when I talked.*

Astrid got up. She told herself she was getting up to look through the spy-hole, purely for reconnaissance, because knowing what waited outside your door was just good business. She crossed the cold boards, put her eye to the brass—

—and saw nothing at first, because whoever stood in the corridor's lamplight was too small to reach the peephole's line of sight. She crouched. Adjusted. Looked again.

A girl. Eight years old, maybe nine, though it was hard to tell because she was caked head to foot in grey mud that had dried in layers, geological strata of filth, as if she'd crawled rather than walked some considerable distance. Hair plastered flat. Bare feet, blue at the toes, standing in an inch of corridor floodwater that the storm was driving under the tenement door. And in her arms, held against her chest the way children hold things they've been told never to let go of, was a shard of black glass the length of her forearm.

Even through the door, Astrid felt it.

That was the part that made her blood go strange. Memory-work gave you a certain sensitivity — you spent your life handling the residue of feeling, and after enough years you could walk into a room and read it like handwriting. Grief left a particular smudge. Fear another. But this shard wasn't leaving a residue. It was *radiating*, a slow deep pulse like a second heartbeat under the floorboards, like the building itself had grown a heart overnight, and the pulse said — and Astrid hated herself for having the vocabulary to think it — the pulse said *I am awake, I have been asleep a long time, and I remember everything.*

The girl looked up. Directly at the peephole. As if she knew exactly where Astrid's eye was.

"I know you're there," she said, in that ruined little accent. "You smell like the doors between."

"The what?"

But the girl swayed, caught herself against the wall with a muddy palm, and slid slowly down until she was sitting in the floodwater, the black glass cradled in her lap. Her eyes stayed open. That was the worst of it. She fell asleep sitting upright in freezing water, mid-catastrophe, with the absolute trust of something that had been running for so long that stopping anywhere felt like safety.

Astrid stood in her room with her hand on the locks.

Every instinct she owned voted no. The instincts were right, probably. Mud-caked children who appeared during storms speaking dead languages and carrying humming relics were not customers; they were plot, they were trouble, they were the beginning of exactly the kind of story that got unlicensed cutters hanged from the Tollgate arch. Nine years of careful arithmetic sat on her shoulder whispering costs, costs, costs.

And beneath the arithmetic, in a voice she hadn't permitted herself to hear in six years, the laundress's stolen kitchen hummed its tuneless song. *Flour to the wrists. Rain on glass. Someone should have opened the door for that drowned boy. Someone didn't, and she remembered it, and now Astrid did too.*

"Oh, rot it," Astrid said, and threw all three locks.

* * *

The girl weighed almost nothing. That was the first thing. Astrid half-carried, half-dragged her over the threshold, and the child came up out of the floodwater light as driftwood, all sinew and mud, the black glass clutched even in sleep. Astrid pried at it once — gently, testing — and the hum climbed a note, a warning note, a note she felt in her fillings, and she let go and didn't try again.

She got the girl onto the cot, stripped off the worst of the mud with rags and a basin of cold water, and worked by lamplight with the detached efficiency she brought to everything, cataloguing damage. Scraped knuckles. Feet cut in four places, nothing deep. Bruises along the ribs in a pattern that meant someone had grabbed her and she'd twisted free. Old scars on the forearms, thin and parallel and deliberate, that made Astrid's stomach drop and her professional mask slam down over her face, because she knew what memory-extraction sites looked like when they were done badly, and these —

No. Not extraction. Something else. Cuts in a pattern, almost a script, healed long ago.

Under the mud the girl was pale as paper and shaking with cold, so Astrid wrapped her in the good blanket, the wool one, the only luxury she'd ever allowed herself, and set a kettle on the little spirit stove, and sat back on her heels in the middle of her wrecked, mud-flecked, suddenly-inhabited room and thought: *what have you done.*

What she had done was let the whole wide dangerous world through a door she'd spent nine years bricking shut. She knew how this went. She'd seen it happen to other cutters — the ones who took in strays, who partnered up, who loved people. Love was inventory. Inventory got seized. People got taken, and then you could be made to do anything, pay any price, cut any memory out of any skull, because someone held what you couldn't afford to lose.

The girl stirred. Opened her eyes — grey-green, startlingly clear in the filthy face — and looked around the attic with an expression of grave, systematic curiosity, like a scholar arriving at a ruin.

"This is your den," she said. Common tongue, still accented, better than before. Talking in her sleep, apparently, had been practice.

"It's my home."

"A den is a home." She said it like a correction offered kindly. Then her gaze found the shelf of vials, forty-one small fires of lamplight in amber glass, and something moved across her face — recognition, grief, homesickness, all three tangled. "Oh," she breathed. "You keep them. The little prisons."

"They're not prisons. They're memories. I'm a cutter. It's a trade."

"You cut them out of people." Not accusatory. Wondering, the way you might wonder at a man who caught birds and kept them in cages because he'd forgotten they were meant to fly.

"Some people want to forget," Astrid said, sharper than she meant to. "Some people are drowning in what they remember, and they'd pay anything to put it down. I give them that. It's mercy, sold by the vial."

The girl considered this with the seriousness of a judge. "Is it?" she asked, genuinely asking, and Astrid found she had no answer prepared, which was alarming, because she had answers prepared for everything; she'd been rehearsing her own justification in the dark for nine years.

"What's your name?" she said instead.

The girl went still. Not hesitant — still, in the specific way of someone reaching inside themselves for something and finding the shelf bare. Her hand drifted to the black glass, resting beside her on the cot, and touched it lightly, the way you'd touch a scar.

"Wren," she said at last. "I think — someone called me that. Or I heard it somewhere. On the wind, maybe. It fits." She frowned. "Everything else, the true names, the real ones — they're too big now. I was bigger before. I don't know how to explain it. I was—" She gestured vaguely upward, outward, at the ceiling, at the mountains beyond the ceiling, at something under the mountains. "*Wide*. And then I was here, and small, and it was raining, and everyone who saw me ran, and the men in grey followed, and I walked a long way because I could feel—" her eyes came up and fixed on Astrid with terrible directness, "—a door. A door between. You're standing in it. I don't know why. Neither do you, I think."

Astrid's mouth was dry. "Men in grey."

"Inquisitors. Crown-grey. They eat what you bottle." Wren said it simply, factually, and watched the color leave Astrid's face with that same grave attention. "You know them."

Everyone in Greyhollow knew the inquisitors. Everyone knew to be elsewhere when their grey cloaks came down a street. But *they eat what you bottle* — that was not street knowledge. That was cellar knowledge, archive knowledge, knowledge from the sealed side of the passes.

"How do you know that?"

Wren didn't answer. Instead she lifted the shard of black glass, turned it in the lamplight, and held it out — offering, not threatening — and up close Astrid could finally see what she'd missed: veins in the glass. Fine threads of light moving sluggishly inside it, like embers under ash, pulsing in time with that deep slow hum. In time, Astrid realized, with her own heartbeat. With both their heartbeats. Possibly with the mountain's.

"When I touch people," Wren said, "I see what the doors between them have written. What they've carried through walls. What stone remembers." She turned the shard so its flat face caught the light, and for one instant the moving veins arranged themselves — Astrid would swear this later, swear it on whatever oath anyone demanded — into a shape. A sigil. A knot of interlocking lines that looked, absurdly, impossibly, like the watermark pressed into the wax seal of every royal document Astrid had ever forged or fenced.

The binding mark of Thornfell. The seal on the passes. The one that hadn't been broken in three hundred years, because breaking it was supposed to be impossible.

"It's cracking," Wren said quietly. "The big one. Under everything. It's cracking, and the pieces are waking up, and I'm one of the pieces." She looked at her own muddy hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "I didn't ask to be. I was just walking, and then I was small."

Outside, the storm redoubled, and beneath the thunder — so far under it that Astrid could spend the rest of her life pretending she hadn't heard it — the shard hummed one low note, and the floorboards of the chandler's warehouse answered, and somewhere beneath Greyhollow, beneath the smugglers' tunnels and the drowned cellars and the roots of the mountains themselves, something vast rolled over in its sleep and began, slowly, to listen.

Astrid pulled the blanket higher around the child's shoulders, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Sleep," she said. "In the morning we'll figure out what you are."

Wren's eyes were already closing. "You'll figure out what *we* are," she murmured. "You're in the doorway now. Doors go both ways."

Astrid sat up the rest of the night with the stylus under her pillow and the lamp burning low, watching the child breathe, telling herself she'd turn her out at dawn.

She did not turn her out at dawn.
