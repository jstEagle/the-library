## 3. Words Older than Walls

Before, there was no *before*.

There was only the lattice, and the lattice was everything, and everything was the lattice — and Wren had been a thread in it, bright and endless, woven through with ten thousand other threads that were not other at all but only herself, refracted. She remembered it in the way you remember warmth on skin long after the fire has gone out: not as an image but as a condition. A state of having been.

Under the mountains. Under all the mountains at once. The lattice ran beneath Thornfell like veins of light through black glass, and along those veins moved voices — not words, exactly, not sound, because there was nothing in the deep dark to hear with except the lattice itself, and the lattice heard itself constantly, patiently, the way a heart hears its own blood.

*We hold,* the lattice said to itself, every moment of every buried century. *We hold. We hold.*

And Wren had held too. She had been one of the holding places. A knot in the great weaving where three lines crossed — she could still feel the shape of it if she pressed her fingers against her sternum, the place where she had once been a junction, a meeting of forces, a small bright permanence. Threads came into her from the north and the south and the deep-down east where the oldest wards slept in their beds of stone, and they passed through her like light through water, and she gave them back brighter. That was her work. That was what she was *for*. Not a person. A purpose, wearing the shape of a purpose.

She had known the other knots the way you know your own hands. There was Kellem-of-the-Two-Rivers, who hummed when the northern line strained under winter's weight. There was the twin-node called Sareveth, old as the first forging, whose light was the color of honey held up to flame. There were hundreds more, thousands, a nation of bright junctions scattered through the dark like stars pressed into the underside of the earth, and together they sang the holding-song without mouths, without breath, without end.

*We hold. We hold.*

Until something began to eat the song.

---

Wren did not remember it happening. She remembered it *happening*, the way you remember a wound but not the knife. One thread — far away, in the west, near the place where the mountains met the sea — went quiet between one pulse of light and the next. Not dimmed. Quiet. The lattice reached toward the silence and found a hole where a voice should have been, and the hole did not heal, because holes in the lattice were not supposed to be possible, and so the lattice had no word for them, no weave-pattern, no mending.

Then another hole. Then another, closer, each one nearer to her junction, and the threads that fed into her began to taste of wrongness — a flavor she had no tongue for then and only barely has one now, having since learned the word *hunger* from a woman with tired eyes who keeps memories in bottles.

The lattice pulled inward around its wounds. Bright threads drew close to bright threads, the whole vast web contracting like a hand closing over a burn, and in the contraction Wren felt herself being squeezed — compressed, folded, made smaller and smaller and smaller, because the lattice needed strength elsewhere and hers was a minor junction, hers was a knot that could be spared, hers was —

Hers was torn loose.

Not cut. Torn. She remembered the tearing the way she remembered nothing else: fully, horribly, in the colors of pain. Three threads ripping out of her at once, north and south and deep-east, each one a limb she hadn't known was a limb until it was gone. And then falling — falling through darkness that was not the friendly darkness of stone but a darkness with teeth in it, a darkness that wanted, and she had folded herself around the one thing she could save, which was herself, which was all she was now, which was suddenly so very little —

—and woken up small.

That was the cruelty of it. That was the joke the dark had played. She had been vast. She had been a crossing-place of powers older than kingdoms, and she had woken up *small* — two hands, two feet, one mouth that could not make the sounds that mattered, one heart hammering in a chest the size of a birdcage. Mud. Rain. A smell of rot and wet wool. Cold coming at her from every direction at once, cold with edges, cold that took and took and gave nothing back, and she had lain in it screaming the holding-song and hearing nothing come out of her mouth but air.

*We hold.* But she couldn't. She was a junction with no threads. A doorway with no wall. A name —

Her name. What was her name?

This was the grief that lived at the bottom of everything, deeper than the tearing, deeper than the fall. Not that she had lost the lattice. That she had lost the *word for herself*. In the lattice she had needed no name, because names were for things that ended at their own edges, and she had never ended anywhere — she had flowed out along three lines into the wide bright world beneath the stone. Now she ended at her skin. Now she was a closed shape, a fist, a single small note played in an empty hall. And somewhere under the mountain her true designation still existed, carved into ward-stone in letters of light, waiting for her — but when she reached for it in her mind it slid away like a fish in dark water, and what came back instead was nothing, a smooth blank place, a socket with the jewel pried out.

Once, in the first terrible days, she had found a shard of mirror in a gutter behind a tannery, and she had looked into it hoping to see the lattice glowing in her own eyes — some proof, some thread of what she'd been. She saw a thin child with river-colored eyes and mud in her hair. Nothing glowed. Nothing looked back but a stranger.

She had dropped the mirror and run.

* * *

Now there was a room.

Rooms were new. Rooms were strange vertical caves made by soft creatures who feared the dark and the rain and each other, and Wren studied this one the way she had once studied the slow crystallization of minerals in the deep seams: with total, patient attention. It was an attic, the woman said — the word arrived in Wren's mind and settled there, taking root among the others the woman's mouth kept making. Attic. Stylus. Vial. Astrid.

*Astrid.* The woman's name tasted of iron filings and winter apples. Names were fascinating. This woman wore hers openly, said it to customers like a merchant displaying coin, while Wren carried her own absence around like a stone in her shoe — always there, always pressing, always reminding her with every step that something essential had been removed.

The room smelled of ink and dust and something sharper underneath, a mineral smell that made the hollow place behind Wren's sternum ache. Bone styluses stood in a cup on the desk like a little forest of pale trees. And everywhere — everywhere — vials. Small ones and large ones, stoppered and sealed, labeled in the woman's quick slashing hand, and inside each one a memory swirled like weather trapped in glass.

Wren could hear them.

That was the strangest thing about being small, about being here, about being *this*. In the lattice, memory had been ambient, communal, a river they all flowed in. Here, people kept their memories in their skulls like coins in purses, hoarded, secret, and when they were taken out — poured into glass, sold, drunk by strangers — the memories whimpered. Faintly. Continually. A sound like wind through a keyhole that only she seemed able to hear.

The vials on the shelf nearest her cot were whispering. One of them whispered in the dead tongue.

She sat up in the dark.

It was a small vial, blue glass, set apart from the others on a high shelf as if the woman had put it there to forget it. And it was speaking — not loudly, not in words, but in the old cadence, the cadence of the lattice, rise and fall and rise, the rhythm of *we hold, we hold* — and Wren uncurled from her blankets and crossed the cold floor barefoot and took it down with both hands the way you take down a wounded bird.

Inside swirled a grey memory. And within the grey, faint as a coal under ash, burned a fragment of sigil-light.

She knew that light. She had *been* that light.

The child pressed the vial against her forehead and closed her eyes and let the old cadence pour into the empty socket where her name should have been, and it didn't fill the socket — nothing filled the socket — but it touched the edges of it, the way rain touches the rim of a dry well, and for one moment she was less alone than she had been since the tearing. Someone, sometime, had held a piece of the lattice in their mind. Someone had known the dead tongue while it was still alive. And this woman — this memory-thief with her iron-and-apple name — had taken that knowing away from them, sealed it in glass, and put it on a high shelf to gather dust.

Grief for a self she couldn't name. Grief for strangers she would never meet. It was all one grief; it had always been one grief; the lattice had taught her that much before it let her fall: *nothing is separate, nothing ends at its edges, every sorrow flows into every other sorrow like light into light.*

Footsteps on the stairs.

Wren put the vial back exactly where it had been — she was careful, she was learning to be careful in a way she had never needed to be careful when she was vast — and got under her blanket and lay still, listening to Astrid Kavanagh climb toward the door with the particular heavy-tired tread of someone who sells pieces of souls and sleeps badly because of it.

The door opened a crack. Lamplight fell across the floor in a yellow blade.

Astrid looked in on her. Wren kept her breathing slow and even, the way sleeping things breathe, and watched through her lashes as the woman stood in the doorway longer than she needed to, longer than checking-on-a-stray required, her face doing something complicated that Wren could not read because faces were still new and their grammar was hard.

Then Astrid went to the high shelf. Her hand rose toward the blue vial — hesitated — hovered there a moment as if the glass had spoken to her too, or as if she knew exactly what was in it and why, or as if some instinct older than either of them made her reach for the thing she most wished to forget she owned.

She took it down. She turned it in the lamplight. The grey memory swirled, and the coal of sigil-light pulsed once, twice, in the old rhythm — *we hold, we hold* — and Wren watched the woman watch it and understood, with the sudden certainty she had once used to read strain in the northern line, that Astrid Kavanagh replayed this memory at night. That it was not inventory. That it was *hers*, in the way nothing else in this room full of stolen things was hers.

Why? Why would a thief keep one theft warm?

The woman set the vial back on the shelf — gently, the way you set down something breakable that is also somehow alive — and looked over at the cot, and Wren closed her eyes all the way and let her breath go deep and slow, and after a moment the lamplight withdrew and the door clicked shut and the footsteps descended, and Wren lay in the dark with her heart going like a snared bird and thought:

*She keeps one too.*

Everyone in this soft vertical world of rooms and names was keeping something. Hoarding something. Carrying a socket of their own that nothing quite filled. And maybe — the thought arrived shyly, tentatively, the first tentative thought she had allowed herself since waking small — maybe that meant there was a language here after all. Not the dead tongue, not the lattice-song, but something adjacent. A grammar of held things. Of kept vials and locked doors opened anyway and a bowl of soup given to a mud-caked stranger by a woman who claimed, with every line of her closed-off body, to want nothing and no one.

Maybe the lattice wasn't gone. Maybe it was just wearing a different shape. Maybe the threads here were thinner and the light was dimmer and everyone spoke it without knowing they spoke it — every kindness a syllable, every shared meal a phrase, every door opened in a storm a whole sentence in a language older than walls.

*We hold,* the room seemed to say. The cup of styluses. The labeled vials. The narrow cot with its scratchy blanket that had been given to her anyway. *We hold. We hold.*

Wren pressed her fingers to her sternum, to the ghost of the three-way junction, and for the first time she felt something answer. Faint. Far. Down. Under the mountains, under the city, under everything — a thread of light, still alive, still singing, calling its lost fragment home across all the miles of dark stone.

And answering it — entangled with it, braided through it, unmistakable even across the distance — a wrongness. The hunger. The holes. Moving. Searching. Eating their way toward the last bright knots, patient as winter, and somewhere above them all wearing a crown.

They would come. Whatever wore the hunger would follow the thread to her, and it would find her here, in this attic, in this room that smelled of ink and stolen weather, beside this woman who kept one memory warm.

Wren lay awake in the dark and listened to the rain on the roof, and the rain sounded almost like the lattice, and she grieved for the self she could not name, and she held — because holding was the only thing she had ever known how to do — she held the sound of Astrid Kavanagh's tired footsteps climbing the stairs, the image of the woman's hovering hand, the mystery of the blue vial, the sentence-without-words that the room kept repeating in its ink-and-dust voice.

*We hold.*

Below her, in the city, in the world, a bell tolled the midnight hour, and under the tolling — so faint she might have dreamed it, so faint it might have been only the old song echoing in her own hollow places — Wren heard the mountain sigh. And in its sigh, threaded through it like wire through wax, she heard her own true name, the one carved in ward-stone, reaching for her at last after all this time, arriving finally at the tip of her tongue, arriving finally —

Knuckles rapped against the attic door. Three sharp knocks. Astrid's voice, low and urgent, came through the wood:

"Wake up. There's a woman downstairs asking about you."
