## 4. The Inquisitor's Smile

The knock came at the ninth bell of the morning, and Astrid Kavanagh knew before she opened the door that it would be trouble.

Trouble had a sound. Twenty years in the memory trade taught you the difference between the knock of a client — hesitant, guilty, always a half-second too long between raps, as though their knuckles were negotiating with their conscience — and the knock of a creditor or a watchman, which came fast and flat and expected nothing. This knock was neither. It was patient. It was the knock of someone who already knew the door would open, who knocked only because the formality amused them.

Astrid set down the stylus she'd been cleaning, wiped her hands on the leather apron she wore for work though she hadn't touched a client since yesterday, and crossed the attic's creaking boards. Through the window at the stair-turn, Greyhollow lay gray and dripping under a sky the color of old tin. Rain still clung to everything from last night's storm — it beaded on the gutters, ran silver down the drainpipes, pooled in the cracks of the street below where a lamplighter was wading through puddles with his ladder on his shoulder.

Behind her, in the curtained alcove that served as Wren's room, there was silence. Deliberate silence. The child had learned in three days of hiding that when Astrid said *stay quiet*, she meant it with her whole spine.

Astrid opened the door.

The woman on the landing was tall and dry, which was the first impossible thing about her. Three days of rain, and not one drop seemed to have touched her charcoal-gray traveling coat; its hem hung clean above boots that showed no mud from streets that were all mud. Her hair was bound back in a severe coil the color of dark honey, and her face was handsome in the way of a well-cut key — precise, functional, made to fit into something and turn.

But it was her eyes that told Astrid what she was.

Glassy. That was the word that rose unbidden, the word Astrid used with clients who'd bought too much and too often, the ones whose own recollections had been crowded out by other people's until they moved through their days like sleepwalkers wearing borrowed lives. The inquisitor's eyes held too many reflections. When the light shifted, something behind them seemed to shift separately, like sediment settling in a jar of river water.

*Memory-eaten.* The crown's inquisitors were rumored to consume the recollections they confiscated — drinking them straight from the vial rather than sealing them in the archive vaults, taking the lives of traitors and dissidents into themselves as both punishment and prize. Astrid had always assumed the rumors were exaggerated. Standing three feet from Serane Voss, she understood they had been understated.

"Good morning," the woman said. Her voice was pleasant, low, unhurried. "Mistress Kavanagh, I presume. The memory-cutter of Copperlane."

"I cut memories for people who can pay," Astrid said. "Whether I'm a mistress depends on who's asking."

"Inquisitor Serane Voss." She did not produce credentials. She produced, instead, a smile — small, controlled, arranged rather than felt. "May I come in? Your stairs are treacherous, and I'd rather not conduct this conversation for the whole of Copperlane."

Everything in Astrid's body said *no*. Every instinct honed by two decades of dealing with dangerous men and desperate women screamed at her to bar the door, to claim illness, to invent a dying aunt in Brackwater who needed her at once. But refusal was itself an answer, and answers were things this woman collected.

She stepped aside. "Mind the third stair. It bites."

Serane Voss climbed past her with the unhurried grace of someone entering a room she already owned. She looked around the attic with the mild, cataloguing attention of a buyer at auction: the workbench with its row of empty vials waiting like little glass coffins, the bone styluses laid out in order of fineness, the shelves of sealed memories glowing faintly amber where the morning light caught them, each one tagged in Astrid's small sharp hand. Her gaze passed over the alcove curtain without pausing — and that non-pause, that studied carelessness, frightened Astrid more than any stare could have.

"You have a fine collection," Serane said.

"They're stock, not a collection. Stock moves."

"Of course." The inquisitor drifted along the bench, gloved fingertips hovering above the vials without touching. "I confess I've heard interesting things about your technique. The bone stylus is old work — the guilds abandoned it generations ago as imprecise. But you've refined it somehow. Clients say your extractions are *cleaner* than guild work. No fraying at the edges. No lost years around the wound." She turned, and the glassy eyes fixed on Astrid with an expression that might, in another face, have been admiration. "Where did you learn that?"

"My mother taught me." It was true, as far as it went, and truth was the best mortar for a lie.

"Mm. And she learned from?"

"The road. We traveled. She's dead now." Also true. Also useless to anyone.

Serane smiled again, that same arranged smile, and Astrid realized she was watching a woman do with conversation exactly what Astrid did with the stylus — working carefully along the edge of something, feeling for the seam, looking for the place where pressure would make it open.

"You're wondering why I'm here," Serane said.

"The thought had crossed my mind."

"Three nights ago, during the storm, there was an incident in the Undercroft district. A breach at one of the crown's containment facilities — a facility you will not have heard of, because it does not officially exist." She said it lightly, almost apologetically, the way one mentions a embarrassing relative. "Something escaped. Something small." A pause, weighted precisely. "A child."

Outside, the lamplighter had finished his rounds and gone, and the street below sat empty and shining. Somewhere a cart rattled over cobblestones, and the sound seemed very far away, as though the world had drawn back to give this conversation room.

"A child," Astrid repeated, and was pleased at how flat her voice came out. Bored. Mildly inconvenienced. "What does the crown want with a child?"

"The crown wants many things. What *I* want is simpler." Serane moved to the window, looking out over the wet roofs, presenting Astrid with her back — a confidence so complete it was either genuine or theatrical, and Astrid couldn't decide which was worse. "The asset is... unusual. It speaks a dead language. It carries a fragment of something old and valuable. It is, in the most literal sense possible, crown property — inventory, if you like. Lost inventory." She glanced back over her shoulder. "Lost inventory tends to find its way to specialists. People who deal in rare things. People who know how to move goods quietly through a city like this one."

"You think I'm a smuggler."

"I think you're a survivor, Mistress Kavanagh, and survivors keep options." Serane turned fully now, folding her gloved hands. "I'm offering you one. Bring the asset to me, or tell me where it is, and there will be no inquiry into your trade practices. No audit of your licenses. No uncomfortable questions about how a woman with no guild standing extracts memories cleaner than the guild itself." The smile again, warmer by a degree, and no more real than before. "You would be left entirely alone. Which I understand, for a woman like you, is the closest thing to heaven on offer."

The silence stretched. Rain dripped somewhere in the eaves, counting out the seconds.

Every calculation Astrid ran came back the same: this woman knew. Not suspected — *knew*. The storm, the timing, the Undercroft facility Astrid had never heard of and now could never unknow. The crown had lost its asset in a flood of rain and had traced the loss to Copperlane within three days, and the only question was how much Serane Voss already knew versus how much she was fishing for.

And behind the curtain, twelve paces away, a child who spoke a dead language was holding perfectly still, listening to every word.

"No," Astrid said.

Serane's eyebrows rose a fraction. Not surprise, exactly. Interest. "No?"

"No child has been here. No child has *been* here. You can search the loft, the cellar, whatever you like — there's nothing but dust and my stock." Astrid folded her arms, and let a lifetime of practiced boredom settle over her face like a mask she'd worn so long it had grown to fit. "I don't know what escaped from your nonexistent facility. But if the crown loses children in storms and then comes shaking down doors in Copperlane, maybe the problem isn't smugglers."

For a long moment, the inquisitor simply looked at her. And Astrid watched the sediment shift behind those glassy eyes — dozens of consumed memories turning over like silt in disturbed water, lives not hers pressing against the inside of her skull — and understood suddenly, intimately, what she was looking at. A woman so full of stolen others that the original had been buried deep. Perhaps deeper than the false mother ever was in Astrid. Perhaps so deep she'd need a stylus of her own to find her.

It should have made her feel pity. Instead it made her feel cold, because she could see the shape of the road that led from this attic to those eyes, and it was short, and it was paved with exactly the kind of bargains Serane Voss was offering right now.

"You're refusing," Serane said softly. "Knowing what refusal costs."

"I know what *acceptance* costs. I've seen it. I sell it back to people, sometimes, when they regret it." Astrid nodded toward the shelf of amber vials. "There's a man in Goldrow who sold information to your office eight years ago. He came to me last spring wanting to forget he'd done it. I told him memory doesn't work that way — you can't cut out what you did, only what you remember doing, and the thing itself keeps happening underneath anyway." She held the inquisitor's gaze. "So no. Whatever this child is, whatever the crown thinks it's owed — I haven't seen her, and I wouldn't hand her over if I had."

"*Her.*"

The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water.

Astrid kept her face still. Kept her breathing even. But inside, everything went white and ringing, because she had said *her*, and she had said it without thinking, and Serane Voss's smile had widened by a single millimeter — the smile of a fisherman feeling the line go taut.

"I said *if I had*," Astrid said. "If I had a child, her, him, it — pronouns are cheap, Inquisitor. Your facility lost something. Describe it better next time and maybe someone can actually help you."

"Mm." Serane smoothed the front of her coat, unhurried, and Astrid understood that the game had shifted — that the inquisitor had come fishing and caught something, and was deciding whether the catch was worth hauling in today. "You're quite good at this," she said. "The lying. It's nearly a shame. You'd have gone far in His Majesty's service."

"I prefer being alone to being served."

"Yes." The glassy eyes moved once more around the attic, lingering nowhere and everywhere — on the alcove curtain, on the shelves, on Astrid's steady hands — a gaze like a hand patting down a coat for hidden letters. "Well. I'll leave you to your solitude, Mistress Kavanagh. For now." She moved toward the door, and paused on the threshold with her back still turned. "You should know that I'll be in Greyhollow for some time. The crown's business here is... extensive. And you should know that I remember faces. All of them. It's rather my profession." She glanced back, and the arranged smile returned one final time, and up close Astrid could see that it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes — that nothing reached those eyes, that they were windows into a house where the furniture belonged to the dead. "Do take care of yourself. The city's been dangerous lately. Strange weather. Strange runaways. One never knows what the storm washes up."

Then she was descending the stairs, her footsteps soft and even, and the street door opened and closed below, and the lamplighter across the way looked up as she passed and touched his cap to her as if he'd known her for years.

Astrid waited until the count of a hundred. Then fifty more. Then she crossed the attic on legs that had gone strangely unsteady and pulled back the alcove curtain.

Wren sat on the narrow cot with her knees drawn to her chest, the shard of black glass clasped against her sternum, her enormous dark eyes fixed on Astrid's face. She had not made a sound. She had not breathed loudly enough to stir the curtain. She was very good at vanishing, this child — better than any thief Astrid had ever known.

"She knows," Astrid said quietly. It wasn't a question, and Wren couldn't have answered anyway, so it was really just the sound of a woman admitting something to herself. "Not where. Not yet. But she knows you exist, and she knows you came here, and she was toying with me the whole time like a cat with a knot of yarn." She sank down onto the cot's edge, and the child leaned into her side without hesitation, without invitation, the way she'd done every night for three days — a small warm weight against Astrid's ribs that had already become, terrifyingly, familiar.

Wren looked up at her and said something in that dead tongue, that language older than the walls of Greyhollow, older than the kingdom it bound together. Astrid didn't know the words. But she knew tone — tone was half her trade — and the tone was a question, and the question was *what happens to us now?*

"We leave," Astrid said. The words came out steadier than she felt, and then, saying them aloud, she found she actually did feel steadier — the fear in her chest hardening, cooling, taking on edges. Fear was a client like any other. You could cut it. You could shape it. Or you could let it sharpen itself into something with a blade's geometry. "Tonight. Before she comes back with warrants instead of smiles. There are roads out of these mountains that the crown's maps don't know exist — roads my mother bled to learn. If she wants to hunt us, she can chase us through the dark and see how her fine dry coat fares."

Wren tilted her head. Then, slowly, deliberately, the child raised one small hand and pointed east — toward the mountains, toward Thornfell, toward whatever slept beneath them humming in that black glass shard — and spoke again, two syllables, urgent and certain.

Astrid didn't understand the words. But she understood the direction.

She got up and began pulling her go-bag from under the floorboards, and outside, far off in the gray morning, a bell began to toll — not the hour, not the alarm, just the city clearing its throat — and Astrid Kavanagh packed her stolen life into a bag while the rain finally stopped and the mountains waited.
