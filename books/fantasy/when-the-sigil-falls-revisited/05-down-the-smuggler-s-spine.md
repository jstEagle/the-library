## 5. Down the Smuggler's Spine

The storm had not followed them out of Greyhollow so much as it had been left behind, sulking over the rooftops like a cheated creditor. Astrid kept glancing back at it anyway, the way a woman checks the street after she's picked a lock that wasn't hers. Two days of rain had turned the smugglers' road into a brown ribbon of mud and loose shale, and the child walked it as though mud were a rumor she'd heard about but never personally confirmed.

Wren did not complain. That was the strange part. Children complained — Astrid had extracted enough childhoods from enough merchants to know that complaining was practically the architecture of being small. But Wren simply put one bare, cracked foot in front of the other and watched everything: the cairns, the switchbacks, the way Astrid's hand never strayed far from the bone stylus at her belt.

"You'll want boots," Astrid said, for the third time since dawn.

The girl looked down at her feet as if noticing them for the first time, then up again. She said nothing. She hadn't spoken a word of anything Astrid understood since that first night on the doorstep, when the syllables had come out of her like water forced through a crack in old masonry — that dead tongue, Thornfell's own, the language carved into wards that no living smith could read anymore. Astrid had told herself the silence was convenient. It was easier to shepherd a mute child across three mountain passes than one who asked questions.

It was also a lie, and Astrid was too good a liar not to recognize one in her own keeping.

The pass narrowed ahead where the mountain shouldered in against itself, walls of grey stone rising on either side like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die a very long time ago. Astrid stopped and pressed her palm flat against the rock. Cold, yes. But beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, there was the other thing — the hum. Every smuggler who worked the Thornfell spine knew the passes were warded; that was why they were safe. What none of them knew, what only a Kavanagh knew, was how close the wards still lived.

"Stay behind me," she said. "Touch nothing that looks carved."

Wren touched everything that looked carved.

Astrid turned once, halfway through the narrows, and caught the girl with both hands flat against a section of wall where centuries of weather had scoured the stone smooth — except it wasn't smooth, not quite. There were lines in it, hair-fine, the kind of work you'd walk past ten thousand times without seeing unless you'd been raised by someone who made you trace such lines with your finger until your eyes bled. Astrid had been raised that way. She had hated every minute of it, and she had never forgotten a single stroke.

But Wren wasn't tracing. She was *listening*. Her head was tilted, her eyes half-closed, her lips moving around shapes that weren't words yet — or were words in a language Astrid's ear refused to catch.

"Wren." The name still felt borrowed when Astrid said it, a coat handed to a stranger. "We don't have time."

The girl opened her eyes, and for a moment they were not a child's eyes at all. They were old and lit from underneath, like windows into a cellar where something vast had been burning for centuries. Then she blinked, and she was small again, muddy, shivering, looking at Astrid with an expression that was unmistakably an apology.

And then she lifted her hand from the stone and — spoke. Not the dead tongue this time. Something else, halting and broken, each word dragged out of her like a nail from green wood.

"There was... a man. Here. He was afraid." Her brow furrowed with the effort, as though translation were a physical labor, hauling weight up a stair. "He hid... coins. In the wall. He was running from—" A pause. Her small hand flexed against the rock. "From his brother."

Astrid stared at her.

She knew that story. Not the details — those were new — but the shape of it. Twenty years ago, give or take, a smuggler named Corvin Hale had stashed a purse in these narrows before doubling back to settle accounts with his brother, and neither of them had come back for the money. Astrid knew it because her mother had known it, because her mother had known *everything* the mountain remembered, and had passed along fragments of it the way other mothers passed along recipes.

Except Astrid's mother was twenty years dead, and the only person alive who could have confirmed the story was standing in front of her with mud to her knees, reading it out of solid rock.

"How," Astrid said. It came out flatter than she intended. She tried again. "How did you know that?"

Wren considered the question with the grave patience of someone deciding how much truth a person could carry. Then she pressed her palm back to the stone and said, in that same halting, effortful common speech:

"The stone remembers. I can... hear it. Like voices under water." Her face tightened. "Loud places are hard. Quiet places are—"

She didn't finish. Perhaps she didn't have the word. Perhaps the word frightened her.

Astrid looked at the wall, at the faint ghost-lines of the ward-script running beneath the lichen, and felt the ground shift under assumptions she'd built her whole life on. Memory-work was her trade. She knew exactly what memory weighed, what it cost, what it did to the vessel that carried it. And if the stone remembered —

Then the whole mountain was a vault, and this child was a key walking around barefoot in the rain.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang that some long-dead traveler had roofed with slabs of slate — one of the way-stations along the spine, marked by a cairn with a single white stone on top. Astrid built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand and heated the last of the travel bread over it. Wren ate like a bird that had been told about famine secondhand: quick, precise, watchful.

Astrid told herself she was watching the girl for tactical reasons. Escape routes. Signs of crown pursuit. Whether the child would be a burden or an asset on the road ahead.

What she was actually doing was cataloguing the way the firelight moved on Wren's face, and hating herself a little for how familiar it already felt. Two days. Two days and the attic already had a second blanket in it, even here, even on the run. That was the trouble with letting things in — they brought their belongings.

"Again," Astrid said, because the silence was worse than the question. "The stone. You hear memories in it. All of them? Always?"

Wren chewed, swallowed, thought. Then she reached out and laid two fingers on the slate floor between them, and closed her eyes.

Her face changed. Astrid had seen faces change during extractions — the merchant's slack rapture, the rich man's tears when his first love came out of him in a vial — but this was different. This was the face of someone listening to a crowd through a door.

"Old," Wren whispered. "So many. Walking. Carrying. One is singing, badly. One is dying here, and he is not afraid, he is *relieved*—" Her eyes snapped open, wet. "It doesn't stop. It never stops. Even asleep, I hear the ground remembering."

Astrid sat with that. Outside, wind worried the cairn stones, and somewhere below them a night bird called once and got no answer.

"And Greyhollow?" she asked quietly. "The city. All that stone, all those streets. What does it sound like?"

Wren wrapped her arms around her knees. "Like shouting," she said. "I couldn't find myself in it. That's why I was lost." A pause, and then, smaller: "That's why I found you. Your house was quiet. Your stairs were quiet. Only one memory in the wood of your door, and it was yours, and it was lonely, and I—" She stopped, ears going red, and buried her face against her knees.

Astrid looked at the fire for a long time.

*Only one memory in the wood of your door, and it was yours.* Which meant the child had read her before Astrid ever opened the lock. Had stood on that landing in the storm, drowning in the shouting of a whole city, and chosen the one quiet, lonely voice — and knocked.

"I keep a vial," Astrid heard herself say. She hadn't planned to say it. The words came out the way confessions do, sideways, when the guard is tired. "Under the floorboard. A memory I took off a dying woman years ago. A mother, teaching a daughter to bake bread. Flour on her nose. I play it most nights." She turned the stylus over in her fingers, watching lamplight slide along the bone. "It isn't mine. None of it is mine. But it's the closest thing I have."

Wren uncurled slowly. Looked at her. And then, with the terrifying directness of children and saints, she reached across the fire and touched the back of Astrid's hand — just briefly, just skin to skin — and said:

"You're allowed to keep one."

Astrid pulled her hand back as if burned. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, loose stone clattered down the scree, and both of them went still, listening, until the mountain settled back into its old murmuring sleep.

"We move before light," Astrid said, gruffly, pulling her cloak around her shoulders. "There's a tollhouse past the second ridge. A man named Ferro owes me a debt he doesn't know he owes. We'll be safe there for a night."

Wren nodded. Then, settling into her blanket with the slate floor under her cheek, she murmured something in the dead tongue — soft, melodic, almost a lullaby — and the faint lines of ward-script in the overhang's stone above them glowed, once, gently, like embers breathing, and went dark again.

Astrid lay awake a long while after that, staring at the place where the light had been.

* * *

The second ridge nearly killed them.

Not with danger — with weather. The cloud came down the mountainside like something poured, swallowing the trail, erasing the cairns, turning the world into a grey room with no walls. Astrid had crossed this pass in fog before; she knew the trick of counting paces between markers, of reading the wind's angle against her left ear. But the wind had died, and with it her last fixed point, and by midday she was navigating by stubbornness alone, Wren's hand locked in hers, both of them leaning into a slope that might have been going up or down.

"We stop," she shouted, or meant to shout. The fog ate the words.

And then Wren tugged her arm. Pointed — not along the trail, but off it, thirty degrees to the right, toward a wall of blank grey stone.

Astrid shook her head. There was nothing there. She'd memorized this route as a girl; there was nothing there but cliff.

Wren insisted. Tugged again. And then, releasing Astrid's hand, she walked straight into the fog toward the blankness, and Astrid's heart went up into her throat and she lunged after her, catching the girl's shoulder just as—

—as the fog thinned, just there, just in that one pocket of air, and Astrid saw it: a fissure in the cliff face, narrow as a coffin lid, invisible from any angle except head-on, hidden behind a curtain of hanging moss that some smuggler a hundred years dead had trained to grow across the entrance. The Moss Door. Her mother had described it once, in the flat voice she used for things that mattered: *If you're ever hunted, girl, and the ridge is blind, look for the door that isn't there.*

No map marked it. No living soul but Astrid should have known it existed.

"How," Astrid said again. It was becoming her only word. "How did you see that?"

Wren pointed at the stone beside the fissure, where the fog had left a film of moisture, and where — Astrid saw it now, now that she knew to look — faint lines of script woke under the wetness like fish rising in a pond. Wren touched them and said, simply:

"It remembered being a door. So I asked it to be one."

Astrid stood in the dripping dark of the entrance for a moment longer, doing arithmetic she didn't like. The child could read echoes out of stone. The child could *ask* the stone for things. The child was a fragment of the sigil that bound the kingdom, and King Aldric's inquisitors wanted her badly enough to send glassy-eyed Serane Voss to a garret in Greyhollow — which meant the crown knew what she was, or guessed, and either answer was bad.

And Astrid was leading her, knowingly, deeper into the mountains, toward the one place in Thornfell where whatever slept inside the girl might wake all the way up.

She should turn around. She should march the child back down to the lowlands, hand her to a river barge, wash her hands of sigils and crowns and dead tongues, go back to vials and wealthy clients and the nightly ritual of a mother who had never been hers.

Instead she ducked through the Moss Door, and held it open for Wren, and said, "Mind the drop. Three steps down. It's dry inside."

The passage beyond was exactly as her mother had promised: dry, warm-ish, smelling of cold granite and old smoke. Someone had cached supplies here once — rusted hooks, a rotted rope, a clay jar that crumbled when Astrid touched it. And on the walls, everywhere, the script. Not faded here. Sheltered. Sharp-edged. Alive.

Wren walked the length of the passage with one hand trailing the wall, and the script woke as she passed, line by line, a slow ripple of pale gold light following her like a tide following the moon. She wasn't commanding it. Astrid realized that with a jolt. The girl was *greeting* it, and it was answering — the way old dogs answer a particular footstep on the stair.

"These are way-marks," Astrid said, mostly to herself. "Smiths' marks. Guidance script. My mother—" She stopped. Started again. "Someone I knew drew these. Long ago."

Wren looked back at her, gold light washing up her small face, and smiled — the first true smile Astrid had seen from her, wide and crooked and entirely a child's.

"She's in here," Wren said. "A little. Her hands are in here. I can feel her hands."

Astrid turned away quickly and busied herself with the fire kit, and if her eyes stung, the passage was dark enough that no one could prove it.

* * *

They descended through the next day and half the night, and the mountain changed around them the way mountains do — gradually, then suddenly. The pine gave way to black fir, the fir to scrub and scree, and the air began to carry the mineral smell of deep stone and, faintly, woodsmoke from somewhere human. The tollhouse sat in the saddle of the second ridge where the smugglers' spine crossed the old king's road: a squat stone building with a turf roof, a crooked chimney, and a fence made of mismatched antlers that announced to anyone who cared to read it that the owner answered to no law Astrid recognized.

Ferro was outside splitting wood when they came up the track, and Astrid watched him see her — watched the axe slow, stop, rest. He was older than she remembered, which was unfair of him; she'd last seen him nine years ago, and she'd been nobody then, a girl with a stylus and a reputation still wet behind the ears. Now he was grey to the eyebrows and his hands had gone knuckled with age, but his eyes did the same thing they'd always done: measured, weighed, decided.

"Well," Ferro said. "You've got her walk."

Astrid blinked. "Her walk?"

"Your mother's walk." He set the axe against the block and looked at Astrid the way men look at a debt they'd hoped was forgiven. "Same stride. Like the mountain owes you rent and you've come to collect." His gaze slid past her to Wren, and something moved through his face — recognition, or its ghost. "And who's this, then?"

"This is Wren," Astrid said. "She's mine."

The words were out before she'd decided to say them, and once out, they refused to be taken back. Wren's hand found hers — small, cold, certain — and squeezed once.

Ferro looked at their joined hands for a long moment. Then he sighed, the sigh of a man whose evening has just become complicated, and jerked his chin toward the door.

"Inside, then. Both of you. There's stew, and there's questions, and we'll see which runs out first."

As Astrid climbed the tollhouse step, she felt it — a prickle along her spine, the old instinct that had kept her alive through nine years of dangerous trade. She glanced back down the track, into the blue dusk gathering under the ridge.

Far below, tiny with distance, a light was climbing. Not a campfire. Campfires sit still.

This one was moving. Steady. Patient. Following the spine.

Astrid stepped inside and pulled the door shut, and began, silently, to count the exits.
