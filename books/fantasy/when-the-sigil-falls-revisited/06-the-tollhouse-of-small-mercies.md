## 6. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

The rain found them an hour before the tollhouse did.

It came off the high passes in gray sheets, the kind of cold that did not merely wet you but seemed to want something from you, pressing in through seams and collars, taking warmth like a tax. Astrid pulled Wren along a goat track she had not walked in six years, one hand hooked in the child's collar, both of them bent against the wind. Below them the valley was a rumor of black pines and river-sound. Above them the mountain shouldered into cloud and stayed there.

"There," she said, and had to say it again, louder, before Wren lifted her face.

The tollhouse sat where the smugglers' road pinched between two rock shoulders — a squat stone building with a slate roof furred green and a lantern burning in its single window. Smoke curled from a chimney that leaned like a tired man against the gable. To anyone coming up the legal road from the south it would have looked abandoned; that was the point of it. Ferro had never believed in comfort, only in camouflage.

Astrid stopped ten paces from the door and made herself look at it properly. Made herself remember what standing at this door had cost her the last time. There was a scorch mark on the lintel where a crown patrol's fire-pot had missed in the dark, and the bench beside the door where she'd once sat through half a night waiting for a guide who never came, and the low stone sill worn hollow by forty years of boots. A place remembers, her mother's trade said. Places are just memories that can't run.

"Listen," she told Wren, crouching to the child's height. Rain ran off Wren's hood in threads; beneath it her eyes were wide and dark and far too old for the small face they lived in. "The man inside is called Ferro. He's rude, he'll feed you until you're sick, and if he asks you questions, look at me before you answer. Can you do that?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then nodded once. She had not spoken since the storm on the attic stairs — not in the dead tongue, not at all — but she took Astrid's sleeve in her fist and held it the whole way to the door, and Astrid found she did not mind.

She knocked: two, pause, three, pause, one. The old signal, older than the crown, older probably than Ferro.

Shuffling inside. The scrape of a bolt. Then the door opened a hand's width and one eye appeared in the gap, bloodshot and suspicious under a thicket of white brow.

"We're closed," said a voice like gravel poured slowly. "Closed for eleven years. Closed for good."

"Ferro." Astrid pushed back her hood. "It's raining."

"It's the Thornfell passes, girl. It's always raining." But the eye had found her face and stopped there, and something moved behind it — recognition first, and then, strangely, grief, arriving ahead of any welcome. The door opened wider than it needed to. "Get in before you drown. Both of you. Saints, get *in*."

* * *

Inside, the tollhouse smelled of peat smoke, tallow, and the particular mustiness of a man who lived alone with his own opinions. One room, and every inch of it put to work: coils of rope, crates stenciled with harmless labels over less harmless contents, a stove squatting in the corner like a toad, two chairs, one bed, and above the hearth — Astrid saw it and her stomach dropped — a dried bundle of rowan twigs tied with blue thread, hung the way you hang a charm against spirits, or in memory of someone.

Ferro shut the door, threw both bolts, and turned.

He had gone white-haired and thicker through the shoulders since she'd seen him last, but his hands were the same — huge, scarred across the knuckles, the hands of a man who had hauled contraband over passes that ate soldiers alive. He looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at Wren, and his whole weathered face changed as though someone had pulled a cord inside it.

"Sit," he said roughly. "Both of you. Don't argue."

They sat. He moved around the small room with surprising economy — peat on the stove, a kettle filled, bread and hard cheese and a crock of pickled things brought down from a shelf Astrid would have needed a ladder for. He set the food before them without ceremony, then stood over the stove with his back to them, arms folded, watching the kettle as if it owed him money.

"You're thinner," he said to the stove.

"You're fatter."

"Hah." It came out more like a cough. "Eleven years, Astrid Kavanagh. Eleven years, and you knock on my door with—" He turned, and his eyes went to Wren again, and stayed. "With that."

Wren, sensing herself discussed, went on eating bread with the single-minded devotion of the truly starving. Astrid watched Ferro watch her, and felt the shape of something she couldn't yet name moving under the floorboards of the conversation.

"She's under my protection," Astrid said. "That's all you need—"

"That's my *rowan*." Ferro pointed at the bundle above the hearth, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word like ice under a boot. "Do you know why it's there?"

"No."

"No," he agreed. "You wouldn't." He dragged the second chair to the table, its legs shrieking on stone, and sat down heavily opposite her. Up close she could see his age now, the tremor in the great hands, the yellowed whites of his eyes. "I knew your mother."

The kettle began to sing. Nobody moved toward it.

Astrid heard herself laugh — a short, ugly sound she hadn't authorized. "I don't have a mother. I have a coat that smells of someone, and a name some clerk gave me when they scraped me off a workhouse step. You've confused me with—"

"Maren." Ferro said the name quietly, and the room went very still. "Maren Kavanagh. Sigil-smith's daughter out of Cael, married the trade to the road because somebody had to move what couldn't be moved by honest men. Saved my life twice. Once from a rockfall in the Nether Spine, once from myself." His eyes were wet, and he glared at the wall as though daring it to comment. "She paid a debt here once. For me. A big one. I've been paying it back to nobody for thirty years, and now you sit at my table eating my bread looking exactly like her from the brows up, and you tell me you don't have a mother."

Astrid's hands had gone cold around the cheese she wasn't eating. *Maren.* The word landed in her like a coin dropped down a well — she listened for it to hit bottom and heard nothing, no splash, no echo. Just the well. Just the dark.

"I don't remember her," she said finally. It was the closest thing to the truth she owned. "Whatever you knew — I wasn't old enough to keep it."

Ferro studied her for a long time. Outside the rain redoubled against the slates, and somewhere up the valley thunder cleared its throat.

"Then that," he said softly, "is the saddest thing I've heard all year, and I've heard a lot." He rose, took the kettle off the heat, and poured three cups with hands that weren't quite steady. When he set Wren's cup before her he did it with a gentleness that didn't match anything else about him. "Drink. Sleep here tonight. Road's flooded anyway; even the mountain's against travel."

"Ferro—"

"Kindness is free, girl." He sat back down and looked at her over the rim of his cup, and there was something in his face she'd spent her whole life learning to read on other faces, because it was the face she wore herself in the mirror after the vials were corked and the shop went dark. Loneliness, wearing hospitality like a borrowed coat. "But nothing's free up here. Not passage, not shelter, not love. Everything costs somebody something. Your mother understood that better than anyone." He drank. "So does the crown. That's why they'll win, unless people start being willing to pay."

Astrid looked at Wren — bread crumbs on her chin, dark eyes tracking the firelight, the black glass shard peeking from her shirt where it hummed its low, almost inaudible note, like a struck bell remembered rather than heard.

"What do you know about the crown these days?" Astrid asked.

"Enough to be frightened." Ferro set down his cup. "Inquisitors on roads that haven't seen a uniform in twenty years. Tolls tripled. And men coming back from Vaelmoor wrong — smiling too much, remembering too little. My best runner went down in spring to move a shipment of sigil-ink. Came back in summer. Didn't know my name." His jaw worked. "Knew his own, though. Barely. Kept saying it over and over, like a man holding onto a rope."

Wren had gone still. Her cup hovered halfway to her mouth.

Then, so quietly Astrid almost missed it, the child hummed — two notes, a third, a falling fourth. The same phrase she'd hummed on the attic stairs. The shard in her shirt brightened faintly, and for one heartbeat the firelight in the room bent toward her, every flame leaning like grass in wind.

Ferro froze with his cup at his lips. Slowly, he lowered it.

"That song," he whispered. "Your mother used to hum that. Working the ink, sealing a crate, always that tune. Said it was a counting song. Said it counted things down." He looked between them — the child glowing faintly at the collarbone, the woman gone pale as paper. "Counted what down, Maren never told me. But I'm an old man and I've buried too many friends to be polite about asking anymore." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, rain hammering the roof like a fist demanding entry. "What is she, Astrid? What in the name of every saint the Purge burned — what did your mother leave on this earth?"

Astrid opened her mouth.

And that was when the dog started barking.

* * *

Ferro kept no dog. The voice that erupted from the rocks above the tollhouse belonged to something bigger and angrier, and it was answered a heartbeat later by a second voice, human, carrying flatly through the rain:

"—tracks come off the ridge here! Light in the window — someone's home!"

Chairs went over. Ferro was already moving with a speed that shamed his years, snuffing the lantern, kicking a woven mat aside to reveal an iron ring set in the floorboards. "Cellar," he hissed. "Don't breathe. Don't touch the second shelf, it's wired to a bell. Astrid—" He caught her arm at the lip of the dark, and in the last light of the stove she saw his face stripped of everything except urgency. "If they take you, they don't get her. Whatever else happens. Swear it."

Astrid looked at Wren, who was already climbing down into the cellar on her own, small hands finding holds in the dark as though darkness were a country she had citizenship in.

"I swear," Astrid said, and dropped into the black, and heard the boards close over her head like water.

* * *

From below, the world was sound.

Boots in mud — four sets, heavy, unhurried, the tread of men wearing issued boots rather than bought ones. The knocking at the door. Ferro's voice transformed, pitched up into a wheedling senility he could summon like weather: *yes, yes, come in, mind the step, cold night, cold night.* Then the crunch of boots indoors, the clink of gear, and a voice that made the skin crawl up the back of Astrid's neck — smooth, dry, amused, a voice that sounded like it had been assembled from other voices.

"Inquisitor's warrant, grandfather. We're looking for a woman and a child. The child is crown property."

"Crown property," Ferro repeated, chuckling wetly. "Everything's crown property these days. Even the rain, eh? Even the rain."

Silence. Long enough that Astrid's lungs began to burn with the effort of quiet. In the pitch dark she felt rather than saw Wren find her in the blackness — a small cold hand closing around her fingers, and the shard between them warming like a coal, steady, patient, *there*.

"The toll records," said the smooth voice. "Who's passed your gate this month?"

"Records? Ha! I keep records up here." A pause, and Astrid heard it — heard the exact moment Ferro decided to pay. She had watched men make that decision her whole life; she could hear its weight in a breath. Kindness always costs. Sometimes it costs the kind one. "Got 'em somewhere. Give me a hand, these old eyes—"

Boots moving away from her. Toward the far wall. Toward the ledger shelf.

And then, deliberately, carelessly, catastrophically, Ferro knocked a lamp off a table.

Glass burst. Oil caught. Fire unrolled across the tollhouse floor with a sound like applause, and the room above exploded into shouting — *put it out, put it OUT, you mad old—* — and in the chaos, in the smoke already seeping down through the boards, Ferro's voice rang out clear and cracked and utterly unafraid:

"Run, girl! The Nether gate, the old shrine road! *Run!*"

Astrid didn't hesitate. She scooped Wren onto her hip — the child weighed almost nothing, another small horror to add to the pile — shouldered open the cellar's rear hatch, and plunged out into the rain and the night, downhill toward the tree line while the tollhouse blazed orange against the rocks behind them and men shouted in it.

At the edge of the pines she risked one look back.

Ferro stood in his doorway, framed by his own burning house, waving the soldiers' attention toward himself with both arms like a man welcoming guests to a feast. Even at this distance, through rain and smoke, she could see he was laughing.

*Nothing's free up here,* he'd said. *Everything costs somebody something.*

She ran. Wren's face was pressed to her shoulder, and Astrid realized only hours later, when she finally let herself think about it, that the whole way down the black mountainside the child had been humming — that same falling phrase, over and over into the dark, soft and steady as a pulse. Not frightened. Counting.

Behind them, above the drowned valley, Mount Cael waited under its cloud. And on the shrine road, half a night's climb ahead, something carved into living stone was already beginning, very faintly, to glow.
