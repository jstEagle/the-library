## 7. What the Mountain Remembers

The wayshrine appeared out of the snow like a tooth.

Wren saw it first — she always saw things first now, though she could not have said why. It stood at the shoulder of the pass where the smugglers' road bent around a spine of black granite, a squat arch of weathered stone no taller than Astrid's chest, crusted with ice and lichen and centuries of travelers' neglect. Someone had carved symbols into its face long ago, deep grooves worn shallow by wind, and someone else had tried to chisel them out after that, and someone after that had tried to carve them again over the scars, so that the whole surface was a palimpsest of devotion and fear.

Wren stopped walking without deciding to stop walking.

Astrid took three more steps before she noticed, turned back, and followed Wren's gaze to the arch. Her breath came out in white plumes, her hood was rimed with frost, and there was a new wariness in her eyes that had not been there six days ago, before the inquisitor with the glassy smile had come asking questions in Greyhollow.

"What is it?" Astrid asked. She asked that a lot lately. Not *what do you want* or *why are you staring* — those were questions for a stranger, a burden, a thing dragged along out of guilt. *What is it?* was a question you asked something you were beginning to listen to.

Wren didn't answer, because she didn't have words for it yet, even in the old tongue. The language inside her — the one that poured out of her mouth in the storm on Astrid's doorstep, the one that made Astrid go pale and still — had no name for this either. It only had recognition.

The shard of black glass in her pocket had begun to hum.

It hummed sometimes, usually at night, a vibration more felt than heard, like a trapped bee against her ribs through the wool of her coat. But this was different. This was louder. This was the glass waking up, straining toward the carved arch like a plant toward light, and beneath the humming Wren felt something else, something vast turning over in its sleep under the mountain's skin, and her whole small body knew what it was before her mind caught up:

It was home.

Not home the way the word meant when other children said it — a door, a fire, a voice calling them in from the dark. Home the way a river means the sea. Something in her had been poured out of that sleeping vastness, broken off like an icicle snapped from a roof's edge, and it had fallen a very long way into a small cold shape with hands and feet and hunger, and now it stood in the snow before a stone mouth full of old words and *ached.*

She reached out.

"Wren." Astrid's voice, sharp. "Wait—"

Her fingers touched the carving.

* * *

The world came apart like wet paper.

There was no falling, but there was falling-ness, a sensation of dropping through layers, and each layer was a memory but none of them were hers — except they were all hers, they were in her blood, in the black glass, in the tongue behind her teeth —

Stone. Heat. A cavern so large the ceiling was a rumor, lit by a thousand forges whose flames burned colors flame should not burn: green-gold, white-violet, a blue so deep it looked like looking down into night water. And people — no, not people, though they wore bodies like coats wear shoulders. They moved around the forges with hammers longer than their arms, and every strike rang not as sound but as *meaning*, and the meaning sank into the rock and stayed there, glowing.

Smiths. The word arrived whole, unasked-for: *smiths*. Not smiths of iron. Smiths of binding.

And in the center of the cavern floor, kneeling on bare stone, was a king.

He was young — younger than his crown, which sat heavy on his brow, a circlet of unworked dark metal, plain as a shackle. His knees had gone white against the stone; he had been kneeling a long time. His face was the kind of face that tries to be brave and mostly succeeds, and there were tears cutting clean lines through the dust on his cheeks, and he was not weeping for himself.

*"Do it,"* he said, and his voice echoed strangely, doubled, as though two throats spoke at once — no. As though the mountain itself repeated him. *"While I still mean it."*

The eldest smith stepped forward. She was ancient in a way that had nothing to do with years; her skin was like bark, her eyes like banked coals, and when she looked at the king, Wren felt the look land in her own chest, here, now, in the snow, because the eldest smith was looking at him the way you look at something you are about to hurt forever.

*"You understand what we take,"* the eldest smith said. It was not really a question. Nothing in this place seemed capable of being merely a question; everything was oath-shaped.

*"Everything,"* the king said. *"That is the price. That has always been the price. A crown that remembers nothing cannot be tempted by anything."*

*"And if the hunger comes anyway — if something older than hunger finds the hollow we make and moves in—"*

*"Then it will wear a king who cannot be reasoned with, flattered, frightened, or bought,"* the king said, and here his voice did break, just once, just slightly, like ice under a boot. *"And the passes stay sealed. And the thing beneath stays sealed. And my children's children live."* He bowed his head until his brow nearly touched the stone. *"I would rather be a locked door than a failed father. Forge it."*

The smiths raised their hammers.

And they sang.

Wren had thought, until this moment, that she understood the old tongue, that the words that fell out of her mouth in storms and nightmares were the whole of it. She had been a child reading one page of a book and calling herself literate. The forging-song was the language entire — every syllable a pillar, every harmony a wall, and the meaning of it did not pass through her ears into her mind but *through her bones into whatever she was underneath*, and the meaning was:

*Hold. Hold fast. Hold even against the holder. Let nothing in, let nothing out, let the door have no key, let the lock have no memory, let the memory live in the stone and the stone live in the blood and the blood — *

The blood. The song bent toward her, all of it, every hammer-strike and every harmony, bending like iron toward a lodestone, and the eldest smith turned her coal-fire eyes away from the king and looked directly — impossibly, across centuries, across death — at *her.*

*"This one carries our mark,"* the eldest smith said, and her voice was wonder and grief in equal measure. *"Look. Look how it answers. Little fragment. Little splinter of the great work. What hand broke you loose?"*

And Wren wanted to answer, opened whatever she had instead of a mouth in that place, and found she could not, because she did not know. She remembered being vast. She remembered being lattice — a web of light threaded through the roots of the mountains, ten thousand sigils burning in the dark like a city seen from above at night, and herself part of it, a single bright knot among thousands, singing with all the rest, holding with all the rest, *whole.* And then she remembered — not the breaking itself, that was torn out, that was a wound shaped exactly like a missing memory — but the aftermath: cold, small, alone, rain, mud, a door, a woman with tired eyes who should have turned her away and didn't.

Astrid.

Even here, even inside a vision older than kingdoms, the thought of her landed warm, and the eldest smith's gaze sharpened.

*"Ah,"* she said softly. *"You've been carried. By hands. By a keeper's hands, if I know the weight of it."* Something moved across her bark-ancient face that might have been hope. *"Then perhaps the breaking was not an ending. Perhaps it was a seed."*

*"Eldest."* One of the other smiths, urgent, hammer lowered. *"The king weakens. The hollow must be cut while his consent holds true. Consent fades like milk in summer."*

*"Yes."* The eldest smith lifted her hammer, and the forge-light ran along its edge like water. *"Forgive us, majesty,"* she told the kneeling man, gently, terribly. *"We will make you a door that can never open. And we will hate ourselves for it every day of our long lives. And it will be the right thing, and it will not feel like it, and that too is part of the work."*

*"I know,"* whispered the king, eyes closed, tears drying on his face. *"Strike."*

They struck.

And the crown — the plain dark metal on his brow — began to drink.

Wren felt it happen and screamed, or tried to, and discovered that in this place her scream was also part of the song, that her horror harmonized with the forging whether she willed it or not, because she was *made of the same material,* she was a knot of the same lattice, and the crown drank the king's memories the way a drain drinks water: his mother's lullaby, gone. His first snowfall, gone. The face of the man who taught him to ride, the smell of his childhood kitchen, the name of the nurse who bandaged his knee when he was five — gone, gone, gone, each one a small light extinguishing behind his eyes, and his body stayed kneeling, stayed consenting, because consent had been given *before* the forgetting and the forgetting could not undo it, that was the terrible cleverness of it, that was the trap working perfectly.

The last thing to go was not a memory. The last thing to go was his *name.*

He said it himself, one final time, as a gift, as a key turned deliberately in a lock: *"My name is Aldric, son of Maren, and I give it freely."*

And then his eyes opened, and they were empty as winter windows, and the crown sat on his brow shining faintly with everything it had eaten, and the smiths wept as they worked, all of them, even the eldest, hot tears hissing on hot stone, and the song changed key — from forging to sealing — and the seals went out from that place like ripples from a dropped stone, out through the mountain's bones, into every wayshrine and boundary-marker and buried gate, and one ripple passed through a small squat arch on a smugglers' road and into a girl standing in the snow with her fingers on a carving—

* * *

"—breathe. Breathe. *Breathe.*"

Astrid had her by the shoulders, shaking her, and the sky was gray and ordinary above them both, and Wren was lying half in the snowdrift beside the wayshrine with her boots in the air and her heart slamming like a fist on a door.

"I'm here," Wren said.

It came out in the old tongue, automatic, and then she caught it, wrestled it, dragged it across the gap into common speech, clumsy as a child carrying water in cupped hands. "I'm here. I'm — I saw it. I saw *him.*"

"Saw who?" Astrid crouched over her, and her face — Astrid's face was doing several things at once, fear and suspicion and that stubborn unwilling tenderness she kept trying to smother and kept failing to smother. "Wren. Your nose is bleeding. You touched that stone and your eyes went white as milk and you started speaking — not speaking, *singing* — and the carvings lit up, Wren. The ones that have been dead for five hundred years. Lit up like embers. So you're going to tell me what you saw, and you're going to do it slowly."

Wren sat up. Snow slid off her back. Her head ached in a way that was not like pain but like fullness, like a cup filled past the brim, and the vision was already fading at its edges the way dreams fade, but its center held: the kneeling king, the drinking crown, the eldest smith's coal-fire eyes finding her across the centuries.

*A keeper's hands.*

"There was a king," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, deeper than usual, scraped. "Before. Before Vaelmoor, maybe. Or the first Vaelmoor. He knelt in front of the smiths and told them to do it. To take his memories. All of them. Even his name." She watched Astrid's expression and saw the exact moment the words landed, saw the memory-cutter flinch at the mechanics of it — *take his memories, all of them* — because Astrid of all people knew what that extraction looked like, knew the cost of it in a way no one else alive could. "They made a crown that eats. So that nothing could get inside the king's head and turn him. No bribes, no lies, no whispers. An empty room can't be argued with."

"And the thing beneath," Astrid said slowly. Her eyes had gone distant, calculating, and Wren realized with a small cold shock that Astrid was fitting pieces together faster than Wren had. "The passes. The wards. If the crown was forged to keep something sealed under Thornfell—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Wren. The king in the capital. Aldric. Everyone says he's changed these last few years. Withdrawn. Cold. The inquisitors, the memory-tithes, the villages going quiet." Her jaw tightened. "Everyone says he's become cruel. But if the crown eats — if it's been eating for hundreds of years, and something found the empty room—"

"Then the king isn't cruel," Wren finished, and the old tongue surged up in her throat again and she let one word of it out because some things could only be said properly in the language they were born in: "*Vessel.*"

Astrid went very still.

"That's what it's called," Wren whispered. "The thing wearing him. That's its name. Or — no." She pressed her palms against her eyes, chasing the fading vision. "No, that's what the smiths called the *crown.* The vessel. Made to hold. Made to be filled with one king's emptied self and nothing else, forever." Her hands were shaking. "Something else climbed in. Something's using it wrong. Using *him* wrong. And the wards are failing because — because the crown is supposed to hold the seals shut from the inside, like a hand on a door, and the hand belongs to whoever wears it, and if the thing wearing it wants the door *open*—"

"Then it's opening the door." Astrid rose to her feet in one motion, sharp and fast, and began pacing the length of the wayshrine, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, her breath smoking. "The tithes. Gods, the memory-tithes — the inquisitors collecting memories from whole districts, whole villages. I thought it was control. I thought it was the crown hoarding power the way crowns do. But if the Vessel needs to eat to unbind — if every ward it breaks needs feeding—" She stopped pacing. Looked at Wren. Really looked, and Wren saw something in her face she had never seen there before, not in the attic in Greyhollow, not on the road, not once: Astrid Kavanagh looked afraid, and did not bother hiding it.

"You saw all this," Astrid said, "from a rock."

"It's not a rock." Wren touched the arch again, lightly, and this time the carvings only warmed under her fingers, dully glowing, a hearth rather than a flood. "It's a door. A small one. A listening-post. The smiths put them everywhere, along the roads, at the boundaries — places where the seals run close to the surface. So the keepers could check them. Listen through them. Feel if anything was—" she hunted for the word, found it in the old tongue, translated badly— "*sick.*"

"And are they? Sick?"

Wren listened. She didn't know how she did it; it wasn't hearing, it was more like putting her ear against her own ribs and attending to the hum. The black glass in her pocket had gone quiet, sated, and beneath it the vast sleeping thing under the mountain turned again, restless, feverish.

"Yes," she said. "All of them. Everywhere. The seals are stretched thin as old cloth." She looked up at Astrid, and the terror in her must have shown, because Astrid crouched back down, close, close enough that Wren could see the frost melting in the strands of her red hair. "And there's more. There was a woman. The eldest. She saw me — inside it, in the vision, she *looked at me* — and she said I carry their mark. She called me a fragment. A splinter of the great work." Wren swallowed. "Astrid, I think I know what I am. I think I was *part of the seal.* Part of the lattice the smiths made. And then something broke me off, and I don't remember what, or who, or why — that part's torn out — but I wasn't made to be a girl. I was made to be a piece of a door."

For a long moment Astrid said nothing. The wind moved through the pass, keening faintly against the arch, and somewhere below them a hawk rode the thermals above the valley, and the world continued being ordinary, which felt like a small miracle and also like a lie.

"Well," Astrid said at last, dry as gravel, and Wren heard the tremor underneath the dryness and loved her for it. "Then whoever broke you off made a mistake worth thanking them for. Because the door-piece grew hands. And learned to steal bread. And got mud all over my floor." She exhaled, long and slow, and her breath shook only slightly. "Here's what matters. The vision gave us a direction. The smiths, the forge, the seals — that all lives somewhere. Under the mountain. And if the wards are failing, and the crown's been hollowed, then somebody has to reach the place where it was all made before the Vessel finishes unbinding it." She stood, shouldered her pack, and offered Wren her hand, gloved and scarred and steady. "Which means we stop running south like rabbits and start climbing east like fools. Toward Cael."

Wren took her hand and let herself be pulled up.

But as they turned back onto the road, as the wayshrine fell behind them and its glow faded from her mind's eye, one image stayed with her, burned in deeper than all the rest — not the king, not the crown, not the weeping smiths.

The eldest smith's face, in the moment before the hammers fell. The way she had looked at the kneeling, sacrificing king with love and horror braided together, and had said, *it will be the right thing, and it will not feel like it.*

Because Wren had seen, in that same moment, something the eldest smith could not see. She had seen the far end of the thread — the vision's gift or curse, showing her not just what was but what leaned toward being. She had seen a vault beneath a throne. She had seen a woman pouring her memories into the lattice until she glowed translucent as morning mist. And she had seen herself stepping into that glow, taking half the weight, splitting it, holding on.

The past was not finished with them. It was reaching forward with both hands.

Wren walked on through the snow beside Astrid, a fragment of a broken door learning to be a girl, and said nothing about any of it. Some knowledge, she understood already, was a burden best carried alone a little while longer — until the road, and the mountain, and whatever waited beneath them, forced it into the light.

Behind them, faint and fading, the wayshrine's carvings dimmed back to stone.

Ahead, east, Mount Cael stood against the clouds with its summit lost in weather, patient as a held breath.
