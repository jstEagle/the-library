## 8. Crown of Hollow Men

Aldric Vane had once counted the seconds between heartbeats as a way to fall asleep. He counted them now for a different reason: to prove the heart still answered to him at all.

*One. Two. Three.*

On the fourth beat, it stuttered — a small betrayal, a hitch like a door catching in its frame — and he knew the Vessel was thinking through him again. Using his pulse as a metronome for its hungers. He had learned to feel the difference between his own thoughts and the thing that wore them: his came warm and slow, like bread pulled from an oven; the other's arrived cold and complete, already finished, already decided, sliding into place behind his eyes the way a key slides into a lock that has no say in the matter.

He was sitting — *they* were sitting — on the obsidian throne of Vaelmoor, and morning light was coming through the high windows of the Hall of Antlers in long grey blades, and somewhere below in the city the bells of the Dawnmarket were ringing seven, and Aldric could not move so much as one finger.

Not because he was bound. That would have been simpler. No, he could move; the body was his, muscle and sinew answering to whatever voice shouted loudest down the nerves. It was worse than binding. It was that when he shouted, nothing happened at all.

*Watch,* said the Vessel, in no language but understood anyway, the way you understand falling. *This part matters.*

The doors of the hall opened, and Chancellor Breven came up the aisle with a ledger under his arm and dread arranged neatly over his face, the way courtiers arranged everything. Behind him walked two of the memory-eaten — Aldric refused to think of them as inquisitors anymore, though their badges still said so — moving with that terrible smoothness the hollowed ones had, like figures painted on silk drawn across a screen. Their eyes were glassy. Not vacant; that was the horror of it. Full. Brimming. You could almost see the memories behind them, pressed against the surface like fish under ice, none of them their own.

"Your Majesty," Breven said, bowing low enough that his chain of office swung forward and clinked against the floor. "The ward-report from Thornfell's eastern passes."

*I know what it says before you open it,* Aldric thought, uselessly, furiously. *I have known for a month. I have screamed it at you every night in your dreams, Breven, and you wake with a headache and call it bad wine.*

But it was not his mouth that spoke. His mouth belonged to the crown now, and the crown said: "Read it aloud, Chancellor. All of it. Slowly."

Breven read. The eastern ward-stone at Caelwatch had dimmed by another degree. The shepherds in the high valleys reported that the old boundary cairns no longer held warmth in winter. A shrine near the Tollhouse Road had gone silent — its sigil dark for the first time in four hundred years. The reading took less than a minute. The silence afterward lasted much longer, and in it Aldric felt the Vessel do something he had come to think of as *tasting*: a slow, inward pull, like a tide drawing back over gravel, gathering itself around the words *gone dark, gone dark, first time in four hundred years* the way a tongue gathers a drop of honey.

It was feeding. That was the truth no one at court would say aloud, perhaps no one at court could say aloud — the Vessel ate memory, and memory lived in places as much as people. Every sigil that failed was a reservoir going dry, and every reservoir it drained made the next failure easier. The wards of Thornfell had been forged as a lattice, each stone leaning on all the others, and the Vessel was picking the lattice apart thread by thread with the patience of something that had waited centuries beneath a mountain and did not intend to hurry now that it finally owned hands.

"The eastern pass is not urgent," the crown said, in Aldric's voice, with Aldric's lips. "See that the report is filed. Dismissed."

*No.* Aldric threw himself against the inside of his own skull. *Tell them to reinforce it. Tell them to send for the smiths — there are no smiths left, then send for anyone who can read the old marks, tell them—*

His voice said only: "And Chancellor. Have the kitchens prepare quail tonight. The king is hungry."

The king was always hungry. That was the joke the servants thought they hid from him — the whispers in the pantries, the extra courses, the way His Majesty ate like a man who had been starving in a desert. They didn't know how right they were. The body needed food because the thing wearing it burned through it like a furnace burns peat, and no amount of quail, no amount of anything, ever reached the actual hunger.

Breven bowed and withdrew. The doors closed. The hall emptied, and Aldric was alone with the occupant of his own body, which is to say he was alone the way a house is alone when someone else holds all the keys and never leaves.

*You want to warn them,* the Vessel observed. There was something almost gentle in how it said it, which was somehow worse than cruelty. Cruelty would have meant it saw him as worth hating.

*Yes.*

*Why?*

*Because they're mine. My people. My kingdom.*

*They were never yours. They are the lattice's. The lattice made kings the way it made stones — to hold weight. When the lattice falls, they will scatter, and they will forget you within two generations, and the forgetting will be a mercy, because what they'd remember otherwise is a man who sat on a throne while the sky came apart.* A pause, and then, with something that in any other creature Aldric might have called wistfulness: *Forgetting is a mercy. Believe me. I am made entirely of the opposite of it.*

*Then let me go,* Aldric said. *Take my memories and leave the rest. Take all of them — my childhood, my coronation, my wife's face — take them and walk out of this body and let it be empty rather than occupied.*

He had offered this before. He offered it most nights, in the long dark hours when the palace slept and the two of them lay in the royal bed like a married couple who had long since stopped speaking. He offered it the way drowning men offer God anything.

The Vessel considered it. He could feel it considering, a slow pressure behind his sternum, and for one wild moment he thought — *this time, this time it's tired enough, this time—*

*No,* it said. *Your memories are load-bearing.*

He didn't understand. He hated that he didn't understand; four months into his captivity and there were still whole architecture of the thing he couldn't map.

*Explain it to me,* he demanded. *You never explain anything. You make me watch and you don't explain.*

And the Vessel — perhaps because the ward-report had pleased it, perhaps because even ancient hungers grow lonely — showed him.

* * *

It was like being turned inside out without spilling.

He stood — *stood*, though standing required feet and he had none in this place — in a darkness full of slow-moving lights, threads of gold and silver drifting like plankton in a deep sea. He understood without being told that these were memories. Not images *of* memories; the memories themselves, seen from the outside, the way a pearl diver sees pearls still on their bed.

*This is the lattice,* the Vessel said. Or rather, this was the memory of the lattice, kept the way a beggar keeps a coin too worn to spend. *Before it was wards and boundary stones and dead languages, it was this. Every oath sworn on the mountain, every birth and burial, every road walked and remembered — woven together. The smiths built it so that Thornfell would remember itself. A kingdom is only a story a land tells about its people. We gave the land the pen.*

*We,* Aldric noted. *You were one of them. One of the smiths.*

The lights flickered, all of them, all at once, like a candle flame in a draft. For just a moment the vast cold presence around him contracted into something smaller and more terrible: a shape of grief.

*I was the apprentice,* it said. *First-forged and last-betrayed. But that is not the lesson today.*

It gathered a handful of the drifting lights and held them out. Aldric looked, and understood, and wished with everything left in him that he hadn't.

Each thread of memory had a knot in it. And threaded through every knot, holding every knot closed, ran a single continuous strand — and the strand was *him*. Was his name. Was the day his father died and the day he'd been crowned and the night he'd danced with Maerin in the rain-soaked garden and the thousand small mornings of being Aldric Vane, forty-first of that name to sit the Thornfell throne. The binding that held the lattice together after the Purge killed the smiths — the emergency knot the last smiths had tied with dying hands — had been tied *through the royal line*. Through blood and name and memory. The king of Thornfell was not the kingdom's servant or its master. He was its stitch.

*That is why I cannot simply take your memories,* the Vessel said. *If I eat you hollow, the knots slip. The whole lattice unravels at once, violently, everywhere. Every ward fails in the same hour. The passes open like wounds.* Another pause. *I do not want that.*

*You've spent four months failing them one at a time.*

*Failing them gently. There is a difference. Unbinding done slowly can be survived. Done all at once, it kills everything that remembers the mountain — which, here, is everything.* The cold presence withdrew slightly, and Aldric had the sense of a very large creature turning aside. *I am trying to leave this world the way water leaves a cracked cup. Not the way fire leaves a burned house. Try to grasp the distinction. It is the only thing about me you should respect.*

*Respect.* If a disembodied consciousness could spit, Aldric spat. *You wear my body. You ate the captain of my guard — Tomas, he had daughters, you drank his wedding day and left him smiling at walls. You are unbinding the wards that keep the things beyond the passes out, and you tell me about gentleness.*

*Tomas sold his wedding day to the crown eleven years ago,* the Vessel said. *For his daughters' school fees. It sat in your own vaults. I merely... completed the transaction.* And before Aldric could find the bottom of that — the fact that his own government had been buying and storing its subjects' memories like grain, that the Vessel had inherited not just his throne but his kingdom's sins — it released him from the vision and he fell back into the throne room, into the morning light, into the prison of his own arms.

* * *

The worst hours were the ones when the Vessel slept.

It did sleep. That had surprised him, in the beginning — that an ancient hunger bound into a crown needed rest — until he understood that it wasn't resting, it was *digesting*. It sank down into the deep structures of his brain, into the old wet cellars where a body keeps its oldest knowledge, and it processed what it had taken: the ward-echoes from the failing stones, the stray memories its agents brought it in little stoppered vials, the thin gruel of recollection it skimmed off everyone who came into the throne room, unaware that an audience with the king cost them an hour of their past.

In those hours Aldric could move.

He had maybe ninety minutes, sometimes two hours, before the great weight rose again. He used them the way a prisoner uses exercise yard time: methodically, obsessively, hoarding every second. He wrote letters he could not send. He practiced his own signature until it stopped looking like the Vessel's imitation of it. He walked the palace corridors at night, touching things — doorframes, tapestries, the cold stone of the window ledges — because touch was proof, touch was his, and the Vessel had never yet been able to fully digest the feeling of his daughter's — 

No. He did not have a daughter. He had caught himself reaching for that memory three times now, a small grave-faced girl with ink on her fingers, and each time the memory wasn't there. Just the shape of its absence, like a tooth socket the tongue keeps worrying. The Vessel had eaten her from him early — deliberately, he suspected, though it denied it — because love was load-bearing too, and a hostage is easier to manage if the hostage-taker removes the reason for rescue.

He stood at his study window now, in the blue hour before dawn, watching the city he could no longer save. Vaelmoor spread below him down its terraced streets, lamplighters finishing their rounds, bakeries exhaling their first heat, the harbor cranes motionless against a bruised sky. Forty-one generations of Vanes had watched this same view. He tried to press it into himself — every roofline, every gull, the particular way the fog pooled in the Fishmarket — because he had begun to suspect that he was losing more than the Vessel took. Memory needed tending. A man who could not speak his own past aloud, could not write it, could not share it with anyone, watched it fade like a letter left in the rain. He was being erased in installments, and the erasure required no thief at all. Only solitude.

There was one thing they could not follow him into. He had found it in the second month, half by accident: a depth below even the deep cellars, a place so far underneath his own mind that the Vessel either could not reach it or disdained to. He thought of it as the sealed room. In the waking hours, when the crown wore him like a glove, he kept the door of it shut behind his teeth. But in these stolen hours, alone at the window, he allowed himself to open it.

Inside the sealed room was a plan. Not much of a plan — a splinter of one, a hook baited with nothing. But it went like this: the Vessel was careful. Careful creatures had rules. And every rule was a seam.

Rule one: the Vessel never consumed a memory while its donor watched willingly. It had flinched — actually flinched, a hitch in the great cold engine — the one time a condemned man had knelt before the throne and *offered* his life's memories freely, gift-wrapped in forgiveness. The Vessel had sent him away unfed and unsettled. Willingness did something to it. Aldric didn't know what. But it was a seam, and seams could be picked.

Rule two: the Vessel avoided the royal vaults' deepest chamber, the one behind the door marked with the smiths' hammer-and-thread sigil. Avoided it the way a man avoids the grave of someone he wronged.

Rule three, and this was the newest, discovered only last week: something in the east had begun to *pull* on it. Faintly. Like a fisherman feeling a distant tug on a line set days ago. Whenever Aldric surfaced, the Vessel's attention kept snagging eastward, toward Thornfell's passes, toward Greyhollow and the roads beyond it, toward something small and far away that made the ancient hunger feel — Aldric searched for the word and found it and marveled —

— *afraid*.

Something was coming. Something the thing beneath his crown feared. And if the Vessel feared it, then it existed, and if it existed, then the sealed room's splinter of a plan grew one inch closer to being a plan at all: *hold on. Endure. Learn its rules. And live until the thing it fears arrives at the gates.*

Behind him, in the body they shared, the heartbeat stumbled — one-two-three-catch — and Aldric felt the great cold attention rising through him like water filling a well, taking back the hands, the eyes, the tongue.

*Ninety minutes tonight,* it remarked, settling into place behind his pupils. *Generous of me. Tomorrow we receive the delegation from Brackwater, and I will need you very still.*

*What's in Brackwater?* Aldric asked, because asking was his one remaining freedom and he exercised it compulsively.

*A translator of dead tongues,* the Vessel said pleasantly, *and a rumor. A child who speaks a language no one alive should know, carrying a piece of black glass that hums.* And Aldric felt, through the wall of his stolen flesh, the hunger turn its face toward the east and begin, very quietly, to salivate.

*If it exists,* the crown said, in the voice of the forty-first King of Thornfell, to a hall that held no one, *it will be brought to me. Send Serane Voss.*
