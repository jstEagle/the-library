## 9. Serane's Hunger

The rain in Greyhollow had a smell to it, Serane Voss thought — coal smoke and river rot and the particular sweetness of a city that had learned to look away from its own gutters. She stood beneath the eave of the chandler's shop across the lane from the memory-cutter's tenement and did not feel the cold. She hadn't felt cold in eleven years. That was the first thing she'd sold: the memory of winter, of her mother wrapping a scarf twice around her neck, of chapped knuckles stinging as they warmed. She remembered *that* she had known these things. She kept an inventory, meticulous as scripture. But the knowledge sat in her like coins in a dead man's pocket — proof of wealth she could no longer spend.

The child had been here. The evidence was written in the mud of the alley behind the tenement: small footprints, a stride too long for the legs that made them, someone running with purpose beyond her years. And beside them, larger prints, half-flooded already, leading to the stable yard where a lathered horse told its own story of departure in the small hours.

Three days gone. Perhaps four.

Serane turned her face up into the rain and let it strike her skin without feeling it, the way she let most things strike her skin.

*You are what they made you,* said a voice in her that still occasionally spoke — fainter now each year, like a song heard through a wall. She had learned to ignore it. Ignoring was a discipline, and discipline was the one thing the crown had never managed to take from her, though not for want of trying.

She crossed the lane.

* * *

The attic door had been forced badly — a crowbar, impatient hands — and inside, the memory-cutter's workspace lay strewn like a surgeon's theater after a massacre. Glass vials rolled underfoot, empty, their stoppers scattered. The bone stylus had been snapped in half and left on the table like an accusation. Someone had searched this place with fury rather than method.

Not her people. Her people searched like priests.

She crouched by the workbench and touched nothing for a long moment, reading the room the way other women read letters. A memory-cutter's bench told you everything about her if you knew how to look. The arrangement of the vials — sorted by vintage, by intensity, by the color of their sediment. The worn groove in the armrest where a client's head had rested a thousand times. And there, in a shallow drawer kicked half-shut in the panic of flight: a single vial, unbroken, its contents shifting with a light that had nothing to do with lamplight.

Sigil-light. Old light.

Serane lifted it between two fingers and held it to the window, and something in her chest — some atrophied muscle she'd have sworn she no longer possessed — clenched.

She knew this technique. Of course she knew it; she'd been sent to study under the cutters of Vaelmoor before they gave her the gray coat and the license to open heads. But this was different. This was the old way, the pre-Purge way, the cuts made along lines that followed no anatomy she'd ever been taught. This was a hand trained by hands that had been ash for two hundred years.

*Kavanagh,* said the voice through the wall.

"Shut up," Serane said to the empty room, and pocketed the vial.

* * *

Inquisitors were given quarters in whatever town they worked — always the finest room in the second-finest inn, a courtesy that was also a leash. Serane took hers in Greyhollow at the Sign of the Drowned Bell, and that night she did the thing she permitted herself only when a trail ran hot: she opened her own inventory.

It was not theft, exactly. What she was about to do, she did to herself, with herself, and the crown's physicians had assured her a hundred times that recall of purchased memories was perfectly safe so long as one didn't exceed the prescribed dosage. They had said this in the same gentle voices they used for everything, and she had believed them at nineteen because believing them had been easier than the alternative.

She lay back on the narrow bed, pressed the stopper of the first vial to the soft place beneath her ear, and let the winter come back.

Cold. Real cold, the kind that got into bone. A kitchen with a low ceiling and a fire that smoked. A woman humming — off-key, always off-key, and laughing about it. Hands, rough from laundry work, wrapping a scarf twice around a small neck. *Twice, little wren, or the wind finds the gap.* A name. Her name, the first one, the one nobody used anymore. A girl called—

The vial emptied and the memory sealed itself away again, and Serane lay in the dark of the Drowned Bell with tears cooling on her face that she did not wipe away, cataloguing what she'd bought: forty seconds of a mother's hands. She could afford perhaps three more doses before the vial degraded past recall. She had been rationing it for six years.

This was the arithmetic of her life, and she performed it every night whether she drew on the inventory or not. One childhood, sold at nineteen to the Office of Inquiry in exchange for her father's debts forgiven and a place in the gray coats. They had taken it in a single sitting — the whole of it, birth to womanhood, poured out of her into twelve vials while her mother wept in the waiting room below. Twelve vials. The crown had valued her first seventeen years at twelve vials and a salary, and she had signed because her mother's weeping would have become homelessness otherwise, and because she had wanted, more than she had ever wanted anything, to matter.

Eleven of those vials had been consumed by her superiors over the years — "operational necessity," the requisition forms said — fed into the mouths of men who needed to know how a poor girl from the Tannery Row thought so they could better find others like her. She had watched an assistant minister drink her tenth birthday once, at a banquet, as a novelty. He'd laughed at something in it and wiped his mouth.

One vial remained. The winter one. The scarf one.

She kept it the way misers keep their last coin: not to spend, but to know it was there.

*They will ask you to give it up someday,* said the voice through the wall. *When you're close enough to matter. They'll call it duty.*

"I know," Serane said to the ceiling. "I've read my own file."

That was the worst of it — she had read her own file. Clearance enough to see the assessments. *Subject displays exceptional retention of procedural memory alongside total affective amputation. Ideal candidate for field inquiry.* They had measured the hole they cut in her and pronounced the edges clean.

* * *

Morning brought the informant, a rat-faced man from the tallow trade who sold information to both sides of every law in Greyhollow and was therefore, in Serane's experience, the most reliable creature in the city. He sat across from her at the inn's common table, hands wrapped around a cup he was being paid enough not to drink.

"The cutter," he said. "Kavanagh. Nobody knew much about her. Kept to herself, paid cash, went to no services, no festivals. The neighbors say she was polite the way a locked door is polite."

"And the child?"

"That's the strange part." He leaned in, lowering his voice as though the rain outside might carry tales. "Seen her once, week past, buying bread. Mud to the eyebrows, barefoot in that cold. And the baker's boy swears—" he glanced around, savoring it— "swears she talked to the loaves. Not *at* them, mind. Talked *to* them. In the old tongue. And the loaves rose fresh, he says, though they'd been baked since dawn."

Serane said nothing. The baker's boy was a fool and half his tale was fermentation of the ordinary kind, but underneath the embellishment lay a fact that made her palms sweat against the table's wood: the old tongue. Spoken aloud, casually, by a child of seven or eight. The last recorded speaker had died during the Purge, and the crown had spent two centuries and a fortune making certain the language stayed dead, because a language is just a key that thinks, and Thornfell had been locked for very good reasons.

"The Kavanagh woman," she said. "Anyone visit her, regular?"

"One man. Smuggler, by his boots. Older fellow, goes by Ferro, works the toll road up the spine. Comes maybe twice a year, brings her things."

"Ferro." Serane wrote nothing down. Writing things down was for officers who needed paper to think. "Which direction does he run?"

"North and west. Through the passes." The rat-faced man hesitated, and then, because greed is honest even when men aren't: "There's a rumor the mountain roads have been strange lately. Wayshrines lit where nothing's lit them in living memory. My cousin drives goats near Cael's shoulder, says the stones hum at night. Says the sigils are waking up."

*Waking up,* echoed the voice through the wall, and for once Serane did not tell it to shut up, because it was only saying what she was already thinking.

The wards. The passes sealed for two hundred years by the binding, the great lattice of smith-work that kept what slept beneath the mountain asleep and what belonged outside the kingdom outside of it. His Majesty's government had grown very interested in the wards of late — new orders, new priorities, requisitions of memory-vials by the thousand, all flowing toward Vaelmoor. She had asked, once, carefully, what the memories were for. Her handler had smiled with all the warmth of a ledger.

"You don't need to know why, Inquisitor. You need to know who."

So she knew who. The who was a mud-caked child speaking a dead tongue, traveling north and west with a memory-cutter whose hands knew the Purge-forbidden craft, toward a mountain whose stones had begun to sing. And the crown wanted them found not alive, precisely, but *retrieved* — a word her handlers used the way butchers used knives.

*And you?* asked the voice through the wall. *What do you want?*

What she wanted was a question she had not permitted herself in eleven years, and it sat in her now like a swallowed stone. She wanted the trail to end. She wanted the child found, the report filed, the gray coat exchanged for the black coat and a seat at the table where decisions were made — that was the answer she had prepared, the answer that had carried her this far, the answer that tasted of ash when she rehearsed it.

Beneath it, in the place where the winter memory lived, another answer stirred and would not be named: she wanted, just once, to be the one who wrapped the scarf. To stand on the giving side of a small mercy instead of the taking side. Eleven years of opening other people's heads had taught her one thing above all others: everyone, in the deepest room of themselves, was keeping something warm against the cold. Everyone but her. She had sold her keeping-warm-things, and the crown had drunk them, and now she was only cold all the way through, efficient and glassy-eyed, the ideal candidate.

The memory-cutter Astrid Kavanagh stole memories for coin. Serane had sold hers for a salary. Somewhere between those two trades lay a difference so fine you could pass a blade through it, and Serane had begun, in the last few days, to feel that blade's edge from both sides.

*She keeps what she steals,* said the voice. *Look at the drawer she couldn't bear to empty.*

Serene had seen it — the one vial left behind in the ransacked attic, hidden in a kicked-shut drawer while everything else was scattered. Even fleeing for her life, the cutter had protected one memory above the rest. Serane carried it now against her own ribs, and she had not yet reported it. She told herself this was procedure: unregistered sigil-light, potentially evidence, requiring analysis. She told herself many things. She was very good at telling herself things; it was, after all, the family resemblance she shared with every hollowed man in Vaelmoor.

* * *

They rode north on the fourth day, six of them — Serane and five crown troopers whose memories had been lightly pruned for loyalty, which meant they obeyed her without the friction of independent judgment. Good soldiers. Dead-eyed ones. She sometimes caught herself watching them the way a drowning woman watches driftwood.

The smuggler's road climbed along the spine of the Thornfells, a track that appeared and vanished like a thought one wasn't supposed to be having. Twice they lost it entirely and twice Serane found it again by reading the stone — not the way Wren read it, if the reports were true, but the way an inquisitor reads anything: by looking for what someone had tried to hide. Cut steps worn smooth. Cairns stacked by hands that preferred not to be counted. A civilization of the overlooked, running parallel to the kingdom like blood through a body.

On the sixth day they reached a tollhouse wedged into a fold of the mountain, and Serane knew it for the place before her informant's geography confirmed it — Ferro's tollhouse, the Smugglers' Spine's one fixed point of mercy. She dismounted and stood looking at its smoking chimney for a long moment while her troopers waited behind her with the patience of pruned men.

Inside, she found the old man sitting by his fire as though he'd been expecting her for years, which, she suspected, in his line of work, amounted to the same thing as knowing.

"Inquisitor," he said, without rising. His eyes were remarkable — clear and brown and utterly unafraid, which meant he had already decided what he was willing to lose. "Heard your horses on the switchback an hour ago. Slow going, weather like this. Tea?"

"Where are they?" Serane asked.

"Gone through," Ferro said. He poured tea anyway, four cups, set one before her with a courtesy that was almost tender. "Day ahead of you, maybe more if the high passes hold snow. And before you ask the next question — I won't be answering it, and you'll be wanting to know why, and the why is that some debts you pay forward or you're not paying at all. A woman I owed my life to once had a daughter. That daughter has a child now. That's the whole of my arithmetic, and it's older than your warrant."

Serane looked at him for a long time. She looked at the four cups — one for himself, one for her, and two, she understood suddenly, set out of habit for guests who would not sit at this fire again. She looked at his hands, steady on the teapot, and recognized the stillness in them because she practiced it daily in front of her own mirror: the stillness of a person who has finished grieving in advance.

"You know what I'll do if you obstruct me," she said quietly. "You've read the statutes. You know what the gray coats are authorized to take."

"I know exactly what you take." Ferro met her eyes, and there was no hatred in it, which was somehow far worse. "I've seen your kind before, girl. Forty years on this road, I've seen the crown eat its share of children. You're not the first one they dressed in gray and pointed at doors." He slid one of the untouched cups across the table toward her, toward the space in front of her hands. "Drink your tea. You look like someone who hasn't been warm since you were small."

Serane did not drink it. She stood, and thanked him with a formality that cost her more than any threat she'd ever delivered, and walked out into the falling snow — and only at the edge of the yard did she pause, because the last thing Ferro said as she reached the door had followed her out:

"They went to the wayshrine at Cael's shoulder. If you're quick, you'll see what the mountain's showing them. Wouldn't want you to miss that, Inquisitor. Might be the closest thing to a miracle you'll ever be allowed."

* * *

They caught sight of the shrine at dusk, and Serane held up a fist to halt the troopers and watched from the ridge above, down into the bowl of the valley where the wayshrine stood — a ring of standing stones older than the kingdom, older than the crown, carved so densely with sigils that the rock seemed furred with writing.

Two figures stood among the stones. A tall woman in a patched coat. A small figure beside her.

Even at this distance, Serane could see that the sigils were lit.

Not glowing, exactly — *awake*, the way a sleeper's face is awake. Lines of pale gold moving through the carvings like blood returning to a numb limb, and the child had her palm flat against the central stone, and the woman stood close, one hand hovering near the child's shoulder without touching, and the light of the sigils reflected on both their faces.

And Serane Voss, who had not felt cold in eleven years, who had sold her winters and her mother's hands and her name, felt something move through the frozen country of herself — not warmth, nothing so kind, but the *memory of where warmth was supposed to go*. An ache shaped exactly like a missing thing.

She had been sent to retrieve an asset. She understood, watching the lamplight-gold crawl through those ancient stones, that she was looking at the thing the crown had been drinking memories to reach. Whatever slept in the binding, whatever the thousand requisitioned vials were feeding in Vaelmoor — it wanted this. It wanted the child and the lattice and the waking mountain, and it had sent its gray coats out to fetch, and Serane was the fetcher, and the fetchers of Vaelmoor did not ask what happened to the things they fetched.

*Now,* said the voice through the wall, clearer than it had been in years. *Now you choose what kind of hunger you are.*

Below, the woman named Astrid Kavanagh turned her head — sharply, like a deer — and looked directly up at the ridge where Serane stood. Distance should have made it impossible. Serane knew it was impossible.

Their eyes met anyway, across the darkening valley, hunter and hunted, two women made by the same trade pointing in opposite directions, and Serane saw in the stranger's face something that undid her more than any accusation could have: recognition. Not of her. Of what she was. The way one lamp recognizes another lamp burning the same oil.

Astrid's hand closed over the child's shoulder — touching her now, decisively, claiming her — and the light of the wayshrine went out all at once, like a held breath, and the two of them moved into the rocks and were gone.

Serane stood on the ridge a long moment, the snow gathering on her shoulders where she could not feel it.

"Down," she said to her troopers. "We follow."

And beneath her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, the stolen vial of sigil-light hummed against her heart like something answering a call it had waited two hundred years to make — and for the first time since she'd signed her childhood away, Inquisitor Serane Voss was afraid of what she might be hunting, and more afraid of what she was beginning to want.

They went down into the dark together, the gray coats and their hollow captain, following a light they could not name into a mountain full of voices — and somewhere behind them, far to the south in Vaelmoor, a king who was not a king lifted his head from his throne as if he'd heard his name spoken in a dead tongue, and began, very slowly, to smile.
