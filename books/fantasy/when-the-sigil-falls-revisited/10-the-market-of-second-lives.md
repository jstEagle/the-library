## 10. The Market of Second Lives

Brackwater smelled of fish and iron and other people's yesterdays.

Astrid had known that before the ferry touched the pier — she could smell a memory-market from half a mile off, the way some women could smell rain coming. Under the brine and the coal smoke there was always that faint sweetness, like overripe plums, like something going to rot in a beautiful jar. It was the smell of vials being opened in back rooms all over the town, of recollections poured out and sipped and traded, and it made her stomach clench with an old, familiar shame that she had long ago learned to mistake for hunger.

"Stay close," she said to Wren.

The child said nothing, which was her way now even more than before. Since the wayshrine on the pass road — since the vision that had poured through both of them like cold water through a cracked cup — Wren had gone quieter, watching everything with those grey eyes that seemed older than the small face holding them. She kept one hand fisted around the shard of black glass at her throat, and she walked so close behind Astrid that her breath touched the back of Astrid's neck with every step.

The market proper sprawled along the canal district, under awnings the color of drowned things. Brackwater was a free-town, which meant no crown, no wards, no law worth the name — just a harbor council bought six times over and a long tradition of looking the other way. Smugglers ran their routes out of its eastern quays. Memory-brokers ran theirs out of the streets behind the fish-weighing house, where the stalls sold not eels and oysters but first kisses, last words, the taste of a dead man's cooking, the exact blue of a sky someone had seen once as a child and never found again.

Astrid moved through it like a woman walking through her own reflection.

She knew these stalls. She knew this grammar. There: a broker with copper rings on every finger, selling "a nobleman's wedding day, unspoiled, the bride still loves him" to a gray-faced clerk who wanted to know what it felt like to be chosen. There: a woman with a ledger, buying grief by weight — the heavier the loss, the better the price, because heavy grief made rich ink for the vials that rich men drank when they wanted to feel deep without drowning. And everywhere, threaded through the crowd like needles through cloth, the cutters themselves, walking with that particular careful lightness of people carrying stolen years in glass against their ribs.

Astrid's own stock was hidden in the lining of her mother's old coat, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn flat against her left side. She had counted them again on the ferry: forty-one vials, forty-one lives she had lifted from paying customers and never sold, because selling them felt too much like selling people twice. She told herself they were inventory. She told herself a lot of things.

What she needed in Brackwater was simpler and stranger: someone who could read a dead language.

She stopped at the mouth of Sable Row, where the brokers' quarter began in earnest, and crouched to Wren's eye level. Around them the crowd flowed like dirty water around two stones.

"I'm going to ask some questions," she said. "You don't talk. Not a word, not in any tongue, do you understand? If anyone asks who you are, you're my niece and you're simple-born and you don't speak. Nod if you understand."

Wren nodded. Then, with the grave deliberation she brought to everything, she reached up and tugged Astrid's sleeve and pointed down the row — past the awnings, past the stall where a fat broker was haggling over a soldier's dying moments — toward a narrow shopfront wedged between a chandler's and a pawnbroker's. Its sign was blank. Its windows were painted black. But hanging above the door, turning slowly in the harbor wind, was a wind-chime made of tiny glass vials, empty ones, each stoppered with red wax.

Wren pointed at it the way other children pointed at sweet-sellers.

"How do you—" Astrid began, then stopped. Of course. The shard hummed; the child heard things in stone and glass and wood that no one else could hear. She'd led them true on the smugglers' roads; she'd pulled Astrid's sleeve toward safe ground twice already. *Trust the strange gift*, Ferro had told her at the tollhouse, the night before he'd waved them off into the dark with a lantern and a debt he still wouldn't explain. *It knows the roads better than you do.*

"All right," Astrid said. "That one."

* * *

The shop inside was cooler than the street, and darker, and it smelled the way Astrid's own attic workroom smelled — bone dust and beeswax and the ghost of plum-sweet rot. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling, crowded with vials sorted by no system she recognized: not by vintage or emotion or price, but by color, arranged in gradients, blues bleeding into greens bleeding into a whole shelf of golds that glowed faintly, softly, like bottled lamplight. A memory-broker who organized by luminance. Astrid had never seen it done. It was either madness or genius, and in her experience the two traded freely.

"You're standing in my light," said a voice from the back.

A woman came through a curtain of strung shells. She was perhaps sixty, broad-shouldered, with silver-streaked hair cropped short as a sailor's and hands that were unmistakably working hands — scarred knuckles, burn marks across the backs, the hands of someone who had spent decades doing fine, hot, precise work. She wore an apron with a dozen shallow pockets, each pocket holding a stylus. Astrid counted nine different tools and recognized seven of them.

Her heart did something complicated.

"I'm looking for a translator," Astrid said, keeping her voice flat and market-flat, the voice she used for bargaining. "Dead tongues. Old script. I'm told Brackwater has—"

"Told by whom?" The woman came closer, squinting. Her eyes were pale amber, almost yellow, and they went from Astrid's face to Wren's to the black-glass shard at Wren's throat, and something moved behind them like a fish under ice. "Everyone in this town is told by everyone about everyone. Sit down anyway. I don't do translations. I do appraisals. If your script is written on something valuable, we can talk."

"It's written on nothing," Astrid said. "It's spoken."

Something changed in the room. Astrid felt it rather than saw it — the way the air thickened, the way the shell curtain stopped swaying, as if the shop itself were listening harder.

"Sit down," the woman said again, differently this time.

There were two stools by the workbench. Astrid took one and pulled Wren onto the other beside her, keeping her hand on the child's shoulder. On the bench lay a half-finished piece of work: a vial mid-extraction, its wax seal scored but not broken, and beside it a stylus unlike any Astrid had ever handled — longer, curved like a heron's neck, its tip carved from something white that wasn't bone and wasn't ivory and wasn't coral but shared family traits with all three.

"That's a smith's tool," Astrid said before she could stop herself.

The woman looked at her for a long moment. The lamplight caught the amber of her eyes and turned them briefly golden.

"Now that," she said, "is an interesting thing for a Greyhollow cutter to know. Because I've watched you walk down my street, girl, and I know your walk, and I know the way you read my shelves — by color, by age, by how the light sits in the glass. You were trained in the Kavanagh extraction. The three-turn lift, the thumb-anchor, the cooling pause before the pour. Nobody teaches that anymore. Nobody *can*. So either you stole it from a dead woman's notes—" her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse, "—or you learned it at the source. And since the source died twenty-two years ago in a fire that officially never happened, I find myself very curious which it is."

The floor seemed to tilt slightly. Astrid kept her face still. She had lied to inquisitors; she could lie to a broker. "I apprenticed. Papers burned. Names change. You know how it is in the trade."

"Mm." The woman picked up the heron-necked stylus and turned it over in her scarred fingers, not looking at it, looking at Astrid. "My name is Ysolde. This is my shop. And you are standing in front of me wearing Maerin Kavanagh's coat, with Maerin Kavanagh's hands, making Maerin Kavanagh's pauses, and telling me papers burned." She set the stylus down with a soft click. "I knew your mother, girl. I carried her kit out of that fire. So let's not waste each other's time pretending."

* * *

Later — much later, though it couldn't have been more than a few minutes — Astrid would try to reconstruct exactly what had happened inside her in those seconds, and fail. It was like trying to describe the moment a floor gives way: there was the standing, and then there was the falling, and between them was only a terrible lightness.

"You're wrong," she said.

"Am I."

"My mother was a laundress in Greyhollow. She died of lung-rot when I was nine. There was no fire. There was no kit. There was no—"

"There was no one to teach you the three-turn lift?" Ysolde asked mildly. "Then where did it come from, child? Where does the thumb-anchor come from, the one you're doing right now on the little one's shoulder? That anchor isn't taught in any guild school in the five kingdoms. It's a sigil-smith's hold. It's how a smith steadies living material. Your fingers are doing it because your mother's fingers did it, probably while she held you, probably while she sang, and you absorbed it the way children absorb everything — completely, and without knowing."

Astrid looked down at her own hand on Wren's shoulder. Her thumb was pressed flat against the joint of the child's collarbone, angled inward, anchored. She had been holding Wren that way for weeks. Holding doors, crossing streets, pulling her back from cliff edges in the passes — always that grip, always that angle, and she had never once thought about it, the way you never think about breathing until someone tells you that you're drowning.

Wren looked up at her. In the lamplight the child's grey eyes had gone almost silver, and the shard at her throat was humming — very quietly, very steadily, the note it made sometimes in high places, near old stones, near things that remembered.

"The coat," Astrid said. Her voice sounded far away to her, like someone else speaking through a wall. "How do you know the coat?"

"Because I packed it." Ysolde rose, went to the shelf of golds, and stood with her back to them for a moment. When she turned around, she was holding a small wooden box, plain and dark with age. "When the tower at Cael's root burned — and it did burn, whatever the histories say, and I'll thank you to remember that I am old enough to have watched it — Maerin sent three things out ahead of the flames. Her daughter. Her journals. And her coat, because the coat had pockets that mattered, and the coat was meant to reach the daughter when the daughter was ready, and clearly whoever was supposed to deliver it—" a flicker of contempt crossed her weathered face, "—waited until she was grown and alone and had already learned every hard lesson the hard way. Which was, knowing Maerin, possibly exactly what Maerin intended. She was like that. Kindness with teeth in it."

"She was a laundress," Astrid repeated. It came out small. Stubborn. The last plank of a deck going under.

"She was the last Keeper of the Binding," Ysolde said, "and she was a laundress, and she was a sigil-smith of the old line, and none of those things cancel each other. That's the first thing you'll have to learn, if you learn anything: people are not one story. Not even dead ones." She set the box on the bench between them but did not open it. Her scarred hands rested on the lid. "Maerin Kavanagh hid her line so well that even the Purge's records call the sigil-smiths extinct. Extinct. As if twenty-two years ago isn't yesterday, as if blood doesn't keep. I've spent two decades telling myself the line ended in that fire, because it was safer to believe it. Safer for me, and safer, frankly, for you." Her amber eyes went to Wren. "And then you walk into my shop with a shard of the Heart-glass on a string and a child who shouldn't exist, humming like the mountain's own tuning fork, and safety goes out the window with the tide."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed over the canals, and the vial-chime above the door turned and clicked, empty glass knocking on empty glass.

"What was she like?" Astrid asked. She hadn't decided to say it. It simply came out, raw as a wound reopened, and she hated herself for it even as she leaned forward. "My mother. What was she—"

Ysolde's expression softened by perhaps a degree, which from her was like another woman weeping. "Fierce. Careful. Funnier than she had any right to be, given the weight she carried. She used to say that binding a kingdom was mostly plumbing — that glory was for kings and terror was for poets, but a smith's real work was making sure nobody ever noticed the water stayed in the pipes." She paused. "And she was lonely, in the end. Lonely the way only a keeper can be — surrounded by everything she protected and known by no one. When she talked about the future, she talked about you. What she'd give you, when you were ready. And what she hoped you'd never have to take up." Her hands tightened slightly on the box. "I think she hoped right up until the end that the fire would be the end of it. That the Purge would finally believe the line dead, and her daughter could live a small life in a big city, cutting memories for coin, and never once hear the mountain calling her home."

Astrid's eyes were burning. She realized she was gripping Wren's shoulder hard enough that the child had put her own small hand over hers — not pulling it away, just covering it, holding it, the way you steady something shaking.

"And now?" she managed.

"Now," Ysolde said, and the softness went out of her voice entirely, replaced by something old and hard and frightened, "now the king's inquisitors are asking questions in every port from here to the Salt Straights about a runaway asset and a Greyhollow cutter matching your description. Now the wards on the Thornfell passes are failing — I've seen the tremors in the trade-glass, girl; memories are going sour all along the coast, curdling in the vial, and that only happens when the great bindings slacken. And now the last heir of the sigil-smiths has sat down in my shop and asked me about translators." She opened the box.

Inside, on faded velvet, lay a single sheet of paper — old, brittle, covered edge to edge in a dense, flowing script that Astrid did not recognize and yet, horribly, almost understood, the way you almost understand a language you spoke in a dream. Beneath the paper, coiled like a sleeping snake, lay a length of silver wire stamped with tiny repeating marks. Sigils. Living sigils. They seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them.

"This is Maerin's hand," Ysolde said. "This is her last translation — the only one in existence — of the founding covenant of the Binding. And I will give it to you, and everything else I have, everything I kept safe for twenty-two years waiting for someone to come collect it. But not tonight, and not for free, and not until you tell me honestly, heir of Cael: do you want to know what you are? Or would you rather go back out into that market and buy yourself a second life, like everybody else in Brackwater?" She leaned forward, amber eyes burning. "Because I warn you — the truth costs more than any vial on my shelves. And once you've paid, there are no returns."

Under Astrid's hand, Wren's fingers tightened. And the shard at the child's throat began, very softly, to glow.
