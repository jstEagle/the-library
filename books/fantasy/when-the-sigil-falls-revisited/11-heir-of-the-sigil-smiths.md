## 11. Heir of the Sigil-Smiths

The back room of Ysolde's shop smelled of cedar oil and old paper, the way all the best memory-brokers' rooms did — a scent designed, Astrid had always suspected, to put clients in mind of coffins and inheritance at the same time. A single lamp burned behind frosted glass, turning everything the color of weak tea. Shelves rose into shadow on every wall, crowded with vials in ranked wooden trays, each one labeled in a cramped hand: *First Snowfall, Unlabeled*. *A Wedding, Second Attempt*. *The Sea, Before*.

Ysolde shut the door with her hip and turned the key, and the small sound of it closing was louder than it should have been.

"Sit," she said.

Astrid didn't sit. Wren did, immediately, folding herself onto a stool by the cold stove with the boneless ease of a child who had learned that stillness was safer than questions. The shard of black glass hung at her throat on its cord, drinking the lamplight and giving none back. Astrid kept one hand near the girl's shoulder anyway.

"You said you'd tell me why you recognized me," Astrid said. "You have until I decide this room is a trap."

"Everything is a trap," Ysolde said. She was older than Astrid had first guessed in the shopfront — sixty, maybe more, with the fine-drawn look of a woman who had spent decades trading away pieces of other people's lives and keeping careful count of what it cost her own. Her fingers were stained faintly blue at the tips, as if from ink, or from handling too many memories of rivers. "That's not an answer to your question, though. Sit down, girl. What I have to say goes easier seated."

"I've stood through worse."

"Yes," Ysolde said quietly. "I imagine you have." She crossed to a shelf, ran her finger along a row of trays without looking at them — the gesture of someone who knew every object in the dark — and drew out a flat box wrapped in oilcloth. She set it on the table between them but did not open it. "Your technique. When you lifted that memory from my client's man this morning, I watched your hands. The three-finger grip on the stylus. The way you braced the extraction against the subject's breath instead of their pulse. Do you know how rare that is?"

"It's just how I was taught."

"By whom?"

Astrid's jaw tightened. "I don't know."

"You don't *know*, or you don't *remember*?"

"There's a difference?" It came out sharper than she intended, and Wren's head came up sharply at the sound of it. Astrid softened nothing, because softening would have been a lie, and lies were currency she refused to spend in this room. "I was raised in the foundling ward at Greyhollow. Nobody taught me anything. I found the stylus in a dead woman's coat when I was nine years old, and my hands already knew what to do with it. Like they'd done it before. That's all."

Ysolde looked at her for a long moment. Then she unwrapped the box.

Inside, nested in rotted velvet, lay a stylus.

It was longer than Astrid's, older, its bone gone the color of old ivory, carved along its length with characters so fine they might have been scratches if the lamp hadn't caught them — and when the light did catch them, they *moved*, crawling slowly along the shaft like insects waking. Astrid knew them. Not their meaning. But she knew the way her eye wanted to follow them, the pull low in her sternum, the same pull she felt when Wren spoke in the dead tongue.

"That's—"

"A sigil-stylus," Ysolde said. "One of eleven known to survive the Purge. This one was recovered from a house in Greyhollow. An attic room above a chandler's shop, sealed for thirty years. The rent was paid in advance for twenty of them, by a woman who told the landlord she was going away and might not come back." Ysolde's voice went very level, very careful, the voice of a broker handling something more fragile than memory. "The rent was paid in coin that hadn't circulated since before the mountain passes closed. And in the room they found this, and journals, and a coat with false linings, and a child's shoe that no one could explain, because there was no child."

Astrid heard her own breathing. The room had gotten smaller. Or she had gotten larger inside it, some part of her swelling against walls that had held her whole life without her knowing they were walls.

"The coat," she said. "Describe the coat."

"Grey wool. Deep pockets. Mended at the left elbow with thread that doesn't match, because the mending was done in a hurry."

Astrid was wearing a grey wool coat. It hung on her shoulders now, over her shirt, and the mend at the left elbow was a slightly darker grey, and she had worn it every cold day of her adult life without once asking herself where it came from, because foundlings did not ask where their clothes came from. Foundlings took what the ward gave them and said thank you.

She had never taken it off in thirty years.

"Where did I get this coat?" she asked, and hated how young her voice sounded.

"I don't know," Ysolde said. "But I can tell you where *she* got hers. Because I saw her wear it." She sat down across the table, folding her hands like a woman settling in for a long confession, or a long invoice. "Thirty-one years ago, a woman came through Brackwater. Memory-broker, like me, but better — better than me, better than anyone I've seen since, except possibly you. She worked the southern trade routes. She paid her debts. She asked strange questions about the old wards and paid well for answers. Her name — the name she used — was Maerin Kavanagh."

The name landed in Astrid's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. It took a long time coming.

"Kavanagh is a common enough name," she said finally.

"In Greyhollow, perhaps. Not among sigil-smiths. There was only ever one line of them, and they were all killed in the Purge, or so the histories claim." Ysolde opened her hands. "Maerin Kavanagh was not killed. Maerin Kavanagh hid. She changed her face — I know, because I sold her the memory of her own daughter's face so she could carry it without weeping every time she looked in a mirror, and I watched her do it, and I have regretted the sale for thirty years, because a mother should weep when she looks at her child's face. That is what faces are *for*."

Wren made a small sound. Astrid realized her own hand had found the girl's shoulder without her noticing, gripping hard enough that the child should have flinched. Wren didn't flinch. Wren leaned into it, the way she leaned into everything Astrid gave her, as if affection were a food she'd been starving for.

"What happened to her?" Astrid asked. "To Maerin."

"She went north," Ysolde said. "Toward the passes, right before they sealed. Toward whatever she thought was worth dying for." A pause. "Or living for. I never learned which."

"And the daughter. The face she carried." Astrid's throat was dry. "What was the daughter's name?"

Ysolde looked at her the way brokers look at a vial whose contents they're about to pour out.

"Astrid," she said.

Afterward — after the lamp had burned lower and the tea Ysolde poured had gone untouched and cold — Astrid stood at the narrow window of the back room with her arms wrapped around herself, looking out at Brackwater's rain-slicked roofs, and felt absolutely nothing, which frightened her more than grief would have.

Grief she knew how to hold. Grief had shape and weight and edges. This was a hole in the exact center of her, a socket where something had always been missing and she'd never known to miss it, and now it had a *name*, and the name fit the socket perfectly, and still there was nothing there. No memory of a mother's voice. No memory of a kitchen, a lullaby, a pair of hands braiding hair. Thirty-one years of a life built directly on top of an absence, floorboards nailed over a cellar, and she had walked across those boards ten thousand times without ever hearing them hollow.

"You're angry," Wren said.

It was the first time the girl had spoken common words in the room unprompted, and Astrid almost laughed — of course the child who could read echoes in stone would read the echo in her.

"I'm not angry."

"You are. It's loud." Wren touched her own sternum, where the glass shard hung. "It comes off you like heat off a road."

"Fine." Astrid turned. "I'm angry. Does that satisfy you?"

Wren considered this with the grave seriousness she brought to everything. "No. But it's honest. Honest is better than satisfied."

From the table, Ysolde made a soft sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "She's right, you know. Anger's honest. So let me be honest back at you, Astrid Kavanagh." She tapped the open box, the old stylus lying in its rotten velvet like a sleeping thing. "This isn't a keepsake. This isn't a curiosity. The Purge wasn't bandits or plague or any of the stories they tell children. The crown hunted the smiths deliberately, systematically, because the smiths were the only people alive who could *unmake* the binding as easily as maintain it — and a king who holds the only key to his kingdom's walls sleeps better at night. Your family was exterminated for what they could do. And you—" her finger swung from the stylus to Astrid's right hand, to the three-fingered grip that had never needed teaching, "—you can do it. I watched you work this morning. You don't extract memories, girl. You never did. You cut along lines that were already drawn, lines only a smith's hand can see, and every memory in every head you've ever touched has been sitting on top of sigils you could read blind."

Astrid stared at her own hand as if it belonged to a stranger. It looked the same. It had always looked the same. Small, scarred at the knuckles, competent. A tradesman's hand. A thief's hand.

"No," she said.

Ysolde blinked. "No?"

"No." The word came out flat and final, a door shutting. "Whatever you think I am, whatever you think I inherited, I don't want it. I cut memories out of heads for coin. That's the trade. That's the whole trade. I am not a smith, I am not a keeper, I am not the last of anything except possibly the last fool standing in this room listening to you."

"Astrid—"

"Do you know what happens to people who matter?" She heard her own voice rise and couldn't stop it. "They die. They get hunted, or they get used, or they get eaten by kings wearing crowns full of hungry ghosts. My mother — if she was my mother — she mattered, and she vanished north and left her daughter in a foundling ward with a borrowed coat. The smiths mattered, and the Purge scrubbed them off the earth. The king matters, and there's something wearing him like a glove. Every single person in this story who carried a legacy ended up as a warning, and you want to hand me mine wrapped in oilcloth like it's a gift."

"It *is* a gift."

"It's a *chain*." Astrid snatched the old stylus out of the box — she didn't know why, only that her hand moved before her anger finished thinking — and held it up between them. The carved characters crawled under her fingers, warm, eager, horribly familiar, the way a stranger's face can be familiar in dreams. "You feel this? It wants something. It's been wanting something for thirty years, sitting in a box, waiting for a Kavanagh to pick it up. Well, I'm not picking it up. I'm holding it exactly long enough to throw it back in the hole it crawled out of."

She dropped it into the velvet. Her palm tingled for minutes afterward.

Ysolde studied her for a long, unhurried moment — the look of a woman who had spent a lifetime watching people lie to themselves about what they'd pay, and had gotten very good at recognizing the moment the haggling turned real.

"You misunderstand inheritance," she said at last. "You think it's a debt. It isn't. It's a door that only opens from the outside, and you were born with the handle. You can stand in the rain your whole life refusing to knock. The door doesn't care. But the storm doesn't care either, and there's a child in this room who speaks the language of the binding, wearing a fragment of it around her neck, and somewhere behind you the crown's inquisitors are following the trail of unmade soldiers you left on the road." She spread her hands. "Refuse the legacy all you like. The legacy hasn't refused *you*."

"I noticed," Astrid said bitterly.

Outside, the rain thickened against the window. Somewhere in the front of the shop, the bell over the door chimed — a customer, ordinary, harmless — and both Astrid and Wren flinched at the same instant, and Astrid hated that too, hated that fear had become the native language between them.

She crouched in front of Wren, bringing her eyes level with the girl's. Up close, the child's gaze had that depth again, that lattice-light swimming far beneath the brown, like lanterns sunk in a well.

"Wren. Listen to me. Whatever I am — smith, heir, last of a slaughtered line — none of it changes what I promised you. We go to the mountain because you need answers and I keep my word. That's all. I'm not becoming anyone. I'm not accepting anything. Understood?"

Wren looked at her for a long time. Then, gently, in that voice like a struck bell wrapped in cloth:

"You already accepted. In the tunnels. When the sigils lit up for you." She touched Astrid's hand, the scarred knuckles, the fingers that gripped a stylus like they'd been born around one. "The mountain knows your blood, Astrid. You can be angry at it. Mountains don't mind. But it's going to keep calling you home."

The words were simple and terrible and true, and Astrid had no answer for them, and Wren seemed to understand that, because the girl simply folded herself against Astrid's chest and stayed there, small and warm and humming faintly, like something tuned to a frequency Astrid had spent her whole life trying not to hear.

Behind them, Ysolde began, quietly, methodically, to rewrap the stylus in its oilcloth.

"One more thing," the old woman said, "and then I'll let you hate me in peace. If you're going north to Cael — and you are, don't argue, your face decided while I was still talking — you'll need more than smuggler roads. The deep ways under the mountain haven't opened in a generation. They'll open for a smith. Which means you'll need to know what a smith *is* before you reach the first gate, or you'll die in the dark with your inheritance still rattling in your pocket." She tied off the cloth with a jerk. "There's an archive. Under Cael's roots, where your mother's people kept their journals, their charts, their craft. I have half a map to it. The other half—"

She paused, and Astrid watched the broker's face do something brokers' faces should never do: hesitate.

"The other half costs," Ysolde said. "And the price is a memory. Yours."

Astrid straightened slowly. Outside, the rain kept falling on Brackwater, indifferent, ancient, the same rain that had fallen on an attic in Greyhollow thirty years ago while a woman packed a coat and a stylus and walked north and did not come back.

"Which memory?" Astrid asked.

Ysolde met her eyes, and there was no mercy in them, and no cruelty either — only the terrible fairness of the scales.

"The false mother," she said. "The one you replay every night. The one that isn't yours."
