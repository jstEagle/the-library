## 12. Wren Sings

The words lived in her like fish under ice.

Wren felt them moving all day, beneath the surface of her, dark shapes that would not rise. She had learned to keep her mouth shut around them. In the tunnels where the sigils glowed at her passing, in Ferro's tollhouse with its smell of tallow and old smoke, in the wayshrine where the vision had poured through her like molten light — through all of it she had kept them down. Words were dangerous. She had been made of words once, or something older than words, a language that was less speech than structure, less sound than law. The dead tongue came out of her easily when she dreamed; it was the common tongue that frightened her, because the common tongue was for people, and she was still learning what a person was allowed to do.

Astrid walked ahead on the river road with her coat collar up and her hood low, and every so often she looked back — not at Wren exactly, but *for* Wren, the way you check that a coin is still in your pocket. Wren had decided, without deciding it, that this was love. It was not the love from before, from the lattice under the mountains, where everything had been connected and nothing could be lost. This love had edges. It could be dropped. That made it worth more.

The road ran along the Greywash where it came down fast and brown from the high passes, swollen with snowmelt. Alders leaned over it. Somewhere behind them — three days behind, maybe four — Serane Voss was tracking them with her glassy eyes and her smile that never reached anything, and ahead, according to Astrid's maps, was the crossing at Marlow Ford, where the water spread shallow over stones and the far bank dissolved into free-town territory the crown pretended not to notice.

They were almost there when Wren heard the horses.

She heard them before Astrid did. That was new, and she catalogued it the way she catalogued everything now: the child's body had dull ears, but something else in her heard fine, some thread of her that still ran down into the earth and listened the way roots listen. Hooves. Four horses, no jingle of merchant tack, riders sitting heavy and forward. She grabbed Astrid's sleeve.

Astrid read her hand instantly — they had built that language between them, hands and eyes, two people who mostly could not or would not speak. Her face went through its quick arithmetic: the bend in the road, the alder stand, the water. Then she took Wren's wrist and pulled her off the road into the trees, down a deer track slick with last year's leaves, toward the sound of the river.

"They're between us and the ford," Astrid breathed, crouching behind a deadfall. "If we cut along the bank—"

Wren never learned what the plan was, because the soldiers were already there.

They came out of the trees on both sides — she understood that much later, that Astrid's smuggler roads had been sold or guessed or simply outflanked — six men in crown grey with the black sigil of the Vessel's court stitched where a crest should be. Their faces were wrong in a way Wren recognized the way you recognize a smell from childhood: too smooth, too calm, the faces of men whose fear had been eaten out of them. Memory-eaten. Not as far gone as Serane, but fed on. The nearest one smiled at Astrid the way you smile at livestock.

"By order of the regent's court," he said, "surrender the asset."

*Asset.* Wren flinched. Astrid moved — one step, casual as a woman adjusting her stance, putting herself fully between Wren and the line of crossbows. Wren watched her do the other thing too, the small thing, the thing that undid her: her hand came back and found Wren's shoulder without looking, and squeezed once.

*I have you,* the squeeze said.

"The child is deaf and dumb and mine," Astrid said, in the voice Wren had heard her use on the broker in Brackwater, smooth as oil over deep water. "Bought off a plague cart in Thornmark. If your regent wants mute orphans, you can have her for two silver."

The soldier's smile didn't move. But his eyes flicked to Wren — and Wren saw the moment the lie died. He saw what they always saw eventually, what even Astrid had seen that first night in the storm: that whatever stood in the mud looking back at him was not an ordinary child. Something in him leaned forward like a man smelling bread.

"There's a bounty," he said softly. "Alive. The lady inquisitor was specific." His gauntlet rose, and Wren saw the thin bone stylus strapped to his wrist, crown-issue, the kind Astrid used for work but cruder, meant for taking rather than keeping. "And the lady pays bonuses for anything the asset has... touched."

Astrid went very still. Wren knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a drawn blade.

"You touch her," Astrid said quietly, "and I will show you things about yourself you will spend the rest of your life drinking to forget."

One of the soldiers laughed. The leader didn't. He gestured, bored, and two men started forward, and Wren felt the ice above her words begin to crack.

Here is what she remembered, in the half-second she had: she remembered being vast. She remembered being the part of the great lattice that held the word for *hold*, the concept itself, the binding-idea that the smiths had hammered into the mountain's bones until the mountain agreed to keep its passes shut and its wards lit and its grief contained. She remembered that a word in the true tongue was not a sound. It was a small machine made of meaning, and when it was spoken by someone who carried it, it did what it was.

She had never spoken one in this small mouth. She was afraid of what it would cost. She was more afraid of losing Astrid, who had opened a door in a storm for a mud-caked stranger, who gave her the larger share of the eggs, who called her *mine* in front of ghosts.

The soldiers were four paces away. Astrid shifted her weight, calculating odds Wren could see were terrible. Six crossbows. One knife.

Wren stepped around Astrid's arm.

"*No.*"

It came out cracked, rusty, a hinge unopened for years — her first common word spoken aloud since the night on Astrid's stairs, and the soldiers actually paused, startled by the sound of her. And inside her, hearing her own voice open the door, everything under the ice surged upward at once, the dark shapes rising, and she did not choose the next part any more than she had chosen to be born.

She spoke the true tongue.

It was three syllables. They sounded like nothing — like wind through a keyhole, like a stone dropping into deep water — but the world bent around them the way light bends in a vial. Wren felt the word leave her and *do what it was*. The word meant, roughly, in the language of smiths and bindings: *return what you were given.*

The effect was not violence. Violence would have been simpler.

The six soldiers stopped. Their faces went slack, then puzzled, then nakedly afraid — and Wren understood with sick clarity that these were the first fears they had felt in months, maybe years, arriving all at once like water through a broken dam. The leader looked at his own gauntleted hands as if he'd never seen them. One man sat down in the wet leaves and began to cry without any expression at all, tears running down a perfectly blank face. Another said, in a small voice, "Where — where are my daughters? Do I have daughters?" and no one answered him, because Wren had taken only what she had commanded — their recent memories, the weeks of service, the hunt, the orders, the eaten calm — and left them standing in the woods with years missing and nothing but the raw present to hold.

The word finished its work and let go. The silence after rang like a struck bell.

Astrid stared at her.

Not at the soldiers — at Wren. And Wren saw the look and braced for it, the fear, the recalculation, *what have I been carrying*, but that wasn't what was in Astrid's face at all. What was in Astrid's face was awe, and underneath the awe something fiercer and warmer, and she said, hoarse:

"Wren. You *spoke.*"

Wren's knees buckled. Astrid caught her before the ground did.

"I'm sorry," Wren whispered, and her voice broke apart on the second word, because speaking hurt, because the common tongue was a garment that didn't fit yet, because she had just reached into six men's heads and torn pages out and she could feel those pages now, fluttering loose somewhere inside her like moths. "I'm sorry, I couldn't — they were going to take you away from me, and I—"

"Hush." Astrid's arms were around her, tight, muddy, smelling of rain and leather and the smoke of Ferro's tollhouse. "Hush. You don't ever apologize for that. You hear me?" She pulled back and held Wren's face in both hands, and her eyes were bright and hard. "Six armed men, and you put them on the ground with a *word*. Do you know what you are?"

Wren shook her head. It was the honest answer. She was a fragment. She was a shard of black glass given legs. She was a girl with a name Astrid had given her because the sigil's designation had burned away, and none of those answers fit in a child's mouth by a cold river.

"You're *mine*," Astrid said, "and you just saved us both. Now walk."

They walked. Along the bank, through the alders, while behind them the memory-stripped soldiers wandered in circles calling questions at the trees. At the top of the shingle where the ford began, Wren stopped and looked back once. The crying man had found his feet. He was standing very still, staring downstream, and even at this distance she could see him mouthing something over and over, trying to hold onto the shape of a thought that kept slipping. She had done that. She had reached into strangers and hollowed them, and the crown had done worse, and there was a difference — she clung to the difference, the willing pages versus the stolen ones — but the difference felt thinner today.

"Astrid," she said. Testing the name. Testing having a voice at all.

Astrid turned, one foot already in the ford, water breaking white around her boot. "What is it, little wren?"

"If I can do that to six men—" Her throat closed. She forced it. "What happens when I get angry? What happens if I ever get angry at *you*?"

Astrid came back through the water and crouched to her level, which she did rarely and therefore never carelessly. Up close her eyes were grey-green and shot through with exhaustion, and absolutely steady.

"Then I'll tell you to stop," she said. "And you'll stop. Because you asked me to open a door for you, that first night, whether you knew it or not — and doors are a promise that goes both ways." She tapped Wren's chest, once, over the heart. "That's what power is, out here. Not the word. The door."

The ford was wide and loud and bitterly cold, and halfway across the current tried to take Wren's feet out from under her, and Astrid hauled her up by the collar without breaking stride, cursing cheerfully, and for a few minutes there was only the water and the weight of each other and the far bank coming closer, green and lawless and safe.

It was Astrid who saw it first. Wren knew by the sudden change in her grip, the fingers going rigid on her shoulder.

On the far bank, at the point where the deer track climbed toward the free-town road, a figure stood waiting. Slim. Cloaked in travel-grey. Even across the rushing water, even at fifty paces, Wren knew that posture, that terrible patience — and she saw the moment the figure raised one hand, not in threat but almost in greeting, and the light caught a face Wren had hoped never to see again.

Serane Voss had not followed them to the ford.

Serane had *arrived* first.

* * *

"How?" Astrid's voice was flat and very quiet, the voice Wren had learned meant danger, though never — Wren was almost sure — to her.

They stood at the water's edge on the near bank again, retreat swallowed behind them by the trees and the six wandering soldiers, the crossing blocked ahead by a single unmoving figure. The river roared between them and the inquisitor like a wall of white noise.

"She moves like smoke," Astrid muttered. "Or someone told her the ford. Ferro wouldn't—" She stopped. Wren watched her consider it and refuse it. "No. Not Ferro. Someone else, then. Someone on the road."

Serane cupped her hands around her mouth and called across the water, and her voice carried strangely well, clear over the current: "You've grown a tongue, little shard! The lady in the archive will want to know that!"

"Don't answer her," Astrid murmured. "Don't give her anything. She reads reactions like I read ledgers."

But Wren was watching Serane's face, and Wren could see things Astrid couldn't — not thoughts, exactly, but textures, the way she could feel the echo of memory in old stone. Serane's mind was a house with most of the furniture gone. Vast rooms, empty, swept clean and sold off room by room, and in the deepest room something small and locked away that even Serane probably couldn't reach anymore. A child's room. Wren felt it the way you feel heat from a stove through a wall.

"She's empty," Wren said.

Astrid glanced down sharply. "What?"

"Inside. She's — most of her isn't there anymore." Wren struggled with the words; her new voice was still stiff, still learning its hinges. "There's one thing left she keeps locked. Small. It hurts her. She stands near it sometimes, in the night, the way you used to stand outside your mother-memory before you played it."

Astrid went very still. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a woman recalculating everything.

"You can see that? From here? Across water?"

"I can see it everywhere," Wren said. "I think..." She looked down at her own hands, small and chapped and utterly ordinary-looking, hands that had unmade six men an hour ago. "I think I'm getting stronger. The closer we get to the mountain. To the sigil. It's — recognizing me. Or I'm recognizing it."

"Then listen to me." Astrid crouched, gripping her shoulders, and the river spray misted them both. "Whatever she offers, whatever she says — she is not the sad empty thing inside her. She is what the crown made out of that emptiness, and it has hunted children before us and it will hunt them after. We run, we hide, we fight if we must. We do *not* save her today. Do you understand me?"

Wren thought about the locked room. About the small hurting thing in it. About how Astrid had opened her own door in a storm, and what kind of person Wren wanted to be when she grew into her voice.

"Wren," Astrid said. "*Do you understand me?*"

"Yes," Wren lied — her second lie in the common tongue, and it tasted strange, human, hers.

Across the water, Serane lowered her hand to her sword hilt and began, unhurried, to test the depth of the ford with one booted foot.
