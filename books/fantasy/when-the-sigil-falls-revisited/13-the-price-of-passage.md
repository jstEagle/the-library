## 13. The Price of Passage

Ferro had kept the tollhouse for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: nothing on the smugglers' spine came free. Not passage. Not silence. Not kindness. Every mercy had a ledger, and every ledger had a name written at the bottom, and sooner or later the ink always dried into blood.

He was thinking about that now, standing at his window with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hand, watching two figures pick their way up the switchback below — one tall and moving like she expected the mountain itself to sell her out, one small and walking like the mountain had already told her all its secrets.

The girl was the problem. Ferro had seen a lot of strange things come through the passes — salt runners with frostbitten hands, priests of banned gods carrying relics sewn into their bellies, a man once who claimed to be his own twin and could prove it either way. But he had never seen stone *answer* anyone before.

The night they'd arrived, soaked through and shivering, the child had put her palm flat against the tollhouse's foundation stone — the old one, the one Ferro's grandfather had pried out of a ruined wayshrine because it held the heat — and the stone had glowed. Softly, briefly, like an ember remembering fire. And the child had made a sound, small and startled, like a question asked in a language with no more speakers.

Astrid Kavanagh had looked at Ferro across the room and said, "You saw nothing."

"I see nothing most nights," Ferro had said, which was true, and also the first lie he'd told her.

Because he *had* seen something else too. He'd seen her face in the lamplight and felt thirty years fall away from it, down to a night when a woman with those same cheekbones and a different name had sat at this same table and drunk his tea and laughed at his jokes and paid him the only way she could: with a debt. "One day," she'd said, pressing a black iron ring into his palm, "someone will knock on your door wearing my eyes. Open it fast and don't ask what it costs."

He'd thought, for three decades, that she'd meant a lover. Or a soldier. Or maybe just herself again, older, running from something new.

She'd meant this. A woman with her jaw and a child who made stones sing.

*Don't ask what it costs.* Well. He was asking now. He stood at the window with his cold tea and he asked it properly, the way you ask a hard question, alone and out loud.

"Ferro?" Astrid's voice from behind him. She slept little — he'd noticed that, the way she dozed sitting up with a bone stylus still tucked in her sleeve even now, as if the tool might be needed to defend herself. "Road's quiet?"

"Too quiet," he said, which was true, and worse than noise. The crown's riders had been through twice since dawn, moving fast toward Brackwater, and the second party had carried a banner he hadn't seen in years: the eye-and-key of the Inquisitor's Office. Someone important was hunting them. Someone patient.

"They know we're on the spine," Astrid said. It wasn't a question. She came to stand beside him at the window, close enough that he could see the exhaustion worn into her like a groove. "Serane Voss doesn't lose trails. I've sold enough memories to know how her kind are built. They don't forget, and they don't stop."

"Then you shouldn't be here," Ferro said. "You should be over the Saddle Gap by now, three ridges gone."

"The pass is watched." She said it flatly, without apology. She'd been studying his maps for two days, and he'd let her, because the maps were real but incomplete; there were things on the spine that no map showed, debts and dangers that lived only in the memory of men like him. "And the low roads are worse. We need another day. Maybe two. Wren needs sleep that isn't against stone."

*Wren.* Even the name was new — he'd heard Astrid give it to her the first night, quietly, like planting a seed in frozen ground, and the child had taken hold of it the way ivy takes hold of a wall. Ferro looked out at the switchback where the girl was crouched now, palm against a boulder, lips moving. Reading it, Astrid said. Reading the echoes in the rock. As if the whole mountain were a book and she the only literate soul alive.

"She's eating," Ferro said. "That helps sleep."

"She eats like it might be taken away." Astrid's voice went quiet in a way that told him more than any confession would have. "It might be, at that."

They stood together in silence. Outside, Wren rose from the boulder, wiped her hands on her trousers, and looked up at the window — straight at Ferro, unblinking, with an expression he couldn't read and didn't want to. It wasn't accusation. It was worse. It was *recognition*, the look of someone who has already seen the ending of a story and is being polite about it.

Ferro drained his cold tea and set the cup down harder than he meant to.

"Kettle's on," he said, and went to make breakfast, because that was what you did. That was the whole of the smuggler's creed his mother had beaten into him before the crown took her for tax-evasion and the mines took what was left: *you feed people while you can, because someday you can't.*

He just hadn't expected *someday* to arrive wearing an inquisitor's grey cloak.

* * *

It came at dusk on the third day, the way bad news does — slowly, then all at once.

Wren felt it first. She'd been sitting by the hearth drawing shapes in spilled flour — sigils, though none of them held, fading like breath on glass — when she went rigid, head cocked, listening to something under the floor, under the mountain, under everything. Then she looked at Ferro and pointed east, toward the Saddle Gap road, and made a sound like a struck bell.

"She's coming," Astrid translated, already on her feet, already gathering the satchel she kept packed. "How long?"

Wren held up fingers. Three, then folded one down. Two hours, maybe less. The girl couldn't say it in words but she'd never once been wrong about distance or danger, not on this road, and Ferro trusted her the way sailors trust weather in their knees.

"Then we go north," Ferro said. "The Drover's Cut. It's narrow, it's flooded half the year, but it's flooded *now*, which means no horses, which means Voss comes on foot or not at all—"

"She won't come on foot." Astrid was pulling on her coat — her mother's coat, he realized suddenly, the cut of it old-fashioned, the shoulders let out and let in by different hands across decades. "If she's found the Cut, she'll have found every exit off it. You don't understand what she is, Ferro. I stole a look at her once, years ago, through a client who'd been questioned. There's almost nothing left inside her. She's a wall with a woman painted on it. You can't outrun a wall. You can only hope she doesn't notice the door."

"There is another way," Ferro said.

He said it quietly, and the quiet did what loudness couldn't — it stopped Astrid mid-motion, one arm in her sleeve, and brought her around to look at him fully.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard it."

"I've known you eleven days and I already know how this ends. No."

"It's my house," Ferro said, "and my road, and my debt. Your mother left it with me, not with you, and I get to choose how it's paid." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Voss hunts memories. That's what they said — the eye-and-key, the memory-eaters. So we give her one to hunt. Something big, something bright, something that smells like two fugitives and points the wrong way. A false trail made of the real thing."

"And where exactly does a toll-keeper on the ass-end of the spine get a memory worth an inquisitor's attention?"

Ferro smiled, and it hurt his face, because he'd stopped smiling honestly years ago and the muscles remembered badly.

"Same place everyone gets the good ones," he said. "Off a man who can't protect them anymore."

* * *

He made them sit down for it, because ceremony mattered. His mother had taught him that too — the last thing she taught him, really, the night before the collectors came: *when you pay a debt, pay it standing straight, and make them watch.*

He laid out the trade on the table like goods at market. First the kettle, refilled, steam rising between them. Then the vials — six of them, dark glass, bought cheap years ago from a Greyhollow peddler who'd sworn they were for perfume. Then, last, the bone stylus. Not his own. Astrid's, offered handle-first across the table, her knuckles white around it.

"I'll do it," she said for the fourth time.

"You don't know how to take a *willing* memory," Ferro said. "I can see it in your face. All your life you've pulled them out of people fighting to keep them, and that's a different craft entirely. A willing memory comes clean. A fought-for memory comes out in ribbons and takes meat with it." He turned his wrist on the table, palm up, an old smuggler's gesture meaning *look, plain dealing*. "Besides. It has to be me doing the choosing. You'd flinch. You're kind. Kindness ruins extraction."

"That's the stupidest thing anyone's ever said to me, and I buy memories from noblemen."

"Probably," Ferro agreed. "But it's still true."

Wren was watching from the corner, flour still dusting her fingertips, and her expression was the one he dreaded — that recognition again, that terrible patience. She knew. Of course she knew. Children like her, whatever she was, probably saw payment coming the way they saw storms.

So he talked while Astrid heated the stylus, because talking was how he kept standing straight.

"My mother's name was Brenna," he said. "Everyone called her Bear, including the collectors, right up until they dragged her off. I keep her — I kept her — the way you keep anything precious. Sunday mornings, the smell of coal smoke and fried bread. Her hands, cracked like riverbed clay. The song she sang when the ledgers wouldn't balance, four notes, over and over, like a heart deciding to keep beating." He swallowed. "There's a man too. Doesn't matter who. Thirty years back. A night on the roof of the customs house in Brackwater with the whole harbor lit up below us like a spill of coins. Best night of my life and I never once went looking for it since, because I was afraid of wearing it out. That one goes too. Both of them. The whole lot."

"You don't have to give the good ones," Astrid said. Her voice was rough. "Give me dross. Bad winters. Debt collectors. I can build a trail from dross—"

"Dross won't stop Serane Voss." He shook his head. "You said it yourself — there's nearly nothing left inside her. A woman like that is starving, girl. You feed a starving wolf dross, she keeps hunting. You feed her something *rich* — a mother's voice, a lover's laughter, thirty years of a small warm life — she stops. She feeds. And while she feeds, you're over the ridge and gone." He met her eyes. "Belonging means somebody pays for you. Your mother paid for me with a ring and a promise. I'm paying forward. That's not sacrifice, it's arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic," Astrid said, "it's *theft*. I steal for coin. This is—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "This is giving away the only copies of a life. There's no getting them back, Ferro. Once a memory leaves willing, it's *gone*. Not dimmed. Gone. You'll know your mother existed and you'll feel nothing. You'll know you loved someone and you won't know his face. Is that arithmetic?"

"Yes," Ferro said gently. "It's the only sum I ever learned."

The stylus was hot. He could smell it, that faint mineral scent like lightning about to happen. Wren crossed the room without a sound and put her hand on his shoulder, small and warm and steady, and when he glanced up at her she was crying — silently, without drama, tears running down a face that otherwise stayed perfectly composed, as if she'd been built knowing this moment was coming and had practiced the grief in advance.

"Don't you cry for me," he told her gruffly. "You're the inheritance. Whole point of the exercise."

She squeezed his shoulder once, hard, and stepped back.

"All right," Astrid said. "Where do you want it taken from?"

"Heart-side," Ferro said, tapping his chest below the collarbone, where the old sigil-smiths supposedly kept the soul's door. "Might as well be traditional about it."

* * *

It didn't hurt, which was the strangest part. Everyone said it hurt, the taking, and maybe it did when you fought it. But Ferro sat very still and thought *standing straight, standing straight,* and the stylus moved through him like a key through a well-oiled lock, and the memories came out in a slow silver thread that wound onto the vials' dark glass like light given weight.

He watched them go. That was the discipline of it — you had to watch, his mother would have said, or it didn't count as paying. He watched Sunday mornings lift out of him like smoke. He watched his mother's hands go, then her voice, then the four-note song, leaving a shaped silence behind where the song used to live. He watched the customs house roof dissolve — the harbor lights, the laugh, the warmth of another body against his in the wind — and felt the place where it had been close over smooth and blank as scar tissue.

When it was done, Astrid sat back, gray-faced, holding six full vials like they were made of eggshell.

"There," Ferro said. His voice sounded fine. That was good. That was standing straight. "Now the clever part. You plant them. You know how — scatter-trail, isn't it? Drop fragments along the south fork so Voss reads a story: two fugitives, panicked, splitting up, doubling back toward Greyhollow. Feed her the mother. Feed her the lover. Make it rich enough to gorge her."

"I know how," Astrid whispered.

"And when she finds them — and she will, she's thorough — she'll drink, because starving things always drink. And while she drinks, you'll be through the Cut and gone." He pushed himself up from the table. His knees ached. Everything ached, in a vague way, like weather coming. "Kiss the child for me when she's not looking. She hates being kissed."

Outside, the sky had gone the color of a bruise, and somewhere far to the east, faint as a thought, he could almost hear hoofbeats that weren't there yet.

* * *

They left in the dark, as planned. Astrid argued until the last minute — he'd give her that, she argued like the debt was hers to refuse — but in the end she pressed the iron ring into his hand, the one her mother had left, closing his fingers around it the same way her mother must have done thirty years ago.

"When this is over," she said, "I'm coming back. And I'm finding whoever holds your memories in a crown vault somewhere, and I'm buying every vial back at any price. That's not a promise, it's arithmetic."

"Bargain," Ferro said, and meant it, and knew that promises made on the spine were like footprints in scree — sincere, and gone by morning. But he kept the ring, and he stood in his doorway watching them go up the north path, the tall one steadying the small one over the rocks, and he felt the holes in himself ache pleasantly, like teeth lost young.

Then he went inside and waited, because that was the last part of the job.

Serane Voss came an hour before dawn. He heard the horse on the switchback — one horse, no escort, because walls travel light — and he opened the door before she knocked, standing straight, kettle steaming behind him.

"Inquisitor," he said. "You look hungry. Sit. Have tea."

Her eyes were glassy as river ice, and they swept the room, the table, the vial-stain of silver still ghosting the wood, and something in her flickered — hunger recognizing a meal. On the table, where Astrid had "carelessly" left them, lay the six vials, arranged like an offering.

"What am I?" Serane asked, and her voice was soft and dead as ash.

"A toll-keeper," Ferro said. "Thirty-one years on this road. Those aren't mine — fugitives passed through, dropped them running. Woman and a child. Went south toward Greyhollow, I'd guess, judging by the mess they left." He shrugged. "Or take mine instead. Mine are fresher."

Something moved behind her eyes — calculation, appetite, the ghost of a woman calculating what a thing cost before the hunger ate the calculator. Her gaze drifted to the vials, and Ferro watched her reach for them, and he thought of his mother's four-note song, already gone, already smooth blank silence, and he found — to his genuine surprise — that he was smiling.

Standing straight. Paid in full.

Serane lifted the first vial to the light, and her tongue touched her lips, and far to the north two figures climbed through the dark toward a flooded pass, and the mountain — Ferro would swear it, all his life after, he would swear it — the mountain breathed out around them, soft and long, like a door closing gently behind its own.

The inquisitor drank.
