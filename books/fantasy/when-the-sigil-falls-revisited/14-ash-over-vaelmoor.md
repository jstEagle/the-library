## 14. Ash Over Vaelmoor

The king of Thornfell woke in a body that did not smell like him.

That was the first sign, always. His own skin should have carried the cold iron of the palace baths, the tallow-and-parchment musk of the council chamber, the faint ghost of the anise lozenges his physician pressed on him for his cough. Instead there was smoke in the nostrils, woodsmoke and something sweeter beneath it — burning thatch, burning grain, and under all of it, faint as a hymn heard through a wall, the smell of memory coming loose from flesh.

*Ash over Vaelmoor,* he thought, before he could think anything else. The words arrived whole, like a stone dropped into a well. He knew them the way he knew his own heartbeat. Somewhere below him, past the terraced gardens and the garrison walls, past the shrine district with its hundred small bells, a village was burning.

His hand was raised.

He watched it — his hand, or the thing that wore it — holding a quill above a document he did not remember beginning. Wax light guttered across the map table in the war room. The map of the eastern wards was spread out beneath his elbow, and someone had moved a black glass marker to rest upon a village called Emberfen. He could not recall its name arriving in any dispatch. He could not recall the dispatch at all. The marker sat on Emberfen the way a thumb rests on a fly.

*Move,* Aldric told the hand.

The hand wrote on.

It wrote in his script — that was the obscenity of it. Forty-one years of signing charters and death warrants had carved his letters deep into the muscles of these fingers, and the Vessel used them the way a rider uses a horse's mouth, without thought, without cruelty even, just use. He strained against himself the way a man strains in a dream, full of intention, empty of motion, and the quill scratched out another line in a decree that would send soldiers to a village that was already, by the smell of it, sending up its smoke.

*How long this time?* He cast about for anchors. The candle had burned down perhaps two finger-widths since — since what? Since he had last been *him*? There were gaps in the night like missing teeth. He remembered sitting for his portrait in the autumn gallery — no. That had been years ago. He remembered rain on the observatory roof. He remembered his daughter's voice saying something urgent, something with the word *father* in it, and then nothing, a smooth blank stretch like ice over a pond, and now the smell of ash.

The hand set down the quill. Rose. Crossed to the window.

Aldric went with it, because there was nowhere else to go. He was carried inside his own body like a passenger in a closed litter, feeling each footfall land in knees that ached with his age, watching through eyes that swept the burning east with a hunger he could feel but not share — no, that was wrong. He *did* share it. That was the worst discovery of these three years: the hunger was not foreign. It lived in the same chest as his heart, breathed with the same lungs, and when the Vessel looked at the fires of Emberfen, some traitor chord in him vibrated in sympathy, some old starved part of him that had been starving so long it had forgotten what for.

*Not me,* he told the chord. *Not me.*

The window overlooked the lower city. Beyond it, the eastern hills wore a dull orange corona, and the wind brought the bells — not the shrine bells but the alarm bell at Emberfen's meetinghouse, tolling and tolling until it stopped mid-swing, which meant either the rope had burned or the hands on it had.

He counted the ward-villages still dark. Seven. Once there had been nineteen along the passes, each one anchored to a waystone, each waystone threaded into the great sigil that sealed the Thornfell gaps against what lived beyond them. His grandfather had called them the Nineteen Candles. Aldric had visited eleven of them in his first decade of rule, had drunk cider with their reeves and been politely bored by their harvest prayers. Now seven remained, and the thing wearing his crown was eating its way toward them one village at a time, and the court saw only a king grown grave and vigilant, raising levies, doubling watches, doing everything a wise king should do in a time of troubles.

Every levy it raised thinned the villages further. Every watch it doubled emptied the wayshrines of their tenders. It was dismantling the kingdom with the kingdom's own hands, and it signed the orders in Aldric's own name.

The body turned from the window. In the polished silver of a wall sconce, his reflection passed — a tall man, grey at the temples, upright despite his years, face composed into that expression the portraitists called *gravitas* and Aldric had once privately called *the mask I make before breakfast.* But the eyes. The eyes in the silver were wrong the way water is wrong when something vast moves beneath it. They were patient. They were pleased.

"Emberfen," the mouth said aloud, tasting the word. Its voice was his voice, pitched low, thoughtful. "Old root-systems. Three generations of shared grief in that burying ground. Shared joy is thinner fare." A pause, and then, almost tenderly: "But they sang at the spring festival. All of them together. That will be rich."

Aldric threw himself against the inside of his own skull with everything he was. *Stop. Stop, you will not — those are my people—*

The sensation was like striking a tapestry from within. The blow landed everywhere and moved nothing.

But something shifted. A seam loosened. Perhaps the feeding had glutted it, perhaps the fires had made it lazy and slow, but the pressure receded a fraction — a fraction, no more, the way deep water eases off a diver who rises one span — and suddenly Aldric had his eyes. His eyes stung with smoke-grief that was not entirely the Vessel's appetite. He had his hands. They were shaking.

*Ninety seconds,* he estimated. Maybe less. He had learned to measure his surfacings by the candle-flame's behavior; when the flame leaned toward him instead of the door-draft, he was alone in his own head. The candle leaned now.

He did not waste the first second on weeping, though it stood close behind everything he did.

He crossed to the writing desk. Ink — the pot was full; the Vessel never tired of ink. Paper. His hand shook so badly the nib chattered against the desk edge, and he pressed it flat until the trembling became something he could write through.

*If found by loyal hands,* he wrote, and stopped, because the phrase was useless. Loyal to whom? To the crown? The crown was the enemy. To the man? The man was the crown. Every servant who kissed his ring kissed the thing behind his eyes. He struck it through.

*To whoever reads this,* he began again. *I am King Aldric Vane of Thornfell, and I am a prisoner inside my own body. The thing you see wearing me has eaten—*

How many? Did anyone know the true count? The courtiers whose warmth had gone strange and flat. The archivist who had died of what the physicians called wasting, though Aldric had seen the man's memories going down the Vessel's throat like wine. His daughter's dog, which had loved him and which he had not been permitted to love back. Whole villages, poured out. He could not write the number because he did not know it, and not knowing was itself a wound.

*—has consumed the memories of the living to weaken the wards. The Nineteen Candles are going out. When the last waystone fails, the passes open, and what the sigil-smiths bound beneath the mountains walks free. I do not know if the smiths survive. I do not know if anything survives. I know only this:*

His hand cramped. The candle flame wavered, began to right itself toward the door. *Faster.*

*I know only this: the binding can be remade. The Vessel fears it — I have felt its fear, tasted it in the back of my throat when the waystones flare. It is not all-powerful. It is hungry, and hunger can be denied, and somewhere in this kingdom there must be hands that remember how the smiths worked. Find them. My seal is below; it will open doors, though I cannot say what it will open them onto now. Trust no decree bearing my signature dated after the Feast of Saint Verren. Trust the mountain more than the throne.*

*And if my daughter lives — Maren, if it is you reading — I did not forget you. Whatever it made you witness, whatever orders came to you in my hand and my voice, I did not do them. I am in here. I am in here, and I am so sorry, and if there is a mercy left in this world, spend it on the kingdom before you spend it on me. Do not try to free me. Free us all instead.*

The flame bent fully toward the door. Pressure gathered at the back of his skull like a hand closing on a fistful of hair.

*It knows I write this. It lets me sometimes — why does it let me—*

He understood then, in the last half-second, with the terrible clarity the dying are granted, and the understanding was worse than the hunger: it let him because hope was nourishing too. A plea folded into a coat lining, discovered years later by grieving hands — that was a meal seasoned with despair. It wanted witnesses to the trap. It wanted the letter found and read and wept over. Everything fed it.

*Then let it choke,* Aldric thought, and folded the paper fast, and — where? Not the desk, the Vessel would tidy the desk. Not his coat; his coat was brushed nightly by servants. He thought of the lining, the old campaign coat he kept out of sentiment in the cedar press, the one his tailor had rebuilt twice, the one with a seam—

No. Not his. The thought slid sideways, slipped, and landed elsewhere: a woman's coat, a dead woman's coat, a smuggler's longcoat with mended elbows that had hung in his guardroom twenty years ago, belonging to a courier he had trusted and never learned the name of, who had carried messages down the mountain for the loyalists of his father's reign. She had left the coat behind when she vanished. It had been given away, lost, sold into the city's secondhand river — Greyhollow, most likely. Greyhollow, where lost things washed.

There was no time to reason about why the thought had surfaced, whether it was truly his or some seed the Vessel had planted, whether a dead courier's coat could possibly matter. The pressure closed. He folded the letter small, and with the last of his ninety seconds he hid it in the only place left to him:

Inside the memory itself.

He took the image of the coat — the mended elbows, the burn-scar on the left cuff, the hidden pocket stitched into the lining that the courier had shown him once, laughing, saying *a king should know where things hide* — and he drove it down deep, beneath the reach of the thing that rifled him, into the silt where his oldest self lay buried, and he anchored the letter there, in the one vault the Vessel could not open because opening it would mean admitting Aldric still existed.

Then the water closed over his head.

* * *

The body turned from the desk. It straightened the paper stack. It trimmed the candle with two fingers, absently, the gesture so perfectly Aldric's own that somewhere far below, in the dark, the real king felt it like a knife.

In the war room, the Vessel reviewed the decree it had written. Satisfied. It summoned the captain of the eastern watch and gave orders in a voice hoarse with manufactured grief: relief columns at dawn, survivors to be resettled, the tragedy of Emberfen to be mourned publicly and thoroughly. It wept two tears at the correct moment in the address to the court. The tears were real — drawn up from Aldric's own grief, decanted like wine from a cask it kept locked but always within reach.

That night it fed.

It stood on the balcony while the fires of Emberfen guttered down to embers, and it drank what rose from them. Memory does not die with the body; it leaves, the way heat leaves a cooling stone, and the Vessel had learned over centuries to stand downwind. Three generations of Emberfen poured into it: the reeve's pride in his orchard, the miller's shame, a girl's first kiss behind the meetinghouse, the whole congregation singing at spring festival with the bells going, the bells going —

It paused. Something in the harvest had a hook in it. A fragment, recent, sharp: a child. A mud-caked child passing through Emberfen days before, asking questions in a language older than the village, carrying a shard of black glass that hummed. The villagers' memories of her glowed like a coal among ashes.

The sigil-fragment. Walking. Alive.

Below, in the flooded dark, Aldric felt the hunger turn like a great fish turning, and knew — without knowing how he knew — that the clock he had been counting had just begun to run faster.
