## 16. Serane Closes In

The river came down off the mountain in a voice like grinding teeth, white and loud, and Serane Voss stood at the crossing with her boots in the shallows and let the cold climb up through the leather into her bones. She had learned, long ago, to be grateful for cold. Cold was honest. Cold asked nothing of her and gave nothing back, and a body that felt only its own discomfort was a body that could not feel the memories shifting inside her like silt in a flooded cellar.

She had been tracking them for eleven days.

Eleven days of mud and switchback trails and the particular, maddening silence that hung around the child. Wren — she had the name now, pried from a tollhouse keeper whose mind had come apart under her hands like wet bread — Wren left no ordinary trail. No warmth lingered where she slept. Dogs would not bark at her. But stone remembered her, and stone was patient, and Serane had spent enough years learning to read what other people left behind in the world to follow a thread of humming granite across half of Thornfell's spine.

Astrid Kavanagh was harder. Astrid left *nothing*. That was the tell, in the end. Every traveler bled small memories into their campsites — a snatch of song hummed over a fire, the shape of a worry worn into the dirt where they'd paced. The Kavanagh woman's camps were clean as swept floors. A memory-cutter who knew exactly how much of herself she shed into every place she slept. Professional discipline. Serane respected it the way a duelist respects a rival's stance: as something she intended to break.

And yet here they came, right on schedule, because Serane had done her arithmetic well. There was one bridge at Greywater Crossing, one ferryman, and he took no coin after dark — only salt, which neither fugitive would think to carry. They would have to cross by the stepping stones below the falls or double back three days into country where Serane's soldiers already waited. Fugitives in a hurry chose the stones. Fugitives in a hurry made mistakes.

The mist came off the falls and silvered her shoulders. She watched the far bank, where the pines stood black against a bruised evening sky, and she thought about the child.

That was the trouble with this hunt. She kept thinking about the child.

* * *

Serane had been seven years old the winter her father sold her childhood.

Not all of it. The crown's brokers were precise men; they took what had market value. Her mother's face went first — it fetched a good price from a merchant in Vaelmoor who had loved and lost a woman with grey eyes and could not bear to remember her. Then the summer of the plum tree, the sound of her own name spoken gently, the smell of bread rising, the specific weight of being carried to bed by arms that loved her. Sold lot by lot, vial by vial, until the ledger balanced against her father's debts and there was a girl of eight standing in an empty house wondering why the rooms felt so large and why she wept sometimes without knowing what for.

The brokers had been almost kind about it. They always were. *You won't miss what you can't remember,* the senior one told her, crouching to her height, his breath smelling of ink and vinegar. And she hadn't. That was the horror she understood only decades later, standing in her quarters in Vaelmoor with a drawer full of vials that did not belong to her: grief needs a shape, and they had taken hers away before it could set. She had grown up around a wound-shaped hole and called the hole herself.

So she had gone into the crown's service, because the crown paid in memory and memory was the only currency that made the hole feel smaller. They gave her other people's childhoods to fill the gap. A farm girl's first snowfall. A fisherman son's pride in his father's hands. She consumed them like a starving woman eats — fast, undiscriminating, ashamed — and for a little while each one fit, and then it didn't, and the hole remained, patient, exact.

The king's inquisitors were not permitted families. It was written nowhere but understood everywhere: a person with nothing to lose serves longest. Serane had understood it as a promise rather than a threat.

Now she stood in a freezing river waiting to take a child from the only person who seemed willing to keep her, and she found she could not make the arithmetic of duty sit straight in her chest.

*They are unbinding Thornfell,* she told herself. *The child is a fragment of the sigil. Whatever the Kavanagh woman believes she is doing, the result will be passes open, wards dead, and everything north of the mountains pouring south.* She had seen the border villages. She knew what came down out of the passes when the old seals weakened. Duty was simple. Duty was a river you crossed or drowned in.

But then there was the other thing, the thing she had not reported.

Three nights ago, in the ash of the ward-village at Emberlin, she had knelt beside a burned cradle and found, wedged beneath its iron frame, a scrap of parchment. A plea, written in a hand that shook — the king's hand, she was certain, though the letters lurched like a drunk man's. *If any loyal soul reads this: I am still within. Do not trust the crown. The crown is not mine.* She had burned it. She had stood over the fire watching it curl and told herself it was forgery, sedition, a trap laid by traitors.

She had memorized it first.

* * *

She heard them before she saw them — or rather, she heard the water change. The falls' roar stuttered, just slightly, as if the mountain itself had drawn a breath. On the far bank, among the black pines, two shapes detached themselves and started for the stepping stones: a tall woman moving with a cutter's economical grace, and a small figure whose steps made the wet rocks glow faintly, briefly, like coals breathing.

Sigil-light. Living sigil-light, walking on bare mountain stone. Serane felt her professional awe war with something older and less useful.

She stepped out of the mist onto the first stone. "Kavanagh."

Astrid stopped mid-river. Water sheeted around her ankles; behind her, Wren went very still, and the glow along the stones brightened like a warning. "Inquisitor." Astrid's voice was flat and pleasant, a shopkeeper's voice. "You're a long way from your leash."

"I'm a long way from a great many things." Serane advanced another stone. The spray soaked her coat. "Give me the child and walk away. You have skills, Kavanagh. The crown has use for skills. You might even live comfortably."

"You've met me," Astrid said. "You know how that offer lands."

"I do." Another stone. Twenty feet of roaring water between them now. "Then we do it the hard way. I should warn you—" and here Serane let a little genuine regret show, because some part of it was real, "—I've consumed forty-one memories since Emberlin. Soldiers'. Villagers'. Things I'd rather not describe. I am very strong tonight, and you are very tired, and the child is—" she looked past Astrid's shoulder at Wren, small and silent on the bank, clutching her shard of black glass, "—the child is going to stand there and watch. So whatever you're planning, plan it fast."

"Thank you," Astrid said. "That's generous of you."

It happened fast. Later, replaying it in the dark of her skull, Serane could never quite reconstruct the sequence — only the sensation, which was that of a door opening inside her that she had nailed shut twenty years ago.

Astrid moved. Not forward — *sideways*, dropping off the stone into knee-deep water, stylus already out of her sleeve, bone-white and gleaming. Serane lunged, caught a fistful of coat, felt fabric tear — and then the stylus touched her temple, light as a moth landing, and the world *tore*.

* * *

The first thing Astrid saw was a plum tree.

Summer, heavy heat, a garden behind a narrow house. A girl sat in the crook of a branch with purple juice staining her chin, laughing at something below — laughing with her whole body, unguarded, the laugh of a child who has never once doubted she is safe. Below her a woman shaded her eyes and scolded without meaning it. The light was gold. The light was endless. The girl in the tree was Serane.

The second memory was winter. An empty house. The same girl, older by a year, sitting on the floor of a room stripped of furniture, holding a wooden bird someone had carved her, turning it over and over, trying to remember why it mattered. Trying to remember who had carved it. The grief arrived without a name, vast and formless, flooding a child too small to hold it, and no one came, because everyone who might have come had been sold.

The third was a corridor in the palace at Vaelmoor. Serane, young and new-uniformed, standing before a mirror, practicing smiles. Smile one: authority. Smile two: reassurance. Smile three: the one that made people talk. Behind her reflected eyes, visible only to someone who knew how to look, the hole gaped — the wound-shaped absence where a life used to be — and the girl practiced her smiles over it like laying planks across a chasm, and the planks held for thirty years, and she called the chasm *duty*.

Astrid surfaced, gasping, on her knees in the shallows, three vials warm in her palm. Serane staggered back a step, hand pressed to her temple, and for one raw moment her face was naked — no inquisitor's mask, no practiced smile — just shock, and beneath the shock, terror, and beneath the terror something so lonely it made Astrid's chest ache with recognition.

Because Astrid knew that look. She wore it every night over a stolen memory of a mother she never had.

"No," Serane said. Her voice cracked on it. She cleared her throat and rebuilt the wall in front of her face brick by brick, but Astrid had seen the foundations now, and walls never fool the people who've seen foundations. "No. Give those back."

"They're mine now," Astrid said quietly. She rose out of the water, dripping, vials closed in her fist. "That's how the trade works. Isn't it? You taught the whole kingdom the rule: whoever holds them, keeps them."

"That is not—" Serane stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was low and terribly controlled. "You don't understand what you took."

"I understand plenty." Astrid backed toward the bank, toward Wren, whose eyes were wide and whose shard was humming audibly now, a thin bell-note cutting through the falls. "I understand you were a little girl in a plum tree once. I understand somebody sold that. I understand you've spent your whole life trying to buy it back with other people's lives, and it doesn't work, because I've tried the same trick with lesser coin." She reached the bank. Wet gravel gave under her boots. "We're not so different, Inquisitor. That's the worst news you've gotten all year, isn't it?"

Something moved behind Serane's eyes — rage, yes, but underneath the rage a terrible flicker of *agreement*, and it was the agreement that frightened her. She drew her blade. "Last chance. The child."

Wren stepped forward.

It was the first time she had ever placed herself between Astrid and danger, and the mountain noticed. Every sigil carved into the old crossing-stones — marks worn nearly smooth by centuries of flood — flared at once, blue-white, and the river's roar seemed to bend around the light like wind around a spire. Wren looked up at Serane Voss with eyes that held more age than her small body could reasonably contain, and she said nothing, because she didn't need to. The light said it. *This one is kept. This one is chosen. Come through me if you must.*

Serane stood hip-deep in the freezing water with her blade drawn and her composure bleeding out of her, and she found she could not move. Not because of fear — fear she could work with — but because the child's gaze had snagged on something in her, some splinter of the girl in the plum tree, and was looking at it with what could only be described as pity.

Pity. From a child. From the asset. From the fragment she was sworn to drag to Vaelmoor in chains.

"Go," Serane said finally, and hated every syllable. "Run. You have until dawn. Then I stop being merciful."

Astrid didn't wait to be told twice. She caught Wren's hand and pulled her up the bank, into the trees, two shapes swallowed by the dark — but at the treeline Wren stopped, turned, and looked back one last time. And in the flare of sigil-light Serane saw the child raise one small hand, not in farewell but in something older, a gesture like an offering, like *I see what was taken from you*, and then they were gone.

Serane stood alone in the river until the light died and the cold finally, honestly, found her.

* * *

She built a fire on the far bank because her hands were shaking and she needed them occupied, and while it caught she turned the three missing memories over in her mind, cataloguing the gaps the way a jeweler counts stolen stones. The plum tree. The empty house. The mirror.

Gone. All three. Carved out clean, surgical, without cruelty — that was somehow worse. Somewhere in the dark a woman was carrying her childhood in three glass vials, and Serane could not even hate her properly, because she had done the same thing to hundreds. Thousands. She had taken whole lives from whole people and called it law.

She fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch.

The letter from Emberlin surfaced in her memory, unbidden. *I am still within.*

She had filed it as treason. She had filed the child as an asset. She had filed Astrid Kavanagh as a criminal, and criminals were simple, and assets were inventory, and the crown's business was the crown's business.

But the child had pitied her. And Astrid — Astrid had looked at the place where Serane's childhood used to be and had not flinched, had not gloated, had only said *we're not so different* with the weary gentleness of a woman naming a disease she recognized in her own blood.

Enemies were supposed to be simple. Serane had built thirty years on the principle that enemies were simple: they opposed the crown, therefore they were wrong, therefore anything done to them was justified. But the principle had a crack in it now, wide as the one in her memory, and through the crack she could see the whole rotten architecture of her life — the broker's kind voice, the empty house, the smiles practiced over the chasm, all of it erected by other people's choices and maintained by her own.

*Made,* she thought. *Not born. None of us were born this way. We were all of us made, piece by piece, and sold at market rates.*

Dawn was maybe four hours off. She had given them until dawn, and Serane Voss had broken every oath except the ones she made to herself.

She looked east, toward Vaelmoor, where the crown waited and the thing wearing it grew hungrier. She looked west, into the black pines, where a memory-thief and a sigil-fragment were running for the heart of the mountain with three pieces of a childhood that did not belong to either of them.

Her hand went to the pouch at her belt — the one containing the remaining vials, the forty-one consumed memories, the arsenal she had been building since Emberlin. Fuel for strength. Food for the hole.

Or leverage. Or payment. Memories bought things in Thornfell. Memories had always bought things in Thornfell.

Ysolde the broker had a price list. Serane had seen it once, years ago, in a confiscated ledger: names, services, and the strange currencies accepted in trade. If a fugitive wanted passage, or information, or a map to places no soldier could follow —

She put out the fire with her boot and stood, and the decision was not made so much as discovered, already whole inside her, the way a wound-shaped hole discovers its own shape.

Before dawn, Serane Voss turned west, and began to write a message she would deny under torture ever having sent:

*To the memory-broker Ysolde, in Brackwater. I wish to sell. Everything. Name your terms.*

The river ground its teeth behind her, and she did not look back.
