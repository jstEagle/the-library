## 17. The Road Beneath the Road

The smugglers' entrance was a lie told in stone.

Astrid had known it since she was nine years old, when Ferro first pressed her small hand against the mossy face of the cliff behind the Tollhouse of Small Mercies and said, *Feel for what isn't there.* The waterfall that thundered down the rockface looked like any of a hundred waterfalls in the Thornfell passes — white noise, cold spray, the smell of wet granite and pine rot. But beneath her palm, where the water's roar should have been loudest, there was a silence. A seam. The mountain holding its breath around a door.

She found it now the way she always did: blind, by absence. Her fingers walked the stone until the sound of the falls dropped away as though someone had stuffed wool in her ears, and there — waist-high, hidden under an overhang of lichen-slick rock — was the iron ring. She hauled on it. Counterweights groaned somewhere deep inside the cliff, and a slab of granite swung inward on hinges older than the kingdom, exhaling a breath of air that smelled of dust and cold iron and something else, something faintly sweet, like the memory of a forge fire long gone out.

Wren stood at her shoulder, rain streaming off her hood, eyes enormous.

"You first," Astrid said, because the child would hesitate at any threshold unless given no room to. "Mind the third step. It's a liar."

The tunnel swallowed them by degrees. Ten paces in, the storm behind them became a gray rumor; twenty paces in, it was nothing at all, and the dark came down like a lid. Astrid lit the stub of candle she'd carried since Greyhollow — wax hoarded like gold, flame cupped in her palm — and its light reached maybe six feet before the mountain ate it.

Six feet was enough. Six feet was how you survived the deep roads: never look past what you can touch, never trust a passage wider than your shoulders, and above all, never sing. Sound traveled strangely below, and things lived in the old workings that answered songs.

But Wren was already humming.

It wasn't a song, exactly. It was more like breathing with intent — three notes, rising, the same three notes Astrid had heard her murmur in her sleep on the road from Brackwater. The dead tongue again, or a piece of it. The sound went down the tunnel ahead of them and did not echo so much as *settle*, the way dust settles.

And then the wall lit up.

Astrid stopped so fast the candle guttered. Three paces ahead, set into the stone at the height of a child's shoulder, a sigil had woken — a knot of fine silver lines, interlocked like fingers, glowing with a soft blue-white light that threw Wren's shadow long and trembling across the floor. Astrid knew sigils. She'd sold memories of sigils, once, to a collector in Greyhollow who paid triple for anything pre-Purge: smiths' marks, ward-charts, the geometric prayers the old order had cut into every keystone from Cael to the sea. She knew their shapes the way she knew the shapes of coins.

She had never seen one *burning*.

"Wren." Her voice came out rougher than she meant. "What did you do?"

The child turned. In the new light her face was all eyes, and those eyes were wide with something Astrid hadn't seen in her before — not fear. Recognition. "It heard me," Wren said. Her common speech was still slow, still deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Not me. The — " she made a sound in the old tongue, a word like a bell struck underwater, then gave up translating. "The part of me that remembers being big."

Astrid looked at the glowing mark. Then at the tunnel beyond it, stretching black into the mountain's throat.

"Then let's hope," she said, "the rest of you remembers the way."

* * *

They walked through a waking world.

Every fifty paces or so — sometimes less, sometimes more, wherever the old smiths had judged the dark needed a lamp — a sigil waited in the stone, dormant for centuries, and every one of them woke as Wren passed. Some flared bright and then dimmed to embers behind them, like watchmen lighting torches and moving on. Others stayed lit, and those were stranger, because staying lit meant *remembering*, and the mountain, Astrid realized with a creeping wonder she didn't quite trust, was remembering them.

The fourth sigil they passed showed her why the deep roads had been built at all.

It wasn't a ward-mark. It was a picture — no, a record — lines of silver script flowing around a central emblem like water around a stone. Wren stopped in front of it and put both hands flat against the rock, and the light bloomed outward until Astrid could read the flow of it, and though she couldn't read the words, she understood their shape the way she understood the shape of grief: this was a tally. Names. Hundreds of names, thousands, cut in a careful clerk's hand, and at the top, larger than the rest, a single line that made Wren go rigid from crown to heel.

"What is it?" Astrid asked.

"Everyone," Wren whispered. "When we were — when I was the lattice, this was a door we counted people through. Smiths going down. Smiths coming up." Her fingers traced the great line at the top without touching it. "This name is mine. Was mine. It's the longest, because it was the oldest."

She didn't say what the name was. Astrid didn't ask. Some doors you leave shut even when they're open.

But the sigils kept coming, and some of them weren't records at all. One was a warning — a jagged spiral that flared red-orange as Wren neared it, and Astrid, reading the geometry the way she read faces, yanked the child back three full steps just before the floor ahead sagged with a groan into a shaft that breathed up air warm as a furnace mouth. Another was a joke — she was nearly sure it was a joke, a sigil shaped like a fish that pulsed in sequence down a side-passage, pointing toward water, because some smith four hundred years dead had decided travelers underground deserved directions to a spring. The spring was still there, running cold and clean over stones that glowed faintly where Wren knelt beside it, and they drank, and filled the skins, and Astrid caught herself almost smiling.

That frightened her more than the shaft had. Wonder was a currency, and she'd spent her whole life making sure she owed none of it to anyone.

* * *

They camped at the junction Ferro had called the Widow's Elbow, a chamber where three tunnels met under a ceiling lost in darkness, its floor scattered with the bones of an old smugglers' firepit and a rusted lantern-hook still driven into the wall. Astrid built a small fire — smoke was a risk, but cold was a certainty, and certainty killed slower but just as dead. Wren sat cross-legged near the flames, and the nearest sigil, high on the chamber wall, glowed down at her like a moon that had decided to keep watch over one particular child.

"Eat," Astrid said, handing her hardtack softened in melted snow. Wren ate. They'd been three days underground and the rhythm of it had settled over them like a coat: walk, eat, sleep, walk. Astrid found, to her private disgust, that she'd begun to find the rhythm *good*. That was how it started. That was how Ferro had gotten his hooks in her, how anyone did. You let the days stack up next to another person and one morning you couldn't lift them alone.

"Ask," Wren said suddenly.

Astrid blinked. "Ask what?"

"You've been carrying a question since the fish." Wren's mouth twitched — the ghost of the smile she was learning to deploy, carefully, like a tool she wasn't sure wouldn't cut her. "You do a thing with your eyebrows. Ferro says you got it from him. He lies. You got it from yourself."

"I don't have a question."

"You have twelve. Pick one."

Astrid fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Fine. One question. The cheapest one, which meant the most expensive.

"The sigils wake for you. All of them. But back at the wayshrine, on the surface, the carved one flooded us both — pulled me into the vision whether I wanted it or not." She turned the stick in the flames. "Down here they only light. They don't *touch* me. Why?"

Wren was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"Because they know you're a door," she said at last, "and doors are patient."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have." The child hugged her knees. "The lattice doesn't grab. It waits. It has waited four hundred years for — " she stopped herself, and Astrid saw the shape of what she'd stopped herself from saying: *for you.* "You'll be touched soon enough. Everything that sleeps down here is going to want to talk to the heir. The sigils are just being polite."

Heir. The word Ysolde had used in Brackwater, the word Astrid had refused with everything in her, and here it came walking back down a mountain tunnel wearing a child's voice. She should have argued. Instead she looked up at the watching sigil, its silver lines steady and warm, and felt — for one unguarded heartbeat — watched over rather than watched. Like the mountain wasn't hunting her. Like it was keeping a seat.

"Sleep," she said, too gruffly. "I'll take first watch."

Wren curled up against the wall with her back to the glowing mark, and within minutes her breathing went slow and deep. Astrid sat with the knife across her knees and the fire burning low, and she did not sleep, and she did not stop looking at the light.

* * *

The dream came anyway. It always came.

Her mother's kitchen — not remembered, *invented*, built nightly from the stolen memory of a woman who had died in Greyhollow owing nobody and loved by everyone, a memory Astrid had lifted from the woman herself at the edge of the end and never sold, never would sell. Bread on the board. Rain on the glass. A voice saying her name in a tone that meant *you are safe and also late.*

Except tonight the kitchen was wrong. The window looked out not onto Greyhollow's crooked rooftops but onto silver lattice-work stretching away under a vaulted dark, and the bread on the board was a shard of black glass humming against the wood, and the voice saying her name was not the dead mother's voice at all. It was layered. It was many. It sounded like a choir singing through stone.

*Astrid.*

She woke with her heart slamming and her hand on the knife, and the chamber was brighter than it should have been. Every sigil in the Widow's Elbow was lit — all of them, the whole curved sweep of the walls, dozens of silver knots burning steady as stars — and Wren stood in the center of the floor with her arms slightly out from her sides, asleep on her feet, her eyes open and overflowing with soft light, humming the three ascending notes over and over while the mountain hummed back.

"Wren." Astrid crossed the space in two strides and took the child by the shoulders. The contact broke it. The light drained out of Wren's eyes and she swayed, and Astrid caught her, and for a moment they stood there in the sudden ordinary dark with only the low fire and the last few sigils dimming reluctantly overhead.

"I heard them," Wren mumbled into Astrid's collar. "All of them. They were counting me home."

"You're not going anywhere," Astrid said, and heard how much of herself was in it — not the smuggler's caution, not the broker's arithmetic. Something older and less careful. "Nobody counts you but me. Understood? When you walk strange in the night, you come back to my voice. Not theirs. Mine."

Wren pulled back and looked up at her, and in the firelight her expression was the one Astrid was learning to dread and treasure in equal measure: the look of a child cataloguing a promise.

"Mine," Wren repeated. Testing the weight of it. Keeping it.

They packed in silence. Astrid kicked dirt over the fire, checked the blade, checked the skins, checked the child — the last check taking longest, as it always did now. Then she chose the left-hand tunnel, the one Ferro's maps marked with a single word in his crabbed hand: *HEARTWARD.*

They'd been walking perhaps half an hour when the corridor changed. The rough-hewn smuggler's cutting gave way to ancient worked stone, walls dressed smooth and jointed so finely a knife blade couldn't slip between them, and every ten paces a sigil burned in the stone — not waking now, not flickering, but *lit already*, burning before they arrived, as if the light were traveling ahead of them through the rock itself, racing toward something deeper.

And the air grew warm. Not forge-warm. Body-warm. The mountain breathing around them, quickening, and beneath their boots — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable — a pulse.

Astrid stopped. Wren stopped beside her. Ahead, the tunnel opened into a vast dark that their borrowed candlelight couldn't begin to fill, and out of that dark rose a sound that froze Astrid's blood and thawed it in the same instant: a slow, colossal exhalation, like a gate the size of the sky drawing breath.

The sigils along the walls began, one by one, to change color — silver to gold, gold to a white like dawn through ice — and in their shifting light Astrid saw what hung in the opening ahead: a door. No. A *seal*. A disc of black stone thirty feet across, carved edge to edge with interlocking sigil-work, and at its center a socket in the shape of a hand.

A hand-shaped hole, waiting.

Wren's fingers found hers in the dark. Small. Cold. Trembling — but pulling forward.

"Astrid," the child whispered, in the old tongue first and then, deliberately, in common, "it knows your hand."

Behind them, far back down the tunnel they'd come by, something else stirred — a sound like boot heels on stone, and beneath the boot heels, thin and wrong and echoing, someone beginning to whistle a smuggler's tune that only three living people knew.

Serane was in the mountain.

The seal began to glow.
