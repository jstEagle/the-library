## 18. Ghosts Who Kept the Gate

The dark under the mountain was not empty.

Wren had known that since the moment her feet first touched the smugglers' road — since the dormant sigils along the tunnel walls had woken one by one at her passing, kindling like coals breathed upon, throwing their soft silver light across Astrid's startled face. The mountain was waking to its heir, Astrid had said, half laughing, half afraid. But Wren knew it was not waking *to* Astrid.

It was waking to her.

She walked in the middle of the little procession now, close enough to touch Astrid's coat if she wanted to, and she did want to, often, the way a swimmer wants the shore in view. Ahead went Ysolde with her hooded lantern and her merchant's stride, her map of the deep roads folded into memory rather than paper — she had burned the paper at the last junction, saying that some knowledge should exist in only one skull at a time. Behind them came the silence of Ferro's absence, which none of them spoke of but all of them carried, heavier than any pack. And all around them, above them and below them and pressing gently against the walls of the world like faces against frosted glass, were the echoes.

Wren could feel them the way other people felt weather.

They had been growing louder for hours. At first it had been only a whispering in the stone, a sound like pages turning in a distant room. Then it had become voices without words, a murmur with the shape of speech but not the substance. Now, as the tunnel widened and the sigils overhead blazed brighter than starlight, she could almost make out what they were saying, and what they were saying was:

*Come back. Come back. You are late, little piece. Come home.*

She stopped walking.

Astrid noticed at once — she always noticed, which still astonished Wren, who had spent so long being a thing no one looked at twice. Astrid turned back through the silver light, and even before she spoke Wren saw the question forming on her face, the way it did: not *what's wrong*, because Wren couldn't always answer that in common words, but *tell me anyway, I'll wait.*

"They're here," Wren said. Her voice came out small in the vastness. "The smiths."

Ysolde's lantern dipped. "There are no smiths," the broker said flatly. "There have been no smiths since the Purge. I told you — the line is extinct."

"The line is extinct," Wren agreed, and touched her own chest, where the shard of black glass hung warm against her skin. "But they're here anyway."

* * *

The tunnel opened into a gate.

That was the only word for it, though there was nothing to bar — no doors, no portcullis, only an archway of living rock through which the air itself seemed thicker, older, charged the way the sky is charged before lightning. The arch was carved from base to crown with sigils, hundreds of them, thousands, interlocked like scales on a sleeping serpent, and every one of them burned now, every one of them awake, so that the whole gateway shimmered like heat over summer stone.

And in the light beneath the arch stood figures.

They were not ghosts as the stories told ghosts — no drifting shrouds, no mournful moaning. They stood like people. They wore aprons and carried hammers, tongs, styli of pale bone; their hands were scarred in the patterns Wren knew from her own impossible dreams, burn-crossed and calloused in the shapes that sigil-work leaves. Their faces were blurred, the way a face is blurred behind falling water, but their postures were vivid, particular, alive: one leaned on her hammer with her weight on the left hip; one held his stylus tucked behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil; one had her arms crossed and her chin lifted, unimpressed, waiting.

Wren's breath caught. She knew them. Not their names — she had no names for them — but *them*. The lattice of light beneath the mountains, the vast singing thing she had been before she woke small and alone and mute in a storm outside a stranger's attic door: these had been its keepers. These had been her family, once, in a way that predated family, predated bodies, predated everything except the binding itself.

"Little piece," said the woman leaning on her hammer, and though her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, thin as light through a crack, Wren heard joy in it. Real joy. It cracked something open in Wren's chest. "You came back to us. We felt you in the walls. We felt you all the way down the spine of the world."

"I—" Wren began, and found she had no words that fit. Common tongue was too coarse; the dead language rose in her throat and stuck there, because speaking it here, among them, might mean agreeing to something.

"You were lost," said the man with the stylus behind his ear, stepping forward. His blur of a face tilted, and Wren had the terrible, wonderful sense of being *recognized*. "When the crown broke and the Vessel bit into the binding, you were torn loose — the smallest fragment, the keystone's echo, the part of us that remembers why we forged it at all. We grieved you. Do you know we grieved you? For two hundred years we have stood at this gate and grieved."

"Two hundred years," whispered Ysolde behind her, and her voice had gone strange. "The Purge. They died in the Purge. These are—"

"These are echoes," Astrid said quietly. She had moved up beside Wren without Wren noticing, close enough that her shoulder nearly touched Wren's shoulder, and her hand hovered near Wren's arm — not grabbing, never grabbing, just *there*, the way a wall is there in a storm. "Memories with nowhere to go. I've seen the like before, in old houses. Places remember people who loved them." Her eyes swept the shining figures, and Wren watched her do the arithmetic she was always doing — cost, price, toll — and come out uneasy. "Places can also remember people wrong."

"We do not remember wrong," said the crossed-armed woman, and her voice sharpened like a struck flint. "We are the gatekeepers. We kept the gate when the mountain slept. We kept it when the kings forgot their oaths. We will keep it until the binding is remade or the world is unmade, whichever comes first." She uncrossed her arms and extended both hands toward Wren, palms up, the gesture of a smith offering a finished work. "Come, little piece. Come through. Let us set you back into the lattice where you belong. Let us make you whole."

The words landed in Wren like a bell struck in an empty room.

Whole. That was the word for the ache she had carried since she woke in the mud with rain in her mouth and no name anyone could pronounce. *Whole* meant the vast singing thing again. Whole meant no more hunger, no more fear, no more lying awake listening to a stranger breathe in the next room and wondering why the sound made her feel safe instead of afraid. Whole meant going home.

She took one step toward the arch.

* * *

The echoes' light brightened. The sigils sang. Somewhere far below, Wren felt the great lattice turn toward her like a sunflower toward dawn, and every buried ward in the mountain exhaled at once, and it would be so easy, so easy, to let go of this small heavy body with its small heavy heart and pour herself back into the light —

"Wren."

Astrid's voice. Not loud. Not commanding. Just her name, spoken the way Astrid said most true things: quietly, like setting something fragile down on a table.

Wren stopped. Her foot hovered over the threshold stone.

"Look at them," Astrid said, still quiet, and stepped up beside her so they stood together at the edge of the shining gate. "Really look. What do you see?"

Wren looked. The smiths stood in their grace and their welcome, arms open, faces blurred. And now that she truly looked — now that she looked past the joy in her own chest, the way Astrid had taught her to look past the first thing a memory offered and ask what it was hiding — she saw it.

They had not moved. Not one of them. Not in all the time since the gate opened. The woman leaned on her hammer at exactly the same angle. The man's stylus sat behind his ear exactly where it had been. Their mouths shaped words of welcome, but their feet had not shifted a single inch, and their blurred eyes did not track her when she swayed. They were not people waiting at a door.

They were a memory of people waiting at a door. A memory replaying. A memory worn smooth by two hundred years of repetition, the way Astrid's vials wore smooth in the handling, the way a story changes when it is told ten thousand times by someone desperate to keep it.

"You're not calling me home," Wren said slowly. "You're calling the *piece* home. The fragment. The thing I was before." Her hand rose to the black glass at her throat. "You don't know me. You know what I'm made of."

"We know what matters," said the hammer-woman, and her voice had changed — or Wren heard it change, now that her ears were open. Beneath the warmth there was a hunger, patient and enormous, the patience of stone. "The body is a vessel. The name is a coat. Come away from the coat, little piece. Names fray. Bodies fail. The lattice is forever."

"She keeps the name," Wren said. Her voice shook, and she let it. "I *chose* the name."

"Chosen things are temporary things," said the stylus-man gently, terribly kindly. "Forged things endure. You were forged, child. You were ours before you were anything else. Come home."

And the pull came again, vast and sweet, and Wren felt her edges soften, felt the boundaries of *Wren* grow thin as paper held to a lamp — and it would be rest, it would be wholeness, it would be an end to every lonely night —

"She's mine."

Astrid's voice cut across the singing like a blade laid flat across a harp string.

Not loud. Astrid was never loud when it mattered. But the whole gate seemed to flinch, the way a room flinches when a quiet person finally raises their head, and every sigil on the arch guttered at once.

Wren turned. Astrid stood with her feet planted on the threshold stone, between Wren and the shining smiths, and her face — Wren had catalogued that face carefully over the months, the way she catalogued everything about the woman who had opened a door in a storm — her face was doing something Wren had never seen it do. The merchant's calculation was gone. The wariness was gone. Even the grief she carried like a second coat was gone. What remained was bare and blazing and absolutely certain.

"She's mine," Astrid said again, to the gate, to the echoes, to the whole listening mountain. "Not yours. You had her first, I understand that. You made the thing she comes from. But the thing she *is* — that happened later. That happened in my attic, in a storm, when I opened a door I should have kept shut, and she looked at me with those eyes, and I—" Astrid's breath caught, and Wren realized with a jolt that the woman was crying, silently, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand as if the tears were an inconvenience to be managed. "I chose her. Do you hear me? Whatever you are, whatever's left of you, you kept your gate for two hundred years and you forgot the one thing worth keeping. She isn't a piece. She isn't a fragment. She isn't a keystone's echo. She's a girl who learned to speak. She's a girl who unmade soldiers with a word and then cried about it because she was afraid of her own heart. She's mine. And I don't give back what's mine."

Silence filled the gate like water filling a cup.

The echoes stood unmoving in their light. And then, one by one, their blurred faces turned toward each other, and Wren heard — not with her ears, but with the deep sense that had been hers before she had a body — the sound of a memory *remembering itself*. The sound of something long-repeated being questioned for the first time.

"A claim," said the hammer-woman, and her voice was different now, thinner, wondering. "Spoken on the threshold. Freely given. Freely witnessed." Her blurred face turned down toward Astrid, and though Wren could not see the eyes, she felt their regard settle on the woman like a hand on a scale. "Only the blood of the smiths could make such a claim stand. Only an heir may refuse the lattice its own."

"I don't want your lattice," Astrid said. "I want the girl."

The crossed-armed woman laughed. It was a rusty sound, a hinge unused for centuries, and it broke something in the gate's terrible gravity. "Two hundred years," she said, "and the heir arrives shouting love at a haunted door. The old ones would have hated her." A pause. "The old ones would have been wrong."

* * *

What happened next, Wren would spend a long time learning to describe.

The echoes did not vanish. Echoes, she understood now, did not vanish; they *resolved*, the way a chord resolves, the way a held breath releases. The welcoming light dimmed to something gentler, lamplight-colored, honest. The smiths' frozen postures loosened — not into life, for they had none left to live, but into something like rest. The hammer-woman set down her hammer. The stylus-man took the stylus from behind his ear and held it out, not to Wren, but to Astrid.

"She will need this," he said. "Deeper down, where the archive sleeps. Bone of the first forge. It will answer her blood, and only her blood." His blur of a face inclined toward Wren. "And the little piece —"

"*Wren,*" said Astrid.

Something passed across the echo's face that might, in a living man, have been a smile. "And *Wren*," he corrected, tasting the name like a new alloy, "will need to choose, soon, and often, and harder than either of you yet knows. The heart of the sigil does not ask politely. We know. We answered it once, and it kept us." He looked down at his own translucent hands, at the hammer resting in the dust of two hundred years. "We thought answering was our duty. Perhaps it was our vanity. Tell the mountain, when you reach it, that the gatekeepers are sorry."

"For what?" Wren asked.

"For mistaking the lock for the purpose," he said. "For so long. Go. While the gate is kind."

The light folded inward like a closing book. The sigils along the arch sank one by one back into sleep, and the tunnel beyond lay open and dark and ordinary, smelling of wet stone and old air. Where the echoes had stood there remained only the archway, and beneath it, on a low plinth of natural rock, the bone stylus — pale, slender, worn smooth at the grip by fingers long dissolved.

No one moved for a moment.

Then Ysolde exhaled through her teeth, a merchant pricing the unpriceable. "An heir," she murmured, not looking at Astrid. "On the threshold. Witnessed. Well. There are parties in Vaelmoor who would pay kingdoms to know that, and I am *trying*, Kavanagh, I am genuinely trying, to be the sort of person who doesn't sell you." She stooped, wrapped the stylus in oilcloth with practiced tenderness, and handed it up to Astrid like a midwife handing over a child. "Try not to die before this becomes valuable."

"Ferro would have liked her," Wren said quietly, and Astrid made a sound that was half laugh, half wound.

* * *

They walked on. The dark swallowed the gate behind them. Wren walked close to Astrid, closer than before, and after a while Astrid's hand found hers in the darkness — not hovering this time, not a wall in a storm, but a grip, deliberate and warm and slightly too tight, the grip of someone who had shouted a thing aloud and was not going to unsay it.

"You didn't have to do that," Wren said at last. "Step between. Claim me. The lattice — it might have made me whole. It might have fixed everything, the binding, the wards, the king. One piece, returned. Maybe that was the easy way."

"The easy way," Astrid said, "was selling memories in Greyhollow until I died alone in an attic full of other people's best days." Her thumb moved over Wren's knuckles, absently, like reading braille. "I'm done with easy ways."

Ahead, Ysolde's lantern swung, and the deep road bent downward, and the air grew warmer, and somewhere far below them Wren felt the heart of the mountain turning in its sleep, dreaming of crowns and bindings and a name it had not yet asked her to surrender.

But before the dark took them fully, the echoes' voices came one last time through the stone — faint now, fading, many voices braided into one, and this time there was no command in them at all. Only a warning, and a blessing, and Wren could not tell where one ended and the other began:

*"The heart knows the heir. The heart knows the piece. And the heart, children — the heart has begun, after two hundred years, to wake. What it asks of you when it opens its eyes, no gatekeeper can say. Walk fast. Walk loving. And whatever it offers you — remember that we also were offered wholeness, once."*

The voices sank away like water into sand.

Wren squeezed Astrid's hand, and felt her squeeze back, and they went down together into the breathing dark, toward the heart that was waking, carrying between them a bone stylus, a claimed name, and a promise neither of them had spoken aloud yet: that whatever the mountain asked, they would find a third way — or make one.
