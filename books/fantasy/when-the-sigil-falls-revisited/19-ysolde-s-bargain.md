## 19. Ysolde's Bargain

The rain had followed them out of Brackwater for two days, thin and needling, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air and let you walk through it. By the time they reached the waystation at the mouth of the Hollow Road — a lean-to of black timber wedged against the mountain's shoulder like a splinter — Astrid's coat was soaked through at the seams and Wren had gone quiet in the particular way she went quiet when the sigils under the earth were murmuring too loud for her to ignore.

Astrid built a fire from the dry stores Ferro had cached there months ago, back when Ferro was still Ferro and not an empty-eyed man walking south with Serane Voss holding his hand like a lover. She tried not to think about that. She failed, the way she always did.

Wren sat close to the flames, knees drawn up, turning her black glass shard over in her fingers. The shard hummed tonight. Astrid could feel it through the floorboards, a vibration in her back teeth, like a tuning fork pressed to bone.

"You felt something," Astrid said. Not a question. With Wren, questions were luxuries; statements worked better.

Wren nodded. Then she pointed — down, then toward the road they'd come by, then held up one finger and spun it slowly, the way she'd learned to say *someone circling.*

Astrid's hand went to the knife at her belt out of pure habit. "One person."

Another nod. Then Wren touched her own chest, then mimed — clumsily, her face screwing up with the effort of translation — *a woman.* Then she made a shape with her hands: a vial. A memory-vial.

Astrid went very still.

She knew exactly one woman who traded in vials and knew what Astrid carried. And Ysolde did not follow people. Ysolde waited, patient as rot, for people to come back to her — because everyone came back eventually, when the thing they wanted outweighed the price she'd named.

"She found us," Astrid said quietly. "Or she let us go just far enough."

Wren tilted her head, and in the firelight her eyes caught orange and held it wrong, the way the sigils caught light in the deep tunnels. She reached out and tugged Astrid's sleeve — a gesture she'd made a hundred times now, and each time it landed somewhere behind Astrid's ribs like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there.

"I know," Astrid said. "I know you don't want me to do anything stupid."

Wren's expression said, with devastating clarity, that this ship had sailed years ago.

* * *

Ysolde came up the road an hour past midnight, alone, unhurried, her grey cloak beaded with rain and her silver hair pinned up in the severe coiled style of the old broker houses. She looked exactly as she had in Brackwater: composed, faintly amused, like a woman who had already read the last page of every book she'd ever opened.

She stopped at the edge of the firelight and spread her hands, palms out — the old broker's courtesy, showing she carried no steel.

"Kavanagh," she said. "You look terrible. Mountain air disagrees with you."

"You followed a child and a grieving woman through smuggler country," Astrid said, not rising from where she crouched by the fire. "You must want something badly."

Ysolde smiled and stepped into the light. Up close, Astrid could see the new lines around her eyes, the fine tremor in her left hand that she kept folded away when she thought anyone was watching. Grief had been working on Ysolde since Brackwater, quietly, the way water works on stone.

"I want," Ysolde said, "to give you a gift."

"I've seen your gifts. They come with invoices."

"This one comes with an invoice too." Ysolde lowered herself onto the opposite bench with the economy of someone who had spent decades sitting across tables from desperate people. "But first, the gift." From inside her cloak she drew a tube of oilcloth, sealed with wax stamped by no house Astrid recognized, and set it on the bench between them. "A map. Drawn by my grandmother, who drew it from her mother, who took it from a smith's apprentice during the Purge. It shows the road beneath the road — the true descent to the heart of Mount Cael. Every gate. Every ward-door. Every toll the mountain will demand of you on the way down."

Astrid looked at the tube and did not touch it. Her pulse, traitor that it was, quickened. They had been feeling their way downward blind — Wren lighting dormant sigils, guessing at junctions, losing hours to dead-end galleries that the dead smiths had collapsed on purpose. A true map would cut days off their journey. Days they might not have, if the Vessel kept unbinding wards at the pace Aldric's letters — the ones Serane had passed along before she turned — suggested it would.

"And the invoice?" Astrid asked.

"The false mother."

The words went through Astrid like a stylus through the temple. She kept her face still. Twenty years of practice kept her face still.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Astrid." Ysolde said her name gently, which was worse than saying it cruelly. "I watched you work in Brackwater. I've seen a thousand cutters lift a memory, and every one of them has a tell. A hesitation. A flinch. You have one vial you never sell, never trade, never even look at unless you're alone. I saw the shape of it in your kit when you weren't looking. And I know the technique you use — the Kavanagh technique, the deep-cut, the one nobody alive should know because the Kavanaghs died in the Purge. Which means either you stole the technique from a ghost, or—" she spread one hand, delicate, "—you're the ghost."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the loose shingles of the lean-to.

"What I'm offering," Ysolde continued, "is simple. The map for the vial. Your most precious stolen memory, freely given, willingly surrendered. Not extracted — given. There's a difference, in how the sigil takes it. Given memories keep their shape. Stolen ones fray."

"Why," Astrid said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, "would you want a stranger's memory? You're a broker. You deal in volume. One vial is nothing to you."

"One vial," Ysolde said, "is everything, if it's the right vial." For a moment the composure cracked — just a hairline, just around the eyes — and Astrid saw the woman underneath: the one who had sold her own daughter's memory to buy passage out of Brackwater, or so the whispers said; the one who had spent thirty years buying back fragments of other people's lives because she couldn't buy back her own. "You carry a memory of a mother who wasn't yours. I've spent my whole life carrying the absence of a daughter who was. Do you understand what we are, you and I? We are both women keeping warm at fires we stole the wood for."

"That's not—"

"It is." Ysolde leaned forward. "And here is the truth you already know, Kavanagh, the truth you've known since you first lifted it and told yourself you were preserving it, honoring it, doing the dead woman some service: that memory isn't yours. It never was. It's a wound you press your thumb into every night so you can remember what wanting feels like. And it is costing you. It's costing you the ability to love the child sitting three feet away from you with anything less than terror, because you know what it is to lose a mother and you cannot bear to be one."

Wren made a small sound. Astrid hadn't heard her wake, but she was there, at the edge of the firelight, wrapped in her blanket, watching them both with those catching-wrong eyes.

"Don't," Astrid said to Ysolde, and now her voice did shake. "Don't use her."

"I'm using the only lever that works on you," Ysolde said, without apology. "You'd pay any price for the map except the one that hurts. So I'm naming the one that hurts. That's not cruelty, girl. That's tuition. Everything worth learning costs exactly what you can't afford."

* * *

They walked. Astrid and Wren, away from the fire, down the frozen track until the lean-to was a smear of orange behind them and the mountain rose black against the stars. Wren had insisted — tugging Astrid's sleeve, pointing at the dark, and Astrid understood: whatever she decided, the child refused to be discussed like inventory in her hearing.

The cold bit through her wet coat. Astrid pulled the vial from the inner pocket where it had lived for eleven years, through six cities and two false names and one night in a river with men chasing her, and held it up to the starlight.

It was small. They all were. A thimble of smoked glass stoppered with wax, and inside, a curl of pale luminescence that moved when she moved it — lazy, drowsy, like something sleeping. The memory of a woman kneeling in a kitchen in a house Astrid had never lived in, flour on her hands, laughing at something a five-year-old said. A mother's laugh. Warmth without conditions. The whole, impossible, ordinary miracle of being loved first, before you'd earned it, simply for existing.

Astrid had been eight years old when she found the vial in her mother's — in her *keeper's* — effects after the fever took her. She'd lifted it herself that same night, her first theft, clumsy and half-trained, sealing the wax with tears she pretended were from grief over the keeper and not greed over the treasure. She had replayed it nearly every night since. She knew the kitchen. She knew the flour dust in the lamplight. She knew the sound of that laugh the way she knew her own heartbeat.

And she had never once believed it was hers.

That was the thing Ysolde had seen, the tell, the flinch. Because a good cutter knows provenance the way a jeweler knows stones, and this memory fit Astrid the way a borrowed coat fits — warmly, wrongly, sleeves too long. Some dying woman had sold it or lost it or left it, and a frightened child had claimed it, and twenty years later the claim had grown roots so deep that pulling it out would take half the ground with it.

"If I give it up," Astrid said to the dark, to the mountainside, to the child standing silently beside her, "I won't remember why it mattered. That's the cruelty of it. I'll hand it over and feel — lighter. Relieved. And I'll hate myself for feeling relieved, and I won't remember enough to know why."

Wren took her hand.

The child's palm was cool and rough, a worker's hand, though Wren had never done a day's labor in the shape she currently wore. And through the contact came — not words, Wren's gift didn't work in words — but a pressure, an image, a certainty, laid against Astrid's mind like a leaf pressed in a book:

*A kitchen. Different from the false one. Smaller. Colder. A young woman with dark hair and a stylus scar on her wrist — Astrid's own wrist — teaching a mud-covered child to hold bread in both hands. The child laughing. Astrid's own laugh, rougher than the false mother's, realer.*

*This,* Wren's touch said. *Is also a kitchen. Is also warmth. Is also yours. You are not choosing between having and not having. You are choosing between a coat you stole and hands that hold yours.*

Astrid stood there in the freezing dark and shook.

Not from cold. Or not only. It was the shaking of a structure deciding whether to fall — the long load-bearing grief she had built her whole self around, the nightly ritual, the secret hearth, the nameless woman in the kitchen who had taught Astrid what tenderness looked like precisely because Astrid had never once received it. If she gave it up, she gave up the only proof she carried that love existed anywhere in the world.

Except.

Except there was a child at her side who had learned to say her name in three languages. Except there was an old man in a tollhouse who had paid for their lives with his entire remembered past and called it fair. Except the mountain itself sang to Wren at night, and Wren sang back, and lately — lately — Wren had started humming the melodies to her, wordless, patient, teaching her.

Love existed. She had evidence now. Living, breathing, mud-caked evidence.

The false mother had been scaffolding. And you took scaffolding down when the building could stand on its own.

"Gods damn you, Ysolde," Astrid whispered, and it wasn't entirely a curse.

* * *

Back at the fire, she set the vial on the bench between them, next to the oilcloth map.

"Willingly given," Astrid said. "You said the sigil can tell the difference. Fine. But I want it witnessed." She nodded at Wren. "By her. Whatever she is — fragment, heir, living piece of the binding — I want the mountain itself to see that I paid this debt clean, so that when I stand in the heart of Cael and it asks what I've surrendered, the answer is honest."

Ysolde studied her for a long moment, and something shifted in her face — respect, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "Witnessed," she agreed. "That's more than I'd have thought to ask."

"I'm a professional. We document everything."

Astrid picked up the vial one last time. Held it to the light. Watched the pale curl of the mother's laughter turn lazily, drowsily, unaware.

"Thank you," she said to it — to the woman in the kitchen, whoever she had been, whose grief or desperation or death had put this warmth into the world. "Whoever you were. You taught me the shape of it. That wasn't nothing. That was the whole lesson."

Then she unstopped the wax, tipped the vial over the fire — no. No, not the fire. Into her cupped palm, and from her palm she pressed it flat against the black stone of the hearth behind her, against the mountain's own body, and spoke the old words Ysolde had taught her in Brackwater, the surrender-formula, the ones the brokers used for willing sales:

*"Given, not taken. Freely, not broken. Let it go where it's needed, and let me walk lighter."*

The memory left her like breath leaving lungs. Not pain — worse, a *relief*, an unclenching of something held so long her muscles had forgotten their own tension, and in the space where the nightly ritual had lived there was suddenly room, and the room echoed, and Astrid understood with awful clarity that she had been carrying a stone in her chest for twenty years and had called the weight *love*.

The pale light sank into the stone and was gone.

For a moment she knelt there, empty-handed, hollowed, waiting for the grief to hit. It didn't. What came instead was quieter and stranger: the sense of a door closing softly in a house she no longer lived in. Gratitude, distant and clean, like hearing bells from another village.

"There," Ysolde said softly. "Now you know what the mountain will ask of you at the heart. More of that. Much more. Can you bear it?"

"No," Astrid said honestly, rising, taking up the oilcloth map. "But I've stopped letting 'can I bear it' decide things. It's a bad compass." She cracked the seal and unrolled the map across her knees: fine lines, old ink, gates and wards and descending galleries marked in a script that hurt to look at directly — and there, at the bottom, beneath seven layers of mountain, a single chamber drawn in gold, labeled in the smiths' dead tongue.

Wren leaned over her shoulder. Her breath caught. Her finger touched the golden chamber, and the shard in her other hand flared white-hot, and every dormant sigil within fifty feet of the lean-to lit up along the rock face like veins of dawn breaking underground.

"She says," Wren whispered — her common tongue still rare, still precious, each word placed like a coin on a counter, "the door knows our names now."

Astrid rolled the map closed and stood, and behind them the mountain hummed, awake, waiting.

"Then we shouldn't keep it," she said.
