## 20. The Court of Eaten Things

Aldric had learned to measure time by the smell of tallow.

The Vessel did not sleep, but it permitted the body rest — a few hours each night when the crown's grip loosened and Aldric surfaced like a drowning man breaking water. In those hours he lay in his own bed in his own skin and listened to the candles gutter in the royal chambers of Vaelmoor, and he counted the drips of wax the way other men counted heartbeats. Tallow meant night. Night meant him.

*Tonight,* he thought. *Tonight I am here.*

He did not know how long the thought would last. Sometimes it was minutes. Once — he treasured the memory the way a starving man treasures a crust — once it had been most of an hour, enough to walk to the window, enough to see the moon over Mount Cael, enough to weep without knowing for what. Mostly it was less. The Vessel grew more efficient at noticing when its host stirred, the way a man grows practiced at ignoring the scratching of something small inside a wall.

Aldric lay still beneath the furs and took inventory, as he always did. Hands: present. Name: *Aldric Vane, third of that name, king of Thornfell.* He said it silently, twice, the way one presses a seal into soft wax before the wax cools. Wife: gone eleven years, buried under the rowans at Greyhollow, her name — *Maren, Maren, say it, Maren* — already fraying at the edges like a banner left too long in weather. Daughter: no. There had been no daughter. Why did his chest ache at the shape of the word? Something had been taken from around that absence, scooped out like the flesh of a fruit, and what remained was a hollow shaped exactly like grief with nothing left to grieve.

That was how the Vessel fed. Not by devouring everything — a hollowed man could not rule, could not sign decrees, could not stand at balconies while crowds cheered their king. It ate selectively, precisely, the way a surgeon cuts. It left the scaffolding of a monarch and removed the man inside it, beam by beam, until what stood on the throne of Vaelmoor was a crown wearing a crown.

And it was eating the kingdom now, too.

Aldric felt it through the wards the way a man feels a tooth dying — a deep, sickening looseness, a wrongness in something that had always simply *been*. Three hundred years ago the sigil-smiths had bound the passes of Thornfell with a lattice of light running under the mountains like veins under skin, and every king since had worn the binding crown and kept the seals whole. Aldric had been crowned at nineteen with the iron band cold on his brow, and he remembered — barely, distantly, the memory wrapped in wool — the moment the lattice had recognized him. A warmth behind the eyes. A voice not heard so much as *felt*: *We hold the mountain. You hold us.*

Now the mountain was going dark, vein by vein, and the thing wearing his face sat at his desk each morning signing away the wards one parchment at a time, and the court applauded.

*Get up,* Aldric told himself. *While you have the hour. Get up and write.*

His hands obeyed slowly. That was another cruelty — the body was only partly his even when his mind was present. Muscles moved through honey. But they moved. He crossed the cold floor to the writing desk where the state papers waited in their neat stacks, all of them bearing the Vessel's orders in his own looping hand, and he knelt — a king kneeling on his own floorboards, which was, he supposed, the most honest posture available to him — and drew out the false drawer beneath the inkwell.

Inside lay six letters.

Six nights' work, gathered across six stolen hours. He could not send them; there was no messenger he trusted, because trust required remembering whom you trusted, and the Vessel ate exactly that. Every servant who had shown him kindness had been summoned within a week to the tower room with the sweet-smelling brazier, and had come out glassy-eyed and smiling, and had never again looked at their king with anything warmer than obedience. So Aldric wrote letters he could not send, addressed them to names from history — *To whoever wears this crown after me* — and hid them like seeds in the lining of the palace, in the places only a native son would know: beneath the loose stone in the chapel step, inside the hollow leg of his boyhood reading chair, behind the third tile in the bathhouse where he had hidden sweets from his tutors forty years ago.

This seventh letter he wrote now, by feel, in the dark, the words coming slower than the tallow dripped:

*If you find this, the king is not the king. The thing that wears me is old — older than the crown, older than the smiths who made it. It is unbinding the passes to open the roads for something it calls homecoming. Do not fight it with steel. Steel is nothing to it. Find the blood of the smiths, if any survived the Purge. The crown must be unbound by blood that remembers. I am sorry. I do not know what I have done these past two years. If I signed the death of your village or your family, know that I screamed behind my own eyes the whole while, and will scream until someone frees me or ends me. Either mercy is welcome.*

He hesitated over the signature. His hand wanted to write *Aldric Rex*, the formal seal-hand, but that felt like the throne's hand, the hollow hand. Instead he wrote, small and human: *Aldric. Third of that name. Father of no one, husband of Maren, son of the mountain.*

Then he folded it small, and the question came, as it came every night: *where?*

Where was left? He had seeded the palace like a dying man sowing a field. And then he heard, far below, the sound that told him where — the great doors of the audience hall groaning open, torchlight climbing the stairwell in amber stripes, the shuffling tread of many feet ascending toward the midnight court.

* * *

They convened at midnight because the Vessel loved midnight. Aldric had understood this only gradually, over months of surfacing into the middle of these gatherings, standing rigid among his own courtiers while the thing in his skull presided through his mouth. Midnight was when memories were thinnest, the Vessel had explained once, conversationally, to a chamberlain who smiled and nodded and retained none of it. Midnight was when the day's harvest was freshest.

Aldric descended the private stair into the gallery above the audience hall and looked down upon his court, and his stomach turned over, slow and sick.

There were perhaps sixty of them gathered beneath the great sigil-window — dukes and marshals, the Lord Chancellor, the Mistress of Coin, the captain of the palace guard — and every face wore the same expression. It was not blankness. Blankness would have been merciful. It was *pleasantness*. Sixty people smiling gently in the torchlight, eyes soft as candle-flame, listening with perfect attention to whatever the king said, agreeing with whatever the king proposed, and retaining nothing. They were the finest minds of Vaelmoor run through a sieve until only courtesy remained. Some of them had been brilliant. Chancellor Ovett had once argued the tax codes to a standstill for three days straight; now he nodded along to decrees that emptied his own province's granaries, smiling, smiling. The Marshal of the Eastern Marches had been a hard-bitten woman who'd held the Greymarch against raiders for a decade; she stood now with her helmet under her arm like a good soldier at parade rest, and when the king spoke she said *yes, majesty* in a voice with no woman left inside it.

They were not dead. That was the horror of it. Dead things could be mourned and buried. These were eaten things — shells still warm, still walking, still kissing their children's foreheads in the evening (did they? did the Vessel let them go home?) — and the kingdom bowed to them daily and saw only its government functioning smoothly.

*How many?* Aldric asked the darkness of his own mind, though asking the Vessel questions was like dropping stones down a well and hearing no bottom. *How many have you eaten?*

The answer came the way it always did, patient, indulgent, a teacher humoring a slow pupil:

*Enough. Soon: all.*

Below, the session began. The Vessel did not bother with ceremony anymore — no heralds, no petitions. It stood Aldric's body at the throne-dais and spoke directly, and its voice through his throat was the worst thing of all: still his voice, still the baritone the priests had praised at his coronation, but with a texture underneath like silk drawn over gravel. When it paused between sentences, Aldric could feel the pauses. The Vessel was tasting something. It did that during court sessions — sampled the room. Sixty minds, even hollowed ones, gave off a faint warmth of residual memory, crumbs left after the feast, and the Vessel breathed them in the way an old man breathes the scent of bread he can no longer chew.

"Bring the ward-rolls," it said through Aldric's mouth.

The chamberlain brought them. Aldric watched his own hands take the ledger and watched his own fingers trace down the columns of sealed passes — Coldgate, the Serpent's Stair, the Hollow Ford, Wrenmere — and stop at Wrenmere, and tap twice.

"This one weakens," the Vessel said. "It requires attention."

*No,* Aldric screamed inside the prison of himself, and the scream hit the walls of his skull and slid down them like rain down glass. *Wrenmere has four hundred souls. There are children there. What you call attention —*

But he knew what attention meant. He had seen the aftermath once, early on, before he fully understood, when a ward-village called Ashenfell had gone quiet and the crown had sent no aid and the road there had been closed "for repairs." The Vessel had worn his body to a balcony that evening and breathed in the west wind with something like satisfaction, and Aldric, surfacing briefly inside his own lungs, had caught the taste of it: smoke, yes, but under the smoke, faint as a perfume, *memories*. A village's worth. Harvest songs, grudge-holdings, first kisses behind the mill, the smell of a grandmother's kitchen — all of it airborne, all of it drifting off the burning thatch like fragrance off a struck match, and the Vessel inhaling it, savoring it, a gourmet at a table finally fit for it.

"It requires attention," the Vessel repeated, and Chancellor Ovett smiled and said, "Of course, majesty. Shall I draft the closure order?"

"You shall draft the evacuation order," the Vessel corrected pleasantly. "Publicly. For the eastern floods. They will leave willingly, and the ward-stone will be easier to reach unguarded."

*The floods,* Aldric thought, dizzy with hatred. *There are no floods. There has never been flooding in Wrenmere.* But the order would be drafted, and posted, and believed, because the kingdom trusted its king, and its king was a mouth.

The session moved on. Grain tariffs. A petition about bridge tolls that the Vessel approved absently, its attention elsewhere — Aldric felt it ranging outward, down through the stone of the palace, down into the roots of the mountain, feeling along the darkening lattice the way a man feels along a fence checking for gaps. And then, suddenly, it went very still.

Stillness, in the Vessel, was terror. Aldric had learned that too. When the thing inside him went motionless — when even its endless low humming in the base of his skull ceased — it meant it had found something it did not understand.

*A fragment,* it said, to itself, to the dark, forgetting or not caring that its host could hear. *The lattice has produced a fragment. Small. Alive. Walking in the world.*

Aldric's heart — his true heart, the one the Vessel let beat because bodies needed hearts — slammed once, hard. A fragment. The word landed in him and rang like a bell. He did not know why. He should not have known why. But somewhere in what remained of him, in some cellar of memory the Vessel had not yet gotten down to, a door rattled in its frame.

*Find it,* the Vessel said aloud, to the room, and sixty pleasant faces turned toward their king with love and emptiness. "Serane."

From the shadow at the hall's edge stepped a figure Aldric's surface-mind knew and his depths feared: Inquisitor Serane Voss, grey-cloaked, precise, her face composed into courteous attention. She alone of the crown servants did not smile. The Vessel kept her hungry rather than full — Aldric had pieced this together from fragments of overheard report — because hunger made her sharp, and fullness made the others soft. She was the knife that still had an edge.

"Majesty," she said.

"There is a child. Perhaps ten years. Dark-haired. Last seen in Greyhollow, moving south with a woman — a memory-cutter, by the trade-signs. Bring me the child alive and untouched. Its mind is not food. It is a key." A pause, and then the silk-over-gravel voice dropped lower, almost tender, which was somehow worse than all its cruelty: "Fail me in this, Serane, and I will eat your name. Do you understand? Not your life. Your *name*. You will remain, and you will serve, and no one — least of all you — will recall what to call you."

Something flickered across Serane Voss's face. Aldric, watching from his gallery prison, catalogued it hungrily: fear, yes, but under the fear something harder. Something that bent toward the threat rather than away from it, the way a blade bends toward the whetstone. He had seen that look once before, decades ago, on the face of a young officer who volunteered for a suicide rearguard action — not because she wished to die, but because she wished *to be aimed at something*, and the aiming was the only meaning left to her.

"I understand, majesty," Serane said. "I will bring you the key."

She bowed and withdrew, and the court session dissolved, sixty eaten courtiers drifting out into the corridors to sleep dreamlessly beside spouses who kissed them and were not kissed back. The Vessel ascended the stair with Aldric's legs, and Aldric rode inside his own skull, screaming silently, and the scream was becoming a familiar country, and that frightened him more than the screaming did. Even rage wore smooth, given enough repetition. Even prisoners grew fond of their cell walls. *Remember your name,* he told himself as the bedroom door closed. *Aldric Vane, third of that name. Husband of Maren. Son of the mountain. Remember, remember, remember.*

And then, in the dark of the royal chambers, he heard the door below open quietly, and soft feet on the stair.

* * *

She was the undermaid who brought the morning firewood — Liss, her name was, Liss or Lisbet, he had known it once. Seventeen perhaps, freckled, quick-eyed, the sort of servant who noticed things, which in this palace was either a death sentence or a salvation and often both. She knelt at the hearth arranging kindling, and Aldric lay in the bed feigning the Vessel's rest, and he watched her sidelong through his lashes and made the oldest wager a prisoner can make: *her, or no one.*

He could see it in her, if he looked honestly. The Vessel's sieve missed small souls sometimes — it went for the powerful, the memorable, the minds worth eating — and small watchful souls slipped through the mesh. Liss still flinched when the hollowed courtiers smiled too long. Liss still crossed herself, old-fashionedly, when she passed the tower room with the sweet-smelling brazier. Liss had cried once, quietly, wringing out a mop, and the crying had sounded real.

When she rose to go, he acted. It cost him everything he had. Moving his arm was like lifting a fallen roof-beam; moving his voice was worse. But the Vessel was distant tonight, stretched thin along the mountain's roots, feeling for its fragment, and the hour was the deepest hour, and Aldric Vane, third of that name, spent the last coins of his strength on three whispered words:

"The hearthstone. Girl."

Liss froze. Slowly she turned. Her eyes went wide in the firelight, and Aldric saw the calculation move behind them like a fish under ice — *the king spoke, the king is ill, the king is mad, the king will have me whipped, the king will have me eaten* — and he watched her choose. He watched her look at the door, and at him, and at the door again, and then cross the floor and kneel beside the hearth as if tending the fire, which was, after all, her work, and no one watching would think twice.

"Under the hearthstone, third from the left," Aldric breathed, barely louder than the settling embers. "There is a letter. Take it. Read it. Then decide with your own mind — your own, girl, the one thing in this palace they haven't eaten yet — whether you believe it. If you do: the maid Corrin in the west wing, and the gatekeeper Bram. Tell no one else. Trust no one who smiles too easily. And if anyone asks—" his voice cracked, spent, "—if anyone ever asks who told you: tell them a dead man told you. It will be truer than they'll ever know."

For a long moment Liss knelt motionless, firelight moving on her face. Then she lifted the hearthstone, third from the left — she had seen him glance at it, clever girl, clever, clever — and her fingers found the folded paper in the soot-dark cavity beneath, and she palmed it into her apron in one smooth motion that no one had taught her, that she had simply *known* how to do, the way the small and the overlooked always know how to hide things.

"Yes, majesty," she said aloud, for the benefit of any listening ears, in a perfectly flat servant's voice. And then, so quietly he nearly missed it, as she gathered her kindling basket and moved to the door: "My brother was at Ashenfell."

The door closed behind her. Aldric lay in the dark and shook — not with fear, for once, but with the strange violent trembling of a man who has been cold for two years and has just felt, for one instant, the sun.

*Ashenfell.* Her brother had burned with that village, and the crown had sent no aid, and this girl had gone on carrying wood and lighting fires in the murderer's house with her grief folded up small in her apron next to the kindling. That was the kind of soul the sieve missed. That was the kind of soul that, given one letter and one grievance, became a coal that could light a country.

Somewhere far below, down in the roots of the world, the Vessel hummed on, feeling for its fragment, counting its wards as they dimmed. It believed the palace was a larder. It believed the court was a cupboard of eaten things, and the servants were mice, and the king was a coat it wore.

It had forgotten — or had never learned, being old and arrogant and very hungry — that larders can be emptied from the inside. That mice remember where the holes are. That coats, sometimes, in the deep of night, remember whose shoulders they were cut for.

Aldric closed his eyes. Above the sleeping palace the stars wheeled over Mount Cael, where the lattice lay darkening in its thousand-mile sleep, and somewhere south of Greyhollow — he could not know this, and yet he knew it, the way the mountain knew things through him — a mud-streaked child walked beside a memory-cutter with a shard of black glass in her fist, and the wards ahead of them glowed faintly as they passed, like banked fires remembering breath.

The clock ran. Wrenmere would burn within the fortnight unless the key turned. Somewhere in the palace a girl with ash-grey grief in her apron was reading a dead man's letter by shielded candlelight, deciding.

Aldric slept — truly slept, for the first time in months — and dreamed of rowan trees, and a woman's voice saying his name softly, the way you speak to someone you are trying to wake.
