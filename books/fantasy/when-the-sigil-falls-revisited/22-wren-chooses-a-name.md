## 22. Wren Chooses a Name

The archive breathed.

Wren had learned in the weeks beneath the mountain that everything down here did — the walls drew slow lungfuls of air through cracks older than kingdoms, and when the sigils along the stone woke at her passing, they exhaled a warmth that smelled of hot iron and rain on slate. It was not frightening anymore. It had been, once, in the first days after she'd tumbled out of the storm and into Astrid's attic, small and mud-caked and unable to say a single word anyone could understand. Then the mountain's voice had been the only voice she had, vast and lightless and patient as geology, and it had spoken a name into her that was not a name at all but an address, a coordinate, a *where* instead of a *who*.

*Fragment Seven of the Ninefold Binding. Thread-of-the-Lattice. Keystone-That-Was-Scattered.*

She had carried those designations the way a snail carries its shell: without choosing them, without being able to feel their weight until someone tried to take it off.

Tonight, someone was trying.

The hall where the dead smiths kept their records opened off the main gallery like a throat, long and low-ceilinged, its shelves cut directly from the living rock. Not books — Astrid had been disappointed by that, Wren remembered, and had hidden the disappointment badly behind a scholar's frown — but tablets of fused glass, each one holding a smith's work-journal pressed into it while still molten, so that reading meant touching, and touching meant remembering. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The light of the awakened sigils ran along their edges like dawn caught in frost.

Ysolde stood at the center of the room, and Ysolde was the one asking.

Wren did not trust Ysolde. She was fairly sure Astrid didn't either, though Astrid trusted even less the alternative of walking blind through the deep roads with Serane Voss somewhere behind them and the crown's hunger somewhere ahead. But there was this to be said for the memory-broker: she knew things. And tonight what she knew was standing between Wren and the door, hands folded, face arranged into the particular gentleness that people put on for children and for funerals.

"You should understand what you are," Ysolde said. "Before you go further down. Before you stand in front of the heart itself."

Wren folded her arms. She was eleven now, or she had decided to be; birthdays were one of the many things she had invented for herself since leaving the lattice, and she clung to her inventions fiercely. "I know what I am."

"Do you?" Ysolde crouched, which put them nearly eye to eye, which Wren understood was a technique. "You told us the mountain sings to you. That the sigils answer your hand. Child, you are not a girl who can hear the binding. You *are* the binding — a piece of it, given legs. Given breath." She reached into her coat and drew out a tablet, older than any of the others, black glass gone smoky with age, and set it on the shelf beside her so its surface faced outward. "Here. This is the Founding Record. The day the Ninefold was made. Would you like to see your true name?"

Wren's heart did something complicated — a stumble, a lurch, a falling-toward, the way water leans toward a drain. Because part of her wanted it. That was the terrible thing, the thing she hadn't told anyone, not even Astrid. Part of her had spent every night down here straining toward that old coordinate-name like a lost dog toward a whistle. The mountain missed her. The lattice missed her. Somewhere under all this stone was a shape with her edges cut around it, a socket that fit her exactly, and the longing to slot home was so old and so vast that it didn't feel like a feeling. It felt like gravity.

"I already have a true name," she said.

"It's borrowed," Ysolde said gently. "Kavanagh gave it to you from a bird, I heard. Pretty, but thin. A foundling's tag. Your real one is in here—" she tapped the tablet, "—and it has power in it. Names do, down here especially. If you're going to stand at the heart and rebind what must be rebound, you'll need every gram of yourself. You'll need the name the smiths forged *into* you."

The hall was quiet except for the breathing of the walls and, far above, the muffled percussion of the storm that had followed them down from the wayshrine. Water found its way through a hundred hairline cracks and ticked onto stone somewhere behind the shelves.

Wren looked at the tablet. From this angle, with the sigil-light raking across it, she could almost make out the pressed script beneath the smoke, row after row of the old angular letters, and she could feel them the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue — right there, right *there*, one touch away from knowing exactly what she had been before she was small.

She thought about the socket. About fitting.

And then she thought about an attic in Greyhollow, and rain coming down like the sky had given up pretending, and a knock she didn't remember making because she hadn't known yet how to knock, only how to need. A door opening a crack. A woman with careful eyes and ink on her fingers looking down at her, and Wren had seen the exact moment the woman decided not to be careful — seen it happen, the soft collapse of a closed life, like ice going out of a river.

"Wren," Astrid had said that night, wrapping a blanket around shoulders that wouldn't stop shaking. "It's a marsh bird. Ugly-tempered, refuses to leave when the weather turns. Seems about right for something that showed up soaked on my doorstep." And she had smiled — a real one, rare as honest coin in Greyhollow — and Wren, who had never had anything, had held the sound of that name in her chest like a coal.

Nobody had forged that name into her. Someone had *given* it. Out loud. In a language people still spoke. On a night when giving cost something.

"No," Wren said.

Ysolde blinked. "Child—"

"No." Louder now, and the hall heard her; three sigils along the nearest shelf flared amber and settled, attentive. "That's not my name. It's my address." She pointed at the tablet, and her finger shook, and she let it shake. "That says where I was. It doesn't say who I am. Who I am is somebody Astrid Kavanagh opened her door for when every smart bone in her body said don't. You want me to trade that for a coordinate?"

"Power isn't sentiment," Ysolde said. "At the heart, sentiment may be the first thing the binding devours."

"Then it can choke on me." Wren stepped forward and put her palm flat against the Founding Record anyway — not to read it, but to close it, pressing the old script into darkness under her hand. The glass was warm. Underneath her palm she felt the faint pulse of the designation trying to rise to meet her skin, the lattice reaching for its fragment, and she felt, too, how easy it would be. One relaxation. One breath of surrender and she would know everything she used to be, would flood back into the shape carved for her, would become Thread-of-the-Lattice again, enormous and purposeful and utterly without a single person in the world who had chosen her over every smarter instinct.

She lifted her hand away.

"When I was the lattice," she said, and her voice came out low and steady, grown-up, the voice she'd found in the alley in Brackwater when soldiers cornered them and she'd spoken her first common words and unmade nine men's last hour, "I was everything and I belonged nowhere. Do you know what that's like? Being load-bearing? Every thread pulled tight and none of them mine? I held up a kingdom and no one ever asked if I wanted to hold anything." Her eyes stung. She let them. "Astrid asked. First thing she ever asked me, after the storm. Not *what are you*, not *whose are you*. She said, 'Are you hungry?'"

Behind Ysolde, at the mouth of the hall, there was a scuff of boot on stone.

Wren turned, and there was Astrid, leaning against the archway with her arms crossed and her mother's old coat hanging off her shoulders, and Wren realized with a flush how much of that she must have heard. There was an expression on Astrid's face that Wren had catalogued carefully over these months, because Astrid handed out expressions like a miser handed out coins: this one was the one she wore when something had reached inside her chest and squeezed, and she was deciding whether to allow it.

"Hungry now, actually," Astrid said, as if no time had passed at all, as if it were still that same attic and that same storm. "Both of us. Come on. Ferro's got the fire going and he's threatening to cook, which means we intervene immediately."

Ysolde rose slowly, smoothing her coat. For a moment her broker's mask slipped, and underneath it Wren saw something raw and hungry and grieving — the face of a woman who had sold pieces of herself over the years and knew exactly the market price of a name. "The offer stands," Ysolde said quietly. "The record keeps. When the Vessel's teeth are at your throat and you need every scrap of what you are, child, you may wish you'd taken the whole of yourself back."

"I'll have the whole of myself," Wren said. "That's the point."

* * *

Ferro's fire was a smuggler's fire, built in a stone bowl designed to throw its light upward and none outward, so that the whole gallery ceiling became a shifting amber sky and the shadows of the sigils swam across it like fish. He had, despite threats, cooked: some root vegetable Wren couldn't name, fried with salt-cured fat from a wax paper packet he guarded like scripture, and hard bread toasted on a flat stone, and tea brewed from pine needles that tasted like drinking a forest's apology.

Astrid sat with her shoulder against Wren's shoulder. She had been doing that more since the letter — since finding the warning stitched into the lining of her mother's coat, *the crown must be unbound by blood that remembers*, since learning in this very archive that the blood in question was hers. Wren thought the touching was less comfort than counting. Astrid was taking inventory of what she had left, the way she inventoried her vials, and Wren was on the list, and Wren intended to stay on it.

"Eat," Astrid said. "You argued with a memory-broker for ten minutes. That burns fuel."

"She started it."

"Mm. And you finished it." Astrid turned her cup slowly on her knee. Firelight moved in her eyes. "I've been thinking about names."

"Ferro calls himself the Tollhouse of Small Mercies," Ferro offered from across the coals. "Named it myself. Named myself after it, really. Some of us build ourselves out of signs on doors."

"Some of us get built whether we like it or not," Astrid said. Her thumb moved to the ring on her right hand — her mother's ring, too big for her, worn on a cord lately because it kept slipping. She didn't seem to notice she was doing it. Wren noticed. Wren noticed everything about Astrid Kavanagh; it was the first skill she'd taught herself in the world above the stone. "My mother named me for her grandmother, who I'm told was a horror. I never got to ask her if she regretted it. Whether she looked at baby-me and thought, *well, she'll grow into it or grow around it.*" A pause. Steam curled off her tea. "Then I find out the name Kavanagh comes with a forge attached. A duty. A dead order and a live war. And I've spent two weeks furious about it — inheriting a chain and being told it's a necklace." She looked sideways at Wren. "And then I watch an eleven-year-old tell Ysolde where she can put her Founding Record."

"You heard that."

"All of it. Especially the hungry part." Astrid's mouth did the almost-smile. "For the record: you were extremely hungry that night. You ate four of my biscuits and cried into the fifth."

"They were good biscuits."

"They were terrible biscuits. I'd bought them from a man in Greyhollow who I'm fairly sure baked them out of spite." Astrid set the tea down and turned to face her fully, cross-legged, knees nearly touching Wren's knee, and Wren understood that something formal was happening, that Astrid was building up to a sentence she'd rehearsed, because Astrid rehearsed sentences the way other people sharpened knives — privately, obsessively, and only when she meant to use them. "Wren. If you want a different name — a truer one, whatever that means — you don't take it from a tablet. You choose it. Or you keep the one you've got. Either way it should be yours, not theirs, not mine, not the mountain's. I just—" and here the rehearsal visibly failed her, and she said the unrehearsed thing, fast and rough, "—I'd be lying if I said I didn't hope you'd keep it. The one I gave you. It's the only thing I ever made out of nothing, and I'm selfish about my work."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark of the gallery, water ticked on stone, keeping its own patient time.

Wren found that her throat had gone tight, which annoyed her, and she let the annoyance carry her through it. "I'm keeping it," she said. "Obviously."

"Obviously."

"But I want it to be obvious *on purpose*." She sat up straight, the way she imagined the smiths sitting at their anvils, except they'd probably never had to say anything this hard, they just hammered metal and let the metal do the talking. "Not a foundling tag. Not a bird joke. Mine. Chosen. So — I'm telling you, formally, the way Ysolde wanted me to tell the tablet. My name is Wren. Wren Kavanagh, if that's — if the offer—"

"Yes," Astrid said, before she'd even finished, fast enough that it cracked something open in her voice, and then, recovering, gruff, "if you're taking my name you're doing chores. That's the law."

"There's no law."

"There absolutely is. Article one: vials get washed."

Wren laughed — really laughed, the kind that came up from the place the lattice used to occupy, filling the empty socket with something warmer than purpose — and Ferro raised his tin mug in solemn benediction, and the sigils along the ceiling rippled gold, and for one whole minute the deep road under the world felt like a kitchen.

Then Astrid grew serious again, and touched Wren's chin, tilting her face up the way she did when she was checking her for injuries and pretending she wasn't. "You understand why Ysolde pushed, though. Down at the heart, the binding will call you by what you were. It won't care about paperwork."

"Then it can learn," Wren said. "Everything down here learns. That's all a sigil is — something that remembers on purpose." She took a breath. "When it calls me Fragment Seven, I'll answer *here*, and correct it. Politely the first time."

Astrid's laugh was short and startled and real. "And the second time?"

"The second time I'll do what you taught me." Wren grinned, and it felt strange and new on her face, a muscle still in training. "Lie beautifully, and steal something while it's listening."

* * *

Sleep came late and shallow in the archive, as it always did now. Wren lay on her bedroll watching the drowned-gold light move on the ceiling and listened to the mountain murmur to itself in the old tongue, and for the first time since she'd fallen out of the lattice, she answered idly, lazily, the way you chat with an old relative who talks mostly to hear themselves — *yes, still here; no, still small; yes, still hers.*

The mountain did not sulk, exactly. Mountains are incapable of sulking. But it went quiet in a way that had weight, and in the quiet Wren felt the vast shape of it turning something over, slow as sediment, and then — distantly, from far below, from the direction of the buried heart — something *else* stirred.

Not the mountain. Not the lattice. Something older and hungrier and sadder, something that had been listening to her refusal, the way a starving thing listens to a table being set for someone else.

It did not speak in words. It spoke the way the storm had spoken outside the attic, pressure and omen. But she understood it anyway, and the understanding landed in her stomach like swallowed ice:

*I had a name too, once. They took it and hammered it into iron. Will you weep for me, little fragment, when yours costs the same?*

Wren sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and looked across the dying fire to where Astrid slept with one hand curled near her mother's ring on its cord, guarding even in sleep the small round proofs of who she was.

Tomorrow they walked into the heart of the mountain. Tonight, for the first time, Wren understood that something down there was waiting for them — and that it knew her name, both of them, the forged one and the chosen one.

And that it had decided, in its long hunger, that it preferred the first.
