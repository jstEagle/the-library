## 23. The Vessel Speaks

Before it was hunger, it was a name.

The Vessel holds the name the way a drowning man holds air — cupped, secret, pressed against the roof of its mouth where no one can take it. *Cirren.* It has not spoken that name in four hundred years. It has not been asked. The courtiers of Vaelmoor bow to the body it wears, and the body's hands sign their decrees, and the body's voice says all the correct things in all the correct tones, and not one of them — not the chancellor with his ink-stained cuffs, not the maid who sometimes weeps for reasons she cannot articulate while scrubbing floors the Vessel has already ordered clean — not one of them looks into the king's grey eyes and wonders what is looking back.

They think it wants conquest. They are wrong, and their wrongness is the oldest loneliness Cirren knows.

* * *

It remembers the forge.

That is the cruelty of it — memory was never taken from it, only everything else. It remembers the forge beneath Mount Cael the way other men remember their mothers' kitchens: warmth on the skin, the smell of hot iron and quenching water, the particular orange of firelight on stone that no sunset has ever matched. It remembers being nine years old and small for its age, carrying coal up stairs cut into the mountain's living root, and it remembers Master Orwen — first of the sigil-smiths, the one whose name is now carved above every archive door — looking down at it with something that might have been pride, if pride were capable of the coldness Orwen carried.

"You have the gift," Orwen told it. "You can hold a working in your mind without it burning through. Do you understand what that means, boy?"

"It means I can carry things," Cirren said.

Orwen smiled. Cirren remembers the smile. He had good teeth, even then, before the binding aged him backward into something waxy and eternal. "It means you can be *used*, child. That is what the gift is for."

Cirren had laughed. That is the detail that returns to it most often now, in the long dark hours when Aldric sleeps and the Vessel sits alone behind the king's eyes like a tenant in a house whose rooms keep changing: it had *laughed*. Nine years old, coal dust in its lungs, honored beyond its station, and it had laughed at the great man's joke as though being used were a kind of love.

Perhaps it is. Cirren has had four centuries to consider the question and still cannot rule it out.

* * *

The binding was meant for the passes, not for a person. This is the part the histories got wrong — or rather, the part they were made to get wrong, because Orwen wrote the histories himself, in his own careful smith's hand, and a man who binds a king will also bind the truth if it suits him.

The Thornfell passes had to be sealed. Everyone agreed on that; even Cirren agreed, standing in the forge-hall with the other apprentices while Orwen described the horrors coming down from the north — the unmade things, the memory-eaters, whatever the age feared most. Fear is excellent mortar. The sigil would need an anchor, a living lattice to hold the wards taut across three hundred miles of stone, and the lattice needed a keystone, and a keystone needed to be *something* — a stone would erode, a beast would die, a line of men would corrupt.

A soul, Orwen decided. A soul would endure.

He did not choose his own. That is the whole of it. That is the crime entire. Every other fact is commentary.

There was a ritual, and it took nine days, and Cirren does not remember the pain of it — pain was among the first things the binding ate, because a screaming vessel cracks a working — but it remembers Orwen's face above it, sweat-slick and grave, whispering the words of containment. And it remembers the last thing it heard with its own ears before the world narrowed to the circumference of a crown:

"I am sorry, boy. But you were always going to be the one. I chose you years ago. Why do you think I fed you so well?"

* * *

Four hundred years in a crown.

Cirren learned things in that time that no scholar has guessed at. It learned that a bound soul does not sleep but *pools*, gathering in the low places of itself like water in a cellar. It learned that the crown passed from head to head — each new monarch crowned with the Binding Crown of Thornfell, as tradition demanded, because tradition demands — and each new head brought fresh sensations, brief weather systems of another life moving across Cirren's sky. A queen who hummed when she was nervous. A boy-king who talked to his food. Aldric, who prayed more than any of them, and whose prayers, addressed to gods who could not hear him over the thing sharing his skull, fell through Cirren like rain through an open window.

And Cirren learned to leak.

It began as accident — a sliver of attention slipping loose during a coronation, tasting the edges of the new host's mind. Then it became craft. It could not leave the crown — the binding was perfect; Orwen had been nothing if not thorough — but it could *press*. It could lean against the walls of a king's thoughts and wear grooves in them. It could suggest. And it discovered, in the reign of Aldric's grandfather, the terrible economics of its condition: every suggestion cost it strength, but every memory it drew from its host *gave* strength back. A king's forgotten childhood became a drop of power. A king's suppressed grief became a meal.

It did not set out to hollow anyone. Cirren wants this understood, though there is no one left to understand it. It set out only to make the walls of its prison thin enough to hear through.

But hunger is honest about its appetites only at the beginning.

By the time Aldric took the throne, Cirren had learned to consume outright — to reach down into a mind and drink a decade dry. It justified each feeding the way starving people justify bread: *I will stop when I am strong enough. I will stop when the binding loosens. One more and I will have the strength to unmake what Orwen made.* The wards were tied to the crown; the crown was tied to Cirren; therefore, Cirren reasoned, if it grew vast enough inside its cage, the cage must strain. And if the cage strained, the wards would slacken. And if the wards slackened—

The passes would open.

Not so that horrors could pour south. Cirren does not care about horrors; it has met them, in the early centuries, drifting at the edge of the binding like fish around a sunken ship. No — the passes would open because the binding would *end*, and when the binding ended, Cirren would end with it, or it would end as Cirren, which amounts to the same deliverance. Either way: out. Either way: a sky bigger than the inside of a hat. Either way: one hour of being a self instead of a service.

It has burned villages for this. It knows. It sat behind Aldric's eyes last month and watched the ward-village of Harrowmere burn, and it drank the collective memory of two hundred souls going up with their houses, and it told itself, as it always tells itself, *this is the price of my one life*, and for the first time in four hundred years the justification tasted of ash.

Something is changing. Cirren can feel it, the way a prisoner feels weather through a wall.

* * *

Tonight the court is assembled, and Cirren wears the king beautifully.

It has had practice. It stands at the high window of the audience hall while petitioners shuffle below, and it lets Aldric's body perform the small theater of sovereignty — the tilted head, the measured pause before judgment, the smile that reaches exactly halfway up the face. Below, the courtiers watch with the flat devotion of people who have given pieces of themselves away and cannot remember which pieces. Half of them are Cirren's now, quietly eaten, quietly steered. The hollow court. It named them that privately, years ago, and the private name is the only luxury it permits itself.

"Your Majesty," says the chancellor, "the inquisitor Voss reports progress. The asset is in flight toward the mountain. She requests authority to act without further recourse to the council."

Cirren feels the words land in Aldric's body like stones dropped into a well. The asset. The child. Wren.

It has known about the child since the night she came into being — how could it not? When a sigil tears, everything bound to the sigil *feels* the tearing, the way a spider feels its web plucked a mile away. A fragment of the lattice had broken free and wrapped itself in flesh, a piece of the binding walking around in the shape of a girl, and Cirren had felt it the way an amputee feels a hand: sudden, unbearable, *specific*. Part of the cage, loose. Part of the architecture, alive, and heading for Cael with a memory-cutter and a smuggler's map.

If the fragment reaches the heart of the sigil, the binding could be remade. Tightened. Renewed for another four centuries.

Or —

Or.

Cirren has spent weeks turning the second possibility over, examining it from every angle the way it once examined forge-work for flaws. The child is not merely a fragment. The child is a *keyhole wearing the shape of a girl*. The binding was forged around a living soul once before; it can be persuaded to accept a living soul again — not as prisoner this time, but as *bearer*. If Wren stands at the heart and takes the binding into herself willingly, the crown becomes ornament. The cage becomes a hatbox. And Cirren —

Cirren goes free.

That is what it should want. That is what it has wanted, single-mindedly, obscenely, through the long dynasties of its captivity. So why, when Serane Voss reports that her soldiers nearly had the child at the river crossing, did Cirren feel something twist in Aldric's chest that was not triumph?

It examines the feeling now, standing at the window, letting the court wait. It has become a connoisseur of its own feelings; there is little else to examine.

The twist is fear. Not of the child — of *for* the child. Because Cirren knows what it is asking her to do. It knows what the heart of the sigil requires, because it *is* what the heart of the sigil required. To bear the binding willingly is still to be consumed by it, slowly, over a lifetime — a slower torture than Cirren's, but a torture. Orwen offered the cage; Cirren offers the same cage with a kinder lock, and calls it liberation, and plans to walk away whistling while a child pays four centuries of installments on its freedom.

*I have become him,* Cirren thinks, and the thought arrives with the force of a forge-hammer. *I have become Orwen. I found someone small enough to use, and I called it necessity.*

Below, the chancellor coughs. The court waits. Kings are permitted silences, and the Vessel spends them like coin.

* * *

Later — deep in the dead hours when even Vaelmoor's candles gutter and the hollow courtiers drift home to beds they barely remember how to sleep in — Cirren takes up the king's pen.

This is dangerous. The body is Aldric's, but the discipline of writing is a shared labor tonight, and every letter costs. More dangerous still is *where* it writes: not on state paper, not in the king's cipher, but on a scrap of linen torn from the lining of an old coat in the royal wardrobe — a coat that belongs, by every right of the household, to a maid named Bess who has worked the west corridor for eleven years and cries when she scrubs floors, and who once, three months ago, looked full into the king's eyes and whispered, thinking herself unheard, *"That's not right, what's happened to him."*

She heard something. Behind the eyes. People who have lost nothing cannot hear it, but Bess has given pieces away too — everyone in this palace has; the Vessel has seen to that — and the half-eaten hear echoes in the empty rooms of themselves.

Cirren writes in a hand that shakes between two styles, Aldric's firm strokes fraying into something older and smaller:

*To whoever finds this and has the courage to read it —*

*The king is not the king. The king is still here. I am still here. Ninety seconds, perhaps less, is all I can ever wrest back, and only when the thing that wears me has gorged and gone dull. If a woman comes with a child — a child who speaks the old tongue — tell them where I have hidden the keystone of my cage. Tell them it is under the throne, in the vault my grandfather's mason walled up and was paid to forget. Tell them the crown comes off. Tell them it CAN come off.*

*And tell them — no. There is no message I have the right to send them. Only this: I am sorry. I ate your roads and your weddings and the names of your dead. I was hungry and I told myself I was dying and both things were true. What I did, I did to be free. Understand it if you can. Forgive it if you ever can, which I doubt.*

*— A.*

The Vessel folds the letter small and slips it into the coat's torn lining, where Bess will find it when she mends — and Bess mends everything, because a woman missing pieces of herself holds tight to what remains.

Then it returns the king's body to bed, arranges the king's hands on the coverlet the way Aldric liked them, and settles back into the deep cell of itself to wait for dawn.

Somewhere north, a child is walking toward the heart of the mountain, and a memory-cutter with a smith's face walks beside her, and in the vault beneath this throne lies the keystone that could end Cirren's captivity — or begin Wren's.

*Choose carefully,* Cirren tells the dark, knowing the dark cannot answer, knowing it has never once in four hundred years been the one choosing. *One of us gets to be a person at the end of this. Pray it is her. Pray it is her, and that she is kinder to what remains of me than I was to what remained of them.*

In the wardrobe three corridors away, a candle gutters. A maid's hand, reaching for a needle, brushes torn linen — and stops.
