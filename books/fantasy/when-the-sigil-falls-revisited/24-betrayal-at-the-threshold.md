## 24. Betrayal at the Threshold

Astrid had learned long ago that betrayal rarely announced itself with drawn steel or shouting in the night. It arrived the way rot arrived in a house's timbers — quietly, from within, wearing the face of something already trusted.

She thought of that later, in the hours after, when she had time enough to take the thing apart and examine its workings like a stylus lifted from a wound. But in the moment itself, in the lamplight of Ysolde's back room in Brackwater, all she felt was the particular cold that comes when a door you thought was locked swings open on darkness.

They had come back to Ysolde because there was nowhere else to go.

The road beneath the road had ended three days ago, spitting them out through a collapsed smuggler's arch into the reed flats south of Brackwater, and since then they had been living on dried fish and fear. Wren's sleep had grown troubled — she spoke in the dead tongue now even in her dreams, a low litany of syllables that made the lamp flames lean toward her as if listening — and twice Astrid had woken to find the girl sitting upright in the dark, one hand pressed flat against the floorboards, listening to something far below them that Astrid could not hear.

"She says the sigil is fraying," Astrid told Ysolde, in the back room that smelled of tallow and old paper and the copper tang of memory-vials racked in their hundreds along the walls. "She says it's like hearing a rope unwind, strand by strand. She can feel where the next break will come, but not how to mend it, and I—" Astrid stopped, heard how thin her voice had gone, and started again. "I need the rest of my mother's journals. The ones you said existed. The ones you wouldn't sell me before."

Ysolde sat behind her ledger desk the way a spider sits at the center of a web it has no intention of leaving: still, patient, her hands folded over a ledger whose pages were written in three colors of ink. She was a broad woman, gray-streaked, with the heavy forearms of someone who had once done coarser work than brokering, and her face had the settled calm of a millpond — nothing moved on it that she did not permit to move.

"Journals are dangerous goods," Ysolde said. "Purge-sealed materials. You understand my position."

"I understand your position is whatever price you decide it is."

Something flickered at the corner of the broker's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of respect that passes between people who work the same trade. "Your mother's technique lives in your hands, girl. I watched you cut a memory in the market square and I knew the Kavanagh grip before I knew your face. There aren't five people alive who hold a stylus that way." She unfolded her hands. "So yes. The journals exist. And yes, I will give them to you."

Astrid let out a breath she had been holding for three days. Beside her, Wren looked up from the vial rack she'd been studying — the child was endlessly fascinated by other people's bottled lives, though she never asked to taste them — and Ysolde's eyes followed the girl, and stayed on her a moment too long, and Astrid should have understood everything in that instant. Later, she would curse herself for missing it. But grief makes us slow, and hope makes us slower, and Astrid was carrying both like stones sewn into her coat.

"What's the price?" she asked.

"For the journals?" Ysolde opened the ledger. "Nothing. Consider them paid for by what you're about to do for me."

* * *

The thing Ysolde wanted did not seem, at first, like a payment at all. It seemed like a kindness, which was the first wrong note, though again Astrid did not hear it. Kindness always costs; Ferro had taught her that on the tollhouse road, and she had agreed, and she had still walked straight past the lesson when it came back around wearing a softer face.

"There is a vault," Ysolde said, "in the undercellar of this house, warded with smith-work older than anything you've ever touched. In it I keep memories that no one should own and no one should lose. Fragments of the Purge. Testimony from the last days of the sigil-smiths, carried out by people who died getting it here." Her thumb moved across the ledger without reading it, a blind habitual motion. "And among them, one vial I have owned for twenty-two years and never sold, though I have been offered sums that would buy this whole district."

Whose? Astrid almost asked. But she saw the answer forming in the pond-still face before the words came, saw it rise like a shape under clear water, and she knew.

"Your daughter," Astrid said quietly.

The millpond broke. Only for a heartbeat — a single ripple crossing Ysolde's face, mouth tightening, eyes going somewhere twenty-two years distant — and then the stillness returned, and Astrid understood that the stillness itself was the monument. Twenty-two years of practicing not feeling it.

"Mira," Ysolde said, and the name came out worn smooth from handling. "She was taken in the second wave of the Purge, when she was nine. Not killed — worse. The crown's memory-eaters consumed her. All of her. They left a body breathing and nothing behind the eyes, and they put that body in an asylum in Vaelmoor where it sat for eleven years until it caught a fever and stopped breathing altogether." Ysolde's hands were perfectly steady on the ledger. That steadiness must have cost more than any trembling would have. "But consumption leaves leavings. The eaters were sloppy in those early years — they took the bulk of a mind and discarded the dregs, the fragments too small to be worth the trouble of swallowing. A first word. A scraped knee. A lullaby sung badly. Someone gathered those leavings, that year, and sold them on the grey market, and I spent every coin I had and several I didn't have buying back every fragment of my daughter that surfaced. Fourteen fragments, over six years. I have them still. Sealed. Untasted."

"You've never listened to them," Astrid said. It wasn't a question. She knew the shape of that refusal; she wore its twin every night when she chose, again, not to open her eyes during the false mother's memory, keeping it intact and unexamined like a letter never unsealed.

"Listening spends them," Ysolde said. "You know this better than anyone alive. A memory tasted begins to fade. A memory tasted often dies. I have kept my daughter's scraps sealed for two decades so that *something* of her would remain in the world, whole, untouched." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible in their patience. "And now I am going to ask you to help me spend them. Every one."

Astrid sat back slowly. "Why now? Why would you—"

"Because of the child." Ysolde's gaze went to Wren again, and this time Astrid watched the look and understood it: hunger and grief braided together until they were indistinguishable. "Because I have heard her speak the old tongue through that wall, and felt the wards in my cellar answer her, and I know what she is, and I know what is coming. The mountain kingdom is coming down, Astrid Kavanagh, and everyone in it will be bargaining with what they love before the end. So I am bargaining first, while there is still time to set the terms." She drew a breath. "I want you to weave fourteen fragments into one continuous memory. Your mother's technique — the lattice-work, the sigil-stitching that lets severed pieces sit flush against each other. No one else alive can do it. When it is done, I will taste my daughter whole, once, before the world ends. And I will die having heard her voice again. That is my price for the journals. That, and one hour of your stylus."

It was, Astrid thought, the most human request she had ever been offered in this trade, which dealt almost exclusively in the wreckage of human hearts. She looked at Wren, who was watching Ysolde with an expression far too old for her face, and Wren gave the smallest nod — *yes, we help her* — and Astrid felt the warm pull of pity and said yes.

That was her second mistake. Her first had been coming back to Brackwater at all.

* * *

The weaving took the better part of a day and most of what remained of Astrid's strength.

She worked in Ysolde's undercellar, at a stone table carved with binding-sigils that predated the Purge, and the work was the hardest she had ever done. Ordinary memory-cutting was surgery; this was resurrection. Each of Mira's fragments was a sliver of a life — a taste of honey cake, the sound of rain on a specific roof, the specific terror and glory of being lifted onto a father's shoulders — and each had to be eased into contact with its neighbors without tearing, stitched at the seams with lattice-work so fine that Astrid had to slow her own heartbeat to see the threads. Twice the join failed and the fragment began to fray and she had to catch it, cradle it, coax it back into coherence with a patience she did not know she possessed. Her mother's journals — the real ones, the ones Ysolde produced from an iron box as promised, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of two decades of secrecy — lay open beside her, and her mother's handwriting guided her hands, and Astrid worked with tears running freely down her face and did not notice them until they dripped onto the sigils and made the ink bloom.

Wren sat against the cellar wall the whole day, watching, and partway through the afternoon Astrid became aware that the child was humming — very softly, under her breath, in the dead tongue — and that the binding-sigils carved into the table were glowing faintly in time with the hum, and that every stitch Astrid placed was holding faster and cleaner than it had any right to. The child was singing the work steady. Singing her through it.

By dusk it was done. One seamless memory, resting in a single vial of smoked glass: a girl named Mira, aged nine, alive in every particle of her — the honey cake, the rain, the shoulders, a dozen other moments Ysolde had ransomed back from the crown's leavings across six desperate years, now flowing into one another like a river remembering it was always one water.

Ysolde held the vial in both hands the way the devout hold relics, and her face did not move at all, and Astrid saw that the not-moving was the loudest sound in the room.

"Take the journals upstairs," Ysolde said, when she could speak. "Read them tonight. We'll talk in the morning about the road to Cael." She turned away, cradling the vial against her breastbone, and her voice came back over her shoulder roughened at the edges. "Thank you, Astrid. Whatever happens after this — thank you."

*Whatever happens after this.*

Later, that phrase would be the splinter Astrid worried at, the thread that unraveled everything. A broker does not say thank you. A broker sets a price, collects it, and moves the ledger on. Gratitude was what people said when they had already decided something else — when the transaction in front of them was cover for a transaction behind it.

But by then Astrid had read four pages of her mother's journals and wept herself empty, and she slept in the attic room above the shop with Wren curled against her side and the iron box of journals under her coat for a pillow, and she did not hear the back door of the shop open at the hour when even the lamps conspire to be asleep. She did not hear the low voices below, the clink of coin, the wet sound of a vial changing hands — not Mira's vial, never Mira's vial, but others, others from the same warded vault, testimony-fragments of the Purge, each one bearing a date and a description and, on the newest one, a location. A road. A river crossing. An attic above a memory-broker's shop in Brackwater, where a woman and a strange child had slept for exactly one night.

She did not hear Ysolde's voice, low and level and utterly without tremor, saying: *Inquisitor Voss. This is everything I have on the heir. Now honor the other half of our arrangement. My daughter's memory — the original, the one your eaters kept in the crown archive. I want it in my hand by week's end, or the next report I write goes to the free-towns and not to you.*

And she did not hear Serane Voss laugh — softly, the way glass laughs when it begins to crack — and answer: *Everyone bargains with what they love, broker. It's the only prayer any of us still knows.*

* * *

Astrid woke to silence of the wrong kind.

Not the soft hush of sleeping streets — the pressed, held-breath silence of a house that knows something is happening inside it. She was on her feet with the stylus in her hand before her eyes finished opening, and Wren was already awake, already standing, one small hand raised for quiet, head tilted toward the floorboards.

Then she heard it: footsteps on the stairs. Heavy, measured, unhurried. The tread of someone who did not need to hurry because they already knew how the night would end.

"Wren." Astrid's whisper came out steady, which surprised her; inside, everything was falling with the smooth acceleration of a landslide. "Back window. Now."

The child didn't move. She was staring at the floor, and her face had gone the color of old paper, and when she spoke it was in common tongue, flat with dread. "Iron. A lot of iron. Coming up the stairs."

Astrid hit the window, eased the sash, and looked down into an alley full of soldiers. Six of them, maybe eight, lanterns hooded, crossbows strung, arranged around the back door of the shop with the drilled economy of people who had done this many times to many frightened people in many attics. And at the alley's mouth, leaning against the wall with her arms folded and her glassy eyes tilted up toward the attic window as if she had known exactly where Astrid would look — Serane Voss. Smiling that patient, consumed smile.

Below Astrid, in the room beneath her feet, she heard Ysolde's door open. Heard the inquisitor's boots cross the shop floor. Heard — muffled, level, unbearably calm — Ysolde's voice say, "She's upstairs. The child is with her. As agreed."

Astrid stood with the window sash in her white-knuckled grip and the journals digging into her ribs through her coat, and she understood it all at once, whole, the way one understands a cut only after the stylus comes free: the kindness, the price, the daughter, the thank-you. Ysolde had not betrayed them for coin. She had not betrayed them out of fear. She had traded the location of the last heir of the sigil-smiths to the crown's memory-eaters for one chance — one slim, probably doomed, entirely human chance — of holding her dead daughter's mind in her hands again before the end of everything.

*Everyone bargains with what they love.* Ferro had paid with his memories. Ysolde had paid with Astrid. Somewhere ahead of her, Astrid knew with sick certainty, she herself would be asked to pay with something just as irreplaceable, and she also knew — the knowledge arriving cold and complete, like a signature at the bottom of a contract — that she would pay it.

That was the bitter understanding, and it burned going down: there was no treachery in the world except love, misdirected. Every betrayer was a lover with the aim slightly off. Ysolde had aimed at her daughter and hit Astrid instead, and Astrid, standing in the dark with iron coming up the stairs, could not even find it in herself to hate her.

"Wren." She swung her pack over her shoulder and pulled the child toward the window, and her voice was low and hard and did not shake. "The roof, then the chimney line, then down. Don't look back. Don't wait for me. If I tell you to run, you run until the mountain finds you, do you hear me?"

Below them, the stairs began to creak — one step, then the next, then the next — and through the floorboards rose the sound of Serane Voss, calling up in a voice sweet with appetite:

"Heir of the smiths. Your mother's coat, please, and the child. And do come quietly — I'd hate to waste any more of your memories than I have to."

Astrid looked at Wren. Wren looked back — terrified, resolute, hers — and took her hand.

Together, they went up through the window into the dark.
