## 25. Iron and Ash

The rain had followed them for three days, and on the fourth morning it stopped all at once — the way a held breath stops before the scream.

Astrid felt it in her teeth first. She had learned, over these weeks on the road beneath the road, to read Wren's silences like weather, and this morning the child had gone still over her bowl of barley porridge at the mouth of the tunnel, her spoon halfway to her lips, her eyes fixed on the grey seam of daylight where the smugglers' stair climbed toward the river crossing.

"What is it?" Astrid asked.

Wren's fingers curled around the black glass shard at her throat. She didn't answer with words — she rarely did when the answer frightened her — but she turned the shard in her hand, and it hummed, low and wrong, a note Astrid had come to know as the sound of sigils waking.

Not waking. *Calling.*

"They found us," Astrid said quietly.

Wren nodded once.

The river crossing was the last choke point before the deep tunnels — the only place where the smugglers' spine surfaced wide enough for soldiers to bring horses and iron and fire. Ferro's maps said as much. Ferro's maps had cost Ferro everything, and Astrid carried them now like a debt she could never repay, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs alongside the letter from her mother's coat lining, the one that spoke of blood and memory and Mount Cael's buried heart.

Three days' walk remained between them and that heart. Three days, if the crossing stayed open.

"We go through fast," Astrid said. "No stopping. If they're watching it, we cut east along the millrace and—"

She never finished. The morning split open.

It began as a sound like the world clearing its throat — a horn, deep-throated, blown from the ridge above the crossing — and then the light came. Not sunlight. Crown-light: the sick amber glare of memory-lanterns, the kind the inquisitors used to burn recollection out of hiding places, out of wardstones, out of people. Astrid had sold such light once, in vials, to men who wanted to forget their wives. She knew its color the way a drowning woman knows water.

"Down," she breathed, and pulled Wren behind the fallen lintel of the old tollhouse wall.

They watched from the rubble as Serane Voss rode down into the crossing at the head of twenty crown soldiers, her grey cloak beaded with rain, her face bare of expression in that particular way Astrid had learned to dread — the glassy calm of someone whose own memories had been eaten down to the quick, leaving only function.

But Serane was not glassy now. That was the horror of it. As she dismounted and turned her face up toward the ruin where they hid, Astrid saw something burning in those consumed eyes that had not been there at Greyhollow's door, weeks and a lifetime ago. Something that looked almost like grief.

"She knows I'm here," Astrid whispered.

Wren shook her head slowly. Then she pressed two fingers against Astrid's wrist, the way she did when she wanted to show rather than tell, and let the shard sing.

And Astrid felt it — faint, filtered through stone and rain and the child's strange gift: the echo-memory hanging over the crossing like smoke. A soldier's recent recollection, left behind the way footprints are left in mud. In it, Serane stood before her troops, and her voice — that beautiful, terrible voice — had said:

*The child is to be taken alive. The woman —* a pause, and in the pause something had cracked, hairline-fine, in Serane's composure — *the woman is mine. No one touches her.*

"Astrid." Wren's voice was small. She had been speaking more these past days — short sentences, hard-won, each one an act of will against the vast wordless thing she'd been before. "She wants you."

"I know."

"Not your memories." The child's dark eyes were enormous. "*You.*"

Astrid looked at her — this mud-caked miracle who had knocked on her attic door speaking a dead tongue, who had unmade soldiers with a single spoken command, who had chosen her name from Astrid's mouth the way you choose bread from a shelf — and felt the thing she had spent forty years refusing to feel for anyone rise in her throat like floodwater.

"Then she'll have to catch me," Astrid said. "Stay behind me. When I give the word, you run for the tunnel mouth. Don't look back. Don't wait. The deep roads will take you to Cael even if I don't."

"No."

"Wren—"

"No." Louder now, and the shard flared, and somewhere below them a dormant sigil in the tollhouse foundation glowed briefly gold, like a coal breathed upon. "You said—" The child's jaw worked. Words were still a labor for her, and she built this one carefully, brick by brick. "You said I was yours. Not theirs. Not the mountain's. *Yours.* Yours doesn't run."

For a moment Astrid could not speak at all.

Then the second horn sounded — closer, from the east bank, from the millrace, the route she'd meant to take — and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

They went west instead, along the collapsed towpath where the river chewed at old pilings, and for a quarter mile Astrid allowed herself the dangerous, delicious belief that they might actually make it.

Serane let them believe it. Astrid understood that later, lying broken in the dark. Every step of that quarter mile was permitted. Every gap in the soldiers' line was a door left deliberately open. Serane Voss had been a hunter of runaways for fifteen years, and she knew the oldest truth of the trade: prey that thinks it is escaping runs exactly where you want it.

The net closed at the weir.

Soldiers rose out of the reeds on either side — six, then ten, then too many — and ahead, on the narrow stone spine of the weir itself, stood Serane, alone, unarmed, her hands empty at her sides.

"Inquisitor," Astrid said. Her knife was already out. Her other hand was already fumbling under her coat for the vials — the working stock, three months of harvest, enough raw memory to blind a squad if she shattered them and let the stuff loose as vapor. It was a crude weapon. It was the only one she had.

"Kavanagh." Serane said the name like she was tasting it. "Or should I say *heir?* Ysolde's message was very thorough."

Ice went through Astrid's chest. "What did you do to her?"

"Bought what she asked to buy. Paid what she asked to pay." Serane took one step forward along the weir, sure-footed as a cat on a fence rail, rain sliding off her cropped hair. Behind her, the soldiers fanned out across the shallows, pikes levelled, memory-lanterns raised. "You've been a hard woman to find, memory-cutter. You hide well. Your mother hid better, and it bought her eleven years. I wonder how long you thought you'd have."

"You're going to stand there and talk about my mother," Astrid said, "to me."

"I'm going to offer you a trade." Serane's voice dropped, and here — God help her — here was the crack again, widening, the loneliness underneath the iron showing itself like bone through torn skin. "The crown wants the child. Only the child. Walk away, Kavanagh. Take your roads, your tunnels, your little vials. I'll report you dead. I'm owed a few lies by now — I've fed enough truths into their lanterns to buy an army of them."

Behind Astrid, Wren made a sound. Small. Feral. A hand closed on the back of Astrid's coat, gripping fistfuls of wool, and Astrid understood that the child had planted herself there and would sooner be torn in half than moved.

"No," Astrid said.

"Think—"

"I've done my thinking." Astrid drew the first vial. "You want to know what I see when I look at you, Serane? I've seen inside you. At the river, weeks ago — I took three memories off you while we fought, and I've carried them ever since, because I couldn't decide whether to give them back or keep them as ammunition. And you know what they were?" She stepped onto the weir herself, boots wet-slick on ancient stone, river roaring white below. "A girl in a snow-field selling her childhood to a crown clerk for a bowl of soup and a uniform. A girl who cried for a week and then never cried again. You're not a monster, Voss. You're what's *left* when someone takes everything and calls it payment."

Something flickered across Serane's face — pain, rage, recognition, all at war — and in that flicker, Astrid saw the opening and took it.

She threw the vial.

It burst at Serane's feet and the harvested memories went up in a silver-brown cloud — a merchant's wedding day, a fisherman's terror of drowning, a child's first snow — raw recollection without a mind to order it, and it hit the inquisitor and her lantern-bearers like a wave hitting a seawall. Soldiers staggered. Lantern-light stuttered. Serane cried out — not in pain but in *recognition*, because one of the memories Astrid had stolen from her at the river was mixed in the cloud, coming home to her uninvited, the snow-field girl rising up out of the mist to meet her at last.

"*Run!*" Astrid spun, seized Wren's hand, and bolted.

They made thirty feet. Maybe forty. The towpath, the reeds, the grey curtain of drizzle — and then the world tilted sideways, because Astrid had forgotten, in the arithmetic of escape, the simplest variable of all: the soldiers she hadn't blinded.

The crossbow bolt took her high in the back, below the left shoulder blade, punching through wool and muscle, and the riverbank whirled up and hit her face.

Sound went strange after that. She heard it all from very far away and very close at once: Wren screaming — screaming *words*, dead-tongue words, and the shard blazing so bright it burned blue shadows through the rain — a soldier's armor shrieking as he clutched his skull and forgot why he'd come — Serane's voice cutting through the chaos like a blade through silk: *"Alive! I said ALIVE, you fools—"* — boots hammering toward her — Wren's small hands hauling at her shoulders, trying to lift her, trying to drag her, the child's strength impossible and insufficient—

"*Go,*" Astrid gasped. Blood filled her mouth. She spat it into the mud. "Tunnel mouth — fifty yards — *go*—"

"No no no no—"

"WREN." Astrid caught the child's wrist, hard, and poured everything she had left into her eyes. "You are mine. And mine *runs*. Mine lives. Mine gets to Cael and finishes this, so that this—" she coughed, wet and horrible— "so that this was worth something. Do you hear me? Do you *hear* me?"

Wren heard her. Astrid watched the hearing land, watched it break something in the child's face — watched her make, in the space of half a heartbeat, the same terrible choice every soldier and smuggler and memory-cutter makes eventually, the choice between the person and the purpose.

Then Serane's hand closed on Wren's arm.

The inquisitor had fought free of the memory-cloud, bleeding from the nose, tears cutting tracks through the grime on her cheeks — tears she clearly did not consent to and could not stop — and she wrenched the child away from Astrid's side with a grip that would leave bruises.

"I'm sorry," Serane said, and the awful thing was that she meant it. "I'm sorry, Kavanagh. But I sold my whole life to hold this rank, and I'll be damned before I watch it be for nothing."

The last thing Astrid saw, before the world went black at the edges, was Wren turning in Serane's grip — not struggling anymore, just turning — and looking back at her with those vast dark eyes, and shaping one word with her mouth. No sound. Just the shape of it, silent, across the rain and the blood and the closing distance:

*Astrid.*

Then a soldier's boot, mercifully, connected with her temple, and there was nothing at all.

* * *

She woke to cold, and dark, and the taste of copper.

For a long time she simply lay there, breathing, cataloguing her body the way she'd taught apprentices to catalogue a damaged vial. Shoulder: bolt wound, bound — bound by whom? Her own hands, apparently, some sleepwalking fragment of her survival instinct having torn strips from her coat and knotted them one-handed in the dark. Temple: split scalp, crusted. Ribs: bruised where she'd fallen. Everything else: present, aching, hers.

Alone. That was the entry that mattered. Alone.

She was in the deep tunnels — she realized it slowly, by degrees, the way you realize you're dreaming. The air had that particular mineral stillness, that cellar-breath of stone that has never seen sun. Somewhere near her hand lay the oilcloth bundle: Ferro's maps, her mother's letter, intact. Someone — Serane? — had left them with her body. A courtesy. A cruelty. With Serane it had always been impossible to tell the difference.

They hadn't killed her. That was the part she kept circling, unable to land on it. Serane had had every reason and every opportunity, and had instead dragged her — she could see the drag-marks in the mud of her memory now, fragments surfacing — dragged her to the tunnel mouth and put her inside, and taken the child, and walked away.

*The woman is mine,* the echo had said. *No one touches her.*

Serane hadn't spared her. Serane had *claimed* her. Set her aside like a piece in a game, like something to be dealt with later, and the humiliation of it was worse than any wound.

Astrid sat up. The motion tore a sound out of her that she didn't recognize as her own voice.

Darkness, absolute. She groped for her satchel and found it beside her, and her fingers, moving with the dumb competence of forty years' habit, found the last vial — a memory-lantern charge, concentrated recollection, enough to cast light if she broke the seal and let the stuff burn itself out around her. It was wasteful. It was sacrilege, practically — burning memory for mere illumination, like burning letters to keep warm.

She broke it anyway.

Light bloomed, soft and sepia-gold, made of someone's remembered summer — she could almost see it, ghost-images in the glow, a garden, a woman's laugh given shape as shimmer — and by that dying stranger's light Astrid Kavanagh looked down the tunnel that ran toward Mount Cael, and understood precisely where she was.

Rock bottom has a shape. She had imagined it, in her years of careful solitary competence, as something dramatic — betrayal, ruin, the loss of her trade, her hoard, her hidden life. She had never imagined it would be this quiet. This simple. She was alone in the dark under the world, bleeding, and the only person who had ever made her life larger than her work had been taken by a woman made of sold-off pieces, to be delivered to a king made of nothing at all, and Astrid had failed her. Had told her to run and then been the reason she couldn't. Had promised her — *you are mine* — and mines, it turned out, could not protect anything. Mines only dug holes.

The false mother's memory was gone; she'd paid it to Ysolde. Her stock of harvested lives was spent, shattered into a weapon on a weir. Her maps ended at the crossing. Her body was a ledger of debts. And ahead of her lay three days of the deepest roads in Thornfell, the roads her mother had walked, the roads that led to a heart of stone where something waited that wanted her blood and her name and every scrap of what remained.

She thought about not going. She examined the thought honestly, turning it over, testing it the way she tested goods. Lie down in the warm dark. Let the mountain keep her. It would be easy, and no one would ever know, and wasn't that the life she'd built anyway — comfortable, hidden, unwitnessed?

The shape of a silent mouth crossed the darkness: *Astrid.*

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one, to the stone, to whatever was listening down there in the roots of the world. Her voice cracked in the middle. "Oh, you absolute fool."

She got up.

It took three attempts and the wall's indifferent shoulder to do it, and when she finally stood upright in the memory-light, swaying, one hand pressed to the soaked binding at her back, she saw something that stopped her breath.

The tunnel walls were glowing.

Faintly. Faint as starlight through fog — threads of pale gold running through the black stone in lines and loops and interlocking curves, sigils, hundreds of them, thousands, a lattice-work script flowing away into the deep dark in both directions like veins seen through skin. Dormant for centuries. Dead since the Purge.

They were brightening. Slowly, gently, the way coals breathe when the bellows first sighs — and they were brightest nearest to her, dimming with distance, as though the light were pouring out of her wounds, out of her blood, out of the heir the mountain had been waiting forty years to recognize.

Astrid stood in the middle of it, bleeding and ruined and utterly alone, and the walls of the world began — softly, patiently, in a language older than walls — to speak her name.
