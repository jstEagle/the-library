## 26. The Dark Between Sigils

She did not remember falling.

There was a gap in her — a clean-edged absence, like a vial lifted from its rack — and she understood, distantly, that the wound had taken it. Pain had a way of cutting memory loose. Somewhere behind her was iron and ash, Serane's blade, Wren screaming her name, and between here and there lay a stretch of nothing she could not account for. Her body had crawled without her.

Now she lay on her back in a darkness so total it felt less like absence than presence, something with weight and patience, pressing gently down on her chest. She could not see her own hand when she raised it. She could hear water somewhere below — not running but seeping, the slow breath of stone — and above her, far above, the faintest grey suggestion of the tunnel mouth she'd tumbled through, already impossibly distant.

Her left side was wet. That was bad. Wet meant open, open meant blood, blood meant time.

"Get up," she told herself, because no one else would say it.

She got up. Or rather, her body negotiated with gravity and won by inches, one hand finding the wall, the wall answering with cold and grit and a faint vibration she chose, at first, to ignore. The world tilted, righted, tilted again. She stood in the black with her palm against ancient masonry and breathed, and each breath cost more than the last, and she counted them the way she'd once taught herself to count during extractions — four in, seven hold, eight out — because counting was a kind of rope, and if you held the rope you didn't fall into whatever waited at the bottom of a person.

Wren was gone.

The thought arrived without drama, the way the truest thoughts do. Not *they took her* — though they had, she thought, she was almost sure, there had been hands and chains and that awful efficiency of crown soldiers who moved like men with their memories shaved down to duty — but simply *she is not here*, and Astrid's arms, which had carried the child across rivers and ridgelines and half the smuggler's spine of Thornfell, hung empty at her sides.

"I'll find you," she said to the dark. Her voice came out cracked and small and immediately embarrassed itself.

The dark did not answer.

But the mountain did.

It began as the vibration in the wall — she had noticed it and ignored it and now it ignored her refusal, swelling under her palm like a second pulse that wasn't hers. Slow, enormous, patient. The heartbeat of something that measured years the way she measured minutes. Then the sound changed register, dropped beneath hearing and surfaced inside it, and Astrid Kavanagh, who had made her living in the intimate geography of other people's minds, felt something touch the edges of her own.

*Not an intrusion.* She knew intrusions. She had felt the greasy shove of Serane's kind digging for leverage, the crown's inquisitors pawing through a skull like tax collectors through a dead man's drawers. This was nothing like that. This was a knock. Courteous. Old. The knuckle-rap of someone standing at a door that has been shut a very long time.

"No," she said aloud, because she knew what lived in mountains, or believed she did — echoes, ghosts, the residue of every desperate thing that had ever died in the deep roads. Smugglers' superstition said the deep tunnels ate travelers' minds and wore their voices afterward. She'd sold memories extracted from men who claimed to have heard their grandmothers singing in the rock, and she had privately filed all such testimony under grief.

But her hand stayed on the wall.

*Answer,* said something that was not quite a voice. It arrived the way a memory arrives when you smell bread from a bakery you visited as a child — whole, sudden, wearing context like clothes. *You carry our mark.*

"I don't know what you are." Her back found the opposite wall and she slid down it until she sat, because standing was becoming a theory rather than a practice. Blood ticked onto the stone beside her, unhurried. "I'm nobody's heir. I'm a thief with good hands. Whatever you think I am, you're reading the wrong girl."

The silence that followed had texture. Amusement, maybe. Or sorrow. With things this old it was hard to tell the difference.

*We read the blood,* the mountain said. *The blood does not lie about itself. Only its owners do.*

* * *

She bled and listened, and the listening became the larger part of her.

The dark between sigils — that was what she came to think of it, later, though at the time she had no words for where she was — was not empty. It was full the way a bell is full before it rings. She began to perceive shapes in the blackness, not with her eyes but with some faculty she had never been permitted to use before, the way a woman born colorblind might suddenly see red: not a new sense exactly, but an old sense finally unlocked. Lines ran through the rock around her. Threads of light, dormant, dim as coals raked over, running in geometries too vast and deliberate to be natural — converging, branching, diving deeper, all of it flowing downward and inward, the way roots flow toward a taproot.

Toward Mount Cael. Toward the heart.

And along those threads, in the pauses between the great slow pulse, she began to hear them.

Not voices, precisely. More like the impressions people leave in rooms. She had spent ten years extracting memory from flesh and selling it in glass; she knew how a life adhered to places. But this was adherence on a scale that made her trade look like children pressing leaves in books. The walls around her were *lined* with the dead. Sigil-smiths. The word arrived in her mind unbidden, wearing certainty like a fitted glove, and she hated it, hated the ease with which it fit.

*I don't want this,* she told them.

They showed her anyway. That was the thing about the dead, she would learn — they were no more respectful of consent than the living were.

A woman with burn-scarred forearms kneeling at a workbench, drawing light out of raw ore with a stylus very much like the bone one Astrid kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack. A man arguing passionately with a council of shadows, his fists on a table, saying *binding is not caging, binding is promise* while the shadows leaned away from him. Children learning letters that were also circuits, tracing glowing lines in sand. And everywhere, everywhere, the same gesture: hand pressed flat to stone, head bowed, listening. They had been listeners first and makers second. That was the whole discipline, she understood suddenly. You did not impose the sigil on the mountain. You asked the mountain what it wanted to hold, and then you gave it the grammar to hold it.

"You're dead," she said stupidly. "All of you. The Purge —"

Grief moved through the threads like weather. Yes. Yes. They did not deny it. She felt the Purge the way she might feel a scar she couldn't see — the crown's soldiers coming with fire and null-hammers, the archives burned, the smiths dragged out by name and lineage, hunted down through three generations because a king had feared the power he'd begged them to forge him. She felt it and understood that she was feeling not one death but a *pattern* of deaths, and that the pattern had a shape she recognized, because she had seen its mirror in Ysolde's face in Brackwater, and in the letter hidden in her mother's coat lining.

*Kavanagh.* The name rose through her like water rising in a well shaft, and she fought it, clawing at the sides.

"Stop." Her voice broke apart on the word. She pressed both hands over her ears even though the voices weren't in her ears, and laughed, a horrible wet laugh, at the uselessness of it. "Stop. I know what you want. You want me to be the heir. You want me to go down there and rebind your precious sigil and give up—" Her throat closed. Give up everything. Every stolen year in every vial. The mother she replayed nightly in the blue hour before dawn, the false warm kitchen, the humming, the hands smoothing hair that was never hers to smooth. Her name. Herself.

"I have given enough," she whispered. "Do you hear me? I grew up with nothing. Nothing. I built myself out of scraps — out of *other people's* scraps, fine, I'm not proud, but I built, and I will not let a mountain and a chorus of corpses take apart the only thing I ever finished."

The threads went quiet.

And in the quiet, bleeding into the dark between sigils, Astrid Kavanagh finally stopped running from the question she had been running from since a mud-caked child knocked on her attic door.

Because the truth was — and she had always known it, the way gamblers know the odds — the truth was that she had never finished anything. She had assembled herself out of stolen parts, yes, brilliantly, beautifully, a watchmaker's masterpiece of other people's moments. But a self made entirely of borrowed goods is still a pawnshop. And she had felt it for years, the hollowness under the competence, the loneliness that no vial could fill because the loneliness wasn't missing a memory. It was missing a *belonging*. Every night she replayed a mother she'd bought off a dying merchant, and every night the memory ended the same way — with waking, and the ceiling of her attic, and the knowledge that love experienced secondhand is a coat that fits no one.

Then Wren had come through the storm, speaking the dead tongue, holding a shard that hummed, and for the first time in her life Astrid had opened a door without calculating the cost.

And now the child was gone, in chains, in the dark somewhere above or ahead, and Astrid was lying in a pool of her own blood being offered the one inheritance in the world that could matter, and she was refusing it on the grounds that it hurt.

She laughed again. Softer this time. It came out sounding almost like crying, which it was.

*You misunderstand us,* the mountain said, gentle now, the vast pulse slowed like a hand laid on a frightened animal. *We offer nothing. We ask nothing. We have asked nothing for two hundred years. We simply stopped being able to pretend you were not ours.*

"What do you mean, you ask nothing? The rebinding needs—"

*A smith's surrender, yes. Freely given. It always has been. That is why it failed before.* Something old and bitter moved through the threads — the Purge again, the null-hammers, the last smiths dragged to Vaelmoor and forced, forced, forced, their blood spilled on the wards unwillingly, and the binding cracking instead of strengthening because the sigil had been forged on promises and promises cannot be raped into keeping. *We do not want your sacrifice, daughter-of-our-daughters. We have had two centuries of sacrifice and it bought nothing but ruin. We want your answer. There is a difference, and everything depends on the difference.*

She lay with that.

Blood ticked. Water seeped. Far above, through leagues of stone, she imagined she could feel the crown of Thornfell turning like a rotten tooth in the kingdom's jaw, the Vessel gnawing at the wards, and somewhere in that machinery a small girl with a shard of black glass, waiting, believing — because Wren did believe, that was the unbearable part, Wren believed in her with the simple total faith of a child who had been a lattice of light and had chosen, out of everything she could have chosen, one tired thief with good hands.

"Astrid," she said to the dark. Testing it. Her name, in her own mouth, in a place where names apparently had weight. "My name is Astrid Kavanagh."

*Yes,* said the mountain, with something that in any living creature would have been joy.

"And I'm not giving you my memories." She pushed herself up the wall, hand over hand, teeth set against the scream in her side. "I'm not pouring them into your sigil because you ask nicely, or because destiny says so, or because some letter in a coat lining told me to. If I come down to that heart — *if* — I'll come because there's a kid up there who thinks I'm worth following, and I intend to prove her right. Do you understand me? My reasons are my own."

Silence. Then, rippling through every thread in the rock around her, unmistakable this time, impossible to misread:

Laughter. The dead, laughing — warm, startled, delighted laughter, two hundred years of grim vigilance breaking like ice on a spring river.

*Oh,* said the mountain, and the laughter rang in it like bells. *Oh, she is one of ours. Listen to it. Refusing even her own heroism. Smiths to the marrow.* And then, softer, closer, as if the voice had bent down to her ear: *Go, then. Come for your reasons. The road remembers your feet. We will light what you can bear to see, and we will keep the rest dark, and when you stand at the threshold and choose — freely, Astrid Kavanagh, freely — we will be waiting either way.*

Light bloomed.

Not all at once — in a line, a single thread at her feet igniting like a fuse burning away from her, cool silver-white, revealing the tunnel in strokes: the collapsed section she'd have died crossing blind, a ledge skirting it, stairs beyond descending in long spirals. Her ancestors' handwriting, written in luminous thread across the dark. An escort, not an order. A door left open.

Astrid looked down at her side. The blood had slowed; whether from the cold or from something older than medicine she neither knew nor cared. She tore a strip from her shirt, bound it hard, gasped, swore with a fluency that would have made Ferro proud, and stood.

Somewhere above her, Serane was moving with her prisoner toward Vaelmoor. Somewhere above that, a hollow king sat on a throne eating his kingdom's past. And somewhere in between, in a cell or a cage or a marching column, a child who had once been a lattice of light was keeping faith with a woman whose name she'd chosen for her.

"Wren," Astrid said to the dark, and the dark carried the name down and down along the silver threads, into the deep, toward the heart, the way a bell carries a struck note into every corner of the tower. "Hold on. I'm coming."

She put her hand flat against the wall — the gesture came without thinking, palm and spread fingers, and the sigil beneath her hand woke at the touch like a cat recognizing footsteps — and she walked into the lit dark, bleeding, furious, unbroken, answering.

Behind her, far above, the mountain closed the tunnel mouth with a sound like a held breath released.

Ahead, the threads of light converged, and converged, and pointed down — down toward Mount Cael, toward the buried heart, toward everything she had ever stolen and everything she had yet to give.

* * *

Three days' walk below, at the place where the smugglers' deep roads met the roots of the world, the silver threads gathered at a gate no map had ever shown and no living soul had opened in two hundred years.

As Astrid Kavanagh came down the final stair, the gate was already glowing.

And carved into its lintel, in the dead tongue that a mud-caked child had spoken through her broken door in Greyhollow, were words she could suddenly read — the language arriving in her the way her inheritance had, unwelcome and certain:

**WE KEPT THE LIGHT. WHO COMES TO CARRY IT?**

Astrid pressed her bleeding palm to the gate.

"I do," she said. "On my own terms."

The gate considered her for the space of one long heartbeat — hers, or the mountain's, she would never be sure which — and began, slowly, grindingly, to open.
