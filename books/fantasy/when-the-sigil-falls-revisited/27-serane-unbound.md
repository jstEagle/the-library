## 27. Serane Unbound

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Serane Voss decided she could not bear the silence any longer.

The girl slept curled against the far wall of the shepherd's hut, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of lanolin and old smoke. Wren — that was the name the memory-thief had given her — slept the way children sleep in stories: deeply, completely, as though nothing in the world had ever taught her to do otherwise. Her small hands were folded beneath her cheek. The shard of black glass lay beside her, dull and quiet now, its humming stilled since they had crossed the river.

Serane sat across the hut with her sword across her knees and told herself she was guarding a prisoner.

She had been telling herself that for two days.

* * *

She had caught the girl alone at the crossing — that was the truth of it, whatever her report to Vaelmoor would say. Astrid Kavanagh had gone down bleeding into the dark beneath the broken bridge, and Serane had stood on the bank with water streaming from her coat and the child in her grip, and she had felt, for one long moment, the pure cold satisfaction of a job nearly finished.

Then the child had looked up at her.

Not with fear. That was what undid her. Serane Voss had spent eleven years cultivating the look that made people afraid — the flat grey eyes, the smile that arrived a half-beat late, like a courtesy performed in a language the speaker didn't speak. She had worn it into magistrate's chambers and smugglers' dens and the homes of men who begged, and it had never once failed her.

The child had looked at that smile and tilted her head, the way one listens to a distant bell, and said — not aloud, not with her mouth, but somewhere behind Serane's eyes —

*You're so loud inside.*

Since then, Serane had not slept. She had marched the girl up out of the river valley into the high sheep country, following the old drover paths toward the pass road where reinforcements would meet them, and every step of the way she had felt the child's presence like a hand resting lightly on the back of her neck. Not gripping. Just *there*. Patient. Curious.

Now the girl slept, and the hand was still there, and Serane understood with a clarity that felt like falling that it was not the child touching her at all.

It was her own mind, leaning toward the open door.

* * *

She had sold her childhood when she was nine years old. Everyone in the inquisitor corps knew the story, though no one spoke of it: the Voss girl, daughter of a debt-ridden chandler in Greyhollow's tannery quarter, whose father had carried her to the Crown Assessors in the winter of the grain riots and signed the ledger with a shaking hand. Nine years of memories — every snowfall, every scraped knee, every lullaby — weighed, priced, and decanted into crown vials. In exchange: her father's debts erased, her board at the Schola Memoriae paid, and a future.

*The crown gives you yourself back,* the assessors had told her, in the kind warm voice they used on children. *Better than you were. Sharper. Clean.*

They had been almost right. What came out of the Schola eleven years later was sharp indeed, and clean, and very good at her work. She could read a face like a ledger. She could take a memory through the eye with a stylus thinner than a hair, and she never flinched, because flinching required knowing what was being lost, and she did not know. She had no childhood to measure loss against. When other inquisitors spoke of their mothers' kitchens, she smiled her half-beat-late smile and felt nothing at all, and she had always believed — had built her entire self upon the belief — that the nothing was strength.

The child shifted in her sleep. The firelight moved across her face.

*You're so loud inside,* Wren had said. And then, softer, with something that was almost pity: *But underneath the loudness there's a little room with nothing in it. Who took the things out of the little room?*

Serane had struck her. She was not proud of it. A backhanded cuff, quick and reflexive, the kind of blow that kept prisoners compliant — and the child had not cried, had only touched her own cheek and looked at Serane with those vast grey-dark eyes, and Serane had seen reflected in them, for one unbearable instant, not herself but a small girl in a chandler's shop, standing on a stool to reach the candle molds, laughing at something her mother had said—

She did not have that memory. She had checked, a hundred times over eleven years, in the deep archive where the crown stored what it purchased. Shelf after shelf, vial after vial, labeled in the assessor's neat hand: VOSS, S., AGE 9. And every time she had lifted a vial to the light she had seen only amber fluid and her own distorted reflection, and heard the assessor's voice saying *those are sealed by order of the Lord Protector, inquisitor; even you may not drink from your own well.*

Even you may not drink from your own well.

The fire popped. Outside, wind worried the thatch.

Serane set down her sword. She did not decide to do it; her hands simply acted, the way hands act when the mind has been arguing for days and finally loses. She crossed the hut on soundless feet — an old habit, hunting-habit — and knelt beside the sleeping child.

*I must be mad,* she thought, and the thought was true, and she let it be true.

She reached out and touched the girl's brow with two fingers, the way an assessor touches a subject before extraction. An inversion, then. A blasphemy. Every rule of her order ran backward through her mind — *the reader reads, the taken are taken, never open yourself, never open yourself* — and she pushed past all of it the way one pushes through a door into weather.

Wren's eyes opened. They were calm. They were not surprised.

*Oh,* said the voice behind Serane's eyes, gentle as snowfall. *You want it back.*

And the door opened all the way.

* * *

Snow.

Snow falling past a high window, catching the light of tallow candles, and the smell of beeswax and hot metal — a chandler's shop, her father's shop, and she is small, she is six, she is sitting cross-legged on the counter because the floor is cold, and her mother is singing. Her mother has flour on her hands from the morning's baking and she sings off-key and does not care, a song about a fox and a moon, and Serane — Mira, her name is Mira then, Mira Voss — laughs until she hiccups, and her mother taps her nose and says *you'll wake the whole street, little wren*—

Little wren. Little wren. The child's name, the thief's name for her, and Serane gasped aloud in the dark hut because the word had come first from here, from this, from a dead woman's mouth—

Winter market. Her father lifting her onto his shoulders so she can see over the crowd, his beard scratching her knees, the whole of Greyhollow strung with lanterns below her like a handful of scattered stars. He buys her a honeycake and she eats it too fast and burns her tongue and he laughs and calls her *my greedy little magpie*, and the snow comes down soft as sifted flour, and she thinks, with the absolute conviction available only to the very young: *I am the luckiest person in the world. I am the luckiest person who has ever lived.*

The little room filling. Filling, filling — a dog with a lame leg she nursed through a winter. The taste of rain off the slate roof, drunk from cupped hands on a dare. Her mother teaching her letters, tracing them in spilled flour on the table. Her father's hands, cracked and capable, folding a paper boat. The fox-and-moon song, all seven verses, every wrong note.

Nine years of it. It went on and on, richer than she had ever imagined richness, and Serane knelt in the dark drinking it like a woman dying of thirst, weeping without knowing she wept, because she had never known what she lost and now she knew, now she knew the exact shape and weight and color of it, every hour of it, and it was more beautiful than anything the crown had ever put inside her—

Then the ledger. Then the winter of the riots. Then her father's face in the assessor's office — not shaking with debt but shaking with shame, unable to look at her — and the needle-touch, and the amber light, and the room going white and clean and empty.

And after the emptying: the Schola. The drills. *Feeling is leakage; leakage is failure.* The first time she took a memory from a living prisoner and felt nothing, and the instructor's approving nod, and the terrible cold pride of it. The years of smiling her manufactured smile. The promotions. The glassy-eyed hunger she had learned to call purpose.

And underneath everything, all along, the little empty room — and now, at last, she could see it clearly, could stand inside it and see the pale rectangles on its walls where pictures had once hung, the dust-shadows on its shelves where beloved things had stood. The room had been hers all along. The emptiness had been hers. She had called it discipline. She had built a career on its floor.

The vision released her.

* * *

She came back to herself kneeling in the straw with her forehead pressed against the child's small shoulder, sobbing in great ugly tearing sounds, the kind of weeping she had not done since — since never. Since she had been emptied and taught not to. Wren's hand rested on her hair. Small fingers, careful, stroking once and again, the way one calms a frightened animal.

*There,* said the voice, wordless and warm. *There. You found it. I just showed you where it was.*

"They took it," Serane rasped. Her voice sounded ruined, unfamiliar. "They took it and they *kept* it. It's in a vault under the Schola, it's — it's labeled. They have my mother's song in a *vial*—"

*Yes.*

"I served them." The words came out of her like splinters, each one drawn separately. "Eleven years. I did to other people's children what was done to me. I stood in kitchens and I watched mothers beg and I felt *nothing* because I didn't know what they were losing, and that was the design, don't you see, that was the whole design — they hollow us first so we can hollow others without—" She pressed her fist against her mouth. "I hunted a woman across half of Thornfell to bring them *you*. Another child for the vaults."

*Yes,* Wren said again, unflinching. That was the worst of it — the child did not soften it, did not absolve her, only held the truth up between them like a lamp. *And now you know. What will you do with the knowing?*

Serane lifted her head. Through the one small window of the hut she could see the pass road below, white under the moon, winding north toward Vaelmoor. Toward the Schola and its shelves. Toward the palace where the king wore a crown that ate, where wards were failing village by village, where she had reported, week after week, with her neat ledgers and her half-beat smile, and been praised for her thoroughness.

Toward the vault where a nine-year-old girl's whole world sat in sealed amber, waiting for a thief who had finally learned to want it.

"I spent my life," Serane said slowly, "taking things for the crown. Carrying things to the crown. I'm very good at carrying." She rose. Her legs shook. She let them shake. "So I'll carry something else."

She picked up her sword. For a moment she looked at it — the crown's mark stamped in the pommel, the edge she had kept honed for eleven years — and then she drove it point-first into the earth floor between hearthstone and wall, where it stood upright, quivering, a grave-marker for a woman who had never properly existed.

From the bundle at her belt she drew her working kit: the bone stylus, the empty vials, the tools of her trade. Tools that could take. Tools, if a smith's heir and a sigil-child taught her otherwise, that might also give.

"The Lord Protector expects me at the pass with a prisoner," she said. "He'll get a report instead. And then I'm going to walk into the Schola Memoriae, and I'm going to open the Voss shelf, and every vial on it is going home." She looked down at the child, and for the first time in eleven years her smile arrived exactly on time, and it was real. "All of them. Mine and everyone else's. Let the crown learn what it feels like, having its pockets picked."

Wren studied her for a long moment with those old, old eyes. Then the child did something no prisoner had ever done: she reached up and straightened Serane's collar, a small domestic gesture, absurd and tender, the gesture of someone fixing a friend's cloak against the cold.

*Astrid went into the mountain,* Wren said, and her mental voice trembled now, grief threading through it. *Alone, and bleeding, and I couldn't go after her, because you were holding my arm. She's down there in the dark with the dead smiths and the thing that ate the king, and she thinks no one is coming.*

Serane pulled her travel cloak from its peg and settled it on her shoulders, feeling its familiar weight become, in a single night, a different garment entirely.

"Then we'd best not keep her waiting," she said. "Which way to the deep roads? And Wren—" she paused at the door, hand on the latch, the wind pushing cold against her face, "—when we find her, the first thing I'm going to do is apologize. And the second thing is teach her how to steal from a vault. I imagine she'll be a fast learner."

Behind her, the child slipped the black glass shard into her pocket and followed, and together — inquisitor and fragment, hunter and hunted, neither anymore — they stepped out into the snow and turned their backs on Vaelmoor's road, walking instead toward the mountain that was waking to its heirs, while far above them, unseen, the first ash from the burning wards began to fall like grey, unholy snow.
