## 28. Storming the Hollow Court

The gates of Vaelmoor had never opened for Serane Voss before. They had opened *around* her, swallowed her, made her one more grey stone in the crown's machinery — but never for her, never as herself, never with her own hand on the winch.

Now she stood in the shadow of the Barbican of Teeth with rain running down the back of her neck and a dead man's sword at her hip, and she pulled the lever that would let the enemy in.

The chains took the weight of the portcullis grudgingly, link by groaning link, and Serane found she was counting them the way she had once counted the memories she sold — each one a small death, each one a price. Ninety links. She'd counted those too, once, on the night she'd signed away her childhood: ninety coins of gold for a girl's whole summer of learning to swim in the Brackwater shallows. She could not remember the water anymore. But she remembered the number. The numbers were all that was left of some things.

Behind her, in the dark beyond the barbican's murder-holes, they were waiting. The memory-cutter and the child. The heir of the sigil-smiths and the living fragment of the binding. Serane had spent eleven days hunting them across half a kingdom, had burned bridges and bought informants and broken a smuggler's mind to get close to them — and then she had touched Wren's hand in a guardhouse cellar and drowned in her own stolen girlhood, and everything she was had cracked like river ice in spring.

She did not think of herself as redeemed. Redemption was a word for people who still owned their pasts. She thought of herself as a blade that had finally been shown its proper edge.

The portcullis cleared the arch. Rain swept through the gate like a tide.

"Come," she said, not turning around. Her voice carried flat and hard across the wet stones. "And whatever you see inside — however wrong it looks — do not stop walking."

* * *

Astrid Kavanagh walked into the palace of Vaelmoor with blood still crusted in her hair from the vault road and a child's small hand locked in hers, and she thought: *I have robbed better-looking houses than this.*

It was a joke she told herself, an old habit, armor made of cynicism. It didn't work. Nothing about the Hollow Court permitted jokes.

The throne hall opened before them like the throat of something vast and starving. Torches burned along its length, but their light seemed to fall *into* the room rather than out of it, as though the walls drank it. And the courtiers — gods, the courtiers. They stood in ranks on either side of the runner, lords and ladies in silk gone grey with dust, and none of them moved, and none of them blinked, and when Astrid passed close enough to see their faces she understood why the smugglers called this place the Hollow Court.

Their eyes were empty. Not vacant the way a sleeper's are vacant, or a corpse's. Vacant the way a house is vacant after the family has sold everything and gone. There were shapes behind their eyes, faint and wrong, like furniture covered in sheets — memories worn secondhand, devoured and re-worn by the thing that sat the throne. Some of them swayed very slightly, in unison, like reeds in a current Astrid couldn't feel.

Wren's grip tightened on her hand until the bones ground together.

"I know," Astrid breathed. "Don't look at them."

"They're still in there," Wren whispered. It wasn't fear in her voice. It was grief, and it was worse than fear. "Little pieces. He keeps little pieces of them, so they can stand."

Ahead of them, Serane Voss walked with her sword drawn and her shoulders squared, and Astrid watched the inquisitor's back and marveled at the strangeness of the world — that she should trust this woman's spine more than any wall, any ward, any promise. Three of Serane's memories lived in Astrid's head now, taken in a fight at the river crossing: a cold nursery, a ledger with a child's name written in the payment column, a door closing. She knew what the crown had made of her. She knew what the crown would have made of her, given ten more years.

"Left," Serane said sharply. "The throne hall is a mouth. We go around its teeth."

They went left, through a servants' passage that smelled of tallow and old smoke, and the walls here were marked — Astrid saw them even by the guttering light of Wren's presence, the way the child made dormant sigils wake. Faint lines under the plaster, older than the kingdom, spiraling toward the mountain's roots. The binding ran through Vaelmoor like a vein through flesh, and every step they took deeper into the palace, the lines brightened, answering Wren the way iron answers a lodestone.

And somewhere above them — or below; Astrid could no longer tell which direction the throne truly faced — something noticed.

* * *

Ninety seconds.

He counted them the way a drowning man counts waves. He had learned, over years of captivity inside his own skull, that the Vessel's attention was not infinite. When it fed — when it poured a courtier's memories down its endless throat, or reached through the wards to drink from a burning village — there was a slackness in the reins. A moment when the body it wore was his again.

Most times, the moment lasted three heartbeats. Five, if he was lucky. Once, in the third year, he had held his own right hand for almost a minute and wept into its palm.

Tonight the Vessel gorged itself. He felt it distantly, the way a man in a cellar feels a feast overhead: the wards over Thornfell's eastern passes fraying thread by thread, the hollowed lords standing in their rows while their borrowed eyes rolled back, the great slow *drinking*. The Vessel was preparing. Something had come home to the mountain — a fragment, a smith's blood, a threat — and the ancient hunger was gathering strength to meet it, and in its greed it grew careless.

Aldric of Vaelmoor gathered himself the way he had learned to gather himself, from the far dark corner of his own mind where he kept the last things it hadn't eaten: the smell of his daughter's hair, the weight of his coronation oath, the name of the man who had taught him to ride. He built himself out of those. He always did. A king is a story a country tells itself, and he had to be enough story to fill a body.

*Ninety seconds,* he promised himself. *Make them count.*

The slack came. He took the hands first — his hands, scarred, one knuckle crooked from a boyhood fall — and then the breath, dragging it deep because the Vessel breathed shallowly, carelessly, the way you breathe in a house you own. Then the legs. Then the eyes.

The throne hall swam into focus around him, torchlit and terrible, and Aldric looked out through his own face at the court of eaten things and felt the old scream rise in him and forced it down, down, because a scream wasted time.

*Move,* he commanded the body. *Walk.*

His legs obeyed. His legs had always been loyal; it was the head the thing wore best. He descended from the dais on legs that shook, past the swaying lords who did not see him, down the steps behind the throne where no guard stood because no guard dared, into the cold throat of the under-passage that led to the keystone vault.

*Faster.* Somewhere in the walls he could feel the Vessel turning its attention, a vast slow eye beginning to remember it had left a door unlatched. *FASTER.*

There was a servant's alcove off the under-passage, and in it, pressed against the stone with her broom clutched like a spear, a maid — young, terrified, alive. Maren. He knew her name. The Vessel didn't bother knowing names.

"Girl." His voice came out ruined, gravel and rust. "Maren. Look at me."

She looked. Her eyes went wide — because everyone in the palace knew the king's face, and everyone knew the king's face had stopped meaning anything months ago.

"There is a letter," Aldric said, "in my study. In the lining of the eastern chair, beneath the cushion, sewn into the velvet. Take it. Give it to whoever comes up from the deep ways tonight — they will not look like friends. They are friends. Swear it on whatever you still have that is yours."

Maren's lips moved. No sound came. Then, barely: "Your Majesty—"

"Ninety seconds," he said, and heard the madness of it, and said it anyway. "That is what I have. Tell them: the keystone is not in the vault. The vault is the trap. The true keystone is in the crown itself — the ninth band, the band of the First Smith, the one that does not turn. Do you hear me? The ninth band."

He felt the attention come down on him like a portcullis of ice. The edges of his vision silvered. His right hand began, finger by finger, to stop being his.

He used the last of himself to do the only kingly thing left to him. He turned back toward the throne hall, straightened his spine, and walked — staggering, fighting every step — toward the dais, away from the deep ways, drawing the hunger's gaze after him like a man waving a torch to lead a bear from a village.

*Let them run,* he thought, as the dark closed over him again. *Let them run, and let someone remember I tried.*

* * *

They met the letter halfway.

Serane heard the footsteps first — small ones, quick ones, slapping wet stone — and had her blade up and at the girl's throat before Astrid could speak. Then she saw the paper crushed in the maid's fist, saw the sigil-smith's mark stamped in black wax, and lowered it.

"From the king," the maid gasped. "He — he woke, he *woke*, he talked to me, he said—" She was shaking too hard to finish. Wren stepped forward and took the letter from her hands with a gentleness that undid the girl entirely; she slid down the wall and put her face in her arms and sobbed.

Astrid broke the seal. The handwriting lurching across the page was elegant and wrecked at once, a scholar's hand having a seizure:

*To whoever reads this — if I wrote it, part of me was free when I wrote it, and that part says: hurry.*

*The vault beneath the throne is a mouth, not a door. It wants a smith's blood to finish the unbinding. If you have come this far, you are what it wants. Do not enter it.*

*The true keystone has never left the crown. Nine bands were forged; eight bind the passes. The ninth binds the binder. It is the band that does not turn. To loose the Vessel from the king, the ninth band must be unmade by blood that remembers — and it must be done while the crown is ON THE KING'S HEAD, or the binding fails and every pass in Thornfell opens at once.*

*I am sorry. I did not know what I crowned.*

*Aldric*

Silence, except for the maid crying and the distant, subterranean groan of the palace settling around its secret veins of light.

"The crown," Serane said at last. Her voice was very quiet. "We have to take the crown off a king's head, in his own throne hall, surrounded by his court of puppets, while an ancient god wearing him fights us for it." She laughed once, short and awful. "I spent my life serving that crown. Of course this is how it ends."

"Not ends," Wren said.

They all turned to her. The child stood in the passage with the sigil-light rising off her skin like heat-shimmer, and her eyes had gone bright and strange, lit from within by the lattice under the world.

"Not ends," she said again. "The mountain is awake. All the way awake. I can hear the smiths' echoes moving in the walls, and the wards are singing, and the Vessel is *afraid* — it's eating faster because it's afraid." She looked up at Astrid, and there was no trace of the mute, mud-caked child from the storm-night in her face anymore. There was something older than kingdoms looking out through her, and it had chosen, deliberately, to be a small brave girl. "It will try to feed before we reach the hall. It will pull everything it has out of the wards and the courtiers and the stones. So we go now, while its mouth is full."

"And how," Astrid said, folding the letter into her coat beside her mother's, "do two fugitives, a traitor inquisitor, and a crying maid get through a palace full of hollowed soldiers to a king's head?"

Wren smiled. It was not entirely a comforting smile.

"We don't get through them," she said. "I take their night away. All of it. Every soldier in this palace sleeps like nothing happened — and when they wake, the crown will already be off the king." She flexed her small fingers, and down the length of the passage, every dormant sigil in the walls flared at once, blue-white, blazing, so that the whole under-palace lit up like dawn trapped in stone. "But I need to touch the lattice to do it. Which means I need to stand in the throne hall. In front of it."

"No," Astrid said.

"Astrid—"

"*No.*" The word came out cracked. "You are not bait. You are not a tool. You are—" She stopped, because the true sentence was too large for the corridor: *you are mine, I claimed you, I chose you, you are the one thing I ever took in without paying for it.* "We find another way."

"There is no other way," Serane said flatly. "I know this palace. I helped empty it. The throne hall is the only chamber with floor access to the lattice-root — the Vessel made sure of that, so no one could work the binding behind its back. If the girl stands anywhere else, she's just a child in a hallway full of soldiers." She met Astrid's eyes, and for once there was no glassiness in them, no consumed fog — just a woman looking at another woman across the wreckage of both their lives. "You cut memories, Kavanagh. You know better than anyone: sometimes the only way through a lock is to pay what it asks."

Astrid looked at Wren. Wren looked back, steady, unafraid, and that steadiness was the worst thing in the world, because Astrid recognized it. It was her own steadiness. The one she wore to markets when she sold strangers their dead mothers in vials. The armor of the competent and the alone.

She had sworn, on a storm-night at an attic door, that this child would never have to wear it.

"Then we all go in together," Astrid said. "No one stands alone in front of that thing. Not you. Not the king. Not even—" she jerked her chin at Serane, "—her. Together, or I turn around and we take our chances with the open passes and the end of the world."

"Together," Wren agreed softly.

Serane rolled her shoulders, drew her sword, and started up the stairs toward the light of the throne hall, toward the rows of swaying, empty-eyed lords, toward the throne where something ancient sat wearing her king.

Above them, faintly, through miles of stone, they heard the Vessel begin to sing — a sound like a thousand voices remembering at once, hungry and beautiful and wrong.

Wren took Astrid's hand.

"Hold on to me," she said. "Whatever happens in the next few minutes — whatever it shows you, whatever it takes — hold on."

They climbed.
