## 30. Everything I Ever Stole

The heart of Mount Cael was not a chamber so much as a wound that had learned to sing.

Astrid stood at its edge and felt the song through the soles of her boots — a low, patient thrumming, like a heartbeat buried under miles of stone. The vault opened around her in concentric rings of black glass, each ring carved with sigils that glowed faintly as she passed them, waking one by one like lamps lit by an unseen hand. The air was warm. It should not have been warm. Nothing this far beneath the mountain had any right to warmth, and yet it pressed against her skin like breath, like welcome, like a door held open for someone expected home.

Wren stood beside her, small and steady, her hand curled into the fabric of Astrid's coat. She hadn't let go since they'd come down out of Serane's wake — since the inquisitor had turned her blade toward Vaelmoor and walked back up into the dark to open gates only she could open. That had been hours ago. Or days. Astrid had stopped trusting time somewhere around the third turning of the deep tunnels.

The crown's keystone floated at the center of the rings.

It did not look like much. A fist-sized shard of black glass, twin to the fragment Wren had carried into Greyhollow on a storm-night that felt like it belonged to another life — which, Astrid supposed, it did. But where Wren's shard had hummed, the keystone *sang*, and the lattice of light that webbed beneath it filled the hollow of the mountain like a second sky turned upside down. Rivers of pale gold ran along the vaulted ceiling and dove into the walls and vanished, running on toward the passes, toward the wards, toward every sealed gate between Thornfell and the hungry dark beyond it.

And threaded through all of it, thin as spider silk, ran the fraying threads.

Astrid could see them now. She had been able to see them since the mountain began calling her by name. The wards were coming apart the way old rope comes apart — not torn, but unwoven, strand by patient strand, wherever the Vessel's hunger had gnawed through. Gaps in the golden weave. Places where the light simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets what they were saying.

"The rebinding wants a smith," Ysolde had told her, back in Brackwater, in another lifetime. "Not just blood. Not just hands. A smith pays in the coin the craft actually runs on." And then the old broker had looked at her with those eyes that had sold their own grief away and said the rest plainly: "*Memory.*"

Astrid understood it now. Standing before the keystone, feeling the lattice lean toward her like a starving thing that could not help itself, she understood it completely.

The sigil was made of memory. That was the whole secret the Purge had tried to burn out of the world. The first smiths had not built their binding from stone or blood or prayer. They had built it from *remembering* — from the accumulated weight of everything the mountain's people refused to forget. Every ward was a held thought. Every gate-seal was a promise kept so long and so fiercely that it became load-bearing. The binding held because someone remembered holding it.

And to rebind it — to close the gaps the Vessel had chewed — someone had to feed it memory again. Fresh memory. Willing memory. Given, not taken.

Every stolen thing must be returned or given.

She heard it in the lattice's voice, though the lattice had no voice. She felt it as instruction, the way you feel the shape of a stair in the dark before your foot finds it. Her inheritance, arriving at last, in full: the Kavanaghs had been thieves once too, in the beginning. Memory-thieves. They had stolen from kings and battlefields and dying strangers, and then they had done the only thing that redeemed theft — they had given it all away, poured it into the sigil, spent themselves into the binding until there was nothing left of them but the work.

That was why the line was extinct. Not murdered. *Spent.*

Astrid looked down at Wren.

The child was watching her with those grey eyes that were older than the mountains and younger than the year, and Astrid saw that Wren already knew. Of course she did. Wren was a fragment of the sigil; she could feel the lattice's hunger the way Astrid felt cold. Her grip on the coat tightened.

"Don't," Wren said. It was barely a word. Most of what Wren said these days wasn't words at all — meaning arrived in Astrid's head directly, warm and wordless, the way heat comes off a hearth. But sometimes, when it mattered most, she still used her mouth. "Don't do it all."

"Someone has to."

"I can—"

"No." Astrid crouched, brought herself level with the child, and took the small face in both hands. Wren's skin was cool, faintly luminous at the temples where the lattice-light showed through her like dawn through curtains. "Listen to me. You are a piece of the sigil. If you pour yourself in, the lattice takes *all* of you — every thread, every name, everything you've become since the storm. You'd be a patch on a wall. Wonderful for the wall. Gone for everyone else." She smoothed a thumb across Wren's cheekbone. "I'm not a piece of anything. I'm just a woman who stole too much. There's a difference, little bird. Mine is debt. Yours is self."

Wren's jaw set in a way that Astrid recognized with a lurch of terrible tenderness — it was her own stubbornness reflected back, borrowed, grown in a child who'd had no one to borrow it from. "You'll go translucent," Wren said aloud, deliberately, cruelly precise. "I've seen it in the lattice's dreaming. Smiths who gave everything. They fade. Their own mothers walk past them."

"I know."

"You won't know me."

"I know that too."

They looked at each other, thief and fragment, heir and orphan, the two least likely family in all of Thornfell, and the lattice sang its patient song, and the frayed threads above them thinned another hair's width, and somewhere far overhead — she could feel it even here, a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the stone — the Vessel was still eating.

There was no more time for arguing. Both of them knew it.

Astrid stood. She unbuckled the satchel she'd carried since Greyhollow, since before Greyhollow — the satchel that had never left her side in fifteen years of trade, through smugglers' roads and tollhouse fires and the long dark under the mountain. Inside, packed in straw like precious wine, sat the vials.

Hundreds of them. Small glass teeth, each stoppered with wax, each holding a curl of light that moved when you watched it. Fifteen years of the trade. Fifteen years of lifting the weight of other people's lives out of their skulls with a bone stylus and selling it to the desperate — the grieving widow buying back her husband's laugh, the disgraced soldier renting his courage by the hour, the old man purchasing a single summer afternoon he'd never lived. She had told herself it was mercy. Mostly it had been rent. Sometimes, honestly, it had been both, and she had never fully untangled which was which, and she suspected now that no one ever did.

But some vials had never been for sale.

She lifted those out first, and her hands shook, and she let them shake, because pretending otherwise would have been one more kind of theft — stealing steadiness from herself that she hadn't earned.

* * *

The false mother came first.

Astrid had bought the memory at nineteen, from a dying woman in a Greyhollow charity ward, paying with nearly everything she had. The woman had been no one to her — a stranger, feverish, grateful for the company — and the memory was nothing remarkable: a kitchen. Rain on a window. A woman with flour on her hands singing badly, turning, opening her arms. *Come here, my girl. You're soaked through.*

Astrid had replayed it every night for fifteen years. Lying in her attic bed with the storm rattling the tiles, she had stepped into that kitchen and been loved, briefly, cheaply, endlessly — a mother she never had, purchased secondhand, worn soft as a coin passed through too many hands. She had known it was false. Knowing had never once helped.

She held the vial up to the lattice-light and watched the little curl of gold move inside it, restless, alive.

"You're not real," she told it quietly. "You never were. She didn't love me. She was dreaming about somebody else, and I climbed inside the dream and lived there." Her voice cracked on the last of it. She let it. "And it kept me alive anyway. So thank you. Thank you, whoever she was. Thank you, whoever I pretended to be."

She pulled the stopper with her teeth and tipped the memory into her palm.

It poured out like honeyed light, pooling in her cupped hands, and it *knew* her — of course it did; she had worn its shape nightly for half her life. For one unbearable moment the kitchen rose around her whole: rain, flour, the bad singing, the open arms. Warmth on her wet shoulders. *My girl.*

"I'm sorry," Astrid whispered into it. "I'm sorry I only ever visited. I'm sorry I never stayed to be real back."

Then she stepped forward, to the innermost ring, to the place where the keystone's song was loudest, and she let it fall.

The memory struck the black glass and spread like water striking hot iron — hissing, blooming, climbing. Light raced outward along the shattered threads, and three of the fraying strands above her head *knit closed*, gold fusing to gold, and the lattice shivered with something that could only be called relief. The warmth in the air deepened. Somewhere in the walls, sigils flared bright enough to throw shadows.

It worked. God help her, it worked.

And it cost exactly what Ysolde had said it would. Astrid swayed. The vault tilted. For a heartbeat she could not remember why she was standing in a cave, and then it came flooding back — the mission, the child, the crown — but slower than it should have, like a page turning through syrup. Something was gone. Something small. A room she'd rented in Brackwater, maybe. The taste of a particular cheap stew. She couldn't find it anymore, and the absence had edges.

That was one vial. One night's worth of a mother.

The satchel held four hundred and six.

"Oh," Astrid said softly, to no one, to the mountain. "Oh, you're going to take all of me."

* * *

She did not hesitate after that. Hesitation was a luxury priced beyond what she carried.

She worked her way through the satchel in order — she was a professional, even now, even here; order mattered, sequence mattered, the lattice wanted the memories woven in the pattern the old smiths had used, oldest foundations first — and the vault became a cathedral of other people's lives.

She gave back the merchant's memory of his daughter's wedding, lifted from him in Greyhollow on the night he died, sold twice over since. The lattice took it and a ward-gate thirty miles east sealed with a sound like a struck bell, felt rather than heard, a note that moved through the stone and up through Astrid's boots and settled in her chest.

She gave back the soldier's last morning at home — bread and a wife's sleepy smile and a son on his shoulders — and two more threads closed.

She gave back roads. Dozens of roads. Fifteen years of smugglers' paths lived in other people's heads: cliff trails in fog, river fords at flood, the tollhouse door and Ferro's crooked grin and the smell of his terrible coffee — and she paused there, at Ferro's memories, the ones he'd paid Serane with, the ones Astrid had recovered from the inquisitor's own satchel in the fight at the crossing. She held them a moment longer than she needed to. *You paid for us, old man. Let this count toward your refund.* The lattice drank them, and the pass-wards above the Tollhouse of Small Mercies blazed back to life along a whole league of broken weave.

Faces. So many faces. Clients and strangers, victims and monsters. Inquisitor Voss's childhood — the lonely girl in the empty house, trading her birthday parties away one by one for a place at court — Astrid gave that back too, gently, and felt the lattice carry it upward like a lantern being passed hand to hand toward the surface, toward whatever remained of Serane walking her penance through the palace halls.

The vault grew brighter with every giving. The rings of black glass burned now, the sigils awake and roaring silently, and the inverted sky of the lattice overhead was closing, gap by gap, the way frost heals across a window — beautiful, inevitable, complete.

And Astrid was fading.

She felt it before she saw it. Her hands went strange — not numb, but *thin*. When she raised them against the light, the glow came through her fingers the way it came through Wren's temples. She looked down at herself and found her edges softening, her outline losing its argument with the air. Her shadow on the vault floor had gone grey, then pale, then barely darker than the stone.

One vial at a time. She kept counting. It was the only discipline left. Two hundred. Three hundred. Three hundred and fifty.

Names went. Small ones first — landlords, rivals, the names of streets she'd slept on. Then larger. Ysolde's name flickered and steadied, flickered again. Ferro's held — she clung to that one, fed it attention like a fire, refused it to the lattice until the lattice, with something that felt disconcertingly like patience, waited and moved on and took other things instead.

Her own history thinned to gauze. She remembered that she had been born; she could no longer remember where, or to whom, or whether anyone had ever sung badly in a kitchen while rain came down. She remembered the trade. She remembered the stylus's weight, the wax, the vials' small glass teeth — muscle-memory outlasting biography, the hands remembering long after the woman forgot.

She remembered Wren.

She made herself remember Wren. Storm. Door. Mud to the knees. A language no one alive should know, and a shard humming in a small fist, and Astrid — against instinct, against sense, against fifteen years of keeping the attic door locked — opening it.

*I will keep that,* she told the lattice, and the lattice, incredibly, seemed to consider, and seemed to agree, and the memory of the door stayed rooted in her like a post driven deep. Perhaps it was hers by right. Perhaps the sigil had always meant for its heirs to keep one thing. She didn't ask. Asking felt like tempting it.

Four hundred. Four hundred and six.

The last vial was empty of anything but her decision.

Astrid stood at the center of the rings, translucent as sea-glass, her copper hair gone white-gold at the ends where the light passed through it, and she held the final vial — the one containing the memory she had taken from herself, years ago, on the worst night of her life, and hidden even from her own satchel's inventory. She didn't remember what it was anymore. That was the joke, the awful perfect joke of her whole profession: she had cut it out precisely so she wouldn't have to carry it, and now she would give it away without ever knowing what she'd lost.

Maybe that was fitting. Maybe the sigil deserved one honest wound.

She tipped it into her palm, felt its weightless weight, and gave it.

The lattice roared.

Every remaining thread closed at once — a soundless detonation of gold, a loom completing itself, the inverted sky healing whole from rim to rim. The wards along the passes snapped taut, and Astrid felt them lock, felt the sealed gates of Thornfell settle into their ancient sleep, and felt — distant, muffled, furious — the Vessel recoil from a door that had just been barred against it forever.

The rebinding was done. The mountain exhaled. The song changed key — from hunger to rest.

Astrid Kavanagh smiled at the keystone, at the child, at the work, and found she could no longer reliably say whose smile it was. Her feet were going transparent below her. The vault floor showed through her ankles like water showing stones. It didn't hurt. That was the strangest part. It felt like being *spent* — like a candle that has done its whole job and is only now discovering how short it has become.

"Wren," she said, and her voice came out thin as paper. She knelt — slowly, carefully, a woman made of morning mist — and gathered the child against her, and the embrace held, because love apparently weighed more than flesh. "Hey. Little bird. Look at that. All sealed up. We did it. *You* did it."

Wren was crying without any noise at all, tears cutting bright tracks down the luminous face, gripping Astrid's fading coat with both fists as if fabric could anchor a person to the world.

"You're going," Wren said. "You're going where I can't—"

"Shh. No. Listen." Astrid tilted the small face up. Her own features were hard to find now, smoothed toward something generic, something almost anyone, something almost no one — but her eyes, the lattice had left her eyes. "I don't remember much right now. I know that. Some of it might come back. Most of it won't. But I remember the door. I remember opening it. Whatever else the mountain keeps — I opened the door, and you walked in out of the rain, and I would do it again in every life I'm given. Do you hear me? That one doesn't fade. That one is load-bearing."

And Wren — Wren went very still.

Astrid saw it happen: the grief in the child's face shifting, quickening, becoming something else entirely. Resolve. The same stubborn set of the jaw. The look of someone who has just found a flaw in a bargain and intends to exploit it.

"Wren?" Astrid said. "What are you—"

"You're a thread," Wren whispered. "I forgot — I *forgot*, I'm part of the lattice, I can see the weaving — you're not gone, you're *woven in*, you're a loose end hanging outside the knot—" She scrambled up, tears still falling, eyes blazing with the lattice's own light. "Loose ends can be tied back in. Half and half. The burden splits if the second giver is *of the sigil*. It balances. It *balances*, Astrid, it's arithmetic, the first smiths never had anyone like me, they always paid alone—"

"Wren, no—"

But the child had already stepped past her, into the circle, into the keystone's song, small bare feet planted on the black glass, arms thrown wide, offering herself up to the lattice with her chin lifted and her whole fragment-self blazing —

"*Take me instead,*" Wren said to the mountain, in the dead tongue, in the living tongue, in both at once. "Take half of hers. Take it out of me."

The lattice turned toward her like a great eye opening.

Astrid reached for her with hands that were barely hands at all — and caught, at the very last instant, a handful of sleeve, a wrist, a pulse, *my child, my door-in-the-storm, mine* — as the light rose up around them both like a tide deciding.
