## 31. Wren's Offer

Wren had learned, in her second life of being a person, that love was not a word. It was a weight. It was the specific gravity of Astrid's hand on her shoulder in dark places. It was the taste of hard bread shared without complaint. It was the sound of someone saying *she's mine* to a room full of ghosts.

And now it was this: watching Astrid Kavanagh come apart into light, and knowing there was no word in any language — not common speech, not the dead tongue of Thornfell, not even the language under the mountains that Wren had been born from — that could be spoken fast enough to stop it.

The sigil-heart was a cavern the size of a cathedral drowned upside down. Its ceiling was the roots of Mount Cael itself, thick as temple pillars and threaded with veins of pale gold light that pulsed like a slow heart. The lattice hung through all of it: a web of sigils older than kingdoms, older than the smiths who'd forged them, glowing now with a desperate brightness because Astrid stood at its center and fed it everything she had ever taken from anyone.

She was translucent. That was the part Wren could not stop looking at. Not fading, exactly — not like smoke or mist. More like glass becoming aware of the light passing through it. Wren could see the far wall of the cavern *through* Astrid's chest, through the curve of her jaw, through the hand she still held outstretched against the nearest thread of the binding. And threaded through that translucence, moving like fish under ice, were the memories.

Faces. Hundreds of faces, thousands — merchants and widows and dying men who had paid their last coin to forget. Roads. Greyhollow's crooked streets slick with rain. The tollhouse where Ferro had laughed. The archive where Astrid had finally wept for the mother she never knew. And beneath all of them, deeper than the rest, glowing softest and burning brightest: the false mother. The stolen memory of a woman's hands braiding hair, replayed nightly in an attic room by a girl who had grown into a woman who had never once been held.

Astrid was giving it all away. Every vial. Every theft. Every borrowed year.

"No," Wren said.

It was barely a sound. Her voice — her real voice, the one that could unmake soldiers' memories with a single command — cracked against the stone and came back to her small and useless. She had spent so long learning when *not* to speak, learning that words made of the old tongue were knives whether she meant them as knives or not. But this wasn't the old tongue. This was just a child's voice in a vast dark place, and it changed nothing, and Astrid kept pouring.

"You said half," Wren said, louder now, walking forward across the floor of woven light. "You said you would take only what was yours. Your name. Your own life. You promised me—"

"I lied." Astrid's voice came out strange, layered, like two women speaking at slightly different moments. She turned her head, and her eyes were still hers — grey-green, sharp, exhausted — but behind them the lattice moved. "I've been lying since Brackwater, little bird. Since before that. I always meant to pay the whole price myself. Because if I don't come back—" the light flared along her arm, and she winced, and went on anyway, stubborn as a mountain goat on a cliff face, "—then nothing is lost except me. One memory-cutter from Greyhollow who was already mostly alone. But if *you* step into this circuit, Wren, you're a fragment of the sigil itself. If it takes you, it might not stop. It might take the whole binding down with you, and every ward in Thornfell goes with it, and the passes open, and everything the smiths bled for comes undone."

"That's not true," Wren said.

"It's close enough to true."

"It's *fear*," Wren said, and heard the old tongue slide underneath her common words like a blade sliding into a sheath, and did not fight it, because some things needed to be said in both languages at once to be believed. "*That's fear wearing your voice.*"

Astrid laughed — a broken, bright thing. "Where did you learn to talk back?"

"From you."

The lattice sang. Wren felt it in her teeth, in the hollow of her throat, in the place behind her sternum where the shard of black glass used to sit before she'd understood she didn't need it anymore — that *she* was the shard, that she had always been the shard, a splinter of the great binding given legs and hunger and a name that a memory-thief had given her one storm-dark night at an attic door. The sigil was starving. The Vessel had gnawed at it for years from its throne in Vaelmoor, and the rebinding required fuel, and the only fuel that would burn hot enough was memory freely surrendered, and Astrid was surrendering all of hers, and she was going to dissolve into a pattern of golden light and leave Wren standing alone in a cave holding a name no one would remember giving her.

*I don't know your face,* Wren thought, imagining the morning after. *I'll wake up and there will be a stranger's ring on my finger and I won't know whose hand it came from.*

No.

* * *

There was a moment — later, when she tried to describe it to herself, there would only ever be this one moment, held apart like a jewel in velvet — when Wren understood the lattice completely.

She had been born from it. That was the truth Ysolde had hinted at and the ghosts under the mountain had confirmed and Astrid had refused, in her fierce and frightened way, to make into a chain. Wren was a fragment of the sigil-smiths' great work: a piece of the binding that had torn loose during the Vessel's first feeding, decades ago, and fallen into the world as something small and screaming and mute. She remembered being vast. She remembered being a lattice of light under the mountains, holding ten thousand wards in balance, singing with a thousand smiths' intentions. Then she had woken small, in the dark, in the cold, with grief in her mouth for a self she couldn't name, and she had walked out of the deep places on bare feet because something in her blood said *find the heir, find the Kavanagh, find the one who remembers.*

She had found her. Through storms and smugglers' roads and inquisitors and betrayal, she had found her.

So when Wren stepped toward the center of the circuit, the lattice did not resist her. It recognized her. Threads of golden light leaned toward her like wheat toward sun, and the nearest sigils brightened, and somewhere far above, through miles of stone, Wren felt wards waking in old watchtowers that hadn't glowed in a century.

"Wren." Astrid's voice had gone thinner. She was more light than woman now; Wren could count her ribs through the glow of the far wall. "Don't. Please. Whatever you're thinking — I know that look, I taught myself that look, that's the look of someone about to do something stupid and noble—"

"You always say noble people are just stubborn people with better stories."

"I stand by it."

Wren reached the edge of the innermost circle and stopped. Close enough to see that Astrid's tears, when they fell, fell as light. "Tell me why," Wren said. "Before I do this. Tell me why you lied to me."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. The lattice hummed around her like a hive deciding whether to swarm.

"Because everyone I have ever loved has been sold to me," she said at last, "or bought from someone, or rented by the hour. Because I built a whole life on the idea that nothing given freely stays given — that everything has a price and it's safer to set the price yourself than let someone else set it for you. Because when you knocked on my door I thought, *this is the most dangerous thing that's ever happened to me,* and I meant the crown, the inquisitors, the Vessel. I didn't mean you. I didn't understand yet that the dangerous thing was going to be loving you, because loving you meant having something to lose, and I had spent thirty years making sure I had nothing to lose." Another flare of light ran up her spine; her breath caught; she steadied. "So I lied. Because the math was simple. One erased thief against a kingdom. Any merchant in Greyhollow would take that trade."

"But you're not a merchant anymore," Wren said.

"No," Astrid whispered. "I'm a smith. Apparently."

"Then hear a smith's daughter," Wren said, though she was no such thing, though she was nothing so simple as a daughter of anything — and stepped over the line of light into the circuit.

* * *

It was like stepping into a river that remembered being an ocean.

The memories hit her sideways. They weren't hers; they came with edges, with the fingerprints of other lives all over them, and for one dizzying instant Wren understood what it must feel like to be Serane Voss, gulping down stolen childhoods until nothing original remained. But then the lattice did what it had been forged to do — it *bound*. It took the flood and sorted it, wove it, distributed the weight across two anchors instead of one, the way a bridge carries what would crush a single pillar.

Half. The word settled into her bones like a law.

She felt the false mother arrive first — of course she did; the lattice gave the heaviest things to the younger shoulders. A kitchen smelling of cinnamon and coal-smoke. Hands braiding her hair — someone else's hair, someone else's skull, but the feeling of those hands was so gentle that Wren gasped aloud. A lullaby in a dialect of the lowlands. The particular safety of falling asleep while another person keeps watch. Thirty years of nightly replays, worn smooth as a river stone from handling, hoarded in an attic by a lonely woman who told herself it was practice, who told herself it was stock, who knew all along it was the closest thing to a heart she owned.

*Mine now,* Wren thought, cradling it. *I'll keep it safe. I'll give it back to you someday, one night at a time, and you'll fall asleep while I keep watch.*

Other things poured in. The roads of the smugglers' spine, every switchback and hidden door, arriving in her mind like a map drawn in fire. Ferro's laugh. Ysolde's ruined face when she spoke of her daughter. The letter in the coat lining, the smell of old paper. The exact color of Serane Voss's eyes in the moment Wren's gift flooded her with her own sold childhood and broke her free. The taste of hard bread on the road. Rain on an attic roof. A door opening.

And underneath all of it, the anchor-weight, the thing Astrid had saved for last: her own name. Astrid Kavanagh. Memory-thief. Sigil-heir. Stubborn person with a very good story.

Wren took hold of it with both hands, in the place that was not a place, and held.

"Astrid!" Her voice rang through the cavern, doubled now, harmonized with a thousand threads of light. "*Let go of the half that's mine!*"

For a heartbeat nothing happened. The lattice shuddered. Astrid hung at the center like a lantern in fog, nearly gone, nearly all gift, and Wren felt the terrible pull of her — the circuit wanting to complete itself through a single soul, clean and total, the easy arithmetic.

Then Astrid *listened*. Of course she listened. She was the most stubborn creature Wren had ever known, and stubbornness, it turned out, worked both directions.

The transfer reversed like a tide turning. Half the light streaming out of Astrid halted, hung, and began to flow instead into Wren — into the fragment, into the living shard, into the child who had been built to carry exactly this kind of weight. Wren felt herself grow heavy. Glorious, unbearable, holy heavy. She felt her feet root to the floor of the cavern. She felt the lattice accept her as an anchor, felt new sigils bloom along her arms like frost-flowers made of dawn, and she did not scream, because screaming would have wasted breath she needed for holding on.

"*Together,*" she said in the dead tongue, and then again in common speech, because the vow needed both languages to be true. "We carry it together. Neither erased. Neither alone. That was always the point of a binding, wasn't it? Not one pillar. Many."

Astrid's form solidified — not all at once, but in pieces, like frost forming on glass: first the outline of her shoulders, then the grey-green eyes, wide and wet and furious with relief, then her mouth shaping words that had no sound yet. The lattice redistributed, rebalanced, found its new equilibrium: two anchors instead of one, the burden split down the middle, each carrying what the other could not carry alone.

And through the sharing, for one impossible moment, they were inside each other's memories. Wren felt Astrid feel the false mother leaving — the grief of it, huge and quiet, like a tide going out. And Astrid felt Wren feel it arrive — the tenderness of it, the honor of being trusted with someone's most secret wound. Their griefs touched. In the space between the two halves of a divided burden, something passed between them that was not memory at all, that was newer than the sigil, older than the smiths, simpler than either:

*You are mine,* Wren said into that space. *Not the lattice's. Not the mountain's. Mine, and I am yours, and no crown or hunger or hundred-year-old forging will get a vote.*

*Yes,* said Astrid, in the voice of a woman who had spent her whole life pricing everything and had finally, at the end, been given something priceless for free.

* * *

The rebinding, when it came, did not roar. It rang.

The lattice drank from them both — measured sips, fair shares, the way water finds level between two vessels — and the great web of sigils blazed from root-thread to root-thread, and far above them, through the whole skeleton of the mountain, Wren felt the wards seize tight one after another like fists closing. Somewhere north, a pass sealed itself with a sound like a mountain exhaling. Somewhere south, in a throne room in Vaelmoor, a crown grew suddenly, violently heavy on the head of the thing wearing it.

But that was the next battle. Here, in the ringing aftermath, there was only the two of them standing in the settling light, holding each other up.

Astrid looked at her own hands — opaque again, flesh again, scarred again — and laughed, and cried, and laughed while crying, which was the most honest thing Wren had ever seen her do. "Half," Astrid managed. "You absolute, impossible—"

"You said any merchant would take your trade. But I'm not a merchant," Wren said. "I'm a fragment. And fragments exist to be part of something." She pressed her palm flat against Astrid's sternum, over the heartbeat, over the place where the lattice had almost swallowed her whole. "You gave me a name when I had none. You gave me a door when every other door was shut. So I'm telling you how it's going to be, Astrid Kavanagh, and this is the one order I will ever give you: you don't get to disappear on me. Not tonight, not tomorrow, not ever. We do this together or we don't do it at all."

Astrid covered Wren's hand with her own. Her grip was warm. Her grip was solid. Her grip was there.

"Together," she said.

The lattice pulsed once, gently, like a heart accepting a new rhythm. And then, from the darkness at the far end of the cavern — the deep tunnel that led up and out, toward Vaelmoor, toward the throne, toward the crowned hunger that had just felt its meal turn to iron — came a sound neither of them expected.

Footsteps. Slow, deliberate, echoing off the sigil-lit walls.

And with them, faint but unmistakable, threading through the stone like smoke: the voice of the Vessel, speaking for the first time in its long hungry centuries not with threat but with something that sounded almost like hope.

"You carry her craft in your hands," it called down the tunnel, in a voice like wind through a burned house. "Come and finish what my master started. Or come and unmake me. Either way, little smiths — *come.*"
