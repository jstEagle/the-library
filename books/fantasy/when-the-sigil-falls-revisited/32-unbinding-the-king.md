## 32. Unbinding the King

The vault beneath the throne breathed.

That was the only word Astrid had for it. The air moved in slow tides, warm and cold by turns, as though something vast slept below and above them at once — the whole weight of Mount Cael pressing down through a thousand feet of living rock, filtered through sigils older than any kingdom. Her blood knew this place. It sang in her wrists, in the old scars where she'd gripped styluses for twenty years. *Smith's blood,* her mother's letter had said. *Blood that remembers.*

She remembered everything now. That was the cruelty of it. Ysolde's map, Serane's treachery and Serane's turning, the archive of journals, Wren's hand in hers through the dark between sigils — all of it burned bright and present, because tonight every stolen memory she carried would have to be spent. But not yet. Not before the crown came off.

Aldric knelt at the center of the vault on a dais of black glass, and he was barely a man anymore. His body remained — broad-shouldered, gray-bearded, still wearing the ceremonial white of a king even here, even now — but his eyes were windows into an empty house. Behind them something else looked out, patient and ancient and terribly sad.

The crown sat on his head like a wound that had learned to smile.

It was not gold. Astrid had expected gold, or iron, or some dark metal quarried from the mountain's bones. Instead it was light — a circlet of woven radiance, thin as wire, humming against the king's brow, and woven through its lattice Astrid could see threads of memory moving like fish under ice. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Everything the Vessel had eaten since the first smith bound it there, circling and circling inside its cage, never digested, never released. A hoard with no use for what it hoarded.

"It knows we're here," Wren whispered.

The child stood beside her, and Astrid still could not look at her without her chest tightening. Two days ago Wren had been small and mud-caked and mute. Then the deep tunnels had fed her, the dead smiths' echoes had poured through her like water through a broken dam, and now she stood tall and luminous in a way that made the torches seem dim — a fragment of the great lattice wearing a girl's body, twelve years old or so, with eyes like the underside of glaciers. She was still Wren. That was the miracle Astrid clung to. When the ghosts had tried to take her back, when they'd called her *Fragment Nine-and-Twenty, return to the weave*, Wren had said, in her new voice, rough as a fledgling's: "No. I'm hers."

And now, gods help them both, Astrid had to spend her.

"Say it again," Astrid said quietly. "Before we start. I want to hear it one more time."

Wren didn't roll her eyes, which would have been the normal thing, the child thing. She just turned and looked at Astrid fully, and the vault seemed to lean in around them.

"You pour your memories into the keystone. All of them. Every face you ever cut from someone's skull and sold in vials, every road you memorized, every lie you told, the mother you replayed every night until you almost believed her." Wren's voice didn't waver, but her small hand found Astrid's sleeve and held on. "The sigil takes them and uses them as mortar. Old memories are already shaped for binding — that's what Ysolde meant, that's why the smiths used their own lives to build it. And while you're pouring, I pull the crown. The binding will be loose for exactly as long as it takes you to empty. If I let go too soon, the Vessel snaps back and eats us both. If you stop too early—"

"I know."

"—the binding half-forms and the passes open anyway, and everything Aldric broke stays broken." Wren swallowed. "You'll forget my face. You might forget your own name."

"You'll give it back," Astrid said. "One vial at a time. You promised."

"I keep my promises." And there, beneath the glacier-light, was the child Astrid had pulled in out of the storm — stubborn, frightened, fierce. "I kept yours. I kept *all* of them. Even the ones you told me not to."

Behind them, at the vault door, Serane Voss stood guard with her sword drawn and her back to the room. She hadn't spoken since the river crossing, since she'd flooded with her own sold childhood and come out the other side a different weapon. Now she said, without turning: "Something's coming up the stair. Fast. Whatever you're going to do, do it."

* *

Inside the crown, inside the man, Aldric dreamed of snow.

He had dreamed of snow for eleven years. It was the last thing he could hold onto — the day of his coronation, flakes settling on the shoulders of ten thousand cheering subjects, the weight of the crown descending, and then the *other* weight descending with it, the thing coiled in the forging, waiting for a head worthy of it. He had felt himself fold up like a letter being sealed. He had screamed for years, and then stopped screaming, and then learned to live in the small space left over, the way a hermit crab lives in a borrowed shell.

Now, for the first time in eleven years, the shell was cracking.

He could feel them below — the smith-heir and the fragment, two small fires in the dark, and the keystone opening like a mouth. The Vessel felt them too. He felt its attention turn, slow as a glacier calving, and for a moment — a moment — its grip loosened, distracted, and Aldric surged up through eleven years of silence with the strength of a drowning man breaking water.

*Not the girl,* he thought, pouring everything into it. *Listen to me. Listen, thing in my crown. I don't remember my daughter's name anymore. I don't remember my wife's face. I signed the orders that burned Harrowmere and I watched them burn and I couldn't even grieve properly because you had eaten the grief. Whatever mercy you have left — and I know you have some, I've heard you weep in the night when you think I can't feel it — spend it here. Let them bind you. Let me die a man instead of a door.*

The Vessel did not answer. It never answered. But for one flickering instant, Aldric was looking out through his own eyes, standing over his own hands, kneeling on cold glass in a vault that smelled of hot metal and rain, and he saw the woman with the stylus-scars raise her arms full of light, and he thought, with the last coherent part of himself:

*So that's what a Kavanagh looks like. Mother forgive me. Hurry.*

Then the tide took him under again.

* *

Astrid opened her coat.

Inside the lining, sewn flat against the fabric where her mother had hidden a letter for twenty-five years, were the vials. All of them. She had spent three sleepless days collecting them from every hiding place of her old life — the false-bottomed case, the hollowed prayer book, the slot beneath Greyhollow's bridge where she'd cached her most dangerous stock. Forty-one vials of stolen memory, each one glowing faintly with borrowed life: a fisherman's dawn hauls, a general's battle-plans, a bride's wedding morning, a dying merchant's entire fortune of years. And one vial that was no one's but her own — the false mother, the memory she'd bought rather than stolen, the warm kitchen and flour-dusted hands and the voice saying *there you are, love, I wondered when you'd come home*, which she had replayed nightly for fifteen years because it was the only mothering she had ever known.

Her hands shook. She let them. Fear was honest; she'd earned it.

"Wren," she said. "Last chance to run."

Wren shook her head once, and stepped forward onto the dais, and raised both hands toward the crown of woven light.

"Ready," the child said.

Astrid pulled the first stopper.

The memory came out like breath in winter. She didn't pour it so much as aim it — tilted the vial toward the keystone, a slab of black glass rising from the floor like a tooth, carved with the master-sigil in her family's own hand — and the memory *went*, streaming out in silver ribbons, and the moment it touched the stone the whole vault lit up like a struck bell. Somewhere far above, through a thousand feet of rock, Astrid felt the wards of Thornfell shiver awake.

She pulled the second stopper. The third.

Faces went. Roads went. The smell of the fish market in Brackwater, the exact blue of a lake she'd once swum in to escape inquisitors, the taste of victory after her first successful extraction — all of it, everything she'd cut from other people's skulls over twenty years of trade, flowing out of her and into the stone, and with each vial she felt herself grow lighter and thinner and more translucent, like paper held up to a lamp.

The Vessel felt it too.

The king's body rose off its knees. The crown flared, threads of stolen light thrashing like netted fish, and a voice came out of Aldric's throat that was not one voice but many — layered, ancient, worn smooth as a riverstone by centuries of screaming.

***"Smith-blood."*** The sound scraped the walls. ***"Little heir. Do you know what you carry? Do you know what those memories weigh? Pour them into the keystone and you will build your prison and mine together, and I will wear it another thousand years, and I will hate you for every single one—"***

"That's the idea," Astrid said, and pulled the tenth stopper.

The Vessel lunged.

It wasn't a body moving; it was the crown itself reaching, threads of light whipping off Aldric's brow like burning whips, striking for Wren, for Astrid, for the keystone — and Wren caught them.

Astrid would remember that image forever, even if everything else went: a small girl in a storm-gray coat catching threads of annihilating light in her bare hands, her feet sliding on the glass, her whole slight body straining backward like someone holding a kite in a hurricane, and her voice — her new voice, the one the mountain gave her — ringing out steady over the roar.

"Hold the line, Astrid! *Pour!*"

Astrid poured.

Twentieth vial. Twenty-fifth. She was crying without knowing when she'd started, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face, and she was laughing too, because it hurt so much and felt so much like flying. Thirty vials. The general's war went. The fisherman's forty years of dawns went. The bride's whole wedding morning, gone, returned to the stone that would use it to seal the passes against the things beyond. She could see her own hands fading, the veins showing through like leaves held to sun.

Thirty-eight.

Thirty-nine.

Her fingers closed on the last vial and stopped.

The kitchen. Flour-dusted hands. *There you are, love.* Fifteen years of nightly visits, the only warmth she had ever owned, bought secondhand from a stranger's childhood. If she poured it, she lost the mother she'd never had. If she kept it, the binding would be short one thread, and short bindings failed.

"Astrid!" Wren's arms were shaking now, the light-threads straining. "*Now!*"

Astrid looked at the vial. Looked at Wren — really looked, memorizing her the way she'd once memorized roads: the chapped knuckles, the scar on her chin from the tollhouse stairs, the way her hair stuck up where the ghost-light had passed through it.

"Goodbye, Mum," she whispered, to a woman who had never existed, who had loved her completely for fifteen borrowed minutes a night. "Thank you for pretending."

She pulled the stopper and gave away the last warm thing she owned.

* *

The keystone blazed.

Every sigil in the vault ignited at once, and through them Astrid felt the mountain itself answer — felt the lattice wake beneath Cael's roots, the vast sleeping network of binding-light her ancestors had forged, taking the offered memories and spinning them into mortar, thread by thread by thread. The passes were sealing. She could feel them closing far away, north and south and east, gates of force slamming shut across the smugglers' roads she'd run all her life.

"Now, Wren!" she screamed, and her voice was coming apart with the rest of her. "NOW!"

Wren let go of the light-threads, planted both feet, and reached up with both hands for the crown.

What happened next happened in every tense at once.

The Vessel understood — in the last instant, it finally understood — and it threw everything it had left into holding on, and Aldric, feeling the grip loosen, threw everything *he* had into letting go, father and parasite and prisoner all fighting over the same skull. Wren's fingers closed on the circlet of woven light and *pulled*, and the crown resisted, and the vault screamed with friction like a ship tearing on rocks, and Astrid — translucent, nearly gone, held upright mostly by the keystone's hunger — pressed her fading hands against the black glass and *pushed* with everything the sigil-smiths had left in their line.

The crown came free.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The Vessel hung above Aldric's bowed head, a shapeless knot of light and hunger, crownless, unanchored, furious —

—and Wren did the thing she had not told Astrid she would do.

She stepped *into* it.

"**I know what you were,**" the child said, and her voice filled the vault the way thunder fills a valley. "**Apprentice to the First Smith. Ninth of nine. They bound you into the crown because you were strong enough to hold it, and they never asked if you wanted to, and they never wrote down your name because a named thing cannot be bound — so they took it from you first.**"

The Vessel stopped struggling.

Astrid, dissolving, watched. She couldn't move. She could barely think. But she saw the knot of light go very, very still, the way a wild animal goes still when a hand approaches slowly.

"**But I'm a fragment of the lattice,**" Wren said softly. "**I was there when you were forged in. I held the edge of your memory before they cut it away. And I kept it. All these centuries, in the deep places, I kept it.**"

She opened her hands. In them lay a thread of light different from all the others — warmer, older, shaped like a name.

"**Your name is Maerin.**" Wren's voice cracked, gentled. "**Maerin, apprentice of the First Smith. You loved the forge. You loved the mountain. You loved your master, and you thought the binding would honor you, and when it didn't — when they sealed you in the dark instead — you got hungry. Eleven hundred years of hunger, Maerin. That's not evil. That's just what happens to anyone locked in the dark long enough.**"

The Vessel trembled.

"**Here.**" Wren held out the name-thread. "**Take it back. Take yourself back. And then let go.**"

The light-shape reached out one tendril — hesitant, disbelieving, the gesture of a starving creature offered food it had forgotten how to eat — and touched the thread.

And the Vessel *wept*.

There was no other word. The knot of light convulsed and began, from the outside in, to come apart — not torn, not consumed, but *released*, unraveling into threads, and each thread as it separated carried a sound, and the sounds braided themselves into sobbing. Eleven hundred years of it. The grief of an apprentice who had only ever wanted to matter. The rage of a prisoner. The horror of a thing that looked back, suddenly, at everything it had eaten — Aldric's daughter's name, Harrowmere's burning, ten thousand small stolen mornings — and found, now that it had its own memory back, that it could finally understand what guilt was.

"**I didn't want—**" The voice was single now, young, wrecked. "**I didn't want to be the hunger. I wanted to be the smith—**"

"I know," Wren said. "Come home anyway. The lattice has room. It always had room. They just forgot how to ask."

The last of the Vessel — the last of Maerin, apprentice of the First Smith, ninth of nine — flowed downward, through the vault floor, into the roots of the mountain, trailing light like a bride's train, weeping, and the mountain received it the way soil receives rain.

Silence fell like snow.

Aldric collapsed forward onto his hands. He was breathing — ragged, human, astonished breathing, the breathing of a man surfacing from deep water — and when he lifted his head, his eyes were only eyes, tired and ruined and his own.

"The girl," he rasped. "Where—" His gaze found Astrid, or the place where Astrid was, a figure of skin and lamplight and little else. "Kavanagh. You're fading."

"Yeah," Astrid said. Her voice sounded far away even to herself. "Worth it, though."

"Your Majesty!" Serane was through the door, sword still drawn, taking in the scorched sigils, the fallen king, the translucent woman, the small girl glowing like a lantern. "The palace — the courtiers — some of them are still hollowed, they'll follow the last order the Vessel gave—"

"Serane Voss." Aldric tried to rise and failed, and laughed — a broken, wondering sound. "The inquisitor who sold her childhood to my crown, standing in my vault with a drawn sword. The world ends strangely." He managed one knee. "Help me stand. If the court needs a king tonight, they can have ninety seconds of one. After that—" He looked at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger, and perhaps they did. "After that, we will discuss what a king owes the people he doesn't remember breaking."

* *

Astrid slid down the keystone's face and sat on the warm glass, and the last of her light guttered.

Shapes moved around her — Serane's armor, Aldric's white robes, torch-flame — but they were going soft at the edges, becoming colors without names. She wasn't afraid. That surprised her. She had spent her whole life selling fear by the vial, and now that it was her turn to be emptied, she found only a vast quiet, like a house after the guests have gone home.

Small hands caught her before her head hit the stone.

"Astrid. *Astrid.* Stay with me." Wren's face hovered above her — and Astrid looked at it with desperate attention, carving it into whatever was left of herself: the chapped knuckles, the chin-scar, the hair sticking up. "I've got them. I told you. I took half when you weren't watching — I stepped into the circuit, I split it, they're safe, they're *mine* now, and I'm going to give them back, one at a time, however long it takes. But right now—" The child's voice broke, and she pressed her forehead to Astrid's cooling one, and her tears fell hot on Astrid's cheek. "Right now you have to hold on. Because I'm about to forget your face too, and somebody in this vault has to remember both of us."

Somewhere above them, muffled by a thousand feet of stone, the bells of Vaelmoor began to ring.

They rang wrong — cracked, frantic, the alarm-pattern Astrid had heard only once before, the night the wards first weakened. But underneath the alarm, if you listened, if you were a smith's daughter lying emptied on the heart of the mountain, you could hear the deeper bell beneath all bells: the lattice singing as the final threads set hard and true. Sealed. Bound. Whole.

The passes were closed. The king was a man. The hunger had gone home weeping, and been forgiven.

Astrid smiled at the girl holding her, and opened her mouth to say her name — *her own* name, the last thing left in her — and found, where the name should have been, only a soft and total dark.

"Wren," the child whispered, answering anyway, holding her tighter as the bells rang on. "Your name is Astrid. Hold onto that. I'll bring you the rest."
