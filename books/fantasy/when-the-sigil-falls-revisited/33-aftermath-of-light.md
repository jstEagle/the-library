## 33. Aftermath of Light

The first thing Aldric Vael knew of the world was the taste of ash.

Not metaphor — actual ash, fine and grey and bitter, sitting on his tongue like a confession he had not yet been permitted to speak. It lay on everything in the great chamber beneath the throne: on the shattered keystone, on the scorched sigil-rings inlaid into the floor, on the girl with the hollow eyes who knelt beside a smaller figure wrapped in a cloak that had once been white.

He tried to remember how he had come here. He reached for the memory the way a man reaches for a stair in the dark — and found nothing where his life should have been.

*Nothing.*

Not emptiness exactly. Emptiness would have been merciful. What he found instead was a landscape of absence shaped precisely like a life: the outline of a wife's laughter with no laughter inside it; the silhouette of a son's first steps with no small feet; corridors of years — coronations, harvests, winters, wars — all of them rendered in perfect negative, like footprints left in snow by someone walking backward out of the world.

He remembered learning letters. He remembered nothing of what he had written with them.

He remembered the weight of a crown being lowered onto his head at nineteen — the cold gold, his father's breath against his ear saying *serve them well, boy* — and then a gulf, and then a throne room seen from very far away, as though through water, as though through another man's eyes.

Because they *had* been another man's eyes. That was the horror of it, the thing that made his hands begin to shake now, twenty years too late: for longer than he could measure — and he could not measure it, that was the point, the *point* was that he could not measure anything — something else had looked out through him. Something had kissed his queen with his mouth. Something had signed death warrants with his hand. Something had sat at his daughter's bedside while she fevered, wearing his face, and she had called it *Papa*, and whatever answered her had not been him.

Aldric put his palm flat against the cold stone floor and was quietly, thoroughly sick.

When he raised his head, the tall woman with the scarred knuckles was watching him. The one they called Kavanagh. She looked translucent, he noticed — not ill but *thin*, as if someone had taken a blade to her and instead of blood had drawn off whole seasons of her life. Beside her, the child — Wren, someone had said, or perhaps he had only dreamed the name — slept with her cheek against the woman's knee, and even asleep her small hand gripped the fabric of Astrid's coat as if letting go were a thing she had decided, once and forever, never to do.

"You're back," Astrid said. Not gently. She had the voice of someone who had run out of gentleness somewhere in the last three days and was operating on reserves she resented spending.

"For ninety seconds," Aldric said. His own voice sounded foreign. Rusty. A hinge unused. "I told you about the vault. I remember telling you." He touched his temple, half-expecting to find the other presence still coiled there behind his eyes, listening. There was only silence, and the silence itself was terrible, because for two decades the silence had never once been empty. "Is it gone? Truly gone?"

"It wept," said the child, waking. Her voice was small and clear and old, an instrument played by someone far beyond her size. "It gave back its name and it wept, and the mountain took it home." She studied him with those eyes that had seen the inside of things. "You can stop listening for it now. It isn't there."

And Aldric understood that she was right, and that he would spend the rest of his life listening anyway.

* * *

They brought him up through the tunnels because the palace stairs were still choked with the debris of the unbinding, and because Serane Voss — Serane, his own inquisitor, his own hound — stood at the head of the column with her sword still drawn, guarding him as though he were a prisoner.

Perhaps he was. He could not honestly say what else a man should be called, who woke from twenty years of theft into the wreckage of everything his hands had done.

Grey light met them at the tunnel's mouth — real daylight, the first true morning after the wards had closed, and the whole sky above Vaelmoor was the colour of woodsmoke. Below the citadel, the city spread out in its terraces and hanging gardens, and Aldric stopped dead on the threshold because the eastern quarter was *gone*.

Where the ward-village of Emberlin had clung to the hillside — he knew its name suddenly, dredged up from some sediment the Vessel hadn't bothered swallowing, a name attached to nothing, a label on an empty jar — there was only a blackened slope. Roofs like burnt ribs. Chimneys standing sentinel over hearths that no longer existed. He did not remember burning it. That was the unbearable architecture of his guilt: every crime had a wall between it and him, and the wall was built of the very memories that might have let him grieve properly, repent properly, *know* properly what he had done.

"Three hundred souls," Serane said, without turning. Her voice was flat as slate. "Emberlin. You ordered the evacuation refused. You said—" and here something moved under her flatness, a tremor, quickly killed, "—you said the mountain fed better on a full village."

"I don't remember saying it."

"No," Serane said. "That's rather the whole of it, isn't it."

He deserved the cruelty of it. He took it the way he imagined a man takes a surgeon's blade — holding very still, knowing the cutting was the kindness.

* * *

The council chamber smelled of lamp oil and fear. His councillors had assembled within the hour — those few who remained, whose minds were their own; the others sat in the antechamber under guard, glassy-eyed and placid, hollowed men and women waiting for someone to decide what to do with the shells. Aldric looked at them through the open doors and felt the sickness rise again. He had dined with some of them. Had trusted at least one with his seal.

Lord Chancellor Iven Marsh stood when Aldric entered, and the old man's face did something complicated — relief and grief and a flicker of fear, all fighting for the same ground.

"Your Majesty. Thank the Nine. We had reports that— that you were—"

"That I was the thing that did this?" Aldric gestured at the smoke-dark window, at the ruined quarter, at the world. "I was. Sit down, Iven. All of you. This will not take long."

He remained standing himself. He had thought, on the long walk up from the dark, about how a man does this — how a man stands in front of the ruin of his reign and says the necessary words — and he had concluded that there was no good way, only ways less bad, and that the least bad of them began with not hiding behind furniture.

"The crown is unbound," he said. "The passes are sealed. The wards hold — the Kavanagh woman and the child saw to that, at costs I am only beginning to comprehend. And the thing that wore my face for twenty years is gone." He let that settle. Let them see him plainly in the grey light: thinner than they remembered, he supposed, older, with a man's fresh grief carved over the ghost-grief of twenty borrowed years. "I want to be precise about one thing before any of you find kind words to say. The crimes of the last two decades were committed by my body, with my hands, under my seal. Whatever was driving it, the debts are mine. Emberlin is mine. The purges of the border clans are mine. The children taken for the inquisition's memory-tithes are mine. I will not stand before this kingdom and plead possession like a man excusing a bad debt on a technicality."

"Your Majesty," began Lord Hessa, the treasury minister, a woman with ink-stained fingers and the courage of a much larger person, "no reasonable court could—"

"There is no court," Aldric said, "that could give this sentence teeth enough. Which is why I am not going to wait for one."

He drew off his signet ring. It had not fit properly in months — in *years*, some part of him noted clinically; the Vessel had grown leaner than he was, or he had grown leaner than it preferred — and it came away with a small sound, gold on skin, the softest thing in the room and the loudest.

He set it on the table in front of Iven Marsh.

"I abdicate."

The word dropped into the chamber like a stone into a well, and the echoes went down and down.

"Your Majesty—" 

"Iven." Aldric heard his own voice steady itself, and was grateful for whatever fragment of the old king survived in it — the part his father had forged, the part that had said *serve them well, boy*, the part the Vessel had never quite managed to digest because it was made of something the hunger couldn't taste. "Look at me. Truly look. You have known me since I was nine years old. Tell me what remains."

Iven Marsh looked. The old chancellor's eyes went wet and furious with it.

"A king," he said hoarsely. "Whatever's missing. What's standing here is a king."

"What's standing here," Aldric said, "is a man who cannot remember his wife's face. Who learned this morning that he had a daughter who died of fever eight years ago, and cannot grieve her because the grief was eaten along with everything else. Who signed the order that emptied your granddaughter's school into the tithe-wagons and felt, I am told by the records, nothing at all." He laid both hands flat on the table, and they were steady, and he marveled a little at that. "You want to hand such a man a kingdom? Iven. My friend. A kingdom is a memory. It is the accumulated remembering of a people — their laws, their grievances, their dead, their debts. I am a man without memory presiding over a nation that needs remembering more than it has ever needed anything. If I rule one day longer, I rule as a hollow thing, and hollow things attract what filled me. I have seen where that road goes. I burned a village at the end of it."

Silence. Somewhere below, in the recovering city, a bell rang the hour, and the sound came through the smoke-stained glass muffled and strange, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"Then who?" Hessa asked quietly. Not challenging. Genuinely asking, the way one asks in a country where the answer has just burned to the ground.

"Not me. Not my bloodline — the succession is tainted by the same twenty years; whoever wears that crown next must wear it clean, chosen, accountable. Convene the full assembly. Every province, every free-town, Brackwater included. Let them choose a steward-regent until they can choose a king — or let them choose no king at all. Perhaps," and here something almost like humor surfaced in him, dry and rueful, a survivor's reflex, "perhaps a kingdom that has just watched its monarch get eaten from the inside has earned the right to try governing itself."

"And you?" Iven asked. "What happens to you?"

Aldric looked out the window at the smoking east, at the black slope where three hundred lives had fed a hunger wearing his smile.

"I'm going to learn the names," he said. "All three hundred. Then the border clans. Then the tithed children, as many as the records name. I'm going to sit with the survivors of Emberlin — those that fled, those that were away — and I'm going to let them look at my face for as long as they need to, and say whatever they need to say, and ask whatever they need to ask, and I am going to *answer*. Not as a king. Kings deflect. As a man paying what can be paid of a debt that can't." He straightened. "And then, when they're finished with me — if they're ever finished with me — I'll submit myself to whatever judgment the new assembly devises. Memory-work, exile, the block. Their choice, not mine. I've had twenty years of choices made inside my body without my consent. I'll not make a habit of returning the favor."

* * *

He walked out of the palace alone.

There were guards at the gates who did not know whether to salute him or bar his way — he saw the confusion move across their young faces, loyalty and horror wrestling — and he solved it for them by simply nodding, the way one nods to strangers, and passing through. Behind him the citadel rose against the bruised sky, and he did not look back, because looking back was a habit of kings and he had resigned the habit along with the rest.

The streets of Vaelmoor were coming back to life the way a body comes back after a long fever — tentatively, painfully, one function at a time. Bakers had lit their ovens. Someone had strung a line of washing between two gutted houses in the east quarter, absurd and defiant, shirts drying in the ash-fall like flags of a country not yet named. Children ran past him chasing a dog, and one of them — a girl, seven or eight, gap-toothed — stopped dead in the street and stared at him with the frank, merciless stare of the young.

"That's him," she whispered to her friend. "That's the king what got ate."

"Yes," Aldric said. He crouched down, joints protesting, until his eyes were level with hers. "It's me. The eating's over."

"Did it hurt?"

He considered lying, decided that lies were the one currency he had devalued past recovery, and said: "More than anything. And it hurt other people far worse than it hurt me. That's the part I have to fix now, or try."

The girl absorbed this with the gravity of a magistrate, then nodded once, sharply, and ran on after the dog. Out of the mouths of the city's smallest citizens: a verdict more merciful and more damning than any tribunal could render. *Go and fix it, then.*

He walked on toward the east quarter. Toward the names. Toward the long, unglamorous labor of a guilt that could never be discharged, only honored — paid down year by year like a debt to the dead, knowing the principal would never touch zero, and doing it anyway, because the alternative was the other thing, the easy thing, the hollow thing, and he had spent twenty years as a hollow thing already and had no appetite for a second term.

At the edge of the burned quarter he paused. On a charred doorpost someone had chalked, in a child's unsteady hand, three words:

*WE REMEMBER US.*

He read it twice. Then he took out the knife he'd carried since the tunnels, and beneath it, in the careful script of a man who had relearned his letters only yesterday, he added a fourth word:

*I know.*

Behind him, far up the mountain, in the tower rooms where the new keepers kept their vigil, a light was burning. He had heard, on his way down, the servants whispering about the tall scarred woman who woke each morning not knowing the face of the child she loved — and about how the child brought her a small glass vial every evening at dusk, and how the woman drank, and how sometimes she wept at nothing, and sometimes laughed, and how the servants had begun, without anyone ordering it, to call those hours the remembering.

Aldric looked at the tower light a long moment.

Then he turned and walked into the ashes, to learn the names, beginning with the first house on the left.
