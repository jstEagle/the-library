## 34. The Forgetting Sick

Astrid woke to the taste of iron and the smell of rain that had not yet fallen.

She knew the ceiling above her — grey stone, veined with pale light, the work of hands long dead — but she did not know its name. She knew she was lying in a bed of woven mountain-grass in a chamber somewhere beneath Mount Cael, and that her body ached the way a body aches after it has been emptied and refilled with something colder. She knew, with the absolute certainty of bone rather than thought, that they had won.

She did not know how.

That was the first gap. It opened beneath her like a well shaft, and she lay very still at its edge, testing it the way a child tests river ice. *The Vessel.* The name surfaced whole, slick and terrible. *The crown. Aldric. The rebinding.* Those she had. But between the moment she had knelt in the vault beneath the throne and this quiet room lit by sigil-light, there was simply nothing — a corridor in her mind with every door sealed and no memory of walking through it.

Astrid Kavanagh had spent twenty years mapping other people's minds. She knew what a cut memory looked like from the inside: the smooth absence, the way the mind grew scar tissue over the wound and told itself stories about why the road ended there. She had left those scars in a hundred wealthy skulls across Greyhollow and never once lost sleep over them.

Now she lay in one of her own making, and understood, finally and completely, what she had sold all those years.

*Every stolen memory must be returned or given.*

The words came back to her like a debt collector. She remembered the vault — Ysolde's map spread across her knees, Serane standing guard with her blade turned at last toward Vaelmoor, Wren's small hand gripping hers — and she remembered beginning. That was all. Beginning. Pouring herself out vial by vial into the lattice of light, feeling each stolen face dissolve off her tongue like sugar: the merchant Halvoric laughing at his daughter's wedding, the duelist's last sunrise, the countess weeping over a letter she could not remember writing. Hundreds of them. Years of them. A city's worth of borrowed living, flowing out of her and into the mountain, and the lattice drinking it down and glowing brighter, and her own edges going thin as paper held up to a lamp—

And then the doors. All of them. Sealed.

Astrid sat up too fast and the world tilted. She caught herself on the wall, and the stone was warm under her palm, faintly pulsing, alive the way a sleeping animal is alive. Somewhere far below, she could feel it — not hear it, *feel* it — the great slow heartbeat of the rebound sigil, thrumming through the roots of Cael like blood through a wrist. Whole again. Bound again. The passes sealed against whatever waited beyond them.

They had won. She was sure of it the way you are sure of your own name.

Except she reached for her name and found only the shape of it, an impression in snow. *Astrid.* Yes. That much remained; the sigil had taken everything except the things that were hers by birth — or perhaps it had simply needed something to carry the rest home in. But around that single word, the architecture of her life stood hollowed. Her mother's face: gone, though the grief for it remained, vast and sourceless, a tide without a moon. Greyhollow's streets: gone, though she knew she had walked them for two decades. The coat with the letter in its lining. Ferro's laugh. Ysolde's bitter tears at the threshold. The false mother — the one stolen memory she had replayed every night of her adult life, the woman braiding a child's hair in a sunlit kitchen, the warmth Astrid had rented nightly because she could never afford to own it.

Gone. Given. Poured into the mountain along with everything else, and the mountain was warmer for it, and Astrid sat alone in a cold room unable even to remember what she was mourning.

"That," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on the second word, "is a filthy price."

"It's called the Forgetting Sick." The voice came from the doorway — low, dry, familiar in the way a scar is familiar. "The smith-archive has records of it. Survivors of the old bindings used to write about it. They said the first week is the worst."

Serane Voss stood leaning against the doorframe, one arm bound in fresh linen, her inquisitor's coat exchanged for plain wool that made her look younger and more exhausted than any uniform ever had. Her eyes were still glassy — they would always be glassy now, Astrid understood; some prices wrote themselves permanently on the face — but behind the glass there was something new. Something that looked, cautiously, like a person.

"You're alive," Astrid said.

"Thanks to you. Thanks to her." Serane did not say the name. Astrid noticed that, and noticed that she noticed it, and the noticing opened a second well shaft in her mind, wider than the first. "You don't know who I mean," Serane said quietly. It wasn't cruel. It was careful, the way you speak to someone standing at the edge of ice.

"I know I'd—" Astrid stopped. Started again. "There's someone. There's a shape in my chest where a person should be, and when I try to look at it directly my heart goes like a hammer, and I don't have a face to put on it. I don't have a *name*. I just know—" Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the warm stone. "I know that if anything in this mountain threatened her, I would tear it apart with my teeth. I know that the way I know which hand is mine. And I don't know her face, Serane. I don't know her voice. I don't know if she's tall or small or—"

The floor creaked.

Astrid turned, and a girl was standing in the doorway behind Serane — a girl of perhaps twelve winters, dark-haired, sharp-eyed, wearing a coat three sizes too big for her with the sleeves rolled back again and again. Mud still ghosted the hem, though the mud itself had surely washed away days ago. She was holding a shard of black glass against her sternum the way another child might hold a talisman, and it hummed, faintly, in time with the heartbeat of the mountain.

Astrid looked at her and felt the shape in her chest flare so bright it hurt — love with no address, devotion with no story, a whole house burning with every door locked from the outside.

"I'd die for you," Astrid said, before she could stop herself. "I'm sorry. I don't know why I said that. I don't know your face. I've never seen you before in my life."

The girl's face crumpled — not into tears, though tears were clearly close, but into something older and more disciplined, the expression of someone who had already grieved this once and steeled herself to do it again. She took one step into the room. Then another. She moved like someone approaching a frightened animal, or a shrine.

"You gave everything," the girl said. Her voice was steady by force of will alone. "Every memory you ever stole. Every memory you ever made, near enough. You poured yourself into the lattice until the smiths' ghosts said you'd go past the point of no return, and then—" her breath hitched, and she mastered it, "—and then I stepped into the circuit and split it. Half of you is in me, Astrid. Half of you has been in me since before you woke up. So you don't get to apologize for not knowing my face. You paid for my face. You paid for everyone's face."

"I don't remember paying."

"No," the girl said softly. "You wouldn't."

A silence filled the room, thick as floodwater. Astrid became aware that she was crying — not dramatically, not the clean grief of funerals, but the stupid leaking of a body overwhelmed, tears running hot down her face while her expression tried to remain composed and failed utterly.

"The Forgetting Sick," Serane murmured from the doorway. "The records say the memories aren't destroyed. They're *held*. In the lattice, or in whoever carries them. The old texts called the carriers 'vessels of return.' Some survivors got pieces back over years. Some never did. Some went mad trying to claw them out early." She paused. "One smith wrote that the only cure was patience. Another wrote that the only cure was being loved by whoever held your missing years, and that the second cure was worse, because it meant waiting on someone else's kindness."

The girl flinched at that. Astrid saw it — saw the guilt move through the young face like weather — and understood it without understanding its cause. This child carried half of her. This child could give her back, piece by piece, and hadn't yet, and the reasons for that delay were written plainly enough in the fear behind her eyes.

"How much do you have?" Astrid asked her.

"All of it. Almost." The girl hugged the black shard tighter. "When I split it, I took the half closest to me. The recent things. The road, the tunnels, the vault. Everything since the night I knocked on your door." Her chin came up, defiant in the way of children defending the one thing they refuse to surrender. "I kept those. I'm keeping them. If you want them back, you'll have to take them from me, and I'll fight you, and I'll win, because I'm stubborn and you taught me that."

"Did I?" Astrid whispered.

"Everything I am that isn't the sigil," the girl said, "you taught me. Or you fed me. Or you lied to crown soldiers on my behalf while I hid under your bed shaking. So yes." A wet, furious little laugh escaped her. "*Yes*, you did."

"What's your name?"

The question landed on the girl like a blow. She actually staggered, caught herself against the wall, and for a moment Astrid watched her wrestle with something enormous — watched her decide, visibly, whether to answer honestly or spare them both.

"Wren," she said at last. "You named me. In a storm, in your attic, in Greyhollow. You said I looked like a bird that had flown into glass, and you said, 'Well, Wren, you can stay until you stop bleeding.'" Her eyes were blazing now, tears spilling freely and ignored. "I chose to keep it. Out of everything the sigil tried to make me be, I chose that name, and I will not let the Forgetting Sick take it from either of us. So you'll learn it again. You'll learn it the way you learn anything worth knowing — slowly, badly, and forever."

Astrid laughed. It came out broken, half sob, but it came out, and Wren's face changed at the sound of it — softened, steadied, as though that broken laugh was proof of something she'd been desperate to confirm.

"There she is," Wren whispered.

"I don't—" Astrid pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I don't know what you mean."

"You will." Wren crossed the room in three strides and climbed onto the bed beside her — uninvited, unhesitating, exactly the way Astrid suddenly, desperately hoped she always had — and tucked herself against Astrid's side with her head beneath Astrid's chin, fitting there as naturally as water finding its level. The black shard hummed between them. The mountain's heartbeat slowed, just slightly, as though listening. "You'll learn me the way I learned you. One day at a time. One story at a time. And when the gaps scare you — and they will, Astrid, they'll wake you at night screaming sometimes, the archive warned us — you'll put your hand here." She took Astrid's hand and pressed it flat against her own sternum, over the humming glass, over the place where half a person was stored like light in a lantern. "And you'll feel me carrying you. And you'll remember that you are not empty. You are *held*."

Astrid held her. She didn't know the child's face five minutes ago; now her arms knew the weight and angle of her as if they'd done this ten thousand times, and maybe they had, and the knowledge lived in muscle where the sickness couldn't reach. Her body remembered what her mind had lost. Perhaps that was the mercy buried in the price. Perhaps that was the whole point of it.

"Tell me something true," Astrid said into her hair. "Something from the part you kept."

Wren was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was very small and very certain.

"The night we met, you gave me soup you couldn't afford, and you pretended it was leftovers. It wasn't. You'd bought it that morning because you'd dreamed someone was coming." She swallowed. "You told me later you'd spent your whole life renting other people's memories of family because you couldn't remember having one. And then I knocked on your door, and you said—" her voice fractured, rebuilt itself, "—you said, 'Well, the mountain sends strange gifts,' and you let me in, and you never once made me feel like a gift you regretted opening."

Astrid wept for a stranger's kindness and knew, in the marrow-deep way beneath all the gaps, that the stranger was herself.

* * *

The days that followed taught Astrid the geography of her loss.

It was a country with borders. Some regions survived intact: her craft, astonishingly, remained — her fingers still knew the weight of a bone stylus, still knew the precise pressure that parted memory from mind without tearing, though when she picked up a stylus in the archive and examined it, she could not recall learning the skill, only that she possessed it, the way a bird possesses flight. Language mostly held. Names were the worst casualties — people especially, places nearly as bad. She could describe Greyhollow's skyline in perfect detail and could not have found her own attic in it.

Mornings were hardest. Dreams leaked through the seals at night, she discovered — not full memories but their residue, emotions detached from events, and she would wake drowning in terror or tenderness with no idea what had caused it. On the fourth morning she woke sobbing from a dream of a kitchen, sunlight, hands braiding hair, and sat clutching the bedframe while Wren held her and Serane stood uselessly in the doorway with a cup of tea, until the wave passed.

"A false memory," Wren explained afterward, gently. "One you stole from a client decades ago. A mother braiding her daughter's hair. You replayed it every night. It was the closest thing you had to—" She stopped herself.

"To a mother," Astrid finished.

"Yes."

"And I gave it away."

"You gave it to the mountain. Which means it's real now, in the way the mountain is real. Somewhere in the lattice, some part of the binding is woven out of a mother's love for a child that wasn't even hers." Wren smiled, watery and fierce. "You didn't lose it, Astrid. You *spent* it. There's a difference."

"There bloody well isn't," Astrid said, and Wren laughed, and the laugh did something to the room, warmed it, and Astrid thought: *I know that sound. My body knows that sound. Whatever else is sealed, that sound gets through.*

* * *

On the sixth day they climbed, slowly, to the smith-archive proper — a vaulted hall deep in Cael's roots where the dead smiths' journals lined the walls in niches of glowing stone. Serane walked ahead with a lamp, and Wren kept close to Astrid's side, and the dormant sigils along the passage brightened at Wren's passing like flowers turning toward sun.

Here, Astrid read the old accounts of the Forgetting Sick in her ancestors' cramped hands. *The carrier may restore what was given,* one wrote. *But restoration must be chosen, not seized. To seize is to unmake the bond. To wait is to trust.* Another, in a shakier script: *I waited nine years for my brother to return my wife's face to me. Nine years. It was worth every one.* And a third entry, unsigned, that made Astrid sit down hard on a bench:

*Do not ask the carrier to choose what to return first. That choice is theirs. It is the only thing they have left of the giving.*

She closed the journal. Wren was watching her from across the aisle, the black shard glinting at her throat, her expression carefully neutral in a way that told Astrid everything.

"You've been deciding," Astrid said. "Since I woke. Which ones to give back first. You've been carrying that choice around like a hot coal."

Wren's jaw tightened. "You read that just now."

"I read it just now. But I've known you for six days, and I already know what your thinking face looks like." Astrid crossed to her. "So don't tell me what you decided. Just—tell me when. Tell me you'll give them back someday, and I'll wait however long it takes, and I won't ask which comes first. Deal?"

For a long moment Wren said nothing. Then, slowly, she reached into the breast of her oversized coat and drew out a small object wrapped in oilcloth — a vial, stoppered with wax, its contents swirling with a faint gold luminescence that Astrid recognized with a jolt that went through her like lightning: memory-light. Living memory, decanted. She'd seen a thousand such vials. She'd *made* a thousand such vials.

"This one," Wren said, "I'm giving back today. Not because it's the most important. Because it's yours by right, and because I want you to have one true thing back before the doubts set in." She pressed it into Astrid's palm and folded Astrid's fingers over it. "Drink it slow. Don't fight it. Let it find its place."

"What is it?"

Wren's smile trembled at the edges. "The night we met."

* * *

The storm arrived before she did.

Memory returned the way a tide returns to a tidal pool — not all at once but in a rush that filled every channel: rain hammering the attic roof of her rooms above the chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane, the gutter overflowing, the candle she'd allowed herself burning low because tomorrow's client would pay in silver and tonight she could afford one candle and a bowl of stew she'd bought that morning for no reason she could name, only a dream she'd had of knocking, of a small fist on wood, of the mountain sending strange gifts.

Then the knock. Three small blows, almost lost under the thunder. She'd opened the door expecting debt collectors and found instead a mud-caked child with eyes like struck flint, clutching a shard of black glass that hummed against her sternum, speaking words in a language dead two hundred years — and Astrid, who had built her whole life on the principle that every knock was a trap, had stood aside and said, *well, the mountain sends strange gifts, come in before you drown.*

The soup. The lie about leftovers. The child falling asleep sitting upright in a chair because she didn't yet trust beds, and Astrid covering her with her own blanket and sitting awake until dawn watching her breathe, feeling something crack open in her chest that twenty years of competent loneliness had welded shut.

Astrid came back to herself kneeling on the archive floor, the empty vial in her hand, weeping without restraint while Wren gripped her shoulders and Serane pretended very hard to study a shelf.

"She knocked," Astrid gasped. "You knocked. In the storm. And I let you in." She pulled Wren against her and held on. "I remember letting you in. Oh, gods, I remember — I remember being afraid of you and doing it anyway. I remember the soup. I remember the blanket. I remember—" She pulled back, searching the young face above her, and this time, when she looked, the face had an anchor. Not the whole history — the gaps yawned on every side — but a beginning. A fixed point. "I remember you, Wren. Not everything. But the door. I remember the door."

Wren was crying too now, laughing through it, both hands pressed over Astrid's where they gripped her arms. "One," she said. "That's one. We'll go slow. One at a time, like you taught me — like you taught me everything. And the rest—" she glanced up at the glowing journals, at the patient dead, at the mountain holding its stolen-and-given treasures in its luminous veins, "—the rest will keep. The mountain keeps what it's given. And I keep what I'm carrying. Nothing's lost, Astrid. It's only waiting."

Outside the archive, far above, dawn was coming to Vaelmoor, and bells were ringing in the capital for the abdication of a king who could not remember his own crimes, and somewhere in the world a school would need building and a charter would need forging and a sentence would need serving. But all of that belonged to the hours after this one.

In this hour, Astrid Kavanagh — memory-cutter, sigil-heir, a woman with vast countries missing from her map — held an empty vial in one hand and a child in the other arm, and understood that she had traded a lifetime of rented memories for a single true one, and that the trade had been, by any measure she trusted, the best bargain of her life.

"One at a time," she agreed, into Wren's hair. "Starting tomorrow. And Wren—"

"Mm?"

"Tomorrow, you start with the tunnel where you sang. I want to know what my body does when I hear it."

Wren pulled back, eyes wide. "How did you know I kept that one next?"

Astrid smiled through the last of her tears, and felt, for the first time since waking, that the woman smiling was entirely, presently, her.

"Because you're my daughter," she said, "in every way that matters. And daughters are terrible at hiding what they're planning to give their mothers."

The sigils along the archive walls glowed a little brighter, and somewhere in the deep heart of Cael, the mountain turned over in its sleep — and began, patiently, the long work of giving back.
