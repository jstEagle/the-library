## 35. Relearning Her Own Hands

The vials lived in a cedar box that had once held sigil-smith's chalks, and Wren had carried it for three hundred and eleven days.

She knew the number because she counted the mornings. Every dawn since the rebinding, she woke in the grey light of Greyhollow and lay still with her palm flat against the box's lid, feeling the faint warmth of the memories inside — they were warm the way coals are warm long after the fire dies, the way a stone stays sun-touched into evening — and she told herself: *not yet. Not today.* And then one morning, three hundred and eleven mornings after Astrid poured herself into the lattice beneath Mount Cael and came back out the other side as a stranger wearing her own face, Wren lifted the lid and chose.

Not the first memory. The first was easy — anyone could have chosen the first. It was the second that mattered, and the seventh, and the fortieth, because each one built on the ones before it like stones in an arch, and if you set them wrong the whole thing came down on the person you loved.

Wren had been a fragment of a lattice of light once. She understood architecture. She understood load-bearing things.

She also understood, in the wordless way of someone who had spent her first life as part of something vast and her second life being small and carried, that Astrid would hate this. That Astrid Kavanagh, who had cut memories from dying merchants with a steady hand and sold strangers their own pasts in amber vials, who had faced down inquisitors and hollow kings and the hunger at the heart of the world without flinching — Astrid would rather have died on the mountain than sit in a chair and be handed herself back a spoonful at a time by a child she did not know.

That was why Wren hadn't asked. That was why she'd waited until Astrid left for the market, until the door below the attic stairs clicked shut, until the silence of the little rooms settled like snowfall. Then she took down the box, and she set it on the table between the two chairs, and she began to work.

* * *

The first vial she gave back was not a memory at all. It was hands.

She had thought about this for months. She'd rehearsed it against the inside of her own skull the way other children rehearsed apologies. Because when Astrid came home and found her sitting there with the box open, the first thing out of Astrid's mouth would not be *what are you doing* or *where did you get those* — it would be *I don't know you*, and Wren needed an answer ready that wasn't a wound.

So she didn't hand her a vial. She handed her a stylus.

It was Astrid's own bone stylus, worn smooth at the grip where ten thousand extractions had polished it, its tip carved into the fine spiral that only Kavanaghs used, the spiral Ysolde had wept over in Brackwater, the spiral that named Astrid heir to everything buried under Cael. Wren set it on the table between them and said, in the voice she had grown into — low, careful, hers —

"You don't remember me. But your hands do."

Astrid had stopped in the doorway with wet wool smell and market mud on her boots, and her face had done the thing it always did now, the flicker of polite blankness over grief, the mask she wore for the strange child who lived in her attic and would not explain herself. But then her eyes dropped to the stylus, and Wren watched it happen — watched the hand rise before the mind agreed to it, watched fingers find the worn groove of the grip and settle into it like a bird onto its nest-branch, watched Astrid turn the tool over twice and stop, breathing strangely, because her thumb knew exactly where every ridge was.

"What is this," Astrid said, but her voice had changed. It wasn't a question anymore. It was the sound of a door opening in a house the owner thought empty.

"Your trade," Wren said. "Your craft. I'm giving it back first, because everything else needs it." She slid the first vial across the wood — a slim amber thing no longer than her finger, glowing faintly, holding the memory of the first extraction Astrid ever performed alone, aged nineteen, on a smuggler with a gut-wound in Ferro's tollhouse, the way the man had gripped her wrist and said *gentle, girl, gentle, it's the last of me that's any good* — and the way she had been gentle. "You'll need to know how to receive. Nobody taught you receiving. You only ever learned taking."

Astrid looked at the vial. Then at her own hands, which were still curled around nothing, remembering the stylus. Then at Wren, with an expression that Wren had never seen on her before — because the Astrid who owned this expression had poured it away under the mountain, along with every stolen road and borrowed face and the false mother she'd replayed nightly in a rented room, and what walked around now was the same woman rebuilt from the lattice's memory plus whatever the mountain itself remembered of her, which was apparently very little and very hard.

"Who are you?" Astrid asked. Not cruelly. Wearyly. The way you ask a question you've asked three hundred times hoping the answer changes.

"You told me my name," Wren said. "You gave it to me in a storm, at your door, when every instinct you had said shut it and lock it. You said I looked like a wren — small and brown and louder than anything my size should be." She pushed the vial another inch closer. "That's in here too. Not this vial. Later. I'm saving it."

"For what?"

"For when you can bear it."

* * *

Astrid drank the first vial standing up, defiant about it, as if drinking it quickly might make it cost less. Wren watched the amber light go through her — saw it settle in her shoulders first, then her hands, then behind her eyes — and after a long moment Astrid sat down slowly in the chair, staring at the stylus, turning it, and a laugh came out of her that cracked in the middle.

"Gentle," she murmured. "He said gentle." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I remember his grip. I don't remember his face."

"That one's in a different vial," Wren said. "His face comes with the tollhouse. With Ferro. With the debt he owed your mother. They're all braided together — you can't give someone the thread without the weaving." She kept her voice level. Inside, she was screaming with the effort of it, because she had carried these memories for nearly a year and some of them were so heavy she'd had to sleep with the box under her hand just to bear the weight of knowing what was in it, and now she was handing them back one at a time and watching Astrid flinch, and every flinch was a small knife. Tenderness, she was learning, was not a feeling. It was labor. It was lifting and carrying and setting down carefully, over and over, until your arms shook.

"How many?" Astrid asked.

"In the box? Two hundred and six." Wren hesitated. "There are more than that missing. Some went into the lattice permanently. The mountain keeps them now. Your name among them."

"My name." Astrid said it flatly, testing the absence. "So what do I call myself?"

"The school calls you the Keeper. Serane calls you Smith-Kavanagh, when she bothers with titles. Aldric calls you—" Wren almost smiled. "He calls you 'the woman who unbound me,' which is wrong, but he's old and stubborn and grieving, so we let him. And I call you—" She stopped.

Astrid waited.

"I call you Astrid," Wren said finally. "Even though you don't. Because you'll want it back someday, and names keep better than most things. Like bread in frost."

* * *

They fell into a rhythm over the following weeks, and the rhythm became a ritual, and the ritual became the shape of their days.

Each evening, after the school's lanterns went dark and the last student clattered down the stairs, Astrid would climb to the attic and sit in the chair by the window — the good chair, the one Wren had dragged up three flights and positioned so the light fell right — and Wren would open the box and choose.

Choosing was the work. Anyone could pour. Choosing was where the tenderness lived.

Some nights she gave pain early, while Astrid was strong: the night of the storm at the door, the mud and thunder and the shard of black glass humming in a small fist; the flight down the smuggler's spine with ice on the switchbacks; the fight at the river crossing, and the three memories torn bleeding out of Serane Voss mid-blade-stroke. She gave those early because she'd learned that Astrid's courage worked like a muscle — exercised at dusk, it held all night — and because those memories contained Wren herself, contained the moment Astrid had claimed her before the ghost-smiths at the gate (*she's mine, not yours*), and Wren wanted that claim returned while the daylight was still in the room, wanted Astrid to relearn it with her eyes open rather than wake to it at 3 a.m. wondering who this brown-haired girl was and why her heart insisted on protecting her.

Other nights she gave sweetness, and those were harder to watch, somehow. The false mother — the single stolen memory Astrid had replayed nightly for years, a stranger's hearth and a stranger's hand smoothing her hair, purchased from a dying woman and kept like contraband — Wren saved that one for a night when rain hit the windows, because she'd felt the memory's texture from inside the box for a year and she knew it was rain-shaped. When Astrid surfaced from it, she was crying without noise, the way people cry who learned young that noise brought questions.

"I mourned her already," Astrid said. "On the mountain, Ysolde took it as payment, and I grieved, and I thought that was the end of it. But having it back—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum. "It's like a letter arriving after the funeral. You're glad and it hurts worse."

"You can refuse the next one," Wren offered, as she offered every night. "Any of them. You can refuse all of them. The box is yours."

"No." Astrid wiped her face with a motion that was almost anger. "No. If I'm going to be rebuilt, I'll be rebuilt by choice. All of it. Even the ugly ones." She paused. "Especially the ugly ones. What did I ever steal that wasn't somebody's worst day or best day?"

Wren didn't answer. She reached into the box and drew out a vial that glowed a deep, uneasy violet, and set it down, and watched Astrid recognize the color before she recognized the content — because the body remembers ahead of the mind, that was the whole doctrine of the new charter, that was what Astrid taught her students without yet remembering she'd learned it in Ferro's kitchen over stew.

"Serane's childhood," Astrid said quietly.

"She asked me to give you that one second-to-last," Wren said. "After the mountain. Before—" She touched the final vial, the one wrapped in cloth at the bottom of the box, the one she had not yet decided about. "She said you'd earned the right to know what you saw in her. And she said — her words — *tell her I was a lonely kid and I got bought, and if the Keeper's new order means anything, mean it for kids like me.*"

Astrid was silent a long time. Outside, Greyhollow chimed the ninth hour, and somewhere below a student laughed at a joke Wren couldn't hear.

"Then we'll drink it together," Astrid said at last. "Some memories shouldn't be carried alone. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? That's what you did under the mountain."

Wren's throat closed. She nodded, and poured two cups of the amber-and-violet light, and they sat shoulder to shoulder in the lamplight and let a broken inquisitor's nine-year-old self walk through both their hearts at once, hand in invisible hand.

* * *

The box grew lighter. That was the trouble with giving things back — the giver got lighter too, and lightness, Wren discovered, could ache like a wound.

She had carried the memories so long they'd grown roots in her. She knew Astrid's whole past the way she knew the lattice under Cael: structurally, intimately, load by load. She knew which roads Astrid loved (the salt road above Brackwater, wind off the water), which lies she was proudest of (the one told to Serane Voss in a doorway, delivered with a smile that should have won awards), which customer she'd secretly refunded (a widow buying her husband's last day, overcharged by a broker Astrid despised — Astrid had slipped the difference back into the woman's purse with the stylus, a sleight of hand worthy of her mother). Two hundred small weights, and now they were leaving her one vial at a time, and what remained in her was just — Wren. Small and brown and louder than her size, sitting in an attic with a cedar box growing emptier every night.

On the two hundred and fourth evening, Astrid caught her crying.

Not dramatically. Wren was reaching into the box for the next vial and her hand simply stopped moving, and the tears arrived the way weather arrives, and Astrid — who now remembered the storm, remembered the door, remembered every version of herself that had ever opened a door — crossed the room and knelt beside the chair and put her arms around her.

"I know what you're afraid of," Astrid said into her hair. "I remember it now. I remember everything. Including what you haven't given me yet." She pulled back and looked at Wren with eyes that were fully, dangerously, entirely her own again. "The last vial. The one you've been skipping. Night after night. You think I haven't noticed the gap in the sequence?"

"It's not ready," Wren said.

"It's been ready for months. *You're* not ready." Astrid's voice gentled. "What is it?"

Wren looked at the cloth-wrapped vial. Her hand hovered over it and withdrew.

"It's the night we met," she said. "From my side. My memory, not yours. What I felt when you opened the door." She swallowed. "You've had yours back for weeks. You know why you let me in — pity, instinct, the thing your mother's blood does, opening doors. But mine is different. Mine is the first thing I ever owned. Before that I was a fragment, a designation, a piece of a lattice. That night is the first moment I was *someone*, because someone looked at me and decided I was worth getting wet for." Her voice frayed. "And if I give it to you, then it exists outside me. And then the box is empty. And then—"

"Then you're not the carrier anymore," Astrid finished softly. "You're just my daughter."

The word landed between them like a struck bell.

They had never said it. Not in the year since the mountain, not in all the vials and rituals and shared cups of other people's grief. It had lived in the unwritten spaces — in the chair placed by the window, in the seat kept for her at the school, in the way Astrid said *we* when she spoke of the charter's future. But the lattice had made them what it had made them, split burden and shared blood-of-the-craft and a bond no smith-charter had words for, and Astrid had just reached into that space and named it aloud, casually, the way she did everything brave.

"I—" Wren started.

"Don't answer tonight," Astrid said, rising, retrieving her coat from its hook — her mother's old coat, the one with the letter in the lining, the coat she wore to market every week now like armor made of inheritance. "Answer tomorrow. I'm late, and Ysolde will lecture me, and you know how she lectures." At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and looked back with an expression Wren recognized from a vial she'd carried for a year — the look Astrid wore when she was about to do something reckless and kind. "For what it's worth — the night we met isn't the last thing in the box, Wren. Look again. Under the lining."

The door closed. Footsteps descended. And Wren, heart hammering, turned the cedar box over and peeled back its worn velvet lining — and found, sewn into the wood beneath where no vial should fit, a flat folded paper she had never placed there, in handwriting she knew from the archive journals, from the letter in the coat, from two hundred years of dead smiths' patient records:

Her mother's hand. Astrid's mother's hand. Writing to a granddaughter she would never meet:

*If you are reading this, the binding held, and someone small and impossible is carrying the rest of us home. Keep one thing back from the returning — one memory, any memory, yours to keep forever. Even smiths deserve a private country. Choose well, little wren. And when the last vial is given, come to Cael. The mountain has something for you both.*

Below it, a map. Not of roads — of the deep tunnels, the road beneath the road, drawn in sigil-light that was even now beginning to glow faintly against the dark paper, warming like a coal, like a door left open.

Like an invitation.

Wren sat for a long time in the lamplight with the paper trembling in her hands, listening to the rain begin against the attic glass — the same sound, she realized, the same exact sound, as the night a stranger opened a door and changed everything.

Tomorrow she would answer. Tonight she would choose what to keep.

And the sigil-map glowed on, patient as mountains, waiting for morning.
