## 37. Serane's Sentence

The cell they gave her in Greyhollow had a window.

That was the first mercy, and it nearly undid her more than any blade could have. Serane Voss had spent eleven years in the crown's service sleeping in rooms without windows — barracks, waystations, the black dormitories beneath Vaelmoor where the inquisitors were kept like tools in a case, polished and shelved and never asked what they dreamed about. A window meant the people who built this room had considered that a person might want to look out of it. She had not been considered a person for so long that she had almost forgotten what it felt like to be one.

She sat on the narrow bed with her back against the stone wall and watched the light move across the floor, and she did not sleep, because she was afraid of what she would find waiting behind her eyelids.

Her memories had come back. That was the cruelty no one had warned her about — that redemption, if this was redemption, would feel exactly like drowning in reverse. For years she had been hollow in a way she'd mistaken for discipline: clean, efficient, unburdened. The crown had taken her childhood when she was nineteen — sold, actually, she had signed the papers herself with a flourish she still remembered, still *felt*, the scratch of the quill and the clerk's approving nod — and what remained had been smooth and empty and perfectly suited to carrying out orders without flinching. She had thought that was strength. She had thought the ache she sometimes felt at dusk, an ache with no name attached to it, was just fatigue.

Then Wren had touched her, there on the riverbank with iron manacles already closing around the child's thin wrists, and the floodgate had opened, and Serane had spent three days on her knees in the mud weeping over a girl named Lissa Voss who had loved raspberries and her father's off-key singing and a wooden horse with one ear chewed off by a dog, a girl whose own mother had traded her memories away to pay a debt, a girl who had grown up into a woman with no childhood at all and had called that woman *promoted*.

Lissa had liked raspberries. Serane had eaten them once, two months ago, in Brackwater, from a street vendor's cart, and had cried into her sleeve while the vendor pretended very hard not to notice.

So no, she did not sleep. She sat with the window and the light and the whole ruined landscape of her returned past spread out inside her like a battlefield after the fighting stopped, and she waited to learn what the new order intended to do with her.

* * *

They came for her on the fourth morning. Not soldiers — she had expected soldiers, had braced for the tribunal, the black hoods, the long walk to whatever wall they still shot people against in this half-broken kingdom. Instead it was Astrid Kavanagh, alone, carrying two tin cups and a paper-wrapped bundle that smelled of honey bread.

Serane stood. It was reflex; you stood when your superior entered, and some traitorous part of her had decided somewhere between the river and now that Astrid Kavanagh was her superior, or at least her better, which amounted to the same thing in the arithmetic of guilt.

"Sit down," Astrid said. "You look terrible."

"You look worse." It came out before she could stop it, old habit, the fencing of two women who had spent their lives making words do violence. But Astrid only snorted and handed her a cup, and the tea inside was bitter and oversteeped and real, and Serane held it in both hands like something that might be taken away.

Astrid lowered herself onto the stool across from the bed. Up close, the changes in her were harder to ignore. Everyone in Greyhollow spoke of what Astrid had paid at Cael — the memories poured into the sigil like wine into a thirsty god, the translucence, the weeks afterward when she hadn't known her own hands. She had them back now, most of them, returned vial by vial by a girl who had chosen to carry half a stranger's life rather than let her be erased entirely. But there were gaps. Serane could see them the way she'd been trained to see gaps in anyone: the small hesitations, the places where a person reached for something familiar and found a wall instead. Astrid's left hand kept drifting to her wrist, touching it as though confirming it was hers.

"I'm not here for the tribunal," Astrid said. "There isn't going to be one. Not for you."

Serane set down the cup. Her mouth had gone dry. "Then there should be. I burned Hallowmere." The ward-village — no. No, that had been the Vessel, wearing Aldric's hands. But she had burned others. She made herself say it plainly, because she had earned the right to nothing soft. "I burned Ferrowick. I pulled the memory from a woman in Dell's Crossing while her son watched, because the boy had seen sigil-light in the hills and my orders were to leave no witnesses with working recollections. I hunted a child across half a kingdom for the crime of existing. If the new charter means anything—"

"It means we don't kill people for what was done to them," Astrid said. "That's rather the entire point."

"The charter also ends the Purge," Serane said. "The Purge executed smiths. My family wasn't smiths. We were nobody. We were poor, and my mother owed money to men who broke things when they weren't paid, and the crown offered—" She stopped. The papers. The quill. The clerk's approving nod. "I signed."

"Yes," Astrid said quietly. "You did. And then you spent eleven years being very good at a job designed to hollow people out, including yourself. And then you opened the palace gates." She leaned forward. "Wren told me what happened on the riverbank. What you saw. What you chose after. You turned your blade toward Vaelmoor while your own people were hunting you. Don't tell me you don't know why you're sitting in a cell with a window instead of a grave."

Serane looked at the window. The light had climbed the wall; somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones, and a vendor was calling prices in the flat Greyhollow drawl, and the world went on doing its ordinary business around the fact of her.

"What happens to me, then?" she asked. "If not a tribunal."

"That depends on you." Astrid unwrapped the honey bread and set it on the bed between them like an offering at a shrine, which was, Serane supposed, exactly what it was. "The charter has need of someone with your particular education. Memory-work isn't just cutting and selling — that's the butcher's version, the trade I ran, gods forgive me. Done properly, done *lawfully*, it can mend things. Trauma that won't scar over. Memories that fester. People who came out of the Vessel's court with holes in them shaped like other people." She paused. "It requires precision. It requires knowing exactly how much a mind can bear before it breaks. And it requires someone who understands, down in her bones, what it costs to take a memory from a person who didn't consent."

"You want me to practice the trade that destroyed me."

"I want you to practice the trade that destroyed you," Astrid said, "*better*. With rules. With consent. With oversight. The charter ratified yesterday — nine keepers, elected, not inherited. Ysolde is one of them, gods help us all. There's a seat open." Astrid's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "Nobody else applied. Apparently everyone with a conscience finds the work unbearable, and everyone without one is dead or in prison, and I'm told I can't hold the seat myself because I'd have to give up the school, and I refuse."

Serane stared at her. "You're offering me a position. On the governing body of the order my king used to hunt your family to extinction."

"I'm offering you work," Astrid corrected. "Hard, ugly, necessary work that will make you face every one of those memories you got back, every day, in the service of people who'll hate you on sight. Half the kingdom wants you hanged. The other half wants you forgotten. This way, neither gets what they want, and Thornfell gets something it actually needs." She picked up her tea. "You'd live under sanction. Any misuse of the craft — any cutting without documented consent, any taking — and the sentence reverts. There are cells without windows for that."

"And if I say no? If I ask for the tribunal instead?"

Astrid looked at her for a long moment, and Serane recognized the look, because she'd worn it herself in interrogation rooms across three provinces: the look of someone measuring not whether a subject was lying but how much truth the subject could survive hearing.

"If you ask for the tribunal, you'll get one," Astrid said. "And I suspect you'd get a verdict that satisfied everyone except you. Justice likes clean endings. But you don't get to end, Serane. That's the sentence. You get to *continue*." She rose, brushed crumbs from her coat — Maren Kavanagh's coat, Serane realized, the dead mother's coat, worn now like armor — and moved toward the door. Then she paused, hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "the first thing Wren showed me from your memories — the ones I took during our fight at the crossing, before all this — wasn't the burning or the hunts. It was a girl hiding under a stairwell with her fingers in her ears while her parents screamed at each other about money. I've thought about that girl more times than I expected to." Now she turned, and her eyes were steady and tired and without pity, which was somehow the kindest thing anyone had given Serane in years. "Think about the offer. The bread's for whoever comes next; I know you won't eat while I'm watching."

She left. Serane sat with the cooling tea and the untouched bread and the window full of ordinary light, and understood, slowly, the way one understands a wound's true shape only after the numbness fades, that she had just been sentenced to the one punishment she had never prepared for.

She had been sentenced to a future.

* * *

She said yes. Of course she said yes. There had never really been a question, only the time it took her pride to die quietly in a corner, which took about six hours.

They installed her in a narrow office in the lower level of the school — Astrid's school now, though it had been a counting-house in another life, and before that, if the ledgers were honest, a smuggler's drop that Ferro had run before he gave his memories away at a tollhouse and forgot why he'd ever trusted anyone. His chair was still there, actually. Serane sat in it her first day and felt the ghost of the old man's laughter in the wood, and decided she would earn the right to it or break it trying.

The work was everything Astrid had promised and worse. The first client was a woman from Hallowmere — *Hallowmere*, of all the ash-choked names in the world — who had survived the Vessel's feeding and come out the other side unable to remember her husband's face, though she remembered everyone else's. The grief had curdled into something that kept her from sleeping, from eating, from living in any way that deserved the word. Under the old regime, Serane knew exactly what would have been prescribed: cut the grief out, leave a tidy obedient citizen with a hole where her love had been. She had performed such surgeries dozens of times. She could still feel the bone stylus' balance in her hand, the little professional click as it found the seam.

Instead she sat across from the widow for four hours and did nothing but listen, and map, and ask questions that must have felt like knives — *tell me about the last morning, tell me what his hands were doing* — and at the end of it she had not cut anything at all. She had built a bridge. A small, careful lattice of association anchored in memories that remained, so that the husband's face could be reached again sideways, through the smell of his shaving soap and the crooked way he hung his coat, until the direct road healed enough to walk on its own.

The woman wept when it worked. She clasped Serane's hands and thanked her, and then she saw the scar on Serane's collarbone — the mark of the crown's service, the brand every inquisitor wore — and her face closed like a door, and she left without another word.

Serane sat alone in Ferro's chair and found, to her genuine surprise, that she was smiling.

This was the shape of the sentence, she came to understand. Not the sanctioned status, not the oversight, not even the daily confrontation with her own history. The shape of it was this: she would help, and she would not be forgiven for it, and both of those things would be true forever, and she would have to carry them together the way Wren carried half of Astrid's stolen life — willingly, knowingly, because the alternative was a lie.

Some nights she worked until the lamps burned out. Some nights she walked up through Greyhollow's crooked streets to the schoolhouse attic, where Astrid taught the first generation of lawful memory-keepers, and stood in the doorway listening to her drill them on consent protocols, on the ethics of the seam, on the difference between mending and theft. Astrid always pretended not to see her. Then, one evening in late autumn, she tossed her a piece of chalk and said, "Well? You're the only one here who's ever seen the craft used as a weapon. Get up here and show them what it looks like, so they know what they're sworn against."

Serane walked to the front of the room on legs that shook, faced thirty young faces that watched her the way one watches a wolf that has, inexplicably, lain down by the fire, and began to teach.

* * *

Winter came early to the passes. The first snow fell on a grey afternoon while Serane was reviewing a case file — a soldier from Aldric's old garrison who couldn't stop reliving ninety seconds he'd spent possessed, his own mouth saying things he'd never chosen — when a knock came at her office door.

Not Astrid's knock. Not a student's tentative tap. Three slow knocks, evenly spaced, deliberate.

"Enter."

The door opened on Wren.

The girl was fourteen now, or passed for it; it was difficult to say with creatures who had once been a lattice of light beneath a mountain. She had grown tall in the year since Cael, and quiet in a way that was not silence but *attention* — she listened to rooms the way other people listened to music. The sigil-light had faded from her veins, mostly, though on certain days Serane had heard reports of it: faint gold tracery along her forearms when she stood too near the mountain's heart, like frost forming in reverse.

She had never once come to see Serane before. They had met exactly twice since the riverbank — once at the formal reading of the charter, where Wren had studied her across the hall with that vast, unreadable patience, and once by accident on a staircase, where Wren had stepped aside to let her pass and said nothing at all.

"Warden Voss." The title was the new one — keeper-warden, third seat, sanctioned practitioner — and hearing it from this particular mouth made Serane's spine straighten involuntarily.

"Lady Wren." There was no protocol for what to call her. *Lady* had been Astrid's joke and had stuck, the way true things often arrive dressed as jokes.

Wren closed the door behind her and stood there a moment, snow melting in her dark hair, looking at the office — at the files, the stylus rack with its lawful, registered instruments, the window Serane had insisted on when they built the room. Then her gaze settled on Serane's desk, on the case file lying open, and something moved through her expression like weather.

"You're giving them back," Wren said. "The memories. To the broken ones. One careful stitch at a time."

"I'm helping them reach what's theirs. It's different."

"It isn't, though." Wren crossed the room and laid something on the desk beside the file. Small. Wrapped in grey cloth. Humming — very softly, very low, a frequency Serane felt in her teeth rather than heard, and her blood went cold, because she knew that hum. Every inquisitor who had ever served the crown knew that hum. Black glass. Sigil-shard. The thing the whole war had orbited like a wound around a knife.

But the shard on her desk was no larger than a coin, and it did not hunger. It simply glowed, faintly, from within, like a coal remembering fire.

"A fragment the mountain gave back," Wren said. "One of many. We've been gathering them — Astrid and I. Returning them to the deep places where they belong." She looked up, and her eyes held that layered depth that still unnerved Serane, as though several ages were watching through one pair of pupils. "This one doesn't belong in the deep. It belongs here. In this room. With the work you're doing."

"I don't understand."

"You will." Wren moved toward the door, then paused with her hand on the frame — and Serane thought, absurdly, of Astrid in this same doorway months ago, and wondered if the pose was contagious. "The soldier in your file. Aldric's garrison. He's not the only one from the hollow court who's asking for help. Word travels. There are others coming, Warden Voss. Courtiers. Guards. People who wore the Vessel's leash and remember what their hands did. Dozens of them. Maybe hundreds." The snow-light caught her face. "They won't trust the school. They won't trust the charter. Most of them won't even come through the front door."

"Then how—"

"They'll come to the one person alive who knows exactly what it feels like to have been the crown's instrument." Wren's voice was gentle, and that gentleness was the most terrifying thing in the room. "The mountain remembers what you did. It also remembers what you chose. It sent me to tell you the second one weighs more." A pause. "Also, Astrid says dinner's at seven, and you're expected, and if you bring your excuses instead of yourself again she's assigning you the Brackwater ledger audit."

Despite everything — despite the shard humming on her desk like a small patient heart, despite the hundreds of haunted strangers marching toward her office through the snow — Serane laughed. It startled her, the sound, rough and rusty, a hinge long unused.

"Tell her I'll be there."

Wren nodded once, opened the door onto the falling snow — and stopped. Turned back. For a moment the girl looked at her with an expression Serane couldn't parse, ancient and young at once, and when she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"When I touched you on the riverbank, I didn't just show you your childhood. I saw all of it. Everything the crown took, and everything it left." Her gaze dropped, briefly, to the black-glass shard glowing on the desk. "There's one memory you haven't opened yet. You know the one. You've kept it wrapped in grey cloth in the bottom drawer for five months, and you tell yourself it's because you're not ready." The sigil-faint light flickered along her wrists, gold as dawn. "It's time, Serane. Your mother left it for you. And she's not the only one waiting on the other side of it."

The door closed. The snow fell. And Serane Voss sat alone in the lamplight with the shard humming its low patient note, staring at the bottom drawer of her desk, where for five months something had been waiting for her with her mother's name on it.
