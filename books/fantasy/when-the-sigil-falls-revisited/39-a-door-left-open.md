## 39. A Door Left Open

The rain came down Greyhollow like a verdict.

Astrid stood at the tall window of the old counting-house on Vellum Street and watched it fall — the same grey, needling rain that had fallen a year ago tonight, when a mud-caked child had hammered at her attic door and changed the shape of her life. She had learned since then that weather remembered. That stone remembered. That everything remembered, if you knew how to listen, and listening was now her trade — not cutting, not selling, but teaching others to hold what they held more gently.

Below her, lamplighters were making their rounds along the canal, flames cupped against the wet. The school's students would be settling into the dormitory wing by now: eleven of them this term, ranging from a fisherman's daughter who could taste other people's griefs in salt water to a former crown archivist who had come to unlearn the habit of hoarding. Eleven keepers-in-training. Ysolde would have called them an absurdity — memory-work passed hand to hand like bread instead of hoarded like gold — and then, Astrid suspected, Ysolde would have quietly enrolled.

She turned from the window. The classroom smelled of chalk dust and lamp oil and the faint mineral sweetness of sigil-ink, which Wren had taught her to mix from crushed slate and mountain water. On each desk lay a bone stylus — real bone, ethically sourced, Ferro's old smuggler contacts still good for that much — and a practice vial filled with dyed water that glowed faintly when a memory touched it properly.

*Tomorrow*, she thought. *Lesson nine: the ethics of return.* She'd been dreading it all week. It was the lesson where she told them about the vials she used to sell in the Brackwater markets. About the merchant whose last summer she'd lifted for forty crowns. About the false mother — the warm kitchen, the humming, the hands braiding hair she didn't have — that she had carried every night like a candle until Ysolde priced it out of her.

The students deserved the ugly version. Wren had insisted on that. *If you teach them the pretty version,* Wren had said, in the flat, certain voice she used when she was channeling something older than herself, *they will become Seranes.*

Astrid picked up the chalk and wrote the day's date on the board, and beneath it, in her own careful hand: *A memory kept is a debt. A memory given is a gift. Know which one you are holding.*

Thunder rolled over the rooftops, and somewhere below, the front door of the school groaned on its hinges.

*

She heard it first as wind — Greyhollow's streets made flutes of every alley in a storm — but then the sound resolved into knocking. Fists on wood, small fists, rapid and desperate, the way children knock when they have stopped believing anyone will answer.

Astrid went down the stairs two at a time, past the dormitory doors where she could hear the muffled breathing of sleeping students, past the noticeboard pinned with Wren's drawings of the lattice under the mountains. The knocking continued. It did not pause. Whoever was out there had committed to it the way drowning people commit to floating.

She unbarred the door and pulled it open, and the storm fell into the hallway like an uninvited guest.

The child standing on her step could not have been older than eight. Mud to the knees, hair plastered flat, shivering so hard her teeth clicked. She wore no coat, only a soaked wool dress that might once have been fine — the hem embroidered with a pattern Astrid's eye caught and refused to release: interlocking lines, a binding motif, sigil-work of a style she had seen only twice before. Once in her mother's journals. Once glowing in the dark beneath Mount Cael.

And clutched against the child's chest, wrapped in both arms like something rescued from a fire, was a shard of black glass.

It hummed.

Astrid felt it through the floor, through her boot soles, through the old scar-tissue of her trade — a frequency like a struck tuning fork buried in the world's marrow. She knew that sound. She had spent a year learning every variation of it: the low drone of dormant wards, the bright chord of Wren's presence, the sick sub-harmonic the Vessel had left behind in the vault, fading now but not gone.

This shard sang none of those songs. It sang something new. Or something very, very old.

"Please," the child said. Her voice was raw, storm-scraped, half-swallowed by thunder. "They said — the woman at the toll bridge, she said — find the house with the open door."

Astrid crouched, unhurried, letting the rain hit her shoulders because moving the child seemed suddenly less important than not looming over her. "What's your name?"

The girl's mouth worked. Her eyes — wide, grey, ringed with exhaustion — flickered with something Astrid recognized with a lurch: the look of someone reaching for a word that isn't there. Someone whose name has been taken, or lost, or never given.

"I don't—" The girl stopped. Started again. And then, in a language with no living speakers, a language of stacked vowels and consonants like stones dropping into deep water, she said six words.

Thornfell's dead tongue. The tongue of the sigil. The tongue Wren had spoken on Astrid's doorstep one year ago tonight, mud-caked, clutching a shard of black glass that hummed.

Astrid's breath left her in a long, steady stream, and she found that her hands were not shaking. That surprised her. A year ago they would have shaken. A year ago she would have counted the exits, weighed the risk, calculated the cost of kindness down to the copper.

Now she simply reached out and took the girl's icy hand.

"You'll want soup," she said. "And dry socks. We'll manage the rest after."

The child let herself be drawn inside. At the threshold she paused — one small hesitation, one glance back at the rain as though leaving something behind in it — and then stepped over the lintel, and the black glass shard hummed a note that made the lamps gutter and flare, and Astrid closed the door against the storm without locking it.

Some doors, she had learned, were meant to stay open.

*

The kitchen of the school had been Wren's idea — a big hearth room with a table long enough for twenty, because, Wren had argued, *memory-keepers who eat alone start hoarding*. Now Astrid sat the girl at that table, wrapped in a blanket that had been knitted by the fisherman's daughter's grandmother, and set before her a bowl of barley soup and bread thick with butter, and watched her eat with the desperate, guilty speed of someone who has gone hungry long enough to forget how to stop.

Wren arrived without Astrid hearing her — she still did that, moved through the world like a thought rather than a body — and stopped in the doorway, dish towel over one shoulder, flour to the elbows. She'd been baking. She baked whenever the mountain spoke to her too loudly; Astrid had learned to read the state of the wards by the smell of the bread.

Wren looked at the girl. Then at Astrid. Then at the shard of black glass sitting on the table between the salt cellar and the butter dish, humming its strange, old-new song.

"One year," Wren said. "Almost to the hour."

"Storms are punctual."

"Astrid." Wren's voice dropped into the register she used for serious things, the one that made the lamp flames lean toward her. "That's a keystone fragment. I can feel it in my teeth. There were seven keystones in the original binding. Six are accounted for. That's—"

"The seventh." Astrid turned the shard over with one fingertip, careful not to grip it. Even through a finger's touch, the hum climbed her arm like cold water. "Or the first. Depending on how you count."

"And she speaks the smith-tongue." Wren crouched beside the girl slowly, the way Astrid had, the way they had both learned — smallness first, safety second, questions last. "Little one. Can you tell me where you came from?"

The girl looked up at Wren, and something passed across her face — recognition, or its ghost. She raised one muddy hand and pressed it, briefly, against Wren's cheek. And then she answered, and the words were common speech this time, halting and rusted, as if she were translating from a great distance:

"The door under the roots was closing. Someone had to come out. They chose—" She frowned, hunting the words. "They chose the smallest piece. Because small things fit through cracks."

Wren went very still. Astrid watched her — watched her friend, her student, her family, the person whose face she had relearned one vial at a time — absorb the sentence, and saw in the set of her shoulders that it landed somewhere deep.

"They sent you," Wren said softly.

"They sent the part that remembers the way back."

The fire popped. Rain drummed on the roof like fingers on a drumhead. In the dormitory above, a student coughed in her sleep, and the sound was so ordinary, so human, that Astrid felt her chest ache with tenderness for it.

"Then you're in the right place," Astrid said. "Because we remember the way back too. All of us here. That's rather the point of the school."

The girl looked around the kitchen — at the long table, the twenty chairs, the wall where Wren's charcoal maps of the lattice hung beside a child's drawing of two figures on a mountaintop at dawn — and something in her exhausted face loosened, a fraction, the way a fist loosens in sleep.

"It's warm here," she said, wonderingly, as if warmth were a language she'd forgotten.

"Eat," Astrid told her. "Sleep after. Tomorrow there will be lessons, and you don't have to join them yet, but the door stays open either way." She paused, and smiled — the smile that had taken her a year to rebuild, the one that no longer calculated anything. "I'm Astrid. This is Wren. And you don't need a name tonight. Names come when they come. But if you'd like one while you wait—"

The girl swallowed a mouthful of soup and nodded so fast her damp hair bounced.

"Wren?" Astrid said. "You were better at this than I was."

But Wren wasn't listening. She had gone to the window, and she was looking out into the storm with an expression Astrid had seen only once before — the night in the deep tunnels, the night the mountain woke. Beyond the glass, far off across the black rooftops, in the direction of Mount Cael, lightning was falling in a pattern. Not random. Rhythmic. Striking the same high place again and again, like a heartbeat, like a signal, like something vast turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to say a name.

"Astrid," Wren said quietly. "The lattice is calling her home. And it's calling us with her."

Outside, the thunder gathered itself, and knocked — once, twice — like a fist on a very large door.

Astrid rose from the table, took up her coat, and opened the door without hesitation.
