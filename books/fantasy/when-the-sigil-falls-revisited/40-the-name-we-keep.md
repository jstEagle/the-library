## 40. The Name We Keep

The mountain did not want them to climb it at dawn. That was the joke of it, Astrid thought, half a mile up the pilgrims' switchback with her breath smoking in the cold and her boots slick with frost: for two thousand years the sigil-smiths had bound themselves into Cael's bones, and now that the binding was theirs again — hers, in the way a garden is yours, which is to say by labor and not by blood-right alone — the mountain had softened, gentled, become almost hospitable. The paths that had tried to kill them were swept clean. The wayshrines glowed faintly at their passing, like old dogs lifting their heads when the family comes home.

Wren walked ahead of her, because Wren always walked ahead of her now, the way a compass needle walks ahead of the hand that holds it. She was fourteen this spring, or nearly — they had decided on a birthday together over Ferro's grave, picking a day out of the air, the first warm one after the thaw — and she had grown the way spring water grows things: quietly, and all at once. Tall enough now that Astrid had to look up to meet her eyes. Her hair was still the color of wet slate. But her voice — her voice was a settled thing, low and certain, a voice that had once unmade soldiers' memories and had since learned gentler uses.

"Stop dragging your feet," Wren said, without turning around.

"I'm savoring," Astrid said. "There's a difference."

"You're stalling. You've been stalling since Greyhollow. You checked the stove twice."

"I checked the stove *once*."

"Twice." Wren stopped at the bend where the switchback turned its face to the east and waited, arms crossed, wearing the expression — arms crossed, eyebrow raised — that she had absolutely, shamelessly stolen from Astrid herself. It should have been infuriating. It was, instead, the single best thing Astrid owned. "You gave me the ring last night and then you couldn't sleep, so don't pretend this is just a walk."

Astrid came up beside her and looked out. Below them, Greyhollow spilled down the mountainside in its jumble of slate roofs and chimney smoke, small and stubborn as a lichen. Beyond it the valley opened gold-grey in the pre-dawn light, the Thornfell passes stitched across it like seams, all of them sealed now — properly sealed, warded by keepers who had been *elected*, an idea that had made three separate noble houses apoplectic and made Astrid happier than almost anything else in her second life. Far off, a shepherd's fire. A cart on the river road. The ordinary world, going about its ordinary morning, unaware that it hung from threads these two women — this woman and this girl — had spent a year reweaving by hand.

"It's not just a walk," Astrid admitted. "But it isn't only stalling either." She pulled her coat tighter — her mother's coat, the lining long since restitched where the letter had hidden, though she'd kept the original thread, kept everything, kept too much probably; Ysolde would have told her a memory-broker who hoards is no broker at all. "I wanted to be somewhere high when I said what I have to say. Somewhere you could see the whole of it. Everything you saved."

"We saved," Wren said. Automatically. The correction had become reflexive over the months, and Astrid had stopped fighting it, mostly because Wren was right and they both knew it.

"You split the burden," Astrid said. "Half of everything I ever stole went into you. Dozens of faces. Whole lifetimes. Roads I'd walked, names I'd sold, the false mother—" Her throat did the thing it still did, even now, even healed. "You took all of it into yourself knowing you might never give it back. Knowing I might wake up a stranger to my own life."

"You woke up," Wren said simply. "That was the part that mattered."

"And then you spent a year giving it back. One vial at a time. One memory every week, chosen by hand, like—" Astrid laughed, a little unsteadily. "Like a woman doling out medicine. Or a smuggler paying out rope. You made me *earn back my own life*, Wren Kavanagh—"

"That's not your name anymore."

"—Wren *of the Charter*, whatever we're calling ourselves this season," Astrid barreled on, "and I let you, because I understood what you were doing. You weren't punishing me. You were making sure that when each piece came home, I'd be there to receive it. That I'd *feel* it arrive. Belonging isn't something that happens to you. It's something you do, daily, on purpose." She blew out a breath that steamed in the cold. "Someone taught me that. Took me a year to learn it properly."

The sky in the east had gone from iron to rose. Below, Greyhollow's bells began the dawn peal — the school bell, Astrid's bell, the one she rang herself most mornings because the charter said the First Keeper should know the weight of every duty personally, and also because she liked the noise.

"So say it," Wren said. "Whatever it is. Before the sun comes up and you find another reason to stall."

Astrid reached into her coat.

The ring had lived against her heart through everything — through the Purge archives and the vault beneath the throne, through Serane's trial and the long slow work of the new order, through the year of vials. Her mother's ring. Sigil-smith's work, the band wrought of mountain-iron and a thread of the same black glass Wren had carried the night she knocked on Astrid's door, the stone worn smooth by generations of hands Astrid had never known and was only beginning to remember. When she had given it to Wren the night before — pressed it into her palm without ceremony, because ceremony would have killed them both — Wren had gone very quiet, and then very fierce, and had not slept either, though neither of them had said so.

She held it out now on her open palm, between them, where the growing light could find it.

"I gave you this last night," Astrid said. "And I meant it. But I want to say the words in daylight, on the mountain, where the smiths can hear. This was my mother's. It's the mark of the line that forged the binding, and the line ends where I decide it ends — that's the whole point of the charter, that inheritance is a *choice* and not a chain. So." Her voice caught; she let it. "I'm choosing you. Not as heir — the charter doesn't do heirs anymore, and good riddance. As family. As mine. As the person whose name I keep when I can't remember my own. Astrid Kavanagh died in the dark under Cael and was reborn without half her life, and the first thing she knew when she woke was that she would die for a girl whose face she didn't recognize. That's the truest thing I've ever known. Truer than anything I ever stole." She closed Wren's fingers over the ring. "Keep it. Wear it. And when some other strange child knocks on our door in a storm someday, give it to *her*, if she earns it. Let it keep traveling. Let it mean what we decide it means, not what dead men meant it to mean."

Wren looked down at her closed fist. The light was full on her face now, gilding the slate of her hair, catching the wet at her lashes that she would deny later with great dignity.

"You know I already put it on," she said.

"I know. I heard you drop it three times in the night."

"It doesn't fit properly yet. I'll grow into it." She said it lightly, but her hand had gone to the cord at her neck, where the ring now hung — too big for any of her fingers, threaded on leather like a talisman, resting over her sternum where the lattice-light had once lived. Where, Astrid knew, a small warm glow still answered it, faint as a banked coal. "And I have something for you too. Since we're being unbearably sincere before breakfast."

"Wren—"

"No. No stalling. You've done your speech, I get mine." She drew a vial from inside her coat.

Astrid stopped breathing.

It was one of hers — one of her own, the old blown-glass vials from her Greyhollow days, stoppered with wax and wrapped in wool, and it shimmered with that interior light she knew better than her own heartbeat: a memory, kept whole. Kept *perfectly* whole, undiluted, in the way only Wren could keep things, the living fragment of a living sigil, a vessel built for exactly this purpose.

"You gave everything back," Astrid whispered. "Every vial. I counted."

"You counted wrong," Wren said. "Or I lied. One of those." She turned the vial in her fingers, and the light inside shifted, and Astrid felt it before she saw it — felt the shape of it through her chest like hearing music through a wall. A storm. Rain on an attic roof. Mud on her floorboards. A knock.

"No," Astrid said.

"The night we met," Wren said. "I kept it safe. From the very beginning — before the mountain, before the choice, before I knew what I was or what it would cost. When Ysolde asked for your most precious memory, you gave her the false mother, and I stood in the next room and decided that *this one* was mine. That someone in this story was going to hold onto the night everything started, no matter what it cost anyone." Her thumb moved over the wax seal. "I've carried it a year. I watched you relearn your own hands, relearn your own roads, relearn grief. And I waited. Because I wanted to give it back here. High up. At dawn. So you'd feel it land."

"Wren. If that's what I think it is—"

"It is." She smiled, and it was the smile from the tollhouse, from the wayshrine, from every hard-won moment of a year that had remade them both. "Happy birthday, Astrid. Both of ours. I picked the day."

She unstopped the vial.

The light rose out of it like breath in winter — silver and rain-colored, smelling impossibly of wet wood and woodsmoke — and sank into Astrid through her open hands, through her skin, through the places under Cael where the lattice still lived in her.

And the night came home.

Rain hammering the attic roof of the Greyhollow garret. The gutter overflowing its music. Herself, younger and harder and lonelier than she liked to remember, sealing a vial of a dying merchant's last summer and telling herself the ache in her chest was just the trade, just the cost, just what cutters felt. The storm rising. The knock — three small fists' worth of knock, hesitant, then desperate. Opening the door on a mud-caked child with wild eyes and a shard of humming black glass, speaking words older than walls. Every instinct saying *close it, close it, you survive by closing it.* And then — this was the part she'd lost, the part she'd mourned without knowing its shape — the child shivering, and looking up, and *choosing her*, with the whole terrible faith of the utterly lost. And Astrid, against every law of her own survival, stepping aside to let her in.

Warmth. Blankets. Stolen soup. The first word of a dead tongue spoken in her attic like a bell ringing in an empty house.

Astrid came back to herself with tears running freely down her face and no memory of having started, standing on the shoulder of Mount Cael with her daughter — because that was the word, wasn't it, the true one, the one they'd circled for a year — gripping her hands, watching her with fierce attention, ready to catch her if the memory landed too hard.

It didn't land hard. It landed *soft*. It landed like coming home to a lit window.

"I remember," Astrid said. Her voice was wrecked and she didn't care. "I remember the door. I remember opening it." She laughed through the tears. "I remember thinking I was making the worst mistake of my life."

"You were," Wren said. "Best one too."

Below them the sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the valley all at once — Greyhollow blazing, the river road gilding, the sealed passes shining like soldered seams, the whole wounded kingdom lit up and holding. The bell finished its peal. Somewhere a dog barked at the light. The ordinary world, which they had bought with everything, going on.

Wren leaned her head against Astrid's shoulder, the ring glowing faintly against her chest, and for a while neither of them said anything, because nothing needed saying. The mountain breathed under them, slow and deep, its old wound closed, its voices at rest.

Then Wren straightened suddenly.

"Astrid," she said, and there was something new in her voice — not fear, exactly. Recognition. She was looking past the valley, south along the river road, where a small figure had appeared in the early light. Small. Alone. Walking with the stumbling, stubborn gait of someone who had come a very long way and had nowhere else in the world to go.

Walking toward Greyhollow. Toward the school. Toward the door Astrid always left open.

Astrid followed her gaze, and felt the ring-warmth at Wren's throat answer something distant in her own chest, and understood that the story was not ending. It had only ever been learning how to begin.

"Well," Astrid said, taking her daughter's hand. "We'd better get down there. Before the storm does."

There was no storm yet. But there would be. There always was.

They started down the mountain together, side by side, toward the knocking.
