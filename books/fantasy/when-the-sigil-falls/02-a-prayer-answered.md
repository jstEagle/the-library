## 2. A Prayer Answered

The fisherwoman came to the Ledger House at the grey hour, when the tide was out and the fog had not yet decided whether to be weather or water. Cyrus saw her first from the counting window: a broad-shouldered woman in oilskins gone stiff with salt, standing at the guild's brass door as though she expected it to bite her. She held something wrapped in oilcloth against her chest the way other women held infants.

Cyrus knew before the door opened what the woman carried. The dead in her carving tools had been restless since dawn, murmuring like gulls over a fish-gut heap, and their murmur had a taste to it—brine and cold and the particular pressure of deep water pressing on a chest that no longer needed breath.

"Fairweather." Vess appeared at her shoulder, as she always did, silent on feet that should have announced themselves across marble. Her mentor was small and dry as an old moth wing, and her eyes were the pale grey of things kept too long from light. "You'll take this one."

"I always take them."

"You'll take this one *gently*." Vess's hand closed briefly on Cyrus's wrist, her grip surprisingly strong for fingers so thin. "The eastern parishes are saying prayers again. Saying them *loudly*. And getting—" She stopped herself, jaw working around words she clearly found distasteful. "Rumor. That's all it is. Rumor. But if it's true, we want no part of it written in our books. Appraise what she brings, pay her fair, log it under minor acquisitions, and send her home."

"And if she won't go home?"

Vess looked at the woman through the window for a long moment. "Then we'll see," she said, "what the sea has taught her about patience," and she withdrew into the dimness of the archive stacks, where Cyrus suspected she would listen at every keyhole anyway.

* * *

The appraisal room was the only room in the Ledger House Cyrus truly loved: a long oak table scarred by two centuries of knives, lamps of green glass burning whale oil, and shelves of ledgers bound in leather the color of dried blood. She seated the fisherwoman opposite her and set out the tools of her trade with the little ritual that steadied her hands—a bone folder, a jeweler's loupe, a scale of brass and agate, a shallow dish of clean sand.

"Name?" she asked, dipping her pen.

"Maren Cobb. Of Saltrow parish." The woman set the oilcloth bundle on the table between them, carefully, as though setting down something sleeping.

"And you've come to sell?"

"To trade." Maren Cobb unwrapped the cloth with fingers gone blunt from rope-work, and inside lay three objects arranged in a row: a child's shoe, crusted white with coral; a knot of hair tied with fishing line; and a smooth grey stone, oval as an egg, that Cyrus's whole body recognized at once and recooned from.

She did not touch any of them. She had rules about touching, learned young and paid for dearly.

"Drowned memories," Maren said. Her voice was steady in the way of voices that have been rehearsed in front of mirrors and masts. "I'm told the Ledger buys them. I'm told they're worth more than silver, these days, to certain buyers."

"They're worth whatever someone will pay for forgetting," Cyrus said. It was guild doctrine, recited flatly. Inside her apron pocket, her smallest carving knife—the one made from her grandmother's finger bone, though she told no one that—had begun to hum.

"The boy in the shoe," Maren said. "Tam. Eleven years old. Went out with his father in the *Kindly Gull* fourteen months ago. The boat came back. He didn't." She smoothed the oilcloth flat with both palms, once, twice, a motion like soothing an animal. "These are my memories of him. All of them I could pull loose. The shoe I pulled from the wreck line myself. The stone is—" For the first time her voice wavered. "The stone is how he felt when he laughed. You can't get that back once you've given it away. I know. I checked. There's nothing left where it was, just a smooth place, like river rock."

Cyrus wrote without looking down. *Maren Cobb, Saltrow. Consignment: three articles, memory-laden.* Her pen moved because her pen always moved; the rest of her had gone very still, the way she went still when the dead began to speak through wood or horn or tooth.

Because they were speaking now.

Not the dead in her knife, exactly—though the knife hummed along like a tuning fork finding its note—but the objects themselves. The coral-crusted shoe whispered in a small boy's cadence, half-swallowed syllables, the way children talk when they're talking mostly to themselves. *—and then Da said hold the line taut, tauter than that, and I said—* The knot of hair said nothing but *ache*, a single sustained ache like a bow drawn across a string and never lifted. And the stone—

The stone sang. Low and round and wordless, a laugh pressed into mineral, and hearing it Cyrus felt her throat tighten with a grief that was not hers, that belonged to a mother sitting in a kitchen listening to a boy laugh at nothing, at everything, at his own elbows.

She had spent thirty-one years pretending she heard nothing. It was the family art, after all: the Fairweather women carved bones into instruments of memory, and everyone knew the carvings *held* feeling, the way amber holds insects. Everyone knew the guild's appraisers had a knack for reading what relics contained, the way some people could taste rain coming. What no one knew—what only Vess knew—was that Cyrus didn't read the memories. She *heard* them. Live. In voices. And had since she was six years old, since the day she'd picked up a comb her great-aunt had carved and a dead man's lullaby had poured into her ear like warm water, and she'd screamed until the neighbors came running.

So she sat very still and let the drowned boy talk, and her face showed nothing, because showing nothing was the first thing she'd ever been taught and the last thing she still believed in.

"These are genuine," she said. "Memory-saturated, well-preserved, recent provenance. The guild will offer you forty crowns."

"I don't want crowns."

Cyrus laid down her pen. Here it was, then. "What do you want?"

Maren Cobb leaned forward. Up close, Cyrus could see that her eyes were red-rimmed but dry, scoured dry, the dryness that comes after crying has stopped being useful. "I want to make a purchase," she said. "Not a sale. I want the guild to carry a prayer."

"We're accountants, Mistress Cobb. Not priests."

"You're worse than priests. You're *brokers*." She tapped the ledger with one cracked fingernail. "My sister works in the Saltrow customs house. She sees your couriers come through twice a season, carrying sealed cases marked with the old signs—the spiral, the antler, the wave-hook. Signs nobody's used in three hundred years, except—" she lowered her voice, though there was no one to hear, "—except in prayers. Old prayers. To the old gods. You're not buying memories to forget them. You're *feeding* them somewhere."

Inside the knife, the humming changed pitch. The dead were listening too.

Cyrus kept her voice level, which cost her more than she let show. "Whatever our couriers carry is guild business under seal. If you have a grievance—"

"My grievance," Maren said, "is that my son is at the bottom of the Channel Deep, and there's an old god called Merrow who owns everything the sea takes, and I have three memories and forty crowns' worth of grief, and I am *asking you*, carver, broker, whatever you are, to tell me how much it costs to ask Merrow for him back."

The green glass lamps flickered, though no door stood open.

And Cyrus—who had audited accounts for a bottled lullaby, who had logged a borrowed shadow at eleven percent interest, who had spent her life believing the guild's strangest trades were strange in the way of rare coins, oddities, curiosities, harmless—felt the first crack run through her safe distance like frost through a windowpane.

Because the guild masters *did* keep sealed cases. Because Vess had warned her, only that morning, about rumors from the eastern parishes. Because the dead in her knife had been singing sea-songs for a week, songs in no living language, and she had told herself it was coincidence, the same lie she told herself every morning with her bread and every night with her lamp.

"Wait here," Cyrus heard herself say.

* * *

She did not go to the guild masters. She went to Vess.

Her mentor was in the archive stacks, as predicted, standing beneath the high iron lamps among ten thousand ledgers, and when Cyrus told her what Maren Cobb wanted, Vess went still in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have. Vess never went still. Vess was a woman made entirely of motion—rustling, tapping, adjusting, a bird dressed as a librarian.

"No," Vess said finally.

"She'll find another way. They say prayers are being answered in the eastern parishes whether we broker them or not. If she prays alone, with no rite, no price—"

"If she prays alone, likely nothing happens, because the gods are *sleeping*, girl, they have been sleeping for three centuries, and the world is better for it—"

"Then it costs us nothing to carry the prayer properly. With structure. With a price paid up front, so the account balances." Cyrus heard her own voice and barely recognized it: reasonable, measured, the voice of a good guildswoman. Beneath it, something else was speaking, something that had listened to a drowned boy describe holding a fishing line. "If the gods are asleep, Vess, then a prayer is just words. But if they're not—wouldn't you rather it came through us? Priced? Bounded? *Audited?*"

Vess stared at her for a long time. Somewhere in the stacks, dust settled with a sound like sighing.

"You're too good at argument," she said at last, bitterly. "Your mother was the same. Your grandmother. It's in the blood along with everything else." She turned, robes whispering, and walked deeper into the archive, and Cyrus followed her past ledgers whose titles she'd never dared ask about, past a case locked with black iron, until Vess stopped at a shelf and drew out a slim volume bound in sealskin, cracked and dark with age.

"Merrow rites. Tide-bound. The old form." Vess held it without opening it, her knuckles white around the cover. "We do this once. As a courtesy transaction, logged and witnessed, because refusing a grieving mother looks bad even by our standards. And you will conduct it precisely as written, Cyrus. Every clause. Especially the clauses about *limits*. Do you understand me? A prayer without limits isn't a request. It's an invitation."

* * *

They performed it at slack tide in the guild's lower vault, where an old stone channel ran, dry for centuries, built—it had never been explained to Cyrus—for carrying seawater. Maren Cobb stood at one end with her memories laid out in a row. Vess read the rite in a voice like gravel sliding. And Cyrus, because the rite required a hearer—one who could confirm receipt, the text said, in language that made her stomach clench—stood at the channel's mouth with her palm pressed flat against the stone, listening the way she was never supposed to listen.

The payment went first. The memories left Maren the way tide leaves a beach: you watched it happen and couldn't see the moment anything actually moved. One breath the shoe was coral-crusted and heavy with a boy's small history; the next it was just a shoe, just leather and lime, and Maren swayed as though something had been drawn out through her sternum. The hair-knot went grey and scentless. The stone became merely a stone.

And the water came. Not through any pipe—there was no pipe—but up the dry channel from darkness, black and patient, rising without sound, filling the trough cut into the floor until it stood level as a mirror. The lamps guttered. The air tasted of low tide and lightning.

Then the prayer, spoken by Maren in a voice scraped raw: *"Merrow, keeper of the taken, I have paid. Give me back my son."*

Silence. Long enough that Cyrus began to hope—that was the trap, she understood later, hope was the hook the whole thing hung on—and then the water in the channel shivered, all at once, like a struck drumhead, and a voice rose out of it. Not from the water. From everywhere the water was. A vast, slow, amused voice, the voice of something that had waited a long time and found the waiting interesting.

*"Paid,"* it said. *"Paid in full. Small griefs, sweet griefs. The sea keeps honest books. Come then, mother. Take back what the tide borrowed."*

The water sank. The lamps steadied. And dripping in the empty channel, lying on wet stone in a puddle that had no source, was a boy.

He was breathing. He was soaked through and streaming water that tasted of the deep ocean when Cyrus knelt beside him—she touched him before she thought, rules forgotten—and his pulse beat quick and alive under her fingers. His skin was cold as cellar stone. His eyes opened, and they were clear, and brown, and eleven years old, and terrified.

"Da?" he said. "The line went slack. Da, the line went *slack*—"

"Tam." Maren Cobb made a sound Cyrus had never heard a human being make, a sound that started as a word and ended as a wound, and she gathered the boy up, both of them collapsing together into the wet, laughing and sobbing and praying all at once, and Cyrus knelt in the seawater and felt gratitude move through the room like sunlight.

That was when dread arrived. Dread arrived wearing gratitude, the way a thief wears a stolen coat.

Because the boy's voice—his living voice, his gasping, weeping, resurrected voice—was the voice from the shoe. Exactly. Note for note. The half-swollen childhood consonants, the particular crack on the word *taut*, the rhythm of a boy talking mostly to himself. Cyrus had spent her life learning to hear the dead with terrible precision, and she knew voices the way musicians know pitch, and this was wrong in a way she could not yet name but felt in her teeth.

She got them out quickly—guild protocol, signatures, receipts, Maren signing with shaking hands while Tam dripped on the floorboards and asked why the room smelled like candles, hadn't they lit candles, had he missed something—and she watched them go through the brass door into the fog, the boy walking stiffly, oddly, his arms swinging a half-beat behind his stride, and Cyrus stood in the doorway and told herself it was shock. Fourteen months drowned. Of course he moved strangely. Of course.

* * *

That night she could not sleep, and so she did what she always did when she could not sleep: she carved.

The workshop above her rooms was small and warm, smelling of linseed oil and bone dust. She took up a commission piece—a memorial comb, a merchant's wife's order, ordinary work—and set her knife to a blank of cow femur, and let her hands do what her hands knew while her mind circled the thing that troubled her.

The dead murmured in the blade, as they always did. Usually she kept them at arm's length, a murmur like wind in eaves, easy to ignore if one practiced, and she had practiced for twenty-five years. Tonight they were louder. Tonight they wanted to tell her something.

*Taut,* the knife whispered in a boy's voice. *Hold the line taut, tauter than that—*

She stopped carving.

Slowly, deliberately, she set down the commission and took from her shelf a finished piece she had made months ago and never delivered: a small whistle of grey whalebone, carved for a Saltrow client, decorated with wave-hooks. She had heard the dead sing sea-songs the entire week she'd worked on it. She had assumed they were ambient. Background. The sea was far away; the dead were local; coincidence.

She raised the whistle to her lips, though she had never once blown one of her own pieces, because blowing them was another rule, and blew.

The sound that came out was not whistle-song. It was a boy's laughter, pressed into mineral, round and wordless—the exact laugh from the stone, the laugh Maren Cobb had sold. And beneath the laughter, threaded through it like wire through wax, another voice spoke. Older. Vast. Slow as a tide turning, amused as a cat with a mouse it intends to keep.

*"Small griefs, sweet griefs,"* said the voice of Merrow through her own carved bone, in her own quiet workshop, at midnight. *"The sea keeps honest books, little hearer. And now you have heard me twice. Hearken, carver. The thirteenth moon is coming. The sleeper on the throne stirs. When the moons stand thirteen, the crown will wake—and I would have you listening when it does."*

The whistle went silent. Ordinary. Bone.

Cyrus sat in her lamplight with her heart hammering and the taste of deep water in her mouth, and outside her window, far off toward the eastern parishes, a church bell began to ring—not the hour, not the quarter, but ringing wild and unpatterned, the way bells ring when someone has seen something they cannot explain.

She reached for her ledger, her oldest habit, and wrote in a shaking hand:

*Consignment returned. Account closed. Something answered.*

Then, because the truth would not stay below the watermark any longer, she added:

*I think it knows my name.*
