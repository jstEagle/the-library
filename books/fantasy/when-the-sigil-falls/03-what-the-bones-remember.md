## 3. What the Bones Remember

The femur arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the black-and-gold cord of the Gilded Ledger, and it was heavier than bone had any right to be.

Cyrus weighed it in her palm before unwrapping it, the way she weighed everything that came into her workshop. Weight told you things. A lie was light; it wanted to escape your hand. Truth settled in the wrist like river stones. This truth had heft to spare—a thigh bone from some large man, yellowed the color of old candle wax, dense as oak at the knuckle ends. Someone's grandmother's grandmother had carried this through the world, and someone since had decided it ought to become something else.

The commission slip pinned beneath the cord read, in Master Corvel's precise clerk-hand:

*One flute. From the heirloom bone of the late King Osric, second of his name. For presentation at court. Handle with reverence. Bill accordingly.*

Cyrus read it twice.

*A flute.* From a dead king's leg.

She set the slip down and looked out the narrow window of her workroom above the tannery lane. Rain was doing its slow gray arithmetic against the glass, each drop subtracting a little more light from the afternoon. Somewhere below, the tanner's boys were singing a song about a girl with two noses, off-key and cheerful, and the smell of hides and lime rose up the stairwell the way it always did, familiar as her own pulse.

It should have been an ordinary commission. Bone flutes were half her trade—hollow long bones sang truest, the guild had learned centuries ago, and nobles paid obscene sums to hear music made from their honored dead. She had carved flutes from saints and smugglers, from a famous duelist's radius and an opera singer's clavicle that could hold a note no living throat could match.

But kings were different. Kings went into crypts under marble, guarded by vows and vigilance, not into the hands of guild appraisers. Which meant someone very high up had sold this bone, or given it away, or lost it in one of those impossible trades the Ledger recorded and never explained—and Cyrus did not want to know which. Knowing was a debt. Vess had taught her that before she'd taught her anything else.

*You carve what they ask,* Vess had said, twenty years ago, pressing Cyrus's small fingers closed around a sliver of sheep bone. *You do not listen to what they don't.*

She had been good at not listening for most of her life. Good at it the way a swimmer is good at not breathing: a discipline, practiced daily, that never quite stopped being effort.

Because bones talked. That was the ugly secret at the bottom of her gift, the inheritance no Fairweather woman had ever put in writing. Not all bones, and not always—but enough. The dead left echoes in the calcium the way fire leaves smoke in a chimney, and when Cyrus worked a piece with blade and rasp and oil, when her hands fell into the old rhythms her mother's mother's mother had worn into the family like a groove in a stair, the echoes sometimes rose up through her fingertips and spoke.

Mostly it was nothing. Fragments. A miller humming a harvest tune inside a cow's shinbone. A child counting to nine over and over in a lamb's rib, never reaching ten. The dead who lingered in bone were rarely the dead with messages. They were the dead with habits, caught mid-loop, going about their afterlives the way a beetle goes about being fossilized: without any awareness anything had changed.

She could have gone her whole life carving around them. She nearly had.

Cyrus laid the king's femur on her bench, lit two lamps against the graying day, and picked up her finest rasp.

*Don't listen*, she told herself, and began.

* * *

The first hour was only craft, and craft was mercy. The bone took the edge beautifully—dense, close-grained, almost ivory in its response—and Cyrus lost herself in the geometry of hollowing, measuring wall-thickness by tap and ear, opening the bore in slow increments, feeling for the resonant spots where the material seemed to lean toward the blade. Outside, the rain thickened. The tanner's song ended. Lamps bloomed in the windows across the lane, and the city settled into its evening mutter of cartwheels and gulls and distant bells.

She was fitting the mouthpiece, holding the unfinished flute to her lips to test the embouchure angle without blowing—just the shape of it, just the geometry against her skin—when the voice came.

Not through the air. Through the wood. Through her hands.

*—mind the third finger hole, carver, my father always said the third hole is where the soul sits—*

Cyrus froze.

The voice was dry and courteous and unmistakably royal, with the particular rounded vowels of a man who had never once needed to raise it to be obeyed. It sounded amused. It sounded tired. It sounded, underneath both, like a door left open on a room where something terrible was happening.

*I can feel you listening,* King Osric said. *Good. I've been shouting into this leg for eleven years.*

Her training held, barely. She did not drop the flute. She set it down on the felt with the exaggerated care of a woman disarming something, and she did not answer, because answering was the first step down a road Vess had forbidden with a vehemence Cyrus had never understood until this exact moment.

*They'll tell you I died in my sleep,* the bone said, when she didn't reply. It didn't seem to need her answers; eleven years alone had taught it monologue. *Old men die in their sleep. Very tidy. But I want you to know, whoever you are, whatever guild sent you—I was awake. I remember the last thing I saw. Do you know what I saw?*

Cyrus stood very still in her own workshop, rain loud on the roof, lamps burning, heart going like a trapped bird.

*I saw my grandson smile,* the dead king said, *and it wasn't his smile. It was arranged on his face like flowers at a funeral. And behind his eyes there was something patient. Something that has been waiting so long that waiting is all it knows how to love.*

The boy. Aldric. Six years old at Osric's death, if Cyrus's arithmetic held—the grandson who now sat the throne at sixteen, pale and quiet in his portraits, whom the ballads called Aldric the Gentle.

*I tried to warn them,* Osric said. *I wrote letters. I burned letters. In the end I did the only thing left to a man whose words can be intercepted: I put the warning where no one would think to look. Inside myself. Inside the one part of me too humble to be relic, too dull to be treasure. My own damned leg.* A dry sound that might have been laughter. *And then I waited eleven years for a carver with ears.*

*Cyrus Fairweather,* she thought, before she could stop herself, and hated herself for it.

*A Fairweather.* The name moved through the bone like wind through wheat. Something in the voice sharpened—not fear exactly, but the attention of a man who has finally found the right door after years of knocking on walls. *Then you know what I'm about to say already. Your blood knows. That's why they sealed it, isn't it? Generation by generation, payment by payment. You're what remains of the lock, girl. So hear the last testimony of Osric the Second, and carry it if you dare:*

The lamp flames leaned, though no draft moved. The rain seemed to hold its breath.

*The thing that wears my grandson's crown is not a demon and not a ghost and not any madness men have names for. It is one of the Thirteen—the old gods, the hungry ones, the ones your mothers' mothers buried in relics and prayer-books and paid for with their hearing. Twelve were bound deep and bound well. But the thirteenth—they never found a grave for the Thirteenth. They only starved it. Sealed it somewhere it could not feed, and told themselves that starving was the same as killing.*

A pause, long enough for three drops of rain to fall.

*It has spent four hundred years learning patience. And ten years ago, it got hungry enough to stop waiting for a door, and it made one. Out of a lonely boy. Out of a crown. Out of us.* The voice frayed, then gathered itself, courteous even now, even at the end of itself. *Bind it again or burn it, I care not which. But do not let it finish the crowning. There is a moon coming that does not belong to the calendar. When it rises, the glove fits forever. Whatever you are willing to pay, carver—pay it before the thirteenth moon.*

Then, quieter, in the voice of a grandfather rather than a king:

*Tell my grandson, if a scrap of him is still in there, that his grandfather loved him more than the crown ever loved either of them. Tell him the smile was his. Only the arrangement was the god's.*

And the bone went silent—not empty, Cyrus understood with a carver's certainty, but finished. An echo that had been holding itself together for eleven years by the sheer tension of its message, and having delivered it, let go. What remained in the femur was just bone. Yellowed. Heavy. Ordinary.

She stood in her workroom for a long time. The lamps burned lower. The rain resumed its arithmetic.

When her hands finally stopped shaking enough to move, she wrapped the flute-in-progress back in its oilcloth with fingers that would not quite obey, and went to find Vess.

* * *

Vess kept rooms above the Ledger's counting hall, three floors of stairs that Cyrus climbed two at a time with the oilcloth bundle pressed to her chest like a feverish child's doll. The old woman answered the door before the third knock—she always knew, somehow, though she claimed deafness in one ear and discretion in the other—and took one look at Cyrus's face and pulled her inside without a word.

Vess's rooms smelled of camphor and cold tea and the particular dust of ledgers. Maps papered the walls, annotated in a cipher Cyrus had never been taught. On the table, half-covered by a cloth, lay a bone stylus and a page of figures that Vess swept out of sight with the smoothness of long practice.

"Sit," Vess said. "Talk."

So Cyrus talked. She told it badly and completely: the commission, the weight of the bone, the voice rising through the bore, the testimony. She did not weep until she reached the grandfather part—the smile was his, only the arrangement was the god's—and then she wept briefly and furiously, furious mostly at herself, and Vess let her, standing at the window with her back turned while the lamps guttered.

When it was done, Cyrus said: "The Thirteenth. The old gods. You told me they were stories. You told me the whispering was a flaw in me, a leak, something to be managed—"

"I told you it was a burden," Vess said. "I never told you it was small." She turned. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadows, seventy years of careful expression worn down to bedrock. "Show me the bone."

"It's a king's heirloom. It's a commissioned piece. Corvel expects a flute by—"

"*Show me the bone.*"

Cyrus unwrapped it on Vess's table. The femur lay in the lamplight, yellowed and heavy and silent, looking for all the world like exactly what the commission slip said it was: raw material.

Vess bent over it. Her lips moved—counting, perhaps, or reading something written in grain and density that Cyrus's eyes couldn't parse. Then she touched it with one fingertip, at the spot where Cyrus had begun the bore, and snatched the finger back as if the bone had bitten her.

"Osric," she breathed. "You absolute stubborn old lion. Eleven years."

"You knew him?"

"I knew *of* him." Vess straightened, and something in her posture had changed—some load redistributed, some decision arriving at its final station. "He came to the Ledger House the winter before he died. Asked questions no client asks. About the founding accounts. About the *first* contracts." Her jaw tightened. "We told him nothing. We reported the inquiry to the masters, and the masters filed it under *curiosities*, and eight months later the man conveniently dies in his sleep, and everyone breathes easier, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I wondered, for years, whether we'd signed his death warrant by our silence. Now I know we merely failed to save him. There's a difference. It's a small difference. It will have to do."

"Vess, what is wearing the king? What did he mean, *the glove fits forever*? What did he mean, *what remains of the lock*?"

Vess looked at her for a long moment. Cyrus watched the old woman weigh truths the way Cyrus weighed bones, and watched her decide, visibly, that the hour for light loads was over.

"Not tonight," Vess said. "Tonight there's only one thing to do, and it's this."

She crossed to the hearth. The fire had burned down to a red meditation of coals. She fed it split oak until it stood up roaring, and then she held out her hand to Cyrus, palm open, commandment-shaped.

"The bone."

"The bone is worth more than this building. Corvel will—"

"Corvel will receive ash and a story about flawed material." Vess's voice did not rise; it never needed to. "Cyrus. A dead king spent eleven years hiding a message in his own skeleton because every other channel was watched. And the message found the one living person in this city who could hear it, and that person is you, which means the Thirteenth—if it has any sense at all, and it has had four hundred years to grow clever—will soon suspect the same. Every word in that bone is a thread, and threads lead back to the hand that holds them. To *your* hands." She flexed her open fingers. "The bone burns. Tonight. Now."

"And my hearing? Does that burn too? Because I heard him, Vess. I heard all of him. I've heard the dead my whole life no matter how hard I didn't listen, and now I've heard *this*, and you can't unhear a king telling you the crown is a cage—"

"No," Vess agreed quietly. "You can't. That's what a burden is." She stepped closer, and her hand closed around Cyrus's wrist—not cruelly, but with the grip of a woman who had carried this weight longer than Cyrus had been alive and knew exactly where it bruised. "You think knowledge is a prize. Every young person thinks that. It's a prize the way a wound is a doorway. Everything gets in now. Everything." Her voice dropped. "Including the thing on the throne. If it learns what you are, girl—if it learns a Fairweather is listening—it will come for you the way water comes for a crack. Patiently. Completely."

"The fisherwoman's son," Cyrus said. "Last month. The one who came back from Merrow wrong-sized and dripping. I recognized his voice from a carving I did years ago—he'd been in a whalebone rattle, he used to sing inside it. He came back *because someone prayed and something answered*. It started before the bone. Before Osric." Her throat worked. "It's already loose, isn't it? Whatever the thirteenth moon is. It's already beginning."

Vess said nothing. The silence was worse than any confirmation, because it had the shape of a confession held for another night.

"The bone," Vess said at last. "Now. Please."

Cyrus looked at the femur one last time—at the place where her blade had opened the first inch of bore, the beginning of a flute that would never play. *The smile was his. Only the arrangement was the god's.* Somewhere across the city, behind marble and guards and a crown that whispered, a sixteen-year-old boy was wearing that arrangement, smiling when the god smiled, sleeping when it permitted sleep. And his grandfather's last testament was about to go up in oak-fed flame, witnessed by two women and a tannery's worth of rain-smell, because keeping it was a luxury the world no longer sold.

She gave Vess the bone.

Vess held it a moment—an appraiser's farewell, honoring weight and grain—and then placed it into the coals with both hands, the way one lays a wreath.

Bone does not burn quickly. It glows first, along the seams, a network of orange veins brightening in the dark, and for one vertiginous moment it looked less like destruction than like the bone waking up, lighting its own roads from within. A thin thread of smoke rose, sweetish, mineral. Cyrus fancied—for one breath she genuinely fancied—that she heard the very end of a voice in the crackle, not words anymore, just the warm residual hum of a man finally setting down a weight he'd carried eleven years.

Then the glow collapsed inward, and the femur split, and it was only fire doing what fire does.

Vess stirred the coals until no fragment larger than a thumbnail survived, and then she stood with her back to the hearth, and the firelight made her shadow climb the maps on the walls, huge and wavering.

"Now you'll listen to me," she said, "as you have never listened in your life. No more commissions from the palace. No relics of the royal house, no bones older than sixty years, nothing with a pedigree. You carve sheep and cattle and the honest anonymous dead, and nothing else. You do not seek the boy-king. You do not seek the Thirteenth. You do not speak of this night to anyone living, and you do not—" her voice caught, actually caught, the first crack Cyrus had ever heard in it, "—you do not follow the road your blood is already walking. I have seen where it goes. I buried women on that road. Swear it, Cyrus. Swear it on the Ledger."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The silence it left behind was enormous, and in it, faint from the direction of the palace hill, the cathedral bells began the midnight count—one, two, three—and Cyrus, who had spent her whole life pretending not to hear the voices of the dead, realized with cold and perfect clarity that for the first time she was tempted to disobey Vess not out of curiosity, but out of duty; that the burden Vess feared was no longer a burden but a summons; and that somewhere in the dark above the city, a moon that did not yet belong to any calendar was waiting for its turn to rise.

She did not swear.

She said, "Who were the women you buried?"—and watched the color leave her mentor's face.
