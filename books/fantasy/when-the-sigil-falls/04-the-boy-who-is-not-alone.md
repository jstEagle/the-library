## 4. The Boy Who Is Not Alone

The summons came on a morning so cold the harbor fog froze mid-drift, hanging over the water like something drowned and forgotten to sink. A courier in crown livery stood at the door of my workshop with a letter sealed in gold wax, and behind him two guards who looked as though they had been told to be polite but not told why.

"Cyrus Fairweather," the courier read, though he did not need to; my name was carved into the lintel above my own door. "By order of the Lord Chamberlain, you are commanded to attend the palace at your earliest convenience, there to appraise certain relics of the royal collection."

*Earliest convenience.* The phrasing was a courtesy wrapped around a chain. One did not inconvenience a summons.

I looked past him at the guards. Their spears were ceremonial—gilt tassels, blunted points—but their eyes were not. They had the particular watchfulness of men who had recently begun standing outside doors they did not understand.

"I'll want my tools," I said.

"You'll want nothing of the kind," said the elder guard, before catching himself. "That is—the appraisal will concern provenance only, mistress. Documents. Records."

"Provenance," I repeated. In eleven years with the Gilded Ledger I had learned that when an institution said *provenance*, it meant *we do not know what this is, and we are afraid*. "Then you've summoned the wrong woman. I appraise objects, not paperwork."

The younger guard shifted. The elder gave him a look like a hand on a shoulder, then turned back to me, and for a moment his professional blankness thinned. What showed beneath was tiredness, and under the tiredness, fear.

"Mistress Fairweather," he said quietly, "the relics have begun to hum."

---

They put me in a carriage with drawn curtains, which told me more than any briefing could have. The Gilded Ledger's carriages did not draw curtains; we were proud of our visibility, our respectability, the way money is proud of being seen. Curtains meant the court did not want its people knowing the Ledger was inside the walls. Or it did not want me seeing where we went.

Through the gap in the fabric I watched the city climb away from the river. The eastern parishes still bore their scars from the autumn—scorched chapel doors, whitewashed windows where painted gods had been scrubbed from the glass—but new things had grown in the damage's place. Little shrines, hastily built, stacked with river stones and fish bones and cups of milk going sour in the cold. At one of them, a woman knelt with her forehead against the frozen ground while her children stood behind her holding candles that would not light.

Prayer, I thought, used to be a letter posted to no address. Now it was knocking on a door, and the door was opening, and nobody had taught these people what might answer.

The dead in my tool roll murmured as the carriage jolted. They always traveled with me—I had long since stopped pretending otherwise, at least to myself—and they had opinions about palaces.

*Gold,* said the voice that lived in my smallest chisel, the one I'd carved from my grandmother's finger bone. *So much gold. Gold remembers everything it's been melted through.*

"Hush," I said under my breath.

*You hush,* it said fondly. *We're only counting.*

Counting was what the dead did best. Counting and remembering. Between them, they made up most of what the living called history.

At the palace gates a chamberlain's clerk met us, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the expression of someone who had signed too many documents too quickly. He did not introduce himself. He walked fast, talked faster.

"The appraisal will be limited to four pieces. You will touch nothing without gloves. You will speak to no member of the household except through me. You will make no note of—" he hesitated on a stairwell landing, and his voice dropped as though the walls had ears with titles attached, "—of anything unusual."

"And if everything is unusual?" I asked.

He looked at me then, properly, for the first time. He was perhaps thirty, with the pallor of a man who worked indoors in a season of bad news.

"Then you will make no note of it anyway," he said, "and you will be paid triple."

I had audited enough guild accounts to know that triple pay was never for the work. It was for the silence. The Ledger called such payments *discretionary fees*, and entered them under Small Debts, because there was no honest column large enough to hold them.

---

The reliquary room sat in the oldest wing of the palace, a round chamber with a ceiling like the inside of a skull. Four objects waited on a table draped in black velvet, each attended by a soldier who watched them the way one watches a sleeping animal.

A scepter, silver, its orb dull as an old eye.

A marriage crown, small, meant for a queen who had died young; the pearls had gone yellow.

A sword with a plain leather grip worn to the shape of some ancestor's hand.

And last—a coronation crown. Heavy gold. Ancient. It sat apart from the others on its own table, as if even the soldiers had agreed to give it room.

I began, sensibly, with the sword.

Gloved, I turned it under the lamplight and recited what I knew: forge-marks of the Second Dynasty, a blade re-hilted twice, the grip replaced within the last hundred years. The dead in my tools stirred faintly—metal held echoes the way wells hold voices—but nothing spoke clearly. Ordinary. Old. Safe.

The scepter: same story, older. The marriage crown: I lingered over the pearls and felt a faint sorrow in them, the residue of a grief so old it had worn smooth, like sea glass. A dead girl's disappointment, pressed into nacre. Sad, but only sad. Relics are like houses; most of them are just full of ordinary ghosts doing ordinary haunting.

Then I came to the crown.

The clerk hovered. The soldiers stood very straight. And the crown sat on its velvet doing absolutely nothing, which was itself remarkable, because every object in that room had given me *something*, and the crown gave me a silence so complete it had texture. A silence shaped like listening.

"May I?" I asked, though my hands were already moving.

I lifted it. It weighed far more than gold alone should weigh—not physically; my arms reported a normal burden. But somewhere deeper than muscle, something in me registered the weight the way a scale registers a stone. As though the crown were pressing down on a part of me that had no name.

I turned it slowly. Filigree work of astonishing fineness, pre-dynastic by the tool marks. Thirteen points. Twelve tall spires and a thirteenth at the front, shorter, set slightly apart, as if it had been added later—or as if the other twelve were leaning away from it.

And through the gold, soft as breath on the back of my neck, a whisper.

Not words at first. Words would have been a mercy. It was the feeling of being addressed—the sensation of attention turning toward you across a great distance, the way you feel eyes between your shoulder blades in an empty street. My grandmother's finger bone chisel grew warm against my hip through three layers of cloth, and the voices in my tools went quiet all at once, every dead thing in my kit holding its breath like a congregation.

Then, threaded through the metal, faint and patient:

*...you can hear Me.*

I set the crown down. Carefully. The way one sets down a sleeping child one does not wish to wake, or a trap one does not wish to spring.

"Well?" said the clerk.

"It's genuine," I said. "Pre-dynastic work. Thirteen-point construction, which is—unusual. Singular." I stripped off my glove and flexed my fingers, which had gone cold. "Who wears it?"

The clerk's mouth opened. Closed. One of the soldiers looked at the floor.

"The king," said a voice from the doorway, "is fourteen months into a reign he did not ask for."

---

He stood framed in the arched entrance with no escort at all, which told me either that he trusted everyone in the palace or that no one dared approach him. Sixteen years old, maybe seventeen, dressed simply in gray wool that must have scandalized the tailors, with dark hair cut short and practical and eyes that were the problem.

Hollow-eyed, the plan of my life would later say, but that isn't right either. His eyes weren't empty. They were *occupied*—full of something that looked out through them with terrible patience, the way a tenant looks out a window of a house he has rented indefinitely.

Behind him, on the pale stone floor, his shadow lay at his feet.

And the shadow was late.

It is difficult to explain how one notices such a thing, except that noticing is my trade. When he stepped forward, his body moved and his shadow followed a half-breath afterward, the way sound follows lightning. No one else saw it. The soldiers stared ahead. The clerk studied his ledger. Either they had learned not to see it or they had never been able to.

But I had spent eleven years watching shadows keep honest time with their owners, and I saw it, and my heart went cold and steady as a coin.

"Your Majesty," the clerk began, bowing so deeply his spine cracked, "we did not expect—"

"No," the boy-king said. His voice was a young voice, unbroken still at its edges, but he spoke with the cadence of someone quoting patience rather than feeling it. "You didn't." He looked at me. "You're the bone-carver."

"Cyrus Fairweather, of the Gilded Ledger."

"I know what you carve." He came closer, and up close the wrongness resolved into details: the way he blinked slightly too seldom; the way his breathing seemed arranged rather than felt; the way, when the lamplight moved, his pupils adjusted a fraction after the light changed, as if the machinery of him ran on a delay. "My grandmother kept a comb you made. She said the teeth never caught, never split, that it was as if the bone remembered how to lie smoothly against skin. She talked to that comb." A pause, delicate and dreadful. "Did she talk *to* it, Mistress Fairweather? Or did she talk *through* it?"

Every instinct I owned screamed at me to perform ignorance. Ignorance was the armor Vess had fitted to me at sixteen: *be invisible, be dull, be a woman with a trade and no curiosities.* Eleven years I had worn it without a scratch.

But the crown was whispering again, and this time I caught shape in it, words forming like frost:

*She hears Us. Ask her. Ask her what We are.*

And the boy's eyes—those occupied eyes—went suddenly, briefly, *his*. Wide and wet and sixteen and drowning. For half a heartbeat the tenant was gone from the window and the house stood empty and terrified in its own light.

"Please," he whispered.

Then it passed, smoothed over like water closing above a sinking man, and the patient look returned, and Aldric—King Aldric, I reminded myself, though it felt like lying—smiled politely and said, in a tone of mild curiosity that did not belong to a teenage boy:

"What do you hear, Mistress Fairweather? In the crown. Be precise."

* * *

I have thought, since, about why I answered honestly. Cowardice, probably—the strange courage of the cornered. If the thing wearing a king already knew what I was, pretending otherwise was a candle held up to sunlight.

"Instruments," I said. "Voices. Not all at once—one at a time, usually, and faint. Bone holds them best. Metal less. Gold least of all." I looked at the crown on its velvet. "Except this one. This one is loud."

"Loud," he repeated.

"As a struck bell. It doesn't speak *to* me exactly. It speaks *at* me. Like something that hasn't had company in a very long time and has forgotten the difference between conversation and weather."

Something flickered across the boy's face—pain, quick and involuntary, quickly mastered. The shadow at his feet rippled, a half-second behind the expression, like a reflection in disturbed water.

"You should go," he said abruptly.

"Your Majesty—"

"*You should go.*" And then, softer, with that terrible half-second lag between the words and the shadow's agreement: "Before it learns your name."

The clerk materialized at my elbow with the efficiency of a man who had been waiting for precisely this dismissal, and I gathered my tools and bowed and let myself be steered toward the door, and none of it was fast enough, because at the threshold I heard it—clear now, unmistakable, threaded through the gold behind me, through the whole gilded humming weight of that room:

*Fairweather.*

My grandmother's name. My mother's name. The name on the lintel of my shop, the name I had just given freely to a king and whatever wore him.

*We knew your grandmothers. All of them. They were so tired, little carver. They were so very tired of listening.*

*Ask Vess what she buried under the workshop floor.*

I stopped walking. The corridor stretched ahead, torch-lit and empty, and behind me the boy-king stood in his doorway with his late shadow pooling around his boots like spilled ink, and between us, in the space of one held breath, something ancient settled its attention on me the way a cat settles on a warm stone—with comfort, with possession, with the absolute confidence that the stone does not get to leave.

The dead in my tools woke all at once, whispering in a dozen dead dialects, and underneath all of them, clear as a bell struck in an empty church, my grandmother's finger-bone chisel said the only word that mattered:

*Run.*

I ran. I did not stop running until the carriage doors closed and the curtains drew and the city swallowed me back into its ordinary noise—and even then, even now, writing this, I can feel the crown's whisper lodged behind my sternum like a fishhook:

*Thirteen moons, Fairweather. Then We stop asking.*

When I reached the Ledger House that evening, Vess took one look at my face and dropped the ledger she was carrying.

"What did you hear?" she said.

"Not what," I said. "*Who.* And Vess—it knows my name. It says you know why."

For the first time in the thirty years I had known her, Vess looked old. She looked at the floor of the workshop—the flagstones, the worn place near the cold forge—and then at me, and her lips moved around a word she did not let out.

"Pack a bag," she said finally. "And Cyrus. Whatever happens next—whatever I tell you—remember that I loved your mother. Remember it, when you hate me."

Under the workshop floor, something that had slept through my whole childhood shifted in its sleep.

And turned over.
