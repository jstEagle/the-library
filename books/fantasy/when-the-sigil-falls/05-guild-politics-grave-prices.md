## 5. Guild Politics, Grave Prices

The Gilded Ledger kept its masters in a hall above the counting floors, up a spiral stair worn hollow by two centuries of ambitious feet. Cyrus had climbed it perhaps six times in nine years of service, and never once without the sensation that she was ascending into the gullet of something patient.

Today she carried the quarterly audit—three ledgers bound in green calfskin, their spines cracked from her handling—and she climbed slowly, letting her thumb trail the banister's grooves, listening to what the wood had to say. Wood said very little. It was bone that spoke, bone that remembered, and so the stair was mercifully dumb. She needed the quiet while she could get it. The dead had been restless all week, murmuring through her awls and burnishers like wind through reeds, and their murmurings had changed pitch since the fisherwoman's son came back wrong-sized and dripping. They were not whispering secrets anymore. They were whispering warnings.

The doors to the council chamber stood open, because doors in the Ledger House always stood open when the masters wished to be overheard being magnanimous. Cyrus paused at the threshold with her ledgers held against her chest like a shield.

Nine masters sat at the long table of black oak, each before a small brass lamp, each lamp throwing its light upward so their faces went shadowed and their hands glowed—the effect deliberate, Cyrus had always suspected, because hands were the guild's true face. Hands signed contracts. Hands weighed coin. Hands, in the guild's oldest engravings, held scales.

Master Quillon sat at the center. He was not the eldest, but he had the fattest ring and the softest voice, which in the Ledger counted for more than either age or wisdom.

"—and the eastern parishes are burning candles at four coppers apiece," he was saying as Cyrus entered. "Four coppers. For wax. Imagine what they would pay for certainty."

Cyrus stopped just inside the door, in the strip of floor where clerks stood to be seen and not heard. None of the masters looked at her. That was the beauty of being the guild's bone-carver: she was furniture with excellent penmanship.

"The faithful don't want certainty," said Mistress Aveline, dry as old vellum. "They want the *feeling* of it. There is a difference, and the difference is margin."

"Then let us discuss margins." Quillon drew a paper toward himself, and the lamplight caught the ink still wet upon it. "The gods are waking. The parishes know it, the fishwives know it, and by month's end the merchant houses will know it too. Fear is coming into season, gentlemen. I propose we harvest."

*Harvest.* The word moved down the table like a draught of cold air. Cyrus watched the masters' glowing hands. Some stilled. Some drummed. Old Master Corvane, whose fingers were knotted with arthritis so that his rings sat on them like fruit on branches, folded his hands flat and said nothing, which from Corvane was a shout.

"We are an accounting house," Aveline said. "We trade in impossible things—memories, shadows, the last breaths of saints—but we do not trade in salvation. Salvation is a church's commodity. It cheapens us."

"It did not cheapen us when we sold protection against the plague," Quillon said mildly. "Nor against the fire of the Year of Ash. We wrote policies then; we can write policies now. *Indemnification against divine affliction.* For a modest annual fee, the Gilded Ledger guarantees its clients shall not be chosen, claimed, consumed, or otherwise inconvenienced by any deity returning from confinement."

"And how," asked Master Doss, who dealt in borrowed time and had the gray skin to prove it, "do we guarantee such a thing?"

Quillon smiled. It was a small smile, precise as a ledger line. "That is a question of fulfillment, not of sales. And fulfillment, brothers, sisters, has always been this guild's particular genius."

The lamplight trembled. Or Cyrus's hand trembled, and blamed the light.

She looked down at her ledgers and made herself open the top one, made her eyes travel the columns she herself had written, and found that the columns were already answering Quillon's question in a hand she had never thought to read them in. Policy 114: indemnification against plague, paid out in full to forty-one families, funded by a reserve drawn from—she turned the page—the *Dormancy Trust*. Policy 203: fire of the Year of Ash, settled quietly, no questions, funded again from the same trust. The Dormancy Trust appeared throughout the guild's history like groundwater, surfacing wherever catastrophe threatened the client rolls, always ample, never explained.

She had audited that trust twice. Both times she had been told its assets were "ecclesiastical holdings" and both times she had accepted it, because accepting it kept her invisible and invisibility was the whole architecture of her life.

Vess's voice came back to her from three nights ago, sharp as a snapped thread: *Stay small, girl. When the big houses start moving, small things don't get stepped on.*

But Cyrus had never in her life managed to stop listening, and so she stood among the lamps and listened now.

* * *

"There is a second matter," Quillon said, when the murmur of assent had settled. "A housekeeping matter. Our friend in the east has been generous to us for a generation. It is time we were generous in return."

Cyrus's pen had been moving on its own, taking minutes as it was paid to do, and now it stopped mid-word.

*Our friend in the east.* She knew the phrase. Every senior clerk knew it; it appeared in expense entries going back decades—candles purchased, tolls paid, "considerations rendered" to a party unnamed. She had assumed it meant a magistrate, or perhaps a monastery that guarded some relic the guild wanted kept quiet. She had assumed many things.

"The Thirteenth does not want generosity," said Corvane, speaking at last, and his ruined voice filled the chamber like gravel poured into a bell. "It wants *room*. You do not feed a thing like that, Quillon. You keep it fed on schedule and you pray the schedule holds."

The silence that followed had texture. Cyrus felt it press against her ears like deep water.

"You speak as though it were still asleep," Quillon said gently.

"I speak as though it *should be*." Corvane's hands unfolded, refolded. "I was on this council when we renewed the terms. My father was on it when *he* renewed them. There are entries in our oldest ledgers written in a hand none of you can read without bleeding from the nose. We are not a guild that sells protection against the gods, boy. We are a guild that *manages* them, and has grown fat on the management, and forgotten the difference. There is a difference."

"Is there?" Quillon spread his wet-inked paper flat. "The Dormancy Trust stands at eleven thousand crowns. Eleven thousand crowns, gathered from a sleeping god's rent, invested wisely for a hundred years. If the sleeper wakes—if the trust fails in its purpose—shall we return the money? Shall we tell the widows and the candle-burners that their premiums purchased nothing but our comfort?" He looked around the table, unhurried, letting each master feel the arithmetic of it. "Or shall we sell them the only honest product left to us: the promise that when everything else breaks, the Gilded Ledger will still be standing, taking fees?"

"You're proposing to profit from the collapse of your own collateral," Aveline said.

"I am proposing that collateral is only ever worth what someone will pay to be rid of needing it."

Corvane rose. It cost him visibly; his knuckles whitened on the table's edge. "I have heard enough. Minute my dissent. Full minute, in the old form." The old form took half a page and named consequences in language borrowed from contracts older than the guild itself. "And when your premiums come due, Quillon, when the thing you've been feeding comes to collect in person rather than in coin—I hope you remember that I told you what we are."

He shuffled past Cyrus on his way out. For a moment his eyes, milky with age but not yet blind, passed over her face, then down to her open ledger, then back up. Something moved behind the milk. Recognition? Warning? Pity?

"You'll want the historical volumes," he said quietly, to her or to the room. "Sub-vault. The ones with the iron clasps. Ask yourself why a trust that holds eleven thousand crowns keeps its founding documents locked away from its own auditors." Then he was gone, his footsteps descending the spiral stair like a slow heartbeat winding down.

* * *

The meeting resumed without him, voices smoothing over the absence the way water closes over a stone, and Cyrus stayed through all of it—resolutions drafted, fee schedules initialled, a motion to expand the fulfillment department "in anticipation of increased claims activity"—and her pen recorded every word in her neat, narrow hand, and none of it touched her, because she had learned young how to be a vessel instead of a person.

It was only afterward, alone in the sub-cellar with the iron-clasped volumes stacked around her on a stool too low for sitting, that her hands began to shake.

The sub-vault lay beneath the counting floors, past the memory-cellar where the guild kept its more volatile inventory in salt and dark. The air here tasted of iron and old paper and, faintly, of the sea—which made no sense, this far under the city, unless one understood that certain old things leaked. Vess called places like this *thin*. Cyrus called them loud, though nothing here was speaking yet. The silence had the quality of a held breath.

The iron clasps opened at her touch—her blood, apparently, was sufficient key; another fact that should have surprised her and merely slid into place beside all the others, click, like a bead on an abacus.

The founding documents of the Dormancy Trust were not deeds. They were not titles to ecclesiastical holdings. They were, in the oldest volume, drawings.

Cyrus turned pages by lamplight with hands she forced to stay steady. Bones, first—a full skeleton laid out in ceremonial arrangement, every joint annotated in that archaic hand Corvane had promised would make her bleed. Then diagrams of vessels: jars, urns, reliquaries, and—her stomach turned slowly, recognition rising in her like water in a well—*carved instruments*. Flutes. Rattles. A lyre strung with something the artist had shaded very carefully and labeled only *not gut*.

Instruments like hers. Like the ones she had made all her life, believing herself a craftswoman. Believing the whispering was a curse she suffered, private and meaningless.

The annotations resolved, page by page, into procedure. A rite. Steps. Requirements. And at each step, in the margin of every page, in a different hand—a rounder, steadier hand, updated across generations like a family Bible—one recurring notation:

*Paid by F.*

*Paid by F.*

*Paid by F.*

Fairweather. Her name, or her mother's, or her grandmother's, marked against every stage of the binding like initials against a debt. Because it was a debt. It had always been a debt. She thought of Vess saying *the Fairweather women pay their accounts*, and how she had taken it for proverb, for family pride, for nothing.

The last page of the oldest volume was a contract. Not between the guild and a client. Between the guild and *the bound*—and the signature line for the counterparty was not signed but carved, the letters gouged deep into the vellum as if by a fingerbone dragged with terrible patience, and the name so carved was one Cyrus had heard only once before, whispered through a burning heirloom femur: **Thirteenth**.

Below it, the guild's seal. Below that, the terms. Rent payable annually. Confinement maintained. And a clause, underlined twice in faded red:

*Should the bound awaken ere the term, the binders' heirs shall render satisfaction in kind: hearing for hearing, voice for voice, until the account stands closed.*

Cyrus sat back on her low stool in the thin-silenced dark and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the fisherwoman's son came home dripping. The guild had not merely known the gods slept. The guild had put them to sleep—or commissioned it, or contracted it—and had charged rent on the prison for a hundred years, and the wardens' wages had been paid not in crowns but in the hearing of women. Her mothers. Herself.

Her employers were not merchants who happened to fear the waking. They were jailers grown fond of their fees, and now, with the prisoner stirring, they intended to sell tickets to the riot.

She closed the volume. The iron clasps met with a sound like teeth.

Somewhere above her, muffled through stone and coin and two centuries of careful bookkeeping, the counting floor went about its business—clerks calling figures, coins singing against coins, the great abacus of the city's greed clicking through its beads. And threaded through it all, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, she heard the dead in the walls begin, very softly, to sing.

Not warnings anymore. A summons, low and patient, in a voice she recognized the way one recognizes one's own reflection: the voice of the drowned king, the one who had named the thing on the throne.

*Cyrus Fairweather,* it sang, or seemed to. *Come see what your house has built. Come count what it owes.*

She gathered the volumes against her chest and climbed toward the light, and behind her in the sub-vault the held breath of the dark went on holding, waiting, she understood now, for her—for the last of the F.'s—to decide what an account like theirs could possibly be worth, and who would live long enough to pay it.
