## 6. Vess's Confession

The sickroom smelled of tallow and crushed yarrow and something older underneath—bone dust, perhaps, ground so fine into the walls of Vess's chamber over forty years that no amount of scrubbing could lift it. Cyrus had known that smell her whole life. It was the smell of the workroom upstairs, of the ledger vaults below the guildhall, of every Fairweather woman's hands. She had never thought to call it what it was: the smell of a debt being paid.

She sat at Vess's bedside with a bowl of broth going cold in her lap, watching the old woman's chest rise and fall in shallow, uneven swells. The window behind the bed framed a sliver of grey sky where the twelve moons hung like coins someone had nailed up and forgotten. Twelve. Everyone counted twelve. But for three nights now Cyrus had felt an itch at the back of her skull, a fourteenth presence pressing against the edge of things, and she had begun, against every instinct of self-preservation, to count again.

"You're staring," Vess rasped.

"You're awake. The physician said you wouldn't be."

"The physician," Vess said, "counts ribs for a living. I count other things." Her eyes opened—milk-pale, clouded, but still sharp as an awl beneath the cataract. "Set the broth down, girl. I need to tell you something before my mouth forgets how."

Cyrus set the bowl on the bedside table. Her hands were not quite steady, and Vess saw it, because Vess saw everything, had always seen everything, from the first day she'd caught six-year-old Cyrus whispering to a sparrow's wing bone and instead of punishing her had simply said, *So. Another one.*

"I've been dying for a week," Vess said conversationally, as though discussing the weather or the price of tallow. "I intend to do it properly, which means doing it honestly. Fetch the lamp closer. And shut the door. What I have to say is not for walls with ears in them—and in this house, girl, all the walls have ears in them."

Cyrus rose, latched the door, trimmed the wick. When she sat again, Vess reached out with a hand like yellowed parchment and took her wrist—not gently. Vess had never been gentle. Gentleness, she used to say, was what people offered when they didn't have the courage for truth.

"Tell me about your mother," Vess said.

The question landed wrong-footed. "You knew her better than I did. You trained her."

"And she trained me. And her mother trained her." Vess's grip tightened. "Go back further. Do you know how far the line runs?"

"To the founding of the guild. Mother said we were accountants before there were guilds. Keepers of impossible trades."

"That is what we became." Vess closed her eyes. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flattened, stripped of its usual dry irony, laid bare as a bone before the knife. "It is not what we were."

* * *

Outside, rain began against the shutters, soft as fingers drumming. Cyrus did not move. Vess's hand stayed on her wrist, light now, the grip of a woman spending her last strength on words.

"There were thirteen gods once," Vess said. "You know the stories—the harvest gods, the sea mothers, the hearth-fathers. Pretty tales for pretty children. Here is the ugly tale. They were not worshippers' inventions. They were real, and they were hungry. Not wicked—hunger has no wickedness in it, any more than a flood does. But they fed on devotion, and devotion, freely given, was never enough. So they learned to take. A village that prayed too fervently would wake with its prayers answered and its children hollowed. A city would build a temple and find itself building forever, generations pouring their love into stone while the god drank it and gave nothing back but the feeling of giving."

Cyrus thought of the fisherwoman, of the boy returned wrong-sized and dripping, wearing gratitude like a mask over something else. Her stomach tightened. "What happened to them?"

"We happened to them." Vess opened her eyes again, and in the lamplight they seemed almost luminous, as though the cataracts had thinned to let something through. "The first of us was named Maren Fairweather. Before the guild, before ledgers, before any of this. She was a bone-carver in a fishing village on the eastern coast, and she discovered what you discovered at six years old, what every woman in our line discovers: that worked bone remembers. That the dead speak through it. That the boundary between the living and the dead is not a wall—it is a membrane. Thin. Permeable."

"And?"

"And Maren asked herself the question you should have asked years ago, and were too afraid to ask." Vess smiled without warmth. "If the dead can speak through carved bone, what else can pass through? What else listens?"

The rain thickened. Somewhere downstairs, the night clerk's quill scratched across the ledger—a small, steady sound, the sound of the world continuing to pretend it was ordinary.

"The gods listened," Cyrus said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"They listened through everything. Prayer, grief, love, bone. Maren learned that a carving made with intent could become a door—or a lock. She learned that a soul, properly bound into worked bone, becomes a key that turns only one way. She gathered eleven others—women, mostly, midwives and grave-keepers and one mad priestess who'd seen too much—and they built the first bindings. Not prisons exactly. Contracts. Each god sealed into a relic, each relic hidden, each binding paid for with—" Vess paused, and Cyrus heard the pause stretch, heard the cost of the word before it came.

"With what?"

"With hearing." Vess lifted her own hand from Cyrus's wrist and touched her ear, an old, absent gesture Cyrus had seen ten thousand times and never understood until now. "To bind a god is to stand at the membrane and hold it shut with your own body. The dead are loud, child. Louder than you know yet, though you know more than you let yourself. To hold a god still while you stitch him into bone—to feel him fighting, screaming, promising, pleading—you must give up the sense that lets you hear anything else ever again. Every binder went deaf to the dead. Forever. That was the price written into the first contract, and contracts among us are sacred, as you well know."

Cyrus sat very still. The broth had gone cold and a skin had formed on it; she stared at it as though it might offer some alternative explanation. "But you can hear them. I can hear them."

"Yes."

"But if every binder lost—"

"One binding per generation," Vess said. "One woman. One god. One price. Maren bound the first and went silent, and her daughter took up the tools and bound the second, and so on, mother to daughter, down the years, until eleven relics held eleven gods and the world forgot they had ever been hungry. We kept the accounts. Of course we did—we were bookkeepers to our bones. The Gilded Ledger wasn't founded despite our secret. It was founded *to hide* it. An archive of impossible trades is the perfect place to bury an impossible history. Who audits the auditors, after all?" She laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, wet and terrible, and Cyrus held the cup of water to her lips and waited for her breath to come back, and all the while her mind was moving with terrible speed, laying figures side by side the way she did in the vaults, finding the sum.

"Eleven gods," Cyrus said. "Twelve binders' worth of generations. And you're the last Fairweather except me."

Vess wiped her mouth. "Your mother refused."

"What?"

"She refused. She was meant to bind the twelfth—the hungriest one, the youngest, the one they couldn't catch. The Thirteenth, we called it, though it has no proper name; names are doors, and we weren't fools enough to make it one. Your mother stood at the threshold with the relic in her hands and she looked at you—you were three years old, asleep in the next room—and she put the relic down and walked away. Said the price was theft. Said no child should inherit a debt she never agreed to pay." Vess's voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I called her a coward. I have regretted it every day since, and I will die regretting it, which is a fitting price, I suppose. There is always a price. That is the only law that has never failed us."

Cyrus stood. Her legs felt strange, distant, like limbs borrowed from someone taller. She walked to the window and pressed her palm flat against the cold shutter wood, and through it she felt the rain, and beneath the rain she felt—faintly, horribly—the thing she had been refusing to name for weeks. The pressure at the edge of hearing. The itch. The Thirteenth, loose in the world because her mother had loved her enough to break a chain.

And now it had found a boy on a throne.

"The twelfth binding was never completed," she said. "So the chain has a broken link. Me."

"You are the last link," Vess said behind her. "The last Fairweather who hears. The last one who could finish what Maren started—or undo it entirely. Either way, child, the choice your mother dodged has come due, and it has come due in your hands, and I am sorry. Gods, I am sorry. I taught you to carve and I taught you to keep accounts and I never taught you this, because I hoped—" She stopped. Drew a long, ragged breath. "Because I hoped you'd be spared. Hope is the one trade the Ledger never mastered."

Cyrus turned around. Anger arrived first, sudden and hot, rising through her chest like floodwater. It surprised her with its size. She had spent her whole life managing her feelings the way she managed accounts—neat columns, balanced books—and this would not balance.

"You knew," she said. "All these years. You watched me flinch at funerals. You watched me sleep with wax in my ears. You watched me pretend, year after year, that the voices were nothing, that I was nothing, and you knew I was standing in a chain of women who sold their souls' ears to cage starving gods, and you said *nothing*."

"I said nothing because saying it makes it true, and unspoken, there was always a chance—"

"A chance of *what?* That the gods would stay politely asleep? That the Thirteenth would take up knitting?" Her voice climbed; she forced it down. "There's a boy on the throne right now with something wearing him like a glove. There's a fisherwoman who traded drowned memories for a son and got something else wearing his face. And I have been auditing bottled lullabies while the whole rotten inheritance sat under my feet the entire time!"

"Yes." Vess did not flinch. She never flinched. "Yes to all of it. And when you are done hating me—and you should hate me for a while, it's good for the digestion—you will open the master ledger, the black one, third vault, behind the false spine of *Customs of the Salt Coast*, and you will read what your great-great-grandmothers wrote in their own hands. The rites. The prices. The names of every god and every relic and every woman who paid. And then you will understand that I did not stay silent out of cowardice." Her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "I stayed silent because every woman in that ledger, on the day of her binding, wrote one sentence more than the rites required. Do you know what it was?"

Cyrus shook her head.

"*I chose this.*" Vess's eyes held hers, milky and burning. "Freely. Knowingly. That was the clause, Cyrus. The bindings hold because they were chosen, not coerced. If you go looking for the Thirteenth with anger in your hands—if you try to finish the chain out of rage or duty or anyone's expectation but your own—the rite will fail, and it will fail *through* you, and everything those women bought with their silence comes undone. So I waited. I waited for you to choose. I'm sorry it's a terrible choice. All the true ones are."

The anger did not leave. But it changed shape, the way grief does when it finally finds its object—settling lower, heavier, braiding itself into something she suspected she would carry longer than sorrow. Her mother had refused the debt and died anyway, young, leaving it to her daughter regardless. Debts like this did not care about refusal. They only cared about time.

"How long have I had?" Cyrus asked quietly. "How long has the Thirteenth been loose?"

"Since your mother put the relic down. Twenty-three years, wandering, listening, growing hungry. It found the crown two winters ago—I felt it happen, a cold spot in my hearing, like a tooth gone bad." Vess sagged back against her pillows, spent. The lamplight guttered. "Coronation season. Thirteen moons. It will wear him fully by then unless someone stands at the membrane again."

Cyrus thought of Aldric—sixteen, hollow-eyed, his shadow arriving late to his own body—and felt something in her chest turn over and settle into a new position, the way a bone sets.

"Rest," she said. "I'll bring the black ledger up tomorrow. We'll read it together."

"No." Vess's hand caught hers one last time, weak now, trembling. "Read it alone. Some debts you must see with your own eyes. And Cyrus—" She pulled her close, close enough that Cyrus could smell the death already working in her, sweetish under the yarrow. "Whatever you decide—refuse, like your mother, or finish, like Maren—decide it as *yours*. Not the Ledger's. Not mine. Not the dead women's. Yours alone. Promise me the choice, even if you can't promise the answer."

"I promise the choice," Cyrus said.

Vess smiled then—a real smile, small and crooked, the first Cyrus could remember seeing on her in years. "Good girl," she whispered. "Now bank the lamp. The dark and I have accounts to settle."

* * *

Cyrus did not sleep. She sat in the vaults until the small hours with the black ledger open before her, and the false-spined book gave up its secrets one by one: Maren Fairweather's careful hand, the diagrams of thresholds, the lists of relics and hiding places, and page after page of signatures, each followed by the same four words in different hands across three centuries. *I chose this.* *I chose this.* *I chose this.* The dead murmured faintly through her carving knife where it lay on the desk beside her—an old complaint, a half-heard lullaby, the usual static of the departed—and for the first time in her life she looked at the blade and saw it clearly: not a tool, not a gift. A lockpick, inherited by thieves who'd convinced themselves they were jailers.

Near dawn, her fingers stopped on a page near the end. The handwriting was her mother's. The entry was unfinished—one line only, ink blotted where the pen had rested too long:

*The Thirteenth cannot be bound, only bargained with. If it comes to my daughter, tell her the price is not silence. Tell her the price is—*

Nothing after. The blot had dried over the rest like a wound scabbing shut.

Cyrus closed the ledger. Through the high vault window, the twelve moons were setting in the west—and there, low and thin and wrong at the horizon's edge, a sliver of silver that belonged to no almanac, no chart, no sky she had ever been taught to read.

Thirteen.

She blew out the lamp and went to prepare for a coronation.
