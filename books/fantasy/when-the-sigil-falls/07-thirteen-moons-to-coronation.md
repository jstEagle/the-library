## 7. Thirteen Moons to Coronation

The astronomer's tower smelled of cold brass and burnt juniper, and Cyrus Fairweather climbed its spiral stairs with the stolen fingerbone wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, feeling it pulse like a second heart.

She had come to the wrong part of the city for this. The Observatory stood on the highest spur of Vantage Hill, above the tanners' quarter, above even the bell-tower of Saint Ferrow-Underground, whose bells had not rung in forty years because no one remembered what they were for. The wind up here came clean off the mountains and had never passed through a market or a sickroom or a grave. It was the only air in Corvale that did not carry voices.

Cyrus breathed it like a woman surfacing from deep water.

* * *

Master Elowen Thask received her in the orrery room, a circular chamber at the tower's crown where the ceiling opened onto the sky through a shuttered oculus and the walls were lined with instruments Cyrus could not name—astrolabes nested inside astrolabes, armillary spheres within spheres within spheres, until looking at them too long made her feel she was standing inside the eye of something vast and patient that was doing arithmetic about her.

Thask herself was seventy if she was a day, small and dry as a seed pod, with hands spotted like a plover's egg and eyes the pale grey of moonstone. She did not offer tea. She looked at Cyrus the way astronomers look at things they intend to measure.

"You're the Ledger's bone-carver," she said. "Vess Fairweather's girl."

"Cyrus."

"I know your name. I knew your grandmother." Thask turned back to the great orrery at the chamber's center—a clockwork universe the size of a mill wheel, all silver rings and jeweled planets turning on invisible gears. "I knew her when she came up this hill thirty years ago asking after the same question you're going to ask me. So let's save ourselves the pleasantries. You want to know about the thirteenth moon."

Cyrus's hand went to the bundle at her ribs without her permission. "You know why I've come."

"Child, half the city knows something is coming. The priests feel it in their knees before rain. The guild masters feel it in their ledgers. I feel it here." She tapped the orrery's brass rim. "But knowing *that* something comes is nothing. Astronomy is the discipline of knowing *when*. Sit down. This will take some telling, and my knees are older than your grief."

Cyrus sat on a stool worn smooth by generations of students, and waited.

* * *

Thask worked as she talked, her spotted fingers adjusting the orrery's rings with tiny brass keys, and as she spoke the clockwork heavens rearranged themselves around her words.

"The calendar year has twelve moons. That much every child knows—the Shepherd's Moon, the Seed Moon, the Fish Moon, and so on to the Cold Moon, and round again. But the old reckoning—" she touched a ring of tarnished silver set apart from the rest, a thirteenth band that moved against all the others, slow and contrary— "the old reckoning counted differently. There was a thirteenth moon once. A leap-moon. It rose only when the other twelve had fallen out of step with the seasons, and it rose to *correct* them. To put the world back where it belonged."

"What happened to it?"

"It was bound," Thask said simply. "Along with its eleven siblings. Your family's work, if the stories have any truth in them. When the gods went into their relics, the thirteenth moon went into hiding—it stopped rising and became instead a *turning*, an accumulation, a debt accruing interest in the dark between years. Every time the twelve moons complete their circuit and the seasons refuse to align perfectly—and they never align perfectly, child, the world is built crooked on purpose—the discrepancy feeds the hidden turn. The astronomers of my order called it the Reckoning Debt. We thought it was bookkeeping." Her mouth twisted. "We should have known better. In Corvale everything is bookkeeping."

The orrery ticked. Somewhere below them, the city murmured like a hive in winter.

"Three days ago," Thask continued, "the Debt matured. The thirteenth turn completed itself. And here is what matters, bone-carver, so attend me." She fixed Cyrus with those moonstone eyes. "A coronation has been proclaimed. The boy-king Aldric, crowned at midwinter, on the night of the Long Dark. My calculations say—have said since the Debt matured—that midwinter falls on the thirteenth turn's *culmination*. The hidden moon does not rise, exactly. But it arrives. It will be overhead, unseen, pulling on everything unseen, and whatever waits beneath the veil will be able to reach through at full strength for one night. One night only. Then the window closes for another age—if it closes."

"How long?" Cyrus asked, though her hands already knew, already wanted to start carving the answer into something solid.

"Count the moons with me." Thask spun the outer ring, and the jeweled planets whirled. "One: the Drowning Moon, now waning. Two, the Frost. Three, the Wolf. Four, the Seed. Five, the Green. Six, the Hay. Seven, the Dog Days. Eight, the Harvest. Nine, the Hunter's. Ten, the Fog. Eleven, the Slaughter. Twelve, the Cold. And then—"

Her finger stopped on the tarnished silver band.

"—the thirteenth. Coronation night. Midwinter. The Long Dark." She withdrew her hand. "Thirteen turns, bone-carver. Thirteen moons from tonight, give or take three days depending on how the Fog Moon behaves, and it has been behaving badly all century. That is your margin. That is all of it."

* * *

Cyrus stood very still.

Fear, she found, was not what she had expected. She had imagined panic—a flooding, a drowning. Instead there was a strange crystalline quiet, as if the deadline were a lens and everything behind it had snapped suddenly into focus. Thirteen moons. She could count thirteen moons. She had audited accounts stranger than this: bottled lullabies, borrowed shadows, a widow's forty years of Sundays sold for the price of one good harvest. A deadline was just a debt with a date attached. And debts, however terrible, could be *worked*.

"Say it plainly," she heard herself say. "What happens on coronation night."

Thask regarded her a long moment. Then she crossed to a cabinet, unlocked it with a key from around her neck, and brought out a single sheet of vellum covered in her own cramped script, annotated in three colors of ink.

"On coronation night, the crown touches the boy's head, and the thing inside the gold completes its grip. What wears him now in fragments will wear him wholly. The boy Aldric—young, frightened, half-aware—will go somewhere very far down, and something else will open his eyes and smile with his mouth and reign for as long as it pleases. Gods do not die of old age, bone-carver. It would be king forever, wearing that child like a glove, and the prayers would flow to it, and it would grow fat on them, and the other eleven would wake hungry and come home to feed." She laid the vellum down. "Unless something is done first."

"Something," Cyrus repeated. "That's astronomer's language for 'I don't know what.'"

"Astronomy tells you *when*. It is forbidden to tell you *what*—that's theology, and theology gets my colleagues burned." Thask's voice was flat. "But I will tell you what I told Vess Fairweather thirty years ago, when she climbed these stairs with the same look on her face you're wearing now. I told her: the binding was made at a threshold, under a hidden moon, by willing hands. Whatever can bind can unbind—but thresholds cut both ways, and willing hands must remain willing to the end." She closed the orrery's shutter over the oculus, and the daylight died to a coin. "She thanked me. Then she went down the hill and did not speak to me again for thirty years. I always wondered what she decided."

Cyrus thought of Vess in her sickroom, hands folded on the blanket like two sleeping birds. *Not yet, girl. Not until you know the whole price.* Well. Now she knew the when. The whole price was still owed.

* * *

She climbed down the tower stairs slowly, and the fingerbone against her ribs kept its slow pulse, and by the time she reached the bottom she had stopped flinching from it. That frightened her more than anything Thask had said—not the deadline, but how quickly she was growing accustomed to carrying a god's body through the streets like marketing produce.

At the tower's foot, the city took her back. Noise poured in: cart wheels, fish cries, a preacher on the corner of Tanner's Row shouting about signs and wonders while the crowd laughed and edged closer to listen. And under it all, faint but unmistakable now, the whisper from the oilcloth:

*...thirteen... we counted too... thirteen, thirteen, little carver...*

It had begun counting with her. Of course it had. It had been counting for a thousand years.

Cyrus walked home through the failing light and made herself useful, because usefulness was how Fairweather women survived fear. By lamplight in her workroom she took a fresh slab of cow-bone and began to carve: not a commission, nothing saleable, just a plain rod marked with thirteen notches, one for each moon between this breath and the Long Dark.

The first notch she cut that night, deep and clean, while the fingerbone whispered its thousand-year arithmetic and the dead pressed close to watch.

Twelve remaining.

Somewhere across the city, in a palace she had visited only once, a sixteen-year-old boy was falling asleep with a crown in the next room, dreaming—if the god let him dream—of a life he would not live past midwinter.

Cyrus blew bone-dust from the notches and reached for her knife.

Then, through the floorboards, from the street below, came a sound that stopped her hand mid-stroke: boots. Many boots. Moving in formation. Stopping beneath her window, where a voice—clipped, official, unfamiliar—said words that dropped through the floor like stones through still water:

*"By warrant of the Crown's new inquisition: inquire after one Cyrus Fairweather, carver, of the Gilded Ledger. Knock."*

* * *

The knock came at her door like a debt collector's fist—three blows, patient, certain of payment.

Cyrus did not move. The bone rod lay on her bench, thirteen notches, twelve bare. The fingerbone pulsed warm against her ribs, and for the first time since the tower, its whisper sharpened into something almost like urgency:

*Little carver. They are not here for the relic. They are here for the last Fairweather.*

She looked at the door. She looked at the window, and beyond it the rooftops of Corvale, slick and black under a waning Drowning Moon.

Thirteen moons. She had meant to spend them carefully.

The knock came again.

It seemed the first notch would cost more than bone.
