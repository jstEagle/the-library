## 8. The Market of Impossible Things

The way down was through the counting room, past the ledgers no one audited twice.

Cyrus had worked in the Gilded Ledger for eleven years, and in all that time she had believed—had *chosen* to believe—that the guild's business ended at the ground floor, that the sums and swaps of impossible trade were conducted entirely in lamplit offices above the street. It was a comfortable belief, the kind you build around yourself like insulation, and like most comfortable beliefs it had always been a lie. She had simply never been invited below.

The invitation came on the last night of the waning moon, delivered by a boy of perhaps fourteen who wore the guild's grey livery and would not look at her face. He set a bone token on her workbench—carved, she noticed with a lurch, in a style older than any Fairweather hand still living—and said only: "Third bell. The cellar stair. Bring nothing that remembers you."

She turned the token over in her fingers. It hummed faintly, the way old bones did when they had outlived their owners' grudges, and for one dizzying moment she heard—not words, not yet, but the *shape* of words, gathering at the edge of her hearing like weather on the horizon. She set it down quickly.

"Who bids me come?" she asked.

The boy was already at the door. "No one bids," he said. "Below, everything is offered." And then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the corridor with the particular softness of someone paid not to be remembered.

Vess would have told her to refuse. Vess would have taken the token and fed it to the stove and said something dry about curiosity being the first coin a fool spends. But Vess had been distant lately—more than distant, *careful*, watching Cyrus across the workshop with an expression she couldn't name, as though measuring her against some hidden standard. Three nights running, Cyrus had found the old woman awake past midnight, sitting among the carving tools with her hands folded, listening to nothing. Or listening to something. Or refusing to.

So Cyrus went.

* * *

The cellar stair was behind the wine racks, which should have been a joke but wasn't; the Ledger House was older than its own records, and its foundations predated the founding of the guild itself. The stairs went down farther than any cellar needed to go. Torches burned in brackets without flickering, and their light had a strange quality, as if it were being *remembered* rather than seen.

Her tools whispered against her hip where she'd strapped them beneath her coat—the one concession to sense she'd allowed herself. They murmured in their sleep, the way they always did: fragments of dead voices caught in ivory and antler, half-dissolved prayers, the residue of hands long gone to dust. She'd spent her whole life learning not to listen. Tonight, descending into the dark under the Gilded Ledger, she found herself listening harder than she ever had, because if there was ever a place where the dead might speak plainly—

There was. That was the whole horror of it. The air below grew thick with them, layer upon layer, a sediment of voices going back further than she could bear to plumb. Not whispering *at* her—they didn't know she could hear—but speaking to each other, or to themselves, in the endless murmur of a crowd that had nowhere else to be. She pressed her palm flat against her sternum and kept walking.

At the bottom stood a door of black wood banded in iron, and beside it a woman with a ledger of her own—a small one, bound in something that was not leather and did not want to be touched.

"Name?" the woman asked.

"Fairweather."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes. Recognition, or calculation, or both. "You're expected after all," she said, and marked something in the book that made a sound like a held breath released. "The house seat is yours tonight. Observe. Do not bid unless you mean to pay. Below, every offer is honored."

"I'm not here to buy."

"Everyone says so." The woman pushed open the door. "That's how it starts."

* * *

The market was not what she expected, and she had expected much.

It was a cavern, natural once, now dressed with hanging lanterns of colored glass that threw no heat and cast shadows pointing the wrong direction. Rows of stalls circled a central floor where an auction block rose like a pulpit. Perhaps two hundred people moved through the aisles—merchants in masks of plain white silk, scholars whose robes bore the sigils of three different universities that officially denied this place existed, a cluster of soldiers with the creases of royal service still pressed into their coats. No one spoke above a murmur. The whole cavern had the hush of a cathedral, or a sickroom.

And the goods. Gods forgive her, the goods.

A stall of bottled sounds: lullabies, she saw from the tags, harvested from mothers who'd sold them willingly, each vial glowing faint amber when the light touched it right. A cage of moths that were somebody's forgotten names—you could see the syllables shimmering on their wings. A tray of teeth that let you taste any memory you swallowed them with. A jar labeled, in careful guild script, *one borrowed shadow, returned nightly, lessee assumes all risks of meeting oneself.*

Cyrus walked the aisles with her collar up and her heart going like a drum, and told herself she was only looking. Only observing. She had come, she admitted at last, in the honest dark behind her own eyes, because of the eastern parishes—because prayers were being answered there, and because Vess had gone silent as a locked room, and because somewhere in her gut a certainty had been growing like frost: that the guild knew more than it sold, and that whatever was waking in the world, the Gilded Ledger had helped tuck it into bed.

Find the binding relics, she'd told herself. See what the guild keeps below. Understand the shape of the thing before it understands yours.

Then the auction bell rang, and everyone moved toward the block, and she moved with them because standing still was the fastest way to be noticed.

* * *

The auctioneer was a small man with a voice like poured honey and eyes like two coins left in a well. He opened with modest lots—a decade of sweet dreams in a cedar box; a promise extracted cleanly from a dying man, still warm; the last word of a language nobody spoke. Each sold for sums that made Cyrus recalibrate everything she thought she knew about the guild's true ledger. These were not side profits. This was the spine of the house.

"The next lot," the auctioneer said, and the hush deepened until Cyrus could hear her own blood, "requires no introduction, but tradition demands one. Recovered from the eastern parishes, from a churchyard that has recently become—generous. A fingerbone. Provenance: older than provenance. We are advised by our assessors that it is *awake*, that it answers questions put to it in the old forms, and that its current owner reports dreams of standing in a great hall with the sea coming through the windows."

He lifted it into the lantern light.

Cyrus stopped breathing.

It was a fingerbone, yes—a proximal phalanx, she knew the anatomy better than her own heartbeat, she'd carved ten thousand of them into flutes and whistles and memory-needles. But it was wrong the way a portrait can be wrong: too symmetrical, too *intentional*, as if the bone had been designed rather than grown. And it was singing. Not aloud. In that other register, the register only the Fairweather gift could hear—a single sustained note, patient and vast, the way a mountain might hum if a mountain had been waiting somewhere for six hundred years.

Around her, the masked faces leaned in. They couldn't hear it. Most of them couldn't hear it. But they could feel it—she watched it move through the crowd like wind through wheat, that involuntary lean toward the block, the quickened breath, the hunger. People wanted it the way the drowning want air, and most of them would have been hard-pressed to say why.

Because it was a god's. She knew it the way she knew her own name. Somewhere in the eastern parishes, prayers were cracking open old seals, and pieces of what had been bound away were surfacing, and the guild was dredging them up and selling them to rich men with empty nights.

The bidding began at four hundred crowns and passed a thousand before Cyrus had finished understanding what she was seeing. Names she recognized from the upper floors called out from behind white silk. A scholar bid his university's entire endowment for the coming year. An old woman bid in memories—Cyrus heard the auctioneer weigh and accept them, *a childhood, a first love, the face of a dead sister*, lot by lot surrendered like coins counted out of a purse that could never be refilled.

"Forgettings," the man beside her murmured to his companion, with the connoisseur's relish of the truly corrupt. "Clean forgettings, certified. You could buy a city's worth, if the city agreed to give up enough of itself."

And the city, apparently, had. Because when the bidding stalled at a figure that made no earthly sense, a representative of the city council—Cyrus saw the chain of office glint beneath his cloak—nodded once, and the auctioneer smiled, and the god's fingerbone passed to the city of Marrowfield for the accumulated forgetting of eleven thousand souls.

They applauded. Softly, politely, the way one applauds a funeral. The bone was carried away in a reliquary case by two men whose hands shook, and Cyrus stood in the applauding crowd with her heart hammering out a single furious rhythm: *wrong, wrong, wrong.*

It wasn't the price. It was the principle. A piece of a god—one of the *old* gods, one of the things her family had spent generations burying—was loose, awake, singing, and the response of the wise and powerful was to sell it to the highest bidder so that it could sit in some council vault and dream of seas coming through windows.

* * *

She told herself she was going to follow the reliquary just to learn where such things were kept. She told herself it was reconnaissance. She told herself a great many things while her feet carried her through the service corridors below the market, past stalls shuttered for the night, following the bone's song the way a compass follows north—because the song was getting louder now that she'd stopped pretending not to listen, and underneath its patience she could hear something else, something that raised every hair on her arms.

It was calling. Not to the city. Not to the buyers. Into the dark, into the deep places under the world, the way a man lost in fog calls out a name and listens for the echo.

Calling, and being answered. Faintly. From far away. From eastward.

The storage vaults were guarded by two men who had been paid, presumably, a great deal to be vigilant, and who were at that moment leaning together over a bottle of something amber, sharing the particular laugh of men who believe nothing down here matters. The lock was old ironwork. Her picks were in her kit because a carver's kit holds a hundred tools and only half of them are honest.

Later—much later, in the years after, when people asked her when exactly she had changed, when she had crossed from the woman who kept accounts to the woman who broke them—Cyrus would think of this moment. Not the theft itself. The pause before it. The three full seconds she stood in the dark corridor with her hand on the door, giving her old life every chance to hold her back.

The old life declined. She went through.

The reliquary case sat alone on a stone shelf, and up close the bone's song was almost unbearable—a chord now, not a note, harmonics folding under harmonics, and threaded through it, unmistakably, a *voice*. Not words. Older than words. But meaning came through the way warmth comes through a wall: *I am here. I am still here. Find me. Find me find me find me.*

"Oh," Cyrus said softly, to the bone, to the god inside it, to whatever was listening from the east. "Oh, you poor, hungry thing."

She took off her coat, wrapped the reliquary in it, and walked out the way she'd come in, past the guards, past the black door, up the cellar stairs that suddenly seemed very long, her pulse loud enough to wake the dead—which, given her particular affliction, it nearly did. The murmuring voices in the walls seemed to lean toward her as she passed. *Thief,* they murmured, amused, or admiring, or merely noting it down. *Thief, thief, little thief.* She found she didn't mind. There are worse things to be called by the dead than what you actually are.

Above ground, the night was cold and ordinary and smelled of rain. She crossed the courtyard of the Ledger House with the bundle under her arm and the bone singing against her ribs like a second heart, and she had reached the gate, and the street beyond it, and freedom—

When she felt it. A prickle at the nape of her neck. The specific, animal certainty of being watched.

She turned. At the top of the cellar stairs, framed in the doorway, stood Vess.

Her mentor looked at her—at the coat-shaped bundle, at her face, at whatever was written plainly on it—and Cyrus watched eleven years of shared silence rearrange themselves into something new and terrible. Vess didn't shout. Vess didn't call the guards. Vess only pressed her lips together and shook her head, once, slowly, the way you mourn something already lost.

"You don't know what you're carrying," Vess said, loud enough to reach her across the courtyard. "Or you'd have left it burning where it lay."

"Then tell me," Cyrus called back. "For once in my life, Vess—tell me the truth."

But Vess only turned and went back down into the dark, and Cyrus Fairweather stood alone in the rain with a god's fingerbone against her heart, a thief now in the eyes of every power she had ever served, holding the first piece of a war that was coming for her whether she stole it or not.
