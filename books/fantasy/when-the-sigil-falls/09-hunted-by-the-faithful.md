## 9. Hunted by the Faithful

The city learned my name the way a wound learns salt.

By morning, it was chalked on walls in three districts: *FAIRWEATHER*. By noon, it had grown letters around itself like frost — *thief, god-touched, trafficker* — and by evening, when I slipped out of my own street through the tanner's yard with the fingerbone wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs, there were men at both ends of Copperlane who watched doors for a living and were watching mine.

I had stolen from the Gilded Ledger. Worse, I had stolen from the Market of Impossible Things, which meant I had stolen from everyone who had ever bought a miracle there, and miracles, I had learned in twenty years of auditing accounts, are the only commodities whose owners always remember what they paid.

Dame Serah's proclamations went up on the parish posts before dusk. I read one by the light of a lamp I did not dare stand beneath. *Let it be known*, said the Crown's new inquisitor, in letters so clean they seemed carved rather than printed, *that those who trade with the old gods — who buy, sell, bind, or carry the relics of the Thirteen — shall be taken, questioned, and given to the fire. There is no middle price. There is no honest broker.*

*Given to the fire.* She wrote it the way Vess wrote ledger entries: no ornament, no mercy, only the sum total. I wondered what grief had taught her that hand. Then I stopped wondering, because wondering is a luxury for women who are not hunted, and I folded the proclamation into my coat where it lay against the fingerbone like a threat pressed flat.

The bone was warm. It should not have been warm. It had been cold as riverbed stone when I lifted it from its velvet cradle under the auction house, cold as guilt, but all that night and through the grey beginning of this one it had held a heat like a sleeping bird against my sternum, and once — only once, as I crouched in the reek of the tanning pits listening to boots pass on the far side of the wall — it had whispered.

Not words. Not yet. A pressure, the way deep water presses on the ear before it speaks. The Thirteenth's finger, reaching through its own severed joint toward the thing on the throne, or toward me, or toward whatever thin place my Fairweather blood had opened between them.

*I am not carrying you because I want to*, I told it silently, and felt foolish, and felt the warmth pulse anyway, amused or pleased, and hated it.

* * *

Saltmouth was the only door left open to me, and it was a poor door: leaning, tar-blackened, smelling of fish guts and kelp, at the end of the drowned quarter where the sea takes back a street every winter and the Crown has never once come to collect taxes from water.

Maren Ashpole owed me nothing. Her son had been returned to her wrong-sized and dripping, bought with drowned memories, and I was the guild clerk who had stamped the transaction — I had weighed his voice against a ledger line and found it acceptable. If she remembered me at all, she would remember me as part of the machine that had sold her a boy-shaped thing wearing her son's grief.

But the dead do not let me choose my shelters, and every other door in the city had closed behind me like a mouth.

She opened it before I knocked. She stood in the doorway with a gutting knife in one hand and no surprise anywhere in her face, and behind her, in the lamplight of the single room, sat a boy mending a net with fingers too long and too pale, and he looked up, and he knew me.

"You're the one who counted," he said.

His voice came into me like a hook into soft flesh. I had heard it before — not him, never him alive, but the drowned sing in patterns, and I had catalogued the pattern of this particular drowning years ago in a commission of whalebone, a mourning piece some harbormaster's widow had ordered, and inside it the dead boy Tam had sung of cold and green light and letting go. This was that voice. Older. Salt-scoured. But that voice.

"Yes," I said. "I counted."

"Come in, then." Maren stepped aside. "You look like something the tide refused."

* * *

They fed me barley soup with too much salt — everything in Saltmouth tasted of the sea's patience — and while I ate, the boy called Wren told me about drowning.

He did not perform it. That was the terrible thing. He spoke of it the way a carpenter speaks of joinery, matter-of-fact, hands still working the net. "It takes about four minutes to die in cold water. Longer if you fight. I fought." He turned the mesh over. "There's a moment when the cold stops being cold and becomes a kind of company. That's the part nobody tells you. Dying isn't lonely. It's the opposite of lonely. Everything down there is holding you."

"The Merrow held you?" I asked.

Wren considered. "Merrow is just the name we gave the holding. It doesn't have a shape unless someone prays hard enough to give it one. My mother prayed very hard." He glanced at her, and Maren looked away, jaw tight, and I understood that this tenderness between them was also a wound between them, and that they lived inside it daily, choosing each other anyway.

"I remember the bottom," Wren said. "I remember the other drowned ones. They're not gone, mistress clerk. They're gathered. Like seeds. And something up top is getting ready to plant them."

The fingerbone against my ribs went hot enough that I flinched, and Maren's eyes narrowed at the motion, and I saw her decide not to ask, because in Saltmouth you learn not to ask about what people carry.

* * *

Serah's riders came through the drowned quarter on the third night.

I heard them before anyone — heard the horses' reluctance, the way animals balk near relics, near gods, near me — and I was out the back window into the channel mud before Maren finished saying *Cyrus, the window*, as if I hadn't already been moving since the first hoofbeat. I lay in the stinking shallows under the pilings with the oilcloth bundle hugged to my chest and listened to boots on the boardwalk above me, and a voice like a drawn blade asking Maren Ashpole if she sheltered a fugitive.

"And if I do?" Maren asked. Her voice was flat as slate.

"Then you burn with her. The proclamation is clear."

"The Crown's proclamations are clear," Maren agreed. "So is the tide. One of them comes twice a day and actually does what it says."

There was a silence with violence standing inside it. Then the blade-voice said, "Describe her," and Maren described a woman who matched me in no particular whatsoever except stubbornness, and the boots went away, and after a long time I climbed back through the window smelling like the grave's wet cousin, and Maren handed me a towel and a look that dared me to thank her aloud.

I thanked her anyway. She grunted. In Saltmouth, that was absolution.

* * *

It was Wren who found the message hidden in the fingerbone's wrapping — because of course there was a message; the Ledger never sells anything without an accounting attached, even contraband, even gods.

He noticed it because he notices things the drowned notice: weight, current, what clings. "Your rag's stitched," he said, on the fourth morning, turning the oilcloth in the light. "Double seam. Nobody double-seams a wrapping cloth."

Inside the seam, inked on silk fine as skin: coordinates written in the Ledger's cipher, which I could read the way I could read anything, because reading debts is the family trade — and beneath the cipher, a single line in Vess's hand, cramped and furious:

*THE RITE IS IN THE MASTER LEDGER. THE HOUSE KEEPS IT. THEY WILL SELL YOU BEFORE YOU REACH IT.*

Vess. Alive, then, when she wrote it. Watching me even now, from inside the machine we both served. Or warning me off. With Vess it had always been impossible to tell the difference between protection and control, and I sat there on Maren's floor with the silk shaking in my fingers and understood that my old mentor had known I would steal the bone before I had decided to steal it myself.

"She's telling you to go home," Maren said, reading my face the way sailors read weather.

"She's telling me the home I'm going to wants me dead."

"Same thing, sometimes." Maren took the silk, looked at it a long moment, and handed it back. "My boy died and came back and I've spent two years deciding whether that was mercy or theft. You'll spend less time than that deciding whether your guild is your mother or your murderer. People like us don't get clean choices. We get tides."

* * *

That night I dreamed of the auction house, and in the dream the velvet cradle was empty, and Dame Serah stood over it holding the proclamation with my name on it, and when she looked up her face was not cruel — that was the horror of it, her face was *grieving* — and she said, *Don't you understand what they take? I lost someone to an answered prayer. I know exactly what comes back. It isn't them.* And I woke with salt on my face that might have been sea-spray through the shutters and might not.

The fingerbone lay against my breastbone, patient as a debt.

*Soon*, it did not say, but meant.

* * *

On the fifth day, Wren brought me word from the harbor: the Ledger House had posted its own notice beside the Crown's, offering reward for the return of guild property, describing me precisely, and — this was the detail I made him repeat twice — naming me *the last of the Fairweather line*.

The last. As though the guild had already crossed my name out of its book and was merely waiting to collect the margin.

"They know what you stole," Wren said quietly. "And they know why. Which means they know what you are."

"What am I?" I asked, and heard how raw the question was.

Wren smiled, and it was a drowned boy's smile, gentle and slightly wrong, lit from underneath by green remembering. "You're the one who counts," he said. "And somebody up in that burning house of theirs is terrified of what happens when you finish the arithmetic."

Outside, the tide was coming in, filling the channels, lifting the boats, pressing its cold thumb against every door in Saltmouth. Against my ribs, the god's finger beat like a second heart, counting down to something.

Thirteen moons, the astronomer had said. The coronation. The crowning of the thing that wore King Aldric like a glove.

I had a relic, a rite half-glimpsed, a hiding place built on borrowed grace, and enemies on every road.

And somewhere across the city, a boy my own age sat on a hollow throne, lucid in fragments, leaving pleas in the margins of state papers — though I didn't know that yet. What I knew was simpler and colder: the master ledger held the binding rites, the Ledger House wanted me silenced before I reached them, and Dame Serah was closing the net with the patience of a woman who had already buried someone the gods gave back.

I looked at Maren, at Wren, at the soup pot and the mended nets and the lamplight, at this small country of kindness I had washed up in.

"I need to reach the Ledger House," I said. "Before they burn it. Before they burn me. And I can't do it alone."

Maren began, without a word, to sharpen her gutting knife.

Wren just smiled again, and in his voice, when he answered, I heard the whole drowned choir of Saltmouth shift beneath the words like current under a hull:

"Then you'd best hear what the dead want in exchange."
