## 10. The Drowned Choir

The boy led me down through the city's forgotten throat, and the water rose to meet us like an old friend coming up the stairs.

His name was Tam, though he said it the way a man says a word in a language he has only just learned he speaks. *Tam. I was Tam. I am still Tam.* He had been drowned for three years and returned wrong-sized—his mother had wept over him and fed him and slept with a knife beneath her pillow all in the same night—and now he walked beside me through the flooded underways of Vael Sarrow with his boots making no sound at all on the stone, because the water did not remember to touch him.

"You're shivering," he said.

"I'm cold."

"You're afraid." He said it without cruelty, the way children say true things before they learn that true things are weapons. "The relic knows you took it. It's been humming since we left the auction house. Can't you hear it?"

I could. The god's fingerbone sat wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs, and it hummed the way a struck bell hums after the sound should have ended—a note with no beginning anymore, only continuation. Every hour it grew a little louder. Every hour I understood a little more why the Gilded Ledger kept such things locked below the counting floors, in vaults lined with lead and silence and the ash of burned bones.

"Where are you taking me, Tam?"

"To the choir." He glanced back at me, and in the dark of the underway his eyes caught light that wasn't there. "You stole from the gods' grave-goods. You should know what they were before you decide what they are."

* * *

The catacombs beneath the eastern parishes were older than the Ledger, older than the crown, older—some said—than the habit of burying things at all. In the centuries since the last flood took the low districts, the city had simply stopped going down there. The water had made its own arrangements. Staircases descended into green-black stillness; shrines stood waist-deep, their candles long drowned but their alcoves somehow never silted over, as if something down there still tidied.

Tam stopped at the lip of a staircase where the water began, black as poured ink, and held out his hand.

"It's deep," he said. "Past your height in places. But they'll hold you up. They've been waiting to hold someone up for a very long time."

"That's not comforting."

"No," he agreed, with the ghost of a smile—the first I'd seen on him. "It isn't meant to be. Nothing down here is comforting. But it's honest, which is more than the Ledger ever gave you."

I thought of the master vaults, the bottled lullaby, the borrowed shadow accruing interest. I thought of Dame Serah's soldiers fanning through the upper city with warrants and fire-pokers. I thought of the fingerbone humming against my heart like a second pulse.

I took his hand. His grip was cool and slightly wrong, the way wet silk is wrong, and we went down into the water together.

It closed over my head and did not drown me.

That is a sentence I will never be able to explain to anyone who has not stood in those flooded halls. The water parted around my face the way curtains part for a queen—not pushed aside, exactly, but *deferred*, as if the dead holding it back considered my breathing a courtesy owed. I walked the drowned corridor in a pocket of air shaped precisely like my need, and on every side of me, pressed against the water like faces against glass, were the dead.

Hundreds. Thousands. Not ghosts as the parish priests draw them—no wailing, no chains—but people. A woman in a fishwife's apron with her sleeves rolled as if she'd been gutting cod when death found her. A soldier whose armor had gone the soft brown of drowned iron. Children. Old men. A whole congregation suspended in the green dark, and every one of them turned toward me as I passed, and every one of them was singing.

Not aloud. The water carried it instead—a pressure, a vibration I felt in my teeth and the plates of my skull and the scar-tissue of old carvings on my fingers. A hymn without words, or with words too slow for living ears, each syllable lasting as long as a season.

"They loved them," Tam said quietly, beside me. "That's the part nobody writes down. The histories say the old gods were bound because they were wicked. My mother says it. Your guild says it. But these people—" he gestured at the drowned congregation, "—these people *loved* them. Merrow taught my grandmother's grandmother which tides brought fish and which brought storms. When she died, she asked to be buried facing the sea. This is what answered prayers used to look like, Cyrus Fairweather. Before the binding. Before everything got so small."

The fingerbone against my chest pulsed once, hard, like a heartbeat stumbling.

* * *

They gathered for me in a chapel that had been underwater so long its saint's statue had grown coral for a halo.

I cannot tell you how many there were. The nave stretched back into darkness beyond the reach of any counting, and the dead packed it shoulder to shoulder—or rather, memory to memory, for flesh was long past being the point. When Tam and I entered, the hymn changed. Slowed. Resolved. Became, at last, words.

*Carver,* they sang. *Bone-carver. Last link. Come and see.*

"I didn't come to see," I said, and my voice sounded absurdly loud, absurdly alive, in that drowned hall. "I came to steal a thing and run. That's all this is."

A laugh moved through the congregation—not mocking. Sad. The way you'd laugh at a child insisting the dark is empty.

*You came carrying a god's finger under your coat,* said a voice near the front, an old man's voice, patient as sediment. *You came asking questions with your feet even if your mouth won't admit it. Ask them, then. We have waited ninety years for a Fairweather to come down and ask.*

Ninety years. Since Vess's time. Since the last binder walked these halls and—what? Refused to listen? Was refused? I thought of my mentor's burned bone, her white knuckles, her voice like a slammed door: *Never listen deeper than the surface, girl. Some voices pull.*

"How were they wicked?" I asked. It came out smaller than I intended. "The old gods. Everyone says—"

*Everyone says,* the old man echoed, gentle. *Everyone says what the binders wrote down. And who do you imagine taught the binders to write?*

The question settled over me like the water I wasn't wearing. I thought of the Gilded Ledger's founding charter, the one apprentices weren't permitted to read past the first page. I thought of chapter upon chapter of accounting in a script older than the guild's own seal. I had always assumed the guild had invented itself. Standing there, I understood, with a slow cold turning in my stomach, that institutions do not invent themselves. They are founded by someone, for some purpose, with some price already paid into the ledger's opening line.

"Tell me," I said.

And the choir told me.

* * *

They did not tell it as a single voice would. They told it as a tide tells a shoreline—in layers, in returns, each wave of voices laying down its grain of truth while the next swept it smooth. I will set it down here as best I can, though I know I flatten it, the way a transcription flattens music.

Before the binding, the old gods were not worshipped the way the new church worships its distant, silent sky-father. They were *traded with*. That was the phrase the dead used, over and over, and every time they said it I felt the fingerbone warm against my chest, recognition passing between kindred things. Merrow traded safe passage for first-caught fish. The Hearth-Mother traded hearth-warmth for stories told aloud in winter, because stories were food to her, actual food, the way bread is food to us. The Thirteenth—

Here the hymn faltered. Here the whole congregation seemed to lean inward.

*The Thirteenth was the youngest,* sang a woman whose voice I recognized with a start—it was the shape of the voice I'd heard crying out of a commissioned carving two years ago, a drowned girl's voice, and here she was, whole, unafraid. *The youngest and the hungriest, because youngest always are. It wanted to be needed the way the others were needed. It wanted names said over it at dusk. And when the trading thinned—when people built walls against the sea and lamps against the dark and decided they needed no one—it began taking instead of asking.*

*Not out of malice,* the drowned girl sang. *Out of hunger. There is a difference. A starving man who steals bread and a cruel man who burns a bakery both leave an empty shelf, but they are not the same man.*

*The gods grew hungry as belief thinned. The hungry take. The taken screamed. And the screaming reached the ears of thirteen women who could hear the dead through bone—*

My breath stopped in my borrowed air.

*—thirteen sisters of one line, carvers all, who heard the land crying out under its gods' hunger and devised a mercy. Or a theft. We have argued about which for nine hundred years, down here in the quiet, and we commend the argument to you, last link, because you are the one who must finish deciding it.*

"The binding," I whispered. "The Fairweathers bound them."

*The Fairweavers,* the old man corrected softly, and the name landed on me like a blow. *Weavers first, carvers later, when weaving became carving became keeping accounts for men who trade in shadows. The thirteen wove the gods out of the world and into relics—stone, bone, gold—each god folded small enough to fit inside a thing that could be locked away. It saved the land. The hunger stopped. The screams stopped. And the women paid for it with their hearing, generation upon generation, until the debt ran down the years to a girl in a counting-house who pretends she cannot hear us.*

I put my hand flat against my sternum, over the oilcloth, over the humming fingerbone.

"And the gods?" My voice cracked. "In the relics. All this time. Are they—"

*Asleep,* said the drowned girl. *Dreaming. Starving slowly, in the dark, in the gold, for nine hundred years. You felt it humming. Tell me, carver—does that feel like a prisoner? Or does it feel like something locked in a cellar, waiting for someone to remember it is down there?*

The moral vertigo hit me then, full force, the way Tam had probably known it would. I gripped a drowned pew to stay upright, and the wood was soft as sponge under my palm, nine-hundred-year oak gone to velvet rot, and still I gripped it, because the floor of my certainty had opened underneath me and there was nothing else to hold.

All my life I had believed in the shape of the world the Ledger drew for me: gods wicked, binding just, guild neutral, dead dangerous. And here in the flooded dark stood the dead themselves—not dangerous, *grieving*—telling me that the great mercy of the age had been a starvation. That the heroes of my inheritance had been jailers, or rescuers, or both, and had never written down which, because perhaps they hadn't known either. That Vess—Vess, who burned bones and forbade listening and loved me in her flinty, rationed way—had spent her life maintaining a prison and calling it peace.

"They were hungry," I said aloud, testing it. "So we starved them forever."

*We,* said the old man, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow worse. *Yes. We. The dead helped weave the bindings, carver. Do not think we stand here innocent. The drowned helped drown them. We chose our children's safety over the gods who fed our grandmothers, and we have had nine hundred years of water to reconsider, and some nights we still think we chose rightly. That is the horror of it. Both things are true. The binding was mercy and it was theft and the gods are dangerous and the gods are starving and your line saved the world and broke it, all in the same act, all with the same hands.*

The fingerbone pulsed against my heart, and for one terrible clear moment I felt what it felt: not malice. *Hunger*. A vast, patient, wounded want, pressing outward against its little cell of bone, dreaming of dusk-names and offerings and being needed, growing smaller and angrier and lonelier with every ignored century.

And wearing a boy on a throne. Growing less asleep every year. Counting down, somewhere in its dream, to a thirteenth moon.

"Merrow," I said suddenly. "The fisherwoman's prayer. Her son came back wrong-sized. If the bindings are failing—if the gods are waking—then answered prayers aren't miracles. They're a starving thing grabbing whatever reaches through the bars."

*Now you begin to see,* sang the whole choir, soft and vast, and the sound of it was pity, which I had never expected the dead to spend on me.

* * *

I don't know how long I stood in that chapel. Time moves strangely underwater, and stranger still among the patient dead. At some point Tam touched my arm and said the tide above was turning and Serah's patrols would change shift, and I let him pull me back along the drowned corridor while the choir's hymn followed us out, softer now, almost tender:

*Last link. Last listener. Decide with open eyes. Whatever you choose—unbind or keep the chain—choose it knowing, and not the way the Ledger knows, but the way a daughter knows her mother's hands.*

We surfaced in the underway stairwell, gasping—me gasping; Tam only pretending, kindly—and climbed back toward the grey daylight and the hunted world. The fingerbone had gone quiet against my ribs. Not silent. Quiet. The way a person goes quiet when they have finally been asked how they feel.

At the top of the stairs, grey light. Rain. The city above, unaware that its oldest crime was humming in a thief's coat pocket.

"Cyrus," Tam said, as I made ready to slip away into the alleys. He hesitated, then held something out: a scrap of oilskin, salt-stiff, folded many times. "One of the choir gave me this to give you. Said you'd need it before the moon turns. Said—" he frowned, remembering, "—said *the boy writes in margins*."

I unfolded it with numb fingers.

Inside, in cramped cipher I half-recognized—guild shorthand, ledger-hand, the private code of clerks—ran a single repeated line, in a handwriting far too young and far too careful to belong to any bureaucrat:

*If anyone can read this: I am in here. Please hurry. —A.*

I looked up at Tam. He was already fading into the rain, going home to a mother who feared him, doing his errand between worlds.

"Aldric," I breathed, and the name made a small fog in the cold air, and somewhere across the city a crowned boy sat at a desk writing pleas inside state documents, lucid in fragments, hoping against hope that somewhere a bone-carver was learning to listen.

The paper trembled in my hands. Not from the wind.

From the fingerbone, humming again—softly, hungrily, patiently—as if it knew I had just made a promise I didn't yet understand.
