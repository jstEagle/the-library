## 11. Aldric's Letter

The letter came inside a fish.

Not wrapped in one, not hidden in the crate beneath a bed of ice, but *inside* one—a gutted cod from the eastern market, its belly stitched shut with red thread, and when Cyrus cut the stitches with her smallest bone knife she found a roll of vellum sealed in wax the color of dried blood. The fisherwoman's impossible son had carried it through half the city in a bucket of brine, whistling, daring anyone to search a boy hauling home his mother's supper.

Cyrus read it three times before her hands stopped shaking.

*To the carver who hears me,* it began. No name. No title. The hand was young and careful, the letters of a boy taught penmanship by masters who would beat him for a blot—except every seventh word was wrong. Not misspelled. *Wrong.* Words that did not belong, planted like crooked teeth in an otherwise even row: *crown, hunger, thirteenth, unmake.*

She understood at once what she was holding, and the understanding went through her like cold water through cloth. A king's letters were read. A king's letters were copied, archived, filed away in the Gilded Ledger among ten thousand documents of state that crossed guild desks for tariff and tithe. Any letter from Aldric that said plainly *I am possessed, help me* would be intercepted before it left the palace grounds—and the boy behind the eyes would pay for writing it.

So he had written it sideways. He had buried his plea in the language of trade, where only someone fluent in impossible things would think to look for meaning in the seams.

*The crown is not gold,* the letter said, in the middle of a dull paragraph about grain tariffs. *It is a mouth. I have been swallowed politely.*

Cyrus sat down on the floor of the safehouse—the fisherwoman's loft above the salt-sheds, where the drowned boy slept in the next room and dreamed of water without fear—and she read it a fourth time, aloud, because some part of her needed to hear the words exist in air.

"He knows," she said. "Vess told me he'd be gone by now. Hollowed out. But he *knows.*"

The dead, as always, answered with silence. They had been quieter since the flooded catacombs, since the choir sang their side of the story—as if even they were waiting to see what she would do with what she'd learned.

She sharpened a quill. Then she began to write back—in the same sideways tongue, praying she had learned it well enough.

* *

*To the Guild of the Gilded Ledger, Office of Relic Appraisal, attention C.F.,*

*I write regarding your assessment of the royal collection, which His Majesty's household awaits with impatience. The inventory proceeds slowly. There are more pieces than the catalogs record, and some of them are heavier than they should be.*

*Crown business is long business. Some mornings I wake and do not know what year it is. Some nights I count the stones above my bed and arrive at thirteen no matter how many times I begin again. The tutors say arithmetic is the surest cure for sleeplessness. I have not found this so.*

*My grandfather kept hawks. When a hawk took ill, he would wrap it in his cloak and hold it against his chest until it remembered it was a bird and not the fever. I think of this often. If you have ever held something wild and frightened against your chest until it calmed, you will understand the arrangement here, though perhaps not which of us is the hawk.*

*The physicians prescribe fresh air. I am permitted the east gallery between the second and third bells, when the moon is setting over the harbor. It is the only hour the corridors are empty. Even my shadow keeps other appointments then.*

*I enclose a schedule of the grain shipments, as requested, though I fear the figures are unreliable. Everything in this house is counted twice and arrives at two different numbers. If any of your appraisers can tell me which count is true, I would be grateful beyond the value of the commission.*

*In service of the ledger,*
*A.R.*

* *

He was cleverer than he had any right to be, Cyrus thought, and sadder than anyone should be allowed to be at sixteen.

She read the letter again by candlelight while rain worked at the shutters. The grain tariffs were nonsense, deliberately so—but the rest, the parts that looked like a lonely boy's rambling, rang true in ways that made her chest ache. *Some mornings I wake and do not know what year it is.* She had heard exactly that cadence once before, murmuring up through a carved rib bone she had never been able to finish working, a voice that spoke around something lodged in it the way a river speaks around a stone.

And there, threaded through the pleasantries: the schedule of bells. *Between the second and third bells,* when the east gallery stood empty. That was not a complaint about insomnia. That was a door being held open.

*Even my shadow keeps other appointments then.* She thought of the throne room, of the boy-king's shadow lagging behind him by half a heartbeat, arriving late to his own body like a servant who had learned not to be seen beside his master. The shadow moved when the god allowed it—or the god moved it, wearing it the way it wore everything else of Aldric's.

But *between the second and third bells* —that was the fragment. That was the boy alone in the dark of his own palace, holding a candle up to the crack in himself and hoping someone was looking through from the other side.

She wrote her reply small, in the margin-style of a merchant's correction, and sent it back the way it had come: sewn in a fish, carried by a drowned boy who whistled past the guards as if he had been born to it. Perhaps he had. Drowning teaches a person how to move through places where breathing is not permitted.

* *

*To the appraiser C.F., whose discretion precedes her,*

*Your corrections to the grain figures were most instructive. You will be pleased to know I have adopted them entirely. One does not argue with expertise.*

*You ask after my health. I am well. I am remarkably well. I have never been better, and if I repeat that often enough I almost believe it, which I am told is the definition of health.*

*You ask what the crown weighs. This is an unusual question and I will answer it unusually. It weighs nothing on the head. That is the trick of it. A heavy thing announces itself; you learn to carry it or set it down. This is not heavy. This is comfortable. That is how I know it means to keep me. Nothing comfortable has ever meant well by me—not the soft beds after my father died, not the flatterers, not the wine the steward leaves warm at my elbow each night though I have never asked for it. The crown fits the way grief fits. You stop noticing it is there, and then one day you reach up to touch your own head and find someone else's hands already resting on it.*

*There is a game I play with myself. In the morning I hide one true thing from Him—one small fact, the color of my mother's dress, the name of my first dog, the sound the harbor gulls make at dawn—and I carry it all day like a coin in a boot, and at night, between the second bell and the third, I take it out and look at it and confirm I am still the one holding it. So far I have not lost the gulls. I mention this only because you seem a collector of small valuable things, and I thought you might appreciate the method.*

*If the appraisal cannot be completed—if the piece proves beyond restoration—tell me honestly. My grandfather also kept old dogs, and he taught me that the kindest thing a man can do for a loyal animal past saving is to stay in the room while it is done, and hold it, so it does not go frightened. I ask only that whoever performs the work be steady-handed, and that I be permitted to remain myself until the final moment. I want to see it coming. I have spent enough time surprised.*

*In service of the ledger,*
*A.R.*

* *

Cyrus did not sleep that night.

She sat by the window with the letter in her lap and watched the harbor lamps blur and steady, blur and steady, and understood that she had just read a boy asking her, in the politest language his cage permitted, to kill him.

*If the piece proves beyond restoration, tell me honestly.* Unmake me. Stay in the room. Hold it so it does not go frightened.

"I'm not going to," she whispered to him across the whole sleeping city. "Do you hear me? I'm not going to unmake you. I'm going to take it off."

The dead stirred faintly at that, a rustle like pages turning, and she pressed her lips shut. She had made promises to the dead before. She knew better than to make them lightly now. But this one she let stand.

She wrote back at dawn.

* *

*To His Majesty's household, office of relic appraisal,*

*The commission continues. Be assured that no piece in your collection is beyond restoration. I have examined far worse and seen them returned to usefulness. The work requires patience, correct tools, and above all a clear reading of the original craft—how the thing was made determines how it may be mended. Nothing is joined so firmly that it cannot be unjoined by the same hand, in the same order, with the proper words spoken over the joints.*

*Regarding your question of remaining yourself until the end: the best restorers work slowly, and the owner is present throughout. He watches every stage. Nothing is done to a piece while it sleeps unless the piece itself has asked.*

*You mention hiding true things from a thief. I know this practice. We who deal in rare objects learn to mark our goods in invisible ways—a scratch beneath the gilt, a knot tied wrong on purpose, so that if the item is stolen and returned to us altered, we know it, and can prove what it truly was. May I suggest you continue your game, but change what you hide each day? A thief grows lazy guarding a treasure he believes fully catalogued. Keep one shelf of yourself he has never seen. Do not show anyone. When the time comes, that shelf will be how we know you.*

*A schedule note: the appraisal must conclude before the thirteenth turn of the moon. After that date, the workshop closes and no further work can be undertaken. I say this plainly so there can be no misunderstanding. Whatever is unfinished on the eve of the thirteenth turn stays unfinished forever. We work toward that night or we do not work at all.*

*The gulls at dawn, incidentally, sound like rusty hinges over water. If yours have stopped sounding like that, check who is listening through your ears.*

*In service of the ledger,*
*C.F.*

* *

The replies came faster after that. Twice a week, then three times, each one sewn into fish or bread or the hollow spine of a psalter, each one a masterpiece of doubled speech. To the clerks who inevitably skimmed such correspondence, it was tedious bureaucratic small talk—tariff schedules, inventory complaints, the fussy pedantry of an appraiser and a punctilious young king. Only the two of them stood in the second layer, the real one, passing truths hand to hand through the walls of their prison of sentences.

Aldric told her the shape of the god's habits. *He sleeps shallowest after public audiences; feeding on adoration makes Him drowsy, like a man after too rich a meal.* He told her the geography of his own captivity. *There is a corridor in me where I can still walk freely. It runs from the memory of my mother's voice to the sound of the harbor. He has not walled it off because He thinks it worthless. Let Him keep thinking so.* He told her, once, in a passage that made Cyrus set the letter down and press both hands over her mouth:

*Some nights He lets me watch through my own eyes while He uses my body. He thinks it is mercy. He shows me the petitions He grants and says, see, I am a good king, I give the people what they pray for. Last month a widow asked for rain on her blighted fields and He gave it to her, three days of it, and the river took two villages downstream, and He showed me the bodies floating past the harbor wall and said, see how much I love them. I laughed when He wanted me to laugh. I am sorry for that. I need you to know I am sorry for every time I laughed.*

Cyrus wrote back the only thing worth writing: *I forgive you. Laughing in a cage is survival, not consent. Keep the gulls.*

And she told him things too. Carefully, in the sideways language—about the stolen fingerbone wrapped in oilcloth beneath her floorboards, cold as deep water and humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has technically ended. About Dame Serah, converted now, drilling her defected soldiers in a quarry outside the walls, learning to aim her fire not at gods but at the men who served them. About the drowned boy who carried letters through the city and had begun, shyly, to call her *sister*, though neither of them remarked on it.

About Vess. She told him about Vess dying slowly in the fisherwoman's spare bed, her hands too swollen now to carve, dictating fragments of the old rites while Cyrus transcribed them in shorthand no guild clerk could parse. *The binder must speak plainly. The vessel must consent. The threshold must be moonlit. Get these three things wrong and you do not free a god, you feed one.*

Aldric's reply to that was short, and it was the moment, Cyrus later thought, when the correspondence stopped being strategy and became covenant:

*I understand the three requirements, and I give the second one now, in advance, in writing, witnessed by whatever is holy enough to still be listening. I consent. Whatever must be done to me on the thirteenth night, I consent to it. If it frees Him and kills me, I consent to that too, and I die grateful. But if there is any craft, any art, any trick of your trade that can leave a little of me standing afterward—a shelf He never catalogued—then I ask you, carver, as one keeper of small valuable things to another: save the gulls. Save the sound the hinges make. I should like, someday, to hear the harbor with my own ears, late, when nobody is watching, just once, and know that the listening is mine.*

She read that line forty times. She read it until the candles burned to pools and the drowned boy knocked softly and asked if she was well, and she said yes, and discovered it was nearly true.

Then she sat down and wrote the last letter of that exchange, the one she sealed with her own blood pricked from a carving-scarred thumb, because some promises deserve a price paid up front:

*Your Majesty—I will not call you anything else, whatever they put on the coins—you will hear the harbor. You will hear it late, when nobody is watching, and the listening will be yours, and it will sound like rusty hinges over water, and you will know that every word of it belongs to you. I swear it on the Fairweather name, which I have lately learned is older and owes more than I ever knew. On the thirteenth night the work begins. Be on your shelf when we come for you. Hold the gulls. Count thirteen stones and laugh at the arithmetic, because the number will finally be wrong, and wrong in your favor.*

*We are coming. Steady hands. You will not be surprised.*

*—C.F.*

She sent it at first light, sewn inside a haddock, and stood at the window watching the drowned boy's whistle fade down the salt-stinking street.

It was only when she turned back to pack the letters away beneath the floorboards—lifting the loose plank, reaching past the oilcloth bundle—that her fingers brushed the stolen fingerbone and the humming changed.

It was not humming anymore.

Something in the relic had opened like an eye. And through it, patient and vast and horribly gentle, something was looking back at her—reading her promise, she realized, the way she had learned to read Aldric's letters: every word, and the truth underneath every word.

The bone was warm. For the first time since she'd stolen it, the god's fingerbone was warm as a living hand.
