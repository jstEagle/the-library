## 12. The Price Named Aloud

The fingerbone did not like firelight.

Cyrus had learned this on the third night, when she carried it too near her candle and it went cold in her palm—not the passive coolness of bone, which holds the chill of whatever grave it was dug from, but an active cold, a pulling-away, as if the thing had flinched. She had set it then on the windowsill of the rented room above the chandler's shop, where the drowned boy slept in the loft and the fisherwoman's son dreamed of water he no longer feared, and she had watched moonlight pool along its length like milk poured into a narrow glass.

It was a fingerbone. That much she knew from the auction house, from the wax-sealed provenance that named it *relic of the Thirteenth, recovered from the river silt at Grayford, year of the Long Sleep*. A god's smallest joint, sold for a city's worth of forgetting—every bidder in that black gallery had surrendered a memory to touch the velvet lining of its case, and none of them could now recall what they had given up or why they had wanted to.

Cyrus had stolen it instead. Theft, Vess would have said, is only trade with the ledger closed.

She picked it up now, in the blue hour before dawn, and held it the way she held all her work: loosely, respectfully, the way one holds a bird that might be sleeping rather than dead.

"Show me," she whispered.

The bone obliged.

* * *

It did not speak the way the dead spoke. The dead murmured—they came up through the marrow of her carving knives like water through limestone, seeping, patient, full of the small grievances and small tendernesses of finished lives. This was different. The fingerbone *remembered*, and remembering, it made her remember, and there was no seam between where its knowledge ended and hers began. She was standing in a room she had never entered, in a body she had never worn, and she understood with terrible clarity that she was seeing through Fairweather eyes.

Her grandmother's eyes. Or her grandmother's grandmother's—the face in the bone's memory was hers, would have been hers, cheekbones and crooked chin and hands scarred white at every knuckle from decades of knife-work. The woman sat at a bench identical to the one Vess kept in the back room of the Ledger House, down to the burn mark on the left leg where a candle had been knocked over by someone startled by a voice.

Around the woman stood others. Not living others. The air in the memory-room was thick with them, a congregation of the dead so dense that Cyrus felt her skin crawl even secondhand—the pressure of hundreds of voices all speaking at once, and the woman hearing every one, because that was what Fairweather women did, that was the gift entire: not whispers here and there but a whole drowned choir, always, without mercy or mute.

A voice in the memory said—and the bone let her hear it clearly, let her stand inside the words the way you stand inside a bell—

*"The rite requires surrender, daughter. Understand what we are asking before you kneel."*

And the woman who wore Cyrus's face said, "I understand."

"You do not. No one does, until it is done. Listen." The speaker was old, older than the bench, older perhaps than the guild; her voice came from everywhere in the room because she was everywhere in the room, or had been once. "To unbind a god is to open the seal with your own hand. And the seal is not ink or iron, child. It is us. It has always been us. Our hearing is the lock, our listening is the keyhole, and when the last Fairweather gives up the gift—gives it up *willingly*, wholly, without bargaining or regret—the chain breaks. Every voice you have ever heard goes silent. Every voice you ever would have heard. The dead will pass you in the street like strangers. Your mother, if she dies before you. Your daughters, if you have any. All of it. Gone. Forever is not a word the living should use lightly, and we are asking you to use it."

The room waited. In the memory, dust moved through a shaft of window light, unhurried, indifferent.

"And if I refuse?" the young woman asked.

"Then the binding holds until you die naturally, and your daughter inherits the choice, and hers after her, and the gods sleep on in their relics, hungry and harmless, and the world goes on paying its small debts." A pause, and when the old voice resumed it was softer. "Or you say yes, and you live out your days in silence, and some girl centuries hence will curse you for taking the music out of the family."

"I won't be cursed," the young Fairweather said, and there it was—that stubborn set to the jaw that Cyrus saw in her own reflection every morning. "I'll be thanked. Eventually. By someone who never has to hear what I heard."

The memory shivered. The bone had shown her what it wanted her to see, and now it showed her the cost of seeing: the room dissolved like sugar in rain, and Cyrus was back in her own body, in her own rented room, kneeling on cold floorboards with tears running into her mouth and the fingerbone warm in her hand for the first time since Grayford.

Warm, she thought. Because it had finished telling me. Because it was satisfied.

She sat on the floor until the chandler's cart rattled past below and the light went from blue to gray to the color of weak tea, and she did not carve, and she did not listen to the dead who gathered politely at the edges of her hearing, waiting for her to pick up a tool.

* * *

Vess was dying in the way old buildings die—slowly enough that everyone pretended otherwise, visibly enough that everyone knew. Her cough had gone from seasonal to constitutional, and her hands, which had carved forty thousand instruments of memory in their time, now shook unless she pressed them flat against the table.

Cyrus found her in the back room, of course. However many times Cyrus fled the Ledger House, however far the road took her, she always came back to this room, this bench, this woman. Vess was sorting bone blanks by weight, which was work she could do sitting down, and she looked up when Cyrus entered with the expression of someone who had been expecting bad news for three days and was relieved to finally receive it.

"You've been to the bone," Vess said. Not a question.

"How did you—"

"Because you walk like a woman carrying a ledger she hasn't opened yet, and now you walk like a woman who has." Vess pushed a stool toward her with one foot. "Sit. Tell me what it showed you."

So Cyrus told her. The room of ancestors. The old voice. The terms, laid out plain as a bill of sale: willing surrender, whole and without reservation, the gift severed root and branch, every dead voice silenced forever—including, though neither of them said it yet, the voice of anyone who died after the severance. Including Vess.

When she finished, Vess was quiet for a long time. Outside, the guild bells rang the hour, and beneath the bells Cyrus could hear the dead of the Ledger House going about their unfinished business—a clerk still reconciling accounts sixty years after his death, a laundress humming a song no one living remembered. Ordinary ghosts. Harmless ones. The kind she had built her whole careful life around pretending were furniture.

"That's the price," Vess said at last. "That's what it costs to take the crown off the boy."

"That's what it costs," Cyrus agreed.

"And you're sitting there looking like a woman doing arithmetic." Vess's laugh turned into a cough, and she rode it out with the grim patience of long practice. "Say it aloud, girl. You know my rule. A debt unnamed grows teeth."

"The math is simple." Cyrus heard her own voice go flat, the guild-voice, the auditor's voice she used when the numbers were ugly. "One boy king, sixteen years old, possessed by a god who will wear him until there's nothing left. Against—" and here the flatness failed her— "against every voice I've ever heard. My mother's voice. Yours. Everyone who comes after. Forever. One life weighed against a whole inheritance of listening."

"Not quite." Vess laid both trembling hands flat on the table, the way she did when she wanted to be believed. "You're weighing it wrong. You're weighing *your* silence against *his* freedom. But that's not the trade. The trade is your silence against his slavery. His whole self, eaten slowly, century after century, by something so lonely it wears children like coats. That's what you're pricing, Cyrus. Price it honestly."

"It isn't honest either!" The words came out of her hot and faster than she meant them to. "You want me to say it's noble. You want me to kneel at the coronation and give it up smiling. But you're dying, Vess. Weeks, maybe. And when you go, if I've done it—if I've cut the gift out of me—I won't hear you. Your last words will go into the dark and I will be standing right there, holding your hand, and there will be nothing. Do you understand? Every Fairweather woman for six hundred years got to hear her mother's ghost. Got to keep her. I'm the one who ends it. I'm the last link, and the chain doesn't break clean—it breaks through *me*, and everything on the other side of the break is gone."

The room held still. Even the clerk-ghost had stopped shuffling his papers.

"Yes," Vess said quietly. "That's exactly it. That's the shape of it. Now you've named the price aloud, and it can't grow teeth anymore, because teeth need darkness."

"Don't do that. Don't make it tidy."

"I'm not making it tidy. I'm making it *true*." Vess reached across the table and took Cyrus's wrist, her grip still strong even now, carver-strong. "Child, I have listened to the dead for seventy years. Do you know what most of them say? *Let go. Let go. Let go.* They love us and they say let go, because they can see what the listening does to us—we carry them like stones in our pockets until we can't stand upright. Your mother stayed twelve years past her death because you couldn't release her. Don't think I didn't know. Don't think she didn't ask me, at the end, whether you'd ever be free."

"She never said—"

"She said it every day. You just weren't ready to hear it." Vess released her wrist and sat back. "Here is what I will tell you, and then you'll go away and hate me for a while, which is proper. If you refuse the rite, Aldric dies as himself within the year—the god will finish wearing him out, the way a glove wears through at the fingers. If you accept, he lives, and the gods scatter back into prayer where they belong, and you spend the rest of your life in a quiet that you will curse every day for ten years and then, one morning, wake up inside and find it fits. Silence is only a wound if you keep measuring it against the noise."

"And you?" Cyrus's voice cracked on it. "What happens to you?"

Vess smiled, and it was the smile she wore when she caught a lie in a ledger—fond and merciless at once.

"I die," she said. "I was going to do that regardless. The only question is whether my last words fall on deaf ears or dead ones." She tapped the table twice, an auditor's flourish, closing an entry. "Either way, girl, they get spoken. Go home. Sleep. Come back tomorrow and tell me you've decided, and tell me the truth, because I'll hear it in your hands."

* * *

Cyrus walked back through the city in full dark, and for the first time in her life she resented the dead.

They were everywhere, as they had always been everywhere—the lamplighter's ghost relighting lamps that no longer existed, a child's voice chasing a hoop down an alley that had been bricked over for a century, the drowned singing under the wharf pilings in their slow tidal rounds. She had spent thirty years treating them as weather, as wallpaper, as the constant hum beneath the world's music. Now she walked among them counting them, and each one was a name on the list of things she would lose: the lamplighter. The hoop-child. The drowned choir. Vess. Her mother, whose voice she had not heard in four years but would hear again someday, when Cyrus herself grew old enough to start leaving messages in bone, and who would answer—

No. Would not answer. Would pass her in the street like a stranger.

At the chandler's shop, the drowned boy was awake. He often was, at night; sleep came hard to a boy who had already done his drowning once. He was sitting on the stairs with his knees drawn up, and he watched her come in with those river-gray eyes that had seen the bottom of the world.

"You talked to the old woman," he said.

"How do you know?"

"Your hands are empty." He unfolded himself and came down a step. "They're never empty. You always hold a knife or a bone or something. Tonight you came in with nothing, like you'd put it all down somewhere." He tilted his head. "Was it bad? What you found out?"

Cyrus sat down beside him on the stair. From the loft above, the fisherwoman's breathing rose and fell, steady as surf.

"A boy is going to die," she said. "Unless I pay something I can't get back."

The drowned boy considered this with the gravity of someone who had personally audited the exchange rate between the living and the dead. Then he said, "When I was underwater, I heard my mother praying. Did you know that? Down at the bottom, with my lungs full, I heard her asking Merrow to send me back, and I thought, *she shouldn't do that, prayers cost more than people know.*" He shrugged. "But she did it anyway. And here I am. Wrong-sized and dripping." He looked at her sideways. "Mothers pay. That's just how it works. Maybe you're the mother this time."

"I'm not anybody's mother."

"No," he agreed. "You're everybody's daughter, though. Six hundred years of them. That's heavier."

He went up to the loft, and Cyrus sat alone on the stairs until dawn, turning a bone over in her mind that she wasn't holding in her hand, and somewhere in the grayest part of the morning she realized she had already decided—that she had decided in Vess's back room, maybe, or on the stairs of her grandmother's memory, or six hundred years ago in a woman's throat saying *I'll be thanked eventually.*

She had decided. What remained was only the telling.

Which was when the knock came—three sharp raps at the chandler's door, before first light, from a figure in the street below wrapped in a traveling cloak with the sigil of the Crown's inquisition half-hidden at the collar, and a voice Cyrus recognized from the auction house, from the hunt, from Serah's warrants nailed to every church door:

"Carver. Open the door. I bring news of the Ledger House, and you will want to be sitting down."
