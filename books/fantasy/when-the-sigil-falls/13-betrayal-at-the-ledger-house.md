## 13. Betrayal at the Ledger House

The Ledger House smelled of tallow and old ink, the way it always had, and for one foolish moment Cyrus felt she had come home.

She had told herself the feeling was a trick of habit—the body remembering what the mind had already renounced. She stood in the shadowed mouth of Cartway Lane with her hood drawn low and the master ledger's weight hidden against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her coat, and let herself remember eleven years of mornings that began exactly like this: crossing the flagstones while the gutters still ran silver, nodding to the porter, climbing the stair worn concave by generations of clerks' feet. Eleven years of believing the building above her was a bank of debts, nothing more. A place where impossible things were weighed and priced until they became merely expensive.

She knew better now. She had read what slept between the ledger's pages. She knew the guild had not merely recorded the binding of the old gods—it had performed it, priced it, and profited by it for nine generations. The Fairweather women had been their hands. Her mother's mother's mother had signed her name beside a god's imprisonment and called it an entry.

And still her feet carried her up those stairs.

Because Vess might yet be saved. Because the fingerbone had shown her the shape of the price and the price required resources no fugitive could gather alone—moon-silver, consecrated bone-dust, a threshold carved true. Because some stubborn, stupid organ behind her sternum insisted that the institution which had raised her must contain, somewhere in its marble heart, one person who would choose blood over profit.

She should have known better. She had been raised on ledgers. She of all people knew what happened to anything that could not be valued: it got written off.

* * *

The under-clerk who admitted her was a boy named Pellin, sixteen at most, with ink stains to his knuckles and the nervous devotion of someone who still believed the guild was a temple. He had loved her once, in the shy way apprentices love senior auditors—she had taught him to spot forged grief in memory-contracts, how a false mourning always rounded its numbers too neatly.

"Cyrus Fairweather," he breathed, and looked over his shoulder before he even opened the door wider. "Saints' teeth, you shouldn't be here. There are proclamations. There's a *writ*—"

"I know there's a writ." Serah's face had been nailed to every parish post from the river districts inward, rendered in woodcut so severe it barely resembled the woman. "That's why I came through the cart entrance and why you're going to walk me up the back stair instead of shouting about it."

"They said—" His throat worked. "They said if anyone brought word of your whereabouts, there'd be a dispensation. Forgiveness of family debt. Full forgiveness."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. He could not hold it. His eyes went to the floor, to her boots, to the wet hem of her coat, anywhere but her face.

"Pellin," she said quietly. "Is there someone waiting upstairs?"

"No," he said. And then, because he was sixteen and had not yet learned that a clerk's whole trade is the precise arrangement of truth: "I mean—not for *you*. They're in session. The masters. With the inquisitor. But that's not about you, that's about licensing, about the new protections, the warding contracts—"

"Serah is here."

"The Dame Inquisitor is consulting with the board." He recited it like scripture. "On the matter of god-trafficking."

"God-trafficking." Cyrus almost laughed. The word was perfect. It was exactly the kind of word this house would coin—clean, commercial, deniable. "Pellin. Look at me. Whatever they've promised you—whatever they promised anyone—I need you to go home when your shift ends. Do you understand? Not to your lodging over the chandlery. Home. To your mother's. Tonight."

"You're scaring me."

"Good," she said. "Fear keeps better accounts than hope does. I learned that here."

She left him standing in the entry hall with his mouth open, and climbed the back stair alone.

* * *

The boardroom sat at the crown of the Ledger House, a long chamber paneled in black oak, lit by a skylight that turned everything below into something examined, specimen-like. As an auditor she had stood at its far end twice in her life, presenting findings, watching the seven masters' faces arrange themselves into expressions of grave consideration that meant *no*.

Tonight she did not go to the far end. Tonight she stood where the walls met the ceiling, in the old auditors' gallery—a narrow balcony behind a carved screen, built in the house's first century so that junior clerks could observe the masters' deliberations and learn the sacred art of pricing. No one used it anymore. No one remembered it except her, because as a girl she had hidden there once, fleeing Vess's arithmetic drills, and had heard things a child should not have heard.

She heard them again now.

Seven masters sat below her, and across the table, alone, sat Dame Serah in traveling leathers, her helm on the table beside her hand like a second head.

"—the writ stands regardless," Serah was saying. Her voice was flat as slate. "I don't require your cooperation. I require only that you not obstruct me. What I'm offering is the alternative."

Master Corvane presided—Vess's successor as Master of Audits, a narrow man whose beard was trimmed with mathematical precision and whose kindness had always been of the sort extended to useful instruments. He spread his hands on the table.

"Dame Serah. Let us speak plainly, as colleagues. You believe the Fairweather woman possesses materials dangerous to the Crown. We believe she has stolen guild property of considerable value. These interests align."

"She has stolen *nothing*," Serah said. "What she carries, she carries by right of inheritance older than this house."

Cyrus felt the words strike her like cold water. *Older than this house.* Serah knew. Serah had read enough—or guessed enough—to know the ledger was not the origin but the record.

Corvane's smile thinned. "Be that as it may. Our proposal is simple. When she comes—as she will come; she's sentimental, and sentiment makes debtors predictable—we deliver her to you. Alive, unharmed, and with the stolen property recovered intact. In exchange: full immunity for this house and its officers. All prior transactions involving pre-Ascension relics to be struck from the record. Amnestied."

"And your protection contracts?" Serah's gaze swept the table. "The ones you've been selling since the first answered prayer. Wardings against the old gods, at forty times the honest rate. I have parishes in the east where families sold their *graves*—their burial plots, their ancestors' rest—to buy charms your own audit shows to be painted glass." She lifted a folded paper. "Painted glass, Master Corvane. I've had it tested."

A murmur moved down the table. Cyrus pressed her palm hard against the gallery rail to keep her hands from shaking. She had audited some of those contracts. She had priced the painted glass herself, in her careful clerk's hand, and never once asked what lay beneath the gilding. She had thought herself innocent because she had only counted.

"The contracts were made in good faith," Corvane said smoothly.

"The contracts were made by men who *knew the gods were waking*," Serah said, "because your house put them to sleep, and knew the terms of their waking, and chose to sell fear rather than spend truth. Don't preach good faith to me. I burned my brother's body myself. Good faith is not a currency I accept."

Silence below. In the gallery, Cyrus stopped breathing.

Then Master Oveline spoke—elderly, soft, the keeper of the reliquary vaults, a woman who had once given Cyrus honey-cakes and told her the bones sang sweeter when the carver was happy. "And the girl herself. What happens to her in your custody?"

"That depends on her," Serah said. "If she surrenders the relic and the rites willingly, she answers questions and lives. If not—" A small shrug. "The Crown's law is fire for traffickers. I am not required to enjoy it."

"You'd hang the last binder," Oveline said, "the only soul alive who can undo what our founders did—and we'd be paid in amnesty for the privilege."

"We'd be paid in *survival*," Corvane snapped, and for the first time the polish cracked and something raw showed through, something afraid. "Have you looked out a window, Oveline? Prayers are being *answered*. Things are coming back that we bound—our grandmothers bound—with blood-price and silence-oath. The Thirteenth sits on the throne wearing a boy like a glove, and every day it consolidates, every parish it swallows makes the eventual settling of accounts worse. This house has nine generations of sins itemized in its vaults. When the gods finish waking, do you imagine they'll honor our bookkeeping? We can't fight them. We can't buy them. All we can do is give the Crown its scapegoat quickly and cleanly and pray the new order remembers us as cooperative."

"So we sell her." Oveline's voice was very quiet. "We sell the last daughter of the women who kept the world asleep. The same women who paid for our sleep with their hearing, generation upon generation, so that this house could stand here tonight and discuss her like a line item."

"It is precisely because they paid," Corvane said, "that we owe them nothing. Debts discharged are debts ended, Oveline. That's the whole of our faith. The Fairweather obligation was settled when the last rite was sealed. What remains is not family. It is *residual liability*."

Cyrus closed her eyes. She had expected anger, or fear, or greed. She had braced for all three. She had not prepared for *accounting language*—for her life to be translated without malice, without heat, into a column marked for cancellation. That was the wound. Not that they chose profit. That they chose it *neatly*, the way she had been trained to choose everything, and she could follow every step of the reasoning because it was her own training, her own craft, turned upon her like a mirror.

Below, Serah gathered her papers.

"Then we're agreed," she said. "Deliver her within three days, or I come back with soldiers and take her along with everything else—including your vaults, which I will open with or without your consent, because I have a warrant now that supersedes guild charter. Consider whether amnesty is worth more than your charter."

She rose. And then, at the door, she paused, and without turning said:

"One more thing. The apprentice who let her in tonight—the boy with the ink-stained hands. Your porter will report him by morning. See that he's gone before then, if any of you remember what mercy costs."

The door closed behind her. Cyrus stayed crouched in the dark of the gallery, listening to the seven masters below begin, immediately, without shame, to draft the terms of her surrender.

* * *

She did not remember descending the back stair. Her body carried her on the old routes while her mind stood empty as a cleared account. It was raining harder now, rain ticking on the cart-canopies, and the gutters ran with lamplight, and she walked among the evening crowds of Cartway Lane—fishwives, porters, a knife-grinder singing—and understood that any one of them might be bought for a dispensation. Full forgiveness of family debt. The guild had taught the whole city to think in ledgers. It had taught *her*.

She was nearly to the lane's end when Pellin caught her.

He came out of an alley at a dead run, breathless, hoodless, reckless, and shoved something into her hands—a small canvas pouch, heavy, clinking.

"What—" she began.

"Ward-salt and two vials of the real charm, the vault-grade, I stole them off Oveline's shelf, she'll know it was me by morning anyway so it hardly matters." He was talking too fast, the way the guilty and the brave talk alike. "You hid in the gallery. I saw you go up. I used to watch where you went when you thought no one was looking—you taught me that too, actually, watching. So I listened from the passage."

"Pellin—"

"I'm going home to my mother's. Like you said." He tried to smile and failed at it. "But I wanted you to know it wasn't all of us. The house, I mean. The house is just... the house is a set of books that learned to walk around. Books don't get to choose. But people inside them still do. You did. So."

He was backing away even as he finished, and the crowd took him the way water takes a dropped coin, and he was gone.

Cyrus stood in the rain holding a dead apprentice's weight in stolen salt, and found that she was crying—angrily, uselessly, the tears indistinguishable from the weather. Three days. She had three days before the house delivered her, and less than that before Serah returned with soldiers and warrants, and somewhere across the city a boy-king sat under a whispering crown counting moons he did not yet know had grown so few.

She pulled the hood low and turned toward the river, toward the fisherwoman's district, toward the drowned boy who remembered dying and the choir of grieving families gathering in the dark.

Behind her, the Ledger House windows burned yellow and orderly, each one a candle over a column, each column balanced, every debt assigned—

—and in the highest window, briefly, a silhouette crossed the light: Master Corvane, pen in hand, already writing her name.
