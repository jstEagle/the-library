## 15. Serah's Bargain

The tide pools at Grayharbor held the sky wrong that morning—not reflected but *stored*, as if the water had been drinking light for years and had grown heavy with it. Cyrus crouched at the pool's edge with her back against the barnacled stones of the sea-wall, and she counted the ways out of this cove and found there was only one, and Dame Serah was standing in it.

The inquisitor had traded her white armor for oilskin and gray wool, but nothing could be done about her posture. She stood the way gallows stand. In her gauntleted hand she held not a sword but a small iron censer, unlit, swinging gently—a thing for carrying smoke, or for carrying proof.

"You've run well," Serah said. Her voice carried flat across the water, stripped of the courtroom music Cyrus had heard through palace doors. "Four parishes. Two ferries. A smuggler's hold full of eels, which I'm told you didn't enjoy."

"I didn't," Cyrus said. Her hand found the bundle beneath her coat—the stolen fingerbone wrapped in altar cloth, warm as a sleeping bird. Behind her, the sea heaved its long gray shoulder against the wall. Somewhere above, on the cliff path, she could hear Tam's uneven footsteps moving away, the drowned boy doing what she'd asked him to do: *run, don't look back, don't drown twice for me.*

"There's no one behind me," she said.

"No," Serah agreed. "There's no one behind you. That's what frightens you, isn't it? That I came alone." She walked closer along the tideline, boots crunching shell-hash, and stopped an arm's length from the nearest pool. "You keep waiting for the trap. The soldiers in the rocks. I watched you check when I came down the path. You checked three times."

"Guild habit. We appraise everything."

"Then appraise me." Serah lowered herself—slowly, joints complaining—to sit on a rock, and the gesture was so ordinary it was nearly obscene. An inquisitor of the Crown did not sit on wet stone like a fishwife. "I've been chasing you for nine days. Do you know what my superiors think you stole?"

"A relic."

"They think you stole a *weapon*. They think the old gods left pieces of themselves lying about like dropped coins, and that a woman who can hear them might pick one up and spend it." Serah looked out at the horizon, where sea and sky had made their old gray pact. "They're not entirely wrong. So before I decide what you are, tell me what you intend to do with a god's finger."

Cyrus said nothing. The tide filled the silence, breathing in, breathing out.

"You won't lie," Serah observed. "That's interesting. Liars always lie fastest when cornered."

"Maybe I'm not cornered."

"You're in a tidal cove with one exit and I'm sitting in it. Child, you are the definition of cornered. You simply haven't decided yet whether I'm the kind of corner that kills." Serah pulled off one gauntlet, then the other, and set them aside with the care of a woman disarming herself deliberately, making sure Cyrus saw each piece. Beneath the armor her hands were burned—old burns, silver-scarred, the kind that come from standing too near something that has been set alight. "So let me tell you a story first. Then we'll both know what kind of corner this is."

* * *

Serah had a brother once. His name was Wren, which is a foolish name to give a boy who cannot sing, and he prayed the way other boys whittled—constantly, absently, carving little hopes out of the air. When the fever came through their parish the year Serah took her vows, Wren was eleven, and he did what everyone in the eastern parishes had been taught never to do.

He prayed to one of the old ones. To Vael-of-the-Warm-Door, god of hearths and healing, whose name had been ash for two hundred years.

"And the door opened," Serah said. Her voice did not change register; it stayed flat and careful, like a woman walking across ice she has tested before. "That's the part they don't put in the homilies. It wasn't thunder. It wasn't horror. It was *warm*. He woke the next morning and the fever was gone, and my mother wept, and the whole street brought candles, and for three days we were the most blessed family in Corveth."

She turned her scarred hands over in her lap.

"On the fourth day Wren started bringing things home. Small things. A neighbor's lost ring. A sparrow, dead three days, blinking. He'd say *the warm man asks for it*, and smile, and his eyes were—" She stopped. Started again. "You carve bone. You listen through it. You know what worn things feel like from the inside. My brother was eleven years old and something was using him the way I use these gloves, and every prayer he'd ever said was a stitch in the glove."

"What happened to him?" Cyrus asked, though she knew. She had known since the fisherwoman's son had climbed out of the sea, wrong-sized and dripping, wearing gratitude like a mask over dread. She knew all the endings of answered prayers.

"I burned him," Serah said. "On the fourth day. With the whole parish watching, because that was the law and I was already sworn. He was awake for it." Her jaw worked once. "He was awake, and at the end he stopped being afraid, which I have spent nineteen years telling myself was mercy, and nineteen nights knowing was the god leaving a sinking ship."

The tide breathed. A gull screamed somewhere and thought better of it.

"So when the prayers started landing answers again," Serah said, "when the Thirteenth put on that boy king like a coat, I volunteered for this hunt faster than any sane woman should. Because I want them gone. All of them. Every warm door shut and nailed." She looked at Cyrus then, and her eyes were the exact gray of the cove. "But here is where we differ, carver. I have read your guild's sealed rites—or the fragments Serjeant-Hall could buy. Unbinding requires a vessel willing to release what wears it, yes? And when the vessel releases a god that has *fully settled*—" she spread her burned hands, gentle, terrible, "—there is nothing left of the boy. Aldric dies on the coronation dais in front of ten thousand witnesses, and the god goes free into the wind, and some other child starts bringing sparrows home by autumn."

"That's not true."

"Isn't it?"

"It's incomplete." Cyrus's heart was hammering against the wrapped fingerbone as if the two shared a pulse now. "Fire kills the boy too. Fire kills the boy *first*. You know that. You knew it with your brother. What I'm offering—if it works—is the only version where anyone walks away."

"If it works." Serah rose, retrieving one gauntlet, and Cyrus understood the audience was ending. "Nine days I've chased you, Fairweather. I've read your file. Last Fairweather of a line of binders; the guild's own little hearing-aid; quiet as dust. And I keep asking myself why a woman built so carefully for silence would suddenly burn down everything to save a boy she met once." She worked her fingers into the steel. "Give me one reason not to take you to the Hall and let the fire do it the fast way."

And Cyrus, exhausted, salt-bitten, hunted until her bones hummed, heard herself answer with the truth:

"Because I sat across from him and his shadow moved late, and I recognized it. Being worn by something. Smiling when you don't want to smile. Listening to a voice that isn't yours fill up your mouth." She stood, slowly, letting her stiff legs unbend. "You're right that I met him once. But I've been Aldric my whole life, Dame Serah. The Ledger wore me the way the crown wears him. And someone is going to ask that boy if he wants to be free, and I am going to be the one who asks, and he is going to say yes, and I will pay whatever the rite costs." She swallowed. "Even if the cost is more than you'd burn me for."

* * *

**Serah**

Inquisitors are taught to read confessions the way sailors read weather: watch the pressure, not the words. Cyrus Fairweather's confession had the pressure of truth—all of it, even the parts that made no tactical sense to reveal. Especially those.

Serah had killed three traffickers of relics in her career and interrogated thirty, and every one of them, cornered on a cold shore, would have promised anything, wept anything, invented saints. This woman stood in the wind with her dead mentor's chisels in her bag and offered a price instead of a plea.

*Even if the cost is more than you'd burn me for.*

Nineteen years Serah had carried Wren's face, awake at the end, unafraid. Nineteen years she had told the Order the same story she told herself: that fire was clean, that fire was sure, that sureness was worth more than mercy. And every time some trembling creature like this one spoke of *unbinding*, of the old merciful rite the guild had buried, Serah's certainty had shifted a half-inch, like a shadow moving late.

She hated that phrase now. She had learned it four days ago, reading the intercepts from the astronomer's tower, and it had lodged under her armor like a splinter.

"The rite needs a threshold," Serah said. "Moonlit. That's in the fragments too. And it needs the vessel willing—which means you need the boy, lucid, inside his own skull, long enough to consent. Have you managed that? Has anyone?" She gestured at the bundle under Cyrus's coat. "You're carrying a piece of the Thirteenth itself. It speaks through that, doesn't it. It has spoken to *you*."

Cyrus's silence was answer enough.

"Then understand what I'm about to do," Serah said, "because I will only do it once, and I will not enjoy it." She drew her sword—not to threaten; she reversed it and drove it point-down into the sand between them, a border, a boundary marker, a grave for something. "I will help you reach the coronation. I have twelve men who will follow me past any lawful order, because I've spent nine days telling them we hunt a thief, and I'll spend one more hour telling them the thief is now an asset of the Crown. In exchange—"

"In exchange," Cyrus said quietly, "you keep your way open. If the unbinding fails, or if I'm lying, or if at any moment you believe the boy is already gone—"

"Then I burn it. All of it. The god, the boy, the throne room, and you if you're in the way." Serah held the gray eyes. "I need you to believe that completely, Fairweather. Not as threat. As *structure*. You'll be standing in front of ten thousand people asking a god to leave a body, and the god will offer you everything—your dead mentor alive, your gift restored, your brother-memories polished and returned—and the only thing that will hold you steady is knowing that behind you stands a woman with absolutely nothing left to lose and a torch."

Something moved across Cyrus's face that was almost gratitude, which frightened Serah more than defiance would have.

"My brother," Cyrus said. "You mean Wren. That's why the fires come so easily."

"Don't."

"He'd forgive you, you know. If you got it right this time. If the next child walks away."

"*Don't.*" The word cracked down the middle, and Serah heard, in the wreckage of it, an eleven-year-old voice singing off-key. She picked up her sword, cleaned the blade, sheathed it. "We move tonight. There's a coal barge at Grayharbor quay, bribed, flying grain colors. Your drowned boy and the fisherwoman meet us at the second buoy—I know about them; I've known for six days; I let them run because I wanted to see who you were when you weren't alone."

"And who was I?"

"Someone who shares her bread with ghosts." Serah pulled up her hood against the rising wind. "Come. We have a god to unmake, and only thirteen nights of moon left to do it in—and Fairweather—" she paused at the tideline, not turning around, "—if the Thirteenth offers to give you back the voices of the dead, including mine someday, remember that I asked you now, while I still meant it: refuse it."

* * *

**Cyrus**

The coal barge smelled of tar and old fire, and Cyrus lay in its dark hold that night listening to the engine of the sea beneath the hull and feeling the stolen fingerbone tick against her ribs like a second heart.

It had begun speaking again at dusk. Not words. Worse—*offers*, folded into sensation: the smell of Vess's workroom, beeswax and bone dust; the specific weight of her mentor's hand on her shoulder; the sound, half-remembered, of her mother humming while carving, a melody Cyrus had not consciously known she remembered. The god was building a museum of everything she had ever loved and holding the door open.

*I can give you the choir,* it murmured through the bone, patient as tide. *Every voice. Even hers, when her time comes. Even the unfinished goodbyes.*

Cyrus pressed her palm flat against the wrapping and thought of Serah's burned hands. Thought of Wren, awake at the end. Thought of a boy in a palace whose shadow arrived late to his own body, and of a woman who had burned her own brother and kept the ashes of that choice in her spine for nineteen years.

"Not even yours," she whispered to the dark. "Not even the good offers. That's the whole trick, isn't it—that you only ever show us the door, never the room behind it."

The bone went still, sulking or listening.

Above her, on deck, she heard Serah's boots cross from rail to rail, unhurried, keeping watch over the one person alive whom she trusted least and needed most. Two griefs in the same hull, sailing toward the same moon, armed with incompatible mercies.

Twelve nights left. And far ahead, past the second buoy where the drowned boy waited with lantern raised, past the towers of Corveth just visible as a bruise on the horizon, a crown was already learning the shape of a boy's head—and Cyrus understood, as the barge slipped its moorings and slid into the black water, that the Thirteenth had stopped making offers to her alone.

Through the bone, faintly, in a sixteen-year-old voice she had heard exactly once and would know anywhere, King Aldric whispered: *Hurry.*
