## 16. Reading the Rites

The master ledger of the Gilded Ledger smelled of smoke and tallow and something older beneath—something like a crypt opened after centuries of good behavior. Cyrus had carried it three days now, wrapped in oilcloth against her back, sleeping with it under her head the way other people slept with knives.

She had not yet dared open it by candlelight. She did it in the drowned boy's kitchen instead, on the third night, when the fisherwoman's cottage was dark and the sea made its long argument against the rocks below. Maren—the impossible son, the wrong-sized return—sat across from her, mending nets he had no need to mend, because his hands remembered the work even if the rest of him remembered drowning.

"You're afraid of it," he said. Not accusing. Observing. The dead were like that; they'd lost the energy for flattery.

"I'm afraid of what's in it."

"You've been afraid of what's in your own hands for twenty years." He pulled a knot tight. "This is just the same fear with a cover on it."

Cyrus almost smiled. Almost. Then she broke the ledger's wax seal—a sigil she recognized from Vess's ring, the same interlocked circles the guild masters pressed into every honest contract—and turned to the first page.

The handwriting was not one hand. That was the first thing that undid her. Page after page in different inks, different slants, different centuries: *Aldith Fairweather, binder, year of the Long Frost.* *Sable Fairweather, binder, who paid at the age of nine and never spoke above a whisper again.* *Maren Fairweather*—she stopped there, finger on the name, and Maren looked up from his netting as if the sound of his own borrowed name had reached him across the room.

"That's where they got it," he said quietly. "When they pulled me back. They needed a shape the prayer would recognize."

"Then I owe you an apology for every Fairweather who ever lived."

"Pay it forward," he said, and went back to his knots, and let her read.

* * *

The rites began on page forty-one, and they were nothing like she expected.

She had imagined incantations, grand and terrible, words that bent the world. What she found instead was bookkeeping. Of course it was bookkeeping. Her whole life had been apprenticeship to this without knowing it—the guild's accounts of bottled lullabies and borrowed shadows had been pale copies, children playing shop in the shadow of the original ledger. The old gods had been bound the way anything of value is bound: through terms, willingly entered, precisely witnessed.

*A binding requires,* wrote Aldith Fairweather in a hand so small it might have been apology rather than modesty:

*a vessel that consents, however it is deceived;*

*a threshold lit by no earthly fire, but by the moon alone;*

*a word spoken aloud by the binder, naming the debt;*

*and payment rendered in full, or the seal fails.*

Four lines. Four hundred years of sleeping gods reduced to four lines, and Cyrus read them six times before her hands stopped shaking.

Because she saw it. She saw it the way you see a figure in a tapestry once someone points—suddenly everywhere, suddenly undeniable.

A willing vessel. A moonlit threshold. A word spoken aloud, naming the debt.

The coronation.

Thirteen moons turning toward a crowning that would happen, by every account the astronomer had given them, under a sky with no sun yet risen and no sun yet set—the deep blue hour when the thirteenth moon stood full over the coronation dais and nothing else gave light. A boy-king kneeling. A god descending into him like water into a glove, welcomed, welcomed, because the court would be praying and the prayers would be landing somewhere at last, and Aldric—hollow-eyed, half-shadowed, lucid only in fragments—would consent because consent was all he had ever been permitted.

They were going to perform the binding themselves. The Thirteenth had arranged its own ceremony, dressed it in gold and anthem, and called it a coronation.

Which meant the ceremony could be read backward.

* * *

"What are you grinning about?" Maren asked. It was past midnight now. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width.

"The ledger works both ways." She turned the pages until she found the counter-entry, tucked at the bottom of Aldith's page like a footnote the writer had hoped no one would read: *An unbinding is the same rite spoken in reverse order, at the same threshold, with the debt named honestly. Where a binding deceives, an unbinding must not. The vessel must hear the truth entire and choose freely. This is why we bind and rarely loose. Truth is dearer than gold.*

"There's a catch," Maren said. There was always a catch; the drowned learned that early.

"There's always a catch. The debt has to be named honestly. Whatever the god paid to get in, whatever it promised, whatever price holds it—that has to be spoken aloud, witnessed, and settled. Or the seal doesn't break. It just... cracks." She ran her thumb along the margin, where Aldith had written something in ink gone brown as old blood: *A cracked seal is worse than none. Ask me how I know.*

"And do you know what the debt is?"

"No." But she thought of the fingerbone stolen from the black auction, hidden now in a hollow beneath the floorboards, and the things it had shown her when she'd held it too long: centuries of loneliness, hunger dressed as love, a god that had been bound not for wickedness but for appetite—and the price of its freedom, which was a Fairweather's hearing, surrendered whole. "Not entirely. But I know who does."

* * *

Dame Serah arrived before dawn, as she had taken to doing since their uneasy alliance formed—slipping down the cliff path in plain clothes, armor left behind, looking less like an inquisitor than a tired woman who had once been someone's sister.

Cyrus met her at the door with the ledger already open to page forty-one.

Serah read standing, by the light of the relit candle, and her face did the thing Cyrus was learning to watch for: the flicker of hope arriving and immediately being audited, itemized, priced. A soldier's habit. An inquisitor's disease.

"It could work," Serah said at last.

"But."

"But the rite says the vessel must choose freely. Hear the truth entire." Serah set the ledger down carefully, as though it might bite. "I've seen the boy, Cyrus. Whatever fragments of him surface—he's sixteen, and terrified, and the thing wearing him can make him say anything. How do you know his yes is real? How do you know it isn't the god's yes, dressed in the boy's voice?" Her jaw tightened. "If we begin the unbinding and the consent is false, we don't free him. We crack the seal. And Aldith Fairweather wrote a warning about cracked seals, and I'd very much like to never learn what she meant."

"I know." Cyrus had been chewing that bone all night. "That's why it can't be done from the crowd. It can't be shouted from the back of a square while guards close in. It has to happen *at the threshold*, in the moment of the crowning, with the god mid-descent and the vessel mid-choice—when the yes is being asked for real, for the first time, and the god cannot yet refuse to hear the answer."

"So you stand beside him. At the altar. In front of the entire court."

"In front of the entire court." Saying it aloud made it real, and reality had teeth. "While you hold the guards. While Maren and the choir sing the counter-prayer from wherever the acoustics favor us. While I speak the surrender clause loud enough that heaven itself witnesses it."

Serah was quiet a long moment. Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges, the way it did before the fishing boats went out—men rowing into the dark on faith and habit.

"My soldiers," Serah said finally. "The ones who came with me when I defected. Eleven, and two I trust past death. I can hold the dais steps for perhaps ninety seconds if everything goes wrong, longer if it goes right. After that—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Everyone at this table had already priced their lives; they were only haggling over the change.

"Ninety seconds is enough," Cyrus said, "if the rite takes."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we're all dead, Aldric wears the crown forever, and the thirteenth moon rules a kingdom of answered prayers." She closed the ledger. "I've read the accounts, Dame Serah. I know exactly what that kingdom looks like. I've been keeping its books my whole life."

* * *

Three days. Three turns of the moon's slow wheel, and then the thirteenth.

Cyrus spent them the way she knew how: working. Bone and knife and lamplight, but differently now—not carving instruments to *hear* through, but carving the counter-prayer into physical form, note by note, the melody the drowned choir had taught her in the flooded catacombs. If the dead were going to sing at a coronation, they would need something to sing *from*. She carved thin bars of whalebone tuned to the hymn's intervals and distributed them among the grieving families gathering in the fisherwoman's cottage—mothers and fathers and widows whose prayers had been answered wrongly, whose loved ones had come back dripping and diminished, all of them furious in the specific way of people who had been given exactly what they asked for and nothing like what they wanted.

On the second evening, Maren found her on the doorstep, knife idle, watching the moon fatten toward full.

"The god will offer you something," he said. "At the threshold. They always do. It showed you its loneliness already—it'll show you more."

"I know."

"Do you?" He sat beside her. His hands, she noticed, were still faintly wrinkled from water that dried years ago. "Because I remember drowning, Cyrus. Every minute of it. And I remember what it offered me in the last minute—my mother's voice, warm and close, telling me to let go, that letting go was the same as coming home. And I nearly listened. The kindest voice in the world, saying the most loving thing, and it was a lie shaped like mercy." He looked at her. "Whatever it offers you at that dais, it will be true. That's the cruelty of it. It won't lie to you. It will just love you in a way that costs everyone else everything."

Cyrus turned the knife in her hands. "Vess used to say the guild's hardest trades weren't the cruel ones. They were the kind ones. Cruelty you can refuse. Kindness gets inside you and does the refusing for you."

"She sounds like she's still teaching you."

"She's dying." The words came out flat, and she hadn't meant them to. "The healers give her days, maybe a week. She's holding on to see it finished. And after—" She stopped. After, she would pay the final price, and every dead voice would go silent forever, including Vess's last words, including the goodbye Cyrus wasn't ready to stop hearing. She had known this since the fingerbone told her, and knowing had changed nothing about how much it hurt. "After, the silence comes. All of it. Forever."

Maren said nothing for a while. The sea filled the gap, patient and enormous.

"When I came back," he said at last, "wrong-sized and dripping, everyone kept asking if I regretted it. Being returned. And I didn't know how to answer, because the question assumes drowning was the worst thing. But you know what I lost, coming back? I lost the quiet. Down there, at the end, there was this—stillness. No hunger, no fear, no wanting. Just the water holding me." He smiled, small and sad. "And then they dragged me back into all this noise. So I understand choosing silence. I think it might be the only truly generous thing a person can do. You give away the one sense that makes the world feel crowded, so someone else can have room."

Cyrus laughed—a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her. "You're very wise for a drowned man."

"I'm very wise for a seventeen-year-old. Drowning aged me." He stood, stretching. "Two days. Get some sleep, carver. Tomorrow we rehearse the hymn, and the day after, we go tell a god its lease is up."

* * *

But sleep did not come, and near the smallest hour, Cyrus took out the ledger one last time and read the surrender clause entire—the passage she would have to speak aloud on the dais, committing it to memory the way her hands committed melodies, line by line, breath by breath.

*Let it be witnessed:* the old ink said. *That the debt is named. That the vessel chooses freely. That the binder surrenders her portion in full, asking nothing in return, and that this surrender is given not in hatred of the bound nor in fear of the hungry, but in pity—for hunger is only love that has forgotten its manners, and even gods deserve to be put gently to bed.*

She read it twice. On the second reading, she understood what Aldith Fairweather had really written: not a weapon. A lullaby. The oldest one. The one the guild had bottled and sold and forgotten the meaning of.

And that was when she heard it—faint, from the floorboards, from the hollow where the stolen fingerbone lay wrapped—a vibration like a plucked string, like something vast turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to wake.

It knew she was ready. Two days early, the god was already listening.

Cyrus wrapped the ledger, banked the candle, and sat in the dark with her knife across her knees, counting heartbeats, waiting for the moon to finish its turn.
