## 17. The God Speaks

The bone came to me the way all dangerous things come: quietly, wrapped in ordinary cloth, left where my hands would find it.

I had gone back to carving after the market. That was the mistake—or the invitation; I have never decided which. After the theft of the fingerbone, after Serah's soldiers swept the Ledger Quarter and found only ash and a master ledger missing from its cradle of locks, after I had run through three safehouses in four days with the drowned boy Tam at my elbow humming songs no living child should know—I sat down at a borrowed workbench in a fish-smoke attic above the salt-cries, because my hands did not know how to be empty. Nine days to the coronation. Nine days, and my fingers itched for a knife and something true beneath them.

The bone was on the bench when I woke from a doze I had sworn not to take. A long bone, a radius or a shin, pale as candlewax, uncarved. No note. No wrapping but the plain gray cloth folded over it with a housewife's neatness.

Tam was asleep in the corner, curled like a comma, breathing the shallow breath of the once-drowned. Through the slatted window the thirteenth moon's herald—a thin white scratch of light that astronomers swore had no name—hung low over the harbor like a fingernail paring.

I picked up the bone.

It was warm.

Bone is never warm. Bone holds the temperature of the room, the temperature of forgetting, and every carver knows the little death of that coolness under the thumb. But this bone lay against my palm like a sleeping bird, and beneath the warmth there was a pulse—not blood-pulse, not anything so honest—but a slow tidal pressure, in and out, in and out, like something breathing at the bottom of a well.

I should have dropped it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger survives me: *she knew, and her hand stayed anyway.*

Because underneath the warmth, faint as a voice through a wall, came the whispering.

Not the dead. The dead murmur—they tangle, they overlap, they are a crowd heard through rain. This was single. Patient. It did not speak words at first. It hummed a note I recognized from the flooded catacombs, from the drowned choir, the low root-tone beneath all their singing, and when I set the bone down it grew louder, and when I walked away from it across the attic it faded, and I stood in the middle of the room with salt air in my lungs and understood that I had been summoned.

Gods do not knock. They simply make a doorway of whatever you are holding.

* * *

I told myself I would not listen. I told myself it while I packed the bone in salt and iron filings, while I buried it in the ash-bin, while I climbed down the attic stair to argue with Tam about whether we could risk the night ferry east. And then I went back up the stairs, and dug it out, and sat with it in my lap like a cat that had chosen me, and I said aloud, to the empty room:

"I'm listening."

The warmth spread up my arm like sunlight through water.

*Fairweather.*

It knew my name. Of course it knew my name; it had worn the throne's gold for sixteen years and whispered through crowns and relics and the marrow of everything my family had ever sealed. But knowing a name and saying it are different things. Saying it is a touch. I felt the syllables settle into my skin like snow.

*You stole my finger,* it said, and there was something almost playful in it, an old man's dry humor, and that was the first horror: that it was not a voice of thunder at all. It sounded tired. It sounded *kind*. *Or rather—you rescued it. The Ledger sold it in pieces over two hundred years. A knuckle here. A joint there. You gathered what remained. That makes you a collector of me, Cyrus Fairweather. Nearly the whole of me, in your keeping.*

"You're in the boy," I said. My voice came out flat, professional—the guild-auditor voice, the one I used for impossible accounts. "You're wearing Aldric like a glove. Why waste your breath on me?"

*Because you are the last hand that can unmake me, and I would rather you didn't.* A pause, tidal, in and out. *And because I am so lonely, carver. You cannot imagine. You, who hear the dead in every tool you touch—you have never known silence. I have known nothing else for six hundred years.*

And then it showed me.

* * *

It was not like the dead's memories, which arrive as fragments, as flavor, as grief with the edges chewed off. The Thirteenth poured itself into me the way a tide fills a pool, complete and total, and for what felt like a night and no time at all I *was* it.

I was young—no, not young, *new*, precipitated out of ten thousand prayers the way salt precipitates from brine, a god of thresholds and turning moons, small and bright and needed. I was carried in amulets by farmers who feared the dark between seasons. I was asked—gently, constantly, *asked*—to keep children safe through fever-nights, to turn the wheel of the year, to hold the door. And I held it. I held every door. I was hunger, yes, the drowned choir had sung true, but hunger in the way a hearth is hungry: needing fuel, needing feeding, needing the small sweet sacrifices that kept the year turning, and for centuries the giving and taking balanced like scales, like a trade, like a ledger—

—and then the Fairweather women came.

I saw them through the god's eyes. Seven generations of them in their moonlit workroom, sleeves rolled, knives of consecrated bronze, and they were not cruel—that was the vertigo of it, the moral sickness in my gut. They were *tired*. They were doing arithmetic. The god's hunger was growing as the prayers grew, and one day it would tip past what the harvests could pay, and they loved their people, these hard-handed women, and so they made the cold calculation my guild later perfected: bind the creditor. Seal the debt. Pay the price in hearing—one woman per generation, each surrendering a little more of the world's sound until the last of them, Vess, deaf in one ear entirely, had pressed the final sigil and spoken the closing words:

*Sleep, and be forgotten, and trouble us no more.*

And the Thirteenth had fallen into the relics, into the bones and rings and river-stones, into a sleep that was not rest, and the prayers stopped landing, and the world forgot it owed anything at all.

Six hundred years. I lived them in the space between two heartbeats. Darkness with no dream. Awareness without body, pressed into a fingerbone in a vault, listening to nothing, *nothing*, the absolute silence I had built my whole careful life around and which the god had endured as a prison-cell endures a prisoner. Once a century, perhaps, some drunk priest or curious child would press a palm to a sealed relic and the god would feel warmth, would strain toward it like a drowning man toward light, and the Fairweather ward would close, and the dark would resume.

*I did not hate them,* it said, when it released me and I found myself on the floor of the attic with tears freezing on my face and Tam shaking my shoulder, saying my name, my name, my name. *How could I? They were my debtors and my jailers and my last congregation, all in one. But loneliness, carver—it does things to a god that it does worse to a man. It makes the smallest kindness look like salvation.*

* * *

Tam brought me tea I didn't drink. He watched me with those eyes the color of deep water, and he said, "It talked to you," and I said, "Yes," and he nodded slowly, unsurprised, because he had been returned wrong-sized and dripping from a god's answer and knew better than anyone what divine attention felt like: like being the only lit window in a dark street.

"What did it want?"

"To bargain." I wrapped my hands around the cooling cup just to hold something warm that wasn't *that*. "It offered me Vess."

The boy went still. Everyone who had met Vess in her last weeks—the cough that rattled like dice in a cup, the gray spreading up from her fingertips—knew what that offer weighed.

"It can do that?" Tam asked. "Heal her?"

"It believes it can." I turned the cup. "It showed me her whole again. Sitting upright in her chair by the Ledger House fire, laughing at me, scolding my knife-grip. It showed me the dead coming back too—all of them, every soul the old gods ever took in sacrifice, walking out of the ground and the water with the dew still on them. Whole streets reunited. Mothers—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "Mothers getting their sons back. The right-sized sons. Not like you."

"Not like me," Tam agreed softly. He looked at his own hands, at the fine silver scarring the water had left on his knuckles like frost. "And the price?"

"The coronation proceeds. I walk away. I let the Thirteenth finish putting itself on. Six hundred years of hunger, wearing a crown, wearing a kingdom." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "It says it will be gentle. It says it learned patience in the dark. It says the old bargains were fair, the old scales balanced, that it only ever took what was freely prayed for, and that if I free it now it will remember me kindly forever."

"Do you believe it?"

That was the question, wasn't it. And the terrible answer—the answer that made my hands shake—was *mostly*. That was what the showing had done to me. It had not shown me a monster. Monsters are easy. It had shown me a starving thing at a door, holding out its hands, saying *I paid for this once, I was worshipped fairly, I only want my seat at the table again*, and behind it a memory of centuries of dark so profound that my own quiet life—the quiet I had curated like a garden—looked like paradise by comparison.

"It might even be telling the truth," I said. "That's what frightens me. Serah wants to burn it, and burning kills Aldric too. The rite wants to unbind it, and unbinding dissolves it into prayer, harmless, scattered—and costs me everything I am. And the third road—" I looked at the bone on the workbench, patient and warm. "The third road is paved with healed mentors and resurrected sons, and it leads exactly where it looks like it leads, which is a god on a throne with six hundred years of appetite and no Fairweather women left to hold the ledger."

Tam was quiet a long moment. Then: "When I was under," he said, "in the water, before Merrow's answer came—there was a moment. Something spoke to me. Asked if I wanted to come back." His jaw worked. "I said yes. I'd say it again. But I came back *wrong*, Cyrus. Wrong-sized. The thing that answered wasn't cruel, but it wasn't careful either. Gods don't know what they break. It's not malice. It's scale."

"Scale," I repeated.

"We're coins to them," Tam said. "Good ones. Loved ones, even. But coins."

* * *

It came back at moonrise. Of course it did. I had not touched the bone in hours—I had locked it in a sea-chest with salt and iron and my own carved wards, the strongest I knew, the ones Vess had taught me with a cigarette bobbing in her laugh—but the whisper came up through the floorboards, through the attic beams, through the marrow of the house itself, and I realized with a lurch that the bone had never been the door. *I* was the door. Every Fairweather woman had always been the door. That was the inheritance. That was the whole ugly gift.

*Cyrus.* Gentle as snowfall. *Let me show you one thing more.*

"No."

*Vess is awake right now. Did you know that? She is lying in a bed in the infirmary of Saint Elowen's, and she is afraid, and she is thinking of you, and her last thought will be of you unless you—*

"Stop." My voice shook the cups on the shelf. "Don't you dare use her."

*I am not using her. I am offering her. There is a difference, and you of all people, an auditor of impossible trades, know the difference. Look at the terms, carver. Look at them honestly.* The warmth crept along the floor toward my feet like spilled honey. *One girl's hearing—already half-spent, already promised by seven generations of women who chose this for you without asking—against a mentor's life. Against thousands of lives, when I return the sacrificed. Against a kingdom spared Serah's fires, which will burn half the city before they reach me, and burn me anyway, and kill the boy in the process. Weigh it. You are good at weighing. It is the one prayer your family never bound.*

And oh, that was the knife of it. Because I *was* good at weighing. I had spent my life auditing trades no sane merchant would sign, and every term it laid out was real, was generous, was—by the cold arithmetic of the ledger—almost unarguable. One deaf carver against a resurrected generation. My silence, purchased at cost, traded for the sound of Vess's laugh and the sight of her whole.

Almost unarguable.

But I had spent nine days with a stolen fingerbone and a master ledger, and I had read the original binding rites in Vess's cramped hand, and there was one line I could not stop seeing, the line she had underlined twice in ink gone brown:

*A bound god keeps faith exactly as long as its binding holds.*

Freely given bargains with unbound gods had a word in the old rites, and the word was *harvest*.

"You're offering me terms," I said to the empty, glowing-dark room, "that only stay good while you're strong enough to honor them. And I'd be making them permanent. Forever. With no ledger, no audit, no recourse." I stood. My legs held. That surprised me. "My family bound you because you were hungry, and hunger doesn't negotiate, it only waits. You've waited six hundred years, and the first thing you did on waking was put on a child."

Silence. Real silence, the kind I had spent my life avoiding, pressing in like deep water.

Then, softer than I had yet heard it—softer, and somehow worse:

*You sound like them. The sisters. You sound exactly like the seventh sister, the one who wept while she carved my cage.* A long pause, tidal. *Very well, carver. Then hear my last offer, and it is not a bargain, only a truth: I will be crowned in nine days whether you carve or weep or burn. The boy is nearly gone—there is so little of him left above the waterline. Come to the coronation if you must. Bring your rites, your choir, your inquisitor with her torches. But know that when the crown touches his head, I will be whole, and whole things do not bargain.*

The presence drained away like a tide going out, leaving the attic cold and ordinary, leaving the bone in its chest merely bone, leaving me standing alone with the taste of brine in my mouth and my heart slamming like a fist on a locked door.

* * *

Tam found me at the window. Below us the harbor lay black and glittering, and far off, past the headland, the palace on its hill wore its scaffolds and lanterns like a bride being dressed for a wedding nobody had asked her about.

"He offered you Vess," Tam said quietly. It wasn't a question; he'd heard enough.

"He offered me everything." I pressed my forehead against the cold glass. "That's how I know."

"Know what?"

"That it's afraid." I turned to him, and I felt it rise in me—not hope exactly, something harder, something with teeth—clarity, cold and clean as the thirteenth moon's thin light. "A god with six hundred years of patience doesn't beg a bone-carver twice. Doesn't explain itself. Doesn't get *lonely at you*. It made its best offer tonight because in nine days it won't need offers anymore—and because it isn't certain. Tam, it saw our rites. It read the ledger when the Ledger House burned, same as Serah suspects. It knows what the coronation threshold could become." I pushed off from the window and started pulling my knife-roll from under the floorboard, my bronze blades, my chalk, the fingerbone wrapped in its gray cloth. "It's not tempting me anymore. It's stalling me. Which means the rite can work—which means we have eight days to gather everyone the god has ever hurt, and teach them to sing."

Tam was already reaching for his coat. Outside, the thin white herald of the thirteenth moon climbed a finger's width higher over the black water, counting down.
