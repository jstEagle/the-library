## 18. Preparations and Farewells

The safehouse smelled of tallow and brine, of old rope and newer grief, and Cyrus thought it was the most honest room she had ever stood in.

It had been a net-mending loft once, above the fish-stalls at Grayharbor's eastern edge, with rafters hung thick with cork floats and the bones of boats that would never sail again. The fisherwoman—Maren, though she told everyone to call her nothing at all, since names were how the gods found you now—had given it over without hesitation. She had been feeding strangers off her own table since her son came back from the deep, wrong-sized and dripping, and she seemed to feel that hospitality was the only prayer left that still worked.

Cyrus stood in the doorway a long moment before entering, the stolen fingerbone wrapped against her ribs in oilcloth, and took inventory.

Not of supplies. She'd done that already: forty pounds of salt pork, three barrels of rainwater, Serah's soldiers' arms stacked under a tarp like sleeping animals. She took inventory of people. It was a carver's habit. Every bone she had ever shaped, she had first held up to the light and asked what it carried. Now she held each face in the lamplight and did the same.

* * *

They had begun arriving three days ago, drawn by word passed mouth to mouth through channels no guild ledger could track—the drowned boy's errands, mostly. He moved through Grayharbor like a rumor with legs.

Tam, Maren called him, though Cyrus knew better than anyone that this was not quite his name anymore. He sat by the window now, always by the window, mending nets he never finished because his hands remembered the work but wanted, constantly, to stop and look at the water. He had been dead for two years. He was seventeen forever. When he spoke, gulls outside sometimes answered, and he had learned to pretend not to notice.

"You're doing it again," Tam said without looking up. "Counting us."

"I'm auditing," Cyrus said. "There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"An audit has consequences."

He grinned at her, and the grin was a boy's grin, ordinary as bread, and Cyrus felt again the strange vertigo of loving something the sea had already tried to keep. His voice was one she knew from her carvings—from a whalebone flute she had made at nineteen, whose notes had carried a drowned boy's laughter all the way from the harbor floor. She had heard him for years without knowing who he was. Now here he sat, flesh and memory and salt, mending a net badly.

Serah's soldiers occupied the far end of the loft, six of them, defected in ones and twos over the past fortnight. They kept apart from the others not out of hostility but out of discipline; they were men and women who had spent years learning exactly where to stand in any room, and unlearning it would take longer than they had. Their sergeant, a broad woman named Osk with a burn scar down one side of her jaw, spent her evenings sharpening things that were already sharp.

"Dame Serah sends word," Osk said, coming over to Cyrus with a folded paper she did not offer. "She holds the palace guard rotation. Twelve names bought, four more leaning. On coronation day she can give you ninety heartbeats of clear corridor between the west door and the dais."

"Ninety."

"Ninety is generous. She says to tell you it used to be sixty, before the choir started singing outside the walls every night. The guards are rattled. Rattled men look at their boots."

Outside the walls, faint even now, even through stone and distance, the choir sang. They gathered at dusk on the cliffs beyond Grayharbor—grieving families, Maren's people, mothers and fathers and widows who had paid the guild impossible prices for impossible returns and received instead only the knowledge that their dead were real, and gone, and listening. They did not sing hymns. They sang the counter-prayer, the one from the drowned catacombs, taught to them by Tam and by Cyrus herself, and the sound carried over the water like fog.

Tonight there were perhaps two hundred voices. Tomorrow, Maren promised, more.

"They're frightened," Cyrus said.

"They're ready," Osk said. "Different thing." She looked at the paper in her hand, then handed it over after all. "She says you'll want to read this alone. And she says—" Osk hesitated, which was unlike her. "She says tell the carver that fire is still faster than rite. That the offer stands. That she'll stand in front of the boy either way, but she'd rather stand there knowing everyone walks out."

Cyrus unfolded the letter. It said precisely what Osk had said, in Serah's blunt hand, plus one line more: *I lost my brother to an answer. Do not make me lose you to a choice.*

* * *

Evening came down over the harbor like a lid, and the allies of the last Fairweather gathered around a table built from a ship's hatch cover, and ate stew because Maren insisted that no plan worth dying for should be planned hungry.

Cyrus looked down the length of the table and performed her inventory aloud, in her head, the way Vess had taught her to audit: name every asset, weigh every liability, hide nothing from yourself.

Asset: Tam, who could walk into water and come back, and whose presence among the drowned dead was a passport no living courier could forge. He would carry the signal to the choir when the moment came—a carved whistle, bone, three notes, the counter-prayer's opening phrase made breath.

Asset: Osk and six soldiers who had chosen a girl over a crown, or rather a boy over a god wearing one.

Asset: Serah, somewhere inside the palace walls, wearing her inquisitor's white like armor over a wound, holding doors open with sheer terrifying competence.

Asset: Maren, whose grief had become architecture—who had organized two hundred mourners into a choir, fed an army, and asked for nothing, because asking was how gods got in.

Liability: the fingerbone in its oilcloth, which whispered at night when the moon touched it. Liabilities: thirteen moons until coronation—no. One moon. One moon left, now, hanging thin as a paring over the harbor. Liability: the rite itself, half-deciphered, its surrender clause a chain of words in a dead dialect that Cyrus had practiced until her tongue bled. Liability: Aldric, sixteen and hollow-eyed, lucid in fragments, waiting behind gold.

Liability, she wrote in the private ledger of her heart, and underlined twice: *the price.* A Fairweather must surrender the gift entirely. Every dead voice, forever. She had known it since the fingerbone showed her. She had told no one except Serah, and Serah had offered fire instead, and Cyrus had refused, and they had not spoken of it since.

Because there was one voice she was not ready to lose. And that voice was dying upstairs.

* * *

Vess had come to Grayharbor a week ago, carried in a cart like cargo, insisting the whole way that she was fine, that Fairweather women died standing, that this cart was undignified and someone would hear of it. She had climbed the stairs to the loft bedroom on her own feet, refused the bed until midnight, and then slept for two days.

Now she lay propped against pillows in the loft's small back room, and the cough had settled into her chest like a tenant with a lease, and everyone in the house walked softly past her door except Cyrus, who walked loudly, so Vess would know it was her.

"You sound like a horse," Vess said when Cyrus entered.

"You sound like a landslide."

"Good. Landslides take whole hillsides with them. Horses just get shot." Vess's eyes were bright and terrible and entirely awake. "Sit. Bring the lamp. My eyes are going too, and I refuse to die squinting at your face."

Cyrus sat. Brought the lamp. Held her mentor's hand, which had once been strong enough to snap a femur cleanly across the knee and now felt like a bundle of kindling wrapped in skin.

"I've been thinking about your mother," Vess said.

"She died when I was six."

"Yes. And I lied to you about how. I've lied to you about a great many things, and I'm going to spend my remaining hours correcting the accounts, so pour me something and settle in, apprentice. This will be a long entry in the ledger."

Cyrus poured the bitter tonic Maren brewed, and Vess drank it like an insult, and began.

* * *

She talked until the candle guttered, and Cyrus lit another, and she talked past that one too.

About Cyrus's grandmother, whom Vess had served, and her grandmother before her, back and back until the names blurred. About the night each Fairweather daughter turned twelve and was brought into the binding room beneath the old Ledger House and made to listen—truly listen—for the first time, and weep, and then be told why. About the debt structure of it, because Vess could not explain anything except as accounting: each generation paid a portion, hearing traded away thread by thread, so that no single woman bore the whole cost, so that the chain would hold.

"We thought we were being merciful," Vess said. "Spreading the payment across centuries. Your mother thought so. I thought so. We were being cowards with compound interest, child. We let the debt grow so none of us ever had to pay it entire." Her grip tightened on Cyrus's hand with surprising strength. "You're going to pay it entire. Tomorrow. And I have lain here nights being proud of you and furious at the world in equal measure, and I've decided pride wins by a margin, but it's close."

"I haven't agreed to anything," Cyrus said, which was a lie so transparent that Vess laughed and then coughed for a full minute.

"You crossed the city with a god's fingerbone sewn into your coat. You learned a dead language until you tasted blood. You've been saying yes for months. You just haven't let yourself hear it." Vess's eyes closed. Opened. "I have one more confession. Then you may go."

"Vess—"

"One more. Listen. This is the part I couldn't say while there was still time to argue."

Cyrus leaned close.

"The binding rites require a willing vessel. So does the unbinding. But there's a line in the master ledger I never showed you, because I hoped it wasn't true. I checked it tonight, against the copy in my head, the one I've carried fifty years." Her voice dropped to a rasp. "The surrender must be spoken by the Fairweather *before* the god releases the vessel. Not after. If you wait—if you hesitate even a heartbeat after the crown comes loose—the door closes and the god takes the gift anyway, and keeps it, and keeps *you*, because a Fairweather's hearing is the only key it recognizes. You must speak it while it can still fight back. While it's looking at you. Do you understand? There will be a moment tomorrow when everything in you will scream to wait, to be sure, to do it safely after. That moment is the trap. The Thirteenth is old, Cyrus. Older than hunger. It knows exactly how much you love the voices."

The room was very quiet. Downstairs, faintly, the choir had begun to gather on the cliffs, and their first notes came through the floor like a tide finding a crack.

"And if I speak it early," Cyrus said slowly, "and the rite fails—"

"Then you'll be deaf to us for nothing, and the god keeps the boy, and I will haunt you so thoroughly you'll wish you'd chosen fire." Vess smiled, and it cracked her face open into the old sly beauty she had been at thirty. "But it won't fail. I've audited you, apprentice. Final assessment. You're the finest carver I ever trained, and I trained your mother, and your mother's mother, and I am including myself in that list, because humility was never a family trait." Her breath caught. Steadied. "One more thing. Not a confession. A request."

"Anything."

"When it happens—when you speak the words and the silence comes—I'll be speaking too. I've been composing it for days. A goodbye worthy of the account books. Don't try to answer. Just know that whatever you hear in those last seconds, cut short mid-word—" her voice frayed, and she mastered it, "—that's not me failing to finish. That's me finishing everything I need to. Promise me you won't wait for the end of the sentence. Promise me you'll let go at the first syllable."

"I promise," Cyrus said, and discovered she was crying, quietly, in the manner of accountants.

"Good girl. Now get out. I need to sleep, and you need to carve. You haven't touched your tools all day, and your hands are lying to you about being steady. Carve something small. Something that isn't a weapon or a key. Carve like a woman who expects to live."

* * *

She carved until dawn, sitting on the stairs where she could hear Vess breathing through the wall—slow, stubborn, tidal—and where she could also hear the house below: Tam turning in his sleep with the sea still in his lungs, Osk's soldiers dreaming in formation, Maren rising before anyone to set bread to proof, humming the counter-prayer under her breath like a lullaby she was finally allowed to sing.

Cyrus carved a comb. Ordinary horn, ordinary teeth. No memory in it, no whisper, no key. Just an object for hair, for mornings, for a life after.

Her hands were steady.

When the sky went grey, Tam appeared at her elbow, holding the bone whistle she had made him, testing it silently against his lips. Maren came in from the cliffs with the count: three hundred voices now, and boats coming in from two villages further down the coast. Osk reported Serah's final message in a single sentence: *Doors open at the ninth bell.*

And then, as the sun cleared the harbor water and laid a long gold road across the waves—the road, Cyrus thought, that a god might walk, or a boy, if someone cleared it for him—Maren's knock came soft at the stairwell door.

"She's asking for you," Maren said gently. "She's asking for you now, carver. And she says—" Maren's own voice caught, this iron woman who fed armies, "—she says to bring your tools, because there's one last commission."

Vess was sitting up when Cyrus came in. Sitting up, dressed, hair combed, hands folded on the blanket like a merchant awaiting a client. Her color was wrong and her breathing was a labor, but her eyes were lit from within by some final, banked fire, and she smiled the smile that had terrified apprentices for fifty years.

"There you are," Vess said. "Close the door. We're going to conduct the last transaction of the House of Fairweather, and I intend to dictate the terms while I still have a pulse to swear by."

Down in the harbor, the ninth bell began to ring.
