## 19. Coronation Eve

The bone-carver's route into the palace was older than the palace itself.

Every apprentice learned the story in their first year: when the first stones of the Gilded Hall were laid, the masons had bargained with a carver of relics to make the royal crypt's ossuary gates, and because no carver would trust a guild to pay fairly, they had demanded a door of their own—a way in that belonged to bone, not to gold. The guild called it superstition. The carvers called it insurance. Cyrus called it the only reason she was not currently standing in front of Dame Serah's soldiers with her hands raised.

The entrance was behind the tannery row, where the city's oldest drainage channel ran beneath the walls, and it smelled like everything the palace pretended not to have: rot and lye and the sweet-sour breath of old water. She went down the steps sideways, one hand on the slick stone, the stolen fingerbone wrapped against her sternum where it beat faintly against her ribs like a second heart. It had been beating all day. All week. As though whatever god had lived in that finger knew where she was going and was impatient for the reunion.

*Tomorrow,* she told it. *Be patient. You've waited three hundred years; you can wait one more night.*

It pulsed once, sulkily, and she almost laughed. Almost. Laughter felt like something she'd have to save up, like coins for a fare she might never take.

The passage narrowed. Her shoulder brushed stone carved with small figures—she could feel them under her fingertips without looking, the way she could feel any carving: the pressure of chisel-strokes, the patience in them, and beneath all of it, faint as breath on a winter window, voices. The dead who had been ground into the mortar of this place, or perhaps simply buried near enough to soak through. They didn't speak words. They hummed. A low, continuous note, like a cathedral holding its own name in its mouth.

She stopped walking.

They weren't just humming. They were humming a tune she knew—the counter-prayer, the one the drowned choir had taught her in the flooded catacombs, the one that unmade what crowns made. The dead in the walls were rehearsing.

"They know," she whispered, to no one, to everyone. "Good. Keep singing."

The hum deepened, pleased, and she walked on through the dark with a choir at her back and a god's bone at her breast and tomorrow sitting on her chest like a hand.

* * *

Aldric had learned to count the hours by his own heartbeat.

It was the only clock the Thirteenth hadn't learned to read—or hadn't bothered to. When the god wore him fully, his heart slowed to the pace of centuries, and he drifted somewhere behind his own eyes like a man pressed against the far side of frosted glass, watching a stranger use his hands. But the god slept sometimes. It dreamed, Aldric thought, in gold—long slow dreams of being worshipped—and in those hours his heart remembered its own rhythm, quick and frightened and sixteen, and he came flooding back into himself like air into a bellows.

He had four hours tonight. Maybe five.

He spent the first one writing nothing, which took effort. The coded pleas in the state documents had been his lifeline for months—little knots of nonsense tucked into tax decrees and grain tariffs, prayers disguised as bureaucracy—and he'd begun to hope someone, somewhere, was reading them. But tonight there was nothing left to say that he hadn't said. Tomorrow the crown came down. Tomorrow the thing inside him finished putting itself on, the way a hand finishes closing around an apple, and whatever fragments of Aldric remained after that would be too thin to write with.

So he sat at the window instead and watched the moon come up over the city.

There were thirteen of them now. Everyone said so. The thirteenth moon had risen three nights ago, thin and wrong-colored, hanging low over the eastern parishes like a coin someone had dropped in a well, and no astronomer could explain why it appeared only at certain hours, or why dogs howled at it, or why the tide came in farther than it had in living memory. Tomorrow, when the sun set and the crown touched his head, it would be full. He knew this the way he knew his own name, which was to say: imperfectly, and less every day.

"Tell me your name," he said to the moon, because his mother had taught him that fear shrinks when you name what frightens you. "Just your name. That's all I want."

The moon did not answer. Somewhere below, in the city, a bell rang the hour, and the sound came up through the floor and into the soles of his feet, and for one strange moment he thought the bell sounded like a woman breathing.

Then the candle guttered, and the god stirred, and his four hours began to drain away.

* * *

Cyrus came up out of the earth through a door hidden behind a rack of curing hides, and stood in the dark of the palace's undercroft among crates of candles and casks of salt fish, listening to the building above her breathe.

Palaces breathed differently than other buildings. Ledger House had breathed like a merchant—quick, calculating, always counting. The flooded catacombs had breathed like a lung half-drowned, slow and tidal. This place breathed like a sleeper with a nightmare: long held silences, then sudden shudders. Servants' footsteps overhead. The rattle of a cart. Then silence again, too complete, as if the whole structure were holding its breath between dreams.

Or as if something enormous were lying on top of it, waiting.

She climbed. The bone-carver's route ran up through the walls themselves—a stair no wider than a coffin, built into the masonry, each step worn smooth by three hundred years of carvers who had needed to reach the crypts without being seen. Her fingers found the guide-grooves automatically. Old habit. Vess had made her practice blind stairways until her legs knew them better than her eyes did, saying: *A carver who needs light is a carver who can be caught.* Vess. Six days dead now, and still the loudest voice in Cyrus's head—which was precisely the problem, because in six days she wouldn't hear that voice at all, ever again, and she had not yet figured out how to carry grief for someone while they were still speaking to you.

*You're stalling,* Vess's memory said, in the voice Cyrus kept for her, since the real one was busy being quiet in the ground.

"I'm scouting," Cyrus muttered. "There's a difference."

*There isn't. Go up.*

She went up.

The stair ended behind a panel in the chapel vestry wall—one of the palace's smaller chapels, deconsecrated years ago and used now for storing vestments nobody wore. Cyrus eased the panel open a finger's width, listened, and slid through into candlelight.

The chapel was prepared for tomorrow. Garlands of white flowers draped the altar rail, their perfume thick enough to taste, and rows of folding chairs faced a cushioned platform where the coronation crown sat alone in a glass case, lit from below, glowing like a captive sun.

Cyrus looked at it and felt the fingerbone flare hot against her chest.

The crown was gold, but it wasn't gold the way jewelry was gold. It was gold the way a glove was leather—shaped by wearing, stretched to fit a particular hand. And even through the glass, even across the room, she could hear it. Not words. Appetite. A low, wet hunger, patient as sediment, whispering without language: *tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow.*

Her stomach turned. She forced herself to look away, forced herself to count doors—three, one bolted, two open onto corridors—and to do the work Vess had trained her for. Appraisal. Assessment. *Look at a thing long enough and it tells you its price.*

The crown's price was a boy. She'd come to collect him early.

* * *

Aldric heard the footsteps before he saw anyone—which was strange, because he wasn't supposed to be able to hear anything the god didn't want him to hear. But these footsteps had a sound he recognized from the inside of his own bones: wood on stone, unhurried, deliberate. The sound of someone who worked with their hands.

He got up from the window.

The corridor outside his chamber was guarded, of course. Two soldiers at the door, four more at the stair, and Dame Serah's people everywhere else, gray as pigeons. But the guards had been odd all evening—jumpy, checking corners, whispering—and when Aldric cracked his door and peered out, both men at his threshold were staring down the hall at something behind him rather than in front.

Something behind him. Which meant—

"The panel," one guard said faintly. "In the vestry. There's someone in the—"

And then a woman stepped out of a wall, and Aldric's heart did something it hadn't done in a year: it leapt.

She was ordinary-looking, which somehow made it better. Ink-stained fingers, travel-worn coat, a face like a person who did sums for a living—sharp-eyed, tired, entirely unimpressed by the fact that she had just materialized inside a fortified palace on the eve of a coronation. The guards reached for their swords. She didn't run. She simply looked at them, and held up one hand, palm out, and in it she was holding a small carved figure—a soldier, Aldric saw, exquisite, tiny, the face so honest it hurt to look at.

"I need you to listen to it," she said quietly. "Both of you. Just for a moment. Please."

The guards went pale in perfect unison. One dropped his sword. The other sat down on the floor, hard, like a puppet with cut strings, and began to cry without any change in his expression.

"What did you—" Aldric started.

"Later." She turned to him, and her eyes did a thing he couldn't name—widened, softened, sharpened all at once, like a lens finding focus. "Your Majesty. I'm Cyrus Fairweather. I got your letters."

* * *

Aldric's chamber was warm and close and smelled of beeswax and, underneath, of something mineral—the smell Cyrus had learned meant a god had been sleeping in a room. She barred the door with a chair, mostly for theater, and turned to look at the king properly for the first time.

She had expected hollow. Chapter after chapter of hollow—that was how Serah described him, how the letters read, how a boy worn as a glove should look. But standing here in lamplight, with the god withdrawn to whatever gilded den it slept in, Aldric's eyes were not hollow. They were terrified. Terrified was much worse, and much better. Hollow meant empty. Terrified meant someone was home.

"You're shorter than I imagined," Aldric said.

"People keep saying that."

"It's not an insult. Gods are tall. It's nice to meet someone short." He laughed—a startled, rusty sound, as if the laugh had surprised him as much as her. He pressed a hand to his mouth. "Sorry. I haven't—I don't laugh much anymore. It uses something up. The god notices when things get used."

"Then we'll be quiet." She sat down across from him, uninvited, the way she'd sit at a workbench. "How long do we have?"

His face changed. The laughter drained out of it and left something older underneath, some careful adult architecture a sixteen-year-old should not have had to build. "Hours," he said. "It sleeps shallow before a big wearing. Like a man before a wedding." He looked at his own hands, turned them over, studied the palms as if checking for wear. "I wanted to ask you something before I lose the nerve. In your letters—you wrote back. I got one. Through the baker. Did you really mean it? What you said?"

Cyrus searched her memory. She'd written exactly one letter back, smuggled in a bread loaf, written at three in the morning with shaking hands, and most of what she remembered of it was shame at her own handwriting.

"I said I'd get you out," she said slowly. "However it ends."

"Not that part." His voice cracked and steadied. "You wrote—*you are not the house. You're the one who lives in it.* Nobody's said anything like that to me. Everyone else talks about the god. Save the kingdom from the god, kill the god, feed the god. You're the first person who talked about me." His jaw worked. "So I need to know if you meant it, because tomorrow, if it goes wrong—if the rite fails—if killing me is the only way to stop it—I need to know that somebody in the world will remember that there was a person in here. Not a vessel. Not a throne. Me. Aldric. Who hated lessons and loved his sister and once fell in the fountain trying to catch a heron."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the palace breathed its nightmare-breath. Against Cyrus's sternum, the stolen fingerbone pulsed, slow and covetous, tasting the proximity of the crown.

"I meant it," she said. "And I'll tell you something better than remembering. The rite doesn't require your death. I've read the original binding—Vess's ledger, the first Fairweather's hand. Binding needs a willing vessel. Unbinding does too. You don't have to die, Aldric. You have to *choose*. Out loud, in front of everyone. That's the whole terrible miracle of it. The gods can only wear what agrees to be worn—even if the agreement was tricked out of you when you were nine years old and grieving. Tomorrow you take it back."

He stared at her. His eyes were shining and he blinked fast, furious at the shine. "That's it? Just—say no?"

"Saying no to a god, in front of a court, while it fights you from the inside, with your body as the battlefield." She smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile; it was a carver's smile, the one that acknowledged the difficulty of a cut before making it anyway. "But yes. That's it. And you won't be alone. The drowned will sing the counter-prayer. Serah holds the guards. I hold the knife-work." She tapped her chest, where the fingerbone lay. "And this traitorous little relic holds the door open."

"The fingerbone," Aldric said. "It's been talking to you. I can feel it talking to you right now. It feels like—" he groped for the word, pressing his fist to his own sternum— "like a dog scratching at a door. It wants in here. Or it wants *me* out there. I can't tell."

"Its master wants tomorrow to happen. We're going to disappoint it." She leaned forward. "Which brings us to the signal. Because tomorrow will be chaos, and in chaos you'll drift, and if you drift the god wears you before I can start the rite. So we need a sign. Something only you and I know. Something that pulls you back into yourself no matter how deep you've gone."

"A word?" 

"Words can be spoken by it, wearing your mouth."

"A gesture?"

"Gestures can be stolen too."

He frowned, chewing his lip, and then—he straightened, and something kindled in his face, and for a moment he looked exactly like a boy and not at all like a king. "When I was small," he said, "before any of this—there was a carver who came to the castle. Before my parents died. She fixed my mother's music box and she gave me a little bone bird, and she taught me the game carvers play. Where you tap twice, pause, tap once." He demonstrated on the armrest: *tap-tap... tap.* "She said it means *I see you. I'm here. Hold on.* Do you know it?"

Cyrus went still. Of course she knew it. Every Fairweather knew it. It was the knock carvers gave on each other's doors; it was the rhythm Vess had drummed on Cyrus's shoulder through every bad apprenticeship year; it was older than the guild, maybe older than the binding. *I see you. I'm here. Hold on.*

"She was one of us," Cyrus said softly. "A Fairweather, probably. Checking on the bloodline. On you." And suddenly the shape of it appalled her: generations of women tapping on the doors of kings, keeping watch over a prison they themselves had built.

"Then you know it," Aldric said. "Tap-tap, pause, tap. If I'm gone—if the god has me—if I'm behind the glass and can't get out—do that. Anywhere I can feel it. My shoulder, my hand, the floor under my feet. I don't know if it'll reach me. But my mother's music box used to skip, and that rhythm always pulled me from across the room." He swallowed. "It's the truest thing I own."

"Then it's ours now," Cyrus said. "Tap-tap, pause, tap. I see you. I'm here. Hold on."

They sat with it a moment, the two of them, in the candle-hush, and something passed between them that had no name but had weight—the weight of two people agreeing, deliberately, freely, to stand in the same terrible place tomorrow and hold the same line. Courage, Cyrus thought, wasn't the absence of fear. It was fear with company.

* * *

Aldric watched her go the way you watch a boat leave shore.

She went back through the wall—the panel sighed shut behind her like a mouth closing—and the chamber was suddenly, enormously quiet, and he was alone with the candles and the garlands and the thing coiled in his blood, counting down.

*You spoke with her,* the god said.

It didn't use sound. It used his bones, his marrow, a voice like cold poured slowly into his ears. Aldric had learned not to flinch. Flinching fed it.

*I did.*

*She offers you freedom. She believes this.* The god's amusement moved through him like a shiver through water. *Poor child. Freedom from what? I am the oldest prayer she has ever heard. When they bound us, they did not destroy us—they stored us. And storage, little king, is not the opposite of freedom. It is merely a longer wait.*

"You talk too much," Aldric said aloud, to the empty room, because the guards outside had learned not to question it. "And tomorrow you're going to lose."

The god was silent for a long moment. Then, gently, horribly, with something that in another creature might have been tenderness:

*So shall we find out. Sleep now, Aldric. Tomorrow you will be a god. And she—*

It paused. He felt it turn its attention outward, toward the city, toward the tannery row, toward a woman climbing down through the bones of the world with a dead man's fingerbone against her heart.

*—she will finally understand what she has agreed to carry.*

Aldric lay down on his bed, dressed, boots on, the way he'd learned to sleep in the last year—ready to wake running. Before he closed his eyes he raised his hand and tapped it once against the headboard, softly, in the dark.

*Tap-tap... tap.*

*I see you,* he thought, at a woman he'd known for one hour, somewhere in the labyrinth below. *Hold on.*

Outside, the thirteenth moon rose over the palace, and began, patiently, to fill.
