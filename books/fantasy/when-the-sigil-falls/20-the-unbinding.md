## 20. The Unbinding

The bells began at dawn.

Cyrus heard them through the walls of the undercroft where she waited, thirteen strokes for the thirteenth moon, each one rolling down through stone like a stone itself dropped into deep water. Above her, the cathedral of Saint Verity was filling with every noble house in the city, every guild master in borrowed finery, every priest whose prayers had lately begun to land somewhere other than silence. Above her, a boy was being dressed in cloth of gold so heavy he would need help to stand beneath it.

She had dressed for a funeral. Her own, in a sense. The grey wool of a working carver, shorn of all guild marks, all insignia, everything that might let Dame Serah's soldiers mistake her for anything but what she was: the last Fairweather, come to end an inheritance four hundred years old.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She kept turning them over, palm to knuckle, looking for the tremor that ought to be there, the way you look for blood after a wound too deep to feel. Vess's bone-handled awl sat in her belt where a knife would go. The stolen fingerbone—the Thirteenth's own fingerbone, bought with a city's worth of forgetting and paid for since in blood—lay wrapped in linen against her sternum, warm as a held bird.

*You are going to speak to a god*, she told herself. *You have spoken to its dead all your life. This is only the last conversation.*

The drowned boy had taught her the counter-prayer three nights ago, kneeling in the flooded dark of the catacombs while the choir sang it around them in voices like water finding cracks. He remembered drowning. He said it made the prayer easier to learn, because the prayer was about letting go, and drowning men learned letting go faster than anyone alive.

*When the god fights you*, he had said, with the terrible calm of someone who had already died once, *it will fight you in your own voice. It will say things you want to hear. Don't answer it. Just keep speaking the rite. The rite doesn't need you to win. It only needs you to finish.*

* * *

They came for her at the third bell, as arranged.

Two of Serah's defected soldiers, faces grim beneath plain helms, led her up through the servants' ways into the body of the cathedral—not to the nave but to the triforium, the gallery running above the arches, where the fisherwoman's choir waited among the dust and the doves. Forty grieving families. Mothers who had paid the guild in memories for children returned wrong-sized and dripping. Fathers who had burned offerings to gods they'd been told were asleep. They wore mourning colours. They had come to sing their dead back to sleep.

Maren the fisherwoman took Cyrus's arm as she settled into the shadows of the gallery rail. Her son stood behind her, salt-stiff hair combed flat for the occasion, watching the crowned dais below with eyes that had seen the bottom of the harbour.

"You're pale," Maren said.

"I'm deciding," Cyrus said. "That's different."

"From what?"

"From being afraid." Below, the great doors opened, and the procession began. "Ask me again in an hour."

The court assembled in a wash of colour and murmuring: lords in cloth-of-silver, ambassadors from the coast cities, the Guild masters of the Gilded Ledger seated in a block near the front with their account-books bound in calfskin, because even at a coronation, Cyrus thought bitterly, they could not stop counting. And there, at the edge of the chancel, armour burnished to a mirror-shine that fooled no one—Dame Serah, standing guard over a ceremony she meant to burn down if Cyrus failed. Their eyes met across the height and the noise. Serah touched two fingers to her breastplate. *I lost my brother to an answered prayer.* *Do it right.*

Then the trumpets, and the hush, and Aldric.

He came up the nave between ranks of kneeling nobles, sixteen years old, walking the way she had once seen a marionette walk when half its strings had snapped—each step correct, each step slightly too late, his shadow on the marble flagging a half-second behind him like a dog that had learned not to trust its master. The crown-bearer walked beside him carrying the circlet on a cushion of white silk, and the gold of it caught the light through the clerestory windows and threw little burning coins of reflection across every face in the cathedral, and Cyrus felt the fingerbone against her sternum go hot as a coal.

*Hello, carver*, said a voice like honey poured over iron.

It did not come through her ears. It came through the bone, through every carved thing in the building, through the choir stalls and the reliquaries and the ivory inlays, the whole cathedral suddenly a mouth. The dead in her head—all those years of murmured accounts and half-remembered lullabies—went silent, pressed flat by something vast turning its attention toward her.

*I know why you've come. I have known since you stole my finger. I could give you her back, you know. Vess. Whole. Her heart mended, her hands young, her voice restored. You have only to stand quietly and let me be crowned. Four hundred years of Fairweathers binding me into relics, and for what? For silence? For loneliness? I have been alone in the gold for four hundred years, Cyrus Fairweather. Would you damn another soul to that?*

She did not answer. The boy had told her not to answer. She gripped the gallery rail and watched Aldric climb the steps to the dais, and she made herself see him—not the king, not the glove, but the boy behind it, the one who wrote coded pleas inside state documents and asked, politely, in a dead man's cipher, to be unmade if rescue failed.

*Not today*, she thought at him, as hard as thought could carry. *Today you are unbound instead.*

The high priest raised the crown.

* * *

What happened next, Cyrus would remember all her life in fragments, the way a storm is remembered: not as weather but as pieces. A sound. A light. A hand.

The crown descended toward Aldric's head.

And Cyrus stepped up onto the gallery rail, forty feet above the chancel floor, in full view of every noble house in the city, and began to speak the rite aloud.

She spoke it in the old tongue, the tongue of the first binders, the tongue the ledger had preserved in its opening pages in a fairweather hand four centuries faded. Her voice carried strangely well—she would learn later that the drowned choir below had begun singing the moment she opened her mouth, and that their song lifted her words the way water lifts a boat—and so the whole cathedral heard, in a language most of them knew only from gravestones:

*"What was given, we return. What was taken, we release. O sleeper in the gold, O hunger wearing a child: we name you. We unname you. By moon and marrow, by debt and debtor—"*

The world broke open.

Aldric screamed. It was not a boy's scream; it came out of him in layers, a boy's terror wrapped around something older and vaster, a sound like a ship's timbers tearing, and every candle in the cathedral guttered though no door had opened. The crown fell from the priest's hands and rang on the marble like a struck bell, and Aldric's body straightened as if a hand had seized his spine, and when his eyes opened they were not his eyes—they were molten, patient, ancient, and they found Cyrus on the gallery rail across the whole width of the screaming church.

*Carver*, the god said, out of the boy's mouth, in the boy's voice, which was the cruelest thing it could have done. *"Stop. Please. It hurts. Cyrus, please, you're killing him—you're killing us both—"*

"By debt and debtor," Cyrus said, and her voice did not shake, because she had spent her whole life holding steady while the dead begged, and this was only the last and largest begging of them all. She unwrapped the fingerbone and held it high. "*As the first Fairweather bound, so the last releases—*"

Guards moved below. Serah had promised they would move; the palace captain loyal to the Crown had posted twelve men along the chancel with orders to cut down anyone who interrupted the crowning, and now they drew steel and started up toward the gallery stairs, toward the choir, toward her.

Serah met them.

Cyrus saw it only in glances between verses—the inquisitor stepping off the chancel steps into the guards' path, sword clearing its scabbard, her voice pitched to carry: *"In the name of the Crown's true law, none pass!"* Steel rang on steel in the narrow stair. One of her defected soldiers went down clutching his arm. Maren's husband dragged him back behind the rail. And Serah held—held alone against six, giving ground foot by foot, buying Cyrus seconds the way you spend coin you cannot replace, her face set in the expression of a woman who had decided long ago how she was willing to die and was merely checking whether today qualified.

"*—by the marrow's memory and the moon's witness—*"

The god stopped fighting like a prisoner and started fighting like a god.

The gold of the cathedral woke. Every gilded saint on every pillar turned its head—slowly, terribly, the way furniture turns in nightmares—and the reliquaries burst their locks and the altar's golden facing rippled like the surface of a struck pond, and out of all that waking metal came a pressure, a presence, the sense of something immense unfolding joints it had held folded for four hundred years. Nobles fled shrieking up the aisles. The Guild masters clutched their ledgers. The high priest lay prostrate on the dais, mouthing prayers to gods who were, at present, extremely awake.

And through all of it, rising from the flooded catacombs beneath the cathedral floor, through drains and crypt-vents and the bones of the building itself, came the choir.

Forty voices. Grieving families singing the counter-prayer in round and canon, the song of letting go, the song the drowned had taught the living: *sleep now, hunger, sleep; the table is cleared; the debt is paid; go home to the quiet.* They sang without harmony books and without fear, mothers who had gotten back wrong-sized sons and fathers who had burned offerings to hungry gods, and their song wound around Cyrus's recitation like a rope around a spar, and where it touched the god's pressure the pressure recoiled, hissing, like steam off cold iron.

Aldric was on his knees on the dais, both hands pressed flat to the marble, his shadow thrashing behind him like a netted fish. His mouth opened. The god spoke through it in a voice that filled the dome:

*"YOU WOULD PUT ME BACK IN THE DARK. AFTER I SHOWED YOU MY LONELINESS. AFTER FOUR HUNDRED YEARS OF YOUR GRANDMOTHERS' CHAINS. FINE, CARVER. THEN HEAR THE PRICE. THE RITE REQUIRES A SURRENDER, AND THE SURRENDER REQUIRES A GIFT OF EQUAL WEIGHT TO WHAT IS FREED—AND I WILL TELL YOU WHAT THAT WEIGHT IS BEFORE YOU SPEAK IT BLIND."*

"I know the price," Cyrus said.

*"YOU DO NOT. YOU THINK IT IS YOUR HEARING. IT IS MORE. IT IS EVERY VOICE. FOREVER. AND WHEN YOUR MENTOR DIES—AND SHE IS DYING NOW, CARVER, EVEN AS YOU STAND THERE—HER VOICE GOES WITH THEM. UNFINISHED. UNHEARD. YOU WILL LOSE HER GOODBYE."*

The words landed like knives. Cyrus felt them land. Vess, dying in a sickroom across the city with her confession finally made and her last words still unsaid—and Cyrus had imagined, these past weeks, that she would have time, that she would sit at the bed and hear the ending of the story.

The god saw her flinch. She felt it see.

*"STAND DOWN",* it said, gentler now, honey over iron, using the boy's cracked adolescent voice, using tenderness as a weapon because cruelty had failed. *"Stand down, and I will mend her. I will hold her past winter. I will give you ten more years of her voice. All I ask is the crowning. One crown, carver. Is a boy worth more than your mother's mother?"*

And here was the moment Cyrus Fairweather had been walking toward all her life, though she had called it by other names—denial, diligence, the careful invisible life of a woman pretending not to hear. Here was the accounting. She had spent her life keeping the guild's ledger of impossible trades, and she knew, better than any master in the hall below, how to weigh a price.

Vess had already told her the answer, weeks ago, dying, gripping her wrist with bone-carver's fingers: *Every Fairweather pays, girl. Pay forward, not backward. Don't you dare buy me with that boy.*

"The rite does not bargain," Cyrus said.

And she spoke the surrender clause.

*"Hear me, Thirteenth: I, Cyrus Fairweather, last of the binders, do surrender the gift entire—the hearing and the hearing's heirs, mine and every Fairweather after, from this hour until the last of our name—for the freeing of this vessel and thy dissolution into prayer. Let it be accounted. Let it be witnessed. Let it be DONE."*

She drove Vess's awl into the stolen fingerbone and snapped it in two.

* * *

Afterward, people who had been in the cathedral struggled to describe what they had seen, and mostly they described light, or the absence of light, or a sound like the sea going out very fast.

Cyrus experienced it as a letting-go.

The god rose out of Aldric the way heat rises off summer stone—invisible, immense, reluctant. For one suspended instant it hung above the dais in a shape the eye could not fix, a silhouette of thirteenth-moon silver, and Cyrus felt its attention settle on her one last time—not with hatred, she would swear to that ever after, but with something worse and sadder, the look of a lonely thing understanding that it has lost.

*Four hundred years*, it said, in no voice at all. *And in the end, one small stubborn woman with an awl.*

Then the choir reached the final verse, and the god came apart into prayer. That was the only way Cyrus could think of it: it scattered, syllable by syllable, into the mouths of everyone in the cathedral—a word in the high priest's mouth, a word in a fleeing lord's, a word in hers—so that for one shining moment every person in Saint Verity was saying a piece of a god, and then the moment passed, and the pieces dissolved into ordinary breath, and the old gods went back to being the things people said when they said nothing at all.

Aldric collapsed. Serah—bleeding from a cut above her eye, breathing hard, sword still drawn—caught him before his head struck the dais. And the crown rolled to a stop against the altar steps, empty, just gold, just metal, nothing whispering through it at all.

The silence that followed was the strangest thing Cyrus had ever heard.

Because it was not silent. Not yet. The dead were still there—the cathedral dead, the catacomb dead, the drowned choir's own beloved gone, all of them murmuring, exclaiming, singing, a tide of voices washing over her one last time. *It's done,* the drowned boy's dead brother was saying somewhere. *It's done, it's done, tell her—* And beneath it, faint and familiar as a hand on her shoulder, the voice she had listened for all her life:

*Cyrus. Oh, my girl. My girl, you did it, you—*

Vess. Mid-sentence. Her goodbye, arriving at last, unfinished.

And Cyrus understood, with a clarity that felt almost gentle, that the rite was waiting on her. That the gift would leave when she released it, and only when she released it, and that some last clause of her own inheritance gave her this single breath in which to choose—to hold on, to refuse, to keep the dead forever and let the god dissolve anyway, because the surrender had been spoken and the bone broken and perhaps, perhaps the price was negotiable—

She looked down at Aldric, stirring in Serah's arms. At Maren's impossible son, crying in his mother's arms. At the crown lying empty on the marble.

"Goodbye, Vess," Cyrus said aloud, to a woman across the city who could not hear her. "Tell the rest I'm sorry. Tell them the debt is paid."

She let go.

* * *

The dead went quiet mid-word.

Not gradually. Not fading. Simply—gone, the way a lamp goes when the flame is pinched: one instant a world of voices, and the next a silence so total that Cyrus heard her own heartbeat stumble, heard the blood in her ears, heard the small creak of her own jaw as she began, without meaning to, to cry. Around her the choir was still singing the last verse, but she caught it only with her human ears now, thin and far away, music and nothing more. No murmur beneath it. No whisper in the stone. No voice in the carved saints or the ivory inlays or the bone at her belt.

For the first time in her life, Cyrus Fairweather heard the world exactly as everyone else heard it.

Below, Aldric sat up on the dais steps—sat up himself, his own motion, his shadow moving precisely when he moved—and looked at the shattered fingerbone in her raised hand, and then at her, and mouthed two words across the ruined splendour of his own coronation:

*Thank you.*

The bells began again—no one had ordered it; the ringers simply started, the way people sing after a storm—and the sound rolled through Cyrus like a tide through an emptied shell, and she stood on the gallery rail with tears on her face and nothing at all in her ears but bells, bells, bells.

It was Serah, climbing the gallery stair with blood on her cheek and her sword sheathed at last, who found the flaw in the triumph. She stopped beside Cyrus, followed her gaze down to the empty crown on the marble, and frowned.

"Fairweather," she said slowly. "If the god is dissolved—if it scattered into prayer, like you said—then answer me this." She turned, and her eyes were sharp with a soldier's arithmetic. "Prayer goes *somewhere*. Everything the Ledger ever traded went somewhere. So where—"
