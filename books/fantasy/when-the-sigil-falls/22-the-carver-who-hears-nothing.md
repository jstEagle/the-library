## 22. The Carver Who Hears Nothing

The sea came first each morning now, before the light, before thought. Cyrus learned its schedule the way she had once learned the schedules of the dead: by listening. But this was a different listening—salt air pushing against the shutters, gulls arguing over the harbor's leavings, the long slow exhale of water against stone that never asked anything of her and never, ever answered.

She rose in the dark because her hands preferred it. Her hands had their own opinions these days, unclouded by voices, and Cyrus had discovered she liked them better this way. She lit the lamp over the workbench—a proper lamp, whale oil from the chandlery two doors down—and laid out the day's stock: cow horn, boxwood, a small heap of bone offcuts bought fair and square from the butcher on Harrow Lane, who thought nothing of them at all.

That was the miracle of it, if she was honest, and she was trying to be honest lately, in the quiet way people do when no one is watching them perform it. The butcher thought nothing of the bones. The bones thought nothing of themselves. They were just bones—calcium and memory's empty house—and when Cyrus took up her knife they stayed silent as stones.

She carved combs. That was the whole of the trade now. Combs with teeth fine as rain, combs for thick hair and thin, combs with a heron worked into the spine or a sprig of sea-lavender or nothing at all, for customers who wanted nothing but a thing that worked. She sold them from the little shop fronting the harbor road, between the rope-maker and a woman who mended nets and sang to herself all day in a voice like gravel rolled in honey. The sign above the door said FAIRWEATHER — COMBS & SMALL CARVINGS, painted by Cyrus herself in a hand steadier than she'd expected it to be. She had considered leaving off the name. Then she'd decided that hiding was finished. Hiding had been the old life's whole architecture, and she had watched it burn.

Her knife moved. Outside, the sky went from ink to iron to the pale washed pink of a mussel shell, and the fishing boats went out one by one, lamps swinging, and Cyrus Fairweather carved ordinary things for ordinary people, and heard nothing, and was learning—slowly, the way scar tissue learns to be skin—that this was not a wound.

* * *

The town was called Greywater Reach, though the water wasn't grey so much as green-gold where the morning caught it, and the reach wasn't much of a reach, more an elbow of coast where the cliffs stepped back as if making room for something. What they made room for was the town itself: forty-odd roofs, a chapel with a leaning spire, a market square cobbled with stones worn round and slick as river pebbles. No guild house. No auction cellars. No masters weighing souls against coin behind doors of black oak. When word had come down the coast about what happened in the capital—the coronation interrupted, the god unmade, the Ledger House ash—Greywater Reach had mostly shrugged and gone back to its herring.

Cyrus had arrived three weeks after, with a satchel of knives, a purse of honest savings, and a silence in her head so vast she'd spent the first nights awake inside it, listening to it echo. It had been eleven years since she'd last heard true quiet. Eleven years of murmurs under every task: the dead in the femurs, the dead in the ribs, the dead in the little finger-bone charms the guild sold to widows who wanted one more word from their husbands. She had told herself for eleven years that she couldn't hear them. Denial, Vess used to say, is a load-bearing wall, girl. Don't lean on it too hard, but don't you dare knock it out before the roof is ready.

Vess had died before the roof came down. Cyrus still didn't know whether that was mercy or theft.

She set the first comb of the day in the window display, tilting it so the light ran along its teeth. A boy of maybe eight pressed his nose to the glass outside, fogging it, until his mother pulled him away by the collar. Cyrus smiled at their retreating backs. Children came in often, drawn to the carvings the way children are drawn to anything small and precise, and none of them ever flinched near her workbench, and none of the mothers ever crossed themselves. In the capital, they would have. In the capital, everyone knew the story by now, the way stories flatten as they travel: the bone-carver witch, the boy-king, the god that wore him. Some versions gave Cyrus white eyes. One, which had reached her via a tinker's gossip, insisted she had sung the god apart in a language older than the moon, which was flattering and entirely false. She had spoken plain words in a shaking voice, kneeling on the coronation dais with blood in her mouth, while Serah held the guards and the drowned choir sang and Aldric screamed in a register no sixteen-year-old throat should hold.

Plain words. Shaking voice. It had been enough. That was the part she still turned over at night, like a coin she suspected was foreign currency: it had been enough. All those years of believing the gift made her matter, and in the end what mattered was that she stood up and spoke.

The bell over the door rang. First customer. A fisherman wanting a comb for his wife, coarse-toothed, "for the salt," he said, tapping his own knotted hair apologetically. Cyrus wrapped it in brown paper, took his copper, wished him calm seas, and meant it. He left. The bell rang again. The silence settled back over the shop like dust, and she let it.

* * *

Letters came on the coastal packet, Tuesdays and Fridays, and Tuesday's letter was from the palace, which still made her laugh a little, alone in her shop, holding paper bearing the royal seal as if it were a live crab.

*Fairweather,* it began, in the careful, over-controlled hand of a young man taught that handwriting is character. *The herring this year is excellent. I am told you would know that already, living where you live, but I report it anyway because I have resolved to write to you about ordinary things, and I am keeping my resolution even though it makes these letters very boring, which Master Corvel says is good practice for statecraft.*

*I sat in judgment today on a dispute about a fence. Both farmers shouted. Neither hit anyone. I ruled that the fence should be rebuilt straddling the line, and both men hated the ruling equally, which Corvel says means it was fair.*

*Dame Serah sends her regards and also a complaint: you have not written to her directly in two months and she refuses to admit she minds, so I am telling you she minds, which she will deny, and then we will both be in trouble, which I have decided is worth it.*

*The thirteenth moon rises tonight. I will look at it and think of you. Not with fear. With fear is how I looked at it for so long that I forgot there was another way. Tonight I will simply look.*

*Your friend,*
*Aldric*

Cyrus read it twice at the counter, then a third time standing in the doorway where the light was better, and folded it into the tin box beneath the register where the others lived. Twenty-two letters so far. She wrote back about combs and weather and the net-mender's songs, ordinary things, keeping her own resolution. Once, only once, she had written about the night of the unbinding—what it felt like to speak the surrender clause aloud, to feel the rite take hold of her voice and use it—and then she had burned that letter in the shop's little stove rather than send it, because some things were not for carrying through the post, even sealed, even trusted.

Serah she did write to, less often than the Dame would like, but honestly. Their alliance had survived the war it was built for, which neither of them had quite expected. Grief, Cyrus had learned, makes strange bedfellows and stranger friends; she and Serah had nothing in common except a dead brother and a dead mentor and a stubbornness that could bend iron, and somehow that had proven enough foundation. Serah's letters were terse and full of reports on the eastern parishes—prayers going up unanswered, as prayers should be, the gods scattered back into the space between asking and hearing—and always ended the same way: *The watch continues. You did the right thing. Do not argue with me in absentia; you always win, and it's infuriating.*

The thirteenth moon. Cyrus glanced through the window toward the hills where it would rise tonight, a sliver of bone-white in the dark, and felt—not nothing, but close. A coolness. An old ache with no tooth left in it. For two years that moon had been a countdown, each turn of it a footstep coming closer, and now it was just astronomy, beautiful and indifferent, and she found she could look at it the way Aldric promised to look at it: simply.

She put the letter away and went back to her knife.

* * *

Afternoons belonged to the walk. Cyrus kept it religiously: out along the harbor road, past the salt-sheds, up the cliff path to the bench some forgotten fisherman had planted facing open water. From there she could see the whole curve of the bay, the boats coming home heavy, the gulls wheeling behind them like a debt being collected. She sat with bread and cheese and ate slowly, doing nothing, accomplishing nothing, and this—she was still surprised by it, two years on—this was allowed. Nobody in Greywater Reach required anything of her. The guild had needed her ears and then her blood; the crown had needed her knife and then her nerve; the dead had needed her, always, endlessly, need after need after need. The bench required nothing. The sea required nothing. She was discovering what a person was when she was not required, and the answer, so far, seemed to be: someone who liked cheese, and bad poetry, and the smell of tar, and who laughed more easily than she had ever known.

Some days the drowned boy came up from the village to sit with her. He fished now, properly, with a boat and everything, and had become weirdly famous along the coast as the lad who'd been pulled from the sea twice and refused to drown on principle. He remembered the drowning—all of it, the cold and the dark and the long green singing—and he carried the memory openly, the way other men carried scars, showing it when it helped and covering it when it didn't. He had been the first of the returned, the wrong-sized answer to a grieving mother's prayer, and when the Thirteenth had scattered, he had stayed himself. Stayed warm. Stayed real. Cyrus checked sometimes, without meaning to, some old instinct reaching out to test whether he was hollow—and found nothing to check with. Just her own eyes, looking. He was just a boy eating bread on a cliff.

"You've got sawdust in your hair again," he told her, picking at a curl of shavings caught above her ear.

"It's fashionable."

"It's filthy."

"Fashionable filth." She knocked it loose herself. Below them a boat came in, and the fisherwoman—his mother, the one whose prayer had started everything, whose drowned memories had paid for a miracle that nearly unmade the world—waved up at them with both arms, whole-hearted, shameless. The boy waved back. Two years, and Cyrus still sometimes caught her watching the two of them together with a tightness in her chest she couldn't name: gratitude braided through with guilt, because that prayer had cost the world so much and had also, in its crooked way, saved it. If Merrow hadn't answered. If she hadn't recognized the boy's voice from her carvings. If the thread hadn't started unraveling exactly there.

"Come Saturday," the fisherwoman called. "There's pie."

"There's always pie," the boy muttered fondly.

"There is always pie," Cyrus agreed, "and it is always excellent, and I will be there," and the fisherwoman laughed and went back to her nets, and the three of them sat in the wind a while longer, saying nothing, needing nothing, the sea saying plenty for everybody.

* * *

Autumn deepened. The shop grew busier as the feast-days approached—combs for gifts, small carvings for sweethearts, a commission for the chapel: a new collection plate rim, oak, with a border of wheat sheaves, because Father Anselm had decided that after two years of the congregation eyeing the offering basket with theological suspicion, the church could use some visible gentleness. Cyrus carved the wheat sheaves and thought about harvests and hunger and the old gods bound not for wickedness but for appetite, and found she could hold the thought without flinching. That was the strangest gift of the silence, the one nobody warned her about: without the dead whispering their grievances in her skull, she could finally think about them clearly. Pity them, even. The Thirteenth had shown her its loneliness in those last desperate hours—an eternity of wearing other people, of being prayed to and never spoken with—and she had chosen dissolution for it anyway, and she did not regret the choice, but she permitted herself the sorrow of it. Both things were true. She had room for both things now. The silence was big enough to hold grief without amplifying it.

One gray afternoon near the winter solstice, a traveler came into the shop out of the rain.

He was not from Greywater Reach—wrong boots, wrong accent, a road-dusted cloak beaded with salt spray—and Cyrus sized him up the way she'd learned to in her guild years, quick and unshowy. Middle-aged. Tired. A clerk's hands, ink-stained. He browsed the display case a long time while the rain ticked on the window, and Cyrus let him browse, wrapping a parcel for another customer, calling a price across the room. Eventually he came to the counter and set down a comb he'd picked—"for my daughter, the one with hair like her mother, gods rest her"—and while Cyrus wrapped it, he lingered, working up to something. She knew the signs. Everyone who eventually mentioned the capital, or the god, or her, performed this same small hesitance first.

"You're her," he said finally. Not accusatory. Almost shy. "The carver. From the stories."

"I'm a carver," Cyrus said. "The stories exaggerate."

"My sister prays now," he said. "In the eastern parishes they all do. Just—ordinary prayers. Into the quiet." He turned his hat in his hands. "It used to frighten me, the quiet. After the answered ones, after the wrong-sized miracles. But my sister says the quiet is where you hear yourself think. Says the gods sleeping in us was never the same as the gods being gone, if they were ever anything at all." He looked up. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because I'm a stranger and it's raining," Cyrus said gently, "and strangers in doorways hear true things." She tucked the parcel into his bag, added a second, smaller one unasked—a little carved wren she'd had in the case for months, waiting for whoever needed it. "For your daughter. From the exaggerated version."

He laughed—wet-eyed, embarrassed, grateful—and paid too much, and left, and the bell rang, and the rain went on.

Cyrus stood at the counter a moment, hand flat on the wood, feeling the shape of what he'd said settle into her like rain into dry ground. Prayers into the quiet. Not expecting answers. Just speaking, because speaking is what people do with their hearts, and it turns out the speaking was always the point. The old gods had stolen the answering and ruined the asking both. Now people got to talk into the dark and trust the dark to hold it, and somehow that was holier than any miracle.

She thought of Vess, and did not expect to hear her mentor's voice, and did not, and found that she could remember the voice perfectly anyway—its rasp, its irony, the way it softened only around the words *well done*. Memory, it turned out, was its own kind of hearing. It required no gift. It only required love, and time, and the willingness to keep the channel open on purpose.

*Well done, girl,* said the memory, in Vess's exact dry tone, and Cyrus said aloud, to the empty shop, "Don't push it, old woman," and laughed, and the laugh echoed in the silence and came back to her as nothing but her own sound, and it was enough.

* * *

Winter solstice. The thirteenth moon rose full over Greywater Reach, huge and white as a knucklebone, and the whole town turned out on the harbor road to watch it because Father Anselm had declared it a festival—no theology, he'd been careful to specify, just light, and the turning of the year, and being glad of both. Bonfires on the beach. The net-mender singing. Pie, inevitably.

Aldric's letter had arrived that morning, short, uncharacteristically so: *Tonight is the anniversary. I will stand on the balcony where it happened and look at the moon without fear, and somewhere far north you will be looking at the same moon, and Serah says that counts as a meeting. She is right. Happy solstice, Cyrus. Thank you for my life, which fits me now.* And below, smaller, almost sheepish: *P.S. I am learning to carve. Badly. Send tips.*

Cyrus stood at her shop window after the festival fires burned down, the last few revelers drifting home, the great moon sliding westward over the water. On the counter beside her lay the day's last oddity: an old woman had come in at dusk, a stranger passing through, and bought nothing, and left instead a single object on the counter—an antler tip, polished smooth by decades of handling, the kind of piece that once, in the old days, would have hummed against Cyrus's awareness like a struck bell. The woman hadn't explained. Had only touched two fingers to her heart, the old gesture, the one the grieving families used, and walked out into the snow.

Cyrus picked it up now. Turned it in the lamplight. Bone, yes—old bone, loved bone, the kind that had once held voices the way cups hold water. Somewhere inside her an old reflex reached for it, the trained instinct of twenty years: *listen.*

Nothing.

No murmur. No whisper. No ghost of a murmur, no ache where a voice used to live. Just weight, and temperature, and the faint animal smell of keratin, and the lamplight lying gold along its grain. A thing. Only a thing. Beautiful, in the way all worked bone was beautiful, and mute as the moon.

She waited a moment longer, giving it every chance, out of respect—for the woman who'd left it, for the years, for whatever dead soul might once have sheltered in it. The silence held, vast and clean and hers.

And Cyrus Fairweather smiled.

She set the antler tip on the shelf behind the counter, among the other keepsakes—the tin of letters, a burnt scrap of ledger cover Serah had smuggled to her, a child's drawing of a heron comb done in charcoal by the nose-print boy from the window. Tomorrow she would carve it into something. A button, maybe. A bead. Something small and useful and utterly unhaunted, to be worn by someone alive.

Outside, the tide turned. She blew out the lamp, locked the door, and walked home through the falling snow, whistling—badly, happily—into all that blessed quiet.
