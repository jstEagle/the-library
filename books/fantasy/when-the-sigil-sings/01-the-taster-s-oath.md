## 1. The Taster's Oath

The pheasant arrived under a silver dome, and Ada Marchetti tasted death before the queen did.

She stood at Her Majesty's left shoulder in the Amber Hall, where forty candles burned behind gauze screens so no one could count them, and lifted a sliver of breast meat onto the little gilt fork. Around her, courtiers pretended not to watch. They always watched. A taster was the only person in the palace whose failure was interesting.

The meat was warm, faintly sweet, glazed with quince and something darker underneath — plum? No. She let it sit on her tongue a full ten seconds, because ten seconds was the rule, and rules were the only architecture she trusted. Beneath the sweetness ran a bitter thread, thin as wire.

*Foxglove,* she thought. *Or foxglove's cousin.*

She swallowed anyway. That was the part no one outside the guild understood: tasting wasn't testing. Testing you could do with a silver needle and a drop of reagent. Tasting was a vow. You put the poison in your own body first, because a dead taster proved nothing and a living one proved everything — that the dish had been tampered with, that the amount was survivable, that the queen could eat and live.

Ada swallowed, held up two fingers — the signal for *suspect, but I am not dying* — and watched four guards converge on the under-cook who had carried the dome. He wept immediately, which meant he was probably innocent. Guilty men in the Ashen Court had learned to weep slowly, on schedule.

"Marchetti." The queen's voice was dry as old vellum. "How much?"

"A hair's worth, Majesty. Bitter, not lethal. Someone wanted you ill by morning, not gone."

"And you'll be?"

"Sick by morning," Ada said. "Forgive me if I'm gray at tomorrow's audience."

The queen almost smiled. "You are the most expensive illness I employ. Go home before it takes you. And Marchetti—" a pause, candlelight catching the ash-gray silk of her sleeve, "—if you learn who seasoned my pheasant, I will pay very well to know before the Court does."

"I don't sell secrets, Majesty."

"No," the queen said. "You trade them. That's worse. Go."

Ada bowed, already calculating how many minutes she had before the foxglove reached her heart. Forty, perhaps fifty, if she took the antidote within twenty. She walked out through the servants' corridor at an unhurried pace, because a taster never ran — running said *afraid*, and afraid said *guilty* — and only when she reached the cold air of the mews did she allow herself three fast steps and a curse.

The Vellum Guild kept its back rooms above a chandler's shop in Candlewick Row, behind a door that smelled of beeswax and iron. Ada knocked twice, paused, once more, and heard the lockmaker's mechanism sigh open — nine tumblers, each tuned to a different note. Sefa had built it deaf, they said, which everyone repeated without ever asking what it meant, and Ada had stopped correcting people years ago.

Inside, the guildhouse was warm and dim and smelled of paper, ink, lamp oil, and under it all the faint ozone tang of things that should not exist being stored politely. Corvane stood at the long table with his sleeves rolled, reading a manifest by lamplight. He was sixty and built like a dock crane, with ink-stained fingers and a voice he used like a carpenter uses a level — flat, exact, checking whether you were true.

"You're gray," he said without looking up.

"Foxglove in the pheasant."

"Antidote?"

"On its way down." She pulled off her gloves. "Someone at Court is impatient."

"Court is always impatient. It's their whole personality." He slid the manifest toward her. "Ledger work tonight. Three acquisitions came in from the Salt Road caravan, and I want them entered before dawn, because if the Ashen auditors arrive before they're logged, they're contraband, and if they're logged first, they're guild property. Law is mostly a question of timing."

Ada sat, drew the master ledger toward her, and opened it to the ribboned page. This was the other half of her life, the half that fit better than the first. By day she was the queen's taster, immune to eleven poisons and counting, a woman whose body was a museum of survived attempts. By night she was the keeper of entries for the Vellum Guild, which traded in impossible things, and whose ledgers were the closest thing the capital had to a conscience.

"Item one," Corvane said. "Bottled storm. Small. Coastal vintage, off the Teeth. Don't shake the case again, whatever you write."

She wrote: *One storm, bottled, minor. Acquired: Salt Road caravan, third bell. Condition: restless.* Her pen moved in the tight, upright hand the guild trained into every keeper — handwriting that could not be forged because it could not be imitated, because keepers were taught to write with the wrist locked and the fear showing nowhere.

"Item two. Borrowed years. Six of them, from a miller's son who wanted to forget a winter. We hold the years; he holds the forgetting. Standard terms."

*Six years, borrowed. Interest: none. Return date:* — she left the line blank, as the form required. Some debts had no dates. Some people paid in decades.

"Item three." Corvane hesitated, which he almost never did. "A name. One that was never spoken. Recovered from a burned house in the Marsh District. The family died keeping it."

Ada's pen paused. Names that were never spoken were the rarest stock the guild handled — rarer than storms, rarer than years. A spoken name could be used against you. An unspoken name was pure collateral, a thing with weight and no handle. Guild law said unspoken names were to be entered, sealed in wax, and never read aloud by anyone, including keepers.

She entered it without reading it, writing around the blank space where the name lived, describing its container, its provenance, its wax seal — green, thumbprint, cracked at the edge. Then she sanded the page and sat back, waiting for the foxglove to finish its argument with her blood.

Corvane watched her. "Headache yet?"

"Not yet."

"Liar."

"Yes," she agreed, and he huffed what passed for a laugh.

He had found her eleven years ago, face-down in a gutter off Rope Street, thirteen years old and starving, having just eaten a stolen apple that someone had laced with rat-bane on principle. Most children who survived that became cautious or dead. Ada had become curious — she'd crawled to the apothecary and demanded, hoarsely, to know *what exactly* had nearly killed her, in what quantity, and why it hadn't quite. Corvane had been the apothecary's customer, waiting. He'd looked at her a long time.

"You have the constitution," he'd said, "and the questions. Both are rare. Usually people have one or the other."

He'd fed her, housed her, trained her. Built her immunity dish by dish, dose by dose, year by year — a slow education of her blood, until she could take a bite of anything in the capital and stand there judging it like a magistrate. In exchange, she kept his ledgers. It was, she understood even then, a purchase. But it was the fairest purchase anyone had ever made of her, and belonging bought honestly still felt like belonging.

"Corvane." She flexed her fingers against the ledger's edge. "The pheasant. Foxglove doesn't grow anywhere near the palace kitchens. It comes from apothecaries, or from us."

"We sold none this season."

"So either someone robbed us, or someone remembered an old recipe." She met his eyes across the lamplight. "Who hates the queen quietly right now?"

"The list is a scroll and a half." He closed the manifest. "But here's a thought for a sick woman to sleep on: quiet hate is rarely about the queen. It's about what the queen stands in front of."

"What's behind her?"

"That," Corvane said, "is a very good question, and the answer is older than both of us, and it is not written in any ledger I own." Something crossed his face then — quick, gone, like a fish turning under ice — and Ada, who had spent eleven years learning to read him, filed it away the way she filed everything: carefully, and with the date.

Sefa appeared in the doorway with the antidote — bitter green, smelling of crushed walnut shells — and set it by Ada's elbow without a word, the way Sefa did everything. She signed *drink* in the shorthand only the two of them used, and Ada drank, and the wire of bitterness in her chest loosened by degrees.

"Anything else come in today?" Ada asked. "Odd lots. Strays."

"One odd lot." Corvane's voice went carefully neutral, which was itself information. "A book. Century-old, recovered from a house collapse in the Old Quarter. Burned at the edges. The seller swore it was worthless."

"Everything's worth logging."

"This one," Corvane said, "I'd rather log myself, tomorrow, when you've slept off your poison. It's catalogued as restricted."

Ada raised an eyebrow. In eleven years, Corvane had never once told her a ledger entry was too delicate for her. Restricted entries existed — she knew the shelf, the black binding, the wax — but the *work* of entering had always been hers. That was the deal. That was the whole shape of their bargain: her body by day, her hand by night, and in return, no secrets between them.

"Restricted," she repeated.

"Temporary restriction." He gathered the manifest, and for a heartbeat he wouldn't look at her. "Old prophecies make bad neighbors, Marchetti. Especially ones with dates in them."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere below, the chandler's shop bell rang once — late customer, or no customer at all. Ada's headache was fading, replaced by something colder and more patient, the feeling she got when a dish was *almost* clean but not quite, when the bitterness hid a second bitterness underneath.

"Dates in them," she said.

"Go to bed."

She went — past the black-bound shelf, past the sealed drawer where the unspoken names slept, down the hall to her narrow room with its window over Candlewick Row. She lay down and listened to her heart steady itself, dish by dish, poison by poison, an immunity built one survived attempt at a time.

And she thought: *eleven years, and tonight he kept a book from me.*

She thought: *a prophecy, with dates.*

She thought: *tomorrow.*

Outside, the first gray light of the false dawn crept along the rooftops, and somewhere across the sleeping city, a tinker with burn-scarred hands woke suddenly from a dream he would not remember, in a room where every clock had stopped at the same impossible hour.

Ada did not know that yet.

But the ledgers, she would think later, had known it all along.
