## 2. A Date Written in Ash

The book arrived at the guildhouse in a crate of salted herring, which was how most impossible things arrived, and Ada Marchetti had learned long ago not to ask questions until her hands were clean.

She cleaned them anyway. Habit. The same hands that had held a silver spoon to the queen's lips an hour ago, the same hands that had swallowed three bites of braised quail so the crown could eat without dying, now turned the cracked leather cover of a book that smelled like a cellar fire and something older underneath it — smoke's grandmother, she thought. The smell of a thing that had been hidden on purpose.

"Recovered from where?" she asked.

The courier, a fish-eyed man named Doss who never blinked enough, shrugged. "Ashfall Abbey. Well. What's left of Ashfall Abbey. Brother Ondrey sold it before he died."

"Sold *it*?" Ada said. "Or sold *himself*? There's a difference with abbey books."

Doss considered this with visible discomfort and decided not to answer, which was its own answer. She signed the acquisition slip — *one (1) codex, provenance disputed, condition poor, contents unknown* — and entered it into the ledger in her small, exact hand:

**Item 4,417. Book, prophetic (claimed). Recovered Ashfall Abbey. Value: pending verification. Keeper's note: verify before Corvane sees it.**

The last part wasn't protocol. Protocol said everything went to Corvane first, always, because Corvane had pulled her off the street when she was nine years old and starving and had never once, in fifteen years, made her feel like merchandise. But Ada had also learned that some items changed Corvane's face in ways she didn't like, and prophetic books were historically one of those things.

So she sat down alone in the cataloguing room with its single lamp and its smell of ink and vellum dust, and she opened the book.

* * *

It was a mess of a thing. Water damage along the spine, pages stuck together in places so that peeling them apart felt like surgery. Half of it was devotional nonsense — hymns to saints whose names had been struck through by later hands, marginalia arguing about grain prices. A century-old monk's boredom, preserved.

Then, near the back, the handwriting changed.

It stopped being cramped and became careful. Deliberate. The ink shifted from brown iron-gall to something darker that still looked wet after a hundred years, and Ada felt the hair on her arms rise before she'd read a single word, the way you feel a draft from a door that shouldn't be open.

The page was headed by a date.

She knew the date the way you know your own name — not by thinking about it but by having written it, over and over, in ledgers, in oath-books, in the tasters' log where every dish, every dose, every night of her slow poisoning got recorded in her own hand. Tonight's date. Inked here a hundred years ago by a dead monk who could not possibly have known it mattered.

*On this date,* the script read, *the sealed door will know its key.*

Ada set the book down. Picked it up again. Read it four more times, as if repetition might wear the sentence into harmlessness. It didn't. Sentences like that didn't erode; they eroded *you*.

Below the line, the monk — or whoever had written in the monk's book — had described a man.

*Tinker by trade. Left hand burned, the scars old, gained young. Carries his father's tools and no memory worth the name. He will come to the city on this date, and he will be met.*

Ada's mouth went dry.

She was meeting a tinker at dusk. An appointment she'd made herself, three weeks ago, through the guild's ordinary channels — a minor acquisition, nothing impossible about it: the Solano workshop had in its possession a clock said to run without winding, and the guild collected such curiosities the way magpies collected rings. Routine pickup. She'd chosen Wren Solano himself for the handoff because he was the youngest son and the least likely to ask questions, and because — she told herself now, hearing how thin the excuse sounded even inside her own head — she'd liked his letters. Careful handwriting. A little formal. The kind of person who numbered his pages.

Left hand burned. Gained young.

She had seen that hand once, at the market, when he'd reached across a stall to steady a falling spool of wire and she'd caught the shiny rope of scar tissue running from wrist to knuckles. She had stared — rudely, openly — and he had stared back like a man recognizing someone from a dream he couldn't place, and then they'd both pretended nothing had happened, which is what civilized people do with omens.

That had been before the book. Before the date.

Ada closed the codex gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal, and sat in the lamplight doing arithmetic she didn't want to do. Coincidence was possible. Coincidence was *usually* the answer — she'd logged enough "cursed" items to know that ninety-nine in a hundred were frauds, grief wearing a costume. But the guild didn't pay her to believe in the ninety-nine. It paid her to take the hundredth seriously, because the hundredth was why the Vellum Guild existed, and why it existed in secret, and why somewhere beneath the capital there was supposedly a door that nobody spoke about above a whisper.

And the book said he would be *met*. Not that he would arrive. That he would be met. As if his arrival were scheduled. As if someone, a century ago, had written him into tonight the way Ada wrote entries into ledgers — item, provenance, value pending.

Her duty was simple and she recited it to herself like an oath: log the book, report the book, let Corvane decide. Corvane would know what to do. Corvane always knew what to do; that was the whole architecture of her loyalty, load-bearing since she was nine years old.

Her curiosity was simpler and needed no recitation at all.

* * *

She almost reported it. She had the slip half-written — *Item 4,417, contents verified, prophetic material, recommend immediate review* — when Sefa knocked and leaned in the doorway with the loose grin of a man whose poison tolerance was second only to hers.

"You look like you ate something," he said. "Bad batch?"

"Worse. A book."

"Those'll do it." He came in, glanced at the codex, and — this was the strange part, the part she'd turn over for days afterward — his grin faded completely. Sefa, who joked through everything, who'd once laughed while vomiting up arsenic into a basin, looked at the closed book like it was a debt collector. "Where'd that come from?"

"Ashfall Abbey. Why?"

"No reason." He said it too fast. Then, quieter, in the tone of a man deciding to break a rule: "Ada. If there's a date in there, and the date is soon — don't go alone tonight."

"I haven't said I'm going anywhere."

"You've got your going-out boots on under the desk. You always change boots." He tapped the doorframe twice, their old signal — *watch yourself* — and left before she could lie to him properly.

She looked down. She had, in fact, already changed her boots.

* * *

Dusk came down over the capital like ash settling, gray and slow, and Ada walked to the meeting with the codex wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs and every sensible argument marching in formation behind her and losing ground with every step.

The tinker's quarter was all hammer-song and coal-smoke, shutters thrown wide against the evening heat, and Wren Solano was waiting outside the workshop with a wooden case under his arm and his burn-scarred left hand curled around the handle like the scars were holding on too.

He saw her coming and something crossed his face — not surprise. Recognition, the uncomfortable kind, the kind people apologize for.

"Miss Marchetti." He offered the case with his right hand, keeping the left tucked away, and she hated that she noticed, that the book had taught her to see the wound as a specification. "The clock. Runs eleven days without winding. My grandfather swore it was luck. I've taken it apart twice and can't find the mechanism for it."

"There isn't one," Ada said, before she could stop herself.

Wren went very still. "Beg pardon?"

"There's no mechanism. Some clocks don't run on springs." She heard her own voice the way you hear yourself talking in a fever — accurate and insane. "I'm sorry. That was—"

"No." He shifted the case to his other arm. His eyes had gone bright and slightly frightened, the look of a man finding dry land. "No, say that again. Because my whole life, things—" He stopped. Started over, lower. "Clocks stop around me. Did you know that? Have done since I was a boy. Grandfather said it was my hands, the burns, some metal in the scars. But it's not just clocks. Birds land near me. Strangers bow. Last month a woman in Ashmarket dropped to her knees right in front of me and wept, and I'd never seen her before in my life, and she said—" His voice caught on it, the way a sleeve catches on a nail. "She said, *finally.*"

The lamp-lighters were moving down the street behind him, touching flame to wicks one by one, and the light reached him last, as if arriving on schedule.

Against Ada's ribs, inside the oilcloth, the codex seemed to warm.

Every instinct she owned — the taster's instinct, the guild's instinct, fifteen years of *log it, report it, let Corvane decide* — told her to take the clock, pay the man, walk away, file the entry, forget the date. It was a clean exit and it was right there.

Instead she heard herself say: "Wren. What did your grandfather tell you about your hand?"

He blinked. "That I grabbed a kettle. When I was a baby."

"Did you ever see the kettle?"

A pause. The hammer-song down the street seemed to falter, one smith missing a beat.

"...No," Wren said slowly. "Funny. I asked once, and he just said some tools are born broken and leave it at that. Why? Miss Marchetti, why are you asking?"

Because the book says you're the key, she didn't say. Because a hundred-year-old monk wrote your scars into tonight and called it prophecy, and I am standing here choosing, right now, between the only family I have ever known and a stranger whose clocks stop.

"Because I think you should come with me," she said instead. "Tonight. Before dark finishes falling. And I think you shouldn't ask where."

Wren Solano looked at her for a long moment — the look of a lonely man being handed a map to his own life — and behind him, at the far end of the street, a hooded figure stepped out of a doorway, watched them both, and did not move.

Ada's hand found the knife under her cloak.

"Walk toward me," she said quietly, not taking her eyes off the figure, "very slowly."
