## 3. Wren

The clock on the mantel died at half past seven, the way it always did, and Wren Solano pretended not to notice.

He'd bought it off a widow in Tanner's Row for three coppers and a promise to fix her grandson's boot buckle. It was a good clock. Brass works, walnut case, a pendulum with a satisfying weight to its swing. He had cleaned every gear himself, oiled every pivot, and for six days it had kept perfect time.

Then he'd moved into the room, and it stopped.

Not broken — stopped. He'd checked. The mainspring held tension, the escapement sat clean, the whole mechanism waited like a dog told to stay. The moment he carried the clock across the threshold of his landlady's fence line, the hands began to crawl again, slow and guilty, as if waking from a nap they were ashamed of.

He set his tea down and looked at it. Half past seven. Same as yesterday. Same as the day before.

"Morning," he said to it. "Still dead?"

The clock said nothing. Clocks were good that way. They didn't bow to him in the street.

That had started last week. A fishmonger on Cordwain Bridge had dropped his tray mid-haggle, gone pale, and bent at the waist so low his beard nearly touched a mackerel. "Forgive me," he'd breathed, though Wren hadn't said a word to him. "I don't know why I—" And then he'd straightened up, laughed at himself, and thrown in an extra herring for free, hands shaking.

Since then: a baker's boy who knelt in flour dust. A toll-keeper who pressed both palms flat to her counter like he was something arriving. An old woman who crossed herself twice, kissed her thumb, and whispered *the hour keeps itself* before hurrying away.

Wren was a tinker. He fixed kettles and sharpened scissors and soldered cracked spectacles back together. Nobody bowed to tinkers. Nobody stopped clocks around tinkers. Whatever was happening to him, it was not the kind of thing you could fix with a whetstone and patience, and that fact sat in his chest like swallowed river stone.

He packed his kit — pliers, flux, a coil of tin, the little hammer his father had left him — and stepped out into the morning.

* * *

The city tried to be normal about him, mostly.

Mostly it failed.

By noon he'd collected two bows, one curtsy from a man far too large to curtsy convincingly, and a long stare from a guard at the Ashgate whose hand drifted toward his sword and then, strangely, away again, as if the sword itself had declined the assignment.

And then there was the market.

He went for solder wire and stayed because of her.

She stood at the spice stall with her hood down, dark-haired, sharp-eyed, wearing the plain grey of palace service but not moving like a servant — servants scurried; she *surveyed*. She was counting something. Not coins. Spices, maybe. Or people. Her gaze swept the crowd with the patience of someone used to finding poison in things that looked harmless.

Then she saw him.

He watched it happen — watched recognition hit her face like cold water. She didn't bow. That was the strange part. Everyone else bowed or fled or prayed. She just went still, all the way through, the way a house goes still when it hears a floorboard creak upstairs. And there was grief in it. Actual grief, raw and unguarded, aimed at him like she'd found a grave walking around in daylight.

Then she paid for saffron she didn't look at, turned, and walked away fast, and the crowd closed behind her like water.

He should have let it go. He knew that even then. A stranger's strange look was cheap currency; everyone in the capital was haunted by somebody. But his hands were shaking as he paid the spice merchant, and not from any craft-sickness. Something about being seen like that — known like that — had cracked open a door in him, and everything uncanny about the week came pouring through it.

Clocks that died in his presence. Strangers kneeling. And now this.

He asked the spice merchant if he knew the woman. He did not. Asked the fruit seller two stalls down. Shrugs. Finally a beggar by the fountain, toothless and cheerful, squinted after her and said, "Palace grey. Taster's livery, that is. Poison-taster for the queen." He smacked his lips. "Dangerous work. Short careers."

Taster. Ada Marchetti — no, he didn't know her name yet, but he'd learn it soon enough, because fate apparently enjoyed a tidy arrangement — was the first person in this entire mad week to look at Wren Solano and see something specific instead of something holy.

He wanted very badly to know what it was she'd seen.

* * *

It was waiting for him when he got home.

Not the hooded figure — he missed them by minutes, he was sure, because the alley cat that slept under his stairs was still staring at the corner where the lane met the wall, pupils blown wide, tail puffed to twice its size. But the figure had left something tucked under his door, weighted against the wind with a pebble.

A scrap of vellum.

He knew vellum. Fine stuff, calfskin, the kind that cost more than his tools. This piece was old at the edges, soft as cloth, and written on it in brown ink gone amber with age was a single line:

*Meet me where the bells forget the hour. Bring your hands. You will need them.*

He read it three times. On the third reading, his knees informed him that sitting down would be wise, and he obeyed.

Because it was his handwriting.

Not similar. Not close. His — the cramped g's, the way his t's crossed slightly left, the little hook he put on the end of an *n* because a master scrivener had mocked it once when he was nine and he'd refused, out of spite, to ever fix it. Every letter was his. The ink was older than his grandfather.

He turned the scrap over. Blank. Turned it back. Read it again. The words didn't change, which felt like a personal insult.

*Bring your hands.*

His hands. The burn-scarred left one, which he'd gotten at six years old pulling a kettle off a grate, and which ached on cold nights like it remembered a different fire. The right one, quick and clever, that could mend anything anyone brought him and had never once been able to mend whatever was wrong with him.

Below the message, small and precise, in the same impossible hand, was a date.

Wren did the arithmetic the way you do arithmetic when you're hoping to be wrong. His birthday. Minus the date. He counted twice, then a third time with his eyes shut.

The note was dated eleven years before he was born.

* * *

Night fell on the capital, and the bells rang the hour anyway, forgetting nothing, which meant the meeting place in the note — some chapel yard where the bells had gone silent decades ago — was real, and findable, and the worst idea available to him.

He went anyway. Obviously. Curiosity had gotten him this far in life; pride saw no reason to stop the partnership now.

But the chapel yard was empty when he arrived, moonlight pooling between tilted gravestones, ivy eating the bell tower above him. No hooded figure. Just wind, and stone, and the particular silence of a place that has given up.

Except—

"You're late."

The voice came from behind a tomb. She stepped out with her hood up this time, and even shadowed, he knew her: the taster from the market, the woman who had looked at him like a ghost. Up close she was younger than he'd guessed, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"I didn't have an address," Wren said. "Also, you left me a note in my own handwriting dated before my birth, so if we're keeping score of strange behavior, I'm ahead."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, more like a door opening a crack and then thinking better of it.

"Ada," she said. "Ada Marchetti."

"Wren. Wren Solano." He hesitated. "Though I'm starting to wonder how much of that is true."

Her jaw tightened. "Why do you say that?"

"Clocks stop around me. Strangers bow to me. A fishmonger apologized to me for reasons neither of us understands." He pulled the vellum scrap from his coat and held it up between two fingers, the way you hold something you suspect might be venomous. "And today I received a message in my own handwriting, dated eleven years before I was born, telling me to bring my hands somewhere. So. Ada Marchetti, who looked at me in the market like I was her own funeral — start talking. What am I?"

Ada looked at the scrap for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone quiet and careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice they've already heard crack once.

"You're an entry," she said. "In a ledger I wasn't supposed to show anyone. And tonight I learned what kind of entry." She glanced over her shoulder at the empty street, the dark windows, the listening city. Then back at him, and the grief from the market was there again, banked now, controlled, but unmistakably real.

"Walk with me," she said. "Quickly, and out of the light. Because if my guild knows you've found me — and they will — then the people hunting you aren't going to be strangers who bow." 

She was already moving, coat pulling tight around her, a shadow sliding between the gravestones. Wren looked down at the scrap in his hand — at his own handwriting promising him a future older than his life — and followed her into the dark.
