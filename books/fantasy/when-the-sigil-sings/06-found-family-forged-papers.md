## 6. Found Family, Forged Papers

Sefa opened the door before Ada finished knocking, which meant she'd been watching the street for an hour. She took one look at Wren's burn-scarred hand and the ash still in Ada's hair and stepped aside without a word.

"You're tracking soot on my clean floor," she said, closing the bolt behind them.

"Good to see you too."

"It is not good to see me. It's terrible. Terrible people bring trouble, and trouble brings inquisitors, and inquisitors bring fire." Sefa was already moving toward the kitchen, pulling down cups as though the correct response to catastrophe was tea. "Sit. Both of you. You look like a pair of drowned cats."

The tasters' quarter lived in the shadow of the palace kitchens — narrow houses, narrow lanes, everyone's windows angled away from the walls so no one could be accused of looking at anything they shouldn't. Sefa's house sat at the end of Cress Row, wedged between a chandler and a shuttered dye shop, with a cellar deep enough that the guild had once used it to store contraband vintages back when Ada was new.

Ada hadn't been inside in two years. It smelled the same: beeswax, dried thyme, the faint chemical sweetness of whatever Brannock was brewing upstairs.

Because Brannock was here. Of course he was. He appeared at the top of the stair with his sleeves rolled and his eyebrows raised, holding a flask of something that glowed a color glow should not be.

"Well," he said. "The poison girl and a stranger who smells like burnt oak. This is either very good or very bad."

"Brannock." Ada's voice cracked on the name, and she hated it. "I need—"

"I know what you need." He set the flask down on the banister with exaggerated care, as if it might apologize later. "You need me to not ask questions until you've eaten something. You always get that look when you haven't eaten. Sit him by the fire. I'll make the stew."

* * *

They came within the hour, the way Ada had known they would, because there was no one else left to come to.

Tam Vetch arrived first, slipping through the kitchen door without disturbing it — a habit from a lifetime of picking locks that had never once been offered to him honestly. He was sixty if he was a day, small and neat as a folded letter, and he read lips with such fluency that most people forgot he'd never heard a word in his life. He looked at Wren for a long moment. Then at Ada. Then he tapped two fingers against his palm — the guild's old sign, the one that meant *trouble follows you*.

"Yes," Ada said. "It does."

He shrugged, hung his coat on its peg, and went to help Sefa lay the table.

Brannock came down last, carrying the stew like it was a peace offering to some small angry god. He was a disgraced alchemist in the way that a storm is disgraced weather — the Guild of Chirurgeons had struck his name after the Incident with the Fountain, and he'd never let anyone forget that the fountain had deserved it. He ladled out four bowls, then paused over the fifth.

"He eats first," Brannock said, nodding at Wren. "Before anyone touches anything."

"He doesn't need—"

"He eats *first*, Marchetti." His eyes were steady over the steam. "You're being followed by people who poison things. Old habits."

So Wren ate first, blushing, while four strangers watched him like he was made of glass and prophecy in equal measure, and only when he'd swallowed three bites did anyone else pick up a spoon.

* * *

Later, with the bowls cleared and candles burning low, Tam spread a sheet of paper on the table and began to write in his quick, slanted hand:

*How long do we have?*

"A day," Ada said. "Maybe two. The safehouse burned. Corvane knows I ran. The inquisitors will pull every record in the city by morning, and my name is on enough guild documents to hang us all."

Tam wrote: *Then we move tonight.*

"No." Sefa, from the doorway, arms crossed. "You move tonight. I'm not leaving my house. I've fed half the tasters' quarter through two winters and one plague. Nobody burns Cress Row; even the Court needs someone willing to taste their wine."

"Sefa—"

"Don't *Sefa* me, girl. I buried a husband the Court executed on a rumor. I know exactly what they're worth." She said it flatly, without heat, the way you'd report the price of bread, and Ada felt the floor tilt under her a little, because in eleven years Sefa had never once spoken of her husband.

Tam wrote again, sliding the page across: *What did you bring us into?*

And so Ada told them. All of it — the ledgers, the bottled storms and borrowed years, the prophecy inked a century ago, the entry listing *W. Solano* among assets never delivered, the seal beneath the capital and the bloodline that held it shut. She told them about Corvane ordering the boy handed to the Ashen Court like a parcel, about the safehouse burning behind them, about the tunnel under the tannery and the two days of running on bread crusts and fear.

She told them everything except the thing she couldn't say yet.

When she finished, nobody moved. The candle flames leaned in the draft from the window, straightened again.

Then Tam picked up his pen and wrote, unhurried: *You'll need papers. Good ones. Not guild forgery — guild forgery is the first thing they check.* He looked up at Wren. *You can pass as a journeyman chandler. I have a cousin in Marrowgate who owes me. We'll build you a whole life by dawn.*

"And her?" Wren asked quietly.

Tam's pen hovered. Then: *Her too. If she'll go.*

"I'm not going anywhere," Ada said. "I have to go back. There's a ledger in that guildhouse with my—" She stopped.

Everyone looked at her.

"With your what?" Sefa asked.

And that was how it finally came out, there in the candlelight with the stew cold and Tam's pen still wet: that Ada Marchetti could not remember her mother's face. That she had no memory of any street, any winter, any life before Corvane lifted her out of the poorhouse at nine years old and said *you belong to the guild now*. That other children remembered being frightened, remembered running, remembered *something*, and Ada remembered nothing at all — just a white space where a childhood should have been, smooth as an unwritten page.

"The ledger," she said. "There's an entry. I think there's an entry with my name in it, and I think it isn't a record. I think it's a receipt."

Brannock swore, softly, in three languages.

Wren reached across the table. He didn't take her hand, exactly. He just set his own beside hers, scarred side up, close enough that she could feel the warmth of it.

"Whatever they took," he said, "we'll find the entry. And then we'll find what it cost. And then—" He faltered. "I don't know what comes after 'then.' But I'll be there for it."

"You barely know me."

"I've known you since you stared at me in the market like I was a ghost," he said. "That's more than I've known anyone lately. Clocks stop around me, Ada. Strangers bow to me by mistake. A stranger handed me my own handwriting dated forty years before I was born. You're the first person in weeks who looked at me like I was *real*." A crooked almost-smile. "I'd call that knowing someone."

* * *

They worked through the small hours. Tam forged; Brannock brewed a wash that aged fresh vellum twenty years in an hour; Sefa fed them and bullied them and pretended she wasn't listening to every word. By the time the sky went grey, Wren Solano existed twice over — once as a hunted heir to a sealed door, and once as a journeyman chandler named Aldous Ferrin, apprenticed in Marrowgate, references impeccable, history immaculate.

Ada watched Tam work and felt something loosen in her chest that she didn't have a name for. Eleven years in the guild, and she had called it family because there was nothing else to call it. But this — a deaf lockmaker trading his craft for a stranger's safety, a ruined alchemist giving away his last quiet night, Sefa standing guard at a window she refused to abandon — this wasn't purchased with secrecy. Nobody here owed anybody anything.

Except, she thought, looking at the grey light, they were all about to pay anyway.

It was Sefa who saw it first. She touched Tam's shoulder and pointed at the window, and Tam went very still, and then he turned and wrote one word and pushed it into the middle of the table so fast the pen tore the paper:

*ASHEN.*

Down the length of Cress Row, at the corner by the chandler's, stood three figures in grey coats that drank the lamplight. Not moving. Not knocking on any doors.

Just waiting — and the nearest of them turned his head, slowly, until he was looking directly at Sefa's window.

Directly, Ada understood with a cold clarity that felt almost calm, at her.
