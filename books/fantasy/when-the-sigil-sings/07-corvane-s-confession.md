## 7. Corvane's Confession

They take her at the tasters' gate.

Ada should have seen it coming — the street sweepers gone by midmorning, the baker's stall shuttered, the pigeons that always mob the courtyard absent as if paid off. Instead she is counting coin for a wheel of cheese when four figures in ash-grey close around her like a hand closing. No words. One of them holds out a strip of vellum with her own handwriting on it, an entry she never wrote: *A. Marchetti, recovered asset, one (1).*

"That's not mine," she says.

"It's in the ledger," the inquisitor says, "which makes it true."

She thinks of fighting. She has been trained to fight since she was small enough to be carried under one arm — poisoners make poor brawlers but excellent opportunists, and there is a knife in her sleeve and venom under her thumbnail that would drop any two of them before the third finished shouting. But Sefa is somewhere behind her, and Dez and Ilse are in the house behind *him*, and Wren —

Wren is already gone ahead, down toward the river with Ilse's cousin, wearing a coat too big for him and a face that finally looks like it belongs to someone. If Ada fights here, they will simply go collect him next, and he will not have a knife in his sleeve.

So she lets them bind her wrists with silk — of course silk; the guild does everything beautifully — and walks between them through streets where nobody looks at her, because looking at people being taken is how you end up in a ledger yourself.

* * *

The Vellum Guildhouse has a front door that most members never see. It opens onto a modest clerk's office on Candlemakers' Row, all ledgers and lamp oil and the smell of hot wax. The back door — the one Ada has used every night for eleven years — opens onto the tasting corridor beneath the palace, so that acquisitions can move between worlds without touching daylight.

Today they bring her in through neither.

They bring her through the cellar door, down stairs she has never descended, though she knows the guildhouse the way she knows her own teeth. The stair goes further than the building should allow. That is the guild's oldest trick, she thinks distantly: real estate as sleight of hand. You buy a narrow lot between a chandler and a cobbler and you build downward until geography gives up arguing.

At the bottom is a room with no windows and one table, and Corvane sitting at it.

He looks old tonight. She has watched him lie to ministers and mourn convincingly at funerals for men he ruined, and he has never once looked his age, but now the lamps find every line in his face, and his hands — those precise, ink-stained hands — are flat on the wood as if holding it down.

"Sit," he says.

"I'd rather stand."

"You'll want to sit."

She sits. Defiance is cheap; information is expensive, and she has learned to buy it however it comes.

An inquisitor sets something in front of her. Not chains. A ledger — the master ledger, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth with handling, its spine broken in three places from being opened ten thousand times. She has entered a thousand transactions into its daughters. This is the original. It smells of smoke and iron gall ink and something older underneath, like the inside of a sealed room.

"Page forty-one," Corvane says quietly.

She turns to it.

Her hands do not shake. She is a taster; her hands have never once shaken, not over dead birds, not over dying queens, not over this.

The entry is dated twenty-two years ago. It is written in a young man's script — Corvane's, before the years had pressed it into its current elegance — and it reads:

*Acquired: one name, complete with history attached. Seller: a debtor of the Hollow Street houses, settling arrears in kind. Asset: female child, approx. age six, healthy, quick. Name struck from all records per standard practice. Memory of same withheld from bearer. Bearer to be raised in service. Consideration received: full satisfaction of debt plus interest.*

Below it, in a different hand — older, dryer, pressed hard enough into the vellum to tear — a single line of annotation:

*Payment approved. G.M.*

Guildmaster.

*Approved by the first guildmaster,* some distant part of her notes, the part that catalogs even while it bleeds. *Who has been under the palace for a hundred years. Which means this was ratified before I was born, which means—*

Which means nothing. Which means everything.

"My name," Ada says. Her voice sounds like someone else's. "You bought my *name.*"

"We bought your *debt,*" Corvane says. "The name came with it. Names usually do."

"What was I called?"

The question comes out of her before she can stop it, small and naked, and she hates it more than she has hated anything in eleven years of hating carefully.

Corvane's jaw works. "That's not — the record doesn't—"

"*What was I called?*"

"The seller didn't know," he says, and the honesty in it is somehow worse than a lie. "Children that far down in the Hollow Street don't get named, Ada. They get numbered until someone bothers. Your mother sold the number and whatever answer she'd give if anyone asked. There wasn't one." He leans forward. "You think I'm telling you this to hurt you. I'm telling you because it's the truth you've been standing on your whole life: there was no girl to steal. There was a debt, and a child, and a transaction. We *made* you. Every skill, every loyalty, every dish you've ever survived — that's the consideration side of the entry. And the guild pays its debts, Ada. Always. To the last copper."

"So this is a collection," she says. "I'm the outstanding balance."

"Wren Solano is the outstanding balance." His voice cracks open at last, and underneath it is not cruelty but something exhausted and terrified, and that frightens her more than anything else in the windowless room. "Ada. Listen to me. Listen. I have read the founding contracts. I have spent thirty years paying down what the first guildmaster signed us into, and I know exactly what happens if that door stays open. You saw what came out of it. You *saw.*"

"A man," she says. "We saw a man."

"A man who has been *down there for a century,* planning." Corvane pushes back his chair and stands, and paces, and she realizes she has never seen Corvane pace; Corvane does not waste motion. "Every war needs logistics. Someone fed the last one — supplies, names, borrowed years, bottled storms. You think the guild invented impossible trade? We inherited it. We inherited *him.* And when he gets his hands on Wren, he will not kill the boy. He'll use him. A seal is just a lock, and a lock is just a contract, and Wren is the signature. Give him up — to me, to the Court, I don't care anymore — and we renegotiate. That's all this ever is. Renegotiation. Nobody has to die. Nobody else has to be *erased.*"

The word lands in her chest like a swallowed pin.

*Erased.*

She thinks of Sefa laughing with his mouth full. Of Dez's deaf, deft hands coaxing a lock apart like a stubborn knot. Of Ilse's kitchen and the alchemical smell of burnt sugar. Eleven years of belonging purchased through secrecy, and underneath it, page forty-one: *bearer to be raised in service.* She has loved these people because the ledger told her to. Or worse — because the ledger left her nothing else to love. No name. No mother worth remembering. No self before the guild, because the guild *ate* it and recorded the meal as fair payment.

And yet.

And yet the warmth in Ilse's kitchen was warm regardless of who signed for it. Sefa's friendship doesn't require provenance. Whatever she was before page forty-one, whoever she might have been without it — the woman sitting in this chair chose Wren. Chose the tunnels, the found family, the defiance. That choice isn't inked anywhere. It can't be collected.

"No," Ada says.

Corvane stops pacing.

"I won't give him up. Not to you, not to the Court, not to your hundred-year-old creditor." She closes the master ledger and slides it back across the table like a dish she's tasted and found wanting. "But thank you for the lesson, guildmaster. Truly. I've been trying to understand what the guild is since the night I found that prophecy, and now I do. It's a book. Just a book. And books—" she stands, wrists still bound, spine straight as a ruled line, "—books get rewritten."

Something moves behind Corvane's eyes — grief, or fear, or the terrible flicker of a man watching his best student become his worst error.

"Ada," he says softly. "The amendment clause requires a keeper. And keepers pay equivalent cost. Do you understand what you'd be trading? Page forty-one took your *name*. An amendment of this size takes—"

The door above the stairs bangs open. Boots, many boots, and the ash-grey of the Court pouring down the steps with a hooded figure at their center — and the figure pulls back its hood, and Ada's blood goes cold, because she knows that face from a fresco in the vault, from a portrait a century faded:

The first guildmaster smiles at her.

"Serra Marchetti," he says pleasantly, using a surname she has never heard, "we really must discuss your account."
