## 8. The Sealed Door

They took his boots first.

Wren didn't understand why until they were three landings underground, descending a spiral stair cut from black stone that sweated cold. Barefoot, he felt it through the soles of his feet: a pulse. Slow, enormous, patient as a heart in hibernation. The mountain under the palace wasn't asleep. It was counting.

The Ashen Court's inquisitors walked ahead and behind him, six of them, their grey robes dusted with ash that never fell off. Their leader — a woman with a voice like a drawer closing — had introduced herself only as Verity, which Wren suspected was the last honest thing anyone had said to him all week.

"You'll open the door," she had told him, back when he still had boots. "You won't be harmed. You won't even be touched. The sigil answers to your blood and nothing else. After that, the Court handles everything."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we find your mother's sister's boy in Vell Harbor, who has the same blood and less sense." She hadn't smiled. Inquisitors, he was learning, did not smile. They economized.

So he walked. Down and down, past torch brackets older than the dynasty above them, past walls where the mortar had been replaced so many times it looked like scar tissue. The pulse in the stone grew louder. Or closer. Or more awake.

His left hand ached inside its burn scar. It had been aching since the vellum scrap — since he'd read his own handwriting dated ninety-eight years before his birth. *The door opens from either side,* the scrap said. He'd written that? When? To whom?

"Stop," Verity said.

They'd arrived at the bottom, and Wren forgot how to breathe for a moment.

The door was not a door. That was his first thought. It was a page — an enormous one, thirty feet high, stretched across the mouth of a vault like a sheet of vellum nailed to the world, blank white and faintly luminous, and across its surface ran lines of script in a language he shouldn't have known but did, the way you know the shape of your own name in the dark. A sigil sat at its center, inked in something that wasn't quite black. As he watched, the sigil moved — slowly, like a hand writing itself.

"The Vellum Seal," Verity said. There was something in her voice now that hadn't been there before. Not reverence exactly. Hunger wearing reverence's coat. "A century old. A century holding. And every year thinner."

"It's paper," Wren said stupidly.

"It's *agreement*," she said. "Paper is just agreement made visible. Walk to it. Put your marked hand on the sigil. That's all."

He thought about running. Six inquisitors, one staircase, no boots. He thought about Ada Marchetti, whom he'd known four days and trusted more than anyone alive, which said something devastating about everyone else alive. He thought about what she'd screamed through a closing door yesterday morning before the guildmaster's men dragged her away: *Don't let them use you standing up. Make them carry you.*

So Wren Solano fell down.

He dropped like a puppet with its strings cut, folded onto the cold stone, and went limp, breathing in theatrical little sips. It was not his finest performance. It was, however, enough to make six inquisitors converge on one body at once, which was the entire point, because converging meant crowding, and crowding meant backs turned, and backs turned meant —

The lights went out.

Not dimmed. Out. Every torch on the stair guttered simultaneously, and in the dark someone small and fast said a word Wren didn't catch, and then the dark itself caught fire — green, acrid, screaming — and Verity shouted *"Alchemist!"* in a tone that suggested the Court had opinions about disgraced alchemists.

Hands found him in the blackness. Small hands, calloused in the pattern he now knew belonged to lockmakers. "Up," Odile breathed against his ear. "Can you run barefoot?"

"I can fly barefoot if you get me out of here."

"That's the spirit. Sefa has the tunnel. Go left when I say."

Chaos behind them — the alchemist's green fire eating robes, Verity's voice cutting orders like a blade through wet cloth — chaos, but organized chaos, because Sefa had mapped this. Of course Sefa had mapped this. You couldn't spend ten years as a taster without knowing every throat the palace used to swallow, and the tasters' tunnels ran beneath the palace like veins beneath skin. They'd come up through the wine cellar, through the cistern, through a seam in the rock that Odile had opened with two picks and one muttered insult.

Now they were going back down it, deeper than the cellar, along a passage barely tall enough for Sefa and too narrow for anyone's comfort, lit by the alchemist — whose name, Wren had learned at three in the morning, was Brannoc, and whose fire burned green because "grey fire is for people with nothing to prove."

Ada was at the front. Of course she was. She moved through the dark like someone who'd been born in it, and when the passage forked she chose without hesitating, and when Wren stumbled she was simply there, her shoulder under his arm, as though she'd been waiting for the excuse.

"You came," he said. It was all he could manage. His whole chest was full of it.

"Don't sound surprised. I broke out of a guildhouse cell for you. Do you know how embarrassing that is? Corvane built that cell. I helped pick the locks as a test."

"You picked locks at nine?"

"I tested locks at nine. Picking came later." Her grip tightened. "Listen to me. Whatever's behind that door — whatever comes through it — it isn't your fault. Blood isn't consent. Say it."

"Blood isn't consent."

"Good." A pause, and in the dark he heard rather than saw her almost-smile. "You're a terrible liar, by the way. Your falling-down act needed work."

"It bought us forty seconds."

"It bought us everything. Never argue with results."

Then the passage ended, and they were looking at the vault from the other side — from a gallery of broken stone above and behind the great white page of the seal, hidden in shadow, close enough that Wren could see the sigil's script crawling, and see, with a lurch, that the lines were thinning. Fading. The way ink fades when the page has been read too many times.

Or signed too many times.

Below, the inquisitors were regrouping around the fallen tinker-shaped absence of him. Verity stood at the seal's center, and she was not searching the shadows for fugitives. She was reading the door. Her lips moved with the script, tracking line by line, and Wren watched her face change — watched hunger curdle into something like fear — and understood, with the awful clarity that arrives only when it can no longer help you, that Verity had never intended to reseal anything.

"They're not here to guard the door," he whispered. "They're here to *harvest* it."

"Shh." Ada's hand closed over his wrist. Below them, Verity drew a knife — a thin grey blade, ash-colored — and pressed it to her own palm, and began to write on the air, and the sigil on the door *answered*, leaning toward her like a flower toward sun.

"No," Ada breathed. "No, no — that's not how it works, that's not — the seal needs a Solano, it needs *him*, why would she—"

"Because," said a voice from the door itself, "she doesn't need a Solano. She needs a signature. And I have been signing things for a very long time."

The sigil flared white. The page of the door split down its center like a book opening — which, Wren realized, was exactly what it was — and the vault filled with light that cast no shadows, and out of the opened door walked a man.

Not a monster. That was Wren's second thought of the night, and it was worse than the first.

The man was tall and spare and dressed in the fashion of a century ago, robes cut in the old guild style, and he looked *tired* — tired the way stone is tired, the way mountains are tired, tired beyond any meaning the word still held. His hair was white as fresh vellum. His eyes were ink-black and patient. And on his left hand, old and faded and unmistakable, was a burn scar shaped like half a sigil.

Wren's scar matched it perfectly. Mirror and mirror.

"Hello, Wren," the man said gently. "I wrote to you. I'm sorry it took so long for my letters to arrive — time moves strangely, in here." He stepped over Verity, who had collapsed to her knees, weeping with something that might have been worship, and looked up — directly up, directly at the shadowed gallery, directly at Ada — with eyes that held a hundred years of ledgers.

"Hello, keeper," he said. "Did Corvane ever tell you who taught him to keep books?"

Ada made a sound Wren had never heard from her — small, involuntary, the sound of a floor giving way.

"He didn't," the first guildmaster said softly, kindly, terribly. "Of course he didn't. Come down, child. Both of you. It's time someone in this family finally read the original entry."

And behind him, in the opened doorway, Wren saw the thing the seal had actually been holding: not an army, not a god, but a single table, and on the table a ledger, open, its pages turning themselves — and every name in it was a name he knew.

The war hadn't been sealed behind the door.

It had been *filed*.
