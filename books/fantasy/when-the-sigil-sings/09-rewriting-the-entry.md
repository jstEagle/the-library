## 9. Rewriting the Entry

The first guildmaster stepped out of the dark like a man stepping out of a long nap — unhurried, dusted with a century of it, blinking at the vault's torchlight as though he'd ordered it lit himself.

He had. In a way.

"You're younger than I expected," he said to Wren. His voice was dry paper, patient as interest accruing. "Though blood thins. Sit down, boy. We have work."

Wren didn't sit. He stood in the open doorway — the sealed door, the door that had taken a hundred years and a war and a Solano's hand to open — and behind him nothing came out but cold air and this old man in a coat that had gone out of fashion before Ada's grandmother was born. The first guildmaster. Vessin Corvane's name was on the charter; she'd copied it a thousand times into acquisition logs. *Vessin Corvane.* Except the man in front of her wore that face, aged wrong, preserved wrong, like fruit in syrup.

"You," she said. "You've been under here. All this time."

"All this time is mine," Vessin said pleasantly. "I paid for it. Do you know what a century costs, girl? Everything. Everything except the one thing worth keeping." His eyes went to Wren again, warm as an appraiser's. "Him."

Behind Ada, Sefa's hands were moving fast — *what is happening* — and Tam the lockmaker had gone gray, and Quill the alchemist was backing toward the tunnel mouth with the particular care of a man who did not want to be noticed by history. The Ashen Court's inquisitors lay groaning or still along the far wall where the door's opening had thrown them. Corvane — her Corvane, the current one, the man who had pulled a half-starved child out of the gutter and given her soup and ledgers and a life — stood nearest the door, and his face was not triumph. It was terror.

"Vessin," Corvane said. "The seal held for a hundred years. Let it hold longer. There's no need—"

"There is every need." Vessin flexed his left hand, and Ada saw that beneath the grime his fingers were ink-stained to the bone. A keeper's hands. "War is coming regardless. It always comes. I merely intend to arrive first, with the only weapon that matters." He smiled at Wren. "A key who doesn't yet know what he unlocks."

"He knows," Ada said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't planned to speak. But the words were out, and they tasted like every dish she'd ever swallowed for the queen — bitter things, poison things, things meant to kill someone else.

"He knows, and he's not yours. He's not anyone's asset." Her voice shook and she let it. "I've read the ledgers, Vessin. All of them. You wrote the founding entries yourself. And there's a law in the front of the master book — your own hand, older than the charter —"

"Don't." Corvane said it quietly, and something in his voice made her turn.

He was looking at her the way he had looked at her when she was nine years old and burning with fever after her first true poison dose, when he'd sat up all night holding a cloth to her forehead. Grief. Real grief. That was the worst part — that it was real.

"Ada," he said. "Whatever you're thinking, the cost—"

"—is equivalent," she finished. "Any entry may be amended by its keeper, at equivalent cost. Your oldest law. The one thing even you can't rewrite, because you wrote it as a cage for yourself too."

Vessin moved then — fast, impossibly fast for a man pickled in a century — and the vault went from standing still to screaming. He caught Wren by the burn-scarred wrist and the sigil above the doorway flared white, and Wren cried out not in pain but in *recognition*, because whatever lived in that mark knew its maker's touch, and Ada understood with horrible clarity that the door hadn't opened for Wren at all. Wren had been the handle. This man had been the hand.

Tam lunged; Vessin flicked two fingers and Tam folded like wet vellum. Sefa grabbed Ada's arm — *run, run* — and Ada shook her off, because running was for people who hadn't spent ten years memorizing where everything in the guildhouse was kept, including the thing that mattered most.

The master ledger lay on its reading stand three steps to Vessin's left. Three steps. She'd carried heavier trays through narrower gaps between armed men.

She ran.

* * *

Later, Ada would remember it in fragments, the way she remembered the taste of anything strong: in flashes, in afterburn.

The reading stand. The ledger's cover, warm under her palms as if the book had a pulse — and maybe it did; maybe every entry ever written into it was still in there, breathing. Vessin turning, his face going through surprise into something colder. Corvane shouting her name. Wren wrenching free, or being let go — she never learned which — and the weight of the book in her arms as she put her body between it and the first guildmaster of the Vellum Guild.

"Girl," Vessin said, almost kindly, "that book will eat you alive."

"It's been feeding on me since I was six," Ada said. "One more meal won't matter."

She opened it to the last page — the blank page, the page every keeper knew and no keeper used, because using it ended your keeping. The amendment clause sat at the top in Vessin's antique script: *Any entry may be amended by its keeper, at equivalent cost.*

And below it, waiting a hundred years, the line that mattered:

*The keeper is whoever holds the book and means it.*

"I'm the taster," she said aloud, to the room, to the book, to the war waiting under the floor. "It's my job to find out what's poisoned and refuse it. So."

She found the entry. It wasn't hidden; Vessin had never needed to hide it. Page one of the acquisitions, dated the year of the sealing: *Asset acquired: the war entire. Price: one door, one heir-line in trust, one keeper's vigil.* And threaded through every subsequent page, invisible until now, the fine print of the whole guild — every impossible trade, every bottled storm, every borrowed year — collateral against the door staying shut, leverage stacked on leverage, a hundred years of other people's names pledged to keep a monster fed and sleeping.

Except the monster wasn't sleeping. The monster had written the ledger.

Her pen — Sefa pressed it into her hand without being asked, deaf to the shouting, seeing everything — her pen touched the page.

"Equivalent cost," Vessin warned. "Do you understand what amending the founding entry voids? Every acquisition. Every asset returned or erased. Every debt called in at once. Including yours."

"My what?"

Corvane closed his eyes.

And the ledger, helpful as always, showed her. Ink bloomed up through the page like a bruise: *Ada Marchetti, acquired age six, payment of debt, memory withheld as surety, service rendered in perpetuity.* Not a person. An entry. A line item the guild had bought off the streets of the Tanners' Ward when some creditor wanted settling, and filed, and fed, and raised — because a keeper who remembered nothing could be shaped into anything.

The night she couldn't remember. The mother-shaped hole. Ten years of belonging purchased with a blank first page.

"Oh," Ada said. Very quietly. It should have broken her. Instead it was like setting down a tray she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten its weight.

"Put the pen down," Vessin said, "and I'll give it back. Your memories. Your name. Your mother's face. I can restore any entry — I wrote the restoration clauses too. Just close the book, and stand aside, and when the war comes you'll be safe inside these walls with everyone you love."

That was the trick of it, Ada thought. That was the whole trick, a century deep. He offered back exactly what a person couldn't refuse.

But she had spent ten years tasting poison, and the first rule of the taster's oath was carved into her bones where her childhood should have been: *the deadliest dish is the one you want to eat.*

"No," she said. "New entry."

She wrote fast, before grief could catch her, before wanting could:

*Amendment to founding entry. The war is not an asset. It is refused. The door is closed — not sealed, not bargained, closed — by consent of the key, freely given.* She glanced at Wren, and he nodded once, and his nod steadied her hand like a splint. *All collateral holdings are discharged. All debts are forgiven. The ledger is empty.*

Then the last line. The price. She'd done the arithmetic already; the book demanded equivalence, and the founding entry had been paid for with a century and a soul, and she had neither a century nor a soul to spare, only—

*Cost, paid in full by the keeper:* she wrote, *her recorded name. Her history. Her place in every memory in which she appears. Let her be unlogged, unowned, unkept — hers.*

"That's not equivalent," Corvane said hoarsely. "Ada. That's not—"

"It's exact." Her voice didn't waver now. "You can't threaten me with a war if the war is cancelled. You can't buy me with my own past if my past belongs to me. Take everything I am in your books, Vessin. Every entry, every recollection, every place I stood. It's the only thing I own outright, and I'm spending all of it."

Vessin came for her anyway.

Of course he did. Men like him never believed a door closing they hadn't locked themselves. He crossed the vault in three strides, and Wren hit him shoulder-first, and Tam, bleeding and half-conscious, threw the alchemist's satchel — Quill's whole kit, quicksilver and ash-glass — and the world went white and ringing, and through all of it Ada kept writing, because the ink only took what was finished.

The final stroke landed as Vessin's fingers closed on the ledger's edge.

The book drank.

Ada felt it drink her — not painfully, worse than painlessly, the way falling asleep feels, the way letting go of something heavy feels at the moment your arms finally straighten. Names peeled away. Faces reorganized themselves around the space where she'd been. Somewhere above them, in the palace, a queen would taste her food tomorrow and never wonder why the taster's station sat empty. Somewhere in the guildhouse, ten years of soup and fever-nights and lessons in fine script would simply belong to no one.

The sigil above the door sang — actually sang, one clear note like a struck glass — and went dark forever. Under the floor, something vast turned over, sighed, and became geology.

Vessin Corvane aged. It happened between one heartbeat and the next, the century arriving all at once, collecting itself; he crumbled into the kind of dust that gets swept up and never missed. The ledger fell closed in Ada's arms, light as a bird.

The vault was very quiet.

Sefa was looking straight at her. Sefa, who had never once forgotten a face, frowned — a small, puzzled frown, the frown of a woman trying to place why she was standing in a collapsing vault holding a stranger's pen — and then, because Sefa was Sefa, shrugged and gripped Ada's arm anyway, steady as a wall, and pulled her toward the tunnel with the others.

Stranger. Yes. That was what she was now.

Ada went. She carried the empty ledger against her chest like a child, and she did not look back at the dark door, and somewhere in the last of the torchlight she caught Wren staring at her — really staring, the way no one else in the world ever would again —

—and she watched him open his mouth, about to say her name.
