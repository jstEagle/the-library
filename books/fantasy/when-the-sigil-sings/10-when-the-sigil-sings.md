## 10. When the Sigil Sings

The bells of the Ashen Court rang for a war that never came, and nobody but me knew why.

They rang for three days after the door closed — victory peals, or maybe relief dressed up as victory — and I stood in the crowd beneath them with my bandaged hand pressed to my chest and felt like the only man at his own funeral who hadn't been invited. People wept with joy. A baker handed out rolls stamped with the royal sigil. Somewhere in the crush a child asked her mother what had almost happened, and the mother said, *Nothing, love. Nothing happened at all.*

She was right. That was the whole cruelty of it. Nothing had happened — no war, no monster, no ancient guildmaster stepping out of a hundred years of stolen life to reclaim his collateral. And no Ada.

I had learned in the vault that memory is just another entry in a ledger. Amend the entry, amend the man. When she traded her name, the world's copy of it went with the trade. Corvane stood three feet from me on the palace steps afterward and looked through me like glass. Sefa squeezed my arm and said, "Whoever got us out of those tunnels — we owe them a debt," and meant it, truly meant it, because the woman who had led us through the dark had been subtracted from every mind that ever held her except mine.

Except mine. The guild's oldest law was precise about cost, and apparently the cost of being forgotten by everyone is being remembered by one person. I've decided not to call it mercy. Mercy would have been letting me forget too.

* * *

The Vellum Guild reformed under Sefa's hand, which is a sentence I never expected to write, because six months ago Sefa couldn't read a ledger and didn't want to. She says the deaf hear numbers better anyway — you feel them, she signs, they have weight, they don't lie about how much they weigh. The lockmakers' hall gave her a room with good light. The disgraced alchemist, Pellam, drifted back within a month, ostensibly to complain about the ventilation, actually because he'd nowhere else to go and neither did anyone else who'd helped hide us. Found family, forged papers. We kept both.

The guild trades smaller now. Bottled storms still, borrowed years still, but Corvane is gone — retired to some coastal town where he tells himself he saved the capital, and maybe he did, and maybe the ledger doesn't care what he tells himself. Sefa keeps the master book under triple locks and showed me once, without comment, the page where an entry had been struck through in ink so black it seemed to drink the lamplight. The amendment clause. Equivalent cost paid.

The entry line itself was blank. Not empty — blank. The way a word is blank when it's been taken out of your mouth before you finish saying it.

"You knew her," Sefa said, watching my face. It wasn't a question. She's deaf, not blind.

"I knew someone," I said.

"Someone." She turned her hand over, the sign for *a thing lost*, then the sign for *a person*. "Which?"

I didn't answer, and she didn't press, and that's why the guild survives under her.

* * *

My clocks keep time again. I want that recorded somewhere: whatever Wren Solano was supposed to be, the key, the hinge, the bloodline heir — whatever the prophecy spent him for — the spending is done, and the mechanisms of the world no longer stutter around me. Strangers don't bow by mistake. My left hand aches where the burn scars pull in cold weather, ordinary as any old injury, and I have started sleeping through nights instead of lying awake listening for doors opening in places where there are no doors.

But I kept looking. Call it compulsion, call it grief with a tinker's hands. Every unlogged scrap in the guildhouse archive, every loose vellum leaf in the recovered prophecy books, every margin note in a century of acquisitions — I went through them the way you dig through ash after a fire, knowing most of what you'll find is ash.

In the spring I found the page.

It wasn't logged. It wasn't even bound — a single leaf tucked into the spine of the oldest acquisition ledger, slipped between the boards where only someone taking the book apart rivet by rivet would find it. Someone had hidden it from the ledger itself. From the guild's own law, which records everything, which remembers everything, which took her name as payment and would have taken this too if it could.

Her handwriting. Slanted, quick, the letters leaning forward like they were in a hurry to be finished so the next thought could start. I knew it the way you know a voice through a wall.

Three things were written on it.

A recipe. *Nightshade tincture, quarter measure, cut with honey and fennel so the queen never tastes the bitter.* Underneath, smaller: *Build immunity dish by dish. Poison yourself slowly enough and the poison becomes yours.* And below that, in different ink, added later, wry: *Note to self: this is either wisdom or a very elaborate way of dying tired.*

A joke. She'd written out a conversation between herself and Sefa, rendered phonetically in Sefa's hand-signs — *Sefa signed: if Corvane asks, the storm in the bottle escaped on its own. I said: storms can't escape. Sefa signed: that's what makes it funny.* At the bottom she'd drawn two stick figures laughing, badly, one of them clearly meant to be a tinker with terrible hair.

And a promise. Just one line, set apart from the rest, the ink darker, pressed hard:

*I will not let them use you. Whatever it costs. Even if I have to stop being the one who promised.*

I sat on the archive floor until the lamp burned low, holding a piece of paper that was the last proof in the world that she had existed, and I understood finally what she had done in the vault. She hadn't erased herself. Erasure is passive; it's what happens to things. What she did was authorship. She wrote herself out of the record the way you strike a bad entry — cleanly, at cost, in full knowledge — and the cost was every mind but mine, and she paid it because the alternative was a war, and because she had promised, and because Ada Marchetti did not break promises even when keeping them required ceasing to be.

The guild taught me that. Or she did. Same thing, now.

* * *

I bought a ledger of my own. Plain calf binding, nothing impossible about it, from a stationer who asked no questions because I paid in coin rather than favors. I ruled the first page myself, in pencil first, then ink, because some lines you only get to draw once.

Guild law says any entry may be amended by its keeper, at equivalent cost. Sefa confirmed the clause still stands. She also said, signing slowly so I couldn't pretend to misunderstand: *The keeper amends. Nobody else. You understand what you're proposing?*

I understood. Here is what I proposed, and here is what I wrote, and here is what it costs, and I pay it gladly:

**Entry the First.** *Ada Marchetti. Taster. Keeper of ledgers. Poisoner of herself nightly so others might eat safely. Loyal past all reason, including to a guild that sold her name before she could walk. Braver than the prophecy, smarter than the guildmaster, funnier than either deserved. She found an unlogged page hidden in the oldest book and made it true.*

Underneath: *This entry is not subject to amendment. This entry is not subject to erasure. This entry is kept.*

Because that's the flaw in the guild's oldest law, the flaw she saw in the vault and used and died of: an entry amended by its keeper can be re-entered by another keeper. Cost for cost. She paid the world's memory of her; I am paying the keeping of it. Every day, in ink. Somebody has to remember, and the somebody is me, and I will do it until my handwriting goes slanted and hurried and the letters lean forward like they're late for something.

The sigil sings tonight — the new one, Sefa's design, struck small into the guildhouse door where the old one used to blaze. It's a quiet sound, more felt than heard, a hum in the hinges. I put my hand against the wood on my way out and let it pass through me.

Then I opened my ledger to the second page, because mourning is a room you live in, not a door you close, and rooms need furnishing.

**Entry the Second**, I wrote. *Wren Solano, tinker. Burn-scarred left hand. Kept clocks that stopped around him until a woman stared at him in a market like a ghost and made stopping seem survivable. Owes her everything. Repaying in entries.*

There is a third page waiting. I don't know yet what goes on it. But I know the guild trades in impossible things, and I know its oldest law, and I know that somewhere in a century of acquisitions there may be a transaction that can be reversed — a name acquired as payment for a childhood debt, held in escrow, undelivered.

Ledgers balance. That's their whole religion.

Somewhere there is a debit that matches what she paid, and I intend to find it, and I intend to spend the rest of my life learning the trade well enough to make the withdrawal.

The lamp is low. The ink is drying. Entry the Third begins tomorrow — and this time, I'm not writing history.

I'm negotiating.
