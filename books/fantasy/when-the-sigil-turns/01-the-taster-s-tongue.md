## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The stew arrived at the queen's table in a silver tureen, and Ines knew it was wrong before the lid came off.

Not by sight — the broth gleamed gold and honest, thick with barley and marrow, the way the kitchens had made it for six years. Not by smell, either, or not only. It was something older than smell, something that lived on her tongue like a second tongue beneath the first, a quiet voice that woke when death was near. Her mother had called it *the knowing*. The court called it nothing at all, because the court believed Ines Ravensworth was simply a woman with a disciplined palate and a remarkable tolerance for suffering.

She stood at her post behind the queen's left shoulder, hands folded, spine straight, wearing the grey-and-gold livery of a royal taster as though it were skin that fit. The great hall of Caer Alis breathed around her: two hundred candles guttering in the draft from the doors, roast pheasant and honeyed pears, woodsmoke from the hearths, the low music of a fiddler near the gallery. The high table glittered. Queen Maren sat beneath the raven standard with her crown catching candlelight like frost, laughing at something the Ambassador of Vell had said, one ringed hand resting lightly on the arm of her chair.

Ines stepped forward when the steward nodded. This was the ritual, worn smooth as a river stone: she took the ladle, drew a measure of stew into the silver tasting-bowl, lifted it to the light — always to the light; poison loved shadow — and then she drank.

The knowing spoke before the broth touched anything but the tip of her tongue.

*Atropa.* Belladonna. Nightshade, drawn and clarified until it was nearly tasteless, sweetened with a whisper of honey to mask the bitterness — someone had worked hard at this, someone patient, someone who understood that a careless poison would be caught by any kitchen boy's nose but that this, this refined thing, might slip past even a careful taster.

It did not slip past hers. It never slipped past hers. That was the problem no one knew she had.

Six years ago, when she'd first knelt in the Hall of Oaths and sworn her tongue to the crown, she had made herself a promise: she would detect slowly. She would hesitate. She would let the ordinary instruments of her office do their work — the silver bowl that blackened with certain alkaloids, the crumb of bread floated to draw out oils, the small vial of milk-of-almond she kept tucked in her sleeve for the appearance of testing. She would give the poison time to announce itself through craft, so that her gift could remain what it must be: invisible.

Tonight there was no time for slowness. The queen's hand was already reaching for her own spoon.

"Your Majesty." Ines heard her own voice, steady, pitched to carry exactly as far as the dais and no farther. "I beg you wait."

The hall's noise thinned. Candles hissed. Queen Maren turned, her pale eyes settling on Ines with the mild, weighing look of a woman who had been disappointed by servants before and expected, eventually, to be disappointed again.

"You have a reason, Ravensworth?"

"I do." Ines set down the tasting-bowl. Her heart beat once, hard, against her ribs — not fear of the poison, but fear of how fast she'd found it, how precisely, the way a hound scents a hare across three fields while other dogs are still sniffing the grass. *Slow down,* she told herself. *Be a craftsman, not a miracle.* "The silver," she said, lifting the bowl so the court could see the faint grey bloom spreading along its inner curve. She had seen it coming; the nightshade's strength would darken it within the minute, and she had counted on that. Let the metal testify. Metal could be trusted. Tongues were only flesh. "There is a bitterness beneath the honey. I believe the stew has been adulterated."

A murmur ran the length of the high table. Somewhere behind her, a serving girl dropped a ladle and did not stoop to pick it up.

Queen Maren did not move for a long moment. Then she drew back her hand from the spoon, unhurried, regal even in retreat, and said, "Captain Vane."

He was already coming. He was always already coming; that was Aldric Vane's particular gift, to arrive at the exact moment trouble acquired a name. He crossed the dais in perhaps six strides, grey cloak stirring, his face composed into the courteous blankness he wore like armor over whatever lived beneath it. He had trained her, in the beginning. Six years ago he had taught her the rituals of the table, the signs of arsenic and aconite, the way a poisoned man's lips go numb before they go blue. He had also taught her, without meaning to, how to lie to him — because every lesson in detection was, turned over, a lesson in evasion.

"The bowl," he said now, and Ines gave it to him, and watched him study the darkened silver with eyes the color of wet slate. He looked at the stew. He looked at her. And there — there it was, brief as a fish turning under water: a flicker of something in his face that was not suspicion exactly, but its older cousin. Recognition. As if some part of him had measured the interval between her tasting and her warning and found it too short by half.

"You tasted it and knew at once?" he asked. Quietly. For her alone, though the whole dais strained to hear.

"The silver confirmed it," Ines said. "The tongue suspected."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. "Your tongue has an excellent reputation, Mistress Ravensworth."

"Its only virtue, Captain."

He held her gaze a heartbeat longer than courtesy required, then turned and gave his orders — seal the kitchens, hold the cooks, send for the herb-master, no one leaves the hall. His voice was calm and carried like a blade laid flat on a table. The hall emptied itself of comfort: guests rising, whispers braiding, the fiddler faltering into silence. The feast of Saint Corvane, meant to honor another year without famine, ended with the stew poured out onto the flagstones, where it steamed faintly in the cold like something dying.

* * *

They found the poisoner by midnight, which was far too fast, and Ines distrusted everything about it.

It was not the kitchens. The kitchens were innocent — she had watched the interrogations from the doorway of the serving passage, arms crossed against the cold, and seen the truth in the cook-mistress's red-rimmed bafflement, in the scullery boys' genuine terror. The nightshade had entered the tureen between the kitchen and the hall, in that hundred-pace corridor where the pantries opened onto the processional way. Anyone might have passed. Everyone had passed.

Yet by midnight the guard had a man in irons: a pantry clerk named Osric Fell, gray-faced, weeping, swearing with the hollow fluency of a man who knows confession is the only door out of the room. They'd found a vial in his cot, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a floorboard. Too easily found. Ines stood at the edge of the torchlight in the guard-yard and thought: *a planted vial is a signature.* Someone wanted a body for the crime, and Osric Fell — friendless, indebted, unloved — was convenient.

What troubled her more was the dose. She turned it over in her mind as the guards marched the clerk away, as the yard emptied, as snow began to fall in slow fat flakes that melted on the warm stones. The nightshade in the tureen had been calibrated — not enough to kill outright, but enough to fell the queen, or to sicken her gravely, if a single spoonful slipped past the taster. Which meant whoever had done this had planned for Ines. Had assumed either her failure or her complicity. Had built the plot with her standing in it like a pillar in an architect's drawing.

Or — and here the thought went cold as the snow melting down her collar — someone had *wanted* the attempt discovered. Wanted the court terrified. Wanted a treason to point at.

She was still holding that thought when Captain Vane found her.

"Ines." He used her given name only when no one else could hear, a habit from the early years, when he'd been half mentor and half jailor of her apprenticeship. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he did not brush it away. "You should be abed. You've earned it."

"So should you."

"One of us must stand watch over the other, apparently." He studied her face in the torchlight with that slate-gray patience of his. "The stew. You knew before the silver darkened. I watched you. Your mouth barely closed around the spoon."

"My mouth has closed around worse," she said. "And known sooner."

"That's what frightens me." He said it mildly, almost gently, and that gentleness was worse than anger. "Six years, Ines. I have watched you work a hundred tables. You are very good. Tonight you were—" he searched, and chose the word with care— "*instant*. There is a difference between skill and certainty, and tonight you were certain."

The snow fell between them. She kept her face still, the way she'd learned to keep it during tastings, during interrogations, during the long years of being a secret walking upright in a grey livery. "Certainty keeps queens alive, Captain."

"Certainty of your kind," Vane said quietly, "has a name in the old books." And before she could answer — before her blood could decide whether to freeze or flee — he inclined his head, soldier-courteous, and let it go. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow will be long. The queen means to address the realm about traitors in her kitchens, and I suspect she will not stop at kitchens."

He walked away across the yard, his footprints filling slowly with snow, and Ines stood alone in the falling white with her heart hammering out a rhythm she hadn't felt since her mother died: *he knows, or he guesses, and either way the ground under you just moved.*

* * *

Her chamber was narrow and high, in the tasters' wing above the buttery, a room the size of a generous coffin and furnished accordingly: bed, chest, desk, window. On the desk, locked in a box disguised as a book of psalms, lay the letters her mother had written in the last year of her life — eleven pages of careful, looping script that Ines had read so many times she could recite them with her eyes shut.

She unlocked the box now, by candlelight, and read the last letter again. Not for comfort. For instruction.

*They will tell you your gift is a service,* her mother had written, in the winter before the fever took her. *They will dress it in livery and pay it wages and call it loyalty. But listen to me, little wren: our gift is not the crown's. It was given to our line by an older bargain, sealed in stone at the top of the world, and the crown has forgotten what it owes. When they begin to fear what we are — and they will, they always do — remember Thornfell. Remember the pass. Remember that the borderstone does not forget, even when kings do.*

Below the final lines, pressed into the wax of the seal, was a mark Ines had never shown anyone: not a raven, not a rose, but a circle quartered by four lines, with a fifth line running through it like a river through a valley. A sigil. Her mother had called it *the old covenant's mark*, and had made her swear — a child's oath, sworn with a child's solemnity — that if ever the gifted were named enemies, she would carry that mark north to the mountains and find what waited there.

For six years Ines had kept the sigil folded inside a psalter, a relic of superstition, a dead woman's poetry. Tonight she held it to the candle and saw it plainly: the same mark, worn nearly smooth, stamped into the base of the silver tasting-bowl she had used at the high table for six years. Royal issue. Crown property. The covenant's sign, sitting in plain sight at the queen's own table, and no one alive seemed to know what it meant.

Except, perhaps, a captain with slate-gray eyes who read old books.

Ines fed her mother's other letters to the candle flame one by one, watching each page curl into orange lace and ash, keeping only the single sheet with the sigil, folding it small, hiding it in the seam of her sleeve where she hid the vial of almond milk and the other tools of her honest trade. Outside, the snow thickened. Somewhere below, a bell began to toll the hour, and did not stop at the usual count — it rang on, urgent, arrhythmic, summoning the household to a proclamation.

She dressed in her livery, pinned her hair, and put her face on: the loyal servant, the queen's tongue, the woman who had saved the court tonight and wanted nothing for it. Whatever Maren meant to say, Ines would stand at her post and listen, and smile, and begin — tonight, now, in the space of one tolling bell — to plan how a taster might vanish from a palace that watched her every meal.

The bell tolled. The snow fell. Ines Ravensworth touched the folded sigil in her sleeve and went down to hear her fate read aloud.
