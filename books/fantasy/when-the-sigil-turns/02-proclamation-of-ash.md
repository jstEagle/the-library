## 2. Proclamation of Ash

The heralds came at dawn, and the bells did not stop.

Ines stood in the scullery yard with flour to her elbows and listened to the bronze tongues of the palace ring out the hour of judgment — nine strokes, then twelve, then a pattern she had never heard in six years of service: a slow triple toll, pause, triple toll again, like a heart stumbling before it fails.

*They are ringing the Ash Bell,* said the boy who delivered the queen's morning broth to her chambers. He was eleven, gap-toothed, and his voice shook on the word *ash*. "My gran rang it once, when I was small. She said it means the crown is afraid."

The crown was afraid. Ines had tasted that fear three nights ago, steeped into Her Majesty's venison stew alongside the nightshade — a clumsy dose, a coward's dose, hidden beneath rosemary so thick it might as well have been a confession. She had caught it in the space between one breath and the next, her tongue mapping the bitterness before her mind even understood what it mapped: *atropine, black hellebore, and something older, bought from a hedge-apothecary in the Lowmarket.* She had spat into the silver testing dish, named the poison, named its source by the region of its manufacture, and watched the hall erupt while Captain Aldric Vane watched only her.

He had watched her too precisely. That was the thing that kept her awake since. Not the poison — poisons were simple, honest things compared to people. But Vane's grey eyes had narrowed not in relief when the court was declared safe, but in arithmetic. He had counted seconds. He knew how long detection should take, because he had trained her himself, and she had been too fast, too certain, too *knowing*.

Now the Ash Bell tolled over the rooftops, and every instinct she owned told her the counting had finished.

She climbed to the upper gallery where the household servants gathered to hear proclamations, pressing herself against cold stone behind a pillar of woven ironwork. Below, in the Sunward Court, the heralds assembled on the steps in tabards of white and black — ash colors, mourning colors. Queen Maren emerged robed in grey ermine, and even at this distance Ines could see how carefully composed she was, how the fear underneath had been lacquered into majesty.

Three attempts on her life in as many months. Ines had neutralized two; the third had been real enough to leave a cup-bearer dead. The realm whispered of conspiracies. And now the queen lifted a scroll sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and her voice carried on the still winter air, pitched to reach every corner of the court and every ear beyond it.

"Let it be known," Maren said, "that treachery wears many faces in these darkening days. Poison does not brew itself. Sickness does not spread without cause. There are those among us — *gifted*, they name themselves — whose blood carries arts unnatural, whose touch can kill or cure as whim dictates, who serve no god and keep no oath but their own appetites." A pause, engineered for the murmurs. "Henceforth, by decree of the Crown, every person gifted with such arts shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. They will be examined. They will be marked. And those who conceal their gift—" the scroll rustled, deliberate as a drawn blade— "shall be hunted. This is the Sigil Hunt. Let all the realm hear it: register, or die."

The murmur below became a roar, and Ines stopped hearing any of it.

*Register, or die.*

She had stood at that woman's table for six years. She had swallowed death in measured doses until her body became a fortress no venom could breach — nightshade, arsenic, waters of hemlock, the crushed pearls of quicksilver — building immunity grain by grain because the crown required a taster who could survive the second attempt, the third, the clever ones. She had given them her mouth, her stomach, her years. She had loved the work in a way she never admitted aloud: the precision of it, the quiet heroism, the sense that her strangeness had been made *useful*, made loyal, made hers.

And now the crown she had bled for — not blood, exactly, but something adjacent, something given daily and invisibly — had declared that her strangeness was treason.

Her hands were shaking against the ironwork. She pressed them flat until the trembling hurt.

Because here was the wound beneath the wound, the thing she could not say to anyone in the world: the queen's decree described her perfectly. *Arts unnatural. Blood that carries what it carries.* Her immunity was no accident of training. Every taster's apprentice built tolerance, yes, but Ines had been born already half-built — her mother had warned her of it in letters, careful letters, letters written in code and burned one by one. *Your body keeps what would kill another. You come from a long line of women whose blood remembers old bargains. Never let them count you among the gifted, Ines. Registration is a list. Lists become pyres.*

She had thought her mother paranoid. She had thought the covenant her mother invoked — *the old bargain, the warden's oath, the seal of the borderstone* — was family legend, the kind of story grandmothers embroider to make exile feel like destiny.

The Ash Bell tolled, and tolled, and tolled.

Below in the court, the first arrests began almost immediately. Ines watched a kitchen girl — Marla, who sang off-key and shared stolen honeycomb — dragged from the crowd screaming that she'd done nothing, that her little charms were nothing, that she blessed bread and that was all. Watched guards who had eaten that bread a hundred times bind her wrists without meeting her eyes. The queen watched from her balcony with an expression Ines recognized from the tasting chamber: the face of a woman swallowing something bitter and calling it medicine.

*I served this,* Ines thought. *I kept this table. I saved this woman's life three nights ago and she has answered with a hunt.*

The betrayal did not arrive as rage. It arrived as coldness, spreading outward from her sternum like frost across a windowpane — precise, beautiful, and utterly silent. She found she could see everything with terrible clarity. The way the heralds' tabards had been sewn weeks ago; the decree had been written before the last poisoning, not after. The way Vane stood apart from the other captains, motionless, his hand resting not on his sword but on the ledger tucked beneath his arm.

She left the gallery before her face could betray anything worth reading.

**From the field ledger of Capt. Aldric Vane, Royal Guard, entry dated the fourth day after the Ash Proclamation:**

*Item: Household roster reviewed per decree. Cross-referenced against the registry of suspected gifted, compiled by the Office of Inquiry.*

*Item: One name withheld from the submitted roster. Reason: insufficient evidence. Note for my own record, which no office will see: the taster Ravensworth identified the composition of the nightshade compound in Her Majesty's stew in under four breaths. Trained detection requires thirty. She also identified its regional origin without being shown the residue. I taught her the craft myself. I know what I taught her, and I know what exceeds it.*

*Item: Correspondence recovered from the residence of the late Hedda Maren (no relation to Her Majesty), herbalist of the Lowmarket, deceased under circumstances under investigation. Among her papers, a fragment referencing payment received "for the queen's business, as before." The poisoning of the queen appears, therefore, to have been commissioned through channels that answer to the crown itself. I have read this fragment four times. I have not entered it into evidence. I do not yet know what I am protecting by withholding it — the realm, or my own understanding of whom I serve.*

*Item: Ines Ravensworth. Daughter of Elira Ravensworth, who held a post in this palace twenty-two years ago and left it in disgrace no record adequately explains. I remember Elira. I remember the year the borderstone wardens were dissolved, and the silence that followed, and how the old king forbade inquiry into it. I remember a child brought to court with steady eyes and a tongue that feared nothing.*

*Item: If the girl is gifted, then the decree names her traitor, and duty requires I bring her in. If the decree is founded on a treason of its own, then duty is a word the crown has already broken, and I am only deciding which side of the breaking I stand on.*

*Item: No action taken today. Tomorrow I ride north with the third company, to Thornfell, where the smugglers' maps say the roads go up and the law goes thin. The Office believes gifted fugitives flee to Thornfell.*

*Item: They are right.*

* * *

That night Ines burned her mother's letters.

She did it in the hearth of her narrow room above the taster's quarters, kneeling on cold stone, feeding each page to the fire with the reverence owed to relics. Twenty-three letters, written across nineteen years in her mother's small, slanted hand — coded where caution demanded, plain where love insisted. Instructions disguised as recipes. Warnings dressed as blessings. *Taste everything twice, little wren, once for the crown and once for yourself.* *Never drink from the same cup twice.* *If they ever speak the word gifted in your hearing, run north before your feet finish deciding.*

The flames took the ink in gold threads before the paper blackened. Ines watched each letter curl, remembering the smell of her mother's ink — juniper gall and rainwater — remembering the last hug through a carriage window, the wheels turning, the road going south while her whole life went to the palace.

It was grief doing the burning, she knew. Grief and fury and the cold new knowledge that her mother had been right about everything, and Ines had stayed anyway, had tasted loyally anyway, had believed the crown would shelter what it used. The letters had begged her to be careful, and she had answered by becoming indispensable — as if usefulness were armor.

But she saved one page.

It was the oldest letter, the paper gone soft and amber at the folds, written before Ines could read. Her mother must have written it to be opened later — *when you understand what you are*, the heading said. Most of it Ines had memorized years ago. What mattered was the bottom of the page, where her mother had pressed a scrap of vellum bearing a copied seal: a circle bisected by a vertical line, flanked by two interlocking thorn-branches, and around its rim, worn nearly smooth, a ring of characters in no alphabet used in the human realms. Beneath it, in her mother's hand:

*The Covenant of the Borderstone, sworn between the crown of men and the courts beyond the veil, witnessed in the blood of the wardens. It bound the realms to peace and the gifted to protection — and the crown to honor both. Your grandmother broke it. The stone has been failing ever since. When it fails wholly, wren, the reckoning comes, and our bloodline was chosen for it long before either of us drew breath.*

*If you are reading this, the sigil has begun to turn.*

Ines held the page over the fire until the heat stung her knuckles, then pulled it back. The seal gleamed faintly in the firelight — and as she tilted it, the interlocking thorns seemed very slightly to move, the way a sleeping thing shifts in its den.

Outside, the Ash Bell had finally fallen silent, and in its place came a new sound: hooves in the courtyard below, many of them, saddling in the dark. She crossed to the window and looked down. Torches, grey cloaks, the third company forming up in the snow. And at their head, checking the girth of his horse with the economy of a man who had done ten thousand times, Captain Aldric Vane — turning, just before he mounted, to look up at the one lit window above the taster's quarters.

Their eyes met across forty feet of frozen air. He did not call out. He did not signal his men. He simply looked at her the way he had looked at her over the poisoned stew — counting, always counting — and then swung into the saddle and rode north toward Thornfell with thirty soldiers behind him.

North. Where her mother's letters had always told her to run.

Ines folded the covenant page into the lining of her coat, packed one bag in eight minutes the way she had practiced in her dreams for a year without admitting it, and left the palace by the door meant for carcasses and casks, walking out of the only life she had ever been permitted.

Behind her, the Ash Bell began to toll again — but ahead, past the city walls, the Thornfell mountains waited under the moon, patient as old promises, holding a borderstone that was dying, and a reckoning with her bloodline's name already written on it.
