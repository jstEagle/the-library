## 3. Thornfell Roads

The last coin of Ines Ravensworth's wages bought her a wheel of hard cheese and a lie.

"North for the wool markets," she told the carter at the Greywater toll, and he nodded the way men nod at women who carry themselves like clerks — without interest, without memory. She climbed into the bed of his wagon among crates of winter apples and let the canvas flap close over her like a lid.

By the time the wagon rolled through the gate, the city behind it was already becoming a story. Six years of tasting for the crown, of standing at Queen Maren's left hand with a silver spoon and a face like still water. Six years of swallowing small deaths so that others would not swallow large ones. All of it folded now into the shape of a woman in a borrowed cloak who smelled of apples and had no name worth remembering.

She should have felt afraid. She catalogued the feeling instead, the way she catalogued flavors: fear sat on the back of the tongue like iron, like blood that hadn't been shed yet. Beneath it ran something brighter, sharper, almost sweet. It took her a mile of jostling road to recognize it.

Relief.

*You are a traitor now,* said the voice of her mother, dead eleven years and still the strictest presence in her head. *Or you were always one, and only now the crown agrees.*

The wagon climbed. Thornfell rose ahead of them all day, blue at first, then grey, then green-black as the road entered its skirts. The carter hummed an old song about a miller's wife and a fae lover, one of those border ballads everyone sang and no one thought about anymore. Ines listened with her eyes closed and memorized the tune, though why, she couldn't have said.

At dusk the carter stopped at a waystone where three tracks met, and she paid him the rest of her thanks in cheese and walked north alone into the trees.

* * *

There were two ways through Thornfell. There was the king's road, wide and graveled and watched — every bridge a checkpoint now, every inn keeping a ledger of faces for the Sigil Hunt's riders. And there was the other way, the one Ines knew because food knew it.

Caravans did not take the king's road. Caravans took the smugglers' passes, the drover cuts, the deer trails widened by generations of carts that had never seen a tax collector. Her father had driven the Thornfell route for nineteen years before the fever took him, and she had ridden shotgun from the age of seven, learning the map not as ink but as muscle: where the switchback above Hollowmere was washed out every spring, where the ford at Cold Ash ran shin-deep in August and chest-deep in October, which caves held dry sand in wet weather and which held nothing but the cold. The smugglers' map she carried in her bodice, oilcloth-wrapped against sweat and rain, was mostly confirmation of things her body already knew. That was why she trusted it. A map drawn from someone else's memory could be forged. Her own feet could not.

She walked through the night and most of the next day, eating cheese and apples, drinking from streams she remembered by taste. The mountain gave her birch and pine and the smell of frozen earth turning over; it gave her the particular silence of high country where even the birds kept their opinions to themselves. Once, far below and behind, she saw a thread of torchlight moving along the king's road, orderly as a rosary being counted, and she stood very still among the pines until it passed.

They were hunting sigils. Marked folk. Anyone whose blood did something it shouldn't. The queen's criers had read it from every steps and market cross: register, or be taken; be taken, or burn.

*I am what they hunt,* Ines thought, watching the torchlight dwindle, and found that the thought fit strangely, like a key turned in a lock built for a different hand. She had spent six years pretending her tongue was merely disciplined, trained, hardened by professional exposure. A convenient lie. Poisons did not build immunity in six years, not these poisons, not in doses like hers. What she had was older than her service, older than her mother's caution, older than the burned letters. It was in the blood, and blood was exactly what the queen had declared treasonous.

Grief came on her at odd hours, the way weather comes on mountains. It came at dawn when she woke with her cheek against frost-stiffened earth and realized she would never again feel the palace kitchens' warmth at four in the morning, the great ovens breathing like sleeping cattle. It came at noon when she caught herself reaching for the silver spoon that lived in her sleeve out of habit, the taster's badge she'd left behind on her cot like a shed skin. And it came, worst of all, as freedom — because she was grieving, and she was also *glad*, and the two feelings occupied the same body without apologizing to each other, and she did not know what that made her.

On the third day she crossed the high shoulder of Wolfjaw and dropped into the valley called the Shepherd's Throat, where the pass narrowed enough that a cart's axle brushed both walls of stone. Here the old caravans had carved their names into the rock, some of them centuries deep: *Marrow & Sons*, *The Kindly Route*, a crude apple with a bite taken from it that her father swore meant the road was safe and everyone else swore meant nothing at all. Ines pressed her bare palm flat against the bitten apple as she passed. The stone was cold. The gesture was foolish. She made it anyway.

That night it rained, and the rain turned to sleet, and the sleet taught her the difference between fleeing and surviving. She sheltered in a drover's cave with a fire the size of a cupped hand, burning twigs one at a time, and understood that the mountain would kill her not with drama but with arithmetic: not enough calories, not enough heat, too many miles between her and anywhere warm. She had traded a cage for a wilderness, and the wilderness was patient.

She fell asleep doing sums and dreaming of spoons.

* * *

She smelled the hunters before she heard them — woodsmoke where no camp should be, and under it, faint as a whisper in another room, the green metallic tang of *wolfsbane salve*. Poisoned blades. She came around the shoulder of the ridge at first light with her heart already quickened and looked down into the bowl of a ravine, and there they were.

Three of them, in the grey-and-iron of the Hunt, with a fourth holding horses. They had backed something against the ravine wall. Something small, hoodless, dark-haired, with mud to the knees and a look on its face that Ines recognized from the other side of the queen's table: the look of a person calculating how much defiance they can afford, and finding the sum short.

"Last chance," the lead hunter said. He was young, and bored, and bored men were the most dangerous kind. "Show us your hands and say the words. *No gift given, no gift kept.* Say it clean and maybe you walk to the registry instead of the pyre."

"I don't—" The voice cracked, then steadied itself with visible effort. "I'm a herbwoman. I dry nettles. That's my whole crime."

"Then say the words."

Ines did not decide to move. That was the thing she would turn over later, lying awake under alien stars — that there was no decision, no weighing of odds, none of the careful tasting-and-measuring that had governed six years of her life. Her body simply went down the scree slope the way water goes down a drain, fast and quiet and committed.

She came out of the rocks behind the horse-holder. She had a carver's knife, four inches, good steel, meant for cheese and apples. She put it against the man's kidney before he finished turning and said, in the flat court voice she had used a thousand times to announce dishes, "Drop the reins. Walk away. Tell your captain the trail went cold at the ridge."

He looked at her — at the muddy, starving, obviously fugitive woman holding a paring knife — and something in her voice made the arithmetic work. He dropped the reins and walked.

Which left three, and one of them already spinning toward the noise, wolfsbane blade low, and Ines discovered that six years of poisoning had taught her more than flavors. It had taught her dosages, delivery, and the exact places on a human body where a small amount of force produces a large amount of compliance. She broke the first man's wrist with the knife handle and put her thumb into the pressure point beneath his ear, and he went down making a sound like a kicked dog. The second got an arm around her throat, and the world narrowed to heat and strain — and then the cornered herbwoman hit him with a rock.

It was, Ines reflected afterward, not a subtle rescue. But subtlety had died somewhere on the scree slope, and the third hunter, seeing his colleagues down and his horses gone, chose discretion. His footsteps faded up the ravine at a pace just short of cowardice.

Silence. Sleet ticking on stone. Ines stood in the middle of it, breathing hard, knife still in her fist, looking at a stranger covered in mud and blood — mostly the second hunter's — who was looking back at her with an expression of profound recalibration.

"Well," the stranger said. She was perhaps twenty-five, sharp-faced, wind-chapped, with eyes the brown of dried yarrow. "Either you're the best herb-dryer I've ever met, or we're both in considerably more trouble than we were a minute ago."

"You're welcome," Ines said.

"For what? You nearly got us both—"

"For the horses," Ines said. "You're welcome for the horses. They'll buy you a week of safety in any market town south of here, if you ride fast and don't ask questions." She was already turning away, already calculating the miles to the Cold Ash ford, the days lost, the solstice counting itself down somewhere beyond the peaks. Every instinct she owned told her to leave this stranger behind. Strangers were weight. Weight was death in the high passes. She had learned that before she learned her letters.

The stranger didn't take the horses. Ines heard her boots crunching closer instead, and turned to find her watching Ines with that recalibrating gaze gone narrow and strange.

"They said the Hunt's best tracker is on this route," the stranger said slowly. "Captain Vane himself. And they said he's hunting a runaway with a taster's training, because she moved through the palace kitchens like smoke and nobody marked her leaving." A pause. Sleet ticked between them. "You fight like someone who knows where bodies keep their secrets. And you smell of nightshade. Not on you — *in* you. It's in your sweat. I know that smell. My whole village knows that smell."

The mountain seemed to lean in.

"Who are you?" the stranger asked, and her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice and testing it. "Because if you're who I think you are, then the stories are true, and I've been walking toward you since before either of us knew it. My name is Tam. And I need to tell you what's happening to the borderstone."
