## 4. What the Witch Knew

The cave Tam chose for the night smelled of old iron and older rain, a hollow scooped into the mountainside where the smugglers' path bent east toward Greywater. Tam built the fire small — hedge-witch small, no taller than a cupped hand, burning pine cones and something bitter that made the smoke curl blue and cling to the rock like a living thing. Ines sat across from it, still as a well, watching.

She had not slept since Thornfell Roads. Tam had watched her not sleep: the careful way she ate only what she'd prepared herself from their shared stores, the flick of her tongue against her teeth before swallowing anything at all, the half-second pause before every bite that was so practiced it looked like nothing. But Tam had spent twenty years reading people in market squares and sickrooms, and nothing about Ines Ravensworth looked like nothing.

"You taste everything," Tam said, into the fire's blue hiss.

Ines didn't startle. That was answer enough, but she gave words too. "Occupational habit."

"Six years at the queen's table." Tam turned a pine cone with two fingers, letting it char. "Word travels even to hedges. The taster who caught nightshade in the harvest stew. They say your mouth is worth more than the treasury's gold."

"They say a lot of things."

"Do they say why you ran?" Tam looked up then, and let the witch-light rise in her eyes — just a breath of it, green as foxfire, enough to make the shadows lean in. "Do they say what you are?"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines like a long exhale, and beneath it, faint and wrong, came the other sound Tam had been hearing for three days now — a low harmonic hum from the high pass, felt more than heard, the way you feel a tooth dying. The borderstone singing itself apart.

"I know what I am," Ines said quietly. "I've known since I was nine years old and my mother locked me in the pantry with a dish of poisoned plums and told me not to come out until I'd eaten them all." Her voice didn't shake. Tam thought that was the most frightening thing about her. "I'm immune. Not trained. *Immune.* It isn't discipline. It isn't cleverness. Nightshade should have killed me when I was nine, and arsenic when I was eleven, and the queen's own taster's blend last spring. Nothing has ever touched me. My blood does something it shouldn't."

There it was — freely given, unprompted, more than Tam had asked for. Tam sat back on her heels, unsettled. She had expected denial, deflection, the polished armor of a court servant. Instead she'd gotten truth like an open wound.

Which meant either Ines Ravensworth was honest past the point of survival, or she was very good indeed, and Tam had survived this long by assuming everyone was very good.

"Hedge-witch's honesty," Tam said slowly, "deserves hedge-witch's honesty back. But mine comes with a price, so I'll ask first." She reached into her coat and drew out a small clay bead, grey-white, fired with a spiral pressed into its soft skin. "This is a truth-knot. Old craft, older than the crown, older than the hunt. When I crush it, we both breathe the smoke, and neither of us can say a word that isn't true until it burns out. It doesn't make you loyal. It doesn't make you kind. It only makes you *honest*, for as long as the smoke lasts — maybe a hundred heartbeats. Long enough to know if I'm walking these mountains with an ally or a leash held by Captain Vane."

Ines regarded the bead the way she might regard an unfamiliar dish set before her by a nervous cook. Not fear. Appraisal. "And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow I go my way and you go yours, and we both likely die alone, which is the traditional arrangement."

A long silence. Wind again. That hum beneath it, and Tam saw Ines tilt her head at the sound, the way a dog tilts at a whistle humans can't hear — and something cold walked down Tam's spine. She heard it too. Only wardens' blood heard it. Only wardens' blood ever had.

"All right," Ines said.

Tam crushed the bead.

The smoke rose violet-grey, sweet-smelling, like crushed fennel and rain on hot stone, and it coiled between them without dispersing, hanging in the cave air like a curtain drawn across a doorway. Tam breathed it. Felt it settle behind her sternum, a warm weight, a hand laid flat over her heart saying: *only truth now.*

"Ines Ravensworth," Tam began. "Why did you flee the capital?"

"Because the queen declared war on what I am, and I would rather die free in the mountains than kneel in a register line and be executed politely." The words left Ines flat and clear, no performance in them at all. "Because I burned my mother's letters and kept one page, and the seal on that page matches the sigil on the old border treaties, and I need to know why a royal taster's household would hold a covenant seal at all."

Tam's breath went shallow. "You have the seal?"

"I have the page."

*Only truth,* the smoke reminded her, and Tam asked the question that mattered. "If Vane offered you amnesty — full pardon, restoration of rank, the queen's favor — if all he asked was that you lead him to whoever else is running — would you take it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Ines was quiet for three heartbeats. Four. The smoke waited, patient as stone.

"Because six years ago," she said, "a kitchen boy named Wren stole bread from the palace stores and the master steward wanted him whipped at the gate. I spoke for him because he'd once slipped me extra cheese when I was new and hungry. They whipped him anyway. And I learned that the crown's gratitude is a coin minted in one metal only — obedience — and it spends at exactly one face value, and I have been spending myself at a discount ever since." Her eyes lifted to Tam's, steady. "I am done being spent. Whatever I do next, I do because I choose it. If I lead anyone anywhere, it will be because I decided the destination was worth the road."

The smoke thinned, curled, dissolved against the cave ceiling, and the warmth behind Tam's sternum lifted like a hand withdrawn. A hundred heartbeats, give or take. She counted them all.

"Well," Tam said at last, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "That was more honesty than I bargained for."

"The truth-knot works both ways," Ines said. "Your turn."

And here was the trap Tam had built for herself, because she had meant to reveal only the shape of things — the failing stone, the solstice deadline, the broad strokes — and keep back the part that mattered, the part about bloodlines and wardens and names. But the smoke was gone; the knot was spent; she could lie if she wished. She found she did not wish. There was something about a person who handed you her worst truth unasked that made hoarding your own feel like theft.

So Tam fed the fire another cone and told it all.

"There's a stone in the high pass above Greywater — the borderstone, the one the old covenant raised when the realms were parted. Human side, fae side, one seam stitched shut with oath and law. For six hundred years it held. It isn't holding anymore." She nodded upward, toward the hum. "You hear that? Most folk don't. Wardens hear it. The stone is coming unwound, thread by thread, and when it fails entirely there won't be a border — there'll be a wound, and things will move through it that make the Sigil Hunt look like a children's game."

"The queen's proclamations say the gifted opened it. That fae agents breached—"

"The queen's proclamations say the gifted committed treason. The queen's proclamations are ash." Tam leaned forward, firelight carving her face into ridges and hollows. "I've read the old records, girl. Hidden ones, hedge-copied, passed hand to hand for generations. The covenant didn't bind the realms with magic alone. It bound them with *wardens* — mortal keepers whose blood could hear the stone and speak to it. And do you know whose bloodline the covenant chose as its first wardens?" She let the silence stretch. "Tasters. Poison-eaters. People whose bodies could take in what would kill anyone else and survive it. The same gift. The same rare, cursed, blessed constitution. The realm took its strongest immune bloodlines and set them at both tables — the queen's table and the border table — and swore to protect them forever."

Ines had gone very still. Stillness, Tam was learning, was what she did instead of screaming.

"My mother," Ines said. "The letters. The seal."

"Your family wasn't exiled for treason, whatever story they told. Your family was *relieved*. Retired from service, scattered, stripped of records — because somewhere along the centuries someone decided wardens were inconvenient, and a warden who knows what she is is dangerous to the throne that forgot its oaths." Tam spread her hands. "I don't know everything. Hedge-witches never do; we know enough to plant, enough to cure, enough to run. But I know the stone is failing, I know the solstice eclipse is when the last threads give way, and I know the stone can be re-bound — but only by warden's blood, spoken aloud, at the moment the shadow crosses it."

"And you think my blood—"

"I don't think. I *hear*. You tilted your head at the stone's song three times tonight. You were doing it on the road yesterday too, when you stopped mid-stride and looked northeast like a deer catching wind." Tam's voice gentled despite herself. "Ines. You aren't running from the hunt. Some part of you has been running *toward* that stone since the day you fled, and it's high time the rest of you caught up."

For a long time Ines said nothing. The fire burned down to embers; Tam added nothing to it. Outside, the hum deepened by a fraction, a lute string tightening toward breaking, and both of them heard it, and both of them knew the other heard it, and that shared hearing was its own kind of knot — stronger than any spell in Tam's coat.

"If I go to that stone," Ines finally said, "and it asks something of me — really asks, not metaphorically — what happens if I refuse?"

"Then the border falls, and the realms bleed together, and the queen's hunt becomes the smallest of anyone's problems."

"And if I agree?"

Tam looked at her a long moment — this thin, watchful woman with poison-proof blood and burned letters and a dead kitchen boy named Wren folded somewhere inside her like a coal. "Then you become something the realm hasn't had in six hundred years," she said. "And no one — not the queen, not Vane, not me — knows what that costs. That's the honest answer. The stone takes an oath. What an oath takes in return, only the stone knows."

Wind rattled through the pass. The embers breathed red.

"So," Tam said, and pulled her coat tighter, and hated how young her voice suddenly sounded, how much like a girl asking a stranger to walk her home. "Greywater ford, then the high trail, then the stone. Nine days to solstice, if the weather holds, and it won't. I'm asking you to walk into the mountains with a hedge-witch you met yesterday toward a thing that may eat you. I'm asking because I can't re-bind it alone, and because every other warden-blooded soul I've found is dead or registered or worse."

"And what do you get?" Ines asked. "Out of this pact. Out of me."

"Someone at my back who can't be poisoned," Tam said, with a crooked ghost of a smile. "In my trade, that's practically immortal company."

Ines almost smiled. It transformed her whole face for a heartbeat — made her look nineteen instead of ancient with survival — and then it was gone, banked like the fire.

"Pact, then," Ines said, and put out her hand across the embers.

Tam took it. The handshake was brief, dry, firm — and through the skin contact, faint as a whisper under the stone-song, Tam felt something pass between them, some recognition of blood answering craft, warden answering witness. She let go quickly and told herself the shiver up her arm was the cold.

They settled for the night, backs to opposite walls, the tiny fire between them. Tam drifted toward sleep listening to the stone sing its fraying song, and to Ines breathing slow and deliberate on the far side of the cave, awake, always awake.

It was near dawn when Ines spoke into the dark, so quietly Tam might have dreamed it.

"TAM. Wake up."

Tam's eyes snapped open. Ines was crouched at the cave mouth, silhouetted against a sky gone the color of dirty wool, one finger raised toward the valley below — where, far down through the thinning trees, torches moved. Not two torches. Not five. A dozen at least, spread in a wide arc, climbing.

"That's a hunting line," Tam whispered, throat dry. "That's a *professional* hunting line."

"Yes," Ines said, and her voice in the pre-dawn dark was calm as deep water. "And I know that formation. I helped design it."
