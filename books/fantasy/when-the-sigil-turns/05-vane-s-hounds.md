## 5. Vane's Hounds

The foothills of Thornfell did not forgive a man his certainty. Aldric Vane had learned that twenty years ago, as a raw lieutenant chasing salt smugglers through these same grey slopes, and he learned it again now, in the third week of the hunt, when his hounds lost the scent at a stream crossing and sat down in the shallows to look at him with wet, apologetic eyes.

He could not blame them. The girl — and she was a girl still, whatever crowns had made of her — had been raised on these mountains the way other children were raised on psalms. Every streambed was a verse she knew by heart. She had crossed the Greywater at night, in the cold, wading upstream for half a mile before stepping out, and Vane had stood on the bank in the grey hour before dawn and felt something that was not quite anger move through him. It was closer to pride, and pride made him ashamed.

"Pick up the trail," he told his sergeant. "She'll go high. Food caravans use the low roads; she won't."

"You think she knows them," Sergeant Breck said, wringing water from his gloves. "The passes."

"I think she was born to them." Vane swung back into the saddle. His knee protested. Everything protested, these days. "Ravensworths were running brandy and tallow through Thornfell before the queen's grandmother wore a crown. Ines didn't learn those routes from maps." He paused, hearing himself. "She learned them at her mother's knee."

Breck said nothing. Good man, Breck. Knew which observations were for saying aloud and which were for carrying quietly, like stones in a boot.

They rode north by northeast, following a trail only Vane seemed able to read: a broken fern stem angled against the grain of the slope, a heel print pressed deeper at the toe than the heel — a runner, moving fast, uphill, alone or nearly so — and once, caught on the bark of an alder, a single thread of undyed wool that smelled faintly, when he crushed it between his fingers, of wormwood and tallow smoke. Hedge-craft. So the witch still lived, and went with her.

He should have felt the hunter's clean satisfaction. Instead he felt like a man reading a letter addressed to someone else.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of an old shepherd's bothy, walls of dry-stone furred with lichen, roof long since fallen into a bed of bracken. The hounds circled twice and lay down with their chins on their paws, and the men lit a small fire because Vane allowed it, and Vane sat apart from it because he did not want its warmth to make him soft in his thinking.

His thinking was the problem. It had always been the problem. A good huntsman, his first captain had told him, thinks like the beast he hunts, and Vane had thought like Ines Ravensworth for six years without ever meaning to. He had taught her the palace kitchens, the rotation of the tasters, the etiquette of the spit and the ladle. He had signed the writ that put her name in the register of royal servants. He had stood behind her chair at her first tasting, nineteen years old and pale as whey, and watched her swallow a measure of arsenic that should have stopped her heart inside an hour, and watched her walk out of the chamber on steady legs while the whole court murmured *miracle*, and he had known — known the way one knows a fire is burning, by the heat on the face — that no miracle explained it.

And he had said nothing. For six years he had said nothing, because Ravensworths had served the crown since before there was a crown worth serving, because her mother had pulled him bleeding out of the Saltmarsh rebellion when he was twenty-two, because loyalty was a debt you paid forward even when the ledger grew strange.

Now the crown itself had grown strange, and the ledger would not balance.

He took out the courier's satchel he'd confiscated two days past, from a dead messenger at the bottom of the Ravenfall gorge. Officially, the man had slipped. Officially, Vane had recovered the body, noted the seal, and sent the satchel onward to the Chancellery with a full accounting. Unofficially, he had done none of those things, and the satchel had ridden against his ribs ever since like a coal wrapped in wool.

By firelight, he opened it again.

Inside: correspondence between the Chancellery of Trade and a Lord Ferren Halley, Master of the Woolwrights' Guild. Ordinary paper. Unremarkable seals. Vane had read merchant letters before; they were usually duller than prayer. These were not dull.

*The fae crossings remain closed to licensed traffic,* wrote Halley, *which is precisely their value. Once the gifted question is settled — and the Hunt will settle it — the Crown will hold sole authority over every border gate in the north. Silk, iron-salt, starwine, dreamglass: all of it through crown warehouses, at crown tariffs. Her Majesty need never sign another charter she dislikes.*

And beneath it, in the Chancellor's own cramped hand, a memorandum marked *For the Queen's Eye Only*:

*The treason must appear discovered, not invented. Plant correspondence linking the border-guilds to fae agents — the forger in Coldharbour is reliable. Let the Hunt begin with the hedge-folk, who cannot defend themselves, and work upward until the guild masters beg us for registration on any terms. The girl who fled is useful; a fugitive taster gives the thing a face. Do not take her alive unless convenient.*

Vane read it three times. The fire cracked. One of the hounds dreamed, paws twitching.

*Do not take her alive unless convenient.*

He had told himself, riding north, that this was a rescue he could conduct gently — that he would bring Ines in, speak for her, invoke her family's service, stand between her and the worst outcomes as he had stood between her and the arsenic chamber. He had told himself the Hunt was regrettable but necessary, that the gifted had grown bold, that order was a wall and every wall needs mortar even if some of the mortar burns.

He had told himself a great many things, and here was the memorandum, and here was the truth sitting in his lap like a cat that had always belonged to another house: the Hunt was not about safety. It had never been about safety. It was a key cut to open the fae trade routes, and every hedge-witch dragged from her garden, every cunning-man strung up from his own doorbeam, was a tooth ground into that key's edge by men who had never tasted poison for anyone in their lives.

Ines had run because she was guilty of nothing but being what she was. And the crown — *his* crown, the crown whose bread he had eaten since he was fourteen — had sent him to fetch her back so that her face could hang on the story like a lantern on a hook, illuminating exactly what the Chancellery wished illuminated.

Vane folded the papers along their original creases. His hands were steady. That surprised him. He had expected them to shake.

He fed the pages to the fire one at a time and watched the ink curl and go gold and then to nothing. The memorandum burned last, and longest, the way important things do. When it was ash he stirred the embers with a stick until no legible fragment remained, and then he sat looking at the ruined shepherd's hut around him, at the soot-blackened hearth where some forgotten family had cooked their suppers, and understood that he had just committed treason. Not the manufactured kind. The real kind, the quiet kind, the kind that lives in a man's chest where no one can hang it.

It did not feel the way he had expected. It felt like setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his spine.

"Breck," he said.

The sergeant came over, wiping his hands. "Captain?"

"Change of orders. We're not taking her alive unless she forces it." He heard the sentence come out of his mouth and recognized it, with a bitter taste, as half of the Chancellery's line. He amended it. "We're not taking her at all if we can help it. If we find her, we find out where she's going, and then we lose the trail. Thoroughly. Mountains are treacherous. Weather turns. Hounds tire. These things happen."

Breck looked at him for a long moment. Firelight moved in the sergeant's eyes, and something behind them shifted, settled, accepted. "These things do happen, Captain," Breck said. "My sister's boy has the gift of finding lost livestock. Six years old. Sweet lad." He straightened. "Weather's coming in from the west. I can feel it in my knee."

"Then let us pray it delays us," Vane said. "Sincerely."

* * *

But the mountains kept their own counsel, and the next morning brought Vane something worse than weather.

One of the hounds — Sorrel, the young bitch with the best nose — cast wide from the camp at first light and came back frenzied, quivering, nose full of a scent she could not leave. Vane followed her east through a fold in the hills, Breck and two others trailing, until they reached a shelf of rock overlooking a hidden combe, and below them, sheltering in the lee of a fallen rowan, he saw them.

Two figures by a small, smokeless fire. The witch, sharp-faced, hands moving over a pot in patterns Vane recognized from his grandmother's kitchen and had never seen anywhere lawful since. And beside her, wrapped in a smuggler's oilskin, hair loose and dark against the morning fog —

Ines.

She looked up, suddenly, as if his gaze had weight. Across the whole width of the combe, across the mist and the distance, her eyes found the exact shelf of rock where he stood, and held it.

Vane did not move. Neither did she. The wind moved between them, carrying woodsmoke and the green smell of bruised rowan leaves, and somewhere beneath his breastbone the old loyalty and the new treason turned together like two hands of a millstone, grinding.

She knew him. She had always known him. And now she knew he had found her — and had not yet given the order to close in.

Below, the witch touched Ines's arm. Ines shook her head once, slowly, never taking her eyes from the ridge. Then she lifted her hand — not in surrender, not in challenge — and turned her palm outward, open, the gesture her mother used to make across a market square: *I see you. I mean you no harm. What will you do?*

Vane stood very still on the ridge, with his hounds straining at his boots and his men watching his face, and understood that the next thing he did would follow him to whatever grave the mountains eventually gave him.

He raised his hand — and pointed deliberately, unmistakably, away to the west.
