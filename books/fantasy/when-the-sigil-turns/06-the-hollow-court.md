## 6. The Hollow Court

The waystation announced itself by its silence.

For three days Ines and Tam had climbed through Thornfell's grey shoulders, where the pines grew crooked with wind and the only sounds were their own breathing and the small conspiracies of snowmelt trickling beneath the crust. Then, as the light went copper behind the western ridges, the noise of the mountain simply stopped — no wind, no water, not even the tick of ice settling — as though the world had drawn a breath and was waiting to see what they would do with it.

"There," Tam said, pointing with her chin. Her voice came out flattened, careful, the way it always did near old fae ground. "You feel that?"

Ines felt it the way she felt poison: a shape on the tongue of her attention, sweet at first, then bitter underneath. She had been tasting wrongness for six years, and this was wrongness of a very particular vintage — old magic, left to breathe in a dark cellar, gone slightly to vinegar.

The waystation stood in a hollow between two granite teeth, half-swallowed by a hill that had spent centuries trying to eat it. It had been built by hands that did not think the way human hands think: walls that curved where walls should corner, windows set too high, a door of pale wood carved with interlocking rings that had once been silver and were now only memory. Moss hung from the eaves in long green banners. Above the door, worn nearly smooth, a sigil glowed faintly when the light touched it — the same interlocking rings, the same design Ines had kept hidden for six years inside the lining of her sleeve, pressed into wax on the one surviving page of her mother's letters.

The covenant seal.

"We shouldn't—" Tam began.

"I know," Ines said, and pushed the door open anyway.

* * *

Inside, the air tasted of dust and honey.

That was the strangest thing, Ines thought, standing just past the threshold while Tam muttered wards under her breath. Every abandoned place she'd ever entered smelled of rot or mouse or cold ash. This place smelled of honey — faint, faded, like a jar opened and forgotten in a pantry for a hundred years. The fae had kept food here once. Travelers' provisions, freely offered, so that no one crossing between realms would go hungry. That had been part of the covenant too; Tam had told her that much on the road, in the flat, reluctant voice she used for truths she wished weren't true.

The great room rose around them, vaulted in pale timber, its rafters carved with vines that might have been moving if you didn't look directly at them. A hearth dominated the far wall, wide enough to roast an ox, and before it stood a table.

Not just any table. A long table of white stone, big enough to seat forty, its surface unmarred after all these years, laid — impossibly — with dishes. Clay bowls glazed in colors that had no names. Cups of horn and of glass. A single candlestub, burned down to nothing, still sitting in its holder as though someone had meant to come back and light it.

"They left mid-meal," Ines said quietly.

"Or they were made to leave." Tam set down her pack and did not touch anything. Her eyes moved over the carvings, the sigils, the door, cataloguing threats. "Waystations were neutral ground. Both realms could stop here, share bread, pass through. When the covenant broke—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together.

"When my grandmother broke it," Ines finished.

Tam said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement.

They made camp without fire, in the corner farthest from the hearth, because Tam insisted the hearth was *listening* — old fae chimneys carried voices, she claimed, to places Ines preferred not to imagine. They ate hard cheese and black bread and dried apples, and Ines found herself setting out the food on the edge of the white stone table rather than eating off her knees. She couldn't have explained why. It felt rude, somehow, to eat in this room and not offer the table its due.

The moon rose. Through the high, strange windows its light fell in long silver bars across the floor, and the carved vines on the rafters cast shadows that curled, very slowly, like things waking.

Ines slept badly, and then she did not sleep at all.

* * *

It began with the taste of nightshade.

She surfaced from thin sleep with her tongue coated in it — sweet, then bitter, the exact signature of the queen's stew six years ago, the first poison she'd ever caught, the catch that had made her name. But there was no stew here. There was only the moonlight, and the table, and the candlestub, which was burning now.

She hadn't lit it. Tam hadn't lit it. Its flame stood perfectly still, a small gold teardrop that did not flicker even when Ines's breath stirred the air.

"Ines Ravensworth."

The voice came from everywhere — the hearth, the rafters, the space just behind her ears. It was an old woman's voice, dry and precise, with the court accent that Ines had spent her childhood scrubbing out of her own speech.

Ines did not reach for the knife at her belt. Some instinct older than fear told her it would be useless, and worse than useless — an insult.

"You know who I am," the voice said.

"Elira Ravensworth." Her grandmother's name felt like a stone in her mouth. "Last royal taster."

"And you are sitting in my house, child. Or what is left of it." A pause, and the candleflame bent though there was no draft. "I kept this station for thirty years. I fed travelers from both realms at that table. Do you know what that meant, in those days? A human woman trusted with a fae key. Trusted to hold the border's courtesy in my two hands."

"Why are you showing me this?" Ines whispered.

"Because you asked nothing, and that is the only prayer this house still answers." The flame stretched taller, and the room changed.

* * *

The vision took her the way cold water takes a swimmer — suddenly, and all at once, pulling her under.

She stood in the same room, but it lived. Firelight filled the hearth. The table groaned under real food — roasts and honeycakes, fae fruit that gleamed like lanterns, wine the color of garnets. Figures sat along both sides of the table: humans in wool and fur, and beside them the fae, tall and lovely and wrong in ways the eye refused to hold — skin like birchbark, eyes like foxfire, smiles a half-beat too slow. They passed dishes hand to hand. They laughed. At the head of the table sat Elira Ravensworth, and Ines's breath caught, because she knew that face. She saw it every morning in her own reflection, aged forty years and wearing a crown of woven rowan.

Her grandmother lifted a cup. "To the covenant," Elira said. "May the stone hold and the table never empty."

And the whole hall answered, human and fae together, and the sound of it went through Ines like a blade going through silk — because it was joy, real joy, and she had never once heard joy like that in the queen's court.

Then the vision tore.

* * *

Winter. The same hall, but the fire was banked and the table held only three cups and a single candle, and Elira sat alone with a letter in her hands, and Ines — hovering, bodiless, unable to look away — recognized the seal on the letter before she understood anything else.

The royal seal of Maren's grandmother. The crown of Vellmark.

"The queen summons you to the winter court," said a voice from the doorway — a man in captain's black, young, hard-eyed. Ines's stomach turned over. She knew that face too. Vane's grandfather, or some ancestor cut from the same cloth, down to the set of the jaw.

"I serve the covenant," Elira said. "The crown has no claim on me. That was the whole of the bargain."

"The crown has your daughter." The captain's voice was flat as a ledger line. "And your granddaughter. Two girls, Mistress Ravensworth, currently enjoying His Majesty's hospitality at Greywater Keep. Girls are fragile. Winters are long."

Ines felt her grandmother's fear as if it were her own — a cold bloom under the breastbone, the sour flood at the back of the tongue. Coercion had a flavor. She had never known that before tonight, but she knew it now: fear tasted like copper and rotten apples.

"What do they want?" Elira asked, and her voice had already begun to break.

"A list. Names of the gifted who cross at your pass. And a vial — one vial — of the borderstone's binding-sap, diluted. Nothing more. The crown wishes merely to... study the old protections." The captain smiled without warmth. "Refuse, and the girls winter at Greywater another year. And the year after. You understand how it is. Accidents happen in keeps."

The silence lasted a hundred heartbeats. Ines counted them, helpless, screaming silently at a woman sixty years dead: *Don't. Don't do it. We would have survived. I would rather have died—*

But she wouldn't have. That was the poison in it, the true poison, the kind no taster's tongue could catch. Elira loved them. Love was the nightshade, sweet first, bitter after.

"I will need time," her grandmother said at last, in a voice like a snapped thread.

"Of course," said the captain. "The crown is patient."

* * *

The visions came faster now, months collapsing into moments.

Elira copying names onto parchment by candlelight, her handwriting growing shakier with each line, weeping without sound. Elira scraping sap from the borderstone with a fae sickle, flinching at the stone's cry — Ines heard that cry too, a sound like a struck bell dying, and it hurt somewhere behind her eyes. Elira handing the vial to a royal courier with her eyes fixed on the floor, unable to watch it leave her hands.

Then a final scene, and the cruelest: Elira returning to the waystation, finding the fae waiting for her — the birchbark-skinned woman from the feast, the one who had laughed loudest, standing in the empty hall among the abandoned dishes.

"You gave them our names," the fae said. Not shouting. Worse than shouting. Quiet as snowfall.

"The crown has my daughters—"

"The stone weakens. Hunters crossed last week and killed a hedge-witch's son in the low meadows. The table is empty, Elira. You emptied it." The fae woman's foxfire eyes dimmed. "We do not curse you. That would be mercy — curses end. We simply strike your family from the covenant. Your blood will carry the debt. Your blood will owe the reckoning." She turned toward the door, then paused. "You should know: your granddaughters will inherit your gift. And the crown will hunt what you helped teach it to fear."

Then the hall was empty, and the candle went out, and Ines was on her knees on cold flagstones with tears freezing on her face, and dawn was a grey rumor in the high windows.

* * *

Tam was shaking her shoulder. "Ines. Ines! You've been under since moonset. What did you see?"

Everything. All of it. The feast and the letter and the vial and the quiet, terrible sentence that had followed her family out of these mountains like a debt-collector: *your blood will owe the reckoning.*

"She didn't betray them for power," Ines heard herself say. Her voice sounded scraped raw. "Everyone says — the histories say the last royal taster sold the covenant. Sold it for gold, for favor. But the crown took her children, Tam. Took them and *held* them until she broke. My mother grew up in Greywater Keep as a hostage and never told me. My grandmother broke the covenant because they made love itself the poison, and she swallowed it, because that's what mothers do."

Tam crouched beside her, and for once the hedge-witch had no test, no trick, no guarded truth to barter. She just looked at Ines — at the tear-tracks, the shaking hands — and handed her the waterskin.

"And now," Ines said, when she could speak again, "Maren stands up at the winter court and declares the gifted traitors. Hunts them. Burns them out of their homes." She laughed, and it came out broken. "It's the same wound, opened again. Same crown, same cruelty, sixty years later. And my family has been paying interest on it ever since."

"That's why you ran north without knowing why," Tam said slowly. "That's why you pulled me out of that netting without thinking. The debt. The stone called its debtor home."

Ines looked at the long white table, at the dishes no one had cleared, the candlestub that had burned for sixty years waiting for someone to finish what was interrupted. Shame sat in her chest like a swallowed coal — but underneath it, for the first time, something else was warming. Not forgiveness. Not yet. But understanding, sharp and clear as a detected poison: *the lie was never hers alone.*

Outside, a raven cried — once, twice — a sound too deliberate for any bird.

Tam stiffened. "That's a signal call. Someone's marking the hollow." She was already moving, snuffing their traces, packing with quick fierce hands. "Hunters don't fly ravens. That's military."

Ines rose, and as she did, her eyes caught the hearthstone — where, revealed now by the risen sun through the high window, a fresh mark had been chalked on the ancient fae carving sometime in the night.

A black sigil. The Sigil Hunt's mark.

They had found the waystation — and whoever had marked it was close enough that the chalk was still bright.
