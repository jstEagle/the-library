## 7. Crossing at Greywater

The river spoke first.

Ines heard it long before she saw it — a low, grinding murmur beneath the morning mist, the sound of Greywater dragging its stone bed toward the lowlands. Six days in the Thornfells had taught her to read the mountain's noises: the tick of frost letting go in the pines, the far-off cough of a rockslide, the whistle Tam made when she wanted to talk about something Ines didn't want to hear. But water was new. Water meant they had arrived.

She crouched at the tree line, breath fogging, and studied the ford below. The smugglers' map called it a crossing; the map lied by omission. Greywater ran wide here, shallow enough to wade but loud enough to drown a scream, and its far bank climbed into a wall of gorse and shadowed spruce where anything could wait. The mist lay on the water like milk poured over slate.

"It's cold enough to stop a heart," Tam murmured beside her. The hedge-witch's voice was rough with sleep; they'd taken turns through the night, neither truly resting. "We could go upstream. Find a bridge."

"Bridges have eyes." Ines traced the map's ink with her thumb, the faded line of the old road, the little sigil her mother had drawn to mark safe fords. "This is where the caravans crossed. Where the queen's soldiers don't bother looking."

"Someone bothered," Tam said quietly.

Ines had felt it too — that prickle between her shoulder blades, the same one that had told her the queen's stew was wrong before her tongue confirmed it. Detection was not only for poison. Somewhere behind them, or ahead of them, something was out of true.

"We cross fast and apart," she said. "Ten paces between us. If I go down, you keep going northeast. The pass is two days from here."

"And if I go down?"

Ines looked at her. Tam was small and sharp-faced, her hair the color of dry thistle, her hands always half-curled as if holding some small working ready to loose. Six days ago Ines had pulled her out of a hunter's snare without thinking, and had been thinking about it ever since. *Why her? Why did my feet move before my mind did?* She still had no answer except the one she refused to say aloud: because leaving anyone behind would be like swallowing poison slowly, on purpose.

"If you go down," Ines said, "I come back."

Tam's mouth twisted — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "That's a stupid oath, taster."

"It's the only kind I know how to make."

They went down to the water.

* * *

The cold took Tam's breath the moment her boots broke the surface film. Greywater wasn't water so much as melted winter, running fast around her calves, then her knees, hauling at her skirts like patient hands. She kept her satchel high against her chest, kept counting — *ten paces, nine, eight* — measuring the gap between herself and the taller woman ahead of her, who moved through the current like someone born knowing how to cross things. Ravensworth walked like the world owed her passage.

Tam hated owing anything to anyone, and she owed this woman her life twice over now. Debts were their own kind of magic, her grandmother used to say; they bind tighter than any circle you draw. So Tam watched Ines's back and chewed on the truth-spell she'd cast a week ago in a hollow of pine roots — *show me what you are* — and how Ines had answered with more honesty than Tam had ever gotten from anyone, including herself. *I am the last of the tasters' line. My blood was sworn to a stone I've never seen.* Who said such things under truth-working unless they were true?

Halfway across, Tam's toe found a slick stone and her knee buckled, cold punching up through her thigh, and it was that stumble — that single heartbeat of imbalance — that let her hear it properly. Not the river. Under the river. A rhythm that didn't belong.

"Hooves," she breathed, too late, too quiet, swallowed by the water.

The mist on the far bank tore open.

They came out of the gorse in a wedge — four riders in ash-grey livery, the Sigil Hunt's brand stitched white on black at their shoulders, and at their head a man on a dun mare whose face Tam knew from whispered warnings all down the foothills. Sergeant Hollar. He carried a net weighted with iron beads across his saddlebow, and he was smiling the way men smile when the work is easy and the pay is certain.

"Gifted filth," he called across the water, conversational, almost kind. "You'll stand where you are."

Tam's hands were already moving, dipping into the wet, gathering the river's cold into a shape. Hedge-craft was small magic — knots and fevers, finding lost goats, souring milk — but water was generous to those who asked politely. She pulled the current against itself beneath the nearest rider and felt his horse scream and rear.

"Ines!" she shouted. "Go! Go *northeast!*"

What happened next happened in pieces.

Ines turned — of course she turned, stupid, loyal, maddening woman — and their eyes met across thirty feet of rushing grey water. And Tam saw the exact moment Ines chose, saw it happen behind her eyes like a door being kicked open: the map pressed against her chest inside its oilcloth, the borderstone two days north, the solstice three days out, and here was Tam knee-deep and sinking, and there was the pass. Both. She was going to try for both. Tam could see the arithmetic finish and hated her for it and loved her for it in the same cold instant.

The net came down.

Iron beads caught Tam across the shoulder, drove her sideways into the current. The river filled her mouth and ears. She got one hand above water, dragged a hasty word through it — salt and spit and spite, the oldest ingredients — and felt the rope fibres weaken, fray, give an inch. Then a boot found her ribs and the inch closed again, and the sky became a spinning wheel of grey riders and white mist and Ines, still standing in midstream, still not running.

"*GO!*" Tam screamed, water and blood together.

And Ines went.

Tam laughed into the river as they hauled her onto the shingle, as they cinched the net cruel and tight, because it wasn't a betrayal. It was a promise kept badly, which was the only way promises ever got kept in this rotten realm. Sergeant Hollar crouched beside her, wiped river weed from her cheek with grotesque gentleness.

"Where's your friend going, witchling?"

"To the moon," Tam said, and spat in his eye, and took the back of his gauntlet across her face for it, and held, in the secret workshop of her mind, one small knot of magic unburned: the thread she'd tied to Ines's cloak hem at dawn, while the other woman slept. Hedge-craft. Small magic. *Find what is lost.*

They threw her across a saddle like a sack of meal. As the horses splashed into the ford, Tam looked back once at the northeastern ridgeline, empty, mist-clad, already swallowing the woman who would carry both their lives toward the stone.

"Don't you dare die before I get there," she whispered. "You still owe me a truth."

* * *

Ines ran.

She ran the way she hadn't run since the night she'd fled the palace — not away but *through*, branches whipping her cheeks, lungs full of knives, the map a hard rectangle against her sternum like a second heartbeat. Every instinct screamed to turn. Her body strained backward against her own direction, a hound wrenching at its lead. Behind her, faint, fading, the sounds of the ford: shouts, a horse's cry, silence.

She made herself count her strides. One hundred. Two hundred. At five hundred she stopped retching and started thinking, which was nearly worse.

*Rescue or the stone. Her or the realm. Choose.*

But she had spent six years tasting poison for liars, and if the throne had taught her anything, it was that choices presented as pairs are usually lies. The queen wants you obedient *or* dead. Vane had given her surrender *or* flight. Now the mountains offered Tam *or* the covenant, and Ines stood on the bank of that choice and refused the water entirely.

There was a third thing. There was always a third thing, if you were willing to bleed for it.

She climbed until the trees broke, and on the bare shoulder of the ridge she spread the smuggler's map on a flat stone, weighting its corners with rocks torn from frozen earth. Her fingers shook — cold, guilt, fury, she couldn't separate them anymore — but her mind was the court's finest instrument, trained on vintages and venom, precise as a surgeon's scale. Look. Measure. Do not swallow the first conclusion.

Greywater curved northeast. The hunters' road followed the valley — the fast route, the obvious route, the one Vane would watch if Vane was watching, and Vane was always watching. But the map showed something else: an old drove track, pre-Hunt, pre-queen, cutting switchbacks along the gorge where the river narrowed to a roar. It rejoined the valley road eleven miles downstream, near a place marked with her mother's tiny cipher — the sigil for *witness*. An overlook. A place where one person could see everything below.

Eleven miles of bad ground in failing light. She could beat the prisoners' pace — captives travel slow, especially valuable ones meant for questioning. She could reach the overlook by dusk tomorrow and look down on whatever camp held Tam, and she could count guards, and she could do what she had been made for since girlhood: find the fatal flaw in what everyone else assumed was safe to consume.

And then, with Tam free or dead, she would run the last two days to the stone, arriving raw and ragged at the edge of the solstice with nothing in reserve. Thin odds. Split odds. The arithmetic of a fool.

"Do you hear me?" she said aloud to the ridge, to the mist, to the witch tied to a horse somewhere south of her, bound by a thread Ines couldn't feel but somehow, impossibly, could. "I'm coming. Both times. Both ways."

The wind took the words and gave nothing back.

She folded the map, tucked it against her heart, and started down into the gorge, where the river roared loud enough to drown a scream — the same river, she realized, that would carry her sound downstream to wherever they were keeping Tam. So she walked the whole first mile in the roar of the water and sang, low and tuneless, the lullaby her mother had sung, the one with the covenant's cadence folded into its verses. Let it ride the current. Let it find the witch who collected small magics. Let it say what Ines could not shout from any distance:

*I chose. You are chosen. Hold on.*

Behind her, far to the south, on a road she could no longer see, Captain Aldric Vane stood at the trampled crossing with the hunt's report in his hand and doubt in his jaw, and turned his horse north — not toward the prisoners' route, but toward the pass.

Toward the stone.

Toward her.
