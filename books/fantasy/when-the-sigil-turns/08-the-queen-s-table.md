## 8. The Queen's Table

The palace kitchens smelled the way memory does — woodsmoke and rendered fat, onions sweetening in a copper pot, the sour bite of ale left too long in the barrel. Six years I had walked these stones at dawn, tasting the queen's food while the cooks sang their slow morning rounds, and my body knew this room the way a blind woman knows her own hands.

Now I came to it at midnight, through the ash-chute that fed the great hearth, and nothing about me was loyal anymore.

I had forgotten how warm a kitchen could be. Thornfell had taught me cold as a second language — cold in the bones, cold in the breath, cold in Tam's silence when we parted at the foothills and she went south toward Greywater and whatever cell they were keeping her in. The heat of the banked fires pressed against my face like an old dog greeting a thief, and for one shameful moment I wanted only to sit down among the sleeping scullery boys and let the warmth take me back six years, to a time when the worst thing I carried was other people's poison.

But Tam had kissed my brow at the crossroads and said, *the stone first, girl, then me — or neither,* and I had promised both. Promises made under a witch's blessing do not sit lightly. They itch.

The decree would be in the steward's office above the kitchens — I knew because I had seen the steward carry papers there all my service, and because Vane, in another life, in a voice pitched for only me to hear across a banquet table, had once said: *Everything the crown wants hidden, it hides over the wine cellar. No one searches near what they love.* He had meant it as a joke about the queen's sweetness tooth. Tonight it was a key.

I climbed the back stair with my boots in my hand, the wool of my stockings whispering against stone worn smooth by two hundred years of servants' feet. My heart did not race. That frightened me more than racing would have. Six years of swallowing nightshade and henbane and worse had burned the tremor out of me; I moved through the dark with the terrible calm of something already half-poisoned, already half-dead, already past the point where fear could find purchase.

The office door was locked with a tumbler lock, old and honest. A hairpin and forty seconds solved it.

* * *

Moonlight came through the window in a thin grey blade, and by it I found the steward's cabinets: household accounts, wine inventories, a ledger of tasters' wages with my own name inked and crossed out — *Ravensworth, Ines; deceased, presumed.* Presumed. Someone had not even bothered to confirm my death before striking me from the rolls. There is a particular freedom in being presumed dead. It is also its own kind of grave.

I found the forgery on the third shelf, folded inside a false bottom of the correspondence box, wrapped in oilcloth like something precious.

It was a trade charter. Not a proclamation of safety, not a defense of the realm — a charter granting the crown sole rights to the fae routes through Greywater Pass, dated three days *before* the queen stood on her balcony and declared the gifted traitors. Beneath it, in the chancellor's crabbed hand, a note: *Proclamation to follow. The Hunt will clear the pass of those who might contest our claim. See that the borderstone matter is handled quietly.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, though once was enough; my tongue had been trained to catch the taste of a single drop of deceit in a gallon of honey, and this document was bitter all the way through.

The hunt was never about safety. It had never been about safety. Sixty souls registered and chained in the courtyard of Ashfall — sixty souls, and I had counted them from a rooftop while fleeing the city, their sigils branded into their cheeks so no one would mistake them for people — sixty souls cleared out of the mountains like weeds from a garden bed, so the queen might own a road.

My hands shook now. Late, but thorough.

And beneath the charter, tucked where only a taster's fingers — trained to find the pit in every plum — would think to search, was a second paper. Smaller. Older. Sealed with wax gone amber with age.

A covenant seal. The same broken circle-and-thorn that graced the single page I'd saved from my mother's letters, drawn now in faded violet ink beside a list of names. Wardens of the borderstone, generation after generation. Ravensworth appeared four times. And at the end, in a hand I recognized from the visions that still woke me sweating in the high passes — my grandmother's hand — a single line:

*I withdraw my oath. Let the stone keep itself.*

Coerced. Threatened. Whatever had bent her, the Hollow Court had shown me enough: men in the queen's father's livery, her children named aloud as hostages, her own blood promised to their knives. She had unbound the covenant with a sentence, and the sentence had echoed down twenty years into my mother's exile, into my poisoned apprenticeship, into this moonlit office where her granddaughter now committed treason by candleless light.

I took both papers. I took the wages ledger too, on impulse, because somewhere in its crossed-out lines was proof that the crown had known exactly what I was when it hired me — had *bred* me for it, perhaps, the way one breeds a hound for scent. Proof cuts both ways. I would worry about which way later.

That was when the floorboard outside the door creaked.

* * *

I know the sound of boots on palace stone the way a deer knows the cough of a wolf. These boots paused at the threshold. Did not knock. Did not call out. Simply waited, in the manner of a man deciding whether what he suspected was worth the trouble of confirming.

There was exactly one window, and it looked onto the inner court where the guard changed at midnight bells. There was exactly one door, and behind it stood someone heavy enough to make old boards speak.

I put my knife in my hand anyway. Habit. Hope, maybe, of the bleakest kind.

"Whoever you are," the voice said through the wood, low and rough as river gravel, "you're standing in the steward's office at the third bell, and you are either the stupidest thief in Corvane or someone I owe an apology."

Vane.

Every muscle in me went to stone, then to water. I had rehearsed this meeting a hundred times on the road — in firelight, in rain, in the hollow of the waystation where my grandmother's ghosts had shown me what coercion looks like — and in every rehearsal I had a clever answer, or a blade, or both.

"Captain," I said, and my voice came out smaller than any rehearsal had allowed. "You should open the door. If you're going to hang me, you should see what I'm hanging for."

Silence. Long enough that I heard the banked fires settling below, the wind working at the shutters, my own pulse counting out the years between us.

Then the latch lifted, and Aldric Vane filled the doorway with his grey cloak and his greyer eyes and a lantern he did not raise.

He looked older than six years should have made him. Or perhaps I had simply never looked properly before, never let myself see the man who had taught me to hold a knife, who had stood at my grandmother's memorial with his fist over his heart, who had — I understood it only now, seeing his gaze go to the oilcloth in my hands and knowing, *knowing*, that he knew — perhaps already begun, weeks ago, to drown in the same truth I held dripping in my fist.

"The pass," he said quietly. "Greywater. You've seen what's at Greywater."

"I've seen what's under everything." I held the charter out. Not surrendering it. Offering. "Read it. Then decide what you are."

For a moment — one breath, two — his hand moved toward the paper, and I believed him capable of anything: arrest, mercy, treachery, tears. Vane had always been a fortress, and fortresses are most dangerous at the moment they choose whether to open or fall.

He did not take it. Instead he stepped past me to the window, checked the court below with a soldier's economy, and turned back with his face arranged into something I had never seen him wear: shame.

"Three days ago," he said, "I pulled a hedge-witch out of a holding wagon at Greywater ford. Girl with green ink on her fingers. The escort orders said she was to be questioned at the keep." His jaw worked. "The orders were signed by me. I never signed them."

"Tam." The name came out of me like an arrow loosed before the bow was drawn. "She's alive? You have her?"

"I have her in a cellar under the east barracks with a guard rotation only I know." He met my eyes at last, and there was the whole war in him, duty and conscience tearing each other like dogs over a bone. "I told myself I was keeping her for formal interrogation. A captain can tell himself a great many things at the third bell."

The debt landed on me before I understood its weight — Vane's silence, Vane's forged-forgery of his own orders, Vane risking his neck on a cellar and a lie. This was not a favor. Favors can be repaid. What he was handing me could only be carried.

"Why?" I asked. It came out raw. "You served her your whole life."

"So did your grandmother." He said it without cruelty, and it cut deeper than cruelty would have. "I've read the old wardens' rolls, Ines. I read them the week the Hunt was proclaimed. I've known what your family was since before you could hold a spoon." He looked at the charter again, then away, out the window toward the north, toward Thornfell, toward the broken stone I could feel humming in my blood even from here, faint as a struck bell fading. "Go," he said. "North stair, then the ash-chute. The watch changes at the fourth bell and none of them will be looking at the kitchens."

"And at dawn, when the steward finds his box opened—"

"At dawn I'll report that I searched the office myself and found nothing amiss." A ghost of the old dry humor, the voice across the banquet table. "It will even be nearly true."

I moved toward the door. Stopped. Turned.

"Aldric." His Christian name, spoken for the first time in six years of calling him Captain. "Come to the solstice. Come to the stone. When the truth comes out — and it will come out — be standing on the right side of it."

He was quiet a long moment. Lantern-light moved across his face and made the grey eyes flicker gold, briefly, like a man remembering fire.

"If your covenant holds," he said, "there won't be a right side left to stand on. Only sides that were wrong sooner." He gestured brusquely toward the passage. "Go. Before I remember what I'm supposed to be."

I went. Down the north stair, through the ash-chute that scraped soot into my hair like a benediction, out into the frozen street where the city slept around its own small lies. Behind me the palace rose black against the stars, every window dark, and I felt the stolen papers against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

The hunt was never about safety. The proof was in my shirt, the witch was in a cellar, and the warden's oath was burning a hole in the place where my name used to live.

Three days to solstice. Two rescues. One covenant.

I turned north and ran.
