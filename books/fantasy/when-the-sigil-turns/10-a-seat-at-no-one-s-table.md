## 10. A Seat at No One's Table

The snow came down soft as flour the morning they buried nothing.

There was no body to bury — that had been decided quickly, and with a strange gentleness, as though everyone in Greywater village had agreed without speaking that some graves are better left empty. Ines stood at the head of the hollow they'd dug anyway, because Tam insisted that grief needs somewhere to go, and the villagers of Greywater needed to believe the woman who saved them was gone for good.

"Ines Ravensworth," the priest said, "loyal servant of the crown."

Not anymore, Ines thought. But she let him say it. Let the words fall into the hole like coins into a well, and when the earth went in over them she felt lighter than she had in six years — six years of tasting other people's poisons so that a queen could sleep soundly, and never once being allowed to taste her own life.

Tam squeezed her hand. The hedge-witch's fingers were still bandaged from the ford, still stiff where the hunters' irons had bruised the bone, but her grip was warm and certain. "You're crying," Tam said quietly.

"It's the snow."

"Liar." Tam smiled. "Good. I was starting to worry the oath took all your truth and left none over."

Ines laughed despite herself — a rough, unpracticed sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. Around them the village watched with the wary reverence people give to something they cannot decide is miracle or menace. An old woman stepped forward and pressed a loaf of dark bread into Ines's hands. *For the road*, the woman said. Though there was no road now. Not for her.

* * *

They came for Vane on the ninth day after solstice.

Ines heard it secondhand, from a carter who had come up through the foothills with salt and rumors: Captain Aldric Vane, hero of the Sigil Hunt, the queen's own hound, had stood in the marble hall at Carrowmere and read aloud from a stolen decree. The forgery. The manufactured treason. The trade routes through the Thornfell passes that the crown had wanted cleared of fae tolls, and the gifted folk whose existence made clearing them convenient.

He had laid the decree on the floor before the throne, the carter said, and then he had laid his captain's sword beside it, and said, *"I hunted these people. I will testify to what I was ordered to hunt them for."*

"They'll take his rank," Tam said that evening, in the waystation's kitchen, which was no longer abandoned. Ines had lit its hearth for the first time in forty years, and the fae-stone in the chimney sang faintly when it warmed, a sound like a spoon against a cup.

"Yes."

"And probably his freedom."

"Probably." Ines turned the bread the old woman had given her — stale now, but good — and cut slices with a knife that had belonged to the last royal taster, her grandmother, recovered from a locked drawer in the Hollow Court. She set three places at the long table out of habit before remembering there were only two of them tonight. Then, deliberately, she left the third place standing. It felt less like absence and more like invitation.

"Do you regret it?" Tam asked. "Telling the stone everything. Your name, your gift, your grandmother's crime. Giving it the whole ugly truth of your line." She turned her cup slowly on the wood. "Most people would have lied a little. Just enough to survive it."

Ines looked into the fire. She could still feel the oath in her bones, the way you feel a broken bone long after it knits — not pain exactly, but a new awareness of the place where she had been remade. When she spoke her grandmother's crime aloud, *she sold the covenant's seal to save her daughter*, the borderstone had gone silent as a held breath, and Ines had been certain, in that eclipsed dark, that she would be consumed. That the stone would take her whole, the way it had taken wardens before her, the way it had nearly taken Tam.

Instead the sigil had turned. Like a key. Like a lock recognizing, at last, a hand it had been waiting for.

"No," she said. "I spent six years swallowing truths that weren't mine to swallow. Poison for the court. Silence for my family. Lies for my own safety." She met Tam's eyes. "The stone asked for one true meal, freely given. It was the easiest thing I've ever eaten."

* * *

Spring came late to the pass, but when it came it came all at once, the way forgiveness does.

Ines learned the borderstone the way she had once learned poison: patiently, dose by dose, respect before mastery. The stone was alive in a manner no ledger or grimoire described. It breathed with the seasons. It hummed when fae crossed from the other side, a low note Ines felt in her teeth before she heard it. And it hungered — not for blood, as the old covenant had demanded, not for wardens sealed living into its heart, but for witness. For someone to stand before it and tell the truth of what passed through the pass, both directions, both realms.

That was the third path the sigil had shown her. Not exposure alone. Not sacrifice alone. A covenant reforged on testimony instead of flesh — a warden who gave her whole truth once, freely, and then kept giving small truths daily, the way a taster keeps taking small doses. Her grandmother's generation had built a lock that fed on lives. Ines had rebuilt one that fed on honesty, and it turned out the stone preferred it. The old binding cracked and healed; the crossings stabilized; and on the first warm night of spring, a fae merchant walked through the pass openly for the first time in a century, tipped his hat to Ines as if greeting an old colleague, and paid a toll of three perfect pears, which were the best thing she had ever tasted.

She wrote to Vane in April. Short letters, unsigned by title. He answered in a cramped soldier's hand, always from a different town — he had lost everything, the letter said, and sounded, in the spaces between the lines, strangely unburdened by it. *They stripped my commission,* he wrote. *I find I sleep better as a man than I ever did as a sword.* And once, at the bottom of a letter mostly about weather: *Your mother would have been proud. So would he.* Ines read that line four times and did not ask whom he meant. Her father. It could only be her father. Some doors she was not ready to open, even unlocked.

* * *

By midsummer, people came to the pass.

First a trickle: gifted folk released from the registers when the forgery was exposed, drifting north because word traveled that the Thornfell crossing stood open and unfeared. Then families. Then traders who remembered the old routes and the old tolls — a pear here, a bolt of fae-woven cloth there, a song sung across the borderstone in exchange for safe passage. The waystation filled. Tam planted a garden in the ruins of the fae courtyard and coaxed things from the soil that had no business growing this far north: moonpeppers, silverbeet, a squash that glowed faintly after dark and tasted of honey and smoke.

One evening Ines came down from the stone at dusk and found the long table outside the waystation set for thirty. Tam had dragged it from the great hall, and the fugitives and farmers and two very confused fae diplomats were already sitting, passing bread, arguing amiably about whether the glowing squash should count as human food or fae tribute.

"What is this?" Ines asked.

"Your table," Tam said. "You keep setting extra places. Every night since winter. Three, five, nine, twelve." She shrugged, and there was something soft under her dryness, something that had been growing there since Greywater since the ford since the truth-spell that Ines had answered with more honesty than any spell required. "Tonight I just caught up with you."

Ines sat down. The bread went around. A fae child taught a hunter's daughter a counting rhyme in a language older than the realm, and the hunter's daughter taught her a skipping game back, and the borderstone hummed on the hill above them, contented as a sleeping animal.

This, Ines thought. This was the seat the oath had bought her — not at the queen's high table with its poisoned stews and gilded lies, but here, at a table anyone could join, where the only thing served freely was the truth and the only price was bringing your own appetite.

She raised her cup. "To the covenant," she said.

"To the table," Tam corrected, and thirty voices repeated it.

* * *

Autumn brought the first frost, and with it, a rider on the north road.

Ines saw the horse first — a grey courser, palace-bred, too fine for these hills — and felt the old reflex wake in her blood, the taster's tongue of the eyes, reading danger in the set of the saddle and the length of the stride. But the rider wore no livery. No colors at all. Just a traveling cloak gone grey with dust, and when the figure dismounted at the foot of the waystation steps and pushed back its hood, Ines found herself looking at a young woman with the Ravensworth jaw and eyes the exact color of her own.

"I've come a long way," the stranger said. Her voice shook. "They told me in Greywater there was a warden here. They said—" She stopped, steadied herself the way Ines had learned to steady herself over a suspicious cup. "They said she takes the truth, whatever it costs. And that she feeds people after."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. At the family written plain in her face. At the sealed letter clutched in her hand, bearing a wax mark Ines recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water: the old covenant's seal, the one from her mother's burned letters, the one page she had kept.

Behind her, the borderstone began, very softly, to hum.
