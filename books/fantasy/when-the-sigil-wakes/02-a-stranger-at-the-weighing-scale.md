## 2. A Stranger at the Weighing Scale

The counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things smelled the way it always did at mid-morning: lamp oil, old paper, and the faint vinegar tang of bottled lullabies going off in their crates. Paloma Brightwater sat behind her desk with a jeweler's loupe pressed to one eye, checking the seals on the second crate of the shipment and finding the third bottle in each row wanting.

"Sixteen through twenty are watered," she called to no one in particular. "Somebody in Corvane's Bay is stretching stock with riverwater and hope."

The clerk at the far table—Tansy, quick-fingered, incurably nosy—leaned over. "Watered lullabies? What happens if you drink one?"

"You sleep for six hours and dream about somebody else's laundry." Paloma set the loupe down and made a note in the margin of the manifest. "It's fraud, but it's the boring kind. Dockmaster's fine can handle it."

That was the shape of her days now, nine years deep into exile: the boring kind of fraud, the boring kind of mercy, the boring kind of survival. She had gotten good at margins. Margins were where you wrote things no one was supposed to read; margins were also where you lived.

She was reaching for the fourth crate when the harbor shuddered.

Not an earthquake. Earthquakes rolled under your feet like a wagon over cobbles. This was different—a long, dragging groan out of the sea, as if something enormous had turned over in its sleep beneath the pier pilings. The lamp above the weighing scale swung on its chain. Every bottle in every crate chimed at once, a sound like a room full of small frightened mouths.

Paloma's hand went flat against the desk before she knew she'd moved.

*Not me,* was her first thought, the same thought she'd had for nine years whenever the sky darkened wrong. *I didn't call it.* As if the storm were a debt collector who might still be looking for her, and denial were a legal defense.

"It's just weather," Tansy said, though her voice had gone thin. "Harbor weather. Happens."

"Happens," Paloma agreed, and did not believe either of them.

The sigil sat between her shoulder blades like a coin left too long in the sun—the Brightwater mark, three waves and a key, inked into her skin by a mother who had never explained why and then hadn't lived long enough to be asked. For nine years it had been exactly what the magistrates said it was: a brand, a sentence, dead ink. It had never once done anything except ache before rain.

Now it did more than ache.

It warmed. Slowly, then all at once, like a stove door swinging open, and Paloma felt heat bloom outward along her ribs in a pattern she recognized without ever having seen it—the geometry of the mark unspooling under her skin, tracing lines she could have drawn blind.

She stood up so fast her chair went over backwards.

"Mira?" Tansy said, using the name Paloma kept on the guild rolls. Mira Ashgrove, ledger-keeper, nobody. "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse," Paloma said. "Weather." But that wasn't it either, and she couldn't have said what it was, only that some door somewhere had just heard her name.

The counting room's main doors banged open hard enough to crack the plaster around the frame.

The man who came through them was not walking so much as falling forward with great commitment. He wore courier's leathers, or what had been courier's leathers; the right side of him had been scorched to a black crust from collar to hip, the leather split like burnt bread, and underneath it his skin was blistered and weeping. His hair had burned off on that side. He smelled of char and under the char he smelled of something else entirely, something Paloma had met only twice in her life—once in a brazier at a funeral, once in a dream she'd refused to record in any ledger.

Sulfur and hot iron and a sweetness like sap. Dragon-fire.

"Heads up—" someone started, and then the man collapsed across the weighing scale.

The brass pan took his weight with a shriek of metal. Weights scattered and rolled. The scale arm tipped wildly, settling at last at a reading that would have said, if scales weighed such things, that a very heavy debt had just entered the building.

By the time Paloma reached him, half the guild had reached him first, and they were all doing the same thing: standing in a circle, staring, waiting for someone else to touch him. Burned men frightened people. Burned men carrying dragon-stench frightened them more, though most of them likely couldn't have named why. The guild traded in impossible things daily, but impossibles were supposed to arrive crated and invoiced.

"Back," Paloma said. "All of you. Give him air."

She knelt. Up close, the burn smell was worse and stranger, and there was something else—his leathers were stiff with salt spray, soaked and dried and soaked again. Sea travel. Whoever this courier was, he'd crossed open water recently, and whatever had burned him had burned him near the end of it.

His eyes found her. They were gray and terribly clear, the eyes of a man in the stage of dying where the body has given up arguing and the mind gets everything it wants to say done quickly.

"Brightwater," he said.

Every hair on her arms stood up. Nine years, and no one outside the guild had spoken that name to her face. She kept Mira Ashgrove stitched inside her collar like an amulet, and it worked, mostly.

"You've got the wrong woman," she said quietly. "My name is—"

"I know what your name is." His cracked lips pulled back. It might have been a smile. "Ledger-girl. I know all about you. I carried letters for nine years about you." His hand came up—it was the least burned of his hands, which is to say it looked like a glove left too near a fire—and seized her wrist with shocking strength. "Vessany."

The word went through the room like a dropped knife. She heard Tansy gasp. She heard someone else take a step backward. Vessany was a word that appeared in no atlas, in no charter, in no prayer book. Paloma knew because she had spent three years of evenings in the guild archives looking for it, and found nothing but a hole shaped like a country.

"There is no Vessany," she said. That was the official line. That was every line.

"That's what everyone decided," the courier rasped. "Deciding things doesn't make them true. It just makes them quiet." He coughed, and the cough brought blood with it, dark and glittering. "Listen. Listen to me, girl, I'm spending my last money here. The door out of Vessany is closing. Has been closing for years. My masters sealed it to keep the magistrate out, and now—" another cough, "—now it's sealing them in. There's maybe a season left. Maybe less. And the only key—" his grip tightened on her wrist, hard enough to grind bone, and his gray eyes bored into hers, "—the only key walks around wearing a ledger-girl's face and pretending her name is Ashgrove."

He let go of her wrist only to fumble at his breast pocket. His fingers didn't work well anymore. Paloma heard herself making a choice in the silence of her own skull—the kind of choice she usually recorded for other people—and helped him, pulling the flattened leather wallet free.

Inside was a map.

Or what had been a map. It was a rectangle of hide, scorched at two corners, and the lines upon it were not ink. She saw that immediately. Ink sat *on* paper; these lines sat *in* the hide, sunk below the surface like old scars, and when she tilted the map toward the lamplight the lines glowed, faintly, the deep banked orange of coals an hour after the fire. Heat came off them. Not much. Enough.

One line glowed brighter than the rest, a road of ember-light running from a coastline she did not recognize toward a small dense knot of markings in the interior. Around the knot, someone had drawn a symbol in the same buried fire.

Three waves and a key.

The sigil on her back flared.

There was no gentler way to put it. It flared like a struck match against her spine, heat and light blooming through her shoulder blades, and for one vertiginous second Paloma felt—not pain, worse than pain—*recognition*, the map and the mark answering each other across her body like two halves of a tuning fork. The lamps guttered. The rain that the harbor storm had been holding back came down all at once against the windows, a fistfuls-of-gravel drumming, and somewhere below them a shutter tore loose and clattered down the alley.

"Ah," the courier said softly, watching her face. "There it is. Been asleep since the storm, have you? Nine years?" Something like pity moved through the gray of his eyes. "It wasn't sleeping, girl. It was listening."

"For what?" Her voice came out strange and flat.

"A reason."

He sagged then, the last of whatever had carried him up from the harbor draining out through the burns. Paloma caught his shoulders and eased him down onto the floor, and the guild crowded in again, and someone finally had the sense to run for the physician's kit and someone else for the watch, and Paloma knelt there holding a map inked in dragon-fire while a man died slowly on her counting room floor.

But before any of it—before the kit, before the watch—she looked up, because a silence had a shape, and this silence had the shape of Guildmaster Corvane.

He stood at the head of the counting tables where he took his morning reports, and he was not shouting for order, not demanding answers, not doing any of the hundred competent things Corvane did competently. He was watching the courier with his head tilted, hands folded behind his back, and his expression was calm.

Not shocked-calm. Not grieving-calm.

*Calm the way a man is calm when the thing he has been waiting for has finally arrived.*

Corvane felt her looking. His gaze slid from the dying man to her—to the map in her hands—and for a fraction of a heartbeat something moved behind his eyes, something old and tired and sorry.

Then he smiled, warm as ever, and said, "Well, Mira. Looks like the morning's audit has gotten interesting," and Paloma understood, in the marrow-deep way you understand a false entry in a ledger, that the guildmaster had known this courier was coming.

Maybe before the courier knew it himself.

The courier's hand scrabbled weakly at her sleeve. She leaned close, and his whisper was barely a breath, and it was meant for her alone:

"Don't show him the map. He already sold the door once."

* * *

They got the courier onto a cot in the infirmary off the counting room, which was guild-speak for a pallet behind a curtain. He refused the physician with a shake of his head and asked instead for water, which he got, and for paper, which he did not get, because Corvane ordered the room cleared and the doors watched—"until we know who sent him and what he's carrying"—and Corvane's orders had the weight of law inside these walls. Paloma had helped draft those laws herself, in her own neat hand, in the margins of her own ledgers.

So she sat at her desk again, with the map folded small against her stomach, under her apron, against the skin that was still humming like a struck bell, and pretended to audit crates of watered lullabies while the rain hammered the windows and a man breathed raggedly behind a curtain ten feet away.

*The door out of Vessany is closing.*

There was no Vessany. There was no door. Except that the mark on her back had woken for the first time in nine years, and a dying man had crossed the sea to find a ledger-girl named Brightwater, and the only person in the world she trusted had watched him arrive like a merchant watching the shipment he'd prepaid for.

Corvane crossed the room and set a hand gently on her shoulder, and she did not flinch, because she had nine years of practice at not flinching.

"Whatever that madman told you," Corvane said softly, "we'll sort it together. You know that, don't you? You're family, Mira."

"Yes," Paloma said, and kept her eyes on the manifest, and counted her own heartbeats like entries in a column that had begun, quietly and irrevocably, to refuse to balance.

Behind the curtain, the courier's breathing stopped.

And in the sudden hush, Paloma heard boots on the front steps outside—many boots, unhurried, in the crisp cadence of men who carried writs.
