## 3. What the Magistrate Keeps

The file on Vessany weighed four pounds and eleven ounces, and Halden Vey knew this because he had weighed it himself, every year, on the same brass scale, for nine years.

He did this not out of sentiment. Sentiment was a clerical error that compounded. He did it because a case that could not be measured could not be considered closed, and Halden Vey had never in thirty-one years of service closed a case improperly. The scale sat on the corner of his desk beside the inkwell and the small bell he rang when he wanted nothing at all — merely to watch his secretaries flinch. Order, he had found, was less a thing you imposed than a thing you maintained, the way a man maintains a wall: patiently, daily, with mortar and indifference.

He unlocked the drawer with the two keys — one on his ring, one kept in a sugar tin in his kitchen, because even a magistrate's housekeeper deserved her mysteries — and lifted the file into the lamplight.

*Case 44-VES. Erasure of Municipal Entity.*

Inside: forty-one affidavits sworn by men now dead or promoted. A surveyor's confession, extracted over six days, confirming that the roads leading to Vessany had always led nowhere. Three copies of the Writ of Unmapping, signed by a chancellor whose name had itself since been unmapped, which Vey considered a tidy economy. And beneath it all, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, the item that had no business existing in an orderly registry: a rubbing of a sigil, taken from the skin of a girl of seventeen, done in charcoal by an inquisitor who had wept while he worked. Vey had noted the weeping in the margin. *Officer compromised by subject's age. Recommend reassignment.* The officer had been reassigned. To a lighthouse. Where he still was, if Vey's informants were accurate, and they were paid to be.

He set the rubbing aside and read the ledger of persons, as he read it every year on this date, the anniversary of the erasure. Not the public anniversary — there was no such thing; one cannot commemorate a place that never existed — but the private one, kept in this drawer, in this room, in this careful hand.

*BRIGHTWATER, SERA. Deceased. Cause: recorded as fever. Actual: exposure to unbinding. See appendix.*

*BRIGHTWATER, PALOMA. Status: exiled, self-sustaining. Location: Kellharbor. Occupation: clerk, Guild of Impossible Things. Surveillance: passive, tier two. Threat assessment: dormant.*

Nine years, and the threat assessment had not changed. Vey found this restful. Most of his colleagues mistook him for a man who enjoyed complications — the ones who feared him told stories about cold rooms and long questions — but the truth was simpler and worse. He hated complications. He loved resolution. Everything he had done to Vessany, everything he would yet do, proceeded from a single conviction so old it had stopped being a belief and become a temperament: that the world was better when it contained fewer things that could not be accounted for.

Vessany had been such a thing. A country with no treaty, no census the Crown could audit, no borders that behaved. A country that kept its records in songs and its laws in bloodline sigils, so that power lived in skin instead of paper. You cannot tax a birthright. You cannot subpoena a grandmother's memory. Vessany had refused, politely and then impolitely, to become legible, and a country that cannot be read, Vey believed, will eventually be written over. He had simply been the one holding the pen when the Crown finally admitted it.

The storm had not been his instrument. That was the elegant part, the part he still admired the way a locksmith admires a well-made lock he did not build. The Brightwater woman — Sera — had tried to save her illegible little country by the only means left to it, calling up something enormous from the sigil-line, and it had gone wrong, as enormous things do, and the storm that drowned three villages and wrote Vessany off the map in official memory had been hers. All Vey had done was arrive afterward with paperwork. He had let the catastrophe do the erasing and taken credit for the tidiness. History would call him a destroyer. Let it. Destroyers are remembered; administrators retire comfortably.

Except for the girl.

He turned to the last page, the one he permitted himself to linger over once a year, the way other men permit themselves one glass of good brandy.

Sera Brightwater, dying of the unbinding, had made him an offer in the ruins of her country's last standing room: take the child's name off the lists. Give her exile instead of the scaffold. In exchange, Sera would confirm, on the record, that the storm was hers alone — that no other sigil-bearers survived, that the line ended with her daughter, that Vessany had no heirs and therefore no claim and therefore, legally, no existence worth contesting.

It had been excellent drafting. He had said so at the time, and the dying woman had almost laughed.

So the girl had gone into the world with a sentence instead of a death, carrying the one loose thread Vey had ever allowed to remain loose, and he had spent nine years watching the thread from a distance, telling himself what he told his juniors about dormant assets: *monitor, do not touch.* Because here was the fact he had never written down anywhere, the single datum he kept in the drawer of his own skull where no audit could reach it:

The girl was the key.

Not metaphorically. The unbinding that erased Vessany had not destroyed it. Nothing that thorough is destruction; destruction is cheap. What he had sealed that winter was a door, and behind the door the country persisted — folded, compressed, withheld from the world like a document stamped *pending* — and the seal answered to the Brightwater sigil, and the Brightwater sigil lived now in the skin of a woman who filed ledgers in Kellharbor under a name she surely believed was her only inheritance.

A dead key opens nothing. He had understood this from the first night. If Paloma Brightwater died, the door became furniture, and Vessany became truly erased, and the question of what he had done would close forever unanswered. But a living key could be turned. And Vey had spent nine years learning what was behind the door — through refugees who no longer remembered being refugees, through survey instruments that returned readings from coordinates that were not there, through a single courier's intercepted letter that spoke of *emberlight and the last tenant* — and what was behind the door, if it could be brought out under writ, under seal, under *his* administration, was the most complete act of order ever achieved in the history of governance: a nation, archived. Filed. Kept.

He did not want Vessany burning free into the world with its songs and its skin-born laws. He wanted it exactly where it was. Contained. Catalogued. His.

The problem, for nine years, had been that turning a key requires either the key's cooperation or its capture, and both were untidy. So he had waited. Patience was not a virtue in Vey's estimation; it was a budgeting strategy.

Now the budget had run out.

The dispatch had come that morning, and it lay on his blotter where he could see it without touching it, because some documents change weight when you understand them. A courier out of Kellharbor, one of his, reporting that the Guild of Impossible Things had received a visitor in the night: a burned man, half-dead, smelling of a fire that no hearth produces, carrying papers. Reporting further — and here the boy's handwriting had gone cramped, frightened — that the Brightwater woman had been seen leaving the guild before dawn, and that the sigil watchers posted at the harbor had recorded, for the first time in nine years, an *emission*. A flare of the mark, visible to their instruments for eleven seconds, coinciding with a squall that sank no ships and harmed no one and should not have existed in a clear season.

Tier two surveillance was over. Vey took out a fresh sheet and began, with real pleasure, the only kind of writing he had ever loved: orders.

*To Inquisitor-Commander Dree and attached company. Re: BRIGHTWATER, PALOMA.*

He wrote the way he did everything, cleanly, in numbered clauses.

*1. Subject is to be located via the Guild of Impossible Things and the harbor network. Do not alert the guildmaster until clause four is satisfied; Corvane's loyalty is transactional and therefore reliable, but transactional men perform poorly when startled.*

*2. Subject is to be taken alive and uninjured. I will state this plainly because plainness prevents excuses: intact skin, intact mind, intact cooperation. Any wound above the hands is a career-ending error. She is to be treated, at all times, as something borrowed rather than something caught.*

*3. If she resists, withdraw. Do not escalate. A flare of the sigil in a populated area is an acceptable loss of face and an unacceptable loss of containment. Report and wait. We have waited nine years; we can wait nine days.*

*4. Once she is held, she is to be brought to me personally, not to the assizes. No record of the transport enters any register. Use the old routes. Use the sugar tin if you must.*

He paused over clause four, then struck *sugar tin*, which meant nothing to Dree and which he had written out of private habit, and replaced it with *the coastal road.*

*5. Failure is defined narrowly: her death, her disfigurement, or her arrival anywhere other than in front of me. Success is defined even more narrowly. Be exact. Exactness has kept us both employed.*

He signed it, blotted it, sealed it with the plain grey wax he used for matters that must never be discussed, and rang the small bell.

Dree came in the way he liked his subordinates to enter a room: immediately, silently, and slightly afraid. She read the orders standing. When she reached clause two, her eyes flicked up — he watched her decide whether to ask why gentleness, why now, after nine years of watching — and he saved her the trouble.

"Because," Vey said, closing the drawer on Case 44-VES and locking it with both keys, "for nine years that woman has been a loose end. As of last night, she is an asset. Loose ends get cut, Inquisitor. Assets get handled." He passed her the sealed packet. "Handle her."

Dree saluted and left, and Vey sat alone with his lamp and his scale and his annual glass of brandy, which he poured early this year, because anniversaries ought to be observed.

Four pounds, eleven ounces. He set the file on the pan and watched the needle settle, dead level, perfectly honest. Soon, he thought, the file would grow heavier. There would be new affidavits, a fresh assessment, perhaps — his pulse quickened faintly at the thought, and he noted the sensation with clinical interest — a final page, signed in a hand he had seen only once, in charcoal, seventeen years ago.

Somewhere north, a woman who thought she was running from her past was running toward it, as keys always do. They travel, keys. It is their nature. They go looking for doors.

Halden Vey finished his brandy, put on his coat, and went down to the map room to take out the charts of a country that did not exist, and begin planning how to receive it.
