## 4. Nine Years of Weather

I left the guild by the coal chute, which felt appropriate. Nine years of carrying other people's secrets down those stairs, and in the end I went out through the building's own back passage, black dust to the elbows, my one good coat ruined and not caring.

Behind me, above me, through the floorboards: the sound of boots. Not guild boots. Guild boots shuffle; these struck like a signature being written on a confession.

Vey's inquisitors. Already here.

The map rode against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping animal. Dragon-fire ink doesn't cool, Sef had told me once, drunk on plum brandy and feeling poetic — it keeps the temperature of the thing that made it. I'd thought he was lying. He lied about most things. Apparently not about this.

The harbor storm was still going. It had been going since morning, a low gray grumble with no lightning in it, just pressure, the whole sky leaning down on Saltmere like a hand testing whether a lid fit. Everyone in the streets had their collars up and their eyes down, the way people do when weather feels personal. Nobody looked at me. I'd erased enough names from ledgers to know how to erase myself from a street.

*First coordinate,* the map said, in characters that shifted if I stared too long, rearranging themselves like a man who doesn't want to be identified at an inquest. A line of fire drawn north along the coast to a point where the ink had burned clean through the paper — a hole in the map, which is either very funny or very rude when your destination turns out to be *nothing*.

Except it wasn't nothing. The burn-through sat exactly where every atlas agreed there should be nothing: the Gannel Light, a lighthouse on a stack of rock that no shipping chart bothered to mark because no ship needed to avoid it and no sailor ever landed there. My mother had known its keeper. That much I remembered from before — from the before-before, the childhood I don't count, the seven years before the storm.

I bought passage on a mackerel boat by not mentioning I wanted any. That is, I stood at the quay until the skipper squinted at me and said, "You're not seasick already, are you?" and I said "No," and he said "Then make yourself useful," and that was that. Nine years in a guild that trades in impossible things teaches you that most doors open if you simply decline to knock.

The keeper met me at the base of the light with a lantern already lit, though it was barely noon, and the sky as dark as dishwater.

"Ilse Brightwater's girl," she said.

Not a question. I stood in the spray and the wind took my breath sideways and I could not, for the life of me, remember a single one of the clever things I usually say.

"You'd better come up," she said. "Storms coming that'll want telling about."

* * *

Her name was Maren Goss, and she was older than the light itself, or looked it, her face cured like leather left out in all weathers, which in fairness it had been. She poured tea that tasted of kelp and iron and set a second cup across the table for nobody, out of habit or courtesy to ghosts, I couldn't tell.

"You've got her sigil," she said. "I can see it moving."

"It's not moving."

"It is. Look again."

I looked. On the inside of my wrist, under the skin, the brightwater sigil — the coiled wave-and-star my mother had pressed into me at seven years old, the brand that marked me as a storm-caller and got me exiled for a storm I never called — was *moving*. Slowly. Like something turning over in sleep.

Nine years, it had been quiet. Nine years of ledgers and margins and invisibility, and now, in the same week a burning courier collapsed at my feet, it had decided to wake.

"What did she send you for?" Maren asked.

"Nobody sent me. She's dead."

"I know she's dead. I'm asking what she sent you *for*. Ilse didn't do anything without purpose. She didn't go to market without three reasons and a contingency." Maren's eyes were pale gray, the exact color of the sea below us. "She left you the sigil. Sigils aren't inheritances, girl. They're instructions."

"The map says otherwise."

I put the oilcloth packet on the table between us, and Maren Goss, who had watched me come out of the sea-wash without blinking, went white to the lips.

"Where did you get that?"

"A dying man gave it to me. He smelled like—" I stopped. "Like dragon."

"Dragon-fire ink," she whispered. "That's Vessany work. That's *home* work."

The word hit me somewhere I hadn't been hit in nine years, because Vessany wasn't home. Vessany was a story. Vessany was a country that appeared in old songs and no atlases, a place my mother had supposedly betrayed and been exiled for betraying, along with me, her seven-year-old accessory. Vessany was a fable people told to explain why the Brightwater woman had a storm in her skin and no country that would hold her.

"That's not real," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "Vessany's a story."

Maren laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as driftwood splitting.

"A story. Yes. That's what they wanted. That's what erasure *is*, girl — it's not killing everyone, it's making the survivors embarrassed to say the name." She reached across the table and turned the packet over with one finger, careful as a surgeon. "I was born there. I kept this light for forty years because it's the last thing on the coast that faces where Vessany used to be. Used to be. Present tense. As if the sea ate it."

"Then tell me what happened."

And she told me. And I am going to write it here exactly as she said it, because this is the first page of the true ledger, and I want the words fixed somewhere outside my own skull, where they can't shift and rearrange themselves the way burning ink does.

She told me that Vessany was real, and coastal, and small, and proud of its doors — doors that opened onto elsewhere, cut by craft long forgotten everywhere else. She told me that the Magistrate Halden Vey had come to Vessany twenty years ago as a surveyor, all politeness and instruments, cataloguing the doors for the crown. She told me that when the crown decided the doors were a threat to order — a country you could walk out of in any direction being, apparently, an affront to good governance — it was Vey who drew up the writ of erasure, and Vey who needed one thing to close every door at once: a storm-caller's sigil, used in anger.

She told me that my mother had found out. That my mother had gone to Vey — not to fight him, Ilse Brightwater was many things but never stupid — to bargain. To offer herself, her sigil, her cooperation, in exchange for Vessany being allowed to seal *itself*, quietly, one door at a time, so that the country would survive unseen rather than be destroyed seen.

"And then?" I asked. My hands were flat on the table. I remember noticing how white my knuckles were, distantly, like watching someone else's hands.

"And then the storm came anyway," Maren said. "A week early. Out of season. Enormous. It broke the fleet, drowned the harbor quarter, killed three hundred people — and it gave Vey everything he needed. A natural disaster, witnessed by hundreds, caused — everyone agreed — by the Brightwater storm-caller who had threatened him. He didn't even have to lie, at first. People saw the weather and wrote the story themselves. By the time anyone asked questions, the questions were treason."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Outside, the real storm had arrived properly at last, rain hammering the glass in fistfuls.

"He called it," I said. "Vey called the storm."

"No," Maren said, and here her voice dropped to almost nothing, and she leaned toward me across the table like a woman passing contraband. "He couldn't. He's no caller. Someone else called it, girl. Someone with a sigil, someone willing. Vey just had to stand in the right place and look shocked." She held my eyes. "There were two Brightwaters in Vessany that year. Your mother. And her daughter. Seven years old. Old enough for a sigil to take, if it was pressed young. Old enough to be used."

The room tilted. I gripped the table.

"I was *seven*."

"I know."

"I would have — I'd have *known*—"

"Would you?" Maren asked, gently, terribly. "What's the first thing you remember of that night? Truly?"

And the honest answer, the answer I have spent nine years not saying out loud, is: standing in the rain, screaming, with no memory of arriving there and no memory of stopping. A blank. A gap in the record. I had always assumed grief had eaten it. Grief does that.

But I keep ledgers. I know what a doctored page looks like. And a gap where the crucial entry should be isn't grief.

It's erasure.

"Somebody closed that door in my head," I said. "Somebody locked the night up and threw away the key, and left me holding the blame instead."

"Yes," Maren said.

"Was it her? Did my mother—"

"No." Sharp, immediate, final. "Ilse would have cut off her own hand. Whatever else happened that night — whatever they made you do or think you did — she spent her last nine years trying to undo it. Why do you think she sent you away? Why do you think she let them exile a *child* rather than correct the record?" Maren's jaw worked. "Because correcting it would have meant naming the real caller. And the real caller is someone whose name, if spoken, opens doors that have to stay shut. Even for her. Even to save you."

The sigil on my wrist flared — actually flared, a line of heat like a fresh brand — and I gasped and clutched it, and Maren looked at it with an expression I couldn't read, fear and hope fighting openly on her face like weather fronts meeting offshore.

"It's waking," she said. "It knows where it is. Girl — that map. The first coordinate. This light. You didn't find me by accident. The map is leading you along the path your mother walked. And the next coordinate—" She stopped. Closed her mouth.

"The next coordinate what?"

"The next coordinate is the drowned archive," she said slowly. "Off Emberfall. Where the true records of Vessany were sunk when the doors sealed. If the sigil's awake enough to move, the map might let you sign in. Read what really happened that night." Her pale eyes fixed on mine. "Find out who called the storm."

Rain screamed against the lantern glass. Down on the rocks the sea was climbing, black and furious, and beneath my bandage my skin burned like a lit fuse running toward something I couldn't see yet.

For nine years I had survived on one story: *a storm I never called.* It was a small story, mean and useful, a story you could live in the margins with. I had built my whole self around its single wall.

Now there was a crack in it, wide as a door.

And through the crack I could hear — plain as day, as if the voice belonged to the weather itself — the truth waiting on the other side:

*I might have called it after all.*

* * *

We waited out the worst of the blow in the lamp room, Maren teaching me the coast road to Emberfall by lamplight, tracing it on an old chart with a cracked nail. She gave me bread, a sou'wester two sizes too big, and one piece of advice at the door.

"When you sign the archive," she said, "sign nothing but your true name. Full name. Every part of it. These doors are honest, and they despise half-truths."

"My full name," I repeated. Paloma Brightwater. Exile, clerk, liar, debtor, thief of small mercies. It didn't feel like enough letters.

"And Paloma," she added, as I stepped into the rain, "whoever used you that night — Vey protected them. Which means whoever they are, they're still alive, still close to him, still owed."

The mackerel boat's skipper, mercifully incurious, took me south before dawn. The whole way, the sigil on my wrist stayed warm as banked coals, and the map against my ribs grew warmer to match, and somewhere behind us both — I could feel it, the way you feel a stare between your shoulder blades — the inquisitors' boots were writing their patient signatures along the coast road, following the only key in the world straight to the last door of Vessany.
