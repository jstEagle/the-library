## 5. The Guild of Impossible Things

Corvane had always believed that a man's character lived in his ledgers, not his speeches. Anyone could swear loyalty over wine. But show Corvane a balance sheet—what a man had traded away, what he'd quietly absorbed as loss rather than pass the cost to someone weaker—and you had the whole truth of him, inked and notarized.

Which was why, on the morning the magistrate's carriage stopped outside the Guild of Impossible Things, Corvane spent a long moment in his counting room with the door locked, reading himself.

Three entries, in his own hand.

*Paloma Brightwater. Debt: one name, one door, one debt of shelter. Paid in full by G. Corvane, nine years standing.*

He had written *paid* because he liked the way it looked. Because it made her exile feel like something settled rather than something ongoing. Because ledgers were how he lied to himself in a format that would survive an audit.

He closed the book. Unlocked the door. Went down to receive Magistrate Halden Vey like a host greeting a difficult creditor—which, in every way that mattered, was exactly what this was.

* * *

Vey arrived with two inquisitors and no appetite for the guild's famous hospitality. He declined the tea. He declined the chair by the fire. He stood in the middle of the weighing room, where the bottled lullabies still sat in their crates from the harbor shipment, and looked at everything the way a man looks at goods he already intends to confiscate.

"You received a courier," Vey said. "Two nights ago. Burned half to death."

"We receive couriers," Corvane said. "It's a trading house."

"This one carried a map."

"Maps are also a trade good." Corvane kept his hands loose at his sides, his voice at the register he used for haggling—warm, unhurried, faintly amused. "Though I'll confess, Magistrate, if you've come to buy one, your coin is the wrong shape. We deal in stranger currencies here. Memories. Names. The occasional apology, lightly used."

One of the inquisitors shifted. Vey didn't. That was the thing about Halden Vey that had unsettled Corvane even back when they'd both been younger men serving older masters: nothing landed on him. Insults, flattery, grief—he weighed all of it and found none of it tender.

"I'm not here to haggle," Vey said. "I'm here to inform you. By order of the Chancellery, all relics of the erased country are forfeit to the crown. Bottled memories of Vessany. Doors keyed to it. Documents bearing its sigils or its names. Your guild holds more such items than any institution in the capital. I have the inventory requests here." He produced a folded writ, heavy paper, wax the color of dried blood. "Compliance begins tomorrow. Non-compliance ends the charter."

There it was. Not a threat shouted—a term quoted, like interest on a loan.

"The charter has stood two hundred years," Corvane said.

"Vessany stood longer." Vey set the writ on the weighing scale itself, which Corvane thought was either carelessness or a joke only the two of them would get. "Charters are granted. They can be ungranted. You of all people understand that things exist only as long as someone agrees to record them."

Corvane did understand that. It was practically the guild's founding principle. It was also, he suspected, the principle by which Vessany had been killed—not with armies but with erasure, struck from atlases and registries until a whole country became a rounding error.

"And what," Corvane said slowly, "would preservation look like? If a reasonable house wished to remain reasonable?"

"A list," Vey said. "Every Vessany item in your holdings. Every person who has handled them. And the location of the woman who came through your door carrying the most important relic of all." A pause, precise as a ledger line. "In exchange, the purge passes over you. Your traders keep their licenses. Your cellar keeps its doors. I am not interested in destroying institutions, Master Corvane. Only in correcting records."

*Protecting my traders,* Corvane told himself, and the sentence had been waiting in him for days, pre-drafted, the way a smuggler pre-drafts a cover story. *This is protecting them.* Two hundred souls under this roof. Apprentices who knew nothing of Vessany. Old Marta in the memory-cellar who would hang before she surrendered her stock, and would take three inquisitors with her. Sef, who was already gone—rode out after Paloma the morning after the courier collapsed, contract be damned, and Corvane had let him go without a word, which was its own small entry in the ledger of his sins.

If he gave Vey nothing, Vey would take everything, and take it badly. Inquisitors did not audit gently. People burned in cellars. Charters dissolved into arrests.

If he gave Vey the list—the lullabies, the doors, the dusty reliquary of a dead nation—the guild survived intact. The relics were only relics. Memory in bottles keeps poorly anyway.

That was the arithmetic. It was clean. It balanced.

The problem was Paloma Brightwater, who had come to him at fourteen with storm-scars on her knuckles and nothing in the world but a false name and quick hands, and whom he had taught to read a ledger, to weigh a memory against its market price, to survive in margins. Nine years he had watched her erase herself entry by entry, smaller and smaller on the page, and he had called it mentorship. He had called it protection.

He had never once written down that he loved her like a daughter, because Corvane did not record liabilities he had no intention of paying.

"The courier," Corvane said. "Before he died. He gave the map to no one else?"

"He gave it to her." Vey's eyes were pale and patient. "You know that. You watched it happen. Which means you also know where she went, or can guess within a day's ride—and Master Corvane, I want to be clear about what happens next. If I find her without your help, I will still find your cooperation... incomplete. And incomplete cooperation is a category I file under non-compliance."

There it was, then. No room even for the fiction of reluctance. Vey wanted the betrayal acknowledged as a transaction, because Vey believed—Corvane could see it in that flat grey patience—that betrayal was simply accurate accounting. A correction of sentiment against fact.

Corvane thought of Paloma at sixteen, falling asleep over the ledgers, and covering for her hours so the other clerks wouldn't mock her. He thought of teaching her the cipher they used for impossible inventory, and how fast she'd learned it—faster than him, though he'd never said so. He thought of the storm nine years ago, and the trial, and how he had stood in the gallery and said nothing, because saying something would have cost him the guild, and he had told himself then too: *this is protecting my traders.*

Nine years of that sentence. It had corroded something in him the way water corrodes iron—slowly, invisibly, and all the way through.

"The coast road," Corvane heard himself say. "She'll have gone to ground past Emberfall. There's a keeper at the old light who knew her mother. If she's following that map—and she'll follow it, she's her mother's daughter—it goes through drowned country next. The archive under the fen, if it still stands."

Vey took out a small notebook and wrote it down. Wrote it down in front of him, unhurried, the way you enter a confirmed debt.

"The charter survives," Vey said. "The purge skips this house. Consider us concluded." He picked up his writ from the scale, and paused at the door. "You did the sensible thing, Master Corvane. Most men can't. That's why most men are poor." Then he was gone, and the inquisitors with him, and the weighing room smelled faintly of cold iron.

* * *

Corvane climbed to the counting room and did not allow himself to sit.

Instead he opened the great ledger—the true one, the one no auditor had ever seen, where the guild recorded what it actually traded in—and turned to the page he had kept hidden from Vey's inventory, hidden from everyone, for nine years.

Entry the first: *Brightwater, Maren. One secret, deposited. Contents withheld from depositor's daughter until such time as she asks the right question.*

Her mother had come to him a week before the storm. Before the trial, before the exile, before everything. Maren Brightwater had left a sealed deposit—one page, warded shut, payable only to Paloma, and only against a question Paloma hadn't yet known to ask.

Corvane had promised to keep it. Through the purge, through the trial, through nine years of the girl shrinking herself into margins, he had kept it. It was, he had told himself, the one honest asset in a dishonest life.

And today he had sold the daughter to protect the vault that held the mother's last words.

His hand hovered over the page. One line of ink and it could burn. One candle, one match, and Vey's inquisitors would find ash instead of answers, and whatever Maren had tried to save would die with the guild.

But burning it wouldn't save Paloma. It would only blind her. And Corvane had done enough blinding in her name.

So instead he did the only thing left to a man whose love had become a liability: he wrote. Beneath Maren's sealed entry, in his smallest hand, in the old cipher only Paloma could read:

*You will learn what I did. Do not forgive it. Take the deposit. It was always yours. Ask the question: who paid the storm?*

He blotted the page. Folded a copy small enough to hide in a coin-purse, and rang for a boy.

"Ride for the fen country," he told him. "Find a smuggler named Sef traveling with a red-haired woman. Give him this. Tell no one—not the inquisitors, not me, no one—or the charter dies and everyone under this roof with it."

The boy went. Corvane stood at the window watching the harbor gray over, and understood with perfect clarity that he had just committed his final trade: the guild's survival purchased with a friend's location, and the only thing he had left of value—Maren's secret—sent chasing after a girl who would be right never to forgive him.

Somewhere east, a storm was gathering over a road called Emberfall.

Corvane recognized the weather. He had helped invent it.
