## 6. Emberfall Road

The lighthouse keeper's daughter gave us bread and a warning, and we took both without paying for either.

"The road past Emberfall's watched," she said, wrapping the loaf in cloth like it was a sleeping child. "Inquisitors ride in pairs now. Grey cloaks. They ask after a woman with a mark on her arm."

Paloma pulled her sleeve down over the sigil and said, "Then they'll be looking a long while."

The girl—sixteen maybe, with her father's sea-weathered squint—didn't believe her. Paloma didn't blame her. She didn't quite believe herself either.

Sef was waiting at the crossroads with two horses that had clearly been liberated from someone who'd miss them. He looked the same as he always did in the guild yard: lean, unhurried, a scar through one eyebrow he claimed came from a chandelier. But there was something new in his face when he saw her coming down the cliff path—something she hadn't seen from anyone in nine years.

"You're late," he said.

"I was being followed."

"I know. I shook them at the fish market." He handed her the reins of the bay mare. "You owe me a contract fee, by the way. Corvane had me booked to run silk down the coast until midwinter."

"Break your contracts often?"

"Never." He swung up into his saddle. "First time for everything. Try not to make a habit of deserving it."

They rode east before dawn, out of the salt wind and into country that smelled of pine and turned earth instead of brine. The map rode against Paloma's ribs, inked in dragon-fire, warm as a second heartbeat. Every few miles a new line would surface on it—a road, a river, a name burned so faintly she had to tilt it toward the light—and every name made her chest hurt in a way she couldn't account for. *Vellum Ford. The Ashgrove Tollhouse.* Places that weren't in any atlas. Places that had been taken out of the world on purpose.

"You could tell me what we're riding toward," Sef said around midmorning.

"I could."

"That's not a no, but it's not an answer either."

Paloma kept her eyes on the road. "There's a country that doesn't exist anymore. I'm the key to its door. There's a magistrate who wants me because dead keys open nothing, and a guildmaster who raised me and may have already sold me. That's the shape of it. The rest is arithmetic."

Sef was quiet for half a mile. Then: "Corvane sold you?"

"He watched a courier die in his counting room and never blinked. And he knew my mother. He never told me."

"That's not selling. That's just him being Corvane."

"It's the first installment," Paloma said. "I've audited enough ledgers to know how debts begin."

* * *

Emberfall Road earned its name at dusk. The pines gave way to blackened fields where a fire had passed years ago and never fully apologized—scorched trunks standing like witnesses, grass growing back green and wrong against all that char. A village huddled at the road's bend: a dozen houses, a well, and a shrine at the crossroads, small and old, its stones carved with spirals that Paloma's eyes snagged on like a nail catching cloth.

She knew those spirals.

She'd never seen them before, and she knew them.

"We stop here?" Sef asked.

"We water the horses and keep moving. Villages remember faces."

But the village remembered something else first. As they drew rein at the well, an old woman looked up from her doorway, looked at Paloma, and dropped the pot she was holding. It shattered. She didn't scream. She just stood there with her hands still shaped around nothing, staring, and said in a voice like dry paper:

"Brightwater."

Paloma's blood went cold. "You know me?"

"I knew your mother." The old woman crossed herself with three fingers, left to right, some rite Paloma had never learned. "Gods keep us. You have her face when the weather comes."

"The weather isn't—"

The sky answered before she could.

It started as pressure. Paloma felt it in the sigil first—a heat unspooling under her skin like a wire drawn tight—and then in her teeth, her ears, the horses. The bay mare screamed and reared. Down the road, where the tree line thinned, grey cloaks. Two of them. Four. Six, fanning out between the burnt trunks with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this many times and never once lost.

"Inquisitors," Sef said. "Off the horse. Off the horse *now*."

They ran for the shrine. Paloma didn't decide to; her legs decided for her, some instinct older than sense telling her the carved stone was cover, was sanctuary, was *hers*. She reached it with Sef a stride behind and put her back against the spiral-carved rock as the first crossbow bolt buried itself in the wood beside her head.

"Paloma." Sef's hand closed on her wrist—the marked wrist—and his voice went very calm, the way it did when he talked down customs officers. "Whatever you're about to do, think about whether you can do it small."

"I don't do anything," she said. "That's the whole point. Nine years, I've done *nothing*. I've kept my head down and my hands empty and—"

The sigil woke.

It wasn't pain. It was worse: it was *recognition*. Something under her skin opened like an eye that had been closed since she was nineteen, and everything above it—the whole bruised, low-slung sky—opened in answer.

The storm didn't build. It arrived.

Wind hit the road sideways, ripping needles off the surviving pines, and lightning came down in a sheet rather than a strike, white and total, and Paloma heard herself screaming but couldn't tell if it was terror or command. The rain fell upward first. Then it fell everywhere at once. She could feel the storm the way she could feel her own hand—every drop, every gust, every charge crawling along the ground looking for the tallest thing—and the tallest things were the inquisitors' spears, and the storm *knew*, and she couldn't stop it knowing—

"No," she gasped. "*Not them. Not—*"

Too late. Lightning walked down the road on legs of white fire, hunting iron. Men scattered. Horses bolted, six riderless animals tearing past the village like a prophecy. And the storm, drunk on nine years of rationing itself, kept spending.

"Sef!" she shouted. "Get them clear! The houses—"

He was already moving, hauling the old woman away from her doorway, shouting villagers toward the well house. Paloma stayed pinned to the shrine because moving meant letting go and letting go meant the storm would go wherever it liked, which was everywhere.

That was when she understood what it wanted.

Not the men. Not the road. The *shrine*. The spirals under her palm were drinking her, channeling her, and the stone was old and the storm was young and the stone was going to lose.

"*No.*" She pressed both hands flat against the carvings. "Not this. This is hers. This is *Vessany's*. You don't get to take—" She didn't know who she was talking to. The storm, the sigil, whatever of her mother lived in the marks on her arm. "You don't get to take anything else from me!"

She pulled inward. It was like hauling a drowning animal out of deep water by main strength—every muscle of her mind straining against a force that outweighed her ten thousand times. The lightning bent. The wind screamed around the shrine in a circle, a wall of rain and fury held off the carved stone by nothing but her refusal, and somewhere inside it all Paloma felt something tear—not flesh, not bone, something ledger-shaped, some final page of the person she'd spent nine years writing herself into being.

Then it was over.

The storm collapsed inward and died, all at once, like a held breath released. Rain drifted down gently, absurdly, washing the ash off the black fields. The silence afterward rang.

The shrine still stood. Barely. Its crown of spiral stones was gone—sheared clean off, scattered across the road in pieces no bigger than coins—and the crossroads around it looked scoured, swept by a giant's thumb. Of the inquisitors: nothing visible. Alive or dead, gone or hiding, the road was empty except for wreckage and rain.

Paloma slid down the shrine's broken side until she was sitting in the mud, shaking, her hands blistered where the stone had fought back.

Sef crouched in front of her. He was soaked through, bleeding from a cut on his brow, and he looked at her—at the sigil, still faintly glowing under her wet sleeve, fading like a coal settling—and he didn't step back.

That was the thing. She waited for it. Everyone always stepped back. Corvane had stepped back the moment the courier died, recalculating. Vey had stepped back nine years ago, all the way to a writ of exile. Even her mother, in the stories Paloma allowed herself, had finally stepped back into fire and myth.

Sef knelt down in the mud next to her and said, "Can you stand?"

"That's it? That's all you have to say?"

"For now." He shrugged. "Rest of it can wait till you're upright."

She laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, but it came out. "I nearly killed a village, Sef. I broke their shrine. Their grandmother knew my mother. I'm a weapon somebody aimed at this road, and you're kneeling in the floodwater asking if I can *stand*."

"Yeah." He tore a strip off his already ruined cloak and started binding her blistered hands, clumsy but careful, like she was a shipment worth wrapping proper. "Because in about four minutes those inquisitors are going to come back with friends, and I can't carry you and fight."

"They might be dead."

"Then we robbed them of the pleasure." He tied off the knot and met her eyes. "Paloma. Listen. Whatever that was—I'm not afraid of you. I want that on the record. Guild-trained witness, full attestation. Not afraid."

"You should be."

"Probably." He grinned, bloody and tired. "Bad judgment's kind of my signature move. Broke a silk contract for you, didn't I?"

The old woman approached through the drizzle. Paloma braced for the three-fingered sign against evil, the spit, the curse. Instead the woman studied the broken shrine for a long moment, then knelt and picked up one of the fallen spiral-stones and pressed it into Paloma's blistered palm.

"She carried one of these too," the woman said quietly. "When she left. Said if the weather ever found her again, she wanted a piece of home in her fist." She closed Paloma's fingers around it. "Now it's found you. Go finish whatever she started, girl. We'll rebuild. We always rebuild."

Paloma sat in the mud of a village she'd half-destroyed, holding a piece of a country that didn't exist, and felt the last of her exile crumble. Nine years of margins and ledgers and small erasures, all of it built on the story that she was dangerous only by accident, that the storm had merely used her name.

The storm hadn't used her name.

It had been waiting for her to say it herself.

She got up. Sef got up with her. Down the darkening road, the dragon-fire map warmed against her ribs and a new line burned itself onto the vellum, fine and fresh:

*A drowned archive lies beneath the Ashgrove weir. Bring your true name.*

"Your true name," Sef read over her shoulder. "What's that mean?"

Paloma looked at the spiral-stone in her fist, then at the broken shrine, then at the east, where the rain was clearing off land that had no business remembering her.

"It means," she said, swinging back into the saddle, "that I've been signing the wrong thing for nine years."
