## 8. Doors Close Inward

The Magistrate's office in Saltmere had one window, and Halden Vey kept it shuttered. Light was for men who wanted to be seen. He preferred to work the way erasure worked: quietly, thoroughly, from the inside out.

On his desk lay three objects. A writ of extinguishment, sealed with wax the color of old blood. A map fragment recovered from a dead courier, its dragon-fire ink still faintly warm after six days — an impossibility he intended to have catalogued and then destroyed. And a ledger page, thin as a moth's wing, that his inquisitors had lifted from the Guild of Impossible Things at dawn.

He read it again while the city woke below him, indifferent as always to what was being done in its name.

*Brightwater, P. — debt of nine years' lodging, carried forward. Note appended in a different hand: paid in full.*

Paid in full. Corvane had signed it himself. The guildmaster had known — had perhaps always known — what the girl was carrying under her skin, and instead of delivering her to the Crown, he'd spent nine years teaching her arithmetic.

Vey did not feel anger. Anger was a luxury for people who hadn't planned thirty years ahead. He felt something closer to disappointment, which was colder and lasted longer.

He rang the small brass bell that sat at his elbow, once.

Inquisitor Dain entered the way Vey had trained all his people to enter: without sound, without curiosity, without any visible opinion about the shutters or the dark.

"Guildmaster Corvane," Vey said. "Is he settled?"

"He arrived an hour ago, Magistrate. He's been offered tea. He hasn't touched it."

"Of course he hasn't. He knows better than to accept anything from us." Vey squared the ledger page against the desk's edge. "Bring him in. Gently. I want him comfortable. Comfortable men make honest mistakes."

* * *

Corvane looked older than his file photographs, which pleased Vey in a petty way he allowed himself. Power was easier to negotiate with when it had begun to sag. The guildmaster sat in the visitor's chair with his hands folded on his knee like a man waiting for a ferry, and his eyes went straight to the ledger page on the desk, then away, then back — the flicker of a man confirming a wound.

"You took my counting room apart," Corvane said. "Sixty years of inventory. Bottled lullabies. Names people sold so they could start over."

"We inventoried them. Nothing's been destroyed." Vey folded his hands to mirror the old man's. "Yet. You know how we prefer to work, Corvane. Nothing destroyed that can still be useful."

"I know exactly how you work." Corvane's voice was gravel poured slow. "I've been selling you impossible things for twenty years. Doors. Memories. One country, whole and entire, that your predecessors decided had never existed." He nodded at the ledger page. "You want the girl."

"The Crown wants the key. The girl is simply where the key lives."

"And if she refuses to be where the key lives?"

Vey rose and crossed to the shuttered window, more for the pleasure of making the old man wait than for any need to stand. "Then she dies with the key inside her, and Vessany stays closed forever, and everyone involved has wasted thirty years. I would rather avoid that. Dead keys open nothing. I told my inquisitors to bring her in gently. You may consider this conversation part of the gentle approach."

"She's not yours to bring in."

"No one is anyone's, guildmaster. That is the entire philosophy of order." Vey turned. "But let's not waste each other's morning with philosophy. Here is what happens now. Your charter survives. Every trader in your guild keeps their license, their immunity, their little empire of smuggled second chances. In exchange, you give me her location."

Corvane laughed. It was a real laugh, rusty, almost fond. "You already have my ledgers. You already know I traded her. That's why you pulled the page — to see whether I'd hidden anything else."

"I know you *sold* her," Vey corrected mildly. "There's a difference between selling and hiding, and you've done both, which makes you either sentimental or strategic. I've spent my career assuming men are strategic until proven otherwise. Prove me otherwise and I'll have you transported before lunch."

Silence. The tea went cold on its tray. Somewhere below, a cart driver cursed another cart driver, and the ordinary world ground on, unaware that its shape was being decided in a dark room above it.

"There's a page missing," Corvane said at last. "You noticed."

"Of course I noticed. A ledger with a missing page is a confession wearing a coat."

"It wasn't a location." The old man leaned forward, and for a moment the sag went out of him and Vey saw the trader who had built the Guild of Impossible Things out of other people's discarded lives. "It was a price. Nine years ago, a woman came to my guild with a newborn and a sigil already burned into the child's shoulder, and she asked me to keep the child's name out of every record I touched. She paid everything she had. I gave my word." His jaw worked. "I'm giving you my word now, magistrate: that page doesn't say where Paloma Brightwater is. It says who she is. And you don't have it, because some debts aren't yours to collect."

Vey considered this. Considered, too, the strange ache of hearing a smuggler speak of honor as though it were currency he'd actually held.

"You loved her mother," he said. "Or something adjacent to love. It doesn't matter. What matters is arithmetic." He returned to his desk and laid the map fragment flat beside the writ. "Your courier reached the girl. The courier died carrying a map drawn in dragon-fire — the last of it, we believe, in existence. The map leads to a door. The door leads to Vessany. And Vessany, guildmaster, is the single largest unsolved problem in the history of this kingdom."

"A country that isn't there. You must be very proud."

"I am very *tired*," Vey said, and meant it, and watched the honesty land like a blow because Corvane had expected performance and gotten a man instead. "Do you know what erasure costs? Not the burning — burning is cheap. It's the maintenance. Thirty years of forged atlases. Forty thousand records rewritten by hand. Two generations of clerks taught to flinch at a name they've never heard. Erasure is not an act, Corvane. It is a discipline. And disciplines fail the moment someone leaves a door open."

"So close it."

"It *is* closing. That's the problem." Vey tapped the map fragment, and the fire-ink shimmered, resentful, alive. "This map is keyed to the girl's sigil. Which means the door is keyed to the girl's sigil. Which means the last living seal on Vessany is walking around loose in my kingdom with a courier's map in her pocket and — if your sentimentality is any guide — a growing conviction that she deserves the truth." He smiled, thin and professional. "She'll go to the door. People always do. They can't help it. Give a creature a homeland and tell her it's forbidden, and she will cross burning roads to stand in front of it."

"And when she opens it?"

"Then we go through after her, and we finish what my predecessors started — properly, this time. No half-burned archives. No surviving ember. No witnesses." Vey slid the writ across the desk and held out a pen. "Your charter. My restraint. Her location. Sign."

Corvane didn't move. "You're asking me to sell her twice."

"I'm asking you to choose which version of her survival you'd like. There's the one where she runs, and my inquisitors find her tired and alone on some road, and 'gently' stops being an instruction I gave. Or there's the one where you tell me where she's going, and I arrive first, and she walks into Vessany's last day escorted by the only man in this kingdom disciplined enough not to enjoy it." He set the pen down in front of the old man. "Sentiment got your courier killed, guildmaster. Sentiment gets everyone killed eventually. Order just does it tidier."

For a long moment Corvane stared at the pen as though it might bite. Then he picked it up, and Vey understood — with the small cold satisfaction of a man whose models of human nature rarely fail — that whatever the guildmaster had hidden on the missing page, he valued his traders' necks more.

He wrote four words and a coordinate.

*Emberfall. The roost. She's there.*

* * *

They rode within the hour — Vey, twelve inquisitors, and a wagon bearing the instruments of extinguishment: salt-iron, null-glass, the long tongs used for handling things that refused to stay dead. The road climbed out of Saltmere into hill country gone gold with late summer, and Vey rode with the map fragment open on his knee, watching the fire-ink crawl.

That was the thing no one had warned his predecessors about, back when they'd voted a country out of existence. Dragon-fire ink remembered. It redrew itself. It found its final coordinate the way water finds its level — patiently, inevitably, without anyone's permission. All morning the lines had been migrating eastward, converging, and now they had stopped, and the last mark sat fixed on the page like a pupil dilating.

Vey held the map up against the horizon and matched it to the black tooth of mountain rising above the tree line.

"She's already there," said Inquisitor Dain, reining in beside him. There was something in the young man's voice that might, in a less well-trained throat, have been awe.

"Of course she is." Vey rolled the map closed. "The door calls its key. It's been calling for nine years; she simply couldn't hear it over the sound of her own guilt. Now the courier's map has turned the volume up." He nudged his horse forward. "We ride through the night. If the ember is still lit when we arrive, we put it out. If the door is still shut, we hold it shut. And if the girl—"

He paused. Below them, far off across the valley, weather was gathering over Emberfall Road. Not forecast weather. Not seasonal weather. A column of cloud standing up out of clear air like something answering to a name, and even at this distance Vey's teeth ached with the pressure of it.

"—if the girl has begun to understand what she is," he finished quietly, "then we are already late."

He spurred the horse downhill toward the storm, the writ of extinguishment dry and official against his chest, and told himself — as he had told himself for thirty years, through forty thousand rewritten pages — that what he carried was not cruelty.

It was the highest form of order. Everything in its place. Everything in its place, and the places themselves forgotten.

Behind him, the clouds over Emberfall Road began, very softly, to rain upward.
