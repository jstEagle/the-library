## 9. The Last Gutter of Dragon-Fire

The roost was not what the stories promised.

No mountain of gold. No cathedral of bone. Just a hollow in the cliffs above Emberfall Road where the rock had been scorched so many times it had turned to glass, and in the middle of that glass lay the last dragon of Vessany, small as a fallen oak and dim as a banked hearth. Its scales had gone the color of old ash. When it breathed, the air shimmered once, weakly, like heat off a summer road, and stopped.

Sef stood at the mouth of the hollow with his hand on his knife, though a knife against a dragon—even this one—was a prayer said in the wrong language.

"It's dying," he said.

"Everything from Vessany is dying." I walked down into the glass. It rang faintly under my boots, a sound like a struck bell heard through water. "That's rather the point."

The map had led me here by burning itself away, coordinate by coordinate, until all that remained was a single line of dragon-fire script across my palm: *HERE.* The sigil beneath my skin had been singing since dawn—not the wild, frightened flare of the ambush on Emberfall Road, but something steadier. A tuning fork struck against bone.

The dragon opened one eye.

It was the color of the ember at the heart of a fire, and it looked at me the way an accountant looks at a ledger he already knows is balanced.

"Brightwater," it said. Its voice was not a roar. It was a creak, a hinge long unoiled, a voice that had been saving itself for nine years. "You have her hands."

"You knew my mother."

"I knew everyone who came to the door. That was my work. I was the door's keeper before I was its lock." The eye closed, then opened again, as if even blinking cost too much. "Come closer, little ledger-keeper. I want to see the sigil."

I should have been afraid. Nine years ago I would have been. But I had spent those nine years among impossible things, and I had learned that the most dangerous ones are never the largest—they're the ones that ask you a question you've been avoiding.

I knelt beside the great gray head. Up close I could smell it: hot stone and rain on slate, the smell of my childhood before the exile, before the ledgers, before the name Brightwater became something people whispered instead of greeted.

The sigil woke fully then. Not pain—*recognition*. The mark on my skin, the storm-brand they'd sentenced me for, uncurled along my forearm like a fern finding light, and the dragon made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could rust.

"Yes," it breathed. "There it is. The key."

"Open the door," I said. "Restore Vessany. That's what the map was for. That's why the courier died getting it to me."

The ember-light in the dragon's eye guttered. "And you think that is mercy?"

"I think it's justice."

"They are not the same thing, child, and your country died of confusing them." The dragon shifted, and glass sang under its weight. "Let me show you what you would open."

It did not breathe fire. It breathed *memory*, and I was inside Vessany again—but not the Vessany of my mother's lullabies. This was Vessany whole: the harbor cities with their tide-bells, the archive cities with their drowned libraries—and the judgment halls, white and windowless, where names were weighed and found light or heavy. Where a family called Brightwater had kept the weather-books for three hundred years, and where the weather-books were law.

Where a woman who called no storm was still tried for one, because her mother before her had called two, and judgment in Vessany ran backward through blood like frost through a wall.

"The door opens both ways," the dragon said, its voice coming from very far away now, from inside the memory. "Open it, and everything returns. The cities. The bells. The archives. And the halls. The judgments. The sentences written against families not yet born. You would bring them home, Paloma Brightwater, and home would put them on the scale."

I pulled out of the memory gasping, on my knees in the glass, Sef shouting something from the hollow's mouth that I couldn't parse over the ringing in my ears.

"There's another way," I said.

"There is always another way. There has only ever been another way, and no one takes it, because the other way requires giving something up." The dragon's breath was shorter now. Each word cost it visibly, the ember in its eye dimming between syllables. "You can carry my ember out. Out of the country. Out of the seal. Into the world, where it will burn as a story instead of a nation. Vessany ends—not erased, the way Vey ended it, but *finished*. Honestly. With its true history attached, so no one ever has to dig through drowned archives to learn what happened here. And the door closes forever behind you."

"And if I don't?"

"Then when I die—and I die tonight, key or no key—the door seals anyway. Empty. A country locked in the dark with nothing inside it but its own grudges, preserved like flies in amber." The eye fixed on me. "Vey thinks erasure is order. He is wrong. Erasure is just a slower way of lying. But restoration without reckoning—that is a lie told at full volume."

Somewhere below us, far down the cliff road, I heard wheels. Many of them. And the thin, disciplined sound of horses ridden by men who did not love them.

Sef heard it too. "Paloma—"

"How long do I have to decide?"

"The decision was made nine years ago," the dragon said. "In a counting room, when you erased a debtor's name from a page because you decided his debt was not the truth of him. You have been carrying the ember since then, child. I am only asking you to carry it further."

* * *

They came up the cliff path at dusk, torches first, then writs—Halden Vey rode at the front of them with a document case chained to his saddle and the expression of a man arriving to collect on a loan he considered generous.

I stood in the hollow's mouth with the map burned to nothing in my pocket and the sigil blazing down both arms now, and Sef at my shoulder, and between us and the inquisitors lay forty yards of glass and scree.

"Magistrate Vey," I called down. "You should know the door doesn't open for you. It never did. Your writ is paper."

"Writs burn, Miss Brightwater. So do keys." He dismounted, unhurried, brushing ash from his sleeve. "But I confess I hoped it wouldn't come to fire. I let you live nine years. I'd prefer to let you live nine more—in a room, admittedly, but alive. Dead keys open nothing. I said as much to your guildmaster."

"He knows," said a new voice.

Corvane came around the bend of the path below the horses, walking alone, unarmed, in his good coat, the one with the silver thread at the cuffs that he wore to bargain. My stomach dropped through the glass. Of course. He'd sold me once at the weighing scale; selling me twice was only compounding interest.

"Corvane," I said. "You brought them yourself."

"I brought myself." He didn't look at me. He was looking at Vey, and there was something in his face I had seen maybe four times in nine years—the face he wore when a deal went bad and he was deciding how much of himself to spend making it worse for the other party. "Guildmaster Corvane, Magistrate. As agreed. The location of the Brightwater woman, delivered personally, in exchange for the charter."

"Paid in full upon her detention," Vey said.

"No." Corvane climbed onto the glass. His boots rang with every step. "The charter was paid when I gave you the coordinates in writing, which you have. What I'm delivering *now* is different." He stopped between me and the inquisitors, an old man in a good coat standing in front of thirty crossbows, and spread his arms. "What I'm delivering now is the time it takes to go through me."

The hollow went very quiet.

"You would die for her?" Vey asked, genuinely curious, the way he'd probably been curious at the trials, taking notes.

"I raised her," Corvane said. "Which means I spent nine years teaching her that everything has a price, including loyalty. She learned the lesson better than I wanted her to. And now she's standing at a door about to do the one thing I never priced—" his voice cracked, just once, an old hinge—"the one thing that can't be sold. I find I'd like to see it happen. Even from the wrong side of it."

The first bolt took him high in the chest.

I screamed. I don't remember deciding to scream; the sound simply left me, and with it left everything else—the sigil, the sky, the nine-year dam I had built out of bookkeeping and silence.

The storm did not come *down* from the clouds this time. It came *out* of me.

Lightning walked down my arms and leapt. Rain fell upward out of the sea and came back down as hail the size of fists. The wind arrived not as wind but as pressure, a fist of atmosphere that picked up two inquisitors and set them down gently forty feet away—*gently*, because some part of me, even in this, was keeping the books—and tore the torches out like teeth. Thunder rolled off the cliffs in every direction at once, so that the whole coast became the inside of a bell.

And through it all I walked forward, down the glass, toward Halden Vey, who had not run, who had dropped his reins and stood with his document case clutched to his chest like a child's toy, watching the storm with the expression of a man whose entire philosophy has just been audited and found fraudulent.

"This is mine," I said, and my voice came out doubled, thunder under it. "Not inherited. Not sentenced. *Mine.* You wrote that the storm was hers, then hers, then mine—you passed the crime down like an heirloom. But a storm that saves instead of destroys, that chooses its targets—" lightning struck the writs out of the inquisitors' satchels and burned them to lace without touching a single hand—"that one has no entry in your ledgers, Magistrate. Because it isn't a sentence. It's a signature."

Vey's horse bolted. His men were running, or crawling, or lying very still and reconsidering their careers.

Behind me, Sef was kneeling over Corvane, and Corvane was still conscious, barely, laughing—a wet, astonished sound.

"Told you," he wheezed, when I reached him. "Couldn't sell... the unsellable thing..."

"Hold still. Hold *still*, you impossible old man—"

"Ledger-keeper." He caught my wrist, his grip shocking in its strength. "The page I hid. Bottom drawer. Under the charter. Read it after." His eyes were going somewhere past me, up at the storm I had become. "Knew you'd call it... eventually. Just wanted... to see the accounts settle..."

He didn't finish the sentence. The storm held its breath around us, rain hanging in the air like beads on a string, and I knelt in the glass holding the man who had taught me that everything costs, learning at last what he'd paid.

Then the light behind me changed—from gray to gold—and I turned, and the dragon was standing.

It should not have been able to stand. Its legs shook; its scales slid like autumn leaves. But it stood, and it looked down at me through the rain, and the ember in its chest was visible now through the gaps in its armor, a coal the size of a heart, bright enough to read by.

"Choose," it said. "The door closes with me."

I wiped my face. I thought of white judgment halls. I thought of drowned archives and a mother who had died trying to save a country that would have weighed her daughter on the same crooked scale. I thought of a debtor's name, erased, set free.

I stood up.

"Give me the ember," I said. "We're taking the truth outside."
