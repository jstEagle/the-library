## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There are three hundred and twelve distinct flavors a human tongue can be trained to recognize, according to the manual I was given at thirteen, and by nineteen I had catalogued all of them plus four the manual didn't know about. The extra four were mine. I found them in the king's wine cellar, in a cask of elderberry cordial that a well-meaning vintner had sealed with wax treated with something bitter and faintly metallic — not poison, as it turned out, merely bad luck and cheaper wax. But my tongue flagged it before my brain did, and that is the entire profession, distilled: the tongue knows first, and the rest of you catches up.

My name is Cyrus Nakamura. I am the King's Taster of the Valley of Stillness, and tonight, like every night for eleven years, I will eat better than the man I serve.

Not because his cooks are poor — they are excellent, and I say that with the objectivity of a man who has eaten their failures too. But because everything that reaches the king's table reaches my table first, in smaller portions, with more attention. He gets the meal. I get the meaning of the meal. There is a difference, and the difference is survival.

* * *

The Valley of Stillness sits in a fold of mountains that no mapmaker outside has ever bothered to draw, and there is a reason for that: nothing here draws attention. Not armies, not traders, not weather worth complaining about. And not magic.

I want to be precise about this, because visitors always get it wrong. They arrive expecting some visible absence — a grey patch on the world, grass that won't grow, birds falling out of the sky. But the valley isn't wounded. It's *quiet*. Magic doesn't fail here; it simply declines to enter, the way sound declines to enter a room built for silence. Old-timers call it the Stillness like it's a person. The priests call it a blessing. I call it the reason I'm alive, though it took me years to understand why.

A boy who grows up where magic cannot reach learns early that the world's dangers are all mundane. Knives. Falls. Fever. And poison above all — poison being the weapon of people who can't get close enough for knives. In a kingdom where the king lives behind walls and wards elsewhere, poison is the only honest assassination left. So the valley trains tasters the way other places train soldiers. We start young. We start small. And we never, ever stop.

My first dose was arsenic, administered by Master Corvin in a thimble of plum wine when I was nine years old. A grain smaller than a mustard seed. I remember crying, and I remember him watching me cry with an expression I mistook for cruelty and have since come to recognize as arithmetic. He was calculating how much fear I could carry alongside the poison. It turns out the two weigh about the same.

"You'll build a tolerance," he said. "Your body will learn to treat it as weather."

"It hurts," I told him.

"Everything worth surviving does." Then he wrote something in his ledger, and I have spent my whole life wondering whether it was praise.

* * *

The evening meal arrived at half past six, as it always did, borne up the kitchen stair by Tam, the youngest of the under-stewards, who carried the covered trays with the exaggerated reverence of a boy who still believed kings were interesting.

"Roast capon," he announced, lifting each lid like a priest revealing relics. "Bitter greens dressed in walnut oil. Bread — the dark loaf, not the white. And the honeyed pears, which Cook says are perfect and which Cook says every night, so."

"Cook is usually right about pears," I said. "It's her politics that worry me."

Tam grinned and retreated down the stairs, and I was alone with the meal, which is how I prefer to be. Tasting is not eating. Eating is appetite; tasting is translation. You have to read a dish the way a scholar reads a letter from a stranger — alert to what's said, but far more interested in how it's phrased, because the phrasing is where people hide things.

I began with the bread. Dark rye, sour enough to make my jaw ache pleasantly, crumb tight and even. No bitterness at the finish. Bitterness is where poisons like to live — aconite, strychnine, the gentler lies of foxglove — so I hold the last bite on my tongue longer than any polite dinner guest would, and I let it dissolve, and I wait.

Nothing. Good bread, honestly made.

The greens next. Walnut oil can go rancid, and rancidity is a mask; it sits over the tongue like fog and hides whatever walks beneath it. This oil was sweet and clean. I chewed slowly, cataloguing: the green bite of sorrel, the mineral flatness of the valley's soil, salt, a whisper of garlic burned just past gold. Underneath all of it — and this is where eleven years of training earns its keep — underneath, a thin thread of something sharp. Metallic. Cold, almost, the way a coin tastes cold against your teeth.

I set down the fork and waited. Counted to sixty. The thread didn't grow. It didn't move at all.

Arsenic. Micro-dose. Perhaps a fifth of what would trouble me, perhaps a tenth of what would trouble a healthy man who hadn't spent his childhood drinking poisoned wine on purpose.

I took another bite anyway, and let the arithmetic settle in my chest like a stone finding its place in a wall.

Here is the thing outsiders never understand about my work: the poison in the king's food is not an accident, and it is not entirely an attack. Some of it is attack, certainly — the valley has its share of enemies, distant ones who send their malice through travelers and trade goods the way you'd send a letter. But some of it is maintenance. Master Corvin explained it to me once, late, over the ledger. "The king must never be poisoned," he said. "But the king must also never be *unpoisoned*. If we removed every trace, we would be announcing our defenses. So we leave a little. We season the danger into the ordinary. And the taster—" he tapped my chest with one dry finger— "the taster is the proof that the seasoning works."

I finished the capon. Skin crackled with rosemary fat; flesh pulling clean off the bone; no bitterness anywhere, no almond note that might betray cyanide, no soapy film of oleander. I ate a full portion, as required. A taster who nibbles is a taster the kitchen stops trusting, and an untrusted taster is a dead taster twice over — once by whoever wants the king dead, and once by whoever suspects the taster of being them.

Then the pears. Honeyed, as promised, and perfect, as promised. I saved them for last because dessert should be earned, even when you're eating on behalf of another man. Poached until translucent, bathed in honey cut with a splash of vinegar so the sweetness wouldn't cloy, one clove hidden at the bottom of the dish like a small brown secret. I ate the clove too. Cloves can hide things. Nothing was hiding.

Thirty minutes. That is the rule. Thirty minutes after the final dish, alone in the tasting chamber, doing nothing but existing in my own body and listening to it. If the arsenic meant to climb higher than my tolerance allowed, these thirty minutes would tell me. My heart kept its steady time. My hands lay quiet on the table. The candle flame stood straight as a soldier.

Safe.

I rang the small brass bell that meant *the king may eat*, and somewhere below, Tam would take up the trays again, and the King of the Valley of Stillness would dine on food certified by my tongue, and tomorrow I would do it again, and the day after, and the day after that, until either my tongue failed me or the world ran out of patience. I found, sitting there in the candlelight with pear honey still on my lips, that I wanted neither. This is the confession at the heart of my profession, the one tasters aren't supposed to make even to ourselves: I love it. The precision. The quiet. The certainty that safety is not luck but skill, and skill is not given but built, grain by grain, dose by dose, year by year. Other men pray to gods I've never met. I practice. It has never yet failed to answer.

* * *

After the bell, I did what I always do: I wrote the evening's entry in the ledger. Master Corvin's ledger, now mine — he died four winters ago, mid-meal, of a heart that simply quit, and I have sometimes wondered if the irony occurred to him in his last moment. Probably not. Irony was wasted on Corvin.

*Capon, roast — clear. Greens, sorrel and walnut oil — clear. Rye loaf — clear. Pears, honeyed — clear.* Then, in the column reserved for findings: *Arsenic, trace, 1/5 tolerated dose. Consistent with Tuesday's delivery. Source likely the eastern miller again.*

The eastern miller. Third time this season. Either he was sloppy with his stores or someone was paying him, and either way I'd written the same observation three times now, and three observations make a pattern, and patterns are the one thing in this valley I am empowered to act on. Tomorrow I would walk to the eastern mill, taste the air around his grain sacks, and have a conversation that would ruin someone's week. I confess I looked forward to it slightly. A man needs a hobby.

Dusk came down over the valley the way it always did — slow, blue, without drama. Through the tasting chamber's narrow window I could see the mountains going dark, and beyond the pass, the world I had never seen: the kingdom proper, with its capital and its court and its magic, all of it as real to me as stories. I had crossed the valley's edge exactly once, at fourteen, standing precisely on the boundary line with one foot in Stillness and one foot out, feeling absolutely nothing change, and concluding that the whole business was ceremony. Master Corvin had pulled me back by the collar and said, "The border doesn't announce itself, boy. That's rather the point of borders." I thought he was being mystical. I have since decided he was being literal, which in my experience is far more frightening.

I was trimming the candle when I heard the gate horn.

That sound means a traveler at the outer post — rare enough. What it doesn't mean, what it has never once meant in my eleven years, is the second horn that followed it, shorter, ragged, blown badly by someone whose breath was failing them. I know what effort sounds like. I hear it in a cook's knife-work, in a servant trying not to sob while carrying trays. That horn was a man or woman playing music with the last of their strength.

I went down the stairs faster than dignity allows.

The outer courtyard was torchlit and crowded with the night watch, and in the middle of it, half off a lathered horse, hung a figure in travel-stained riding leathers with the dust of far roads — real roads, kingdom roads — printed on her in mud and worse. She got one boot free of the stirrup and the ground took her the rest of the way. The watch closed around her, and I should have stayed back. Tasters don't run to couriers. Tasters stand near tables.

But she lifted her head as they turned her over, and her eyes found mine across the courtyard with a certainty that raised every hair I own, and she said — not shouted, *said*, in a voice scraped down to wire:

"Nakamura. Cyrus Nakamura."

Nobody in the valley used my family name. Nobody in the valley knew I *had* a family name; I'd been Cyrus since Corvin signed my apprenticeship, and before that I'd been nobody's concern at all. I had assumed, in the casual way one assumes such things, that *Nakamura* was a word I'd invented.

She knew it. She knew it the way you know your own name, the way you know a taste you grew up with.

The watch captain was already waving me off — "Back inside, taster, this is watch business" — but the woman's hand shot out and caught my sleeve, and her grip was astonishing, iron wrapped in ice, the grip of someone spending coins she couldn't afford.

"There's no time," she breathed. Blood was coming up between her words now, pink froth at the corner of her mouth, and some cold professional part of my mind noted it — lung, deep lung, hours at most, probably minutes — while the rest of me stood frozen like a boy with his foot on a boundary line. "Listen. Listen to me. The crown eats him. Do you understand? It *eats* him—"

"The crown?" I managed. "Whose crown? Our king doesn't—"

"Not yours." Her fingers tightened. "Yours is safe. Yours was always safe. That's why—" and then the blood took the rest, and her eyes rolled toward something I couldn't follow, and the watch captain called for the physician, and her hand slid from my sleeve.

But her other hand had found mine in the fall, and pressed into my palm was something hard-edged and leather-wrapped, folded small, still warm from her body.

I closed my hand around it. I don't know why. Every rule I have ever been taught says surrender strange objects to the watch, log them, catalogue them, file them beside the arsenic entries in columns and rows. Safety is a skill, and skills are procedures.

But I looked down at that leather shape in my fist, warm as a living thing, and I understood suddenly and completely that I was not going to give it to anyone. The knowledge arrived the way flavors do — before thought, before permission — and it settled into me with the terrible rightness of a poison recognized.

The courier's chest hitched once. Twice. The physician was pushing through the crowd, too late by any measure I trusted.

Her eyes came back to mine one last time, and they were not afraid, and somehow that frightened me more than anything else in the courtyard.

"Keep it," she whispered. "He'll know what to do. *You'll* know."

And then the Valley of Stillness, which takes everything quietly, took her too.
