## 2. What the Dying Carry

There is an order to a man's evening when that man tastes poison for a living. You wake at the same hour. You take your barley porridge plain, because anything else muddies the tongue. You walk the kitchen corridor at the same pace, greet the same under-cooks, decline the same friendly offers of wine. By the time the king's supper arrives on its cedar trolley, you have already catalogued every smell in the room and found them all boring, and boredom, I can tell you, is the finest seasoning a taster ever gets.

The courier broke my order the way a stone breaks a window — suddenly, loudly, and from outside.

She came through the west gate at dusk, which is wrong for three reasons. Couriers come at dawn. The west gate serves the orchard carts, not riders. And no one rides into the Valley of Stillness looking like *that* — slumped forward over her horse's neck, one hand fisted in its mane, the other pressed against her side as though she were trying to keep something in that had very strong opinions about getting out.

I was on the kitchen steps with a cup of chicory I wasn't supposed to have, committing the small nightly crime of tasting something merely because it pleased me. The first thing I registered was the horse: lathered, blowing, its eyes rolled white. The second thing was the blood. It had soaked through her left side and dripped onto the cobbles in a slow, patient rhythm, tap, tap, tap, like a cook testing a melon.

"Someone get the physician," I called, and even as I said it I was moving, because you do not wait for permission when a body is failing in front of you. That part of me worked fine. The rest of me had gone strangely quiet.

We got her down onto the steps. She was older than I'd first thought — fifty, perhaps more, gray coming in at her temples, sunburned in the particular way of people who spend their lives on roads instead of roofs. There was dirt ground into her knuckles and ink stains on her fingers, the kind that come from drafting, from hunching over charts for hours.

And beneath all the blood and dust, something about her face —

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the only luxury this story has left me. I *knew* her. Not the way you know a face from the market. Deeper than that. The way you know a smell from a house you lived in before you could talk. Some curve of her jaw, some set of her mouth, rang a bell in a room of my mind I didn't know existed, and the bell kept ringing after everything else went still.

Her eyes opened. They found mine with terrible precision, the way a dying creature's eyes will narrow the whole world down to the one thing it came to do.

"Cyrus," she said.

My name. Not "taster." Not "you there." My name, in a voice cracked thin as old varnish, spoken like she'd carried it a long way and had nearly dropped it several times.

"I — yes," I said, uselessly, kneeling in someone else's blood. "You're hurt, we've sent for—"

Her hand shot out and caught my wrist. For a dying woman she had a grip like a winch. She pulled something from inside her coat — a leather case, long and flat, the kind surveyors carry maps in, scarred at the corners and stained dark along one edge where the blood had reached it — and pressed it into my hands, folding my fingers over it with both of hers.

"The crown," she whispered. "The crown eats him."

"What?" I said. Because it was the only word I had. "Who? What crown?"

Her mouth moved again. I leaned close enough to feel her breath fail against my cheek, close enough to catch the smell of her — road dust, horse sweat, and underneath it, faintly, absurdly, rosemary. Someone had cooked with rosemary. Once. Somewhere. The thought arrived whole and uninvited, and it made my throat close.

"You were made for—" she managed, and then the sentence simply stopped, mid-air, unfinished forever, and her grip on my wrists went from iron to nothing, and the bell in that unknown room of my mind rang once more and fell silent.

* * *

Here is what happens after a person dies on the palace steps, in a valley where nothing has happened for two hundred years: everyone panics quietly and blames the nearest person holding something.

I was the nearest person holding something.

"Taster." Sergeant Hale's voice, behind me. Hale is a good man with a bad imagination, which makes him excellent at routine and disastrous at anything else. "Step away from her."

I stepped away. I did not step away empty-handed, and I want it noted by whatever celestial clerk keeps record of my stupidities that I noticed I hadn't let go of the case only after Hale's eyes dropped to it and narrowed.

"What's that?"

"A cup," I said. "For chicory."

It was not a cup for chicory. It was possibly the worst lie I have told in a life otherwise distinguished by never needing to lie, and Hale looked at the leather case, then at the dead woman, then at me, and his bad imagination began, at last, to earn its keep.

"She asked for me," I said, more carefully. "By name. She gave me this before she died. I don't know what it is."

"Nobody comes to the valley asking for the taster," Hale said. "Nobody comes to the valley at all."

He had me there. That is rather the point of a valley whose border turns magic to mist and keeps the world's troubles on the far side of the hills. People come in by invitation or birth, and they leave almost never. A bleeding stranger who knew my name and died pressing parcels into my hands was not merely unusual; she was about as possible as snow in high summer.

They took me to the guardroom, which I permitted, because refusing would have been worse, and they took the case from me, which I did not permit, and which happened anyway, and I remember watching it leave my hands with an emotion so violent and so irrational that for a moment I genuinely could not breathe. It felt less like losing an object and more like someone peeling skin off my palm.

Calm down, I told myself. It's a map case. You don't even know what's in it. You are a man whose greatest professional crisis to date involved a suspicious oyster.

But my hands shook anyway, all night, while Hale and his sergeant-of-sergeants asked their questions in circles. Who was she. How did she know my name. Had I seen her before. Had I left my post that evening. Did I handle her food. Did I handle *her*.

That last question had a flavor to it I recognized professionally, because I have spent my life detecting poisons, and suspicion is one. It enters quietly. It has no smell. By the time you taste it, it is already in your blood.

"No," I said, to most of it. "I don't know," to the rest. Both answers were true and neither was believed, and I understood, sitting in that lamplit room with my empty hands, that the machinery of my safe little world had developed a crack, and things were leaking out of it, and worse, things were leaking *in*.

They kept me under watch. They kept the case under stronger watch, locked in the evidence chest with the key around Hale's neck.

I did not sleep. I lay on the guardroom bench and listened to the valley's perfect silence — no owls, remember; nothing wild survives near the ward — and I turned the courier's last words over like a coin I couldn't read.

*The crown eats him.*

Which crown? We had exactly one king, and he wore exactly one crown, a modest gold thing that I had seen perhaps four hundred times across the width of a dining table, usually between the fourth course and the fifth. Kings' crowns sit on kings' heads. They do not eat anyone. Language does that sometimes — reaches for a metaphor when the truth is too large for its pocket — but the woman had not sounded figurative. She had sounded like a person reporting an emergency in progress.

And: *you were made for.* Made for what? Half-sentences are the cruelest inheritance. Give a man a whole curse and at least he knows what shape his ruin is.

Somewhere past midnight, a new thought arrived, quiet and cold, and it was this: nobody had asked the obvious question. Nobody had said, how did a rider cross the ward? Nothing crosses the ward. Magic dies at our borders and so, mostly, does intent. Whatever brought her here had been strong enough, or desperate enough, or *meant* enough to come through anyway, and the valley's guardsmen were standing around discussing whether I'd poisoned her as if the interesting mystery were the corpse's last handshake and not the impossibility of her arrival at all.

My order was broken. I could taste it in the air, in Hale's careful questions, in the silence where the physician should have been — he'd examined her, pronounced a blade wound, and left quickly, the way people leave rooms containing problems larger than their salaries.

I am, by training and temperament, a patient man. Patience is the entire job. But patience, I have learned since, is just fear wearing good posture, and around the third hour past midnight mine ran out.

* * *

Getting the case back was insultingly easy, which tells you something about how well a valley defends itself against threats it has never met. Hale slept at his desk. The key was under his collar. I have steady hands — it is the whole of my trade — and I used them, and then I stood in the guardroom holding the thing again, feeling that same animal relief flood through me, and I made myself open it before panic could dress itself up as caution.

Inside: a map. Folded, worn soft at the creases, drawn in a fine confident hand. It showed the kingdom beyond the valley — territory I knew only as shapes on other, smaller maps — rendered in obsessive detail. Roads, river fords, towns marked with tiny annotations in faded brown ink. Here the well water runs sweet. Here the toll is doubled at market. Here men disappear.

And along the valley's own border, drawn in a heavier stroke, a single line of script:

*What is dead in here must be answered out there.*

I read it three times. It declined to make sense on any of them.

But there was something else, now that I looked — or rather, something happening that I had no category for. The ink lines seemed faintly, impossibly warm under my fingertips, the paper carrying a heat like a stone left too near a fire. And when I tilted the map toward the lamp, the rivers caught the light a half-second after the flame moved, like they were lagging behind the world.

Magic, presumably. Except magic cannot enter the valley, cannot exist here, cannot so much as ripple a puddle inside the ward. That is the first fact of my life, older in me than language. And yet the map glowed faintly, patiently, in my hands, in the heart of Stillness, like a guest who has not heard the rules.

I should have put it back. I should have returned it to the chest, gone back to my bench, and waited for morning, when reason returns and lamplight stops lying.

Instead I folded it small, hid it inside my shirt against my chest — where it lay warm as a second heartbeat — and walked out of the guardroom past Hale's sleeping head.

I want to be clear that I had no plan. I am a man of plans the way a clock is a man of hours; remove the mechanism and I am just a hollow case with hands. I had no destination, no story, no alibi. I had a dead woman's map warming my ribs and her half-sentence lodged in my skull like a fishhook, and my feet, entirely without consulting me, chose the west path. The orchard path. The path that ends at the ward.

The boundary looks like nothing. That's the trouble with it. A line of old white stones through grassland, and beyond them the same hills, the same air, the same indifferent sky. Pilgrims used to come, centuries ago, to stand at the edge of the Stillness and feel their prayers die in their throats. Now nobody comes at all, except orchard boys stealing apples from trees that grow sweeter the closer they dare.

I stopped at the last stone. Every instinct I owned lined up behind it and refused.

Behind me: the palace, my chambers, my ledger of micro-doses, forty years of a life measured in grains of arsenic and the king's untroubled sleep. Ahead of me: hills, roads, the whole loud dangerous world, and somewhere in it, apparently, a crown that eats kings, and a sentence beginning *you were made for* that some stranger had died trying to finish.

She had known my name. She had smelled, faintly, of rosemary.

I stepped over the line.

I wish I could describe what crossing the ward felt like in the language I actually experienced it, but my native tongue is flavor, and so I will tell you this: it tasted like standing up too fast. Like the moment before vomiting. Like the first bite of a fruit whose name you don't know and whose sweetness is somehow aimed directly at the back of your knees. Every hair on my body stood up. The night air, which had been night air, became *thick*, became textured, became full of currents I could suddenly feel flowing around me like eddies around a stone in a stream, and I understood, with my whole skin, that I had spent my life inside a room and mistaken it for the world.

The map against my chest flared warm. Then warmer. Then, unmistakably, it pulsed — once, twice, in rhythm with my own frightened heart but a half-beat behind it, the way the rivers had lagged behind the lamp.

And into the silence of my skull, where only my own voice had ever lived, another voice arrived — dry, precise, tired, the voice of a man reading measurements aloud at the end of a long day — and it said:

*So. At last. Do try to keep up, boy. There isn't much time, and I have a great deal of ground to show you.*

Beyond the white stones, the hills waited, dark and enormous and full of everything I had never tasted.

I did not go back.
