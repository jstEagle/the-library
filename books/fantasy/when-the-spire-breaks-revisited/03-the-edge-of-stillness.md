## 3. The Edge of Stillness

There is a particular flavor to being hunted, and I am sorry to report it is not an interesting one. It tastes like copper at the back of the throat and cold sweat on the upper lip — the same profile, as it happens, as fear of poison, which tells you something about how much variety my life has offered until now.

I had been a food-taster for eleven years. In that time I had learned to read a kitchen the way priests read scripture: every knife's angle, every servant's glance, every silence that lasted a half-second too long. What I had never learned was how to read a valley turning against me.

They came for me within the hour of the courier's death. Of course they did. A woman bleeds out in the shadow of the palace wall clutching a taster's sleeve, whispering about crowns, and the guards do not conclude *assassin*. They conclude *taster*. The logic is almost elegant: the man whose entire profession is poison was standing next to a poisoned — no, a stabbed, but details are for magistrates — dying woman. Ergo.

I heard them coming up the kitchen stair before I saw them. Boots in a rhythm I knew. Sergeant Olem and two of his men, walking fast, which Olem only did when someone important wanted someone unimportant ruined.

I want to be honest with you, since honesty is apparently the theme of my ruin: my first thought was not *I am innocent*. My first thought was *the map case is under the flour bin and if they find it there I will hang before lunch.* Whatever the woman had pressed into my hands, whatever she'd whispered with her last breath — *the crown eats him* — some instinct older than reason had already decided it was mine to keep. I have interrogated that instinct since. I have not found a satisfactory answer. Perhaps blood remembers things the mind has been spared.

I swapped the case into the satchel I used for carrying sampling dishes, took the back stairs past the larders, and went out through the smoke-house, where the smell of cured ham covered everything, including, briefly, my terror.

The Valley of Stillness is small, if you're honest about it. A bowl of green set into mountains that don't care about us. We grow wheat and apples and a great deal of self-satisfaction. No sorcery works here — hasn't in living memory, longer. Wards or accident or the gods' own indifference; the priests offer all three explanations depending on the size of your donation. What it means practically is that we are a place things flee *to*, not from. And what it meant for me that evening was this: the valley had exactly one edge, and beyond that edge was a kingdom I knew only from Aldric— only from maps, and rumors, and the occasional diplomatic shipment of wine.

The guards would follow me to the boundary. They always did, when the rare fool tried to leave. But they would not cross it. That was law, custom, and — if you believed the old stories — survival. Nothing that entered the wide world from Stillness came back unchanged, and the king's men were hired to protect the valley's peace, not to die curious about the rest of creation.

So I ran toward the one place they could not follow, which is a sentence I never expected to think, let alone live.

* * *

The western road climbs through orchards gone wild at the margins, and by the time I reached the third mile marker I could hear pursuit behind me — not close, but patient, the way hounds are patient. Olem wasn't stupid. He knew where I was going. He was simply letting the valley itself do the work of stopping me, because the valley always had.

Here is what nobody tells you about the boundary of Stillness: it looks like nothing. A line of old standing stones, moss-eaten, leaning like tired shepherds, arranged across the pass in a curve that might be natural and might not. Beyond them, the land continues — more pines, more rock, a river cutting south — utterly ordinary to the eye. Sheep graze on both sides of it. I once watched a hawk cross it without so much as a shiver.

But the people of Stillness do not graze there. Children dare each other to touch the stones and come back pale and quiet, and their mothers say nothing, and everyone agrees not to discuss why.

I stood among the stones with my lungs burning and my satchel heavy against my hip, and behind me, down the slope, torchlight bobbed through the orchards. Ahead of me was the whole of everything I didn't know.

A taster's discipline, drilled over eleven years: assess the dish before you commit the tongue. Look for the shimmer of oil, the wrongness in a color, the bitterness hiding under honey. So I assessed. Option one: go back, surrender the map, explain myself, and trust in a magistrate's sense of justice in a kingdom where the king hadn't aged in years — a fact I suddenly realized I had filed away without ever asking why. Option two: stand still and be taken. Option three.

Option three was a dead courier's whisper and a leather case against my ribs that felt, absurdly, like it was warming.

"The crown eats him," she had said. Whose crown. Eats whom. I had a king. I tasted his meals twice daily. If something was eating him, then every dish I had ever cleared as safe had been seasoning on the meat, and the thought made my stomach turn in a way no arsenic ever had.

The torches crested the slope.

I crossed the stones at a dead run, which I recommend to no one, because whatever threshold exists between Stillness and the world does not appreciate being taken at speed.

* * *

Let me try to describe what magic tastes like, knowing that language will fail me, because language was built by people who grew up on the far side of stones like these, for whom it is water to fish. I grew up in a dry country. To me, it was a flood.

It began as sweetness — not sugar, nothing so crude. More like the ghost of sweetness, the way a wine cellar smells of vintages long drunk. It came up through the soles of my boots first, which should be impossible, taste beginning in the feet, but there it was: a bright mineral note, like snowmelt over limestone, rising through my ankles, my knees, pooling warm in my chest. Then the air itself changed. Every breath carried flavor. Pine became a dark resinous chord on my tongue. The river below announced itself from fifty yards away — cold, clean, faintly green, threaded with something metallic and alive that I can only call *current*, as though the water were thinking.

And underneath all of it, everywhere, a bass note I didn't have a name for yet. Deep. Old. Patient. Like the smell of earth after rain, if the rain had been falling for centuries.

I staggered past the last stone and dropped to my knees in the wet grass of the wide world, and I vomited — not from poison, for the first time in my life, but from abundance. My tongue, trained to detect a half-grain of arsenic in a gallon of stew, had just been handed an ocean.

Behind me, the torches stopped at the stones. I heard Olem's voice, flat and carrying: "He's crossed. Leave him. The world will do worse than we would."

Which was meant, I think, as comfort to his men. It did not feel comforting from where I knelt.

I looked back once. The valley lay below in its evening lamplight — the palace, the kitchens where my knives were probably already being catalogued as evidence, the whole small safe bowl of my life. Eleven years of careful, masterful, meaningless work. I had kept a king's food clean while something else, perhaps, ate the king himself. The irony had a finish I could taste: dry, bitter, long on the palate. A bad vintage.

Then the map case began to glow.

* * *

I should explain that I opened it because I am, at core, a professional, and professionals cannot leave an unknown substance sealed. Also because it was glowing, and I have found that glowing objects left unexamined tend to become glowing objects examined under worse circumstances later.

Inside was a map — fine linen-backed paper, hand-inked, the work of someone very good and very careful. It showed the kingdom beyond the valley in loving detail: roads, rivers, mountain passes marked with elevation notes in a cramped, precise hand. Towns I knew only as names on diplomatic crates. And at the eastern edge, drawn in heavier ink, the capital, with the palace rendered in obsessive detail, room by room, as though the cartographer had walked its halls in his mind a thousand times.

The glow came off the ink itself, a faint gold luminescence like sunlight trapped under varnish, and it pulsed — I swear this — in rhythm with my heartbeat. When my pulse spiked, the roads on the map brightened. When I forced myself to breathe, they dimmed.

"Well," I said aloud, to the pines and the darkening sky. "That's new."

The voice answered before the echo died.

*You taste the world,* it said. *How wonderful. He said you would.*

I did the dignified thing. I threw the map away from me and scrambled backward on my hands until a pine trunk stopped me.

The voice did not come from the map, exactly. It came from inside — behind my eyes, in the space where my own thoughts live, except it stood in that space like a guest who has let himself in and hung his coat on my hook. It was a man's voice. Dry, precise, amused in the way of someone who has been dead long enough to find death funny.

*Calm yourself, taster. If I wished you harm, I'd have chosen a less dramatic entrance. Dramatic entrances are for ministers.*

"Who are you?" My voice cracked on the second word, which I chose to blame on the cold.

*Aldric Vane. Surveyor, cartographer, formerly of the Royal Guild, currently deceased — eleven years now, and I recommend it to no one.* A pause, and when the voice resumed there was something older in it, something worn smooth. *You're holding my map. Or rather, my map is holding you. It does that. It was made to.*

"Maps don't talk."

*Maps don't. This one remembers. There's a difference, and you'll hate learning it.* The presence shifted, and I felt — tasted — something like attention moving through my skull, cataloguing. *A taster. He said he'd send a taster eventually, or the son of one. Immunity layered over immunity. A tongue that could hold what must be held. I confess I imagined someone older.*

"My mother—" I started, and stopped, because I did not know why those words. The courier's face surfaced again, that half-recognition, the feeling of looking at a portrait of a relative. *My mother* had leapt into my mouth unbidden, like a flavor memory triggered by a spice you can't name.

Silence, from the thing in my head. A long one. When it spoke again, the amusement was gone entirely.

*Ask me again in daylight,* Aldric Vane said. *Some answers need you rested, and some need you braver than you are tonight. For now: get up, taster. Walk east along the river until the forked oak. You are being followed — not by your valley's hounds, by mine. Hers. The Crown's. They felt the map wake when you crossed the line, and they will come, and they will not stop at stones.*

"What Crown?" I asked. "What are you—"

But the voice had withdrawn, folding itself away into the gold-lit ink like a man stepping back from a window, and the map lay in the grass glowing softly, patiently, the way a lamp glows in a house where someone is waiting up for you.

I got up. My knees objected; I overruled them. Far behind me, back toward the boundary stones, I heard — or thought I heard, or the map made me hear — hoofbeats. Not Olem's measured trot. Something faster, and lighter, and wrong, the way a familiar dish tastes wrong when one element has been substituted.

I picked up the map. It was warm as fresh bread. East along the river, to the forked oak, into a night full of flavors I had no education for, pursued by riders who served something that ate kings.

In eleven years as a food-taster, I had developed one inviolable rule: never swallow what you cannot identify.

I broke it that night, walking east into the dark with a dead man in my head and my mother's name — though I didn't know it was hers yet — sitting on my tongue like a word I'd been holding under it all my life.

The hoofbeats came closer.
