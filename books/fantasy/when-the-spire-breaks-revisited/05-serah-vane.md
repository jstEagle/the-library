## 5. Serah Vane

Three years of hunting a ghost teaches you patience. It also teaches you that patience is mostly just stubbornness wearing a nicer coat.

I'd been camped two days at the edge of the Stillness ward, in the shadow of a lightning-split oak that had died trying to grow out of dead ground, watching the valley that wasn't there. That's not poetry. The Valley of Stillness doesn't appear on maps — I should know, my father drew most of them — and when you stand at its border and look in, your eyes slide off like rain off oiled leather. You see hills. You see a river you know isn't real because you've walked that riverbed and found no water in it for forty years. Your mind fills in the blank with something plausible and moves on, grateful.

Most people never notice the seam. My father noticed. Noticing things was how he'd ended up dead in a ditch outside Marrow's Ford with his survey kit scattered around him like entrails.

So I watched the blank place on the map, and on the third morning, someone fell out of it.

He came stumbling through the treeline like a man pushed rather than walking — pale, filthy, clutching a leather case to his chest the way drowning men clutch driftwood. He took four steps, looked over his shoulder at nothing, and vomited into the bracken.

I had my knife at his throat before he finished wiping his mouth.

"Name," I said.

"Cyrus," he gasped. "Cyrus Nakamura. Please — I'm not —"

"Everyone's not." I turned him by the shoulder so I could see his face. He was younger than me by a few years, dark-haired, with the kind of hands I'd later learn to recognize as a taster's hands — clean nails, steady even while the rest of him shook. "You came out of Stillness. Nobody comes out of Stillness. Either you're a ghost, or you're very interesting, and ghosts don't vomit."

"I'm looking for—" He stopped. His eyes did something complicated. People lie with their mouths; their eyes are terrible at it. "I'm looking for the capital."

"Wrong direction. Capital's south and east, three weeks if the roads behave. They won't."

"I don't have three weeks." He said it flatly, without drama, which is the only way to say a thing like that. "The solstice. There's something happening at the solstice, and I have to—"

I pressed the knife a fraction closer. "Who gave you that line? Because that's the same line I've been chasing for three years, and everyone who's said it to me has ended up either dead or hollow, and I'd like to know which category you're auditioning for."

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said, and then his gaze dropped to the case in his arms, and I saw it — really saw it — for the first time.

The leather was scarred and travel-worn, but I knew that stitching. I'd learned my letters tracing the decorative border-work my father put on his map cases, the little interlocking chevrons he said were for luck and I suspected were for vanity. Only one man in the kingdom stitched chevrons like that.

My knife hand went unsteady for the first time in years.

"Open it," I said. My voice came out wrong. Smaller.

"It's not mine—"

"*Open it.*"

He fumbled the buckles. Inside lay a map — folded, stained, drawn on good survey vellum in an ink I could smell from a foot away, iron-gall with a drop of clove oil, because my father said ink should be preserved the way food is, with intention. And across the top, in his cramped, beautiful hand, a title:

*When the Spire Breaks.*

The world tilted. I sat down in the bracken without entirely deciding to. Somewhere behind me the dead man's daughter heard her own breathing go ragged.

Three years. Three years of following rumors through towns where people smiled too widely, of digging through records for a king who hadn't aged since before I was born, of striking Lord Mavros across the face in full court because I'd looked into his eyes and seen *no one home* — three years, and the whole time, the answer had been sitting in a valley that eats memories, waiting for a vomiting stranger to carry it out.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

"A dying woman gave it to me. At the valley gate. She—" He hesitated again. That hesitation again, that specific shape of it. "She called me by name. I don't know how she knew me."

"What was her name?"

"She didn't say. She was—" He swallowed. "There was something about her. Something I almost remembered."

Aldric Vane had exactly one rule about his work: *a map is a promise*. He'd gone missing eleven months ago carrying a case with those chevrons. Now here was the promise, delivered by a woman who might have been the one who — no. One grief at a time.

I stood up. I put the knife away, which for me is roughly equivalent to a declaration of marriage, and I said, "Cyrus Nakamura. You and I are going to have a long conversation about what's on this map, and then we're going south, and if you're lying to me I will leave you somewhere no one will find you until the crows have finished their arithmetic. Understood?"

"That's — very threatening, yes."

"Good. Walk."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about exile: you stop being a person and become a function. I used to be Serah Vane, Royal Guard, Third Lance, commended twice for valor. Then I hit a minister in front of the assembled court, and within a day I was Serah Vane, traitor, and the posters went up with an artist's rendering that made me look like I had a lazy eye.

They didn't ask why I struck him. That was the elegant part. Mavros stood there bleeding from the mouth and *smiled*, and thanked me for my concern, and the whole court laughed at the madwoman, and nobody — not one person in that gilded room — admitted what I'd seen, because admitting it would mean the world had come off its hinges.

What I'd seen was this: Lord Mavros, who'd dined with my father, who'd argued wine vintages with him until midnight, looked at me with eyes like the back of a spoon. No anger. No recognition. Nothing behind them at all. And when I hit him, he didn't fall like a man falls. He folded, like a coat slipping off a hook.

So yes. I'd been hunting proof for three years. Proof of the Hollow Crown — though I didn't have the name yet, then. Names came later, from mad priests and half-burned archives, from my father's letters, which I reread so often I could recite them in my sleep:

*Serah — the king does not age. I have compared portraits across thirty years and the variance is less than a painter's mood. Someone must have noticed. Why has no one said anything? I am going to Correnholt to check the granary ledgers. If I do not write by month's end, take the east road and trust no one whose smile arrives before they do.*

Month's end came and went. I took the east road anyway. I've been taking roads ever since.

And now the road had handed me a taster's boy from the Stillness holding my father's last map, babbling about solstices, and I couldn't decide whether the world was finally making sense or had simply found new ways to mock me.

We made camp that night in a hollow beneath a fallen beech, close enough to the ward-line that I could keep an eye on it. I let Cyrus eat first and watched him the whole time, which he noticed and pretended not to.

"You think I poisoned something," he said.

"I think you came out of a magic-dead valley carrying a dead man's map, which means either miracle or conspiracy, and I've never once been handed a miracle."

"I'm a food taster," he said, with a strange, stiff pride. "For the king's household in the valley. I've spent twelve years learning poisons. I'm probably the safest man in the kingdom to share a meal with."

"The valley king," I said. "There's no king in the valley. There's a steward and a ceremonial court and a lot of people who've forgotten why they're there."

His jaw tightened. "The king is real. I taste his meals every evening."

"And what does he look like?"

Cyrus opened his mouth. Closed it. I watched him reach for a memory and find smoke.

"He's — tall," he said slowly. "Gray at the temples. He has a ring — a signet —" He frowned. "Why can't I describe his face? I've seen him hundreds of times."

"Because you've never seen him," I said. "You've seen what the valley lets you see. That place doesn't just block magic, Nakamura. It blocks noticing. It's the perfect hiding spot, and somebody hid something there worth hiding."

He stared at the fire. "Then why would a king hide inside a place where he can't be king?"

"Now," I said, "you're asking the right questions."

* * *

He talked after that. Not all at once — I had to pry it out of him like a nail from wet wood — but over the next day's walk, in pieces, the story came: the courier dying at the gate, blood on his hands, guards deciding he'd done the poisoning, the map glowing when he crossed the ward, and a voice in his head that wasn't his.

"Aldric," I said quietly, when he described it. "That's my father's voice. Or what's left of him. Surveyors used to bind memory into their work — old guild craft, mostly superstition by the time he learned it. He believed maps could hold more than geography." I kept my eyes on the road. "Looks like he was right."

"You knew he bound himself into it?"

"I knew he *could*. I didn't know he had." I adjusted my pack strap. It didn't need adjusting. "How much of him is in your head?"

"Memories. Fragments. A city I've never seen — spires, white walls. A king who looks exactly like every portrait in the palace gallery, except the portraits are older than he is. Ministers standing in rows with glassy eyes. Sometimes it's like watching someone else's dream." He glanced at me sideways. "Does that horrify you? Because it horrifies me."

"Everything about you horrifies me. Keep talking."

By the second night, I'd built the shape of it, and the shape matched the shape I'd been carrying alone for three years. The Hollow Crown. The king taken decades ago, worn like a garment, fed on vessels — ministers, stewards, whoever got too close or too curious. My father had gotten curious. So had the courier. So, apparently, had a woman in the valley whom Cyrus couldn't name but clearly needed to.

"You understand what this means," I said, poking the fire down to embers. "If the Crown finishes merging with the king at the solstice, it's permanent. No unbinding. The kingdom belongs to it forever, and nobody will ever even know it changed hands."

"I understand," he said. Then, quieter: "Your father died for this."

"My father died because he poked a sleeping thing with a stick. I intend to poke it with a spear." I looked up. "Which brings us to terms."

"Terms."

"I need you. The map responds to you — Aldric built it to open for a specific kind of mind, and whatever you are, it chose you. Without you it's parchment. With you it's the only weapon anyone's brought against the Crown in forty years." I held up one finger. "In exchange, you need me. You can taste poison and quote recipes. You cannot fight, navigate, lie to officials, sleep rough, or survive a week outside walls — I watched you try to build a fire this morning and I'm still recovering. Alone, you're dead before the next town."

"That's a fair assessment," he admitted, "though I resent how much you enjoyed delivering it."

"I didn't enjoy it. I *needed* it. There's a difference." I leaned forward. "So here are the terms. We go together. We trust each other exactly as far as the mission requires — no further. You tell me everything the map shows you, the moment it shows you, even if it's nothing, especially if it's nothing. And when this is over, if we live, we part ways and neither of us owes the other a letter."

He was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on his face.

"You've done this before," he said. "Worked with someone."

"No."

"You talk like someone who decided a long time ago that needing people is how you get hurt."

I threw a twig at him. "Terms, Nakamura."

"Accepted." But he smiled — small, tired, annoyingly warm — and added, "For what it's worth, I'm glad it was you who found me. Out of everyone who could have been waiting at that treeline with a knife."

"Don't be sentimental. It makes me want to renegotiate." I rolled into my bedroll. "Sleep. We cross the Marrow tomorrow, and after that we're in country where the roads remember who walks them."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be."

I lay awake long after his breathing evened out, watching the stars through the beech branches, turning the map case over in my hands. Chevrons stitched by a dead man's fingers. A promise kept eleven months late.

*I'm sorry it took me so long, Da,* I thought. *But I have it now. And I've found someone — gods help us both — who can read it.*

Across the ashes, Cyrus murmured in his sleep, and for a moment the voice that came out of his mouth wasn't quite his own — deeper, wearier, shaped by another lifetime. I caught only three words before he settled:

"...the spire... breaks..."

I sat up straight in the dark.

Because my father had titled that map *When the Spire Breaks* — but there was no spire marked anywhere on it. I'd checked. Twice.

And now something inside my father's chosen carrier was dreaming about it, like a man humming a song whose name he'd forgotten.

Eleven days to the solstice. Whatever the spire was, wherever it was, we had to find it first.

I reached over and shook Cyrus awake.

"Get up," I said. "We need to talk about what you're dreaming."
