## 6. Roads That Remember

### Cyrus

The road out of the hills did not exist on Tuesdays.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the last thing I had left of my old life, and I clung to it the way a drowning man clings to a floating tray of canapés. On Mondays the road ran along the river. On Wednesdays it cut through a birch wood that hummed when you touched it. But on Tuesdays — every Tuesday since we'd left Serah's ruined watchtower behind us — the road between the millstone marker and the shrine of some forgotten saint simply wasn't there. You'd walk where it should have been and find instead a field of tall grey grass that bent away from your footsteps as if politely declining your company.

"It's not gone," Serah said, when I pointed this out on our fourth Tuesday. "It's *hiding*. Roads in this kingdom remember who's watching."

"Roads don't have opinions."

"These do." She crouched at the edge of the grass, pressing two fingers to the earth like a physician taking a pulse. "My father mapped them for thirty years. He used to say the kingdom's roads were laid over something older, and the older thing flinches when the Crown looks at it." She glanced up at me, and there it was again — that look she gave me, half suspicion, half grudging arithmetic, as though she were still calculating whether I was worth the trouble of keeping alive. "Which means if the road is hiding, something is looking."

That was how I learned to read the country outside the valley: by its nervous tics.

In the Valley of Stillness, roads are honest. They go where they go, they're muddy or they're dry, and the only thing you have to fear from them is the occasional cart wheel. I had spent twenty-six years believing the world was essentially a large kitchen with better ventilation. Then a dying woman bled out in my arms, handed me a map full of another man's memories, and the world turned out to be a haunted pantry where the shelves rearranged themselves when the master walked by.

"Tell me what Aldric remembers about this stretch," Serah said, standing, brushing off her knees.

I closed my eyes. This part never got easier. Reaching into the map's memories was like reaching into a stranger's coat pocket — you might find a key, or you might find something you'd rather not know he'd been carrying.

Aldric Vane, surveyor, dead these three years, surfaced in my skull the way silt rises when you stir a pond. *The shrine of Saint Ovel*, his memory said, in that dry, patient voice of his, *marks a ward-stone. The Crown's men can't see past it. If you must rest, rest there. And mind the third step — the stone is cracked and I broke an ankle on it in the autumn of the king's thirty-ninth year, which was also the year the king stopped laughing, though I did not then understand those facts were related.*

"He says the shrine blinds them," I reported. "And that the third step is cracked."

Serah snorted. "Of course he does. He annotated everything. He once drew a marginal note on a map of the capital complaining about the quality of the ink." Her voice went strange on the last word — *father* lived inside it even when she didn't say it — and she turned away before I could decide whether to acknowledge that or pretend, kindly, not to have heard.

We rested at the shrine. It was nothing: four leaning pillars, a roof of moss-eaten slate, a stone underfoot worn smooth by centuries of pilgrims who had presumably all tripped on the same cracked step. But sitting inside it felt like sitting inside a held breath. The humming in the birches beyond quieted. The tall grey grass stood still. Even the light seemed to settle, dust motes hanging in the air like punctuation marks.

"Why does it work?" I asked. "The wards. Why can't they see through them?"

Serah tore a strip of dried mutton with her teeth and considered me across the small fire she'd insisted on building anyway, wards or no wards. "You really were raised in a jar, weren't you?"

"A very well-stocked jar."

"Magic isn't weather here," she said. "It's not just... happening around you. It has owners. Everything that works — the wards, the roads' shyness, the way certain bridges won't hold a liar — it works because someone bound it, long ago, to serve the living against the wearing-things." She said *the Crown* without saying it; she'd developed a habit of talking around its name, as if naming it too often might wear a groove for it to travel down. "The Crown eats bindings. That's what it *is*, underneath everything else — a thing that takes what protects people and turns it into what traps them. Every ward it breaks becomes a wall around somebody's mind instead."

I chewed on that, and on the mutton, which was terrible. "And your father spent his life mapping the ones that were left."

"He spent his life mapping the ones that were left," she repeated quietly, "because he believed a map of surviving protections was a battle plan. He was right." She looked up, and for a moment the hostility banked down and I saw the thing underneath it, which was grief wearing armor. "He was right and it got him killed, so let's both take a moment to appreciate being right."

### Serah

Here is what nobody tells you about traveling with someone: you learn their silences before you learn their words.

Cyrus Nakamura had three silences. There was the working silence, when he'd taste something — a berry, a stream's water, air after rain — and go still and inward, cataloguing, his lips moving faintly as he named compounds I couldn't pronounce. There was the map-silence, when Aldric rose in him and his face went somewhere else entirely, older and sadder, a man wearing a boy's features from the inside. And there was the third silence, the one that came at dusk, when he'd sit with his back against whatever we'd decided was shelter and stare at his own hands like they'd been issued to him by mistake.

It took me six days to ask about the third silence.

We'd made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower — not mine; mine was two days south and probably occupied by now — and I was showing him how to set snares, because a man who could detect arsenic at four parts per thousand apparently could not construct a basic slip-loop, and one of us had to keep us fed.

"You hold the cord so," I said, guiding his fingers, "and the noose does the work. You're not catching the rabbit. You're letting the rabbit catch itself."

"That's obscene," he said, but his hands learned fast. Taster's hands. Steady, precise, patient. Of course they learned fast.

"You eat meat."

"I eat meat that other people have already had complicated feelings about. I've never been involved in the process." He tightened the loop, frowned at it, adjusted. "In the valley, food arrives. It arrives and you stand between it and the king, and whatever happens next happens to you. That's the whole job. Nothing arrives to you."

I heard it then, under the dry delivery — the third silence, spoken aloud. *Nothing arrives to you.* Twenty-odd years of being a hinge for other people's safety, a mouth that existed so a royal mouth wouldn't have to.

"What happened to your mother?" I asked, because I am, per my former commanding officer, tactically blunt.

His hands didn't stop moving, but they slowed. "She left when I was very young. Nobody talks about her. In the valley nobody talks about anything uncomfortable; discomfort is considered a form of weather." He set the snare down with exaggerated care. "Why do you ask?"

"Because you look at the map like it's going to introduce itself," I said. "Because the courier called you by name and you told me that meant nothing, and you're a worse liar than you are a snare-setter, and you are *catastrophic* at snares."

He was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, a nightbird sang four notes and then thought better of it.

"I'm afraid," he said finally, not looking at me, in the flat voice of a man reading an inventory, "of finding out that everyone who ever claimed to protect me knew more than they told me. Which would mean my whole life was a dish someone else seasoned." A pause. "There. Now you have one of mine. Give me one back. It only seems fair, and I'm told fairness exists outside kitchens."

I should have deflected. I was good at deflecting; three years of exile makes you excellent at it. Instead I heard myself say: "I'm afraid I struck Mavros for nothing. That the minister I hit was just a man having a bad evening, and I destroyed my life over a misreading, and my father died chasing a ghost I put in his head." My jaw ached; I realized I was clenching it. "Every time I look at you and see the truth in your face, I'm relieved, and I hate being relieved, because relief means I was right, and being right cost me everything."

The fire settled. Cyrus looked at me across it, and didn't offer comfort, thank the saints, didn't say anything kind and useless. He just nodded, slow, like a man tasting something new and deciding whether it was poison.

"No," he said at last. "Your father's map found me. Whatever else is true, that's true. You didn't chase a ghost. You chased a courier line that ended in a kitchen boy holding the proof." He almost smiled. "Being right is expensive. But you were right."

I turned away to check the snares I'd already checked twice, because my eyes had gone hot and I had a reputation to maintain, mostly with myself.

### Cyrus

On the ninth day, the nightmare came for both of us at once, which Serah later insisted was coincidence and which I privately filed under *the map does what it likes*.

I dreamed in Aldric's memory again, but deeper this time — not the surface recollections of roads and broken ankles but something from near the end of him. A room in the capital. A window. The king below in a courtyard, standing perfectly still among courtiers who moved around him like water around a post. And Aldric's own hand — my borrowed hand — sketching frantically in a ledger, writing the same phrase over and over until the page tore:

*He is not alone in there. He is not alone in there. He is not—*

I woke gasping, and found Serah already awake, sitting bolt upright, knife in hand, staring at the fire with an expression I recognized because I was wearing one like it.

"The courtyard," she said hoarsely. "White banners."

"You dreamed—" 

"My father's last night. He packed his maps and told me he was going to the capital to deliver something in person, and he stood in the doorway and said—" Her voice caught. She restarted it like a stubborn engine. "He said, 'If I'm slow coming back, don't come find me. Come find what I found.'" She looked at me. "What did you see?"

I told her. The courtyard. The king, motionless. The torn page.

When I finished, Serah was silent for a long moment, and then she did something I did not expect: she laughed. One short, awful, genuine laugh.

"We shared a nightmare," she said. "Three years I've traveled alone, slept armed, trusted no one, and now I'm having joint dreams with a food-taster." She dragged a hand through her hair. "Aldric, you impossible man. Are you doing this? Are you *introducing* us?"

The map case sat between us where we always kept it — equidistant, neither trusting the other alone with it, which by then had become less a security measure than a ritual, like saying grace. As she spoke, the leather warmed slightly. Or I imagined it did. By then I had stopped keeping a firm line between what the map did and what I attributed to it, the way you stop questioning a colleague who is always right and increasingly strange.

"We should sleep in shifts," I said, because someone had to say something practical.

"We always sleep in shifts."

"Yes, but tonight I'll take second watch. You had the dream harder." I hesitated, then committed to the sentence I'd been building since the shrine. "And Serah — the thing you said. About Mavros. About being afraid you were wrong." I looked at the fire rather than at her, coward that I remain in matters of the face. "I don't think you were wrong. I think you were the only person in that room who saw clearly, and it ruined your life, and that's not justice, but it is... evidence. Of you."

She didn't answer for long enough that I began composing retractions. Then, quietly: "Go sleep, Nakamura. First watch is mine."

But when I lay down, I felt her watching me — not with suspicion, finally, but with something more dangerous, and I fell asleep cataloguing it the way I catalogue flavors, because naming a thing is the only way I know to survive it.

### Serah

The trouble started at the ford, which in hindsight should have surprised no one, fords being historically where trouble keeps its appointments.

We crossed the River Liss on the twelfth day, at a crossing Aldric's memory called the Weeping Stones — shallow water over white rock, safe footing, and, crucially, a ward-line running beneath the current that the Crown's people couldn't easily read. Cyrus led me straight to it, eyes half-closed, murmuring Aldric's directions like a recipe. *"Downstream of the alder, upstream of the cairn, where the water sounds like applause."* Three years I'd fumbled through this country with half a father's knowledge; this kitchen boy strolled through it on a dead man's certainty, and I'd stopped resenting it sometime around the shared nightmare, which frightened me more than the Crown did, honestly.

The crossing itself went fine. It was the far bank that was wrong.

There was a man standing in the reeds. Not hiding — standing. Grey-cloaked, ordinary-faced, the sort of person your eye slides off the way it slides off a fencepost. Except the reeds around him were bent *away* from where he stood, all of them, in a perfect circle, as though the world were politely declining his company.

The tall grey grass. The same gesture. The same flinch.

"Cyrus," I said, very quietly. "Don't stop walking. Don't look at him directly."

"Which one?" Cyrus murmured back, and my blood went cold, because I looked — properly looked — and saw that there were now two figures in the reeds, identical, forty feet apart, and the river behind us had gone silent as a held breath.

They didn't move. They didn't need to. I felt it happen — felt something pass over us like a searchlight, like a thumb pressed briefly against the eye. Cyrus staggered, caught himself on my arm, and made a small sound I'd never heard from him: the sound of a man tasting something he cannot spit out.

Then the figures stepped backward into the reeds and the reeds closed like a book, and the birds started up again as if nothing had occurred, which was the most obscene thing yet.

"What was that?" Cyrus whispered. His hand was shaking on my sleeve.

I knew what it was. My father had written about it in a marginal note I'd read a hundred times, in ink he'd complained about: *Marked roads mean marked travelers. If ever you feel seen on the Weeping Stones, count yourself among the hunted and change your name.*

"They've seen us," I said. "Not followed. *Seen*. There's a difference. Following means agents. Seeing means the Crown itself turned its attention, just for a moment, to check what we were." I forced myself to keep moving, up the far bank, into the tree line, walking at exactly the pace of a pair of unremarkable pilgrims. "We have maybe two days before whatever noticed us sends something worse than watchers."

"Two days to where?"

I unslung my pack and pulled out the copy of Aldric's route I'd memorized until it lived behind my eyes. Beyond the ford, the roads bent east toward Correnholt — trade city, granaries, and Lord Mavros, whose smiling face I had rearranged with my fist three years ago and who had, by every report, not aged a day since.

"Correnholt," I said. "Where the minister lives. Where my father's trail ends." I met Cyrus's eyes, and saw the map-memory flicker behind them — Aldric's dread layered over his own, a double exposure of fear. "And where, if we're very lucky, we'll find out what 'he is not alone in there' means."

Behind us, far back along the far shore, the reeds parted once, briefly, and something grey stood watching until the bend of the river took it from view.
