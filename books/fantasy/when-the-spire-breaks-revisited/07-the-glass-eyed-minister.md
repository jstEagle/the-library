## 7. The Glass-Eyed Minister

Correnholt smelled like money left out too long.

That was my first professional assessment of the trade city, delivered somewhere around midday as Serah and I came down off the hills and onto the great north road, where it widened into a river of carts and cattle and people who all seemed to be in a hurry to be somewhere slightly worse. Salt, tallow, wet wool, horse dung, the cloying rot-sweetness of overripe fruit nobody could afford to throw away — and under all of it, faint as a whisper through a wall, that same wrongness I'd been tasting since the map began to wake.

Copper. Not blood-copper exactly. Coin-copper. The taste of something old being handled by many hands until the hands wear through and the thing remains.

"You're doing the face," Serah said.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you've bitten something and you're being polite about it."

"I'm a food-taster. Politeness about bitten things is my entire profession."

She snorted and pulled her hood lower. Three days on the road had done things to her I probably wasn't supposed to notice: sunburn across her cheekbones, a new scar of a scratch along her jaw from a thornbush she'd sworn revenge against, hair escaping its braid like it had opinions. She looked less like a disgraced royal guard and more like what she actually was, which I was beginning to suspect was the most dangerous kind of person — someone with nothing left to protect except a purpose.

"We need coin," she said. "We need information. And we need to see him."

"Him" required no clarification. She'd said his name twice last night in her sleep, both times like she was spitting it out.

"Lord Mavros," I said anyway, because I wanted to hear how she'd react.

Her jaw did the thing. The thornbush jaw. "Three years," she said. "I struck a minister of the crown and got exiled for it, and everyone acted like I'd lost my mind, and maybe I had, because when I hit him — Cyrus, I *hit* him, full swing, broke two knuckles — he didn't fall down. He just stood there. And smiled. And his eyes were—" She stopped. "You know glass eyes? How they don't track right? His tracked fine. That's what was wrong. Nothing behind them moving them."

"And now we're going to walk up to him."

"We're going to observe him. Professionally." She glanced at me. "You're the professional observer. I'm the muscle. Try not to taste anything that can kill you."

"No promises. Tasting is how I think."

* * *

Correnholt was built for commerce the way a spiderweb is built for flies. Everything funneled inward. Streets narrowed toward the Exchange, alleys narrowed toward the streets, doorways narrowed toward alleys, and every surface was angled to catch your eye and hold it long enough to sell you something. I catalogued it the way I catalogue a banquet: here the rich dishes, there the fillers, there the garnish meant to distract you from the fact that the meat is past its prime.

The meat of Correnholt was past its prime. I couldn't have told you how I knew. It was the same sense that had been growing in me since the valley — the borrowed tongue of a dead cartographer layered over my own — and it read the city like a broth gone subtly off. Not spoiled. Just... simmered too long over a fire that wasn't heat.

The copper taste was strongest near the Exchange. Strongest of all, when we found him, around Lord Mavros.

We saw him from the balcony of a tea house that Serah chose because it had sightlines to the Exchange steps and because the tea was terrible enough that nobody drank it slowly enough to remember our faces. She paid with the last of the coin we'd gotten pawning her guard's signet, which she'd done with the expression of someone amputating a finger they no longer needed.

And then Lord Mavros came down the Exchange steps, and I understood, finally, why Serah had broken her knuckles.

He was beautiful. That's the word. Not handsome — beautiful, the way a wax fruit is beautiful. A broad man in his fifties, silver at the temples, robes of deep ministerial blue that fell in folds worth more than the tea house. He moved through the crowd like a ship through water, and the water parted gladly. Merchants bowed. Children were lifted up to see him. He smiled at each of them, and each smile was perfect, and each smile was identical.

Not similar. Identical. I counted eleven smiles before I made myself stop counting, and all eleven had been the same smile, distributed evenly, like rations.

"Cyrus." Serah's voice had gone flat and careful, the way mine went when I tasted something and needed a moment before I named it. "Tell me what you're reading."

I closed my eyes and let the borrowed senses do their work, and I'll be honest with you, whoever ends up reading this: I didn't want to. There is a difference between tasting poison in a dish and tasting it in a crowd, because the dish can be sent back. This could not be sent back.

"It's not just him," I said. "It's the whole square. There's a compound in the air — no, not the air. In them. Faint. Like..." I searched for the reference. My whole life was references; flavor was my library. "Like when a cook uses day-old stock to stretch a fresh soup. You barely notice. But everything tastes slightly of yesterday."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning I don't think Mavros is the infection. I think he's a symptom." I opened my eyes. "Serah, half these people are wearing it too. The porters. Two of the guards. The woman selling apples — she smiled at a customer and it was his smile. Exactly his smile. The same eleven smiles I keep counting, everywhere, like a cook using the same mold for every pastry."

Below us, Lord Mavros paused at the base of the steps to accept a petition from a baker, a flour-white man holding out a sheaf of grievances. Mavros took the papers. He did not look at them. He placed one hand on the baker's shoulder and smiled the smile, and said something we couldn't hear, and the baker walked away lighter than he'd arrived, comforted, certain he'd been seen by a great man.

Mavros hadn't seen him. I would have staked my immunity on it — the only thing I've ever owned that mattered. Mavros had performed seeing him, flawlessly, the way a well-trained kitchen performs a dish it has made ten thousand times, with no cook behind it anymore at all.

"He looks good," Serah whispered. "He looks better than he did three years ago. He hasn't aged a day. That's — Cyrus, that's not possible, I served at court, I know what fifty-five looks like—"

"The king hasn't aged either," I said quietly. "According to the map."

She didn't answer. Her hand, resting on the railing, had gone white-knuckled, and I noticed she was holding it very still, the way you do when you want to hit something and have learned, expensively, not to.

* * *

Here is where I must set down something I still don't fully understand, and I ask you to believe that I, of all people, understand the value of precision.

For a moment — I don't know what caused it. Perhaps the map warming against my chest, perhaps proximity, perhaps the Crown noticing me noticing it — the world tilted sideways, and I was looking out through eyes that were not mine.

* * *

The horror of it is not the darkness. You expect darkness. You prepare for darkness.

The horror is that there is light, and color, and sound, and none of it is yours to use. I am pressed flat. Do you understand? Flat. Like a flower a child puts between the pages of a heavy book, and the book stays shut for years, and the flower keeps its shape forever, its shape and nothing else, its colors fading slowly into the paper while the pages stay closed.

I am Lord Aldous Mavros, Minister of the Exchequer, and I am in this room with myself, and I cannot move anything. Not a hand. Not an eye. Not one small bitter thought outward.

I watch my mouth smile. I count the smiles. Eleven today so far. He has eleven smiles and I used to have hundreds, thousands, a different one for my wife and a different one for my ledgers and a different one for the moment the harvest numbers came in good, and those are all gone now, pressed into the paper, faded to brown.

When the king sleeps, the Crown comes here, into me, and wears me out into the square, and the people love me, and I am glad — that's the worst part, some remnant of me is *glad*, because being worn is warmer than being closed in the book — and then the king wakes and the Crown goes home and I am folded shut again with my ledgers and my wife's portrait and the memory of my daughter's name.

My daughter's name. I almost had it today. It starts soft. It starts with—

* * *

I came back to myself gripping the tea house table hard enough that the terrible teapot rattled, and Serah's hand was on my wrist, and my face was wet.

"Cyrus. *Cyrus.* What did you see?"

I wiped my face with my sleeve, furious at it, at the tears, at the sympathy in my own body for a man I'd never met. "Him," I managed. "Inside. I was inside him, I think, or he was — Serah, he's awake in there. He's conscious. He's pressed down under it like a flower in a book and he can't move anything, and he was trying to remember his daughter's name, and he *almost had it*—"

"Stop." Her voice cracked like ice. "Stop talking."

"Mavros isn't dead. He's not even gone. The Crown doesn't kill what it takes. It keeps them. It *uses* them. It wears him out into the square when the king sleeps and it folds him away after, and he lies there in the dark with himself knowing exactly what's happening, and the worst part—" I heard my own voice go thin and hated it. "The worst part is some part of him likes it. Because being worn is warmer than being shut in the book."

The tea house noise went on around us — clink, chatter, the hiss of the samovar — indifferent, ordinary, obscene.

Serah stared at the Exchange steps where Mavros had stood, and I watched three years of certainty reorganize itself behind her eyes. She'd thought she'd struck a monster. She'd carried the exile like a wound and maybe like a badge, the one guard who'd seen the truth and swung at it. Now the truth had shifted under her: she'd struck a prisoner. She'd broken two knuckles on a man who was buried alive in his own smiling face.

"I hit him," she said, very quietly. "He felt it. Wherever he is in there, he felt me hit him, and it was probably the last real thing anyone had given him in years, and I got exiled for it, and he got folded back into the book."

"Serah—"

"Don't." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Don't be kind to me right now. I need to be angry. Anger is load-bearing. If I take it out, I don't know what's underneath."

So I did the only useful thing I knew how to do. I watched the square.

* * *

What I saw, once I stopped watching Mavros and started watching everyone else, chilled the broth of me all the way through.

There were more of the smiles. Not just Mavros's eleven — though I caught him distributing them still, to a merchant, to a magistrate, to a beggar whose bowl he filled with coin he counted without looking at it. But others. The harbor master, directing cranes with a smoothness just slightly too smooth. A priestess at the fountain shrine, blessing travelers with identical grace. A customs officer waving through carts.

They weren't possessed — or if they were, it was shallow, a coat worn open rather than buttoned. They were... stretched. Worn thin. Day-old-stock people. And around each of them, when I let the borrowed cartographer's senses widen, I could taste it: that copper note, faint but present, like a coin held in the mouth, like something old being passed hand to hand.

And I understood the mechanism, or the beginning of it, the way you understand a recipe from the finished dish. The Crown didn't kill its hosts. Killing wastes material. It kept them — kept them *fresh*, kept them warm, kept them smiling — and it wore them in rotation, and it wore them *out*. Mavros was further along than the others. The harbor master was earlier. Somewhere between the harbor master and Mavros there was a slope, and at the bottom of the slope was a book closing forever over a flattened flower, and the Crown was walking people down that slope one perfect smile at a time, patiently, in no hurry at all.

Because it had time. It had a king who didn't age. It had forty years of practice.

"Cyrus." Serah had recovered herself into something harder and colder than anger. "You've got that look. The recipe look. Tell me."

"It's not feeding on them," I said. "Or — it is, but that's not the point of it. The point is inventory. Mavros, the harbor master, the priestess — they're not victims, they're vessels. Spares. It's keeping a wardrobe, Serah. And the king is the coat it never wants to take off, and everyone else is what it wears when the coat is at the cleaner's."

"Then the granaries, the roads shifting, the agents — all of it—"

"All of it is maintenance," I said. "A kingdom isn't its borders. A kingdom is its people agreeing, every day, to be governed. It's not conquering Correnholt. Correnholt already belongs to it. It's just... keeping everyone comfortable while it finishes putting them on."

Down in the square, Lord Mavros turned — smoothly, beautifully — and looked directly up at our balcony.

At us. At me.

He smiled the eleventh smile, or the twelfth; I'd lost count, and I think that was deliberate. And though there was nothing behind his eyes, nothing at all, no person, no awareness, only a garment fitted to the shape of a man — I felt, with absolute certainty, the way I feel arsenic in a spoon before it ever touches my tongue:

*Seen.*

The map against my chest went warm as a held coin, and beneath the city's noise I heard — faint, patient, amused — a voice that was not Aldric Vane's and not mine say, in the tone of a host greeting a guest who has finally arrived:

*Ah. The taster.*

Then it blinked, the way you close a book, and Lord Mavros turned away, and went on smiling at everyone.
