## 8. Poison Is a Language

The bread betrayed itself on the third bite.

I hadn't meant to take three bites. Serah had told me — twice, with the particular flatness she reserved for instructions she expected me to ignore — that we were to buy nothing in Correnholt except rope, dried meat, and silence. But the market square had smelled of woodsmoke and warm crust, and there was a baker's stall where an old woman sold dark rye loaves for two coppers, and I had been a food-taster for eleven years. Refusing bread when it is offered is like refusing to read a letter addressed to you.

So I bought one, and I ate, and on the third bite my tongue said: *there*.

Not poison. Not arsenic, not belladonna, not any of the forty-one compounds the king's stewards had fed me in graduated doses until my blood learned their names the way other children learn prayers. This was subtler. A faint bitterness at the back of the palate, arriving late, after the sweetness of the grain had already made its argument. It tasted — and I want to be precise here, because precision was the only religion I had ever been given — it tasted the way a long illness tastes. The way water tastes when you've been drinking it for weeks without noticing it has gone slightly wrong.

I stopped walking.

Serah noticed immediately. She had been walking half a step ahead, hood up, scanning the crowd with the restless economy of a woman who had spent three years being hunted, and when I halted she turned back with her hand already drifting toward her knife.

"What."

"The bread," I said. "There's something in it."

"Poison?"

"No." I looked down at the loaf in my hand, at the honest golden crumb, at the old woman three stalls away laughing with a customer. "Worse. Poison kills you. This *persuades* you."

* * *

I should explain what my tongue knows, because for most of my life it was the only thing about me worth explaining.

In the Valley of Stillness, where no magic can cross the boundary and nothing changes from season to season except the price of salt, I held the office of King's Taster. It sounds ceremonial. It was not. Every dish that reached the king's table passed first through my mouth, and every dish carried its micro-dose — a grain of arsenic in the morning broth, a drop of foxglove in the evening wine — because the stewards believed, with the patient logic of men who have never been poisoned themselves, that immunity is built the way a wall is built: one stone at a time.

I catalogued flavors the way monks catalogue sins. Bitter almond means cyanide; I could find it in a sauce at concentrations that would fell a horse. Metallic sweetness means lead. A numbing tingle at the tip of the tongue means aconite, and you have perhaps the length of a prayer to spit. For eleven years I ate death in homeopathic portions and grew so familiar with it that fear became a kind of arithmetic: *this much, this fast, survivable; this much, this slow, fatal.*

It was, I realize now, a strange way to raise a child. But it gave me a gift, and the gift was this: I did not merely detect poisons. I understood them. Each compound has a grammar. Arsenic is blunt, declarative. Belladonna is a liar — it flatters the pupil before it strangles the breath. And whatever was in that bread spoke a dialect I had never heard, but one whose family resemblance I recognized instantly, because I had spent years tasting something adjacent to it in the king's own kitchens without ever having a word for it.

That word, I now suspected, was *preparation*.

* * *

We took the loaf back to the room above the dyer's shop that Serah had rented under a name that wasn't hers, and I performed the only ritual I knew: I laid out my kit — the small mortar, the charcoal pencils, the strips of wool and silver leaf that a taster carries the way a surgeon carries blades — and I went to work.

Silver leaf told me the compound was organic, not mineral. Charcoal adsorbed it slowly, which meant large molecules, which meant — I ground a pinch of the crumb, steeped it, strained it, and held the filtrate against my tongue with the discipline of a man lowering himself into cold water — a decoction. Something brewed, not mined.

"It's in the flour," I said finally. "Not added after. Grown into the grain, or milled in. Which means it isn't one baker. It's the supply."

Serah stood by the window with her arms crossed, watching the street below through a gap in the shutter. "Correnholt feeds forty thousand people," she said. "And half the garrison towns between here and the capital draw from the same granaries. If someone wanted to dose a kingdom—"

"They wouldn't dose it all at once," I said. "You'd get riots. You'd get bodies, and bodies get investigated." I turned the loaf over in my hands. "You'd dose it thin. Barely perceptible. You'd let people eat it for years." I thought of Aldric's memories, the ones that bled into my skull uninvited: ministers with glassy eyes, smiling at nothing, agreeing with everything. "And you'd choose a compound that doesn't kill the will. One that *thins* it. Makes it pliable. Makes a man agreeable, suggestible, slow to question. You'd prepare the field before you planted anything in it."

"You're saying the Crown is farming people."

"I'm saying the Crown is *seasoning* them." The word came out of me with more fury than I'd intended, and I let it. "Like a marinade. You don't cook the meat yet. You just make sure that when the time comes, it falls apart at a touch."

Serah was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of its usual armor of sarcasm. "My father mapped the kingdom for thirty years," she said. "He wrote in his last letter that the roads were getting *quieter*. That people smiled too easily. I thought he was going mad." She laughed, short and bitter. "He was doing his job better than any of us knew."

* * *

Here is what fear does to me, or rather, what it used to do: it froze me. In the valley, fear was a failure of craft. A taster who trembles is a taster who misreads his tongue, and a misread tongue is a dead king. So I learned to convert terror into procedure, dread into checklist, and for eleven years the conversion was seamless.

But standing in that rented room over a dyer's shop in Correnholt, holding a loaf of bread that contained the slow murder of everyone I had met since crossing the valley's edge — the innkeeper who'd waved us in, the children chasing a hoop in the market square, the old woman selling rye for two coppers — the conversion failed. The fear stayed fear. It sat in my chest like swallowed stone.

And then it did something fear had never done before.

It turned into anger.

Not the hot, useless kind. The analytical kind. The kind that looks at a problem and says: *you are a compound. All things that are compounds can be traced.* I had spent my life identifying what was in a dish. Nobody had ever asked me to follow it backward — upstream, against the current, from mouth to mill to field to hand. But the logic was identical. Every poison enters the body by a road. Roads run in two directions.

"Serah," I said. "How many granaries does Correnholt have?"

She turned from the window. "Four major. The Crown leases them to merchant houses. Why?"

"Because I need to taste all four."

* * *

We spent two days doing it, and I will not pretend it was pleasant work.

Serah procured samples the way she did everything — efficiently, illegally, and with a competence that I was beginning to find distracting. A bribe to a carter here. A cut purse there. By the second evening we had flour from all four granaries laid out on the floorboards in neat lines, and I went down the row like a priest examining relics.

Three were clean. Honest grain, honest dust, the ordinary bitterness of poor milling and older storage.

The fourth — Granary Row, leased to House Mavros — sang the same wrong note as the bread.

I sat back on my heels and felt the anger consolidate into certainty. House Mavros. Lord Mavros, the minister Serah had struck three years ago, the man who *wasn't there anymore*, the vessel the Crown wore when the king slept. His house held the lease. His house held the flour. His house held forty thousand spoons, and every spoon was a small, patient lie.

"It's concentrated near the center of the stores," I told her, mapping it with my finger on the floorboards. "Older sacks are dosed heavier than new ones. They're building toward something — increasing the saturation as the solstice approaches. Whoever runs this operation has a schedule."

"Then we find the schedule."

"How? The ledgers will be guarded, and neither of us reads Mavros's cipher—"

"We don't need the ledgers." Serah crouched beside me, and in the lamplight her eyes had the hard bright look I was learning to fear and admire in equal measure. "Your tongue found the compound in finished flour. Can it find it stronger or weaker? Can you tell me — if we walked the supply backward — sack to cart, cart to warehouse, warehouse to source — whether we're getting warmer?"

I considered it. It was, I realized, exactly the skill the stewards had trained into me without knowing it. A taster learns not just *what* but *how much*. Concentration gradients. I could stand in a room and tell you which corner held the poisoned cup.

"Yes," I said. "I can taste the difference between a river and its spring."

"Then we go upstream." She stood, and there was something almost fierce in the set of her shoulders. "Sack to cart. Cart to mill. Mill to whatever field or fortress grows this filth. We follow it to the throat."

"And then?"

Her smile had no warmth in it at all. "And then we decide what to do when we find it."

* * *

What we found, three days later, at the end of a trail of bribed carters and one very unfortunate warehouse clerk, was a millhouse in the hills east of the city — and attached to it, a processing floor that made my stomach turn in ways my training never had.

They weren't growing the compound. They were brewing it, in copper vats, from a base of something dark and vegetal that even Aldric's memories couldn't name, and they were spraying it onto the grain as it dried — a fine mist, applied by men in leather masks who moved with the glassy calm I had seen in Aldric's recollections of the capital's ministers. The workers weren't prisoners. They weren't even unhappy. They worked twelve-hour shifts with placid smiles, and when Serah asked one — through a gap in the fence, playing a lost traveler — how he liked his employment, he said, "It's good work. Lord Mavros is generous. I don't know why anyone would say otherwise," and his eyes were the color of pond water, and I had to walk away before I was sick.

"They're already wearing some of them," I said when I could speak again. "The heavy exposure. These men are half-hollowed. They're not preparing vessels anymore, they're *practicing*."

Serah stood beside me on the ridge above the millhouse, looking down at the smoke rising from the drying floors, and I watched her arrive at the same conclusion I had, and I watched her jaw set against it.

"Forty thousand people eat from those granaries," she said quietly. "If we report this — to whom? The magistrates are appointed by the court. The court is the Crown. There is no one to report to."

"So what do we do?"

She was silent for a long time. Below us, the masked men worked their vats, patient as priests.

"We burn it," she said finally.

* * *

I want to record, honestly, that I hesitated.

Not because the millhouse deserved mercy. Because I was a taster, and tasters do not destroy; they detect, they report, they stand very still while larger men make decisions. My whole life had been a discipline of observation, and Serah was asking me to become an instrument of consequence.

But I thought of the old woman selling rye for two coppers. I thought of the children with the hoop. I thought of the king — the true king, buried alive inside his own body for forty years while a thing wore him like a coat — and I thought: *someone has to stop being still.*

The fire took quickly. Serah knew fire the way I knew flavor — its grammar, its patience, its points of entry. Lamp oil in the oil store, a fuse of twisted rag, and we were over the fence and up the ridge before the first vat caught. The compound, it turned out, burned beautifully. The smoke rolled black and sweet across the valley, and even at a distance of half a mile I could taste it on the air — the will-thinner, aerosolized, dying — and beneath it, unmistakably, the smell of the vats themselves splitting in the heat.

The half-hollowed workers wandered out into the yard and stood watching the flames with expressions of mild, pleasant confusion. None of them ran. None of them screamed. That was the worst part. That was the part I knew would stay with me.

"They'll wake up eventually," Serah said beside me. Her face was lit orange by the fire, and there was no satisfaction in it. "Some of them. The lightly dosed. The rest—" She didn't finish.

"The rest were already gone," I said.

Below us, a bell began to ring in the millhouse village. Then another. Then, far off toward Correnholt, the deep voice of the city bells answering, and I understood that our window was measured now in hours, maybe less. A granary fire was an accident. A millhouse producing the granary's secret harvest, burned on the same night, was a message — and messages, in a kingdom worn by the Hollow Crown, were answered.

Serah pulled her hood up. "We move tonight. North road, then west through the hill country. If we're fast, we're ghosts."

"And if we're slow?"

"Then we're examples."

We went down the ridge together, away from the burning, and behind us the black smoke climbed into the darkening sky like a signature written in a language only two people in the world could read — and somewhere below, I knew, riders were already being dispatched to teach us what happened to people who wrote back.
