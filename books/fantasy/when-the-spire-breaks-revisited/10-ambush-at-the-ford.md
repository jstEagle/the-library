## 10. Ambush at the Ford

**Serah**

I knew we were dead three miles before the river.

Not because I saw anything — seeing is for amateurs and the recently promoted. I knew because the birds had stopped. Not gone quiet; stopped. A wood full of thrushes does not simply decide to take a collective vow of silence unless something has convinced them, thoroughly and recently, that singing is fatal.

"The ford," I said, reining in at the tree line. "Something's wrong with it."

Cyrus squinted past me at the water glinting gray through the birches. He had the particular squint of a man who had spent his life judging things by taste rather than sight, which meant he was squinting at nothing useful, but I'd stopped holding that against him somewhere around Correnholt. Mostly stopped.

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong like a soup with no salt in it." I dismounted and loosened my sword in its sheath, a habit older than my disgrace. "The ford's a crossing point on the Salt Road. There should be traffic. Carts, pilgrims, smugglers, a toll-man robbing all three. Instead there's nothing, and nothing is never empty."

He looked at the road behind us, then ahead, doing the arithmetic I could see moving behind his eyes. To his credit he didn't argue. Three weeks of traveling together had taught him that when I said *wrong*, I didn't mean *unpleasant*. I meant *ambush*, usually.

We could have gone around. The river bent north through a gorge two days' hard ride out of our way, and pride is cheap when the alternative is arrows. But Aldric's memories were pulling Cyrus south — I could tell by the way his hand kept drifting to the map case, the way a man touches a bruise to check if it still hurts — and the map wanted the ford. The old man's ghost in his skull had crossed here a dozen times in life, and apparently death hadn't cured him of opinions about routes.

So we did the stupid thing, because the stupid thing was also the mapped thing, and because I told myself I was wrong about the birds.

I want it noted, wherever such things are noted, that I was not wrong about the birds.

* * *

**Cyrus**

The first dart took Serah in the shoulder as we reached the near bank, and I learned something about myself that I will spend the rest of my life not being grateful for.

Here is what happened, as best I can reconstruct it from the parts that weren't screaming: one moment we were leading the horses into the shallows, water tugging at our boots, the far bank an ordinary wall of alder and afternoon shadow. The next, a sound like a wasp clearing its throat, and Serah staggered, swore with impressive fluency, and went down on one knee in the current.

"Ambush!" she shouted, which seemed redundant, and then, worse, "Left! They're in the trees left!"

I am not a fighter. Let's be clear about that. My weapons are a spoon, an educated palate, and the ability to swallow small amounts of terrible things without dying, none of which have ever been issued alongside a shield. When the second and third darts came out of the alders — aimed at me, I realized later, because I was the one they'd been sent to take alive — I did what any reasonable man would do.

I flinched.

And then I didn't die.

The first dart hit me high in the chest, just below the collarbone. I felt the little bite of it, the sting, the bloom of something cold spreading under my skin — and my body, my stupid, patient, twenty years of arsenic-and-foxglove-trained body, looked at the poison arriving in my blood the way a customs officer looks at a merchant's declared goods. *Yes. We've seen this before. Move along.*

The cold spread, reached for my heartbeat, found nothing it recognized, and dissolved.

The second dart caught my arm. Different compound — sweeter, I could actually taste it on the air, a cloying almond-rot note that meant some cousin of hemlock — and my blood met it with the weary sigh of a doorman who has turned away this visitor every night for a decade.

I stood in the middle of a river with two darts sticking out of me, feeling mildly seasick and otherwise entirely fine, while men in the treeline began shouting at each other in the aggrieved tones of professionals whose simple plan has developed complications.

"Cyrus!" Serah was up somehow, sword drawn, back pressed to her horse's flank, blood sheeting down her arm where a dart had torn through the muscle instead of drugging it. She'd gotten leather and luck; I'd gotten chemistry. "Are you hit?"

"Yes!"

"Badly?"

"No?" It came out as a question even to me. I pulled the darts out, one, two, and looked at the barbs, at the dark smear on them, at the faint sweet smell rising off the wound. "They're poisoned. Drugged. I think—" I flexed my fingers. They obeyed. "I think they can't poison me."

"Then stop standing there and get behind the horse!"

It was good advice. I took it.

* * *

**Serah**

Six of them. Maybe seven. Crossbows in the alders, two more downstream where the bank dipped — the classic pincer, textbook, the kind of ambush you learn to run toward in training because running away gets you shot in the back. I counted muzzle-flashes of movement, marked the reload rhythm, and did the math every soldier does and hates: numbers bad, ground bad, but the river was waist-deep and slow, and men in armor cross rivers badly.

Cyrus had his horse between himself and the trees, crouched, and I saw him do something strange — press his bleeding forearm to his lips and *taste* the wound.

Later I would ask him about that. Later he would explain it in that flat, appalled voice he used for his own body's betrayals: that the poison in the barb told him everything, the dose, the compound, the intent. That he'd known in one touch of tongue to skin that they wanted him sleeping, not dead, and that whoever had written their orders had written *the taster unharmed*.

At the time, all I saw was a food-taster licking his own blood in the middle of an ambush, and I decided there would be time to be disturbed about it if we lived.

"Hold the horse!" I shouted, and went left.

Going left was against every instinct I owned. Left was where the crossbows were. But left was also a fallen alder with a root-ball like a broken wall, and cover beats courage, and courage mostly gets you buried anyway. I ran the shallow edge, low, letting the water noise eat my footsteps, and came around the root-ball as the nearest crossbowman stood to reset his windlass.

He was young. That registered, the way it always registers, uselessly. Then he saw me and fumbled his crank, and I didn't give him the second he needed to find his discipline. My blade took his weapon aside and my boot took his knee and the river took the rest of him, splashing, scrambling, alive — I wasn't killing boys over a ford I didn't even want to cross — and then the world went loud.

Crossbow bolts hissed past. One opened a seam along my sleeve and kissed the skin beneath, a hot line. Someone downstream was shouting orders in a voice too smooth for a soldier, a court voice, a *glass-eyed* voice, and hearing it made my stomach drop lower than any bolt could. Mavros's people. Or Mavros's people's people. The Crown didn't send soldiers after us anymore; it sent suggestions wearing uniforms.

"Cyrus!" I put my back to the root-ball. "The far bank! Go now, straight across, don't stop for anything—"

"I'm not leaving you!"

"You're not *leaving* me, you're *going first*! There's a difference and it's called tactics!" I risked a look. Two of them were wading into the river after him, swords drawn, moving with that wrong, unhurried confidence the hollowed ones had — pain meant nothing to them, fear meant nothing, and that made them slower than frightened men and infinitely harder to frighten. "Cyrus, they won't cut you down, they'll try to take you! Use that!"

"Use *what*? I'm a *taster!*"

"Then taste them!"

I still don't know what I meant by that. I think I meant: you are stranger than either of us has admitted, and strange things win fights.

* * *

**Cyrus**

*Taste them,* she said, and I have replayed that sentence many times since, trying to find the version where it makes sense, and the closest I can come is this:

They grabbed me.

Both of them, wading out of the spray, hands closing on my arms with the calm of men collecting laundry, and the taller one pressed a cloth against my face — soaked, I knew instantly, in that same almond-rot sweetness, ten times the dose of the darts. Enough to drop a horse. Enough to stop a horse.

And my body *refused it.*

I felt the poison arrive like a guest at a door, and I felt my blood — my careful, cultivated, decades-in-the-making blood — rise up around it like a crowd around a pickpocket. Not immune, exactly. Immunity is a wall. This was something else. Every cell I owned had spent twenty years learning poisons one micro-dose at a time, and they met this one the way a scholar meets a text he's read a thousand times: with recognition, with contempt, with the swift efficient violence of the very bored.

The tall man held the cloth to my face for a count of ten. At six, his eyes changed. He could feel it failing — they could always feel it failing, whatever the Crown used them for let them feel that much — and confusion moved across his face like weather.

Confusion was a crack, and cracks are doors, and I am ashamed of what I did next only because I enjoyed it.

I headbutted him.

Under-steward Vessin taught me that, actually, in the kitchens, years ago, as a joke: *the forehead is the only honest part of a man, Nakamura, everything else negotiates.* The hollowed don't negotiate. But their foreheads were as honest as anyone's, and mine connected with the bridge of his nose with a sound like a dropped melon, and his grip died, and I shoved him into the current and turned to the second one already reaching for me —

— and the second one stopped.

Stopped like a clock. Eyes glassy, mouth slack, staring past me at something over my shoulder, and I heard Serah's voice behind me, ragged and furious, saying words I didn't catch, and the man folded into the water like a coat coming off a hook.

She'd gotten behind them somehow, bloody arm and all, and done something brutal and efficient to the base of his skull that I chose not to examine.

"River," she gasped. "Now. Both horses. Move."

We moved.

* * *

**Serah**

Bolts chased us across the ford. One took my horse through the neck — poor beast screamed the way horses do, apologetically even in agony — and went down in the shallows, and I made the choice you make in war, the one that doesn't wash out, and left him. I got up behind Cyrus on his gelding, which objected to carrying two adults with the moral indignation of his entire species, and we crashed up the far bank into the alders with the smooth-voiced officer still shouting coordinates behind us in a tone I'll hear in dreams until I die.

Calm. He sounded *calm.* That was the worst of it. Somewhere on that bank a man with a court voice was directing murder the way a steward directs table settings, and his voice never once rose.

We rode hard for an hour. Two. Until the trees thickened and the ground rose and even the most optimistic pursuer would call for fresh horses. Then, and only then, Cyrus reined in at a stream, slid off, and turned to help me down, and I discovered that my shoulder had been making independent decisions for some time.

The dart had gone through above the breastbone, clean through the meat, in and out, which is the best place to be stabbed if you insist on being stabbed. But the barb had carried something — I'd felt it from the first minute, a warmth under the cold, a looseness climbing my arm — and when I swung down off the horse my legs informed me they had resigned.

Cyrus caught me. Of course he caught me; the man catches things for a living.

"Sit," he said, in the voice he probably used on kings worried about their soup. "Don't argue. You've lost blood and you're dosed with something."

"How do you know?"

"Because your pupils are wrong." He lowered me against a birch with a gentleness that sat oddly on those precise kitchen hands. "And because I can smell it on the wound. Hold still."

He knelt, tore my sleeve open — apologizing, absurdly, mid-rip — and then did the thing again, the thing I'd seen at the ford. He bent his head and touched his tongue to the entry wound, quick, clinical, a physician's gesture performed by a man who tasted arsenic before breakfast.

I should have objected. There is a list of objections and it begins with *propriety* and ends with *what in the nine hells.* What I said was:

"Well?"

His face had gone strange. Not disgusted. *Reading.*

"It's valerian and something else. Something I don't know." His voice was quiet, focused, almost tender in the way of a man listening to music. "A sedative, but threaded with a binding agent. It wants to hold the wound open. Keep it from clotting. Whoever made these darts wanted a wounded person who wouldn't heal and couldn't fight." A pause. "It's clever. It's really well made. I hate it."

"That's a disturbing way to compliment someone's craftsmanship."

"I know." He was already working, tearing strips from his shirt, packing the wound with moss he crushed and sniffed and rejected twice before finding the right kind, mixing something from the pouch at his belt — antidote powders, I realized, the tools of his old trade, the ones he'd sworn he'd never need outside the valley. "Hold still. This next part tastes appalling and you're going to feel worse before better."

"Is there any part of you," I asked, as he pressed the bitter paste past my teeth, "that isn't a poison cabinet?"

"My heart," he said, not looking up, and then seemed to hear himself, and went very red, and busied himself with the bandage.

I laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt, and I laughed anyway, because the alternative was admitting that the sentence had landed somewhere beneath the wound, in the soft unprotected country underneath, and I wasn't ready to garrison it.

* * *

**Cyrus**

She fell asleep against the birch within the hour, fever-bright, one hand still loosely closed around her sword hilt even unconscious, which struck me as the most Serah thing I had ever witnessed.

I sat across the small fire and took inventory.

Two darts' worth of compounds my body had shrugged off like rain. A cloth soaked in enough sedative to fell a cart-horse, refused outright. And underneath all of it, new since the valley, new since the map, a faint continuous awareness I couldn't shut off — the world arriving on my tongue whether I wanted it or not. The stream behind us tasted of iron and snowmelt and, faintly, miles upstream, of sheep. The smoke tasted of pine and of Serah's blood, which smelled — I checked this three times, horrified — *medicinal* to me now, legible, annotated. My own blood, drying on my collar, tasted of the dart-poison breaking apart, molecule by molecule, and I could name the stages.

Twenty years in the Valley of Stillness, I had thought immunity was armor. A finished thing. Tonight my own body had shown me it was something else: an army, drilled and waiting, and armies, once roused, do not stand down.

Which raised the question I had been circling for days and could no longer avoid.

If I could hold hemlock and valerium and whatever the Crown's alchemists brewed for their darts — if my tongue could carry all of that and live — what else could it carry?

Aldric's memories stirred at the edge of my mind, the way they did when I approached a thought the dead man had also approached. Not words. Just pressure. A sense of a circle chalked on stone, of a king's jaw held firm in living hands, of a final memory that the old surveyor had wrapped in layers and hidden deep, the way you hide a thing you hope no one you love will ever have to open.

"Not yet," I told him, out loud, to the fire.

Across the flames, Serah shivered in her sleep and murmured something. I moved to sit closer, because her fever was climbing faster than my paste should have allowed, and when I took her wrist to check her pulse I saw why.

Around the wound, spreading outward from the punctures like frost on glass, the skin had gone faintly, unmistakably grey.

The binding agent. The one I'd named, the one I'd *tasted* — I'd read it wrong, or half-wrong, or my own immunity had blinded me, because what was trivial to my blood was not trivial to hers, and it wasn't holding the wound open.

It was holding something else open. A door. And through it, thin as thread but threading deeper by the hour, I caught the taste I knew from Correnholt, from the grain, from Lord Mavros's smiling glassy face.

Will-thinner. Crown-work. In her blood, headed for her heart.

Her eyes fluttered open, found mine, and managed, fever-slurred but unmistakably her: "You've got that face. The one where the news is bad and you're deciding how much to tell me."

I looked at the fire, and the map case, and the long night ahead, and told her the truth.

All of it.
