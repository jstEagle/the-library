## 12. The Unbinding Rite

The memory came for me at dawn, the way the worst of them always did — not gently, not gradually, but like a trapdoor opening beneath a man who had just begun to believe the floor was solid.

I woke with Aldric Vane's hands on my hands.

Not his ghost. Not a vision. His *memory* of his own hands, forty years dead, holding a pen over vellum, and I was inside them the way you are inside your own skin, watching him write the last lines of the map in a shaking script while somewhere above him bells tolled a rhythm no city keeps. He was dying as he wrote it. I could taste that too — blood and iron and lamp-oil smoke, the particular flavor of a man working past the point where work is possible.

*They will need a taster,* he wrote, and I thought, absurdly, *how nice, he's thinking of me,* before I understood what he meant.

He wasn't writing instructions for finding something.

He was writing specifications for a person.

---

Let me back up, because my mind did not cooperate that morning and I see no reason mine should behave better than it did then.

We had made camp three days out from Correnholt, in the ruins of a shepherd's cot that Serah swore had been abandoned since the border wars. She was recovering — the wound at her shoulder closing from angry red to sullen pink, the fever finally broken, though she still slept like a soldier rather than a person: on her side, knife hand free, one eye apparently never fully committed to being closed.

I had spent those three days doing what I do best, which is tasting things. The stream water: clean, faintly of limestone, safe. The dried meat from Correnholt: peppered heavily enough to mask something, and when I isolated it, the something was harmless — a merchant cutting costs with older stock, nothing more. I catalogued each flavor the way a monk counts beads, because cataloguing flavors was the only prayer I knew.

And underneath the tasting, quietly, without letting myself look at it directly, I was practicing.

You already know this if you've been paying attention, which Serah would say I should assume people are, since assuming otherwise is how guards get killed. On the second night I'd held a crushed leaf of wolfsbane against my tongue for the count of sixty. On the third, a bead of hemlock sap, which numbed my mouth so thoroughly I tasted nothing else until noon. My body shrugged these off the way it shrugs off everything — thirty years of micro-doses building a fortress in my blood, dose by careful dose, until poison had become less an enemy than an acquaintance I'd grown tired of entertaining.

I told myself I was practicing because knowledge is armor. I told myself it was habit. I am, by profession, an excellent liar to myself; I once convinced myself I enjoyed tripe.

Then came dawn, and Aldric's final memory, and the lie collapsed like a soufflé in a slammed door.

---

Here is what he wrote, here in the last hour of his life, with bells tolling wrong overhead:

*A rite exists. I found it in the archives under the old temple, in a hand older than the kingdom. It is called the Unbinding, and it does exactly what its name promises: it takes a thing that has bound itself to a soul and unwinds the binding, thread by thread.*

*It requires three things. A circle held by someone who has sworn an oath and broken it — broken oaths leave doors, the archive says, and the rite walks through them. That part Serah can manage; gods forgive me for being grateful her disgrace is useful.*

*It requires the moment of solstice, when the boundary between wearing and worn grows thin.*

*And it requires a tongue. A taster's tongue. Someone who can take the Crown's essence into their mouth — onto their tongue, hold it there, unswallowed, while the rite speaks through them — without dying. Because the essence is poison, the oldest poison, the poison of being consumed, and any ordinary mouth would be ash before the first verse finished.*

*Someone immune enough to hold death itself and live.*

*I am not such a person. There may be no such person left. But the map knows what it seeks. Maps always do. I have fed it enough of myself that it will go looking, and it will not stop looking until it finds—*

The memory ended there. Ink blot. His hand had given out mid-word.

But I was still inside him, inside the dying, and I felt what he felt in that final moment — not fear for himself but a terrible, specific hope, the hope of a man who has realized his life's work will outlive him and go hunting without him. And threaded through that hope, faint as saffron in a whole pot of stew, I felt the map itself respond.

Because the map was there, in the memory, under his pen. And the map — I cannot explain this better than I experienced it — *tasted me back.*

It had been tasting me since the courier died in my arms. Every mile I carried it, every drop of my immunity steeping into the leather, every night it glowed faintly against my chest like a tongue pressed to the roof of a mouth — it hadn't been guiding me. Or rather, it had been guiding me the way a fishhook guides a fish. It was seeking a taster. It had been seeking a taster across twenty years and half a kingdom.

And it had found one.

---

I sat up too fast and vomited into the ashes of our fire.

Serah was awake instantly, knife out, scanning the treeline before she even looked at me — three years of hunting will do that to a woman. Then she saw what I was doing, which was emptying my stomach of nothing, since I hadn't eaten, and lowered the blade with the expression of someone who has learned that the worst threats rarely carry knives.

"Nightmare?" she said.

"Worse." I wiped my mouth. "Clarity."

She waited. She's good at waiting. It's one of the things about her that infuriates me, that patient stillness, like a drawn bow deciding whether you're worth the arrow.

So I told her. All of it — Aldric's final memory, the rite, the three requirements, the tongue. I kept my voice level, professional, the voice I used announcing arsenic in the king's wine. I got all the way to the end. *The map was seeking a taster. It found me. It was always going to find me. My mother didn't send it to the world in general, Serah. She sent it to me specifically, or it went looking for me specifically, or — either way, I'm not the messenger. I'm the cargo.*

Silence. A bird started up somewhere, idiotically cheerful.

"No," Serah said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"I heard all of it. I was listening the entire time, which is more than you were doing, because if you'd been listening to yourself you'd have noticed you were describing your own funeral with the enthusiasm of a man reading a menu." She stood, wincing at the shoulder, and planted herself between me and the map case like geography. "No."

"Serah."

"The rite needs a taster's tongue. Fine. We find another taster."

"There are perhaps six in the kingdom. Five serve the Crown's court already, which means they're seasoned vessels-in-waiting, and the sixth is eighty and lives in the valley, where magic can't follow and neither can we."

"Then we find a seventh."

"With what time? Solstice is—" I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do. "Nine days."

"Then we find one in nine days."

"And if we don't? If we stand outside the throne room with your circle and your broken oath and your thin boundary, and the only tongue in the world that can hold the essence is sitting back at camp guarding the supplies?" I stood too. We were close now, close enough that I could smell woodsmoke and fever-sweat still lingering in her hair, and I hated that I noticed. "Serah. The king has been prisoner for forty years. Mavros and whatever's left of the council hollow out a new vessel every season. Children eat grain that thins their wills. You struck a minister because you saw what he really was — you told me that, the first night, you told me you'd have burned the whole palace to stop it. Here's the fire."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what it is. It's the thing you've wanted for three years, handed to you wrapped in ribbon, and the only cost is me."

The bird shut up, sensibly.

Serah looked at me for a long moment, and I watched something move behind her eyes that I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors my whole life: the professional recalculating. The taster weighing the dish. She was measuring whether I was right, and worse, she was finding that I was.

"Don't," I said softly. "Don't you dare start planning around it."

"I'm not planning around anything." Her jaw set. "I'm telling you the answer is no, and I will keep telling you the answer is no, and if necessary I will tie you to a tree and attend the solstice alone."

"You'd fail. The rite fails without the tongue. You'd burn three years of work and the king would merge with that thing forever, and every person in this kingdom would spend eternity as furniture."

"Then we'll both fail together, because I am not delivering you to that throne room like a tray of canapés!"

Her voice cracked on the last word and I understood, suddenly, completely, that this was not about tactics. I stood there with my horror and my clarity and my stupid useful tongue, and she stood there with three years of guilt and one disgraced oath and a shoulder wound she'd earned protecting a stranger who happened to be carrying her father's dying wish, and between us lay the simple unbearable fact that we had each decided, separately and silently, to die for the other, and neither of us had agreed to let the other do it.

Love, I thought. So this is the flavor. Bitter on the approach, and then it won't leave.

---

We argued for another hour. I won't reproduce it; arguments are like recipes, in that the finished dish matters and the intermediate messes don't. Suffice to say I deployed logic and she deployed stubbornness and we each landed several blows below the belt — she called me a coward dressed as a martyr, which was unfair and accurate, and I called her a guard who'd failed one king so badly she needed to fail a second, which was crueler than anything I've ever said sober, and I regretted it before it finished leaving my mouth.

She went quiet after that. Quiet Serah is worse than shouting Serah. Shouting Serah is a storm; quiet Serah is the tide, and tides move stones.

"Your mother," she said at last, "died carrying that map."

"Yes."

"She crossed the valley ward somehow, wounded, hunted, and she put it in the hands of the one person alive who could use it. Do you think she didn't know what it required? She raised the cartographer's ghost in her own blood to draw it. She knew about the tongue, Cyrus. Of course she knew. And she brought it to you anyway." Serah's voice was very gentle now, which from her was the most dangerous register of all. "Either she didn't understand what she was asking. Or she trusted you to find another way. Which do you want it to be?"

I didn't answer. The honest answer was *the first one*, because the first one hurt less, and I have built my whole life around choosing the hurt that comes measured, in doses, on schedule.

"There's another way," I said, without conviction.

"Find it." She turned away, began kicking dirt over the fire ashes with unnecessary violence. "You're the finest taster in the kingdom. Taste the problem. Find the other way. Nine days."

"And if there isn't one?"

She stopped kicking. Her back was to me, her good shoulder squared like a wall, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet and absolute, the voice she must have used giving orders before the fall.

"Then I'll stand in that circle and hold it open, and you'll pour that poison down your own throat, and I will spend whatever remains of my life making sure the kingdom knows the name Cyrus Nakamura. But I will hate you. Do you understand? I'll free the king and save the world and hate you every single day of my long, loud, insufferable life, because you'll have made me survive you, and surviving people is the one thing I never learned how to do."

* * *

That night I took out the map and held it by firelight, alone, while Serah pretended to sleep with her knife hand curled and open.

The leather was warm. It was always warm now, blood-temperature, like something freshly killed or freshly born. And as I turned it, tracing the roads that remembered, the final layer — my mother's layer, the one beneath Aldric's ink — shimmered faintly at the edges, and for the first time I felt it not as a guide but as a summons.

*Come*, said the map, in a voice that was not Aldric's and not quite my mother's and not quite mine. *Come to the capital. Come to the solstice. Come and be tasted.*

Nine days.

In the dark, very carefully, I placed a drop of concentrated nightshade on my tongue, and counted, and did not die.

Somewhere behind me, Serah's breathing changed. She had watched the whole thing. She said nothing at all — and that silence was louder than any argument we'd ever had, and it sounded, unmistakably, like a countdown.
