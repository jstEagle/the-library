## 13. The Solstice Clock

Eleven days.

I did the arithmetic three times that morning, the way you check a lock you already know is bolted. Eleven days until solstice. Nine days of hard road to the capital if we didn't stop, six if we pushed and the weather held, four if everything went wrong in exactly the ways I could predict and none of the ways I couldn't. Subtract a day for the ford, because fords are where ambushes live. Subtract another for Cyrus, who walked like a man carrying furniture in his skull — which, to be fair, he was.

Seven days. Give or take.

Give or take was doing a lot of work in that sentence.

We'd learned it from Aldric's memories two nights before, in the ruin of a waystation chapel: the Crown bound itself by degrees, like ivy on a wall, and every solstice it drove its roots deeper into whatever soil it had chosen. Forty years of solstices had made the king mostly root, mostly wall. But there was one more merging left — the final one, the one where the ivy stopped being a guest and became the house. Aldric had written it in his own hand at the edge of the map, letters gone shaky: *At the longest light it takes all. After, no rite will reach him.*

*No rite will reach him.* Which meant no rite would reach *us*, either, since our entire plan consisted of a dead man's rite, a taster's tongue, and my stubborn refusal to die before this was finished.

I spread the map across an old millstone outside the chapel and weighted its corners with stones. Dawn was coming up grey over the hills, the kind of light that makes even healthy country look like it's recovering from something. Cyrus was still asleep inside — or pretending to be. He'd taken to sleeping badly, or sleeping loudly, muttering in a voice that wasn't quite his own, half his and half a dead surveyor's, arguing about roads that hadn't existed for thirty years.

I planned around him the way you plan around weather. You don't plan around rain by wishing; you plan around rain by deciding what you'll do when it falls.

So. Seven days of travel if nothing broke. One day in the capital to find a way into the palace that didn't involve dying at the gate. Two days of margin for the rite itself — Aldric's memory of it was fragmentary, a circle of salt and iron, words in a language the kingdom had stopped speaking, and a tongue that could hold what no tongue should hold. I'd need time to prepare the circle. I'd need time I probably wouldn't have.

That left the part I didn't write down, because I never wrote it down, because writing it down would make it a line in a plan and lines in plans have a way of coming true.

The rite needed a mouth that poison couldn't kill.

There was exactly one of those within a thousand miles, and he was asleep in the chapel, dreaming someone else's dreams, and every calculation I ran ended at the same place: *the taster holds the essence on his tongue while I hold the circle, and then—*

And then what?

Aldric's memory didn't say. The rite unbound the king; it said nothing about the man holding the poison. A vessel that full might simply pour back out. It might burn through him the way arsenic burns through everyone else, forty years of immunity spent in a single swallow. It might do something worse, something the dead cartographer hadn't lived long enough to learn.

I refused to calculate that column. I left it blank in my head, a hole in the ledger shaped exactly like a man who catalogued flavors for a living and apologized when he stepped on your foot.

Because here is the thing about planning around a person's death: the moment you put it in the plan, you've agreed to it. And I had struck one hollowed minister across the face and lost everything for it — rank, name, home, the whole architecture of my life — and people had told me afterward that it was reckless, that I'd thrown away my future over a moment's horror. They were wrong. I'd thrown away my future because I looked at Lord Mavros and saw that nobody else was willing to say what they saw. That was the whole of my crime: I said it out loud.

I intended to keep saying things out loud. Including the thing about how Cyrus Nakamura was going to walk out of that throne room with his ridiculous precise tongue intact, because I would be holding the circle, and I held things.

"You're staring at the map like it owes you money."

He'd come out without me hearing him. He did that now — moved quietly, ate quietly, existed at the edge of rooms. Three weeks on the road had worn him down from soft to spare, but his eyes were the same: watchful, dry, faintly amused at a world that kept failing to be as orderly as a well-run kitchen.

"It does," I said. "Everything old owes somebody something."

He crouched next to the millstone and studied the route with his hands behind his back, the posture of a man inspecting a delivery of fish he already suspected was off. "You've marked two routes to the capital."

"The river road's faster. The high road's less watched."

"And you've chosen?"

"I'm choosing." I tapped the river road. "We go fast and lie about who we are."

"Your lies are terrible."

"My lies are adequate. Your face is the problem. You look like a man reading everyone's mind, which, unfortunately—" I tapped his temple, where the cartographer's ghost lived, "—you are."

He almost smiled. Then he looked at the map a moment longer, and the almost-smile went somewhere else. "Eleven days," he said.

"Ten and a half. We lost the dawn arguing with your ghost about whether the bridge at Marrow Ford still stands."

"He says it doesn't."

"He also says the king has a kind face. Dead men are sentimental."

Cyrus straightened and looked east, toward the capital, though from here there was nothing to see but hills and haze. "Serah. If I asked you what happens after the rite—"

"You'd get the same answer you got last night, and the night before, and the night before that."

"Which is?"

"That we'll find out after." I rolled the map closed with more force than parchment deserved. "Get your pack. If we make twenty miles today, we sleep under a roof tomorrow."

He went. He always went, when I shut a door in conversation; he was too well-mannered to pick locks. Which was why it took me two more days to notice he'd been picking them anyway.

* * *

I caught him on the tenth night, in the shell of a shepherd's hut below the last ridge before the capital plain.

It was my fault for waking. I'd been dreaming about Mavros — about the flatness behind his smile, the flower pressed flat in a book of flesh — and I woke with my hand already on my knife and my heart going like a drum corps. The hut was dark. The fire had burned to a red memory of itself. And across the ashes, sitting cross-legged like a man at prayer, was Cyrus, with a small clay pot in his lap and a look on his face I recognized from churches and deathbeds.

"Cyrus."

He didn't startle. That frightened me more than anything else could have. "You should sleep," he said. "Long day tomorrow."

"What's in the pot."

Nothing, for a moment. Just the fire ticking, and the wind worrying the loose door, and his silhouette very still.

"Wolfsbane," he said finally. "Prepared. Weak. Weak-ish." He turned the pot so the firelight caught it. "I've been working up the concentration for nine nights."

Nine nights. Every night I thought he was sleeping badly, he'd been sitting in the dark teaching his tongue to hold death, alone, without telling me, the way a man pays debts he doesn't want his family to know about.

"Show me your mouth," I said, which came out sounding like a threat, and I suppose it was one.

"Serah—"

"*Show me.*"

He opened his mouth. I leaned close with the ember of a branch for light. His tongue was blackened at the tip, the way fruit goes bad from the inside — not wounded exactly, but *changed*, stained with everything he'd been asking it to carry. There were older marks beneath it, faded rings like the growth lines of a tree, nine nights of rings.

I sat back. I discovered I was holding the burning branch so tightly the bark was charring against my palm, and I made myself let go.

"How much can you hold?" My voice came out level. I was proud of that. It was the only thing about me that was level.

"Four minutes at current strength. Maybe five." He said it the way he said everything — a sommelier describing a vintage. "The trick isn't resistance. Anyone can clench their jaw against pain. The trick is *welcome*. You have to treat it like a flavor. Catalogue it. Let it be what it is without flinching. Poison is a language, Serah. I'm just becoming fluent."

"And after five minutes?"

"After five minutes, the flavor starts writing itself on the page." He touched his tongue-tip, lightly, matter-of-fact, the way you'd touch a scar you'd decided to stop resenting. "But the rite shouldn't need more than that. Aldric's memory is clear on the dose. Clearer than it is on anything else, actually, which tells you something about where his mind was when he wrote it."

"Stop." The word cracked out of me harder than I meant. Somewhere outside, a night bird went silent, offended. "Stop talking about it like a recipe."

"What would you like me to talk about it like?"

"A person. You're talking about yourself like a person who has already been spent. Like a coin on a table. And I am telling you—" I heard my voice rising and couldn't stop it, three years of exiled fury finding its target at last, "—I am telling you that I did not lose everything I had to stand in a circle and hold the door while you feed yourself to a grief the size of a kingdom. That's not a plan. That's a eulogy with steps."

He set the pot down between us, carefully, precisely, the way he did everything. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough to shame mine.

"When the courier died in my arms, she called me by name. She knew me, Serah. She'd been carrying that map toward me for — maybe years. Maybe her whole life. Someone drew that map knowing it would reach a boy in the Valley of Stillness with a tongue like mine." He looked at the embers. "Do you understand what that means? Someone loved me in advance. Someone I don't even remember planned my whole life around this. I'm not going to waste the shape she made me in because it's dangerous. Dangerous is what I'm *for*."

"That's not love," I said. "Love doesn't spend people. Love doesn't draw maps to other people's funerals."

"Doesn't it?" He smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd ever seen on him, gentle and finished. "You've been planning around my survival since the millstone. I've watched you do it. You leave a hole in every route where the answer should be. You think I don't see it? I taste everything, Serah. Including what people refuse to say."

For a long moment neither of us moved. The fire settled. Somewhere above us, on the ridge, the world went on being indifferent.

Then I crossed the ashes and took his face in both hands — carefully, because of the tongue, because of everything — and I kissed him, ash-smelling and furious, and when I pulled back I said, "New rule. Nobody gets to decide alone. Not you. Not me. Not ghosts in maps. Whatever happens at solstice, we decide it together, and until then you practice your poisons *where I can see you*, because if you're going to do something stupid and brave, you'll do it stupid and brave in front of me."

His breath came out unsteady. For once, the taster had tasted something he hadn't expected.

"Together," he agreed.

We sat shoulder to shoulder until the fire died, and neither of us slept, and neither of us mentioned the hole in the plan again. But we both knew it was there. You can't un-know a hole. You can only decide, together, what shape of hope you're going to pour into it.

We crested the ridge at first light, and the capital opened below us in the valley of its river — white banners everywhere, hanging from every tower and bridge, thousands of them, rippling in the dawn wind like a field of pale flowers.

Solstice banners. Except solstice was still ten days away, and no festival in the history of the kingdom had ever been announced this early, or draped this wide, or prepared with such quiet, patient thoroughness.

The city wasn't celebrating. The city was being dressed.

And standing at the gates, checking travelers one by one with glassy, courteous eyes, were guards wearing the king's colors — and among them, smiling, waving people forward, stood a figure I knew.

Lord Mavros.

Alive. Smiling. And looking up the road directly at us.
