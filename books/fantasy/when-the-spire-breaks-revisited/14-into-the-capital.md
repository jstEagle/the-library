## 14. Into the Capital

*Cyrus*

The capital announced itself the way a rich man announces himself: loudly, and with other people's money.

We crested the ridge an hour past dawn and there it was below us — Vael Arden, the City of Bells — wearing white. Not snow-white, not the honest white of linen hung out to dry. A ceremonial white, banners on every spire and bridge and beggar's crook, thousands of them, all identical, all new, rippling in the wind like a field of teeth.

"Who commissioned those?" I asked.

"No one remembers," Serah said. "That's the point."

She'd told me this on the road, twice, in her flat soldier's voice, the way she delivered everything she was afraid of. The solstice approached and the city dressed itself for it, and when you asked a banner-seller who had ordered the cloth, he'd smile at you with glassy patience and say the orders had always been there, hadn't they? The ledgers said so. The ledgers always said so.

I had expected to smell the city first — woodsmoke, sewage, horses, the great unwashed democracy of a hundred thousand people pressed behind walls. Instead, from the ridge, in the wind, I tasted it.

Sweetness.

Not honey-sweet. Not fruit. Something older and more patient, like milk just beginning to turn, or the smell of a candle guttering in a room where no one has noticed. It lay over the whole valley of the city like a glaze.

"Cyrus." Serah's hand was on my arm. "You've gone pale."

"It's everywhere," I said. "The whole city. It tastes—" I hunted for the word the way I'd hunt for an off note in a sauce. "It tastes *expectant*."

"That's new," she said grimly. "Three years ago you couldn't have tasted it from outside the walls."

"The map is getting stronger." I touched my chest, where the leather case rode against my ribs like a second heart. Aldric Vane's memories had stopped arriving as intrusions and started arriving as furniture. They simply lived in me now, and I moved around them. "Or I'm getting better at listening."

"We go in quiet," Serah said. "We don't taste anything else. We get eyes on the palace, we find the old servant tunnels Aldric marked, and we do not—"

"—do anything clever," I finished. "Yes. You've mentioned."

"You have the look of a man about to do something clever."

"I always look like that. It's the face I was born with."

She almost smiled. On Serah, an almost-smile was a full celebration elsewhere. Then she pulled her hood up, shouldered her pack, and started down the switchback road, and I followed her into the sweetness.

* * *

*Serah*

The last time I entered Vael Arden, I came through the Guard's Gate in royal blue with a sword at my hip and a father's grave three weeks fresh. They struck me off the rolls that same week. Struck my name, struck my oath, struck the minister's blood from my knuckles with official soap and called it justice.

This time I came through the Tanners' Gate in a carter's coat beside a man who flinched at loud smells, and nobody looked at me at all. There are worse disguises than disgrace. Disgrace teaches you exactly how invisible you can be.

Cyrus was doing his taster-walk — that careful, cataloguing stride, chin slightly lifted, as if the air itself were a dish sent up from a kitchen he intended to critique. I'd learned to read it over eleven days of road. When his lips parted a fraction, he was tasting something. When his brow creased, whatever he was tasting displeased him.

His brow had been creased since the ridge.

"The wells," he murmured, not moving his lips, a habit he'd developed for talking to me in crowds and which made him look, I thought, mildly haunted. "There's something in the water. Faint. Sweetness again — the same note. And the bread. I smelled a baker's row back there. The grain carries it too."

"Mavros's compound," I said. "The will-thinner. You said it takes months of exposure."

"Months to hollow someone fully. But a little softens them. Makes them agreeable." His jaw worked. "Serah, everyone in this city is being dosed. Their morning water. Their bread. They're marinating."

I had known this, in the abstract, for three years. Knowing it in the abstract was a map folded in a drawer. Cyrus handed it to me unfolded, and it was worse.

"How do you stand it?" I asked. "Tasting all that."

"I stood it for fifteen years by pretending poison was just flavor with bad intentions." He glanced sideways at me. "The trick is to be angry in a very orderly way."

"That's the most honest thing you've ever said."

"Don't tell anyone. I'll deny it."

The bells began then. All of them at once, every tower in the city, though no hour had struck. The sound rolled down through the streets and into my chest, and beside me Cyrus went rigid, his hand flying to the strap of the map case.

"What?" I said. "What is it?"

He didn't answer for a long moment. Around us the crowd kept walking, unhurried, smiling faintly, faces tilted toward the sound like flowers toward sun.

"They're sweet too," he whispered. "The bells. The bronze — someone cast something into the bronze. Every time they ring, the whole city breathes it in."

* * *

*Cyrus*

I want to describe what the bells did to me, and I find I can only describe it as a taste, because that is the only language I own for things that enter the body uninvited.

It was like holding wine in your mouth and feeling it change — the way a cheap vintage turns vinegary halfway through the swallow. Except the bells turned sweeter. Each peal laid another coat of that expectant sugar over the tongue of the air, and beneath the sugar, so faint you could pretend it wasn't there, something waited. Something with the texture of a held breath.

And threaded through it, thin as wire: the voice from the boundary, the cartographer's ghost, Aldric Vane, dead three years and still working.

*They recast the bells the winter before I died,* his memory said in me, calm as a ledger entry. *Forty tons of bronze across nine foundries. No one questioned it. No one ever questions a bell.*

"Why would no one question a bell?" I muttered aloud.

"Hm?" said Serah.

"Nothing." I was learning to sort the memories now, mostly. They came with a texture — Aldric's thoughts felt like his handwriting, which I knew intimately, having spent eleven nights tracing it by firelight: precise, cramped, patient. A man who measured things because measuring was how he loved the world.

Aldric had loved this city. That was the worst part. In his memories, Vael Arden was market-cries and river-smell and his daughter aged seven on his shoulders demanding to know why the palace had so many chimneys. Now I walked his streets and tasted his enemy in the well water, and grief for him rose in me like heartburn.

We crossed the Weavers' Bridge. Below, the river slid by, and even the river was sweetened. Incense smoke drifted from doorways — households burning it unprompted, I realized, the way you'd set out milk for a cat you believed protected the house. White petals littered the gutters. Children wore white sashes and sang a skipping song whose words, when I listened closely, were not words at all but a single repeated syllable shaped like a bell-strike.

"Serah," I said quietly. "Look at their eyes."

She looked. She was quiet for ten steps.

"They're happy," she said finally, and her voice had a crack in it I'd never heard. "That's what I can't — they're not afraid. They're *happy*. Mavros's people in Correnholt were grey. This is different."

"It's the difference between a sedative and a feast," I said. My professional self had taken over, cold and clear, and I was grateful for it. "In Correnholt they were being prepared. Here, they're being *celebrated*. The Crown isn't drugging this city, Serah. It's feeding it."

"And the city is saying thank you."

"And the city is saying thank you."

Above the rooftops the palace rose, white-bannered like everything else, its ancient grey stone strangled in new cloth. Somewhere inside it a king sat on a throne wearing a thing that had worn him for forty years, and in eleven days — ten now — the wearing would be permanent.

I caught myself thinking of it the way I used to think of arsenic: with respect. With a horrible professional admiration. Whatever the Crown was, it understood flavor. It understood patience. It had spent decades teaching an entire kingdom the taste of its own surrender, one bell, one loaf, one sweet cup of water at a time.

I am immune to poison. I have swallowed death in micro-doses until death grew bored of me.

But standing on that bridge in the sugared air, I understood, for the first time, that immunity is not the same as safety. The Crown wasn't trying to kill anyone. Killing is crude. It was trying to be *liked*.

* * *

*Serah*

We sheltered that night in the undercroft of a shuttered dye-works near the palace district — Aldric's maps showed it, one of his surveyor's secrets, a room the tax rolls forgot. Cyrus built a tiny fire in a brazier meant for dye vats, and we ate hard bread and dried plums, and I watched him turn his ration over before eating it.

"You're checking the plum for poison," I said.

"Habit."

"Is it poisoned?"

"Obviously not."

"Then eat the plum, Cyrus."

"I'm checking anyway." He checked. He ate. Then he sat staring at the brazier with that face he made when the ghost was speaking, and I let him be silent for a while, because I'd learned his silences had layers, like sediment. Road-Cyrus had taught me that much.

"Aldric walked these streets," he said at last. "Every day for thirty years. Measuring bridges. Arguing with harbor clerks. He knew which tavern keeper watered the beer and which ones didn't." A pause. "He'd hate this. Being dead while his city does this."

"Yes," I said, because lying to him about that seemed obscene.

"He also left us a gift and didn't mention it." Cyrus drew the map case out and unrolled a corner of the leather by the firelight. New lines had surfaced on it — they did that now, ink rising like bruises. Servant tunnels under the palace. A kitchen stair. And a chamber beneath the throne room, drawn in Aldric's hand with a note in the margin: *the old chapel of the vow. They bricked it, but brick is just stone that hasn't decided.*

"Tomorrow," Cyrus said. "Through the kitchens."

"The kitchens," I repeated.

"There's a rhythm to palaces. I know it. Kitchens are chaos wearing order like a coat — everyone too busy, too tired, too certain of their own importance to notice one more pair of hands. If I dress as a journeyman taster, I can walk straight through."

"You're describing yourself walking into the mouth of the thing."

"I'm describing the only door it doesn't guard. The Crown guards against swords, Serah. Against armies, against rites, against rebellion. It doesn't guard against a man carrying a tray." He almost laughed, and it wasn't a kind laugh. "Fifteen years of being invisible and useful. Turns out it was training."

I wanted to argue. I had a whole argument prepared; I'd been keeping it warm since the ford, since the fever, since the night he confessed about the courier and I'd held his hand like it was a thing assigned to me. But the truth was his logic was good, and good logic from Cyrus had a way of sounding like courage, and courage from Cyrus had a way of sounding like a suicide note written in recipe format.

"Rule one," I said instead. "You don't taste anything down there. Nothing goes in your mouth. Not a crumb, not a drop. Whatever the Crown offers—"

"It offered me my mother once already. I remember."

"Rule two. One hour. If you're not back at the kitchen stair in one hour, I come in after you, and I am much less subtle than you."

"Noted."

"Rule three." I reached over and took his wrist, the one with the burn scar from the granary fire, and made him look at me. "Come back."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, muffled by earth and brick and forty tons of sweetened bronze, the bells rang again, unscheduled, unhurried, and Cyrus's eyes flicked upward and his lips parted a fraction — tasting it — and I watched him carry the whole poisoned city on his tongue without swallowing any of it.

"Always," he said.

* * *

*Cyrus*

Sleep came in fragments, and in the gaps between fragments, Aldric took me walking.

We stood — he stood, I dreamed — in the palace kitchens forty years ago, young and lean, delivering a commissioned survey of the cellar vaults. And there in memory I saw what I would see tomorrow: the long scrubbed tables, the mountain of copper, the under-stewards shouting cadence like drummers. Except in Aldric's day the cooks sang while they worked. Real songs. Off-key.

In my dream I leaned close to a pot of pottage simmering on a memory-fire, and I tasted it, the way I taste everything now whether I wish to or not.

Beneath the leeks and barley, so faint that only I could have found it, ran that thread of sweetness. Forty years ago. It had been in the palace kitchens from the beginning, from the very first year, seasoning the king's own table — a dose so small no taster alive would catch it, a whisper of will-thinning laid beneath every meal the true king ever ate.

The Crown hadn't seized him. It had *breaded* him. Slowly. Patiently. The way I'd been taught to build tolerance — micro-doses, daily, until the body forgets resistance is possible.

I woke with my heart hammering and Serah's knife already half-drawn beside me.

"Dream?" she said.

"Recipe," I said. "His oldest recipe."

Dawn came grey over the dye-works roof. We dressed in our stolen clothes — me in a journeyman taster's undyed smock, the guild mark chalk-forged onto the shoulder by Serah's careful hand — and stood a moment in the undercroft doorway, looking up through the grate at a sky already filling with white banners.

"One hour," Serah said.

"One hour."

I went up the ladder into the sweetness, toward the palace, toward the kitchens of a king who had been seasoned like meat for forty years — and as I joined the morning flow of carts and servants streaming toward the gates, I heard, beneath the waking noises of the city, the bells begin again.

They were ringing a pattern I hadn't heard before.

And in the back of my skull, Aldric Vane — dead, patient, handwriting precise as a blade — said quietly:

*I know that peal. It means the Crown knows someone has come.*
