## 15. The Palace Kitchens

There is a particular smell a palace kitchen gets after midnight, when the fires have been raked down and the fat has gone cold in its pans and the whole room exhales the day at once. I grew up inside that smell. I knew it the way other men know their mothers' voices.

So when Serah and I came up through the coal delivery tunnel — Aldric's map unrolling its last secret like a flower opening in fast-forward, a door behind the scullery that hadn't existed when he drew it but existed now because walls forget slower than people do — and that smell hit me, my first feeling was not fear.

It was homesickness.

I want to be honest about that, because everything that follows depends on it. Standing in the dark behind the scullery of the royal palace, with the Hollow Crown's city humming overhead like a struck bell that never quite stops ringing, my body relaxed. My shoulders dropped. Some animal part of me thought: *ah. Here. This is where you know the rules.*

I hated myself for it approximately four seconds later, which is about how long it took me to remember what the kitchen was *for* now.

* * *

Let me describe the kitchens, because they were wrong in ways only I could name.

The layout was right — the long prep aisle, the pastry room off to the east where the heat pooled even in winter, the tasting station set apart on its raised dais so that no one could approach the taster from behind. Someone had built this place by the same logic that had built my valley kitchen, or perhaps my valley had been built as a copy of this, a seed carried away from the parent plant. The geometry was home.

But the rhythm was wrong. A living kitchen has a heartbeat: the chop-chop-chop of knives, the argument between the roast cook and the sauce cook, someone always singing badly. This kitchen was silent except for movement. Forty-odd cooks and undercooks and scullery boys worked in perfect coordination, no words, no wasted motion, each one lifting and setting down and turning like figures in a clockwork diorama. Their eyes had the sheen I'd seen in Lord Mavros's ministers back in Correnholt, but thinner. Diluted. These weren't vessels yet. These were vessels *being prepared*, wills thinned out like stock reduced too far.

And every single dish that left this kitchen passed over the tasting dais first.

"Of course they kept that," Serah breathed beside me. We were pressed into the coal shadow behind the wood store. "The paranoia survived."

"It's not paranoia if something really is trying to poison you," I whispered back. "It's just Tuesday." She gave me the look she reserved for my jokes, which is the look one gives a dog that barks at nothing during a funeral. Fair enough. "Listen. That dais is our way in. Every taster in the kingdom reports through the Guild seal and the steward's ledger. I have both. What I don't have—"

"—is a face anyone remembers," Serah finished. "Because tasters are supposed to be forgettable."

"Tasters are *trained* to be forgettable. It's the first thing they teach you. Before knife work, before dosages. Master Oren sat me down my first day and said, 'Boy, your job is to be the most important person in the room and completely invisible, and if you can't manage the second part you'll never survive the first.'" I touched the Guild medallion at my throat — real silver, real assay marks, lifted from a dead man's effects three towns back with Serah looking elsewhere and me pretending not to notice her noticing. "The Crown hollows people. But hollowing takes time — Mavros proved that, three years and counting. Which means somewhere in that ledger there's a taster position, probably several, staffed by people who are half-hollowed and therefore *terrible* at their jobs, because the first thing the thinning eats is attention to detail."

"You're saying the Crown poisoned its own well."

"I'm saying the Crown thinks it owns everything, and ownership makes you careless. It's the same arrogance I used to see in nobles who assumed no servant would dare. The most dangerous thing in any palace," I said, "is a servant who has decided the rules don't apply to him anymore."

Serah looked at me for a moment. In the coal-dark her face was mostly shadow and one bright stripe where the banked firelight found her cheekbone. "You're enjoying this."

"I am not."

"Your voice does a thing when you enjoy something. It goes smooth. It's doing the thing."

I opened my mouth to deny it and closed it again, because she was right, and we both knew she was right, and there is no dignity in arguing with a woman who has spent three years reading assassins' faces for a living. "Fine. I'm enjoying this the way a man enjoys walking back into his childhood house knowing it's haunted. It's familiar. Familiar isn't the same as good."

"No," she said quietly. "But it's the same door."

* * *

Getting into the ledger office required exactly none of the skills I'd feared needing, which should have comforted me and instead made my skin crawl. The under-steward on night duty — a gray man named Pellam, according to the little brass plate on his desk, which was the kind of detail an unhollowed administrator would never bother displaying — waved us through on sight of the medallion. He didn't check the register against the Guild rolls. He didn't ask why a taster would arrive at midnight. He didn't, I suspected, fully remember what questions were.

His eyes were the problem.

They were glassy, yes, like all of them. But underneath the glass there was a residue of the man he'd been, and the residue was *screaming*. I'd learned to read faces across a dinner table for signs of poison panic — the flicker to the cup, the swallow that comes a half-second early, the smile held a beat too long. Pellam's face did all of those things continuously, at low volume, like a house where someone has left a radio playing in another room. He wanted to warn us. He might even have known he wanted to warn us. The wanting was all that was left of him, sealed under the glass like a fly in amber.

"Cyrus," Serah murmured as we passed his desk. "Keep walking."

"I'm walking."

"You've stopped walking."

"I'm *considering* walking." I forced my feet forward. Later — I told myself later, after the king, after the solstice, if there was an afterward — I would come back for Pellam and the forty cooks and every glassy-eyed soul in this building. That promise cost me nothing to make and everything to believe. I made it anyway. You have to spend hope somewhere or it curdles.

The tasting dais stood empty. Of course it did. The night meal had been cleared hours ago; the day meal wouldn't be plated until dawn. Which meant I had four hours to become, convincingly and permanently, the palace's new under-taster.

Here is what nobody tells you about infiltration: the hard part isn't getting past the guards. The hard part is that you have to *want* to belong there, genuinely, with your whole nervous system, because people can smell a liar's hesitation the way sharks smell blood. And I did want to belong there. That was the obscene part. The dais had a stool worn to the exact shape of a taster's posture. The tasting spoons were racked in order of size, smallest left to largest right, which was *my* system, the system I'd built in the valley because no one had taught me one and I'd invented the thing myself at nineteen. Either the palace tasters had independently arrived at the identical arrangement — possible; good methods converge — or some thread of the Crown's great weaving had reached into the valley and copied me the way it copied everything it wore.

I preferred the first explanation. I did not believe it.

"Ledger's here," Serah said, keeping watch from the stair while I settled at the duty desk. "Three tasters on roster. Two marked present. One vacancy — filled yesterday, pending confirmation." She tapped the entry. "Pending confirmation by whom?"

"By whoever tastes next to the king's table." I pulled the ink toward me and wrote my name in the confirmation line in the small, precise hand the Guild teaches — letters like fence posts, nothing flourishy, nothing memorable. Signing my own name to a lie in the Crown's own book felt like spitting into a hurricane, but there's a specific pleasure in small defiance. I recommend it. "By tomorrow midday I'm official. By tomorrow evening I'm standing behind the king's chair."

"And then what? You pour the essence onto your tongue in front of the entire court?" Her voice stayed level, but I heard the seam in it. Three days since the fever broke and the seam was still there, would always be there now, the place where her voice bent around the shape of what I intended to do.

"Then I get close enough to learn the ritual schedule. The rite happens at solstice dawn in the throne room, but the Crown has to *prepare* him — there will be a final meal, a final anointing. Everything it does, it does through food and drink. It's been feeding on him for forty years, Serah. You don't finish a possession like that on an empty stomach." I closed the ledger. "And a final meal needs a taster."

"So you're volunteering to taste the Hollow Crown's cooking."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It sounds insane every way I say it. I've tried six."

* * *

Dawn came gray through the high windows, and the kitchen woke without waking — lamps lit, ovens charged, the clockwork cooks resuming their silent choreography as though the night had been a pause button and not a passage of time. I took my place on the dais in a borrowed whites jacket two sizes too big in the shoulders, and I did what I have done every working day since I was fourteen years old.

I tasted.

First the morning bread — fine white flour, honey in the dough, and underneath, faint as a rumor, the compound. The will-thinner. Correnholt's grain had carried a trace; this was the source, the concentration so refined that an ordinary tongue would find nothing but richness. My tongue found it the way a musician hears a wrong note in a chord everyone else calls pretty. Bitterness with no origin point, a coolness at the back of the throat that had nothing to do with temperature. I catalogued it, dose by dose, the way Master Oren trained me: *present, mild, cumulative, aimed.* Aimed at whom? At everyone. At the whole court, season after season, until the entire palace was soft clay.

Second, the king's private tray.

It arrived under a cloth carried by two footmen who moved like men underwater, and the whole kitchen changed around it — not louder, but *tighter*, every clockwork motion pulling taut. I lifted the cloth. Under it: a cup of warmed wine, a bowl of broth, a small honey cake shaped like a sunburst. Solstice food. The old feast foods, the ones from before, from the free kingdom — I knew them from Aldric's memories, which surfaced now unbidden, a dead surveyor's nostalgia bleeding through my skull like watercolor through wet paper. *He loved the sunburst cakes,* Aldric remembered, remembering. *Made them himself, once, the year the queen was alive.*

My hands did not shake. I noted, distantly, that they should have.

I tasted the wine. Beneath the fruit and the spice, the compound again — but doubled, trebled, wound through with something else, something that tasted the way a held breath feels. Not poison. Poison wants you dead. This wanted you *empty*, and it had been brewed with patience measured in decades.

"Well?" said a voice behind me.

I turned. Pellam stood at the base of the dais. Up close, in daylight, the glassiness of his eyes had a texture like frost on a window — and beneath the frost, that trapped fly of a man beat against the barrier, once, twice.

"The wine is sound," I heard myself say, smooth, professional, the voice of twenty years of mornings. "The broth carries pepper from the southern coast and bone marrow from yesterday's roast. The cake is fresh; the honey is clover, not wildflower, which the king prefers." All true. All verified. And then, because I could not help it — because the fly in the amber was watching me and I have never in my life been able to leave a dying man unwitnessed — I added, quietly, "You'll want to check the cellar seals yourself, though. I count the stores short by two barrels."

Something moved behind Pellam's eyes. Something almost like gratitude, or almost like terror, or almost like a man gripping a rope thrown into deep water.

"Two barrels," he repeated, flat.

"Marked for the solstice feast. I'll recount at noon."

He stared at me for a long moment. Then he wrote it in his book, in a hand like frost itself, and turned away, and the moment passed, and I had no idea whether I had just opened a crack in the Crown's machinery or simply given a hollowed man a task to perform until the glass closed over him again.

Serah found me an hour later, scrubbing the tasting spoons, which I was doing partly to seem busy and partly because my hands needed occupation or they were going to do something foolish.

"You counted the stores short?"

"There are no missing barrels."

"I know. Why—"

"Because if a fraction of Pellam is still in there, then fractions of the others are too. Forty cooks. Two footmen. A whole palace of flies in amber." I set down a spoon. Its reflection looked back at me warped and thin. "And if any one of them can be reached — even for a moment, even just long enough to open a door or look away at the wrong time — then the rite doesn't have to be two people alone against everything."

Serah was quiet for a while. Then she picked up a towel and started drying beside me, shoulder to shoulder, the way servants do, the way conspirators do, the way, I was beginning to suspect, married couples do in kitchens all over the world without anyone noticing.

"You're good at this," she said finally, and it wasn't a compliment. It was a diagnosis.

"I hate that I'm good at this."

"I know." She bumped my elbow with hers, the smallest possible gesture of truce. "For what it's worth, I hated being good at guarding him too. All those years standing behind that same chair, protecting the thing that ate my father's life. Skill doesn't care whose side it's on. It just is."

"That's very bleak for sunrise."

"I'm very bleak before breakfast. Ask anyone."

I was still holding the towel, still almost smiling, when the kitchen tightened again — that same underwater tension, deeper this time, running through the room like current through wire. The double doors at the far end opened. A herald in white entered, and behind him came the under-steward Pellam with the duty ledger clutched to his chest, and behind *him* stood a footman I didn't recognize, whose eyes were not glassy at all but black and calm and ancient, and who looked directly at me across forty feet of kitchen as though he had known my face since before I was born.

"The king," the footman said, in a voice like a bell rung underwater, "requires his taster."

Every head in the kitchen turned to me.

I set down the spoon. I wiped my hands. I walked toward the doors with the smooth, invisible stride of a man going to do the most important work of his life, and behind me I heard Serah's breath catch, and ahead of me the doors swung wide onto a corridor that smelled of incense and cold stone and, faintly, unmistakably, of arsenic — my oldest friend, my first teacher — laid out like a welcome.

The Crown, it seemed, wished to meet me halfway.
