## 16. Face to Face with the Crown

They took my boots.

I want to be clear about that, because history — if there is any history left after this week — will record that I faced the Hollow Crown of the Ninefold Kingdom with dignity, and I would like the record to reflect that I did it in my stockings, on a marble floor cold enough to make a corpse complain, while two guards gripped my elbows as though I were a difficult parcel rather than a man who had once been trusted to put a spoon in his own mouth.

The throne room had been dressed for solstice. White banners everywhere, floor to ceiling, so many of them that the stone walls had vanished entirely and the room felt less like a hall than like the inside of something's throat. No one had commissioned these banners. Aldric's memories told me that; Serah's three years of digging confirmed it; and the servants who hung them, when Serah had asked one in the kitchens, had simply smiled and said they'd always been there. That's the trick of it, I was learning. The Crown doesn't build its lies from nothing. It builds them just far enough back that memory can't argue.

The king sat at the end of the throat.

He had been young once. Forty years ago he'd had a face that coiners engraved and poets compared to autumn wheat, and even now, even worn down to this, you could see where the wheat had been. But the years hadn't touched him — that was the horror of him up close. A man of sixty-odd with the unlined hands of someone twenty-five, skin smooth as poured wax, eyes the color of water left standing too long in a cup.

The glassy look. The same look Lord Mavros had worn in Correnholt, smiling his hollow smile over the grain ledgers. But where Mavros had been a pamphlet, this was the whole library.

*There he is,* said the voice in my head that was not mine and not Aldric's, and I felt the map against my ribs go warm as a live coal.

The under-steward who had escorted me — half-hollowed himself, his will thinned to a whisper by whatever they put in the city bread — announced me with a name that wasn't mine. "The taster, majesty. Come up from the Valley of Stillness for the solstice feast." A lie the Crown itself had arranged. It wanted me here. That should have terrified me more than it did, but terror, I find, is like arsenic: past a certain dose, the body simply stops reporting it.

"Leave us," the king said.

The guards hesitated. The Crown looked at them — didn't turn its head, just *looked*, the way frost looks at a window — and they left. The under-steward left. The great doors closed with a sound like a book shutting.

And then we were alone, the king and I, in a room full of white cloth, and I discovered that forty years of tasting other people's food had not prepared me to be on the menu myself.

"You are Nakamura's son," the Crown said.

My whole body went still, the way it goes still when I taste something unexpected — the discipline of it, the refusal to let the tongue know what the mind is doing. "I don't know anyone by that name."

"No," it agreed, and there was almost gentleness in it, which was worse than cruelty. Cruelty you can brace against. "You wouldn't."

It rose from the throne. I had expected it to glide, honestly — ancient entities in every story glide — but it walked like a man, slightly stiff in the left knee, because the king had injured that knee hawking in his youth and no amount of possession could be bothered to fix what the flesh remembered. That detail undid me more than anything else. The Crown wore its host *accurately*. It kept the limp. It kept the way he rubbed his thumb along his forefinger when thinking. It kept everything except him.

"I have eaten many people," it said, coming down the steps toward me, unhurried. "Do you know how I say that word? Not with pride. With accuracy. I ate the queen's confessor in the ninth year. I ate the archivist Pellam, who came closest of any of them to finding me. I ate your mother."

The room tilted. I am not ashamed to write that my knees informed me they were considering resignation, and that I stayed upright mostly out of professional habit — a taster does not fall down in front of the king, whatever is in the soup.

"My mother," I said, "was a woman named Sera—"

"Was a woman whose name you do not know," the Crown said, "because she gave you to the valley when you were eleven months old, and the valley, being magic-dead, keeps nothing. Not letters. Not portraits. Not names spoken aloud in empty rooms." It stopped three paces away. Close enough that I could smell it, and what I smelled nearly made me laugh at the absurdity of my own senses: nothing. The most powerful thing in the kingdom had no flavor at all. A void in the shape of a scent. My tongue, reaching for it, found only absence, and absence, for a man like me, is the loudest taste there is.

"She came back for you," the Crown said softly. "Did the map tell you that? Of course it didn't. Aldric drew roads, not reasons. She crossed the boundary of Stillness four times in nineteen years. Four times. Each time the ward burned her a little thinner — the valley does not permit what it does not permit, and she was trying to carry a piece of the outside in, which was me. Or the knowledge of me, which amounts to the same thing. She never made it past the first village. She would stand at the edge of your little world and look at the lights, and then she would walk away, because coming closer meant letting me in, and I would have eaten through her to you like rot through wet bread."

"You're lying," I said. My voice came out steady. I was genuinely proud of that for about two seconds before I realized the steadiness didn't matter; the Crown could read me the way I read a broth.

"I never lie," it said. "That is the first thing everyone gets wrong about me. Demons lie. I am not a demon. I am an agreement. And agreements are only as good as their truth." It extended one smooth, ageless hand. "You want proof."

*Don't,* said Aldric's voice, faint, from the map or from my skull or from wherever dead cartographers keep their opinions.

The Crown touched my forehead with two fingers.

* *

I was in a kitchen.

Not my kitchen — the valley kitchen has low ceilings and smells of barley — but a kitchen the same way, a working kitchen, copper pots and steam and a woman shouting cheerfully at an apprentice about the state of his knife work. And the woman was —

She had my hands. That is the thing I cannot explain to anyone who hasn't seen it. She had my exact hands, the long fingers, the crooked second knuckle on the right, and she was tasting a sauce off the edge of a wooden spoon with the small frown I make at my own reflection every morning.

Her hair was dark going grey at the temples. Her apron was flour-dusted. When she laughed at the apprentice's stammering apology, the sound went through the memory and into my chest and lodged there like a fishbone.

*Mira,* said another cook, passing behind her. *Mira, the master wants the plum course tasted before it goes up.*

*I'll bet he does,* Mira said, grinning, and reached for a clean spoon, and I understood with a lurch that this was real — not a vision, not a temptation-shaped dream, but memory, held somewhere in the vast cold archive of the thing wearing the king. It had consumed her knowledge the way I consume a meal, and her days were still in it. Every day of her exile. Every year of the life she'd built two hundred miles from her son because building it closer would have killed him.

The memory moved. Days slid past like cards. Mira learning the capital's markets, Mira renting a room above a chandler's shop, Mira — older now, greyer, careful in crowds — copying documents in a cramped hand by candlelight. Documents about the king's health records going back forty years. About a physician who had died conveniently. About a bargain struck in a sickroom at midnight by a boy-king who did not want to die.

She knew. She had known for years, patiently, quietly, assembling the truth the way I assembled immunity: one small dangerous dose at a time.

And then the memories darkened. The chandler's room. Night. Boots on stairs — too many boots. Mira blowing out the candle with the calm of a woman who has already decided what she will do with her last hour. She pulled a leather case from beneath a floorboard — *my* leather case, the stitching I had traced a thousand times without knowing why my fingertips liked it — and began to draw.

She drew fast and beautifully, from memory, the whole network of roads and wards and safehouses she'd spent years mapping, and at the center of it, in ink still wet, she wrote instructions for a rite, and beside the rite a single line of requirements, and among them: *a taster's tongue. Someone who has spent a lifetime making poison into nothing.*

She sealed the case. She hid it inside the false bottom of a flour barrel in the palace kitchens — the same kitchens, I realized, the same ones, I had walked those flagstones — and then she sat down at the table and waited for the boots to reach her door.

The last memory was short. The door opening. Men in ministerial gray. And Mira Nakamura looking up at them with my crooked-knuckled hands flat on the table and saying, in a voice that did not shake:

"He'll finish it. Whatever I started — he'll finish it better."

* *

I came back to myself on my knees on the cold marble, in my stockings, in the white-throated hall, and I have no memory of falling.

"There," the Crown said, and crouched to my level, which the king's body managed with the bad knee and a small grunt, human all the way down. "Now you know I do not lie. Your mother spent nineteen years loving you at a distance of exactly two hundred miles, because that was the distance at which love stopped being lethal. She died believing in you. And I hold every day of her, Cyrus. Every laugh. Every burnt pudding. Every night she lay awake doing sums of miles and years. I can give them to you again. All of them. In order. You could live her whole life alongside your own, and you would never lose her a second time."

The offer hung in the incense-heavy air.

And here is the part I set down honestly, because a chronicle that flatters its subject is worth less than a well-tasted soup is worth to a suspicious king: I considered it.

Not idly. Not for a heartbeat. I knelt there on the marble and I turned the offer over with a taster's thoroughness, examining it for flaws the way I'd examine an unfamiliar spice, and the terrible thing — the thing I will carry longer than the grief — is that I couldn't find any. What did the kingdom matter to me, really? I had lived in a valley that existed specifically so the world's horrors couldn't get in. Safety was my native country. The Crown was offering me a different kind of safety: the safety of never losing anything again, because everything lost would be preserved forever in me, pickled in perfect memory like fruit in syrup. It wasn't even asking much. Sit at the king's side. Taste for me. Be my tongue in the world, as you were his tongue. Your mother's last faith in you, honored. Everyone lives.

Everyone lives. That was the hook of it. The Crown doesn't threaten; it accommodates. It is not a wolf at the door but a landlord offering reasonable terms, and the rent is only everything.

"Yes or no," it said gently. "You may take until the solstice. But you taste everything twice before you swallow, I think. So taste this twice, Cyrus Nakamura, and tell me — what does surrender taste like?"

And I opened my mouth to answer, and I honestly do not know — even now, even writing this with Serah asleep ten feet away — I do not know what I would have said.

What I know is what happened. The doors of the throne room banged open hard enough to knock one of the eternal white banners loose, and Serah Vane stood in the doorway in stolen palace livery with her sword drawn and blood on her sleeve — not hers, I learned later, and the relief of that arrived several hours late — and she said, in the carrying voice of a woman who had once shouted drill commands at men better armed than she was:

"Majesty! Forgive the intrusion — there's poison in the kitchens, and this fool taster is the only one alive who can name it."

A lie. A beautiful, shameless, structurally unsound lie, delivered with the flat conviction of a royal guard who had spent years striking ministers and getting exiled for her honesty and had clearly decided honesty was overrated.

The Crown's standing-water eyes moved from her to me. For a moment — one long moment, the length of a held breath, the length of a spoon halfway to a mouth — I felt it weighing us, the way I weigh a dish, deciding whether the flavor was worth the risk.

Then it smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile was warm and terrible and entirely patient.

"Take him, then," it said. "Tasters are such useful people. I do hope he finishes what he started."

And as Serah hauled me to my feet and marched me out past the banners, past the guards who strangely did not stop us, as the doors closed behind us on the sound of the king laughing softly to himself — I understood, with a clarity that tasted of iron and cold water, exactly what the Crown's smile had meant.

It had shown me my mother's face to break me.

Instead, I had memorized it.

Serah's grip on my arm was bruising. "Later," she muttered, "you can hate me for the interruption."

"I don't," I said, and found it was true. "But Serah — the rite. The requirements. She wrote them. She wrote them knowing it would be me."

She stopped walking. In the corridor of endless white, lit by candles no one remembered lighting, she looked at me with three years of hunted certainty finally arriving at its destination.

"Then we have eleven days," she said, "to decide whether we spend them running from what she asked of you — or walking toward it."

Behind us, faint through the doors, the king was still laughing.
