## 17. The Mother's Map

Grief, I have learned in the last day and a half, is not unlike a poison.

It arrives without your consent. It does its work whether you fight it or welcome it. And it has a taste — bitter at the root, with something almost sweet underneath, the way hemlock carries a faint note of parsley that has lured the careless to their deaths for centuries. I know poisons. I have spent my whole life knowing them, cataloguing them, letting them into my body a thimbleful at a time so they could never take me all at once.

But there is no micro-dose for this. There was no training. No king's steward measured out small sips of *your mother carried you across a valley she was forbidden to enter, and then she died on the road with your name in her mouth* until Cyrus Nakamura built up an immunity.

I sat on Serah's bedroll in the ruined watchtower where we'd sheltered since dawn, and I did what I always do when the world becomes unbearable: I looked very closely at one small thing.

The map case lay open between my knees. The leather had been dyed a deep brown, oiled often, worn soft as skin at the corners from years of handling. Someone had loved this case. Someone had carried it through weather and exile and whatever roads led a woman to bleed out at dusk outside the only place in the kingdom magic could not follow.

*The crown eats him,* she'd said. And then, when the guards were dragging me away and she should have saved her breath for breathing: *Cyrus.*

My name. Not "taster." Not "boy." She had known it.

"Drink," Serah said, and pressed a cup of willow-bark tea into my hands. It was terrible. She makes tea the way other people make enemies. I drank it anyway, because the bitterness gave my tongue a job, and a tongue with a job cannot tremble.

"I'm fine," I said.

"You're lying."

"I'm a professional liar. You're supposed to be able to tell."

"Not when it matters," she said, and sat down beside me, close enough that her shoulder touched mine, which was as much tenderness as Serah Vane permitted herself before noon. "Look at your hands."

I looked at my hands. They were steady. They were always steady. But they were also white-knuckled around the cup, and the tea inside them was shivering in rings, and I suppose a person who wasn't lying would have noticed that themselves.

"She knew me," I said. My voice came out strange — flat, like a coin dropped on stone. "She said my name. She looked right at me, and she said—"

"I heard."

"—and I just stood there. I stood there like a fool while she died, and I didn't even ask her name. I asked her if she needed a physician. A physician. In the Valley of Stillness. Where the nearest physician is four days' ride and has never once been summoned because nothing in the valley can hurt anyone except arsenic and bad mutton." I laughed. It came out wrong. Most of me was coming out wrong lately. "I offered her a physician."

"Cyrus."

"And she knew. She must have known what I was. What this was—" I lifted the map case. "She walked to the edge of the valley. Do you understand? The valley that no one leaves, that no one enters, that the Crown's agents can't cross. She walked to the only place in the world where she could put this into someone's hands safely, and she did it, and she used her last breaths to say my *name*, and I offered her a physician."

The tower was quiet. Somewhere below us, wind moved through dead arrow-slits and made a sound like breathing. Serah took the cup out of my hands — I hadn't noticed I'd stopped drinking — and set it aside, and then she did something I will remember for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be.

She took my hand. Not gently. Serah doesn't do gentle; gentle is for people who haven't been exiled for punching a hollow-eyed minister. She took my hand the way you grip a rope thrown to someone going under, firm and deliberate, and she held it, and she said nothing, because she understood — before I did — that I did not need comfort.

I needed work.

* *

Here is what I know about maps, or rather, here is what Aldric Vane knew about maps, because he knows it better than I ever will and he has been renting space in my skull for two weeks now: a map is a confession. Every line on it is a decision about what matters. Cartography is the art of deciding what to leave out, and what a cartographer leaves out tells you more than what they draw.

Aldric Vane had left things out of his daughter's map. Or rather — his daughter had drawn over his work, layer by layer, in her own hand, and the topmost layers were the ones we'd followed: the roads, the wards, the safe houses, the long patient line toward the capital. We had read the map like a route.

We should have read it like a letter.

It was Serah who saw it first, and I want it recorded somewhere, anywhere, that in the account of everything that happened, she saw it first. She spread the map flat across both our knees in the grey light of the ruined tower, and she tilted it against the sun coming through the broken east wall, and she went very still.

"Cyrus. Hold this edge. Don't breathe on it."

"That's a strange request from someone who complained about my breathing last night."

"Hold. The edge."

I held the edge. Held at an angle to the light, the map changed. The vellum had been prepared with something — wax, maybe, or egg-white sized with mineral — and under raking light, ghost-lines surfaced between the painted ones. Not roads. Writing. Tiny, dense, angled writing in a hand that slanted hard to the left, marching along the margins and down the river valleys and around the borders of the valley itself, circling it, again and again, like a moth around a flame.

"It's a palimpsest," Serah whispered. "She wrote under the paint. Under Dad's survey work. Gods, she wrote *over* his work — she must have taken his base map after he died and—" Her voice cracked, and I learned in that moment that Serah Vane could crack, that the discipline was a wall and not a lack, and that behind the wall was a woman holding her father's handwriting in her lap three years after he'd been murdered for drawing it.

In my head, unbidden, warm as a coal: *the sizing is beeswax and chalk, my own recipe, heat will reveal more.* Aldric, helpfully dead. Even his memories were bossy.

"Heat," I said aloud. "There's more. Heat reveals more."

So we built a small fire, because Serah builds fires the way she does everything — efficiently, furiously, with an air of having done it better than you would have — and we passed the map above the coals, section by section, and the hidden writing bloomed like ink dropped in water.

And I read my mother's words.

* *

I am not going to transcribe all of it. Some of it belongs to me alone, the way a grave belongs to the family and not to the passersby who stop to read the stone. But you should know what she wrote, at least in outline, because it is the reason for everything that came after.

She wrote it over months — the dates shift, the seasons shift, the ink changes color as her supplies ran down and she made do. She had entered the valley once, years ago, when I was an infant. She wrote of it like a woman describing a prison she'd escaped and couldn't stop dreaming about: the stillness, the sweetness of air with no magic in it, the taste of water that had never passed through an enchanted aquifer. She had gone back in. Past the ward. She didn't explain how, and I suspect the explanation was either illegal or heartbreaking or both.

*I watched him taste the king's soup,* she wrote, in an entry dated six years past. *He is so careful. He holds each flavor on his tongue like a jeweler holding a stone to the light. He thinks he chose this life. He thinks safety is a thing you can learn, the way you learn letters. Oh, my love. Nothing in that valley can hurt you except the truth.*

I had to stop reading there. I put the map down on my knees and looked at the far wall of the ruined tower for a long time, at the mortar lines, counting them, because counting is a thing tongues and eyes can do together and it keeps the rest of you upright.

Serah read on for me. Her voice was rough, but she kept it level, and level was exactly the mercy I required.

The middle entries were the proof we'd been dying for — though neither of us yet knew how literally. She had found records. In a monastery library, in the papers of a court physician dead two hundred years, in a sealed folio that had survived every purge the Crown had conducted since: the origin. The young king, forty years ago, poisoned at his own wedding feast. The physicians giving him hours. The queen — my chest did something complicated at the word *queen*, and I set that aside to be examined later, or possibly never — the queen praying, the whole court praying, a kingdom's worth of desperate wanting aimed like an arrow at one dying man: *do not die. Please. Please do not die.*

And something answering.

*It was not summoned,* she wrote. *That is the error everyone makes, thinking the Crown called. It was not called. It was wished. There were ten thousand people in that hall and cathedral square, and their grief wanted a miracle so badly that grief itself grew teeth. The thing that answered was made of their wishing, and it wore the shape of their wish: a king who lives. It climbed into him like warmth into a cold house, and the king lived, and everyone rejoiced, and nobody asked what it cost, because wishes never itemize.*

*It does not kill its vessels. That is worse. It keeps them. It presses them flat and wears them, and it has been wearing him for forty years, and every year it needs more, because a wish is hunger with a crown on. The ministers first. Then the household. It is patient. It has learned patience the way I learned poison — no. Forgive me. That sentence is not mine to write. He is the taster. I am only his mother, and I taught him nothing, and I am writing this in the dark in a barn in Correnholt with guards on the road, and I want him to know—*

Serah stopped reading.

"Why did you stop?"

Her jaw worked. She is not a woman who cries easily; I have seen her take a dart in the shoulder with less expression than most people give a bad handshake. But her eyes were wet now, and she pressed the heel of her hand against them angrily, as if tears were a tactical failure.

"The next part is addressed to you," she said. "By name. I think — Cyrus, I think you should read it yourself."

* *

*I want you to know that leaving you was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I have done some very hard things, including this map, including walking toward the capital knowing what waits there, including writing these words knowing that if you are reading them, I am dead, and that you will be angry, and that you have every right.*

*They exiled me for finding the folio. They told the valley I had abandoned you. I let them believe it because a mother who left is a wound that heals crooked but heals, and a mother murdered for treason is a wound that never closes. I chose the crooked healing. I have regretted it every single day, and I would choose it again every single day, and I do not know what that makes me.*

*You will wonder why I never came back for you. Here is the truth, and it is the only inheritance I have: I could not cross the ward twice. Once was possible — the ward has a seam, and I found it, and I paid for it in ways you don't need printed on a map. Twice is death. The valley keeps what it takes, and it took the price of my return the moment I stepped out carrying what I knew. So I did the only thing a mother can do when she cannot go to her son. I built a road to him. I spent nine years making this map — your father-in-all-but-blood Aldric's bones beneath my ink, gods keep him, he drew the kingdom and I drew the reason — and I sent it ahead of me, copy after copy, courier after courier. Most never arrived. The Crown eats couriers too.*

*You are reading this, so one arrived.*

*Cyrus. Listen. The rite needs a taster's tongue. You'll find that page soon enough, and when you do, you'll understand why the map had to reach you specifically, and you will hate me for it, and you should hate me a little, but hear this first: I did not send the map to use you. I sent it because you are the only person alive with the craft to hold what must be held, and because you deserve the truth about the man whose food you've tasted all these years. He is in there. Whatever else is true, he is in there, and someone should come for him.*

*If there is another way, take it. If there isn't — then finish it, and live afterward. Live loudly. Live somewhere with seasons. Taste things that aren't trying to kill you. Find the people worth staying alive for and hold on with both hands, the way you hold a flavor to the light.*

*I was watching you once — you were perhaps eight — and you caught a drop of honey off the tasting spoon before it fell, faster than the eye, and you smiled at yourself. Nobody saw. Nobody but me, from the kitchen door, where mothers stand. I carried that smile out of the valley with me and I have lived on it for eleven years like a man living on one jar of preserves in winter.*

*I am out of preserves now, my darling. But it was a good jar.*

*Your mother,
Mira*

* *

The tower was very quiet. The fire had burned down to a red eye. Somewhere outside, a bird made a sound that had no business being ordinary, and I hated it a little.

I became aware, distantly, that my face was wet. This was interesting information, received the way a taster receives news of a contaminant in the kitchens: clinically, at arm's length, filed for later analysis. Later, apparently, was not coming. Later had been cancelled. I was crying in front of Serah Vane, and I found I lacked the energy to be embarrassed, which may be the truest definition of grief I can offer.

"She named me the rite," I said finally. My voice sounded like it had been dragged behind a horse. "She knew. All those years, she knew what the map would ask of me, and she drew it anyway."

"She also wrote *if there is another way, take it*."

"She knew there isn't. Mothers know." I looked down at the map, at the last lines, at the signature. Mira. My mother's name was Mira. I had gone twenty-three years without knowing that, and now I would carry it for whatever span remained, and the unfairness of it — the sheer administrative cruelty of receiving a mother's name and a suicide mission in the same envelope — made me laugh, one dry bark, entirely without humor.

Then Serah's hand closed over mine on the map's edge.

Not gently. Never gently. Firm as a rope thrown to a drowning man, which is, I have come to understand, simply what love looks like when it has been forged in the same fire as duty.

"Listen to me," she said. "I have spent three years chasing this. Three years of exile and cold camps and reading my father's murder in every glassy-eyed minister I passed. And in three years, I never once got further than suspicion. You got further in two weeks than I got in three, and you did it with borrowed memories and a tea habit." Her thumb pressed down, hard, deliberate. "You are not your mother's instrument. You're her son. There's a difference, and the difference is choice."

"She chose this for me."

"No. She drew a map. Maps show roads. Walking is still yours." She turned the vellum toward me, toward the final margin, where my mother's hand had circled the valley one last time and written, small, beneath the border: *He is worth every mile.* "Read that again."

"*He is worth every mile.*"

"Now read it like it's about the king, and then read it like it's about you, and notice you can't decide which she meant." Serah's voice was low and even, the voice she used at card games when she was about to win everything I owned. "That's not a mission brief, Cyrus. That's a mother looking at her son and at the prisoner in the palace and refusing to rank them. She didn't build a weapon. She built a rescue. Two rescues. One road."

I looked at the words until they blurred, which they were already doing, everything was already doing, the whole world had gone soft at the edges like a flavor held too long on the tongue. And slowly — grudgingly, the way Serah does everything — the grief began to change state. It didn't lessen. Grief doesn't lessen; I'm told it just learns to share the house. But it shifted, the way fear had shifted into purpose back in Correnholt, the way everything in my life eventually transmuted into the only language I trusted:

Into work.

There was a king in the capital who had been pressed flat for forty years, wearing his own body like a borrowed coat. There was a woman who had crossed a forbidden ward and walked nine years of bad roads to reach a son she was forbidden to see, and who had died at dusk with his name in her mouth and a finished map under her blood. There were eleven days — no. Fewer now. Ten, and the better part of this one.

And there was me: Cyrus Nakamura, food-taster, immune to nearly everything except this.

I wiped my face with my sleeve, thoroughly and without dignity, because dignity had been cancelled along with later. I folded the map along my mother's creases, carefully, the way you fold a letter you intend to reread. I tucked it back into the case whose corners she had worn soft with handling, and I tied the cord, and my hands did not shake.

"Serah," I said.

"Yes."

"We ride tonight. I want the ford behind us before the solstice agents change their watches." I stood, and my knees reported their complaints, and I ignored them, as one does. "She spent nine years building this road. The least I can do is walk it fast."

Serah looked up at me from the firelight, and something moved through her expression — relief, maybe, or recognition, the look of one soldier seeing another pick their weapon back up. Then she started kicking dirt over the coals, efficient, furious, already three steps into the leaving.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When this is over — whichever way 'over' falls — you learn her recipe. Beeswax and chalk. I want to size paper the way she did." She slung her pack over her shoulder and met my eyes, and her voice went quiet in a way I had heard exactly once before, at the ford, over the body of a man she'd killed to save me. "Somebody in this family should make things that last."

We went down out of the tower into the last of the daylight, two shadows on a road my mother had drawn, and behind us the fire's red eye closed, and ahead of us, far off to the north, the capital's bells were already ringing the hour — and beneath their bronze voices, faint but unmistakable, tasting of ash and honey both, something patient had begun to sing the solstice in.
