## 18. Solstice Eve

### SERAH

The safehouse Aldric's map remembered for us turned out to be a shepherd's hut with a roof that leaked in three places and a hearth that smoked in two directions at once. I'd slept in worse. I'd slept in worse this month. But tonight the leaks sounded like a countdown, each drop of water striking the iron pot I'd set beneath them marking off hours until dawn, until the solstice, until Cyrus walked into a throne room and put death on his tongue like a communion wine.

He was sitting by the fire when I came back from checking the perimeter — third time in an hour, which even he would eventually notice meant I wasn't checking anything, I was just failing to be inside where it was quiet and warm and full of him.

"Any wolves?" he asked, not looking up from the blade he was sharpening. He was terrible at it. He'd been terrible at it for three weeks. The edge looked like a saw.

"No wolves."

"Bandits?"

"No."

"Crown agents?"

"Sitting very still on a hillside to the east, watching us, doing nothing." I unbuckled my sword belt and hung it on the peg Aldric had hammered there forty years ago, which felt like shaking hands with a ghost. "They don't need to rush. They know where we're going."

Cyrus nodded as if this were reasonable, as if being watched by hollow men through a long solstice eve were simply weather. That was the thing about him that undid me — not bravery exactly, because brave men perform their courage, and Cyrus performed nothing. He just stood in the middle of horror and catalogued it, the way he'd catalogued arsenic in the king's soup for fifteen years. Calm as arithmetic.

I sat down across from him. The fire did its smoking trick. The pot went *plink*.

"We should talk about tomorrow," I said.

"We've talked about tomorrow eleven times."

"We should talk about it properly."

He set down the knife. Looked at me with those taster's eyes, dark and patient and far too good at reading what people tried to hide, because reading what people hid in food had been his whole life and apparently the skill transferred.

"You first," he said.

*Plink*, said the pot.

"The circle works like this," I said, because it was easier to start with mechanics than with the truth pressing up under my ribs like a swallowed stone. "Aldric's rite needs four anchors — salt, silver, bloodline, and will. Salt and silver we have. Bloodline is me; Dad's map is half the ritual already, so his line holds one corner. Will is the hard one. Someone has to stand at the fourth point and refuse the Crown, out loud, continuously, while it does everything it can think of to make them stop. It took three priests and a king's champion to hold it last time, according to Dad's notes. We have me."

"And me," Cyrus said quietly.

"No."

"Cy—"

"I said no." My voice came out flat, guard-flat, parade-ground flat, the voice that had gotten me exiled once already. I didn't soften it. Some things you say sharp or you don't survive saying them at all. "You're going to be holding concentrated Crown-essence on your tongue. You'll be the most dangerous object in that room. You can't also be an anchor. You can't be *anything* except the vessel, because the second your attention splits, you swallow, and then the Crown has two of us."

He was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on his face.

"That's not the real reason," he said.

"It's a completely real reason."

"It's a true reason. It's not the real one." He leaned forward. "Say the real one, Serah."

*Plink.*

"The real one," I said, and my throat closed around it and I made myself push it through anyway, "is that I have lost everyone I ever swore to protect. My father. My commission. The king I gave nine years to and couldn't save. Every person who trusted me ended up dead or hollowed or both, and I am not — I am *not* — going to sit in a magic circle and watch it happen again. Not to you. I'd rather fail the kingdom honestly than protect it at the price of you. There. Said. Now you know I'm compromised and unfit and you can take the map and go do this without me."

"I'm not taking the map anywhere without you."

"You should."

"I know," he said. "But I'm not."

And that was the whole argument, really. We'd been having it for weeks in fragments and neither of us had won and neither of us would. He picked the knife back up and started ruining the edge further, and I watched his hands — steady, precise, the hands of a man whose entire profession was putting dangerous things in his mouth and surviving — and I thought: *he's decided something. Tonight. He's decided something and he hasn't told me.*

I decided something too. But mine could wait until morning.

* * *

### CYRUS

She thought she was hiding her face behind the sword-sharpening. Serah Vane hid about as well as a bell hides behind its own ringing. She'd checked the perimeter three times, which meant she wasn't checking it, and now she was grinding the knife against the whetstone at an angle that would embarrass a butcher, and every few strokes her eyes flicked to me and away, quick as a tongue testing for almond.

I knew what she was doing. She was memorizing me. People do it when they've privately concluded they're about to lose you — the guards used to do it to the old king in Aldric's memories, back before the Crown settled in, looking at him a beat too long at every council meeting, filing him away.

Well. Two could play at memorization.

"Tell me about the valley," she said suddenly. "The real valley. Not the version in your official testimony."

I almost laughed. "My official testimony is that it's dull and smells of turnips."

"Lie better, taster."

"It doesn't smell of turnips. That's the lie part." I set the knife down before I ruined it entirely. "It's quiet. That's the thing nobody believes. Magic-dead isn't just no spells. It's — silence underneath everything. Sound lands softer. Grief lands softer. I didn't know that until I crossed the boundary and felt a stranger's memories hit me like a wave, and understood that in the valley, everything arrives pre-diluted. Safety isn't a wall there. It's water in the wine."

"Do you miss it?"

I considered lying. She'd only catch me.

"No," I said. "That's the terrible thing. Fifteen years I told myself the stillness was peace. It wasn't. It was anesthesia. I was proud of my immunity the way a prisoner is proud of his cell — kept clean, well-defended, mine." I fed a stick to the fire. "Your father crossed out of a valley of his own, didn't he? In the memories. He left the Survey Corps because they wouldn't map the wards around the palace. He said a mapmaker who refuses to draw the truth is just decorating a lie."

"He said that?" Her voice cracked slightly on *said*. Present tense dying into past. I hated hearing it.

"He says it. He's still saying it in here." I tapped my temple. "Along with the smell of his ink, the sound of your mother singing off-key, and an extremely detailed opinion about how you held your fork at age six."

Serah laughed — a wet, startled sound, half sob, the kind of laugh that comes out sideways when someone has been holding grief at arm's length for three years and someone finally offers to carry a corner of it.

"He never wrote any of that down," she said. "Three years I've carried his map like a relic and I never once wondered what he smelled like."

"Ink and riverwater and burnt toast. He was hopeless with a stove. Your mother banned him from hers after the porridge incident of—" I paused, let the memory surface, tasted it. "The porridge incident of the Year of the Pale Comet. Which apparently burned so badly the neighbors thought the house was on fire."

"*Burnt toast,*" she whispered, and then she was crying, quietly, the way soldiers cry, facing forward, and I crossed the space between us and put my arms around her because there was no longer any pretending that I wouldn't.

* * *

### SERAH

He held me while the fire smoked and the pot counted down the night, and somewhere in the middle of it I stopped crying and started noticing things I had been very carefully not noticing for weeks: that he smelled of cedar and something faintly bitter, some herb he took daily, some last private immunity ritual; that his heartbeat under my ear was slow, maddeningly slow, a taster's heart, trained never to race; that when his hand moved on my hair it was careful in the specific way of a man handling something he believed was more fragile than himself.

I pulled back far enough to look at him.

"Stop deciding to die in my place," I said.

His expression didn't change, which confirmed everything. "I have no idea what you mean."

"Cyrus Nakamura, you are the single worst liar I have ever met, and I served nine years in a court of professional liars. You've got the look. The same look you had outside Correnholt when you decided to taste the grain yourself instead of making me fetch the sample." I grabbed his collar, gently, the way you grab something you're afraid will dissolve. "Whatever you're planning for tomorrow — whatever arrangement you've made in that head where you trade yourself for me — cancel it."

"There's no arrangement."

"There's always an arrangement with you. You arrange. It's what you do instead of panicking."

He smiled then, small and crooked and devastating. "Would it help if I promised?"

"No. Promises from you are just arrangements wearing formal clothes."

*Plink*, said the pot. The night was thinning. Somewhere east, hollow men sat on their hillside, patient as the Crown itself, waiting for dawn to collect them.

"Then tell me what you want," Cyrus said softly. "Not for the mission. For you. What do you want, Serah Vane, if we live? Nobody's asked you. I've watched you plan routes and rations and contingencies for three hundred miles and not once, not *once*, have you planned a single thing past the throne room door. You don't have a tomorrow after tomorrow."

Because I hadn't let myself have one. Because wanting things was how the Crown found your seams. Because my father wanted to expose the truth and it killed him, and the king wanted to live and it hollowed him, and wanting, in this kingdom, was just painting a target on the soft part of yourself.

But he was looking at me with his dark, patient, poison-proof eyes, and it was solstice eve, and we had maybe six hours left of pretending, and I was so tired of armor.

"A garden," I said. My voice came out wrecked. I let it. "A real one. Not herbs-for-medicine, not a defensive hedge. A stupid garden. Roses. Vegetables I'll complain about. A bench with a bad view. And I want to eat breakfast in it, slowly, without tasting anything for danger first. And I want—" I stopped. Started again. "I want someone across the table whose face I know better than my maps. Whose silences I can read. Who thinks safety is a skill but has learned it can also be a *person*."

The pot dripped. The fire settled. Cyrus Nakamura, the calmest man I had ever known, the man whose pulse didn't climb for kings or Crowns, looked at me with his heart finally, visibly, in his throat.

"Breakfast," he repeated, like a man testing a new poison on his tongue, waiting to see if it killed him.

"Breakfast," I said. "Every day. Loudly. Disgracefully. Enough for both of us, whoever—" My voice failed. He finished it for me.

"Whoever survives lives loudly enough for both," he said. "Yes. I promise."

"Promises are arrangements in formal clothes."

"This one's naked," he said, and kissed me.

* * *

### CYRUS

Here is what I did not tell her, and what I am telling the fire now, silently, in the grammar of a man who spent his life confessing to no one: while she slept against my shoulder, I finished the arrangement.

The rite needs four anchors. She named them — salt, silver, bloodline, will — and named herself for bloodline, and claimed will for her post, and left the fourth corner unspoken because there is no one else. But Aldric's notes are clear, and I've read them forty times: the vessel holding the essence cannot anchor. And a circle with an empty corner doesn't fail gracefully. It fails outward. It takes the nearest soul.

So one of us stands in that corner, and one of us holds the poison, and we have both privately assigned ourselves the poison, because we are two fools in love with the same sacrifice.

Except. Except I am immune, and she is not — that much of my reasoning is honest. But there is a second page to Aldric's final note, the one written in a hand gone shaky, the one Serah hasn't seen because I folded the map so she wouldn't: *the holder must be willing to swallow.* Not to endure the essence. To end it — to take the Crown into himself fully if the unbinding falters, and die with it, so it dies too. A taster's ultimate dose. The one meal you prepare knowing you'll never taste another.

Serah cannot do that. Not because she lacks courage — she has more than I'll ever have — but because the Crown would offer her the same bargain it offered me, and she would hesitate one breath, and one breath is all it needs.

I won't hesitate. I have nothing left it can offer me that matters more than her. My mother's face I've already grieved twice. My valley I've already left. My immunity I'll spend tomorrow regardless.

So: she holds the circle. She believes I believe I'll walk out. And at dawn I'll kiss her like a man with breakfast plans, and mean it, and mean the other thing too.

The pot goes *plink*. The sky east of the hills has gone the color of the inside of a shell. On their hillside, the hollow men rise, unhurried, and begin to walk.

Dawn comes whether we're ready or not.

Solstice.

Time to keep a promise — and, quietly, to break one.
