## 19. The Rite of Unbinding

**Serah**

The throne room doors were taller than any door had a right to be, and they were already open.

That was the part that frightened me most. Not the guards we'd cut down in the corridor — though there had been more of them than Cyrus's plan accounted for, and my arm ached where the ford wound had torn open again. Not the banners, white and endless, or the incense thick enough to chew. It was the open doors. The Crown *wanted* us here. You don't walk into an open trap feeling clever. You walk into it feeling like the mouse who found the cheese suspiciously easy to reach and ate it anyway.

"Tell me you have a better idea," I said.

"No," Cyrus said. His voice was steady. His hands, holding the vial Aldric's map had spent twenty years and one dead woman guiding us toward, were not. "But I have this."

The essence of the Hollow Crown, distilled from the rite's own preparation — every drop of it the palace had brewed for solstice morning, siphoned by a food-taster with steady hands and a truly upsetting relationship with poison. Concentrated. Lethal to anything that could die. And to him — nothing. That was the whole architecture of the plan. The one person in the kingdom the Crown's substance couldn't kill was the one person standing at its threshold with enough of it to drown a god.

"You hold the circle," he said. "Whatever comes through those side doors, whatever wears a face you love—"

"I know."

"—you hold it anyway."

"I said I know, Cyrus." I checked the chalk line of the circle one last time. Aldric's geometry, drawn in his daughter's hand. My father's last gift, delivered posthumously through a stranger's skull. He'd have hated that. He'd have done it anyway.

We went in together, which felt right. We'd come apart; we'd end together. Or neither of us would end at all, and I'd spend the rest of my life being unbearable about it.

The king sat on his throne beneath forty years of accumulated light, and he was not old. That was the horror of it, still, even now, even knowing everything. A man should have been sixty-four. The body on the throne was perhaps thirty, smooth-faced, serene, hands folded on the arms of the chair like a portrait of itself. The Crown sat above his brow — not gold, not metal, nothing so honest as metal. It looked like a hole shaped like a crown, and your eyes kept trying to fill it in.

Its eyes opened. Both pairs.

"Cyrus Nakamura," the Crown said, in a voice like the king's and underneath like a hundred ministers' and underneath *that* like something older than either. "The taster. Come to serve the final course."

* * *

**Cyrus**

I want it recorded — by whom, I no longer know — that I did not answer.

That was the training. Twenty years in the Valley of Stillness, and the deepest lesson wasn't the poisons. It was silence. A taster who flinches tells the assassin which dish worked. A taster who speaks gives the court a lever. You taste, you wait, you report only what must be reported. Your tongue belongs to the king. Everything else belongs to stillness.

So when the Crown offered — and it offered, oh, it offered — I held my silence like a dish balanced on my palm.

*I can give her back.* Not sound exactly. It arrived behind my eyes, warm as broth, patient as arsenic working through a slow morning. *Your mother came to me at the end, did she tell you? She asked about you with her last breath. I keep what I consume, taster. Every voice. Every day. I could let you hear her laugh. I could give you the childhood she couldn't.*

Beside the dais, Serah began the circle. Chalk dust rose. Her voice went low and metrical, my father-of-sorts Aldric's cadence borrowed through me and given back to his bloodline, and around the throne room the air tightened like skin over a drum.

The king's mouth moved. The thing behind it leaned forward.

*Taster. You above all people understand: nothing is wasted. I do not destroy what I take. I preserve it. Perfectly. Forever. Is that not mercy?*

I walked up the steps of the dais. My boots sounded very loud. Somewhere below, the side doors burst open and vessels came through — ministers, guards, kitchen staff, faces I'd catalogued from Aldric's memories and Serah's stories, all of them smiling with the same smile, all of them walking into the circle's edge and stopping as if they'd hit glass. Serah's voice climbed. Something in my chest wanted desperately to turn and check on her. I didn't. Stillness.

I took the king's jaw in my hand.

It was warm. That undid me a little — not the horror, the warmth. Forty years of wearing and the man underneath was still warm, still running the animal business of a living body, waiting under the garment that wore him.

*Cyrus,* the Crown said, and dropped its pretense of subtlety entirely, flooding me with her — brown hair, flour on her knuckles, the specific way she'd said his name, *Cyrus, sweet, look at me* — a whole fabricated memory so complete I felt my knees—

No.

A dying courier had pressed a map into my hands and called me by name, and I had spent months learning to trust that name. This thing offering it back to me, gilded — it was a counterfeit. I had tasted counterfeits before. Honey cut with lead. Saffron stretched with turmeric. Love counterfeited by a thing that had never once loved anything.

I uncorked the vial against the king's lips and poured.

* * *

**Serah**

The circle held, and it was killing me.

They came in waves after the first — vessels from every corridor of the palace, dozens, then scores, pressing against the boundary of Aldric's geometry like moths against a lamp, except these moths had hands and ranks and knew how to use them. The circle didn't stop them so much as make them *hesitate*, a half-second of confusion each time they touched the line, and half-seconds add up, but so does exhaustion, and my wounded arm had gone from aching to screaming somewhere around the third wave.

"Hold," I told myself. "Hold the circle."

The chalk was smearing at the north point. I rebuilt it kneeling, one-handed, reciting the cadence while a chamberlain I'd once served beside — good man, played the lute badly — stood three feet away smiling at me with a smile that had nothing behind it but appetite and instruction.

Above us, on the dais, Cyrus had taken the king's jaw and poured, and then he had gone utterly still, and the stillness was worse than any battle. Because I knew what the Crown did with stillness. I'd seen Lord Mavros sit that way. Consciousness pressed flat like a flower in a book.

"Cyrus." I said it into the cadence, breaking meter, breaking my own rules. "Cyrus, stay with me."

He didn't move.

The vessels surged. The circle flickered where my chalk had failed, and I threw myself across the gap bodily, arm outstretched over the line, and the nearest vessel — a guard captain whose name I'd never learned — seized my wrist with strength no human joint should produce. Bones ground. I held. I thought, absurdly, of the night at the fire outside Correnholt when Cyrus had admitted, in the smallest voice I'd ever heard from him, that he was afraid, and how saying it had made him braver rather than less, and I understood finally that this was the entire secret of courage and everyone who seemed brave had simply learned to say it quietly.

"I'm afraid," I told the guard captain, conversationally, through gritted teeth. "And I'm holding anyway."

The circle blazed.

* * *

**Cyrus**

The poison entered me and I welcomed it home.

That is the only way I can describe it. Twenty years of micro-doses had built in me a country the Crown's essence could enter but not conquer, and it poured across that border like a tide meeting a shore that had been preparing for it since before the tide existed. It filled my mouth. My throat. My skull. It showed me everything again — the origin, the grief, the young king bleeding on a solstice floor forty years ago while a nation's terror congealed into a shape and crawled onto his brow and whispered *never again, never death, never loss* — and it offered.

Everything. It always offered. My mother. The valley unbroken. Serah safe forever, both of us preserved, *perfectly, forever*, two flowers pressed flat in the same book, together for eternity, which is not the same as together at all.

I answered with silence.

Not emptiness — I want that understood too. Silence isn't nothing. Silence is a choice performed continuously. Every second I didn't speak was a second I chose, and chose again, the way a taster holds a spoonful of certain death and waits for the burn that doesn't come. The Crown poured itself into me looking for the crack, the fear, the yes, and found instead the professional calm of a man cataloguing flavors: *bitterness, ancient; grief, concentrated; desperation, notes of a child crying at a deathbed; finish, interminable.*

You are not a god, I thought at it, tasting. You are a wish. Wishes spoil.

And it — hesitated. For the first time in forty years, the Hollow Crown hesitated, because it had worn kings and ministers and captains and none of them had ever *tasted* it, and being tasted is a terrible intimacy. To be tasted is to be known as a thing among things. A flavor. Finite. Finishable.

The king shuddered under my hand.

* * *

**Serah**

Light came out of the king like water from a cracked dam.

I felt the circle take the overflow — Aldric's geometry drinking the Crown's spilling essence, channeling it into the floor, the stones, the deep bones of the palace built for exactly this by architects nobody remembered hiring — and I screamed the closing lines of the cadence while every vessel in the room collapsed at once, dozens of bodies dropping like puppets with cut strings, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Then the king opened his eyes, and they were just eyes. Old ones. Terrified ones.

And Cyrus folded like laundry.

I reached him before he finished falling. He was heavy, deadweight, alive — heartbeat rabbiting, skin gray — and his mouth was moving. I bent close.

"Tastes," he whispered, "like the end of winter."

Then he was unconscious, and I was alone in a ruined throne room with a waking king, a scattered circle, and the man I had privately decided to die for lying in my arms having refused, at the last, to die at all.

The old king looked at me with forty years of stolen time in his face and said, hoarsely, "What have I done?"

"That," I said, "is going to take a while to answer. Help me lift him."

* * *

Outside, the bells of the capital rang noon, and kept ringing, because nobody had told them to stop — and far away, at the edge of the Valley of Stillness, something old and protective quietly came undone.
