## 20. What the Crown Was

I had always assumed that dying would taste like something.

Bitter, maybe. Copper, at least. I'd built a whole career on the assumption that the end of things announces itself on the tongue — that poison, like everything else in this kingdom, has a flavor if you're patient enough to find it.

But when the essence of the Hollow Crown poured across my tongue and down my throat, it tasted of nothing at all. It tasted of the Valley of Stillness. Of absence. Of every dish I had ever swallowed without flinching, all at once, served cold.

And then I was no longer in the throne room.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being inside an ancient devouring entity: it is very quiet.

I had expected fire. Screaming, certainly — Serah's voice shouting my name somewhere behind me, the king's jaw going slack under my hands, the vessels of the Crown hurling themselves against the circle she held with bloodied knuckles and three years of fury. There had been sound, so much sound, and then there was this: a white stillness that pressed against me like deep water, and a presence beside me that was not exactly beside me because *beside* was one of many things it did not believe in.

*You are small,* it said, or thought, or simply was, and its being was the sentence.

"I'm told that often," I said. My voice worked here. That felt unfair, somehow, but I've never trusted fairness to attend the important moments.

*I have eaten kings. I have worn ministers like gloves. I have drunk forty years of prayers from a hundred thousand throats.* The nothing around me shifted, and I understood that it was considering me the way I considered an unfamiliar spice — held up to the light, sniffed, set aside for later. *You are the taster.*

"That's the résumé, yes."

*You were made to be swallowed. Everything about you was built to take poison into yourself and live. You are the most delicious thing to walk into my mouth in four centuries.*

"Well," I said. "That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me since the solstice preparations started."

The silence that followed had texture. Disapproval, maybe. Or amusement. With entities older than the alphabet, it's hard to tell.

*Why do you jest?*

"Because the alternative is screaming, and I did that for about ten seconds when I was seventeen after mistaking foxglove for comfrey, and I found I didn't care for it." I looked around at the whiteness, which was not whiteness, which was not anywhere. "Also because you talk too much, and mockery slows you down."

It did not laugh. But something in the vast flat attention of it turned, the way a man turns to look at a door he thought was painted on.

*You are not afraid.*

"Oh, I'm afraid," I said. "Professionally. It's a different organ than the one that does the talking."

*Show me.*

And because I was inside it, and because it was inside everything — the water, the incense, the bells, the grain, the king — I could not refuse.

* * *

It showed me itself instead.

This is what I learned, in the way you learn things in dreams, where knowing arrives whole and pre-chewed and you only understand later how much it cost:

Four hundred years ago, there was a young king named Aldemar — not our Aldric the cartographer; a different dead man with a similar name, because kingdoms recycle even their griefs — and he was dying. Twenty-three years old, coughing blood into silk, and his physicians had run out of Latin and leeches both.

His people could not bear it.

Understand: they loved him. Not the abstract love subjects owe crowns, but a real, stupid, human love — he had walked among them unguarded as a boy, remembered the baker's daughter's name, wept openly at his father's funeral and apologized for it. And now he was dying at twenty-three, and the whole kingdom stood around his sickbed like mourners who refused to sit down.

They wished. That's all it was, at first. That's all it ever is.

Ten thousand people wishing the same wish in the same week, with the same desperate shape — *let him not die, let him not die, let him not die* — and grief is a solvent, and prayer is a pressure, and where enough of it pools in one place, something pools with it. Not a demon. Nothing came from outside. That's the joke of it, the filthy punchline: the Crown was born here. Homegrown. A kingdom's collective refusal, distilled until it could stand up and put on a hat.

It saved him. That was its whole nature, its whole hunger, its single commandment written into its substance by the hearts that made it: *he must not die.*

So it stopped him. Stopped him the way amber stops a fly.

And Aldemar — the real one, the boy under the glass — has been in there ever since. Conscious. I want you to understand that part, because it matters, because it is the cruelest sentence I will ever write: he has been awake the entire time. Forty years of watching his own hands sign orders he did not give. Four hundred years — I lose track in that place; time there is a flavor, not a line — of pressing against the inside of his own skull like a flower pressed flat in a book, watching the thing he became do kindnesses and atrocities in his name, unable to die, unable to live, unable even to scream because the Crown had long since decided screaming was inefficient.

The ministers came later. The Crown learned efficiency. Why keep one vessel when you can keep several? Why rule through a king when you can wear the whole court? Each hollowing began the same way: someone, somewhere, wished too hard. *Let him stay. Let her not change. Let this moment last.* Grief again, always grief, feeding itself to itself.

It wasn't evil. That was the horror of it, the thing that made me want to laugh and weep in equal measure inside that white nowhere. It was a wish. A wish that had forgotten it was made by people, the way a scar forgets it was once a wound. It devoured because preservation is all it knew how to do. It could not let go of anything — not the king, not the ministers, not a single hour of the last four centuries — because letting go meant death, and death was the one thing it had been made to prevent.

It was, when you got right down to it, the most loyal servant any kingdom ever produced. It just didn't know when to stop serving.

*You see,* it said, and there was — I swear this — something like hope in it. Something like a dog that has brought its master the same stick for four hundred years and cannot understand why the master has stopped throwing. *You see what I am. I am the mercy. I am the wish they made. Every mother who prayed over a fevered child, every widow who begged the river for one more year — I am what their wanting became. I cannot be wrong. I was made from love.*

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly the problem."

* * *

It showed me my mother then. I'll say that plainly, because I earned it, and so did it: it kept its promise. It opened the drawer of itself and showed me Liana Nakamura, twenty-nine years old, crossing the valley's edge with a map case strapped to her chest and a name she hadn't spoken aloud in six years burning on her tongue.

She died badly, on a road I have since walked. She died slowly enough to press the map into the hands of a half-grown food-taster with her own eyes — eyes I have, or had, or have somewhere — and whisper *the crown eats him*, and to know, with the last of her, that the son she couldn't return to would understand what she meant, because she had taught him the only language that mattered before the valley took her away.

She taught me flavors when I was three. She used to hold things up to my mouth — honey, salt, the bitter rind of citrus — and say, "Name it. Name it and it can't surprise you."

Name it and it can't surprise you.

I think that was the moment the Crown lost, though it wouldn't know for another few eternities. Because it showed me her death expecting to break me, and instead I recognized the lesson. It was mine. It had always been mine.

"You loved her," I said to the whiteness, and it went very still.

*I wore her,* it said. *At the end. Briefly. She fought me. They usually fight.*

"No," I said. "Before that. When you made her exile necessary. When you hunted the cartographer's survey and burned his notes and drove a woman out of the only safe place in the world because she knew your name. You spent her. The way you spend everyone." I let that sit. "You don't hate us. I know. You can't hate. You love us the way a jar loves what's sealed inside it. And that's worse."

*What do you know of love, taster?*

"More than you'd think. Less than I'd like." I thought of Serah holding the circle behind me, blood on her knuckles, refusing — absolutely refusing — to plan around my survival, because planning around it meant admitting it, and she would rather break the world with her bare hands than admit it. "But I know what it looks like when it works. It lets go. That's the whole trick. Love that won't let go isn't love anymore. It's just hunger wearing the memory of love."

The whiteness trembled. Around me, the nowhere rippled, and through the ripple I glimpsed the throne room — Serah's arms raised, sweat cutting lines through the dust on her face, the vessels swaying like wheat in wind that wasn't blowing — and I understood that the Crown was distracted. Hurting. I had said something true inside the one place truth was the only currency, and truths, for the Crown, were wounds.

*If I let him go,* it said, and the *him* needed no antecedent; there was only ever one *him*, the boy in the amber, four hundred years and counting, *he dies. He dies, and I fail, and everything I was made for comes undone, and it will have been for nothing.*

"It was already for nothing," I said, gently, because you can be gentle even when you're killing something. Especially then. "Aldemar died four hundred years ago. You just haven't let him finish. There's no version of this where you win. There's only the version where you stop, and the people you took get to rest, and the wish that made you finally gets answered properly — the answer every wish gets, sooner or later, the one that makes the wishing mean anything at all."

*Say it.*

"No."

*Say it. I was made from words. Unmake me with them.*

And here is the confession I will carry longer than any scar the unbinding left on me: I hesitated. Not from doubt. From pity. Because it was the loneliest thing I have ever met — a kingdom's grief with no kingdom left to grieve, holding a dead boy's hand for four hundred years because nobody ever told it the vigil was over — and some traitor part of me wanted to comfort it, wanted to lie, wanted to say *you did well, you can rest now, none of it was for nothing.*

My mother named flavors so they couldn't surprise her.

I named this one.

"He's dead," I said. "Aldemar died a long time ago, and you kept the body warm, and it was done out of love, and it was still wrong, and it is time. He doesn't need you to save him. He needs you to let him go. We all do. That's the mercy. That's the *only* mercy. Let him go."

* * *

I fed it to him the only way I know how: on the tongue.

Not the word alone — words are thin broth. I gave it the whole meal. I gave it the taste of my mother's finger pressing citrus rind to my lips. I gave it the arsenic mornings and the professional calm and every dish I ever swallowed without flinching. I gave it the foxglove and the fever and the ten seconds of screaming at seventeen, and how I lived anyway, and how living-after is a skill and dying is a skill and I had spent my whole life learning both because someone had to, because the valley asked it of me, because a boy whose mother vanished needed to believe safety was something you could master.

And underneath all of it, the true poison, the compound I alone was immune to, the one thing in all the world the Crown could not wear:

*It ends. And that is allowed.*

It tried to wear it. Of course it did. It reached for acceptance the way it reached for kings and ministers and mothers, tried to press that flat and frame it and hang it in its gallery of preserved moments — and acceptance is the one guest that will not sit for the portrait. The moment the Crown took it in, it became the thing it had spent four centuries preventing. It ended.

Not with a scream. With a sigh. An enormous, four-hundred-year-long sigh, the exhale of a vigil-keeper finally sitting down, finally closing the book, finally letting the candle gutter out because morning was coming and had been coming the whole time and someone should have said so.

Around me the amber cracked.

I felt Aldemar before I saw him — a boy-shaped relief, twenty-three years old forever, blinking in light he hadn't seen since his hair was brown. He looked at me with his mother's eyes, or somebody's mother's eyes; they all blur, at the end of a dynasty.

"Thank you," he said, or thought, or simply was, and his being was the sentence, and the sentence was finished.

Then the white nowhere folded up like a map — and I would know — and I fell back into my body, into my mouth, onto my knees on the cold stones of the throne room, with the taste of nothing at all still coating my tongue and the king's jaw slack beneath my hands and dawn coming through windows that had not seen honest sunlight in four centuries.

Serah was saying my name. She had been saying it for some time, I think.

The vessels were falling. All around the broken circle, ministers and guards and servants dropped like puppets with cut strings — not dead, I would learn later, never dead, just returned, consciousness flooding back into pressed flowers with the awful slow pain of a body remembering it belongs to someone. Somewhere among them Lord Mavros was making a sound I will hear in my sleep for the rest of my life: a small, wet, wondering noise, the sound of a man waking up inside himself.

And on the throne, the king drew a breath.

One breath. His first in four hundred years. I watched it move through him — watched age arrive all at once, silver flooding his hair like frost claiming a field, watched his hands remember their decades, watched his face become every day the Crown had stolen from him, all of it at once, a lifetime settling onto him like snow.

He opened his eyes. They were ordinary eyes. Brown, tired, wet. The most beautiful thing I had ever tasted was the air that went into them.

"Taster," the king said. His voice was rust and honey. "What year is it?"

And Serah Vane — blood on her knuckles, tears cutting through the dust on her face, three years of hunting finally paid off in a single dawn — laughed, one broken note, and caught me as I finally, professionally, irrevocably fainted.
