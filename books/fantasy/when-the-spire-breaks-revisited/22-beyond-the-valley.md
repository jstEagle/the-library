## 22. Beyond the Valley

The first thing I tasted after the solstice was a plum.

I want you to understand what that means. For twenty-six years, every flavor that crossed my tongue had been catalogued, weighed, cross-referenced against a mental index of two hundred and eleven poisons and their carriers. A plum was never a plum. It was stone fruit, low toxicity risk, potential for bitter kernel oils if the pit were cracked, acidity masking alkaloids. I ate the way an accountant reads numbers. I could not tell you what a peach *meant*, only what it might hide.

The plum was overripe, bought from a roadside cart three days after the king woke, and I bit into it standing in the dust of the capital road because Serah tossed it to me and said, "You look like a man who hasn't eaten in a week," which was true, though not for the reason she thought.

It was sweet. Not sweet-with-a-note-of-something-underneath. Not sweet-potentially-fermented. Just sweet, the way light is bright, the way her hand felt when it found mine in the wreckage of the throne room. I stood there like a fool with juice running down my wrist, and Serah looked at me the way people look at men who cry at festivals.

"Good plum?" she asked.

"I have no idea how to answer that," I said. "Ask me again in a year."

That was four months ago. She still asks sometimes. I'm getting better at answering.

* * *

The valley let me go the way a fist unclenches — slowly, joint by joint, and with no apology for having held on so long.

We learned this from refugees trickling west along the river road: travelers who'd wandered near the Stillness for generations and always felt a wall they couldn't name, an instinct to turn back at the tree line. After the Crown broke, a shepherd crossed the old boundary chasing a lost lamb and reported that nothing happened. No wall. No wrongness. He described the valley as "just hills, mostly, with very tidy farms." Which is accurate, if incomplete. The valley was never the hills. The valley was the silence underneath them, and the silence had been someone's work all along — a ward laid down before my grandfather was born, I suspect, by people who knew exactly what was coming and wanted one place in the kingdom where the Crown couldn't reach.

One place where magic couldn't enter. One place where a child could be raised without ever tasting the world.

I used to think the Stillness was safety. Now I know it was a quarantine, and I was the specimen being protected, and no one — least of all me — had asked whether the specimen wanted protecting.

Serah asked me once, on the road out of the capital, whether I'd go back. We were sharing watch at midnight because neither of us slept whole nights anymore, and she has this habit of asking the largest questions in the smallest hours, when your defenses are down and your tongue is honest.

"No," I said, and surprised myself with how fast it came.

"Not even to see it?"

"Especially to see it. You know what would happen? I'd walk through my old kitchen, and I'd feel proud of myself. Twenty years of discipline, of mastery, of keeping a monster fed and alive without knowing it. I'd stand there smelling the stock pots and part of me would say, *you were good at this*. And I can't afford to be proud of that, Serah. Pride is how the Crown got forty years of free lodging. Everyone who served it was proud of their service."

She was quiet for a while. Then: "You know that's not the same thing."

"It's adjacent."

"It's *adjacent*," she agreed, "which is the most dangerous neighborhood there is."

I went back anyway, eight weeks later. Alone. Because Serah was right, and because I've learned that the things I refuse to look at are the things that follow me.

The boundary was just a line of birches now. I walked between them and felt — nothing. That was the miracle and the insult of it. Twenty-six years behind a wall, and the wall turned out to be made of air. My mother must have walked through those same birches when she was exiled, carrying whatever knowledge got her banished, carrying me under her heart or already in her arms; Aldric's memories never showed me that part, only her face, drawn once in charcoal on the map's final layer, younger than I'd imagined and older than I remembered wanting her to be.

I visited the palace kitchens. My replacement — a nervous boy called Tam, sixteen, with burn scars on his forearms — recognized me from stories and nearly dropped a tureen. They don't dose the food with arsenic anymore. There's no need. The king takes his meals like an ordinary frightened mortal, and the position of royal food-taster has been quietly retired, which I consider the single greatest achievement of my life: I put myself out of work.

Tam asked me, with the reverence the young reserve for survivors, whether it was true I'd held the Crown's essence on my tongue and lived.

"It's true I held it," I said. "Whether I lived is a matter under ongoing review."

He laughed. I didn't. Some jokes are load-bearing.

I didn't stay the night. I stood in that kitchen until the pride arrived, right on schedule, and then I stood there longer, letting it sit beside the grief and the anger and the strange tenderness I feel toward my younger self, who genuinely believed that caution was a life. Then I walked out past the birches and did not look back, because I am capable of learning.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about killing an ancient entity of grief: the cleanup is worse than the battle.

The Crown didn't die cleanly. It *unraveled* — I was inside it when it happened, so I'll accept the metaphor as eyewitness testimony — but unraveling leaves threads. When the Crown wore a host for decades, pressing them flat like flowers in a book, the person didn't come back intact when the pressure lifted. The king woke aged and shattered, but he woke, because he'd been worn longest and deepest and something at his core had refused, all those years, to lie completely flat. Stubbornness as salvation. There's a lesson in that I try not to examine too closely, given how stubborn I've been about everything else.

But Mavros — Lord Mavros, the glass-eyed minister, the vessel Serah struck three years ago for smiling at nothing — Mavros did not wake. Neither did the other eleven we eventually found: courtiers, a garrison captain in the eastern provinces, a bishop whose parishioners still insist he was holy right up to the end. The Crown left them when it unraveled, and what remained were bodies that breathed, ate, blinked at loud noises, and contained no one.

Or almost no one. That's the discovery Serah and I made, the one that turned our victory tour into our vocation.

In Correnholt, in the minister's own townhouse, we found Mavros sitting in his garden, staring at a rosebush. His eyes weren't glassy anymore. They were *empty*, which is different, the way a burned house is different from a haunted one. Serah knelt in front of him and said his name — she'd struck him once, she owed him that much, she said, though I noticed she also couldn't stop checking that he wasn't going to lunge at her — and he didn't respond.

Then I did something foolish. I'd brought food, because I bring food everywhere now; it's how I pray. Bread, cheese, a pear. And I held the pear under his nose, and I said, "Bosc. Late harvest. Slightly bruised on the north side."

His eyes moved. Just a fraction. Toward the pear.

"There's a man in there," I told Serah, and my voice did something embarrassing. "A small one. But there."

We don't know why taste reaches them when words won't. My theory — and I have theories now, I'm allowed, I've earned my theories the way Serah earned her scars — is that the Crown experienced its hosts primarily through appetite. It consumed. So the last channel it leaves open, the door it forgot to lock behind itself, is the mouth. Flavor goes in where nothing else can. Grief took everything from these people except the animal fact of hunger, and hunger, it turns out, is loyal.

So that's what we do now. Serah and I, and a growing, disorganized, occasionally feuding company of volunteers we absolutely did not mean to found and cannot seem to dissolve. We travel from town to town with carts of strong flavors — preserved lemons, smoked fish, honey fermented with herbs, a spice blend Serah invented that makes grown soldiers weep — and we feed the hollowed, patiently, day after day, watching for the flicker. Sometimes it comes. Mavros spoke his first word in March. It was "*thirsty*," and Serah cried, and pretended she hadn't, and I let her pretend, because love includes respecting a person's fictions.

Sometimes it doesn't come. Those days are harder. On those days I remember what I learned inside the Crown — that it was never a demon, only a wish, a kingdom's desperate collective prayer that its dying king would simply not die, a prayer that curdled in the dark for forty years into something that consumed what it loved — and I remind myself that these hollowed ones are also wishes that went wrong. Wishes with the wisher missing. You don't give up on a wish just because granting it hurt.

Serah teaches the towns how to recognize possession early: the smile that doesn't reach the eyes, the minister who stops aging while his wife does, the official who tastes his wine and never, ever flinches. "Flinching is health," she tells assemblies of farmers and clerks, and they laugh, and then she tells them about her father, Aldric Vane, who saw it first and died carrying the proof, and the laughing stops, and they listen.

I teach them the other half. I teach cooks and innkeepers and grandmothers to taste deliberately — to notice what's in the grain, the water, the incense — because the Crown thinned wills through the food supply, and whoever wears its absence next will probably try the same door. "Your tongue is a sentry," I tell them. "Post it."

An old woman in the river towns took this further than I intended. She organized her village into what she calls the Tasting Circle, twelve grandmothers who sample every shipment and report anomalies to a council. It is either the birth of a new civic institution or the most delicious paranoid conspiracy in history, and honestly, after what I've seen, I'll take delicious paranoia over trusting silence every time.

* * *

Last week, in a market town whose name I've already forgotten — we pass through so many — a boy of maybe nine watched me eat an apricot with what he clearly considered scandalous enjoyment.

"You're supposed to check it for poison," he said. "My da says you're the Taster. The one from the songs."

"The songs exaggerate."

"My da says you drank death and spat on a god."

"I held something bitter on my tongue for about a minute and then fainted. The spitting is poetic license." I handed him half the apricot. He examined it suspiciously, decided it wasn't poisoned, and devoured it. "What do you want to know?"

He thought about it with the seriousness children bring to markets. "Does everything taste better now? Since the bad thing's gone?"

And here's the thing. I started to give him the careful answer, the trained answer, the answer with caveats arranged in order of importance. Everything costs something. Immunity burned is immunity gone forever; the next poison that touches my lips will be my first, full strength, no tolerance, no second chance. Every meal I eat for pleasure is a meal eaten undefended. Serah says I chew like a man defusing something even now, and she's right, some habits are structural.

But he was nine, and it was a market morning, and the apricot was perfect.

"Yes," I said. "Everything tastes better now."

Because it's true, or true enough. Fear was the arsenic in everything I ate for twenty-six years, and I never once tasted it directly, and now that it's gone I understand how much of every flavor it had been soaking up. Bread tastes like bread instead of like survival. Wine tastes like the vineyard instead of like vigilance. Serah's terrible campfire coffee tastes like Serah's terrible campfire coffee, which is to say like burnt pine and stubbornness and the specific mercy of being handed a cup by someone who stayed up half the night so you wouldn't have to take the dark watch alone.

I am defenseless now, is what I'm saying. Completely, permanently, gloriously defenseless. Any assassin in the kingdom could end me with a pinch of hemlock, and I have never in my life felt safer.

* * *

Tonight we camp on a ridge above the eastern road, Serah and I and the nine volunteers who followed us out of Correnholt and whom we have stopped pretending to send home. Below us the kingdom spreads out in the last light — patched, grieving, stubbornly rebuilding, smoke from ten thousand chimneys rising straight up in the windless dusk. Somewhere down there is Mavros, sitting in his garden, waiting for a word bigger than *thirsty*. Somewhere beyond the western hills is a valley of tidy farms where a boy named Cyrus once believed a wall was the same thing as a life.

Serah is sketching tomorrow's route by firelight, jaw set, tongue between her teeth, drawing roads on the map her father died making — the map that was meant for me, carried by my mother, delivered by her blood into my hands. Three generations of one family's stubbornness, folded into leather. I should tell her sometime that I've started thinking of Aldric as a kind of grandfather-by-courier. I haven't yet. Some inheritances you grow into slowly, like boots a size too large.

"Cyrus," she says, not looking up. "There's a report from the coast. Another hollowed one, a harbor official. And the local granary's grain smells wrong — their words — 'wrong like a room where someone died.'"

I feel the old alertness rise, the taster's reflex, the sentry posting itself. But it rises differently now. Not as dread. As appetite.

"Wrong how?" I ask, reaching for my pack, for my kit of little bottles, for the work.

"That's why we're going," she says. "You'll taste it and tell me."

I look at the fire, at the woman who chose me in the wreckage and keeps choosing me daily, at the road unspooling east toward whatever waits with its flavors and its dangers and its flickering, half-empty people worth feeding back to life. My mother walked this direction once, carrying a map and a secret and no way home. I get to walk it carrying a spoon, a spice chest, and company.

I get to walk it hungry.

"Pack the smoked fish," I tell Serah. "The hollowed love it, and I want some for the road."

She smiles at the map like it told her a joke, and rolls it closed, and the fire pops, and somewhere below us the kingdom goes on tasting its own future — carefully, warily, and sweeter than it knows.
