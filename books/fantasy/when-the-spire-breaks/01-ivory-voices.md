## 1. Ivory Voices

The dead in my workshop are better company than most of the living, and considerably more honest.

Take the old fisherman who lives in the whalebone comb on my third shelf. He's been there eleven years now, since his granddaughter sold me the piece for two coppers and a lie about its provenance. In life he was apparently a man who beat his wife and cheated at cards. In death he is remorseful, tedious about it, and desperate for someone—anyone—to know that the comb was never a gift, it was stolen, and he'd like that recorded somewhere before the dark takes him.

I have recorded it. Three times, in the ledger I keep beneath the floorboard, in handwriting so small even I need good light to read it. It changes nothing for him. It changes everything for me, which is a selfishness I've made peace with, mostly, on the nights when the workshop is quiet except for the murmuring.

You should understand: I did not choose this. Nobody chooses to be the one house on the street where the furniture talks.

The valley makes it possible—or rather, the valley makes everything else impossible, and leaves this behind like a coin dropped under a cupboard. Beyond the ridgelines, they say, magic runs thick as cream. Wards and wyrdings, spire-mages reading futures out of bird guts, dragon-fire sealed behind mountain doors. Here, nothing. The valley eats it all. A hedge-witch from over the pass once tried to light a candle with a word in our market square and nearly wept when nothing happened, and the whole village pretended not to notice because that's what valleys do best.

But bone remembers. Bone was alive once, and whatever small ghost of that aliveness lingers doesn't count as magic—not enough for the valley to smother, not enough for anyone to notice. Except me. I notice. When I carve a charm from old whalebone or a grandmother's knuckle-bone, sometimes something clings to it. A voice. A weight. The dead, caught like flies in amber, whispering through ivory.

And so I carve charms that work—not with luck or blessing, because there's no such thing here, but with memory. A mother buys a cradle-charm and swears her colicky infant sleeps better, and she's right, though not for the reason she thinks: the drowned sailor in the bone sings low and rhythmic, an old rowing song, and the baby sleeps because babies have always slept to the sound of grief done gently.

Maren thinks I'm simply a gifted carver. My grandmother—sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued Maren, who raised me on salt cod and suspicion of all things crowned—is proud of my hands and suspicious of my silences, which is fair. I've been lying to her for twenty-two years by omission, and the omission has grown so large we could both live in it comfortably, if either of us ever looked directly at it.

"Ansel." Her voice up the stairs, the particular flatness that means business. "There's a traveler at the gate asking after a carver."

I put down my knife. The hare-bone ring I'd been shaping sat half-finished on the bench, its resident—a chatty woman dead some forty years, full of opinions about my technique—went abruptly quiet.

"A traveler," I said. "In winter."

"In winter," Maren agreed, and her eyes did the thing where they asked a question without asking it. She wore her worry like an apron; you only saw it when she turned just so.

* * *

The traveler had come down the pass on foot, which no sane person did after first snow, and he was neither sane nor, by the look of him, long for anything. He leaned against our gatepost wrapped in a road-rough cloak gone gray with wear, and he held out toward me a charm on a leather cord as if it were the last coal of a fire he needed me to keep.

"You're the bone-carver," he rasped. Not a question. Word travels, even downhill into nowhere. "They said Eriksen takes commissions."

"I take commissions," I said carefully. Travelers were rare. Travelers who knew my name were rarer. Travelers who crossed a frozen pass to find me were carrying something, and I didn't mean in their pack.

He pressed the cord into my palm before I could refuse, and the moment it touched my skin, I understood why he'd walked out of winter to deliver it.

It screamed.

I want to be precise about this, because *scream* is a word I use rarely and mean completely. Most of the dead murmur. They repeat themselves, they circle their obsessions, they grieve in loops. What lived in this charm—some kind of river-stone-colored antler, worked by hands far finer than mine—was screaming. A voice shredded ragged, saying the same thing over and over with the desperation of a man hammering on a door as water rises:

*The hunt has begun. The hunt has begun. They are counting the hollow-boned. Tell the carver. Tell whoever carves. The hunt has begun.*

My hand closed around it reflexively, which was a mistake, because the dying man watched my face do what faces do when the dead shout through them. I felt my own expression crack along lines I'd spent a lifetime keeping smooth.

"You hear it," he whispered. Wonder and horror in equal measure, and something underneath that might have been relief. "Thank the deep dark. I wasn't sure—I wasn't sure it would find the right ears."

Inside the charm, the screamer faltered, caught itself, began again. And underneath the warning I caught other fragments now, pieces of a story told in a rush somewhere on the road: spires. White robes. A king's decree read in market squares across three provinces. A word I'd never heard but would not forget: *hollow-boned*.

"What's your name?" I asked the traveler, because it seemed suddenly important, because the dead always wanted their names kept, and because his breathing had begun to sound like paper tearing.

"Hallvard," he said. "Doesn't matter. Listen—" He gripped my wrist with fingers gone bloodless from cold or fear or both. "They test bones at the gates now. Every town, every pass. They touch a blade to your wrist and if you're one of them, if your soul's been sold, the blade sings. My brother was hollow-boned. They took him in Vellsgate in autumn. Took him smiling, like he was being honored." His laugh came out wrong. "He'd been hearing the dead his whole life too. Thought everyone did, until he learned to stop mentioning it."

The world went very still around me. The kind of still that precedes either avalanche or dawn.

"They're coming here?" I managed. "To the valley?"

"The decree names every province. The valley's charter won't hold forever—not against a crown that wants something badly enough." Hallvard slid down the gatepost until he sat in the snow, and I crouched with him because standing suddenly seemed obscene. "The charm. The man who died making it—he was spire-trained, defected. He said the mages can't hear the dead themselves. They can only harvest them. But a hollow-boned can hear. Can find them." His eyes fixed on mine, and I watched him decide to spend his last strength on one sentence. "They'll want you, carver. When they learn what you are. They'll want you alive."

*Alive.* The word landed strangely, a threat dressed as reassurance.

Inside the charm, the scream had softened to something almost like sleep. *Tell the carver,* it murmured, its work done. *Tell the carver.*

"I'll keep him," I heard myself say. "Your friend in the antler. I'll record his name, his warning. It will be kept."

Hallvard nodded, satisfied, and let go of my wrist. "Good," he said. "That's more than the crown ever—" And then he stopped, mid-word, the way lamps stop, and he was only a man sitting in the snow, and the charm in my hand went quiet as any ordinary bone.

I stayed crouched there longer than I should have. Snow gathered on my shoulders. Inside our fence, Maren stood in the doorway with her arms folded, watching me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to, because trying would have meant questions, and questions were a door I could not afford to open.

* * *

That night I sat in the workshop with the antler charm before me and my ledger open, and I wrote: *Hallvard, traveler. Bearer. And within: a defector of the spire, name unknown, who died to carry a warning.*

Then, because the dead deserve truth even when the living don't get it, I added: *The hunt has begun.*

The workshop murmured around me—the fisherman's remorse, the rowing sailor's song, forty years of small voices in small bones—and for the first time in my life the sound of them frightened me. Because I had always believed the valley protected me. That its great silence was a wall between me and everything hungry beyond the ridgelines. I had built my whole self inside that belief, the way you build a house on ground you've never thought to test.

Hollow-boned. Sold. A soul traded away generations before my birth, and a crown now combing the world for the merchandise.

I touched my own wrist, where Hallvard said the blades would sing.

Upstairs, floorboards creaked. Maren, awake too, moving through her nighttime rituals. Pausing, perhaps, outside my door the way she sometimes did, saying nothing, the way she often did.

And far off, at the edge of hearing—or maybe only at the edge of imagination—the valley's silence seemed to shift, like something vast turning over in its sleep, deciding whether to let us keep it.

Three days later, the soldiers came to seal the pass.
