## 2. The Decree at the Gate

The trouble with living in a valley where magic cannot follow you is that eventually the magic sends someone who can walk.

I was in the workroom, sanding a whalebone comb for the ferryman's wife, when the dead started screaming. Not the usual murmuring—dead bone is mostly quiet, a sort of tide-pull at the edge of hearing—but screaming, high and ragged, from the charm I'd carved for old Halvard two winters back. The one he'd worn into his grave, and which his granddaughter had hung on her doorpost because she couldn't bear to throw it away.

*They're coming,* it said, in Halvard's wheedling voice. *They're coming and they have paper.*

Dead men are rarely specific. I put down the sandpaper and went to the window.

They came up the valley road at midday, eleven soldiers in crown grey with a wagon and an iron-banded chest, and behind them walked a thin man in robes the color of storm-water who did not look at the mountains the way visitors do. He looked at the people. At their bones, I would learn later, or near enough. A spire-mage. First one I'd ever seen outside of Maren's warnings and my own nightmares.

Maren stood in the doorway beside me. She smelled the smoke on me before I said anything—I hadn't said anything—and her hand found my shoulder with the weight of a millstone settling.

"Stay inside," she said. "Whatever happens. Stay *inside*."

"Maren—"

"I'm not asking, child."

She had never called me child before. It was always *Ansel*, flat as a plank, because endearments were for people whose lives weren't built on top of a secret the size of a soul. I stayed inside. It was the last time staying inside would ever be enough.

* * *

By evening the whole valley had been summoned to the gate square by decree, read aloud in a voice like wet slate. Every resident of Eriksdal and the outlying steads would submit to bone-testing. No exemptions. No appeals. The crown thanked them for their cooperation.

Nobody asked what bone-testing was. That's the thing about our valley—we know better than to ask questions that have answers.

I watched from the loft window while they did it, one by one, at a table the mage had set up under a lantern that burned green. A soldier pressed each villager's palm to a slab of pale stone, and the stone either did nothing or it sang. I couldn't hear what it sang from the loft, but I could see faces. Old Sorensen went first, came away shrugging. The ferryman's wife. Halvard's granddaughter, who clutched her doorpost-charm and wept for reasons the soldiers mistook for fear and I knew were grief, because I could hear the charm from three streets away, keening *they will take them, they will take all of them.*

Then Maren stepped up to the table.

I want to say I understood immediately. I didn't. I thought she was being brave, standing in line like everyone else so no one would notice one person missing. It took me until I saw her reach into her coat—the deep inner pocket where she kept the charms I'd made her over thirty years, every one carved from bone that had once belonged to someone who trusted her—to understand what she was actually doing.

She'd spent the afternoon at the firepit. Of course she had. She'd burned them all.

When the soldier asked her to place her palm on the stone, she did, and the stone did nothing, because Maren Winterbourne was exactly what she appeared to be: an old woman with clean hands and no ghosts. But the mage wasn't looking at the stone. He was looking at her coat pocket, where ash still clung to the wool like frost, and then he looked past her, up at the houses, at windows, at doors.

At doorposts.

Halvard's granddaughter stood at the gate with her charm around her neck now, worn openly, defiantly, because she was eighty-one years old and nobody was going to take her dead husband's voice without a fight. The soldier lifted it from her gently, turned it in his fingers, and frowned at the underside.

Every carver has a signature. Mine is a spiral broken by a single straight line—a river leaving a valley. Maren used to say it was morbid. I used to say it was honest.

The soldier carried the charm to the mage. The mage held it to the green lantern, and even from the loft I heard it—not the charm, but the mage, drawing a breath through his teeth like a man who has just found gold in a beggar's mouth.

"Who carved this?" he called out. Loud. Patient. The voice of a man who already knew.

Silence, the particular silence of a hundred people deciding simultaneously to become very stupid. Our valley is good at that too.

"Someone here," the mage said pleasantly, "has been listening to the dead."

* * *

Maren got home after midnight moving faster than a woman her age should. She didn't light a lamp. She packed by touch: bread, cheese, the knife with the antler handle, my good cloak, and forty years of coin saved in a crock that had always been labeled FLOUR, DON'T TOUCH, which I had honored out of respect and never once believed.

"You're going over the ridge," she said. "Tonight."

"The pass is sealed, Maren. They'll see me."

"They'll see a girl going out to check her snares." She thrust the pack at me. "You go east along the water until the gorge narrows, then climb. Nobody climbs the gorge. That's why you climb the gorge."

"And on the other side?"

Her hands stopped. In the dark I heard her breathing change, the way it does when someone is choosing between lies and can't quite afford either.

"I don't know," she admitted. "I've never known. Your grandmother—" she caught herself, jaw tightening. "*My* mother told me stories about beyond the ridge. Fen country. Marsh folk. People the crown forgets exists." Her grip on my arm was fierce. "You find them. You stay hidden until I send word."

"You're coming with me."

"I'm staying to be stupid loudly. Someone has to stand at that table tomorrow and swear up and down that the carver died of fever last spring, and it can't be you, because you'll be gone, and it can't be anyone else, because everyone else is a terrible liar." She almost smiled. "Except me. I raised you. Lying is the one craft I taught you properly."

"Maren." My voice cracked like green wood. "If they test you again—if they look closer—"

"Then I'll have earned it." She pulled me against her, and I felt how thin she'd gotten, how much of her was just stubbornness wrapped around bone. "Listen to me. Whatever you find out there—whatever you *are* out there—you come back if you can, and you don't come back if you can't. Either way, you were loved. That part isn't a lie and never was."

I kissed her forehead, which tasted of woodsmoke, because everything in our house did now. Then I climbed out the loft window and left the only home magic had never been allowed to touch.

* * *

The gorge at night is a throat of black stone with the river growling at the bottom of it. I climbed badly, terrified, certain at every handhold that the rock would betray me, and the strange thing—the thing I still can't explain cleanly—is that the higher I went, the less afraid I became. Not calmer. *Larger.* Like a room I'd lived in all my life was quietly losing its walls.

I reached the ridge top an hour before dawn, breath steaming, palms bleeding, and looked back at the valley one last time. Lanterns at the gate. Smoke rising from our chimney, where Maren was feeding the fire with the rest of the evidence of me. Small. Safe. Sealed like a jar.

Then I turned east, and crossed the line.

I don't know what I expected. Nothing, probably. The stories said magic couldn't follow past the ridge; I assumed it simply wouldn't bother.

It bothered.

It came up through the soles of my feet like cold fire and poured into me, into every hollow place—and there were more hollow places than I'd known, oh, so many, I was practically architecture—and the dead came with it. Not my gentle valley dead, the ones I'd kept company with all my life, murmuring their small regrets. These were strangers. Hundreds. Thousands. A horizon-wide murmur swelling into a roar, names and griefs and hungers pouring through a door I hadn't known was closed because I'd never seen it open.

I fell to my knees on the bare granite of the ridge and screamed, and something answered. Something vast and old and *below*, beneath the mountain ahead of me, something that stirred like a sleeper turning over and said—or sang, or struck me like a bell rung inside my own ribs—one word:

*Found.*

Then darkness took the polite route and knocked me flat.

* * *

I woke to gray light, a splitting headache, and the distinct sensation of being watched by something with feathers. A marsh bird stood a foot from my face, regarding me with the frank contempt of its kind.

Beyond it: fens. Miles of them, rust and black water, stretching toward a mountain that shouldn't have been visible from here and yet dominated the skyline anyway, wearing a spire like a splinter driven into its skull. The dead were quieter now—not gone, never gone again, I understood that with terrible clarity—but settled, like a crowd finding its seats.

And there were footprints in the mud. Recent ones. Large ones, circling my body twice, then leading off toward a thread of smoke rising from the reeds.

Whoever it was had found me unconscious, defenseless, and radiating whatever-it-was-that-made-the-mage's-teeth-click, and had chosen not to cut my throat. I decided this was either very good news or a trap with excellent manners.

Either way, the smoke meant fire, and fire meant people, and I had a pack, a knife, a bleeding pair of hands, and absolutely nowhere else to go.

I picked myself up, told the marsh bird that yes, I knew how I looked, and limped toward the smoke—while somewhere behind my sternum, patient as tide, the mountain waited.
