## 3. The Warden of Embers

Magic found me on the ridge, the way a dog finds a rabbit.

I had crossed the valley's edge at dusk, half-crawling through heather with Maren's burnt charms still smelling of smoke on my skin, and the moment my boot left the last stone of the pass, something reached up through the ground and *noticed* me. That is the only word for it. In the valley I had spent twenty-three years wrapped in silence so thick I'd mistaken it for the shape of the world. Beyond it, the world had teeth. Wind that whispered in languages no living throat could make. Grass that leaned toward me like I was warm and it was cold. And under all of it, faint as a pulse through a floorboard, something vast turning over in its sleep beneath a mountain I couldn't yet see.

I did what any sensible person does when the universe finally pays attention to them. I kept walking, and I talked to the dead to keep myself company, because they were suddenly everywhere and rude about it.

*Cold,* said a shepherd dead two hundred years, whose bones were somewhere under this hill. *You're walking toward the cold place. Everyone walks toward the cold place eventually.*

"Thank you," I muttered, "for the encouragement."

*I'm just saying. Nobody comes back down the mountain happy.*

The fens began where the hills gave out — black water stitched with reeds, mist sitting on everything like a debt collector. I'd been slogging through it for the better part of a day when I saw the light: a single ember-orange glow through the reeds, steady, wrong. Fire shouldn't sit still like that. Fire should flicker, should breathe. This burned like something patient.

The dead went quiet around it. All of them, every whisper in a hundred yards of marsh, pulled back like children called inside before dark. In my experience, when even the dead decide a place isn't worth haunting, you find another campsite.

But behind me, somewhere down the white road I'd fled along, there were soldiers, and one of them knew my carvings the way a priest knows scripture. So I walked toward the fire.

She was sitting cross-legged beside it when I pushed through the reeds, and I will be honest: my first thought was that the fen had grown a woman out of mud and spite. She was broad as a door, grey-haired, with burn scars climbing her forearms like ivy made of wax. Her coat was warden's leather once — I'd seen the pattern on travelers' tales, dragon-scale plates sewn over the chest — but the scales had been pried off, every one, leaving a hundred small pale sockets across the hide. A coat that had been *unmade*, deliberately, stitch by stitch. You don't lose those scales. Someone takes them from you, or you tear them off yourself.

The fire she sat before wasn't wood-fed. It rose out of a bronze brazier shaped like a coiled serpent, and it burned without smoke, without crackle, in colors I had no names for — gold going to white going to something almost blue at the heart. It was the smallest enormous thing I have ever seen.

"You're standing in my snare-line," she said without looking up.

"I don't see any snare."

"That's because you haven't stepped on it yet. Give it a minute." She looked up then. Her eyes were the exact color of the fire's heart, which I decided not to think too hard about. "Bone-carver."

It wasn't a question, and my hand went to my pack before I could stop it. "That's a trade, not a crime."

"Not yet." She turned back to her fire. "Sit down or step on the snare, either way. Standing there dripping, you look like something the marsh coughed up, and I've had a bad decade."

I sat. The ground accepted me with the enthusiasm of wet sponge. Up close the brazier-fire made sounds after all — very faint, very far away, like singing heard through several walls. Like a great many voices, all holding one long note.

"What is that?" I asked.

"Dinner," she said, "if things go poorly. Supper guest, if they go well." She produced a tin kettle, filled it from a skin, set it in the fire's outer glow. The fire did not boil the water so much as *persuade* it. "Name?"

"Ansel."

"Ansel the bone-carver." She tasted the name like tea she suspected of being poisoned. "Ansel Eriksen. Valley-born. Adopted — Maren Winterbourne's grandchild, though she never signed anything, clever old woman." My whole body went still, and she noticed, because she smiled without any warmth in it. "Relax. If I were selling you to the spire-mages, we'd already be in cages. They pay well for hollow-boned."

The word landed in me like a hook finding flesh. "Hollow-what?"

"Hollow-boned." She poured tea into two cups, pushed one toward me with her boot. It was terrible tea. Fenwater and despair, mostly. "You really don't know. Of course you don't. The valley keeps its secrets by keeping everyone's." She drank, watching me over the rim. "Tell me what you hear right now. Not the dead — I know you hear the dead, your kind always does, they cling to ivory like flies to honey. Tell me what you hear under the dead."

I didn't want to answer. Answering meant admitting, and admitting, outside the valley, felt less like confession and more like inventory. But the fire was singing, and the dead had gone silent, and this woman with her flayed coat was looking at me the way Maren looked at a charm she already knew was mine.

"A note," I said finally. "One long note. Under everything. Since I crossed the ridge. I thought it was the mountain."

"It's not the mountain." She set down her cup. "It's a seal. There's a court under that mountain — older than the crown, older than the dragons, older than most of the gods anyone bothers to worship — and it is sealed shut by a fire that has one keeper left." She spread her scarred hands. "Me. Congratulations on your timing. You've met the last drop of dragon-fire in the world, and it is nearly out."

I looked at the brazier. Small enough to carry. Bright enough to read by. "That's it? That's the famous fire?"

"That's what's *left* of it," she said, and for a moment her voice cracked open and I saw underneath: not a grumpy woman in a swamp but someone who had fed everything she loved into a flame and watched it eat. "There was a fire-pit the size of a cathedral once. Two hundred wardens. Dragons — actual dragons, Ansel, creatures the size of weather. Now it's this." She touched the brazier the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "And do you know why it's dying?"

"No."

"Because someone's been drinking from it. Slowly. For generations. Every year a little less fire, and nobody could work out where it went, until I did the arithmetic and got myself disgraced for saying the answer out loud." Her jaw tightened. "The fire doesn't fade. It's being *fed to*. Something under the mountain has a mouth, and the crown has been feeding it. Souls, Ansel. Hollow-boned souls. Yours included."

The marsh seemed to lean in. Even the terrible tea went cold in my hands, or maybe that was me.

"My soul," I repeated. It sounded absurd in my own mouth. "My soul was sold?"

"Before you could walk. Probably before you could breathe." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "That's what hollow-boned means. Your family — blood family, not Maren — sold your soul to the court beneath the mountain. It happens in valleys like yours. Magicless places have to buy their magiclessness from somebody, and the price is always the same: one child's soul per generation, delivered by bloodline, collected whenever the spire-mages come calling." She shrugged. "The dead speak to you because you've got a hole where your soul should be, and the dead are nothing if not eager to fill empty rooms. And the spire-mages hunt you because your soul isn't gone. It's *stored*. Sitting under that mountain, keeping their pet fire alive."

I want to tell you I took this well. That I stood up and declared my purpose and felt the grand architecture of destiny settle around my shoulders. What actually happened was that I put down the cup very carefully, because my hands had stopped being reliable, and stared into the little fire that sang with stolen voices, and said:

"Maren knows."

"She signed the receipt, more than likely. Or her mother did. Valley elders always know. That's the arrangement — they get quiet years, and the mages get their harvest when the crop comes due." Sefa watched me with those fire-colored eyes, and I braced for it — the pity, the careful softness people use when they've decided you're a tragedy. "Your grandmother burning her own charms to cover your escape was either love or guilt. Usually it's both. Doesn't much matter now."

"You're very comforting."

"I'm very honest. Comforting costs extra." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved across the empty scale-sockets of her coat like stars falling into holes. "Here's my offer, bone-carver. I need to reach the mountain. The seal is failing, and when it fails, everything the court has swallowed for a thousand years comes back up, and I am the only warden left to stand in that doorway. I can't do it alone, and I can't do it without knowing how the harvest works from the inside." She tapped her own chest, once. "You're the inside. Your soul is down there. You can feel it — don't lie to me, I watched your face when I said the word *stored*. You felt it turn over. Like a name being called from a house you've never visited."

I had. God help me, I had. Somewhere under that unseen mountain, something that was legally, cosmologically *me* had shifted in its sleep, and every dead voice in the marsh had hushed to listen.

"And in exchange?" I managed.

"In exchange I get you to the mountain alive, which is more than anyone else in the world can offer, because the spire-mages want you breathing and every other power wants you buried." She stuck out one scarred hand. "Passage, protection, and the truth, however ugly. You give me your help getting in, and your ears — those ivory-hearing ears — for whatever's down there. Wary allies, no oaths, no promises we can't keep. Deal?"

I looked at her hand. Burn-scarred, warden-calloused, steady as a keel.

Everyone in my life had loved me *around* the secret. Maren had loved me while burning evidence. The dead had clung to me because I was useful furniture. This woman had heard the worst of what I was — sold, hollow, harvested — and hadn't flinched, hadn't pitied, had simply recalculated and offered terms.

It was, I realized, the closest thing to respect I'd ever been shown by a living person.

"Deal," I said, and shook.

Her grip was warm as banked coals. Then her head snapped toward the dark edge of the reed-bed, and her voice dropped to gravel.

"Except we may negotiate payment later. We've got company." She was already kicking earth over the brazier, smothering her dying god with wet soil. "Horses. Four of them. And horses out here at night means riders, and riders out here means—" 

The first shout came through the mist, high and strange, more song than speech. My own bones recognized it before my mind did, and every hair on my arms stood up.

"—spire-mages," Sefa finished grimly, hauling the buried brazier onto her back. "They're not supposed to reach the fens for three days. Somebody told them exactly where you'd land." She fixed me with a look like a drawn blade. "Question is, carver: who knew your route?"

Somewhere in the reeds, the singing came closer — and it was singing my name.
