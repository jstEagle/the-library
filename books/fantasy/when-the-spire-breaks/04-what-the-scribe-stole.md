## 4. What the Scribe Stole

There is a particular art to stealing from the crown, and I had mastered all of it except the part where you don't get caught.

The ledgers were easy. Ledgers always are. Nobody guards paper the way they guard gold, because nobody in the Spire's service believes a stack of numbers can hang them. Nine volumes of harvest tallies, bound in calfskin and stamped with the triple-crown seal, and I walked out of the Archive Annex with them under my cloak on a Tuesday, nodding to the door-warden like a man carrying nothing heavier than his own conscience. The rations were where my genius failed me. Six wheels of hard cheese, a sack of smoked fish, and a jar of pickled onions — enough to keep a fugitive alive through a mountain winter — and I could not walk out of the provisioning cellar with those without tripping over my own guilt and knocking a lantern off its hook.

Which is how Sefa found me: flat on my back in the dark, a wheel of cheese under one shoulder, three spire-soldiers' boots descending toward my face.

She came out of the reeds like a judgment with a spear.

I will not pretend I fought. I am a scribe. My hands are for ink, and the only thing I have ever killed is an argument. What I did was scream, which startled her, which startled *them*, and in that symphony of mutual alarm she cracked two helmets together and hauled me into the fen by my collar while I clutched the cheese like a talisman. The soldiers did not follow far. Fens make men thoughtful about their boots.

That was three days ago. Since then we have traveled north along the drowned road — Sefa, myself, and the bone-carver Ansel Eriksen, who has not yet decided whether to kill me, and who keeps looking at me in a way that suggests they are drafting the argument first.

I know why. I have read their name in a ledger. I have read it so many times I could carve it myself.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about working in the Spire's archives: you become very good at reading things you were never meant to read. A clerk's error here, a misfiled folio there, and suddenly you are holding the true account of the kingdom, the one that lives beneath the pretty one. For six years I copied decrees about "the protection of gifted persons" and "the relocation of magically sensitive citizens," and I believed them, or I believed in them, which is worse, because belief lets you sleep.

Then, four months ago, I pulled the wrong volume off the wrong shelf and learned the word *harvest*.

Not hunt. Hunt is what the proclamations say. Harvest is what the ledgers say. Column after column of names, each with a valuation beside it — soul-weight, they call it, measured in something called ember-units — and each name marked *delivered* or *pending*. Hollow-boned, the ledgers call us. People whose souls were sold to the court beneath the mountain, generations back, sold so thoroughly that their descendants carry the debt in their blood. The spire-mages aren't hunting them out of cruelty. Cruelty would at least be comprehensible. They're hunting them because the seal on the court below is failing, and the seal runs on dragon-fire, and the dragon-fire has been dying for a century, and harvested souls burn longer than wood.

My brother's name is in the fourth ledger. Row thirty-one. *Delivered.*

He was taken when I was nine. They told our village he'd been conscripted. I wrote his conscription papers myself last spring, as practice, because the archive wanted samples of historical documents reproduced in fair hand. I sat there copying his name into a forgery of his death and told myself it was just work.

So yes. I stole the ledgers. And I ran. And if you want to call that courage, I'd ask you to note that I ran north instead of south, north being away from every border, because I hadn't actually planned anything beyond *not being there anymore* — and that I took cheese, because fear makes me practical and hungry in equal measure.

* * *

We made camp that third night in the shell of a waystation, its roof burned out years ago, stars where the rafters should be. Sefa rationed the fish with the grim economy of someone who has fed soldiers before. Ansel sat apart, turning a scrap of whalebone between their fingers, listening — I didn't know that's what they were doing yet, but even then they had the look of a person attending a conversation no one else could hear.

"So," Sefa said, to me, in the tone of a woman opening a negotiation by setting your fingers on the table. "You're going to tell us what's worth dying over. Because those books weigh more than the cheese, and you've been sleeping with them."

"They're proof," I said.

"Proof of what?"

And here is the thing I want recorded, whoever ends up writing this down — and given the company I keep, it may well be written down on whalebone, which feels fitting — the thing I want recorded is that I hesitated. Not because I doubted the truth of it. Because telling them made it real in a way the ledgers weren't. Paper can be burned. A face across a fire, going pale as you explain that their soul was sold before their grandmother was born — that cannot be unburned.

"Proof that the hunt isn't a hunt," I said. "It's a harvest. The Spire's been collecting hollow-boned people for decades. Their souls feed the seal on the court under the mountain, because the dragon-fire keeping that seal is almost gone." I looked at Sefa when I said it, because everyone knows what happened to the wardens, and her face did something complicated and then went still. "The ledgers have names. Hundreds of names. Valuations. Delivery dates."

Ansel had stopped turning the bone.

"How many," they said quietly.

"I haven't counted. Thousands, across the full set. The recent volumes run maybe forty a year."

"And mine's in there."

It wasn't a question, and I hated them a little for making me say it anyway. "Row twelve of the seventh volume. Sold — the entry says sold — sixty-one years ago. Two names on the seller's line." I recited it because reciting was safer than sympathy; I have never been good at sympathy, only at accuracy. "The payment column lists grain remissions to a valley settlement. Protection tariff, the clerk called it. Your parents bought your valley forty years of exemption with you."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the fen, something with too many legs moved through the water and thought better of approaching us. Ansel looked at their hands for a long time, these hands that had spent a whole life carving charms for the dead, and I watched a person understand that their entire self had been a transaction conducted before they drew breath.

"You knew," Sefa said to me. Her voice was very level. "Before you met us. You saw the name and you said nothing."

"I was deciding how."

"That's a coward's arithmetic."

"Yes," I said. "I'm aware. I've done the sums." I pulled the seventh volume from my pack and set it on the stone between us, open to the page, because there is a kind of courage that is just cowardice arriving late, and late is better than never. "But I also carried these books through four checkpoints sewn into my coat lining, and I can read the Spire's cipher faster than the mages who invented it, and I know the delivery schedule for the next harvest convoy, which passes through the White Road in eleven days. So arrest me later. Use me now."

Sefa studied me the way she probably studied dragons: for the weak point, and for whether the thing was worth the risk of getting close.

"The convoy," she said.

"Six mages, twenty soldiers, and — this is the part I need you to hear — eight prisoners. Pending entries. Alive." I swallowed. "They're transported sedated, but they're alive, and they're headed for the mountain, and once they go under, they don't come back up. Ever."

Ansel spoke without looking up from their hands. "Eight people who are what I am."

"People who are what you are," I agreed. "Minus the part where anyone bothered to sell yours properly. Your entry's flagged irregular — the soul was paid out but never delivered. It's still held in reserve. At the source." I turned the ledger toward them and put my finger under the notation, because showing is braver than telling and I needed all the borrowed bravery I could get tonight. "*Retained — seat of the seal.* Your soul is sitting under that mountain, Ansel Eriksen. On the throne, if the old records are right. Feeding the fire."

Nobody spoke. Above us the stars turned with the patience of things that have watched kingdoms rot before and expect to watch ours do the same.

Then Sefa began packing the fish back into the sack with brisk, terrible purpose. "Eleven days," she said. "The White Road. Then the mountain." She glanced at me, and it was not warmth, exactly. It was the closest thing to trust that a disgraced warden extends to a thieving clerk in a burned-out waystation, and I have decided to treasure it. "Don't make me regret not feeding you to the fen."

"No promises about the cheese, though," I said, because humor is also a kind of armor and mine is thin.

Ansel finally lifted their head. In the firelight their eyes had the flat shine of someone standing at the edge of a decision, and when they spoke, their voice was quiet and absolutely steady.

"Then we'd better move fast," they said, "because I'm going to take it back."

I opened my mouth to tell them everything else the ledgers said about what happens to hollow-boned souls that are reclaimed — about seals, and consequences, and the arithmetic of thousands against one — and found, for the first time in my life, that I couldn't make the words come out in order.

Because Ansel was already looking north, toward the mountain, and the firelight caught something in their face that no ledger I have ever copied had a column for.

Hope. Or something wearing hope's coat.

Either way, we had eleven days, and I had a confession still lodged in my throat like a fishbone: that Maren — the grandmother whose charms bought Ansel's hiding place — appears in these pages too. As a signatory. Twice.

I would tell them. Soon. Probably.

I am a very good scribe, and I know exactly how long a lie of omission takes to write itself down.
