## 5. The Hunt on the White Road

The White Road earned its name honestly. It was old imperial paving, bone-pale stone laid by hands long dead, winding up the mountain's flank like a scar that never healed properly. Sefa walked it the way you'd walk across a sleeping man's chest—softly, apologetically, and with one eye on his face for signs of waking.

"The mages patrol this stretch," she said, for the fourth time that morning. "They can't feel us through the wards in my pack, but they'll *see* us if we're stupid."

"I'm never stupid," Tobin said. "I'm a scribe. We're constitutionally incapable of it. It's why they keep us in basements."

"You were caught stealing rations from your own kitchen."

"That was an act of *bravery* that went slightly wrong."

I let them bicker and kept my eyes on the road ahead, my thumb working the edge of the whalebone charm at my throat. The dead had been quiet since we left the fens—a silence I should have found restful and instead found suspicious, like a forest gone still before something steps out of it.

The traveler's charm back home had screamed its warning about the hunt. What it hadn't told me—and what Sefa had explained over burnt fish and Tobin's stolen cheese—was how the hunt worked. The spire-mages couldn't track hollow-boned souls directly. The souls didn't want to be found. They wanted to be *left alone*, which is more than I could say for most people.

So the mages used the captured dead as hunting dogs.

"They sing," Sefa said. "A soul-song. Every soul has one—the sound it made when it was first taken. When they sing yours back to you, your own soul answers. And then you stop walking."

"Stop walking how?"

"Just stop." She hadn't looked at me when she said it. She'd looked at the fire coiled in the iron lantern she carried, the last ember of the last dragon, small as a fingernail and burning like it was embarrassed about it.

I thought about that now, climbing into the thin bright air, and decided that stopping didn't sound so bad. I'd been stopping my whole life—stopping myself from flinching at voices, from carving certain shapes, from being what I was. Stopping sounded almost like home.

The mountain disagreed.

It happened at the bend where the road crossed a stream, water running silver over white stone. One moment the world was ordinary: wind, gravel, Tobin complaining about blisters in the tone of someone who has never had a real problem. The next moment, the air filled with singing.

Not from throats. From everywhere—from the stones themselves, from the stream, from the sky. A voice like mine would sound if my voice had been pulled out of me, stretched on a frame, and taught to play itself. High and clear and *familiar*, because it was mine. My laugh in it. My grandmother Maren's lullaby folded into it somewhere, the one she sang when she thought I was asleep.

My soul knew the song. Of course it did. It was the song of its own theft, generations ago, sung by the dead who'd been made to memorize it.

And my soul answered.

I felt it happen like a door opening in my chest. Something in me turned toward the music with the terrible eagerness of a dog hearing its name after years of being called nothing. The world went soft at the edges. My legs stopped. My hand stopped carving air. Even my heartbeat seemed to pause mid-thought, listening.

*There you are,* said the song. *We've been looking for you.*

Somewhere very far away, Tobin was saying my name. Somewhere closer, Sefa swore in a language that predated the empire, and the lantern clinked against her belt buckles as she fumbled with it.

I remember thinking, with the small clear corner of my mind that stayed mine: *so this is what stopping feels like from the inside.* It wasn't peaceful at all. It was like being a held breath.

Then Sefa opened the lantern, reached in bare-handed, and tore a piece off the last dragon-fire in the world.

I don't have words that fit it. I've carved ivory for thirty years; I know every word for bone and blood and quiet, and none of them work. The best I can do is this: imagine a sun deciding to scream. Imagine all the warmth you've ever felt gathered into one place and told to fight.

Sefa took a decade of that warmth—a decade, she'd later say, off a fire that had perhaps two decades left—and threw it at the song.

The flame hit the air and became a voice louder than mine. Not a roar. Dragons don't roar, apparently; they *name*. The fire spoke one syllable older than the mountain, older than the court beneath it, and the soul-song shattered around us like ice off a roof in spring.

The paralysis broke. I went to my knees on the white stone, gasping, my whole body ringing like a struck bell. Across the stream, three figures in grey-and-silver stood frozen—not mages themselves, I realized, but their instruments: three corpses propped upright by magic, mouths open, the songs dying in their borrowed throats. Behind them, further up the road, robed shapes were already turning, already running toward whatever had just interrupted them.

"Sefa—" Tobin's voice cracked. "Your hand."

She'd wrapped it in her cloak. Through the wool, light leaked. Not flickering—*leaking*, steadily, the way a wound leaks.

"How much?" I asked. I meant the fire. I meant everything.

"Enough," she said, which is what soldiers say instead of numbers.

Tobin hauled me up under the arms, surprisingly strong for a person built entirely out of ink stains and guilt. "Mages incoming," they said, pointing uphill, where four robed figures were picking their way down through the scree with the unhurried confidence of people who own everything they survey.

"Off the road," Sefa rasped. "Down. There's a village."

"There's nothing down there but cliffs," Tobin said.

"There's a village," Sefa repeated, in the voice of a woman who had once commanded dragons and did not intend to start taking notes from scribes, and we went down the cliffside together, sliding and catching each other, while behind us the fire's last spoken syllable faded off the rocks like breath off glass.

* * *

The village was called Hallowmere, though no sign said so. Sefa told us the name later, quietly, the way you tell someone the name of the deceased.

It sat in a fold of the mountain like a thing that had given up. Roofs fallen in. Doors open to weather. A well with a bucket still tied to its rope, frozen in the act of being drawn up, ten years ago or fifty. Snow lay in every room equally, without preference, the way snow does when nobody's left to sweep it.

We sheltered in the largest house, which had been an inn once—I knew by the taproom benches, by the great hearth Sefa stared at for a long moment before turning away. Tobin barricaded the door with furniture while I caught my breath by the cold hearth, and it was there, warming my hands at a fire that had been ash for a generation, that I finally heard them.

At first I thought it was the wind. Wind does things to ruined buildings, whistles through broken chimneys, moans in eaves. But this wasn't wind. This was *voices*. Hundreds of them. Thin as threads, layered like sediment, rising out of the floorboards, the walls, the frozen well outside, the whole drowned bowl of the village.

Every one of them was humming.

Every one of them was humming *my song*.

I gripped the whalebone charm and listened, because that's what I do, it's the only thing I've ever done, and the dead here weren't speaking in words—they were too far gone for words—but they didn't need words. They were sold souls. Hollow-boned, harvested, carried up this road in chains of song and poured into whatever the spire-mages pour them into. They'd left echoes here. Echoes of the moment they stopped being people and became fuel.

And every echo carried the same melody. Because it wasn't only *my* family line the court had bought. It was lines. Plural. Generations of families who'd traded children to the mountain for protection, for rain, for peace—whole valleys of parents signing ledgers, and Tobin's stolen pages suddenly rearranging themselves in my head from *paperwork* into *graveyard*.

"Ansel." Tobin crouched beside me. They must have seen my face. Everyone always sees my face before I know what it's doing. "What is it? What do you hear?"

I opened my mouth to say *nothing*, the way I'd said it my whole life, the valley-word, the hiding-word.

Instead I heard myself say: "There are hundreds of them. Hundreds of souls, Tobin. Sold. Like mine." My voice came out flat and strange. "This whole village was a harvest. Maybe more than one. They hummed the song until it wore grooves in the stone, and now the stone remembers it, and the stone is *still humming*."

Sefa stood in the doorway with the lantern held against her chest like a wounded bird. In the dimness, the light inside it guttered—actually guttered, the way candlelight does, which dragon-fire is not supposed to do. She saw me notice. For once, the warden of embers didn't bother lying.

"A decade," she said. "Maybe less, now. That's what breaking the song cost." Her jaw worked. "I spent forty years keeping that fire alive so the seal would hold. So the court beneath the mountain would stay sealed. And today I learned the seal doesn't hold *despite* the harvests. It holds *because* of them." She looked at her bandaged hand, at the light seeping through. "They're not guarding the prison, hollow-boned. They're feeding it. And the thing they're feeding it with is sitting right there in front of you, wearing your face, waiting to be collected."

The dead hummed. The snow fell through the broken roof, unhurried, indifferent. Somewhere up the White Road, the mages were regrouping, patient as arithmetic.

I looked down at my hands—carver's hands, scarred, ordinary—and understood, finally and completely, that running had never been the plan. Running was just what you did while you figured out the plan.

"Tobin," I said. "Get out the ledgers. Find my name again. And find me the names of everyone in this village, because somebody wrote them down, and somebody signed, and I want to know exactly who sold whom."

"Why?" Tobin asked.

"Because I'm done listening to the dead," I said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back. "Now I'm going to answer them."
