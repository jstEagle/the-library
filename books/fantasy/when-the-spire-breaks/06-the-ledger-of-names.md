## 6. The Ledger of Names

I have always believed that the worst thing a person can do with paper is burn it. I was raised in the Scriptorium under the Spire, where ink is holy and ledgers are scripture, where we were taught that every entry is a soul made legible. I stole those ledgers because I thought they were weapons. I did not understand that they were graves.

Now, in a ruined village at the roof of the world, I sit among the graves and read them.

The house Sefa chose for our shelter still has half its roof, which by local standards makes it a palace. The dead here do not speak to me—I am not hollow-boned, thank all the indifferent gods—but they press against the walls anyway. I feel them the way you feel weather coming. A pressure behind the eyes. Ansel feels them properly, of course. They went out an hour ago to walk among the empty houses, saying they wanted "to listen," as though that were not the most horrifying sentence I have ever heard spoken cheerfully.

Sefa sits across from me, feeding slivers of something she calls emberwood into a brazier no bigger than a cupped hand. The fire inside it is red as a wound. She watches me read the way a hawk watches a field mouse: patiently, with the assumption that everything ends badly.

"You've been on that same page for a quarter hour," she says.

"I'm being thorough."

"You're being afraid."

I turn another page rather than answer, because she's right, and because the page I'm turning toward has a column heading I recognize from the deepest restricted archive—the one Tobin Vess, Junior Archivist Third Class, was never cleared to enter and entered anyway. The column reads *Provenance: Valley Settlements.* And beneath it, in the tidy clerical hand I was trained to produce myself:

*Eriksen, Ansel. Sold. Year 1 of the Hollow Census. Price: valley exemption, perpetual. Sellers: two names, struck through per standard practice.*

Two names, struck through.

Parents.

* * *

Let me explain what a ledger entry means, because the words are designed to be gentle and the meaning is not.

When a child is born hollow-boned—born with their soul already promised, already carried away to feed the seal beneath the mountain—the court requires a record. The record includes the price paid for the child's soul. Usually it's small. A debt forgiven. A tax remitted. Once, memorably, a pig.

Ansel Eriksen's soul was sold for a valley.

Not land. Not gold. An *exemption*. The crown decreed that the valley where Ansel was born would be forever closed to magic, forever outside the hunt, forever safe—and the payment was one infant's soul, signed over before the child could even focus its eyes.

And here is the arithmetic that made my hands go cold: the valley could only be exempt if there were nothing in it worth taking. No magic. No resonance. Which meant Ansel's parents didn't just sell their baby. They sold their baby and then raised it in a place built specifically to muffle the very thing the sale had created. They built a silence around their child so the court would never come to collect.

It was almost merciful. It was almost monstrous. It was both, entirely, and I sat in the ruin of a dead village holding proof of it while snow ticked against the broken window like someone politely asking to be let in.

"There's more," I said, because Sefa was still watching. "There's a marginal note."

"Read it."

I read it. My voice came out smaller than I intended. "*Custodial transfer approved. Subject remanded to M. Winterbourne, who is aware of terms and accepts supervision obligations.*"

Maren.

Maren Winterbourne, who fed me stew and called me "that skinny scribble" and never once let Ansel out of her sight longer than a market morning. Maren had known. Had *signed*. Had taken a hollow-boned child whose own parents had traded them away like a bad harvest, and known exactly what she was raising, and said nothing for twenty years.

Or—said everything, in the only language available. Burned her charms when the soldiers came. Stood between Ansel and the bone-testing with her old body and her fierce, terrible love. Given up the only home she had so Ansel could run.

I put the ledger down. My eyes were wet, which was inconvenient, because scribes are supposed to be instruments, and instruments don't cry over entries.

"Well," said Sefa into the quiet. "Now comes the hard part."

"Telling them?"

"Deciding whether to tell them." She poked the emberwood. It flared. "You could burn that page. You're good with fire now, I hear."

"That was one time, and the throne room was already on fire."

"Mm." She looked at me with those banked-coal eyes. "My brother would have liked you. He also burned things he shouldn't have."

I hadn't known Sefa had a brother. I hadn't asked, either, because asking people questions is how conversations start, and conversations are how people discover you're a coward wearing a clerk's coat.

"He wasn't warden-blood," she said, before I could decide whether to pretend I hadn't heard. "He was just a boy who lived near the rookery. The spire-mages tested every settlement within fifty miles after the last dragon-fire started failing. He came back from the testing with a mark on his wrist and a smile on his face, because they told him he'd been *chosen*. Chosen for the honor of service beneath the mountain." Her jaw worked. "He was fourteen. His name was Dain. There's a ledger entry for him too. Price: one warden's oath, sworn and kept, thirty years. That was me. I kept the fire alive and they took my brother's soul as my salary."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing adequate. I am a professional with words and I found none.

"So yes," Sefa said. "Tell them or don't. But know that whatever you choose, you'll carry it. That's the family business, carrying things. Everyone in this miserable company is carrying somebody."

* * *

Ansel came back at dusk, snow in their pale hair, cheeks pink with cold, wearing the expression of someone who'd had a long conversation and enjoyed it. Which, given that the other parties were several hundred dead souls, was somehow worse than if they'd looked haunted.

"Good news," they said, stamping snow off their boots. "The dead here don't hate us. Most of them want to help. One of them remembers a cellar under the chapel that connects to the old mine workings, and—" they stopped, tilting their head at me the way they did when they were reading something other than a page. "Tobin. What did you find."

"It can wait until morning—"

"No," they said, gently, terribly. "It can't. You've got your guilt-face on. You've had it since I walked in. You wear it like Maren wears her shawl."

And that—that—was the moment I understood I was going to tell them. Not because courage finally won. Because Ansel said Maren's name and something in my chest tore along a seam I'd been pretending wasn't there.

I set the ledger on the table between us, open to the page. I turned it so they could read. I watched them read. I have watched a lot of people read my work; I have never watched someone read their own name written as a transaction.

"The two struck-through names," I said quietly. "Those are your birth parents. They sold your soul to buy the valley's protection. And this margin note—" my throat closed, opened again. "This one says Maren knew. Accepted custody. Signed for you."

Ansel was very still. Stillness, I had learned, was what they did instead of screaming. The dead in the walls went silent too, as though even they were holding their breath.

"They gave me away," Ansel said at last, "for a *rule*."

"For safety. For everyone."

"And Maren—" Their voice cracked down the middle. "She tucked me in every night knowing what I was. Knowing what was missing. Knowing someday the crown might come, and she—" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "Twenty years. She lied to me for twenty years to keep me alive, and I don't know whether to forgive her or—"

"Yes, you do," I said.

They lowered their hands and looked at me.

"You already know," I said. "You've known since the gate, since she burned her own life's work to buy you ten minutes' head start. Love doesn't stop being true because it kept a secret. Believe me. I'm an expert in secrets that were supposed to be kindnesses." I pulled the ledger back toward myself, and then, before I could lose my nerve, I heard myself say: "Dain."

Ansel blinked. "What?"

"My brother's name was Dain. He was harvested when he was fourteen. I found his entry three years ago and I ran. I didn't tell anyone. I didn't try to save anyone. I copied ledgers and I hid and I told myself knowledge was enough, that someday I'd be brave, that someday—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I stole these books to prove I wasn't a coward anymore. Turns out you can steal a thing and still be exactly what you were."

Ansel reached across the table and put their carved, ink-stained hand over mine.

"Then stop being it tonight," they said. "Read me the rest. All of it. Every name. If we're going down into that mountain, I want to carry them all—not just mine."

So I did. I read until the candle guttered: names and prices, souls and salaries, a whole economy of the stolen. Ansel listened the way they listened to the dead—completely, without flinching—and somewhere past midnight, Sefa leaned over and marked one final entry in the back pages, in her rough warden's hand: *Dain. Brother. Kept.*

Then Ansel stood, went to the window, and went very still in the way that meant listening.

"Tobin," they said slowly. "The dead in the chapel cellar want to show us something. They say there's a door under the mine that the court sealed from *their* side."

Outside, far below us, deep in the mountain's roots, something answered—a sound like a great bell tolling underwater, like fire remembering how to roar, like the last dragon-heart stuttering one beat closer to dark.

"They say," Ansel whispered, "we need to hurry."
