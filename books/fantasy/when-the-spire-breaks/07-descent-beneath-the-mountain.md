## 7. Descent Beneath the Mountain

The dead of the village came to me at dusk, and they did not scream.

That was the first mercy. I had braced for it—the way the traveler's charm had shrieked, the way the sold souls on the White Road had wailed through my teeth until I thought my jaw would crack. But these dead stood in their ruined doorways with their hands folded, and when I passed, they inclined their heads like neighbors at a funeral. *Down*, they said, in a voice like water finding cracks. *Down and left. Follow the water where it forgets to run.*

"They want us in the mine," Sefa said. She stood at the edge of the village square, arms crossed against the cold, watching a dead woman point—patiently, over and over—at a seam in the mountainside where black water wept from the rock. "I don't like it. Dead things that are polite are worse than dead things that scream."

"You'd prefer rude ghosts?"

"I'd prefer no ghosts." She spat into the frost. "But yes. Rude ones, if I must choose."

Tobin crouched by the mine mouth with one of the ledgers open on their knee, cross-referencing something by the light of our last candle stub. "The drowned adit," they said. "Entry seven. The court's original surveyors sealed it after the third collapse because it flooded every spring. They never re-sealed it properly—they just stacked rubble and moved on. Bureaucratic laziness." A pause. "Gods bless bureaucratic laziness."

"And the water?" I asked.

"Rises to your chest in two places. Then it drains. The ledgers say so." Tobin closed the book with a small, decisive snap, as if closing it made it true.

Sefa looked at them. Then at me. Then at the mountain, which loomed over the village like a judge over a dock. Somewhere inside it, I could feel it even without touching bone—a pressure, a hum, the way you feel a storm before you see it. My own soul sat somewhere in there. I knew it the way you know a tooth is rotten: not by pain, exactly, but by the absence where the ache should be.

"Right," Sefa said. "Chest-deep water, collapsing tunnels, a court of soul-thieves, and my fire is down to what would barely light a birthday candle. If anyone wants to walk back down the White Road and take their chances with the spire-mages, now's the time."

Nobody moved.

"Wonderful," she said. "Loyalty. It'll be the death of us."

* * *

The water was worse than chest-deep.

It was also singing. Not the dead-singing I knew—that thin, desperate threading through ivory—but something older, slower, a note held so long it had forgotten what it was waiting for. The mountain itself, maybe. Or the seal beneath it. I put my hand against the tunnel wall as we waded, and the stone hummed up my arm like a plucked string that wouldn't stop.

"Feel that?" Sefa asked quietly. She had gone ahead, one hand trailing the wall, her other cupped around the ember she carried in an iron lantern—the last of her dragon-fire, small enough now to fit in a palm, burning with the color of a coal three days dead. Red going gray at the edges.

"The whole mountain's holding its breath."

"It's been holding it for four hundred years." Her voice did something strange then—not bitter, not quite grief, but the place where those two live next door to each other. "Dragons used to sing this mountain awake. Now there's one fire left, and it's dying because—" She stopped herself.

"Because of me."

"Because of what was done to you," she corrected, sharp. "There's a difference. Learn it."

We waded on. Behind me, Tobin recited the ledger entries under their breath like a prayer—*entry nine, entry ten, support beam rotted, do not trust support beams*—and I understood it wasn't for navigation anymore. It was how Tobin prayed. With paper and numbers and other people's handwriting. When the second pool rose to my collarbone and the dark pressed in close enough to taste, I found I was grateful for it. A coward's voice, steady in the black, is still a steady voice.

The dead walked beside us the whole way. Not bodies—I never saw them, not truly—but a kind of pressure at my shoulder, a crowd moving when the water didn't ripple. They counted us through the deep places. At the worst stretch, where I went under entirely and the world became cold roar and nothing, hands I couldn't see caught my elbows and pushed me up and forward, and when I broke the surface gasping, the polite dead had carried me twenty feet past where my feet could have found bottom.

They wanted me to reach it. That frightened me more than the water did.

Dead things that are polite are worse than dead things that scream.

* * *

The mine ended at a wall of worked stone so old it had stopped pretending to be anything but cave. And beyond that—

Beyond that, the mountain opened like a throat.

I have no honest way to describe the size of it. The valley taught me smallness: low rooms, low ceilings, a life sized to fit through one pass. This was the opposite. A cavern tall as weather, ribbed with columns of black stone that might have grown or might have been carved, and filling it all, filling every gap between the pillars, were the souls.

Thousands. Tens of thousands.

They hung in the air like lanterns in a festival I had no word for—pale motes, each one the size of a fist, drifting in slow currents, and the light they gave was the only light. It fell on Sefa's face and turned her gray. It fell on Tobin, who had gone very still, the way people go still in churches. It fell on me, and I felt every single mote turn toward me at once, though none of them had eyes.

"Oh," Tobin whispered. "Oh, the ledgers didn't—there's no column large enough—"

"That's the harvest," Sefa said flatly. "Four hundred years of it. Every hollow-boned child sold south." Her hand found my shoulder. "Don't look for yours yet."

I looked anyway. Of course I looked.

And there it was.

Not at the center, exactly—the center belonged to the throne—but above it, suspended in a cage of black iron filigree fine as lace, turning slowly like something asleep. My soul. I recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting, your own laugh heard back on a bad day. It was smaller than I'd imagined. Dimmer. Four generations of burning will do that, I suppose. It pulsed once, and far above us, faint through miles of stone, something answered—a great slow exhalation, the dragon-fire's dying rhythm keeping time with my stolen heart.

Every pulse kept the seal. Every pulse fed the fire. Every pulse was me, being spent.

"Well," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "It's ugly work, seeing yourself used as lamp oil."

"Don't joke," Sefa said.

"Joking is the only tool I brought."

But she was right to stop me, because the moment the words left my mouth, the cavern changed. The motes nearest the throne drew inward, gathering, and the currents of pale light began to organize themselves—ranks, columns, order—and out of that order stepped figures. Spire-mages. Six of them, then eight, their robes stitched with sigils that drank the soul-light rather than reflecting it, and behind them, descending a stair of black stone that I swear had not existed a breath before, came the steward's presence like a change in pressure.

"Ansel Eriksen," said the foremost mage, and my name in their mouth sounded like an invoice. "You've saved us the trouble of a second hunt."

Sefa moved before any of us decided anything. That was Sefa—decisions lived in her hands, not her head. She thrust the iron lantern into my arms, and the weight of it nearly buckled my knees, all that dying fire suddenly mine to carry, and she said, fast and low:

"When I break, you run. Down the left gallery—Tobin knows the way, it's in the ledgers—you get to the cage, and whatever happens, whatever you hear—" her voice cracked, just once, "—you don't look back. Promise me."

"Sefa—"

"*Promise.*"

I promised. I have hated myself for promising ever since, and I will hate myself again tomorrow, and I would promise again besides.

She turned to face the mages and opened her hands, and from her empty palms rose the last ember of the last dragon-fire—not the gray-edged coal in my lantern but something deeper in her, some reserve I hadn't known she carried, a decade of her life spent in a single breath. The light of it turned the whole cavern gold. The mages flinched. The souls moaned, a sound like wind through a ship's rigging.

"For the Wardens," Sefa said, "and for every hollow-boned child," and she *burned*.

What followed, I saw mostly from the ground, because Tobin tackled me behind a pillar and held me there with the full desperate strength of a person who has decided that cowardice ends today. Fire filled the gallery in a tide. Mages screamed. Stone ran like wax. And through it all, Sefa stood at the center with her arms spread wide, growing brighter and thinner, a woman becoming a wick—

—and the steward's voice came down the stair like a lid closing on a pot:

"Enough."

The fire stopped. Not died—*stopped*, caught mid-air, frozen in golden rags. Two mages limped from the smoke and took Sefa by the arms, and she did not struggle, because there was nothing left in her to struggle with. As they dragged her toward the throne, she twisted her head once, found me in the shadows behind the pillar, and mouthed three words.

Run. Cage. Now.

Then the dark swallowed her, and Tobin's hand closed around my wrist, and the last thing I saw of her was the lantern-light trembling in my own shaking grip—the last fire in the world, entrusted to a bone-carver who had never burned anything in her life except bridges.

The left gallery yawned ahead of us. Somewhere past it, the cage of black lace turned slowly with my soul inside it, pulsing, spending, keeping the seal alive.

Tobin looked at me. I looked at Tobin.

"Together," they said.

Together, we ran.
