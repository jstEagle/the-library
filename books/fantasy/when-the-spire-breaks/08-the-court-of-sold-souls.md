## 8. The Court of Sold Souls

The dead do not kneel. I learned that within the first hundred steps of the throne hall.

They hung in the air like smoke that had forgotten which way the wind went, hundreds of them, thousands—souls harvested from hollow-boned children whose names Tobin could recite by ledger-line if I let them. Some glowed faintly, the color of old ivory. Some were barely there at all, suggestions of light, a rumor of a person. None of them bowed to the steward's dais. Why would they? They'd been kneeling inside someone else's bargain for generations. They were done with it.

I was not done with mine. Mine was still ahead of me, sitting on a throne of stacked ledgers and fused bone at the heart of the court beneath the mountain, wearing my face when it chose to, singing my song when the mages wound it up.

"Ansel Eriksen," said the steward.

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which is the sort of trick you expect from ancient things and are still annoyed by. The steward itself—I couldn't tell you what it was, only that it had too many joints in all the right places—unfolded from beside the throne like a letter no one wanted to read.

"You know my name," I said. My voice echoed wrong down here. In the valley, sound behaved. Down here it pooled and lingered, like everything else.

"I have known your name longer than you have known yourself." The steward drifted closer. Up close it smelled of cold candlewax and the inside of a locked drawer. "That is not a boast. It is arithmetic. Your soul was entered into our keeping ninety-one years before your birth. Contracts bind bloodlines here, carver. You were sold before you were made."

Ninety-one years. Somewhere behind me, Tobin made a small noise—the noise of a scribe hearing a number they'd already read and never truly believed.

"So the ledgers were right," Tobin said. "Generation to generation."

"The ledgers are always right," the steward said, with something almost like fondness. "It is people who are wrong about what they mean."

Sefa wasn't there to hear it. That was the price of the drowned mine, of the dead village guiding us through, of every step we'd stolen deeper into this place: Sefa had walked backward into the mages' net so we could walk forward, and her last ember was now burning in someone else's lantern. Every few minutes I caught myself turning around, as if she might simply be lagging behind, arms crossed, muttering about fools and fens. She wasn't. The space where she should have been had its own weight, and I carried it.

"Show them," the steward said—not to me, to the hall.

And the hall showed me.

The souls rose, or stirred, or leaned—there is no good word for how the dead move when they want something badly enough to use a living person's eyes. The nearest one, an old woman with light pooling in the folds of her throat, pressed close enough that I felt winter on my teeth.

*Take it back,* she said, in the voice I knew better than any living voice, the voice that lived in whalebone and grave-goods and every charm I'd ever carved. *Take your soul and walk out, child. Let this rotten mountain crack open and spill them all into the sun.*

"Yes," said another, ragged, young. "Let it break. Let it break, let it—"

"No." A third voice, deeper, worn smooth like river stone. "No, hold, hold—if the seal fails, what's kept below wakes. You don't know what's kept below. We do. We've held it with our stolen years. Hold, little one. Hold."

*Freedom,* hissed the old woman.

*Sealing,* said the deep voice. *The world above has children in it.*

*We ARE children, we were taken as children—*

They began to argue then, the whole hall of them, a tide-sound of begging, and I stood in the middle of it with my hands—my carver's hands, steady for charms, steady for funerals, steady for everything except this—hanging useless at my sides.

"There it is," the steward said, and its voice had gone gentle, which was worse than anything. "Every hollow-boned who reaches this hall hears the same arithmetic. Shall I spare you the suspense? There is no answer that comes out clean."

"Try me," I said.

The steward folded itself onto the lowest step of the dais, patient as erosion. "Reclaim your soul, and the seal starves. It has been fed by harvested souls for four centuries; yours alone has kept it alive these nineteen years—you felt that truth in the fire, didn't you? The dragon-flame gutters because the court draws harder on what remains. Reclaim yourself, and the seal breaks within a season. Perhaps within a day. And what sits beneath the seal—" it paused, and for one moment the too-many-jointed thing looked tired, anciently, honestly tired, "—you do not want to meet what sits beneath the seal, carver. I did not build these walls out of spite."

"And if I leave my soul where it is?"

"Then you walk out of here hollow, as you have always been, and you live a long life with a quiet chest and no voices in the bone, and every few decades the mages ride out and find another child like you, and another, and the harvest continues until the ledgers run out of bloodlines or the fire runs out of soul. Whichever comes first." The steward tilted its head. "One of you against the world's safety. Or the world's safety against all of you, forever. Choose your sum."

I have done sums before. Maren taught me on market days—grain against coin, coin against salt, always with a thumb on the scale for widows. Maren, who knew. Who had burned her own charms on the valley floor to buy me ten minutes' head start, knowing full well what I was, what I was sold as, and had never once in nineteen years let it change the way she handed me bread.

*Take it,* the old woman begged.

*Hold,* the deep voice answered.

*Break. Hold. Break. Hold.* The hall became a sea with two currents, and I was driftwood between them.

Here is what nobody tells you about being hollow-boned: you get very good at noticing absences. I had spent my whole life listening to the dead through their leavings, and I knew grief the way a carpenter knows grain—by touch, by flaw, by where it would split. So I noticed, standing in that hall, that the argument had a shape. The ones who begged for freedom were the newly taken. The ones who begged for the seal were the old ones, the ones who had spent so long holding the weight that they couldn't imagine setting it down without the world crushing whoever stood beneath it.

Neither side was wrong. That was the cruelty of it. The steward hadn't built a trap. It had built a balance, and balances don't care about fairness.

"You're waiting for something," I said.

The steward regarded me. "Oh?"

"You've had ninety-one years to prepare for my arrival. You're not stopping me. You're not hurrying me. You gave me the whole speech, both sides, clean arithmetic." I turned to face it fully. "You're waiting for me to choose the way you want. Which means there's a choice you haven't mentioned."

For the first time, the steward was silent. Above us, the throne of ledgers hummed, and at its heart something sang—a thin, sweet, familiar line of melody that I had heard once on a white mountain road, sung back at me by captured dead to pin me to the ground. My soul-song. Nineteen years old and still learning the words.

"You are cleverer than the last one," the steward said at last.

"The last one?"

"The hollow-boned before you. Two hundred years ago. She also heard the currents. She also asked about unspoken choices." Its joints rearranged, a slow settling. "There is a third door, carver. There always is. But it is not a door anyone walks through twice, and it is not a door I can open for you. It must be opened from inside the choice itself."

"That's a very elaborate way of saying nothing."

"It is a very honest way of saying everything." The steward rose, unfolding, unfolding, taller than seemed possible for anything that had just been sitting. "Reclaim, and doom the world above. Refuse, and doom your kind forever. Or—" it extended one long jointed finger toward the humming throne, toward the small bright knot of light at its heart that wore my face when it slept, "—unmake the sum entirely."

The hall went quiet. Even the dead stopped begging.

Somewhere far above us, muffled through four hundred feet of mountain, came a sound I knew in my bones before my brain caught up: a roar, cracked and furious and unmistakably alive.

Dragon-fire.

Sefa.

She was awake, and she was angry, and whatever she was doing up there, the whole ceiling shivered with it—and the throne of ledgers flickered, and my soul-song wavered like a candle in a draft, and every dead soul in the hall turned its face upward at once, waiting to see whether the mountain would hold.

The steward looked at me, and if a thing made of too many joints can smile, it smiled.

"Decide quickly, carver," it said. "Your friend is spending herself faster than either of us planned."

* * *

I crossed the hall alone.

Tobin tried to come—of course they did, tugging at my sleeve with ink-stained fingers, whispering that we should retreat, regroup, find Sefa, find any exit that wasn't this one. And part of me, a large part, wanted nothing more than to be talked out of it. But I looked at them—that frightened, brave, ledger-carrying coward who had chosen to run toward the thing they'd only ever read about—and I saw my brother's face in it, the brother they'd lost to the harvest, the reason they were here.

"You keep the names," I told them, pressing the stolen ledger page into their hands. "Every name in this hall. If I fail, someone above the mountain should know who they were."

"Ansel—"

"You're the scribe," I said. "Make it true."

The throne was smaller up close than it had any right to be. Stacked ledgers, fused bone, and at the center, cupped like a coal in a hearth, a light the color of the first charm I ever carved. My soul. Ninety-one years of contract and nineteen years of theft, and it was the size of a walnut, and it was singing.

I put my hands around it the way I put my hands around every piece of ivory I had ever worked.

And the dead began, very softly, to sing along.
