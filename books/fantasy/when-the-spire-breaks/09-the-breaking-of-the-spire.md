## 9. The Breaking of the Spire

*Tobin*

I had stolen many things in my life at court—candles, ink, an hour here and there, a kiss from the under-steward's daughter—but I had never stolen a person before. It turns out to be much like stealing anything else: you wait until the guard is bored, you lie badly but confidently, and you pray to whatever gods bother listening to scribes.

The gods were not listening. But the dead were.

That was the thing about the court beneath the mountain. Every corridor hummed with the harvested—souls stitched into the walls like embroidery, their light leaking through the mortar seams in pale threads. And every one of them knew my name now. Ansel's soul-song had been sung back at them on the White Road; the mages had catalogued me as an accomplice, a thief, a runner. Somewhere in the ledger-halls there was a page with my handwriting on it, describing me in my own words as *a minor functionary of no consequence.*

I had never been so flattered in my life.

Sefa hung from iron hooks in the antechamber wall—not by her flesh, thank all mercies, but by her coat, though the iron burned her all the same. Dragon-wardens are woven through with fire the way I am woven through with guilt, and cold iron hates both. Her skin smoked faintly where it touched. She was humming. Actually humming, low in her chest, some wardens' work-song, while two mages argued over the proper form for her transfer writ.

"—the steward requires the ember registered before extraction," the tall one was insisting.

"The steward requires nothing," snapped the short one. "The steward is a chair with opinions."

I slipped out of the shadow of the soul-lit wall, holding the one thing I had kept close through fen and mountain and ambush and despair: the last live coal of the last dragon-fire, carried in a lantern of whalebone carved by Ansel Eriksen's own hands. Bone that heard the dead. Bone, therefore, that iron could not silence.

"Excuse me," I said, in my very best clerical voice, the one that has ended marriages and begun wars. "Form Nine-Sixteen requires two countersignatures for extraction. You have neither."

The tall mage turned. The short mage turned. Sefa stopped humming.

And behind them, the wall *screamed*.

Every soul stitched into that stretch of stone let go at once—not free, not yet, but reaching, straining against their stitching toward the bone lantern in my hands. The mages spun, robes flaring, and began the counter-song, the ugly one that folds the dead flat again. Which meant neither of them was watching me walk straight up to Sefa, set my shoulder against the iron frame, and pull her down with a sound like a ship tearing loose from harbor.

"Clerk," she rasped, as we hit the floor together, "you are the worst rescuer I have ever had."

"I'm aware. Hold this." I pressed the lantern into her scorched hands. "And don't drop it unless I say the word."

"What word?"

I was already running at the ledger-throne.

It stood at the chamber's heart, if you could call it standing—a mountain of ledgers, ten generations of harvest bound in vellum that was not vellum, each page a name, each name a light, stacked and mortared into something between a desk and an altar. The steward sat above it on its throne of stitched souls, and past the steward, past the kneeling mages, I could see Ansel.

They stood before their own soul.

I want to tell you what that looked like. I am a scribe; words are supposed to be my trade. But there are things the language simply declines to do. Ansel's soul hung in the air like a lamp made of every morning they'd ever slept through—warm, ordinary, *theirs*—and around it the whole court bent like wheat, because everything in that place, living and dead, drew breath from it. From them. They had spent their whole life hollow and never known they were the hearth at the center of the world.

Ansel raised their hands.

"No," I whispered. Because I knew—I had read the ledgers, I knew the arithmetic—if they reclaimed it, the seal broke, the fire died, the hunt went on forever somewhere else with someone else's name in my columns. And if they left it, they walked out of here hollow until they died, and so did every child born after them with bone that sang.

But Ansel didn't reach for it.

Ansel reached *through* it.

"Sefa," I said into the lantern. "Now."

* * *

*Sefa*

Fire is a promise, my grandmother told me. Not a weapon. A promise—that warmth will come again, that the dark is temporary, that someone will still be awake when you wake.

I had failed that promise once. Warden of Embers, disgraced, demoted, exiled to fens full of frogs and honest weather. Ten years I had carried the last coal of the last dragon and told myself I was keeping it safe, when the truth was I was too frightened to spend it. Fire unspent cannot fail. Fire unspent cannot be your fault.

Tobin said *now*, in the voice of a man who has decided to be brave and is terrified about it, and I understood him completely.

I opened the whalebone lantern.

The ember came out like a bird released—not falling, *flying*—and I sang to it the way I should have sung years ago, the full song, the true one, the one with the grief in it. It answered. Of course it answered. It had only ever been waiting for permission.

It struck the ledger-throne at the exact moment Ansel's hands closed on their own soul—not to take, but to break—and I will swear on ash and bone until I die that these two acts were one act. The fire ate the ledgers. The ledgers were the names. The names were the chains. And Ansel's shattering soul went through the court like dawn through a cellar, and every harvested dead in that black place came loose at once.

You have not heard anything until you have heard ten thousand souls exhale.

The mages fell first—their songs need the ledgers the way I need the ember, and the ember was busy being magnificent. Then the throne, then the walls, then the seams of the mountain itself, the whole rotten court folding inward on the hole where its heart had been. The steward made a sound I refuse to describe even now. Something old. Something that had forgotten it was ever anything but a chair with opinions.

I grabbed Tobin by the collar—he was staring upward, weeping without shame, watching the freed dead rise through cracking stone—and hauled him toward the drowned mine.

"Ansel!" he shouted. "Ansel, *run!*"

* * *

*Tobin*

The mountain came apart around us the way a held breath comes apart—slowly, then all at once, then slowly again, which I maintain is not how physics works but is exactly how it felt.

And Ansel walked toward us out of the ruin. Slowly. Barefoot. Hollow.

I have seen people survive things they shouldn't. I watched Sefa carry dragon-fire in her bare hands. But I had never seen someone walk away from their own soul, deliberately, on purpose, and keep walking. Their face—

Their face was the face of someone setting down a weight they had carried so long they'd mistaken it for a spine. Grief and relief in the same expression, fighting for space, both winning.

"It's quiet," Ansel said, when they reached us. Their voice sounded like the valley they came from. "In here." They touched their sternum. "It's just *me*. Just—" a wet, astonished laugh— "*just Ansel.*"

Sefa put her hand on their head like a benediction, or a ruffle, hard to say with her. "Hollow-boned," she said. "Whole-hearted. Come on. Stairs."

"There are no stairs," I pointed out. "There's a collapsing mine."

"Then swim, clerk."

We swam.

Behind us the mountain settled, groaning, into its own foundations, and the seal that had held for ten generations dissolved not with a roar but with a sigh—an exhale that moved through the rock and the water and the three of us, taking the harvest, the hunt, the decree, the ledgers, all the terrible machinery of it, out with the tide.

Above, through the mine's shattered mouth, I saw them going up. All of them. Ten thousand points of light climbing the night sky like a river deciding to flow the wrong direction, and every single one of them free.

Somewhere among those lights, I am certain, was a boy who liked to copy out maps wrong on purpose. My brother. I did not wave. I am a coward about some things still. But I watched until the last of them was gone, and I thought: *I will write this down. Every name. Even his. Especially his.*

Especially mine.

* * *

We came out of the mountainside at dawn, filthy, half-drowned, alive, into a green valley that smelled like rain and woodsmoke and absolutely nothing else—which is to say, home.

Ansel stopped at the ridge where their whole catastrophe had begun, and stood very still, listening to the quiet the rest of us had always taken for granted.

"Well?" Sefa asked.

"It's beautiful," Ansel said. And then, softer, with a smile I will carry to my own grave: "It's *empty*. And it's beautiful. And so am I."

Below us, at the valley's gate, a small figure was burning something in a brazier—old charms, by the smoke—and looking up the road like a woman who had been waiting a long time and would deny it to her dying day.

"That," Ansel said, "is my grandmother."

"That," said Sefa, "is going to be an interesting conversation."

"She knows," Ansel said. "Doesn't she. She's always known."

Neither Sefa nor I said anything, which was answer enough.

Ansel took a breath—just air, ordinary air, no voices in it—and started down the hill toward the smoke and the small waiting figure, and we followed, because that was the whole point of everything: that someone would follow.
