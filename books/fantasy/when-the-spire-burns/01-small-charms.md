## 1. Small Charms

The night market of Vellamark opened the way a wound opens: slowly, reluctantly, and only after dark.

Paloma Sterling had learned its rhythms over three years of selling there, three years of watching the lantern-sellers make their rounds along Gull Street while the sun still bled orange behind the temple spires, waiting for the moment when the last official light died and the unofficial city could begin. The market did not exist on any map. It existed in the space between the lamplighter's rounds and the watchman's supper, in the hour when the harbor fog came up the hill and made honest faces hard to recognize. By day, Gull Street was a gutter running down toward the fish-works. By night, it became something older—a riverbed where all the small forbidden things of Vellamark pooled and glittered.

She had her table set up by the time the first lanterns caught, wedged between a woman who sold charms that were only carved bone and a man whose entire inventory was stolen prayer candles, their wax stamped with a temple seal he'd never earned and didn't pretend to respect. Paloma's own wares occupied the narrow territory between those two extremes: real enough to work, small enough to deny.

Sleeping knots braided from harbor-grass, so a child's nightmares would tangle in them instead of arriving whole. A sachet of dried feverfew and something else—something she added with a touch and a whispered syllable when no one was looking—that would hold a fever at bay for two nights, maybe three, long enough for a body to decide what it wanted to do. Love-charms she refused outright, though the requests came nightly, because she had seen what happened to people who tried to bend another heart, and it was never anything she wanted to be part of. Luck-knots, though. Luck-knots she sold freely, and told herself they were only knots.

That was the word she used in her head, too. *Only.* Only grass and thread and a little warmth from her fingertips, warmth that any seller might have from handling her goods all evening beside a brazier. Only a murmured word in a language that wasn't quite a language anymore, more a habit of sound than speech. The temple's catechism said magic was theft—theft from the crown, theft from the natural order, theft from God's appointed order of things—and that the gifted were thieves in their own blood. But Paloma had never been able to make her gift feel like theft. It felt like leaning close to something and breathing warmth onto it. It felt like *tending.*

She kept her hands wrapped anyway. Fingerless gloves of undyed wool, the kind every dockworker and net-mender in Vellamark wore against the chill, so common as to be invisible. When a customer took too long choosing a knot, she tucked her hands into her sleeves and let them choose alone. Three years of practice. Three years of keeping the warmth banked like a fire in winter, fed just enough not to go out, never enough to show smoke.

The debt was why she couldn't stop coming here, and also why she couldn't afford to be caught. Both facts sat together in her chest every night like two stones grinding.

*Four hundred and eleven marks,* the temple ledger said, in Mother Sefu's careful hand, updated quarterly with interest that compounded the way rot compounds: quietly, then all at once. Four hundred and eleven marks for nine years of food and lodging and schooling in the scriptorium, for ink and vellum and the roof that kept the rain off her head since she'd arrived at the temple orphanage at age six with nothing but a name and a fever. A fair sum, the steward said. A fair price for a life. Paloma had copied out enough ledgers in the archive to know that no sum ever got smaller. She earned four marks a week copying psalters; she might clear eight on a good night at the market. Even if she sold nothing but luck-knots until her hands wore down to the bone, the number would outlive her. It was designed to. Everyone at the temple owed it. That was how the temple held its people—not with walls, but with arithmetic.

Tonight she counted her earnings twice before wrapping them in oilcloth and hiding the purse inside her boot, which was a habit now, and once more in her head on the walk home, which was compulsion. Eleven marks. Eleven marks against four hundred and eleven, and next quarter the interest would add—

"Excuse me."

Paloma looked down.

A child stood at the edge of her table's light, holding something out in both cupped hands with the gravity of an ambassador presenting tribute. A girl, maybe seven, wearing a coat that had belonged to someone much larger and boots that had been mended more times than the leather could honestly support. Her hair was cut short and uneven, the kind of haircut a child gives herself or receives from a hurried parent with dull scissors, and her face was smudged along one cheek with ash or dirt or the general residue of being poor in a harbor city.

In her palms lay a button. Brass, tarnished green at the rim, missing its shank so it was less a button now than a coin-shaped piece of nothing.

"It fell off my da's coat," the girl said. "Before." Before, the way children said it, meant before whatever had happened that emptied a coat of its owner, or emptied an owner of a coat. Paloma had heard *before* often enough at this table to know better than to ask.

"What would you like it to do?" Paloma asked. It was the question she asked everyone, the question that let her sell without ever naming what she sold. People answered with the things they actually needed, which were almost never what they'd come to buy.

The girl considered this with terrible seriousness. "I want to find things," she said. "Ma says I lose everything. My other mitten. The bread knife. I lost the bread knife and Ma cried."

Something in Paloma's chest pulled tight, the way a knot pulls when you've tied it wrong and have to start again.

"A finding-charm is more than a button," she said gently. "My charms take proper payment."

The girl's chin lifted. Whatever softness lived in her face rearranged itself into stubbornness, and Paloma recognized it immediately, intimately, the way you recognize your own handwriting. It was the look of someone who had nothing and refused to let that be the end of the negotiation.

"I'm paying," the girl said. "It's a good button."

Paloma looked at the button. Brass gone green, shank snapped off, worthless by any measure the temple ledger would recognize. And she looked at the girl, who was watching her with the fierce attention of someone braced to be told, once again, that what she had was not enough.

The catechism had a verse about this. *Do not traffic in the unclean gift; the thief who steals for charity steals still.* Paloma had copied that verse forty times in her novice year, in beautiful hand, under a mother superior's eye, while the warmth sat quiet in her fingertips and lied to everyone including herself.

She picked up the button.

It was warm from the girl's hands. Everything was always warmer when it came to her, as though her skin remembered what it was for even when her mind was busy denying it. Paloma closed her fingers around the brass disc and let the warmth rise—not much, a thimbleful, the smallest working she knew—and murmured the finding-word into it, low, under the noise of the market, disguised beneath a cough.

The charm settled into the metal like water into dry ground. Anyone watching would have seen a scribe examining a customer's coin and finding it counterfeit. That was the beauty of the night market: everything suspicious looked like ordinary commerce, and all commerce looked slightly criminal anyway.

"There," Paloma said, pressing the button back into the girl's cupped hands and folding the small fingers over it. "Keep it in your pocket, not on a string—strings snag. When you lose something, hold it and think hard about the thing you lost, and walk slow. Don't run. Running scatters the finding."

The girl stared at her hands. Then up at Paloma. "Does it really work?"

"Better than most things," Paloma said. "But it works best if you believe it does, so believe it."

The girl nodded once, sharp, a merchant sealing terms. Then she turned and ran—which Paloma suspected would scatter the finding, but childhood was short and cobblestones were long—and paused three stalls away to look back, and raised one hand, not a wave exactly, more a salute between colleagues.

Paloma found she was smiling. It ached, the way smiling does when the face isn't used to it. She put the button-payment in the dish where her coins went, because refusing it would have been worse than taking it, and because somewhere in the arithmetic of her life she needed there to be a record that a child had paid her true and full price for something that worked.

* * *

The market thickened around her as the fog did. By mid-evening the street was a single moving mass of hunched shoulders and lantern-glow, and Paloma sold steadily in the way she thought of as *honest*: sleeping knots for mothers with wakeful babies, a fever sachet for a fisherman whose daughter burned, two luck-knots for a young couple opening a pie stall near Tanner's Row and needing every advantage the universe could be persuaded to offer.

None of them saw her work. None of them needed to. The woman buying the feverfew watched Paloma's wrapped hands with the flat knowing gaze of someone who had bought from her before and chosen, deliberately, not to know what she was buying. That was the covenant of the night market, unwritten but absolute: *I will not see you if you do not make me see you.* Everyone in Gull Street was guilty of something, and the guilt was the glue.

It was near midnight when the mood of the crowd changed.

Paloma felt it before she understood it—the way a flock of birds feels weather, all at once, without discussion. The noise of the market dropped a register. Conversations folded themselves shut. Along the length of Gull Street, sellers began covering their goods with cloth, unhurried, casual, a choreography rehearsed by necessity, and buyers drifted toward the mouths of the alleyways with the studied idleness of people who had suddenly remembered urgent appointments elsewhere.

Then she heard it: boots. Many boots, in step, coming up from the harbor end of the street.

Watch patrol, but not the usual pair. This was a column—a dozen men at least, torches ahead of them turning the fog orange, and at their center a figure in the slate-grey coat of the Inquisitor's office, the high collar, the iron chain of office that caught the light like a blade catching light. Behind him walked clerks with portable writing desks slung from their shoulders, and behind *them* came men carrying something long and rectangular that Paloma's mind refused to name for several full seconds before it supplied the word: *a testing frame.* The ward-frame they used at the gates of noble houses. The frame that hummed when a gifted person passed within arm's reach.

The catechism had a chapter on the great hunt, too. Paloma had copied it. *In this generation the crown has decreed the cleansing of the realm of unlicensed craft, that the sin of the Spire be not repeated, nor the memory of it suffered to fade.* She'd written it in gold leaf once, for a presentation copy. Gold leaf, on the order that would kill her.

"Market's closing," someone said unnecessarily, and then the whole street said it at once, in movement rather than words.

Paloma's hands were already moving. Cloth over the knots, cloth over the sachets, the coin-dish swept empty, the purse from her boot into her sleeve, the table folded flat with the ease of three years' repetition. Around her the market was dissolving into the fog like sugar into water, stall by stall, light by light, until the street looked as though nothing had ever been sold there at all—as though the night market were only a rumor the fog told itself.

The column reached the mouth of Gull Street and stopped. The Inquisitor stood very still in his grey coat, and even at forty feet, even through fog, Paloma felt the weight of his attention move across the street like a hand across a shelf of books, touching each spine. She pressed herself into the shadow of the shuttered candle-stall, hands deep in her sleeves, and did the thing she had practiced in secret for years: she made the warmth go still. Not hidden. *Still.* Still as pond-water. Still as a dead thing.

His gaze passed over her and moved on.

The column turned down Fishwife's Lane instead, toward the tenements, and the sound of the boots faded, and Gull Street exhaled around her in a hundred small movements—cloths coming off goods, lanterns rekindling, the market resuming like a heartbeat resuming after a held breath.

Paloma did not resume. She stood in the shadow with her heart slamming against her ribs and thought about the testing frame, and how it had been carried through her market like a coffin being measured, and how the crown had never brought ward-frames into the low quarters before. The hunt had lived, until now, in proclamations nailed to church doors—in paper, in gold leaf, in the safe distance of ink. Tonight it had grown legs.

And tomorrow, she remembered with a cold drop through her stomach, was the first day of the temple's own internal testing. Mother Sefu had announced it at evening prayers with a serenity that had fooled no one: the crown had requested assurance that the temple's own household was clean, and the temple would comply, and all scribes and novices would present themselves to the ward-warden after matins. *Compliance is our shield,* Sefu had said, in the tone she used for things she did not believe.

Paloma gathered her folded table against her chest and joined the thinning stream of sellers flowing home through the fog, and she counted her coins in her head—eleven marks, eleven marks against four hundred and eleven, and tomorrow morning she would stand in front of a humming iron frame with the warmth still living in her fingertips, because there was nowhere to hide a gift that lived in her own skin.

Unless.

There was ice in the well behind the scriptorium kitchens. There was always ice, this time of year, and she had heard—she had never let herself hear it properly until now—that cold could quiet the warmth, could numb the telling-tremor in the hands the frame listened for. It was a rumor passed among the marked the way all dangerous knowledge traveled: sideways, in fragments, never written down.

Eleven marks against four hundred and eleven. A button in her coin-dish, charmed for free. And tomorrow, ice water and a prayer to a God whose church was hunting her.

Paloma pulled her collar up and walked faster, and behind her, far off toward the harbor, a bell began to ring—not the alarm, not yet, only the midnight bell—but it sounded, tonight, like something beginning.
