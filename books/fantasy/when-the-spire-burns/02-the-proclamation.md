## 2. The Proclamation

The criers came at dawn, as criers always did, when the streets were still soft with sleep and the mind had not yet armored itself against bad news.

Paloma heard the first bell from her cell in the scribes' dormitory—a thin, urgent ringing that did not belong to any of the temple's hours. She lay still beneath her blanket for a long moment, listening to it travel down the cloister like a stone skipping across water, and felt something cold begin in the pit of her stomach before she even knew why.

Then came the voice.

*"By decree of the Crown: a great hunt is declared. All persons harboring the forbidden gift shall present themselves for testing. Shelter them and be counted among them."*

She was already sitting up. Her hands were already warm.

It happened sometimes—her body betraying her before her mind caught up. Fear woke the gift the way wind wakes embers banked under ash. She looked down at her fingertips in the grey light and saw the faint shimmer there, like heat rising off summer stone, and pressed her palms flat against the frozen floorboards until the warmth drained away into the wood and left only ache.

*Present yourself,* the crier said again, farther off now, moving toward the fish market. *Be counted.*

Paloma dressed quickly in the dark. Scribe's robes—undyed wool, three sizes too large because the temple issued by height and not by need, sleeves long enough to swallow her hands entirely if she let them. She let them. She had spent six years learning how to let them.

* * *

The courtyard was full by the time she reached it, though the sun had barely cleared the eastern wall of the scriptorium. Two hundred scribes stood in their rows, breath steaming, faces tilted toward the steps of the Chapter House where Mother Sefu stood in her grey-and-gold vestments, hands folded, expression carved from the same stone as the building behind her.

Beside Paloma, Ivet shivered and elbowed her gently. "Did you hear? They tested the gate-wardens first thing. Old Marnick failed."

"Old Marnick can't light a candle without burning his sleeve," Paloma whispered back.

"That's what everyone said about the baker's daughter in Corrowmere," Ivet said, "and they took her anyway. They took the whole street that day, do you remember? Half of Corrowmere's just empty houses now." She pulled her own sleeves down over her wrists—a gesture so familiar Paloma felt a strange vertigo seeing it on someone else's arms. "They say the new Inquisitor doesn't bother with proof anymore. Just the wards. Touch the ward-stone, and the truth comes out whether you want it to or not."

"What truth?" Paloma asked, though she knew. Everyone knew. The wards were cut from Spire-stone, salvaged from the ruin itself, and whatever dragon-fire still lived inside them knew the taste of magic the way a hound knows blood. A gifted hand laid upon a ward-stone would glow. That was all. No argument, no appeal. Light, and then the irons.

"The true one," Ivet said grimly. "That we're all liars."

Mother Sefu began to speak then, and her voice carried across the courtyard with the flat clarity of someone reading a ledger. The crown's decree would be honored. The temple would comply fully. Every scribe, every novice, every servant of the house would submit to testing beginning this morning, hall by hall, oldest rolls first. There would be no exceptions. There would be no shame in passing, and no mercy in failing, and both of these facts were to be understood as expressions of the same divine order.

Her eyes found Paloma's once, briefly, in the middle of it—the way a lighthouse finds a ship—and moved on.

Paloma's mouth went dry.

* * *

They tested the copying hall first.

The ward-stone sat on a table at the head of the room, a chunk of black rock no bigger than a loaf of bread, veined with hairline threads of dull red. It should have looked harmless. It looked like nothing at all—just another piece of the ruined Spire, and Vellamark was full of those, built into walls and doorsteps and fountain basins, the way a body keeps fragments of shrapnel long after the war ends. But the two crown wardens standing on either side of it wore the king's colors, and their faces wore the particular blankness of men who have been given a task and no permission to feel anything about it.

One by one, the scribes filed past. One by one, they pressed their palm to the stone.

Paloma watched the line move and did arithmetic. There were forty-one scribes in the copying hall. The line advanced at roughly thirty seconds each. Which meant she had perhaps fifteen minutes to solve an unsolvable problem: the gift lived in her hands, and the test demanded her hands.

She thought of ice.

The washroom was two doors down, unguarded—why guard a washroom?—and its water pump drew from the deep cistern, which even in high summer ran cold enough to make teeth ache. In late autumn it was brutal. She slipped out during the changeover between halls, when the wardens consulted their rolls and the scribes shuffled and murmured, and she locked herself in a stall and pumped the basin full and plunged her hands in up to the wrists.

The cold hit like a slap. Then it went deeper than a slap—it went into the meat of her fingers, into the small warm channels where the gift pooled, and began to kill it. Not kill it exactly. Nothing killed it. But it drove it down, buried it, made the ember in her skin shrink to something too small and too numb to catch the ward's attention. She hoped.

She held her hands under until they turned white, then blue-white, then a color she didn't have a name for, and kept them under while tears she refused to acknowledge slid down her face and dripped into the basin. She counted. She was good at counting—coins, hours, debts, breaths. She counted to four hundred, and when her hands had stopped being hers, she lifted them out dripping, wrapped them in her sleeves, and walked back to the copying hall with her heart hammering so hard she could hear it in her ears like a second crier.

*"All persons harboring the forbidden gift—"*

Next.

* * *

The stone was colder than she expected.

That was the first thing—the cold of it, biting through the numbness of her fingers as if her hands weren't even there, as if she were pressing her palm against winter itself. And then, beneath the cold, something else. A pulse. Slow and vast and faint, like a heartbeat heard through a wall, like something enormous sleeping very far below.

*Dragon-fire,* she thought. *This little stone has a piece of a dragon in it, and it's still alive, after everything.*

For half a heartbeat—less—she felt the fire notice her. Feel her reaching toward it, or it reaching toward her, some recognition older than law, older than the burning of the Spire—

Nothing. The stone stayed dark. The veins of red did not brighten. The warden on the left glanced at his roll, made a mark, and said, "Next," in a voice like gravel poured from a sack.

Paloma walked back to her desk on legs that did not feel attached to her body and sat down and picked up her quill, because that was what scribes did, that was the whole architecture of her survival: sit, copy, be small, be nothing, pass. Around her the line continued. Palm, stone, dark. Palm, stone, dark. Each dark stone was a small resurrection, and she watched each one with the desperate attention of a woman watching her own execution commute itself person by person.

Ivet was eleventh from the end.

Paloma remembered that later—that she'd counted, that she'd known exactly where Ivet stood in the line, that some part of her had been doing the arithmetic of survival the entire time and had never once considered warning her friend away. There were ways. There were always ways; Paloma knew three of them herself. Fake an illness. Faint. Slip out to the privy and simply not come back until the wardens had gone. Any of them, offered quietly, a whisper passed along a row of desks—Ivet would have taken it, Ivet trusted her—

Palm. Stone.

Light.

It bloomed up from the black rock like sunrise seen through closed eyelids, red-gold and unmistakable, throwing Ivet's shocked face into sharp relief and painting the whole front of the copying hall in the color of the old covenant. For one suspended moment nobody moved. Even the wardens seemed to hesitate, as if the light itself were an accusation they hadn't expected to see confirmed.

Then the room erupted—not into screams, not yet, but into a sound worse than screaming: the collective intake of two hundred breaths drawn at once, the sound of a community understanding simultaneously that it contained a traitor.

"No." Ivet's voice, high and thin. She snatched her hand back from the stone and stared at it, at her own palm, as though it belonged to a stranger. "No, that's wrong, I don't—you've made a mistake, I'm not—"

"Step aside," said the gravel-voiced warden.

"I'm not gifted! I copy scripture, I copy *ledgers*, I can't even—my mother tested clean, my whole family tested clean, you have to retest me, the stone's cracked, look at it, it must be *cracked*—"

Two more wardens came through the door. Paloma watched them cross the room with the terrible unhurried economy of men who had done this many times, and she watched Ivet understand, in stages, what was happening—the way a drowning person understands the shore receding—and something in Paloma's chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there.

Because here was the truth she would carry for the rest of her life, the truth she could not put down or argue with or burn: Ivet's gift was nothing. A flicker. The kind of low, guttering talent that most people lived and died with and never noticed—warm hands in winter, bread that rose luckier than it should, a knack for calming frightened animals. It wasn't even illegal, really, not in any honest version of the world. But the world had stopped being honest a generation ago, and the stone didn't measure degrees. It measured presence. Light was light. The hunt did not sort the guilty from the innocent. It sorted the lit from the unlit.

And Paloma had passed because she had thought of ice, and Ivet had failed because Ivet had never learned to be afraid early enough.

They took her arms. Ivet did not struggle at first—she stood rigid, staring at the wardens' iron grips as though she might wake up—and then she did struggle, sudden and wild, twisting, and a warden struck her across the mouth with the back of his gauntlet, a short efficient blow that sounded like a door slamming, and blood appeared at the corner of her lips and she went quiet in the way that was somehow much worse than screaming.

Until they reached the door. Then she screamed.

"Paloma!" Her voice cracked across the hall, across the rows of bowed heads, across the careful silence of two hundred people pretending to copy scripture while one of their own was dragged into the street. "PALOMA! *Paloma, help me!*"

Not anyone's name. Hers. Out of every soul in that room, Ivet had called hers.

Paloma's hands were under her desk, hidden in her sleeves, frozen and aching and utterly silent, and every instinct she owned told her to keep them there, keep her eyes on her page, keep breathing, keep being small. The quill trembled in her grip and left a blot on the ledger line, a small black bloom spreading through the record of grain shipments, and she stared at it and did not look up.

*Ivet.* She wanted to say it. She wanted to stand up and say *take me instead, mine is bigger, mine will show brighter, take the one who actually deserves it*—and the wanting was real, and the cowardice was also real, and they existed together in the same chest, in the same breath, and neither canceled the other out. That was the discovery. Not that she was a coward. That courage and cowardice could occupy the same body at the same time and the body would simply choose one, without consulting her, and she would have to live inside the choice forever.

The door banged shut. The screaming faded down the cloister, thinner and thinner, and then the courtyard swallowed it, and then Vellamark swallowed the courtyard, and then there was only the scratch of quills and the smell of ink and the slow drip of Ivet's blood drying somewhere on the flagstones by the door.

Nobody spoke. Nobody ever spoke, afterward. That was the temple's true liturgy: not the hymns, but the silence after.

* * *

She found Mother Sefu at evening office, alone in the reliquary antechamber, trimming candles.

Paloma stood in the doorway with her dead-cold hands tucked in her sleeves and said nothing for a long time. The old woman worked with steady fingers, snipping wicks, catching wax drips on a cloth, and did not turn around.

"You numbed your hands," Mother Sefu said at last. Not a question.

"Yes, Mother."

"Marnick's boy tried the same thing last month in Harrowgate. Passed the ward. Hanged himself a week later. Said he couldn't live with knowing what he'd saved and what it cost." She trimmed another wick. The flame steadied, burned taller. "How are yours?"

"They'll thaw."

"Mm." Mother Sefu set down the snippers and finally faced her. In the candlelight her face was ancient and unreadable, a map worn smooth by centuries of pilgrim thumbs. "You'll want to hate yourself tonight. I won't insult you by telling you not to. But hear one thing before you go." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice to almost nothing, and her eyes—dark, quick, terribly awake—held Paloma's with a force that made the cold in her hands feel like nothing at all. "Ivet called your name because she loves you, child. Do not repay that by making her sacrifice mean less than it does. Whatever you're carrying"—and here her gaze dropped, brief and deliberate, to Paloma's hidden hands—"carry it *somewhere*. Don't let it die in a copying hall."

"Mother, I don't—"

"You do." A pause. "And tomorrow the wardens reach the archive halls. And the day after, they reach the sealed reliquary, and the Inquisitor comes with them—a man named Corvane, who does not pass stones and does not make marks on rolls. He looks at people and he knows." She picked up the snippers again, turned back to her candles, and dismissed her with the smallest possible motion of her shoulder. "Whatever you decide, decide it before dawn. Dawn is when the criers come. Dawn is when things end."

Paloma walked back through the darkening cloister alone. Frost was forming on the flagstones. Somewhere beyond the temple wall, in the city, a bell rang the hour, and beneath the bell, faint as memory, she could almost hear the criers rehearsing tomorrow's proclamation—and beneath *that*, deeper than bells, deeper than the city itself, the old dying fire at the root of the broken Spire, guttering like a candle no one had trimmed in twenty years.

Her hands, thawing at last, had begun—very faintly—to glow.

She hid them in her sleeves, and climbed the stairs to her cell, and did not sleep.
