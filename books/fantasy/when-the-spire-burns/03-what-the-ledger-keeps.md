## 3. What the Ledger Keeps

The temple archive smelled of dust and tallow and old lies.

Paloma had thought that last one only in the way anyone thinks such things—abstractly, idly, the way you might notice a crack in a ceiling and think *someone ought to fix that* without ever imagining the whole roof was about to come down on your head. She had worked beneath that roof for eleven years. She knew its creaks the way she knew her own heartbeat: the groan of the third-floor shelves when the river damp swelled them in spring; the whisper of vellum against vellum when a draft found the reading gallery; the particular hush that fell over the ledger room at night, after the last of the day-scribes went home to their suppers and their families and their uncomplicated lives.

That hush was hers now. It belonged to the debt.

She set her candle on the reading desk and pulled the first ledger toward her with hands that still ached faintly from the ice water. Two days since the testing. Two days since Ivet's voice had cracked across the warding hall like a dropped plate—*Paloma, it's me, it's Iv—* before they'd gagged her—and two nights since Paloma had lain awake cataloguing every reason she deserved what was coming for her. She had passed the test by cheating. Ivet had failed by being honest. There was a ledger entry for that, she thought bitterly. Somewhere in Vellamark's great book of accounts there was surely a column for the difference between the lucky and the good, and the sum was never fair.

But grief, she had learned, could be put to work. Grief made excellent fuel. And so instead of weeping—which would come later, or not at all—she had come down into the archive's deepest level, where the debt ledgers lived, to do what she did best.

Find the numbers. Find the flaw. Find the leverage.

Her own account was a masterpiece of slow strangulation. She knew it by heart, but she read it again anyway, tracing the entries with one fingernail: the loan Mother Sefu had co-signed when Paloma was fourteen and her mother's lungs were failing and the physickers' fees came due faster than any scribe's wage could meet them. Eleven years of interest compounding like frost across a windowpane. Payments made, always, just barely. Penalties assessed for lateness measured in hours. Once—a detail that still made her jaw tighten—a penalty for a payment made *early*, on the grounds that it disrupted the quarterly schedule.

*Balance outstanding: 412 marks, 7 silver, 3 copper.*

It would be 412 marks until she died. The number did not shrink anymore; it only rearranged itself. She understood now, holding the ledger in the candlelight, that this was not an accident. This was design. The temple did not want its debts repaid. Repaid scribes could leave.

"Then let's see what else you don't want repaid," she murmured to the empty room, and turned to the older volumes.

* * *

The archive's deep level was organized by a logic that had died before Paloma was born. The recent ledgers—all of them, everything from the Burning forward—were immaculate. Bound in blue calfskin, indexed in copperplate, cross-referenced with a thoroughness that bordered on devotional. But the older material, the pre-Burning record, lived in a corner behind a screen of warped cedar shelving, bound in whatever leather had been at hand, catalogued in at least three different hands and none of them consistent. As if the temple had lost interest in its own past the moment the Spire burned. As if history, for Vellamark, began on the morning the smoke cleared.

Paloma told herself she was looking for accounting errors. Pre-Burning debts that might have been misfiled—transferred, forgiven, absorbed into general funds—that could be dredged up and used. A scribe who found a discrepancy in a four-hundred-year-old ledger could make a very quiet, very effective nuisance of herself. Mother Sefu respected nothing so much as a well-documented nuisance.

She started with the treasury rolls from the reign of King Aldous III—the last king before the Burning. The script was dense, abbreviations crowding each other like passengers on a ferry, but Paloma had spent half her life deciphering worse. Requisitions for wax. Tithes from the river parishes. A suspiciously large allocation for "candle-sconces, gilded, Spire gallery"—the kings had liked their light, even then.

And then, wedged between an entry for altar wine and one for choir robes, a line that stopped her nail mid-word:

*Maintenance of the Ember Vault — 200 marks, standing.*

She frowned. The Ember Vault. She knew the reliquaries of the Grand Temple the way she knew her own pockets: the Reliquary of Saint Osmund's fingerbone, the Reliquary of the Nine Martyrs, the crystal case that held what everyone was assured was a true fragment of the True Flame itself. There was no Ember Vault in the temple's current inventory. She was almost certain. She was—

She wasn't certain. That was the trouble. She reached for the inventory ledgers and found the gap exactly where her memory said it should be: the inventories ran unbroken, year upon year, reliquary by reliquary, and then in the reign of Aldous III there was simply nothing. Not a redaction—redactions left scars, a scraped patch of vellum, a telltale smoothness under the fingertip. Nothing. As if the vault had never existed. As if the scribes of that generation had agreed, all together, all at once, to stop writing one particular word.

Paloma sat back. The candle flame leaned in the draft from somewhere unseen, and the shadows of the shelves swung around her like a slow turning of heads.

She should have stopped there. She knew that even then—some small cold animal part of her knew it precisely, the way you know the exact moment ice will bear your weight and the exact moment it won't. She was a debtor. Debtors did not go digging in sealed histories. Debtors kept their heads down and their fingertips numb and their small warm gift folded away where no warden's ward-stone could taste it.

Ivet had kept her head down too. Right up until the moment she hadn't.

Paloma turned back to the treasury roll and read on.

* * *

The references accumulated the way water accumulates behind a dam—one drop meaningless, a thousand drops a catastrophe.

In the household accounts of Queen Maren, Aldous III's wife: *alms for the keepers of the Ember Codex, feast of the Covenant Renewed.* The Covenant Renewed. Paloma knew every holy day on the temple calendar. She had copied the calendar out three times as an apprentice, in three colors of ink. There was no Feast of the Covenant Renewed.

In a steward's marginal note, undated but clearly late in Aldous's reign: *His Grace asks again whether the terms may be amended. The dragon-speaker says no. His Grace does not like the answer.* Terms. Amended. Dragon-speaker. Each word sat on the page like a coin from a foreign country—recognizable, legible, and utterly out of place.

In a funeral expense record: *burial of the last dragon-speaker, per old arrangement.* The last. Which meant there had been others, a line of them, an office—like the Inquisitors, like the Lord Chancellor—an office that had simply been allowed to die out, unmourned, unrecorded, sometime in the years around the Burning.

And then, in the final treasury roll of Aldous III's reign, in the closing weeks before the date every child in Vellamark learned before they learned their letters, this:

*Payment to Master Corvane Hale, firewright, for services rendered at the Spire. Sum withheld from record by royal command.*

Firewright. Paloma turned the word over. A firewright built fires—or built *against* fires, designed the flame-traps and hearth-shields that wealthy houses paid dearly for. What services did a firewright render at a bridge between worlds?

And below it, in a different ink, added later but still generations old, a single line that had been written by someone with a very fine hand and, Paloma suspected, a very short future:

*The Codex says he gave nothing. Check the Codex.*

Check the Codex.

Paloma became aware, distantly, that her heart was going too fast. She also became aware that her fingertips were warm. Not the ordinary warmth of candle-work and effortful reading—the other warmth, the secret one, rising unbidden through her skin the way heat rises through floorboards above a kitchen. Her gift answering her fear, or feeding it, or simply refusing, as it always refused, to pretend along with her that the world was the shape the temple said it was.

"Stop," she whispered—to herself, to her hands, to the whole spiraling evening. "Stop. You wanted leverage. This is leverage. Take it to Sefu and go to bed."

But her eyes went back to that fine, doomed handwriting. *Check the Codex.*

Because here was the thing—the thing that had been assembling itself in her mind drop by drop while she read, patiently, terribly, the way the interest assembled itself against her name. Every child in Vellamark knew the story of the Burning. Knew it the way they knew rain falls down and bread costs money. The dragons had grown proud. The dragons had demanded more tribute, more memory-offerings, more of everything, until the first king's patience broke and he burned the bridge rather than kneel. The Spire's destruction was a victory. It was sung at festivals. It was carved over the gates of the Inquisitors' hall: *WE BOWED TO NO FLAME.* The covenant, the priests said, had been broken—by the dragons, in their greed—and the burning of the Spire was the crown's righteous answer.

But these pages said *terms*. Said *amended*. Said a king asking, again and again, whether the bargain could be changed, and disliking the answer. Said a man paid to work fire at the Spire in its final days. Said, in a dead scribe's careful hand, that someone *gave nothing*—and that the truth of it lay in a book called the Ember Codex, kept in a vault the temple had erased from its own memory so thoroughly that even Paloma's well-trained mind had swallowed the absence whole.

You could forge a page. You could forge a hundred. But you could not forge a gap. Gaps were honest. Gaps admitted something had been there.

Paloma looked down at the ledger in front of her and saw, for the first time, what it actually was. Not a record. An alibi. Four hundred years of flawless blue calfskin, all of it pointing away from the Burning like a crowd of witnesses all remembering, conveniently, the same innocent thing.

Her world did not tilt. That was the strange part. She had expected—when she imagined, in the way you imagine earthquakes without expecting them—that revelation would feel like falling. It felt instead like standing perfectly still while the floor revealed itself to have been moving all along. The festival songs. The carving over the Inquisitors' gate. The catechism she had copied in three colors of ink. Her mother's voice, telling it to her as a bedtime story, soft with candlelight: *and so the brave king burned the bridge, and the dragons' greed burned with it.* All of it resting on a foundation of nothing. All of it load-bearing lies.

And the hunt—the hunt that had taken Ivet screaming—was being waged in defense of that lie. Wardens dragging gifted children out of temple halls to protect a forgery. Her own numb fingers in a basin of ice water, cheating to survive inside a story that was never true.

The warmth in her fingertips spread to her palms. She did not fight it this time.

* * *

She worked until the candle had burned down to a knuckle's width, and what she gathered filled six pages of her own small, disguised shorthand—dates, sums, quotations, the fine-handed stranger's fatal little note copied out twice. Leverage, yes. More than she had dreamed of. If the temple's leadership had buried a vault, erased an office, and rewritten the founding myth of the realm, then Paloma Sterling held something worth far more than 412 marks, 7 silver, and 3 copper.

If she could prove it.

*The Codex says he gave nothing. Check the Codex.*

Where did a dead scribe check a codex? Not the open stacks; she knew the open stacks like her own teeth. Not the restricted theology collection, which she had raided years ago out of boredom and knew to contain nothing more dangerous than a heretical commentary on tithing. Which left the sealed levels. The reliquary undercrofts. Places her key opened doors *near*, never doors *to*.

There was one person whose keys opened everything, and whose signature stood at the bottom of Paloma's debt, and who had looked at her across the warding hall two days ago with an expression Paloma had been too frightened to read properly and now understood, with sickening clarity, might have been warning.

Mother Sefu had been a young scribe during the last reorganization of the archives. Mother Sefu signed the annual certification that the reliquary inventories were complete. Mother Sefu, who co-signed a crushing loan for a dying woman's daughter and then never once, in eleven years, offered to forgive a single copper of it—as if keeping Paloma bound were a kindness, a leash worn as protection. As if she knew what happened to unbound people who asked questions.

Paloma pinched out the candle. In the dark, the archive breathed around her: dust, tallow, old paper, and underneath it all, faint as a coal under ash, something she had never noticed in eleven years and could not now stop noticing. A smell of smoke that never fully left the stone. The temple, she realized, still smelled faintly of the Burning. They had never cleaned it out. Perhaps they couldn't.

She climbed the stairs with her six pages pressed flat against her ribs like a second heart, and she had decided—she discovered only as her hand touched the door latch that she had already decided—to go to Mother Sefu's cell tonight, tonight, before courage cooled, and ask her one question.

Not *did you know*. She was afraid of the answer to that one.

Just: *What is the Ember Codex, and where do you keep it?*

Above, in the sleeping temple, a bell tolled the third hour, and somewhere far off in the city a warden's whistle rose and fell like a bird that had forgotten how to sing anything else.
