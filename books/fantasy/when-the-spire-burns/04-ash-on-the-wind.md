## 4. Ash on the Wind

The cuffs had been cold for so long that Dain Corvane had forgotten what warm wrists felt like.

He sat in the interrogation room of the Inquisition's Vellamark chapterhouse and listened to the iron sing against itself whenever he moved—a thin, high note that no one else seemed to hear, though he suspected they did, and simply pretended otherwise. The cuffs were regulation issue, blessed twice by the Provost's own chaplain, stamped with the sunburst of the crown. They were designed to do one thing: to press a gift flat, to smother it beneath cold metal and colder law until the bearer could pass among ordinary men without the wards twitching in his direction.

They worked. That was the cruelty of them. They worked perfectly.

Corvane folded his hands on the table before him—slowly, deliberately, so the iron would not chime—and watched the woman across from him as one watches a candle gutter in a draft.

Ivet Marsh was twenty-three years old, perhaps twenty-four. She had been a scribe of the Temple of the Ledger for nine years, which meant she had spent nearly a third of her life copying out tithes and birth-rolls and the careful, bloodless histories the temple kept for the crown. Her fingers were stained faintly blue-black at the tips from cheap ink. She had been brought in at dawn, wet to the waist—the testing pools at the temple were said to reveal a gift like oil reveals itself on water—and she had not stopped shivering since, though someone had put a blanket around her shoulders an hour ago.

She had failed the test. The ward-water had turned silver around her hands, then gold, then a color the examining priest had no name for and did not want one. She had screamed her own innocence all the way into the wagon. Now, in this room with its grey walls and its single high window, she had gone quiet instead, and the quiet was worse.

"Tell me again," Corvane said, "what you were doing on the night of the Emberfast."

"I told you. I was working. Copying."

"Copying what?"

"A tithe ledger." Her voice was flat, scraped raw. "Grain receipts from the eastern parishes. You can check. The pages will be there. My hand will be on every one of them."

"It will be," Corvane agreed. "That isn't in question."

Her eyes came up then, red-rimmed and furious and afraid, all three at once, and he understood that she had finally grasped the shape of the thing being done to her—that the question had never been whether she was guilty of anything, because failing the test *was* the guilt, entire and self-evident, needing no further proof. The questions were only ritual. Liturgy. A way of making the machinery of condemnation feel like a conversation.

"Then what is in question?" she asked.

Whether you will give me a reason to write something kinder than I must, he thought. He said: "The nature of your gift. What you can do. What you have done."

"Nothing. I've done nothing. I didn't even know—I thought everyone's hands got warm sometimes. I thought it was—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "When I copy certain pages. Old ones. The letters seem to sit up straighter. The ink doesn't smudge where it should. Mother Sefu said I had a careful hand and I believed her, I believed her for nine years, and now you're telling me that's—it's *illegal*? That sitting up late over old pages makes me a criminal?"

The ink doesn't smudge where it should.

Corvane kept his face still. He was very good at keeping his face still; he had practiced for fifteen years in front of mirrors, in front of superiors, in front of the man he had been. But underneath the iron, in the place where his suppressed gift coiled like a drowned man holding its breath, something stirred and pressed against the metal, and he felt the familiar ache begin behind his eyes—the ache that meant the cuffs were doing their work, grinding down something alive.

"You understand," he said, "that I am required to record your answer."

"My answer is the truth. Does the truth help me?"

No, he thought. Nothing helps you now. The rolls have your name. The Provost has your name. There is a cell below this room and a process after that, and the process ends either in the Quiet Cells where the gift is burned out of you with pain and prayer, or—if the examiners find the gift too rooted—in the far north, past the salt marshes, where people are sent and do not come back from.

What he said was: "It helps me."

Something moved through her face then—something that might, in another life, in another city, have become contempt. She looked at him the way people look at a locked door when they are drowning outside it.

"You're one of them," she said. Not a question.

"The cuffs are regulation."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Corvane said. "It isn't."

* * *

Afterward he stood at the window of his office on the third floor and watched the light change over the rooftops of Vellamark.

From here he could see the Spire. Everyone in Vellamark could see the Spire; it stood at the city's heart like a rebuke, a blackened finger of stone rising out of the ruin of its own base, taller than any tower the kings had built before or since. Once, the histories said, it had burned with dragon-fire visible for fifty leagues—a column of gold and ember-light that marked the meeting-place of two realms. Now it was scorched stone and pigeon nests, and the crown had built a memorial plaza around its foot so that citizens could walk past it daily and remember their deliverance from tyranny.

*Deliverance.* Corvane turned the word over the way one turns a coin one suspects is clipped.

A generation ago, King Aldric the Third had ordered the Spire burned and the covenant ended, and the histories recorded why plainly: the dragons had grown proud and demanding; their fire had begun to leech the vitality of human memory; the first king's heirs, wise and merciful, had severed the bridge between realms to save their people's souls. Every schoolchild learned it. Every temple fresco depicted it—the last dragon driven howling into the Ashkarat dark, the righteous flame of human courage climbing the Spire's stones.

And yet.

And yet Corvane had been eleven years old when the Inquisitors came for his mother, and he remembered what she had said—not the things the tribunal recorded, not her confession or her recantation, but the thing she had whispered to him in the cart on the way to the chapterhouse, her cuffed hands folded in her lap exactly the way he folded his now:

*"The fire keeps us, Dain. Without it we forget everything. Ask yourself who wanted us forgetful."*

He had not understood. He had been a child, and terrified, and within the year he had been taken into the Inquisition's orphan wing, where they fed him and schooled him and tested him and discovered, in the fullness of time, that the widow Corvane's son carried the same taint she had died for. They had offered him the choice they offered few: the cuffs, and service. Serve the hunt, wear the iron, keep your gift buried alive beneath blessed steel—or go north like your mother.

He had chosen the cuffs. He had told himself for fifteen years that it was the pragmatic choice, the survival choice, the choice of a boy who understood the world as it actually was.

He was thirty-four years old, and he had just listened to a scribe explain that her only crime was that ink behaved beautifully under her hands, and he had heard his own mother's voice in every word.

A knock at the door. "Inquisitor. The Provost's clerk."

"Enter."

The clerk was a narrow man named Ostrec whose talent, such as it was, lay entirely in the manufacture of documents that ruined lives. He laid a sealed order on the desk.

"From the Provost himself. The sweep proceeds tonight. The temple archives are to be searched top to bottom—all reliquaries, all sealed collections, all restricted stacks. The Grand Prior has raised objections." Ostrec permitted himself a small, dry smile. "The objections have been noted and overruled."

Corvane broke the seal. The order was written in the Provost's own cramped hand, which meant it would be deniable if it needed to be, and it named him specifically: *Inquisitor Dain Corvane to lead the sweep of the Temple of the Ledger archives, effective immediately upon receipt.*

"Why the archives?" he asked. "We've swept the temples twice this year. The tests caught four. The archives hold paper."

"The archives hold *old* paper." Ostrec gathered his sleeves. "There's a rumor the temple's been sitting on covenant-era materials. Restricted collections. Things the Grand Prior would rather the crown didn't read." His smile widened a fraction. "You know how the clergy are about their cellars."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because the Provost wants the sweep led by someone thorough." Ostrec paused at the door, and for a moment the bureaucratic mask slipped, and Corvane saw the thing underneath—which was nothing, which was emptiness, which was the face of a man who had never once in his life felt the iron ache. "And because word travels, Inquisitor. Word about you travels. The Provost likes to keep his thorough men close, where he can watch what they're thorough about."

The door closed behind him.

Corvane stood a long moment with the order in his hand. Then he went to the cabinet in the corner, took out the plain leather case that held his instruments, and began to pack for the sweep: the ward-lamps, the sealing wax, the blank writs. At the bottom of the case, wrapped in oilcloth, was a slim notebook he was not authorized to carry—blank pages, good paper, a pencil stub. He had carried it for six years and never once written in it. He did not know why he kept it. He told himself it was habit, sentiment, nothing.

He packed it anyway.

* * *

Dusk found him crossing the memorial plaza toward the Temple of the Ledger, and it was here, in the open air, with the Spire looming black against a bruised violet sky, that the dread finally caught up with him and he had to stop walking.

It was not fear of the archive. It was arithmetic.

Four gifted caught in the temple tests this season. One of them Ivet Marsh, whose gift was so small she'd mistaken it for competence. If the crown was sweeping archives now, hunting rumors of restricted collections, then somewhere above him—above the Provost, even—someone had decided that paper was dangerous. Someone believed there were words in the temple's keeping that could unmake the official history, and wanted them found and destroyed by a man too loyal to read them closely.

Or too compromised to be trusted reading them at all. The thought arrived unbidden, cold as the cuffs. *Ostrec knows what I am. The Provost knows what I am. Perhaps that is why you. Send the tainted man to touch the tainted books, and whatever happens, he is implicated.*

His mother's voice, again, in the wind off the plaza: *Ask yourself who wanted us forgetful.*

Ahead of him the temple doors stood open for evening offices, spilling candlelight onto the steps. Somewhere inside, in a cell or a wagon or a ledger of names, Ivet Marsh was waiting to learn what the truth was worth. Somewhere inside, in a sealed reliquary, lay whatever it was the Provost feared—and whatever Paloma Sterling, scribe, debtor, charm-seller, whose name had crossed his desk three days ago in a routine debt inquiry and meant nothing to him then, was about to decide to do about it.

He did not know her name would matter. He knew only that the iron at his wrists had begun, very softly, to sing—and that for the first time in fifteen years, Dain Corvane was afraid of what he might find if he did his duty well.

He climbed the temple steps and went in out of the ash-colored dusk.
