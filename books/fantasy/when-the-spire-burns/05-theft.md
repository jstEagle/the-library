## 5. Theft

The reliquary had three locks, and Paloma had spent eleven days learning all of them.

Not by picking them—she was a scribe, not a thief—but by copying them. Every key in the temple archive had its impression recorded somewhere, pressed into wax or sketched in a warden's margin, because the temple trusted paper the way other institutions trusted God. She had spent her nights cross-referencing ledgers the way she'd once cross-referenced hymnals, and what she'd found was this: the reliquary's outer door answered to the Under-Cantor's ring of keys, whose teeth were drawn in the margin of a supply inventory from nine years ago; the inner grate used a word, not a key—a covenant-word, spoken aloud, which she had pried out of a marginalia note in Mother Sefu's own hand; and the third lock was not a lock at all but a warding, a thread of consecrated fire that would burn the name of whoever broke it into the hunt rolls in ink that could not be scraped out.

That third lock was the one that mattered. That was the one designed to make theft impossible—not to stop the thief, but to name her.

She sat in the scriptorium long after midnight with her hands folded on a blank sheet of vellum and thought about her own name. *Paloma Sterling.* Two syllables and four letters' worth of debt. The temple had bought her mother's burial and her apprenticeship both for a sum no honest wage would ever clear, and they had been very careful, over fourteen years, never to let her forget it. Her name was already written down in a dozen places. What difference did one more ledger make?

The difference, she admitted to herself, staring at the candle flame until her eyes watered, was everything. A debt could be worked off. A hunt roll could not.

And still.

*The covenant was mutual,* the Codex fragment had said—the scrap she'd copied and hidden inside the false binding of her prayer book. *Fire given for memory given. Let neither realm take without offering.*

She had read those lines forty times now, and every time, the same cold fact sat beneath them like a stone under a floorboard: the first king had taken the fire. Had taken it and built Vellamark on top of it and burned the Spire so no one would ever climb up and ask him to give anything back. And the temple—the temple where Paloma had copied ten thousand pages of pious history—had written the lie that made it holy.

Ivet's scream was still in her ears. It came back at odd moments: kneading bread, trimming a quill, standing too long in the sun. *Paloma!* Ivet had screamed her name as they dragged her through the cloister, and everyone had heard it, and everyone had pretended not to, and Paloma had stood there with her ice-numbed hands hidden in her sleeves and let it happen.

She could not let it happen again. Not to herself. Not by standing still.

She blew out the candle.

* * *

The reliquary sat beneath the eastern transept, behind the Chapel of the Sundered Flame, in a corridor the novices called the Cold Walk because no lamp stayed lit there longer than an hour. The temple said it was the sanctity of the place that ate the light. Paloma, walking it now in soft shoes with her scribe's robe pulled close against the chill, suspected it was something older and less comfortable—that whatever slept under the transept did not care for fire anymore, having been starved of the real kind for a generation.

Her breath fogged. Her footsteps made no sound on the old flagstones, or perhaps she simply could not hear them over her own pulse.

The Under-Cantor's keys were warm from her pocket. She had taken the wax impression nine years ago—not knowing why, even then, only that some part of her had understood that a girl who copied everything might one day need to have copied *this*—and had cast the working key herself in the cellar of a charm-seller who owed her favors and asked no questions, because asking questions was bad for business in the night market. The metal was cheap brass. It turned the first lock with a sound like a swallowed click.

The inner grate waited beyond it, black iron in a wall of grey stone, and Paloma put her mouth close to the seam and spoke the covenant-word.

It was not a grand word. That was the thing that had struck her when she'd found it in Mother Sefu's marginalia, tucked between two lines of commentary like a hair caught in a book's spine. It was not a word of power or dominion. It was *ashen*—the old Ashkarati greeting, the one the dragons were said to offer when they landed on the Spire's first gallery, meaning, according to the dead language scholars, *we come in peace and leave in memory.* A greeting. The last lock on the holiest vault in Vellamark opened to a courtesy.

The grate swung inward on hinges that had not moved in years and complained about it, a long low groan that seemed to travel down the whole corridor and come back. Paloma froze, one hand on the iron, counting heartbeats. Ten. Twenty. No voices. No lamps kindling in distant cells. The Cold Walk kept its silence, and she slipped through.

The reliquary chamber was small and round and utterly dark except for the third lock itself: a thread of pale fire suspended in the air before a plain wooden case, burning without fuel, without smoke, without warmth. It looked almost gentle. Almost like the glow she carried in her own fingertips, the one she had spent her whole life hiding.

*Consecrated fire,* the warding manuals called it. Fire that remembered. Fire that knew the shape of a soul when it touched one and wrote that soul's true name into the rolls below—written not in ink but in the fire's own substance, unforgeable, unforgivable.

There was no way past it. She had known that from the beginning. Eleven days of research had never been about finding a way around the third lock; there wasn't one. The research had been about making sure that when she broke it, she would break it *knowingly*—that she would walk into her own naming with her eyes open, having chosen it the way you choose a marriage or a martyrdom, fully awake, fully culpable, fully free.

That was the part she hadn't expected to matter. But standing there in the dark with the little flame swaying before her like a candle seen across a field, she found that it mattered enormously. They were going to hunt her either way—she had felt it coming since the criers, since Ivet, since the ice water numbing her hands in the testing line. The hunt was coming regardless. The only question left in the world was whether she would be taken as a thing that happened or as a person who acted.

Paloma Sterling rolled up her sleeves.

"I'm sorry," she told the flame, because it seemed polite, and because she meant it. "But you've been keeping the wrong company."

She reached through it.

The fire closed around her wrist like a decision. There was no pain at first—only a strange, total attention, as if something vast had turned its face toward her and was reading her the way she read manuscripts, line by line, margin by margin. Then the pain came, white and clean and absolute, and she heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize, and she gripped the wooden case with her burning hand and dragged it open and snatched out what was inside—

A book. Small, bound in scarred leather gone dark as dried blood, warm to the touch warmer than the fire that guarded it, warm as a living thing. The Ember Codex. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the world.

The flame released her.

Behind her, far below, she heard the hunt rolls begin to write themselves—a whispering, scratching sound rising up through the flagstones like rain falling upward, like ten thousand quills moving at once, and she knew without looking that her name was being carved into fire-stuff in a vault beneath the Hall of Judgment, and that by dawn every Inquisitor in Vellamark would know it.

She ran.

* * *

The alarm did not ring bells. That was the horror of it, Paloma thought as she fled down the pilgrims' stair with the Codex strapped flat against her chest beneath her robe—it rang *people*. Somewhere in the temple a gong of consecrated bronze had sounded once, and now doors were opening along every corridor, lamplight spilling across floors, voices calling questions to one another in the particular calm tone the clergy used for emergencies, which was worse than shouting.

She took the servants' passage behind the refectory, then the drainage stair that smelled of tallow and old rain, mapping her escape in her head the way she'd mapped the locks: out through the laundry yard, over the wall by the beehives where the stones were mortared loose, down into the tanner's lane where the night market's edges began and a girl with a bundle could become anyone.

She had almost reached the laundry yard when Mother Sefu stepped out of the shadow of the archway and stopped her with one raised hand.

Paloma's heart went off like a dropped lamp. For one insane moment she considered running—she was faster, she was younger—but Mother Sefu's hand did not move, and Mother Sefu's eyes, in the thin light, were not surprised.

"You have it," the old woman said. Not a question.

Paloma said nothing. Saying nothing was, she realized, an answer.

Mother Sefu sighed, and the sigh seemed to come from somewhere much deeper than her lungs—from some cellar of the self she had kept locked for decades—and she looked, just for a moment, very old and very tired in a way Paloma had never permitted herself to see before. "I taught you to copy," she said. "I should have known that sooner or later you'd copy me."

"Mother—" Paloma's voice cracked. "I can explain—"

"No." Mother Sefu stepped closer, and her voice dropped to that register she used only for the gravest corrections, the one that made novices weep. "There is no time to explain, child, and I am done pretending I don't know what needs explaining. I helped write the lie. Forty years ago, when the second king's men came with their new history and their new wards, I was the senior scribe, and I held the pen, and I told myself it was mercy—that a city needs its story even if the story is wrong." Her jaw tightened. "I have had forty years to learn what that mercy costs."

"The hunt rolls—"

"Are writing your name right now. Yes." Mother Sefu's eyes flicked toward the sound of approaching voices—two, maybe three, moving with purpose through the western cloister. "And an Inquisitor named Corvane will be here within hours; his writ arrived this evening, and I read it, and I burned it, and burning it bought us perhaps until matins and nothing more." She took Paloma's face in her two dry hands, and her palms were trembling, and Paloma realized with a lurch that she had never once seen her mentor tremble. "Listen to me. You cannot go out through the laundry yard—they will post it first. Go back through the Cold Walk and out by the ossuary stair; the door sticks and the wardens think it's bricked. Follow the undercity water north until you smell pitch, and find Tamsin of the Low Roads, and tell her Sefu sent you and that Sefu's debt to her is paid in full tonight."

"Your debt—I don't understand—"

"You don't need to understand. You need to *go*." Mother Sefu pushed the hood of Paloma's robe up over her hair with brisk, rough tenderness, the gesture of a woman dressing a child for winter. "And Paloma." Her voice caught on the name. "Whatever you find in that book—and you will find things, terrible ones, ones about me—you must remember that the truth is not a weapon. It's a burden. Carry it like one."

The voices rounded the corner of the refectory. Lamplight swung wild across the yard walls.

Mother Sefu turned away from her and walked toward it, straightening her spine as she went, becoming with each step the full and terrible authority of the Mother Superior of the Temple of the Sundered Flame. "Here!" her voice rang out, strong and furious and utterly convincing. "Here, you fools—the intruder went east, through the chapter house! Seal the east cloister! Move!"

Paloma stood frozen in the archway's shadow for exactly three heartbeats.

Then she ran the other way.

She heard it all as she fled down the ossuary stair: the commotion Mother Sefu raised, magnificent and doomed, drawing the search east like a bell draws birds; the crash of the chapter house doors; a warden's shout of *"Stop her—"* cut off mid-word. At the bottom of the stair, wedged in the sticking door, Paloma paused with her shoulder against the wood and the Codex warm against her sternum like a second heartbeat, and she let herself imagine—for one breath, one stolen, unbearable breath—turning around. Walking back. Giving herself up. Standing beside Mother Sefu in whatever came next, because surely whatever came next would be lighter shared.

Then she thought of Ivet screaming her name while everyone looked away.

She shoved the door open and slipped out into the wet dark of the city, and the door stuck shut behind her, and the temple's towers rose above the roofs against a sky going grey at the edges, and somewhere inside them an old woman was spending everything she had to buy a few hours for a girl with fire in her fingertips and a stolen god-book against her chest.

The point of no return, Paloma understood, was not a place you stumbled across in the dark. It was a door you stuck shut behind yourself, deliberately, with your own two hands.

She pulled the hood lower and went north, following the smell of the water, toward the undercity and the smugglers and whatever came next.

Above her, unseen, the first light of the hunt was already moving through the temple like a lit fuse.
