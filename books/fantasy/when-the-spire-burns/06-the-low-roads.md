## 6. The Low Roads

The water in the undercity was the color of old coins.

Paloma had heard of the Low Roads the way temple children heard of hell: as a warning, a place where the city's refuse and its secrets drained together. She had never expected to descend into it by torchlight, wading hip-deep through water that tugged at her legs like patient hands, with a stolen book pressed against her chest inside an oilcloth wrap that smelled of tallow and fear.

She had come down through a grate behind a tannery, following instructions she had paid for with the last of Mother Sefu's coin—a boy with a scarred lip who had taken the money, pointed at the black square of the grate, and said only: *Ask for the Ferrywoman at Drowned Bell Row. Don't ask anyone else. Don't let them see your hands.*

So she kept her hands beneath the water, where the cold could numb what they wanted to do. It was an old trick now. She had learned it three nights ago, plunging her fingers into the testing basin's ice to pass the ward-scan, and she had been learning it all her life without knowing it had a name—this business of holding warmth underwater until it drowned.

The tunnel opened into a vaulted chamber where the ceiling vanished into dark, and the dark was full of sound: dripping, distant knocking, the lap of water against stone, and voices—low, scattered, human, like rats conversing in a granary. Lanterns hung from rusted chains at long intervals, each one wrapped in smoked glass so that the light came down brownish and weak, the color of tea left too long. Between lanterns there were stretches of absolute black, and Paloma learned quickly to count her steps through them, because the water's floor was not kind. It gave her broken tile, then soft silt that sucked at her boots, then something that might have been a beam or might have been bone.

She thought of Ivet. She had been thinking of Ivet for two days, in the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. *Paloma!* Ivet had screamed across the temple courtyard, screaming her name as the wardens dragged her, screaming it as though the name itself were a rope thrown to a drowning woman—as though saying it would make someone catch hold. And no one had caught hold. Paloma had stood among forty other scribes with her numbed hands folded and her face arranged into appropriate horror, and she had let the scream pass over her like weather.

Now she was carrying a book worth more than every soul in that courtyard, and the irony of it sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. If Ivet had screamed her name to summon help, the name had finally worked. It just hadn't worked in time.

*Don't let them see your hands.* She flexed her fingers under the black water. They ached with the cold, and beneath the ache, faintly, like embers under ash, the warmth persisted. Her gift did not drown easily. Sometimes she thought that was the whole tragedy of being what she was: you could numb yourself, but you could not put yourself out.

* * *

Drowned Bell Row announced itself before she saw it. First came the smell—lamp oil, cooked fish, wet wool, and underneath it something sweeter and stranger, like burnt sugar. Then the sound: a market's murmur, but wrong, subterranean, echoing off stone instead of open sky. Then the light.

The Row was a flooded street from some older, grander Vellamark, its colonnades half-submerged, its marble facades stained green to the height of a man's chest. Someone had strung rope bridges between the upper windows, and on balconies and stair-landings, in doorways and on flat-bottomed skiffs poled through the water, people lived and traded and watched. They watched her especially. A woman alone, hooded, walking the center of the Row with a bundle clutched like a child. Word traveled ahead of her faster than she walked; she could feel it moving through the crowd the way cold moves through water.

She asked for the Ferrywoman twice and got silence the first time, a laugh and a curse the second. The third time she asked a one-eyed woman mending netting on a balcony, and the woman looked at her a long moment—not at her face, Paloma noticed, but at her arms, as if expecting to see something written there—and said, "Third bell arch. Ring if you can reach. Wait if you can't."

The bell arch was exactly what it sounded like: a span of carved stone over a side channel, hung with a bronze bell gone green with age. The channel beneath it ran deeper than the rest; Paloma could tell because the water there was colder, and darker, and moved. She stood in the shallows beside it, shivering, and considered the bell, and decided she couldn't ring it. Ringing meant announcing herself to whoever else was listening. Waiting meant standing still in a place where everyone already knew a stranger had come asking questions.

She waited. She was good at waiting. The temple taught patience the way other institutions taught prayer—constantly, and with punishment attached.

An hour passed, perhaps two. She ate half a heel of bread she'd bought from a skiff-vendor who charged her triple and didn't apologize. She watched the traffic of the Row: smugglers poling goods wrapped in waxed canvas, children running messages along the rope bridges with the surefootedness of squirrels, a man selling charms.

That last stopped her breath.

He had a stall set up on a dry landing, a folding table draped in red cloth, and he sold openly—ward-braids, luck-knots, little glass vials of something that caught the lantern-light and held it too long. No hiding. No ice water. An old woman was buying a knot-charm from him, pressing coins into his hand, and he smiled at her as if this were the most ordinary commerce in the world.

Paloma's eyes stung, and she told herself it was the smoke from the vendor braziers. In Vellamark above, what that man did was a hanging offense. Down here it was a stall with a red cloth. The crown's law, she understood suddenly, was a roof, and the Low Roads lived under the floor of it, where the rain never reached.

"You're bleeding," said a voice behind her.

Paloma turned so fast she sloshed water over her boots.

A woman stood on the low stone step of the bell arch—she hadn't heard her arrive, which should have been impossible, unless the woman had come up out of the deep channel itself. She was short, broad-shouldered, maybe forty, with gray coming in at her temples and a face like a hatchet that had been used kindly for years and resented it. Her clothes were oilskin and wool, patched at the elbows, and her hands were scarred in the particular pattern of rope-work. She looked at Paloma the way a fishmonger looks at a fish: assessing weight, freshness, and how much of it was bone.

"I'm not bleeding," Paloma said.

"Your knuckles are split. You've been clenching your fists for hours." The woman nodded at Paloma's right hand. "People do that when they're scared and pretending not to be. Gives them something to do besides run." She wiped her own palms on her thighs. "You're the one asking after the Ferrywoman."

"Are you—"

"No."

"Oh." Paloma's heart dropped through the water beneath her.

"I'm Tamsin. She's dead eight months. But she left me the bell and the boats and the debts, so I suppose I'm whatever comes after a Ferrywoman." Tamsin's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "What do you need?"

"Passage. Out of the city, or far enough into the undercity that the hunt can't follow. I can pay—" Paloma reached into her coat, and Tamsin's hand shot out and closed around her wrist, fast and hard as a trap.

"Not here." Her grip shifted, turned Paloma's hand palm-up, and for one terrible moment the lantern-light fell directly onto her skin, onto the faint shimmer beneath it that no amount of ice could fully kill—the warmth that lived in her fingertips like banked coals. Tamsin looked at it. Looked at her.

Then she released her, stepped back off the step, and sank into the black channel without a splash, like a stone deciding to be a stone.

"Follow the bell line," her voice said from the dark, already moving away. "Rope on your left. Touch nothing that glows. And whatever you do, don't sing. The things in the deep water like singing, and you don't want them to like you."

* * *

The passage took the rest of the night.

Paloma followed the rope through channels that narrowed until her shoulders brushed both walls, and widened into caverns where her lantern-light couldn't find the ceiling. Twice she passed bones that were too large to explain. Once something vast turned over in the water far below her, and the wave it sent up nearly took her off the ledge, and she heard—or dreamed she heard—a long, slow exhalation somewhere in the dark, like a bellows, like breathing.

Tamsin appeared and disappeared along the route, materializing at junctions to say four words—"left here," "faster now," "duck"—and vanishing again. She never touched Paloma. She never asked her name. By the fourth hour Paloma realized this was not rudeness; it was architecture. This woman had built her entire life so that no one could be extracted from it. No names meant no confessions. No touch meant no leverage. Paloma recognized the design because she had been building the same thing herself, badly, for years, and watching someone do it well was like looking into a mirror that showed only the flaws.

"Why did you help me?" she asked during one of the wide stretches, where they walked side by side along a ledge over water that glimmered faintly with its own pale light—some mineral, she assumed, though the light seemed almost warm.

"I haven't helped you. I've transported cargo."

"Cargo."

"That's what you are until proven otherwise." Tamsin walked with her pole balanced across her shoulders like a yoke, hands hooked over either end. "The Ferrywoman had a rule. Everyone's cargo until they've given you a reason to treat them like a person. Reasons cost extra."

"What's the reason cost?"

"Everything, usually." She said it lightly, but not carelessly. There was a difference, Paloma was learning, between this woman's lightness and the temple's. The temple's lightness was a lid. Tamsin's was a blade kept sharp so it wouldn't have to be used.

They climbed a while after that—handholds cut into rock, slick with spray from some underground fall—and came out at last onto a shelf high above a cavern so large that Paloma's mind refused it at first. It was a drowned cathedral of stone, and at its far end, where the rock fell away entirely, there was sky. Not much. A seam of it, gray-blue with approaching dawn, framed by the ruined arches of what must once have been the Spire's root—the great base of it, the anchor-point, cracked open like a geode by the burning a generation ago.

And through that seam, low on the horizon, Paloma saw it.

The dragon-fire.

Everyone in Vellamark knew the flame atop the broken Spire still burned—guttered, diminished, a shadow of what the histories described, but burning. From the streets it was a rumor, a glow behind clouds, a thing priests pointed to. From here, from below, from the dark heart of the world, it hung above the horizon like a dying star: small, unsteady, pulsing. Not steadily. Like breath. Like a heartbeat with a murmur in it. It flared, dimmed, flared again, and each dimming lasted longer than the last.

"It's dying," Paloma said. Her voice came out strange in the enormous dark.

"Been dying since before I was born." Tamsin leaned on her pole beside her, close enough to speak to, far enough to be untouchable. "My grandmother said it used to fill the sky. Said you could read by it at night, whole city lit gold, and the dragons used to cross over and back like birds. Now look at it." She spat, with feeling, into the abyss. "Crown says it's natural decay. Temple says it's punishment for the first king's sins, which is convenient, since it means nobody living has to fix it."

"It's neither." The words were out of Paloma's mouth before she had decided to say them. She tightened her grip on the bundle against her chest. "It's starving. That's what starvation looks like. A fire fed on nothing."

Tamsin turned her head slowly. In the light of the distant flame and the near-dawn, her hatchet-face was unreadable. "And how does a debt-ridden little temple mouse know what a covenant-fire looks like when it's starving?"

"Because I'm carrying the menu."

Silence. Below them, the deep water breathed. Above them, the dying flame pulsed once, twice, and held its dimness a heartbeat longer than before, and Paloma found her chest tight with a grief she couldn't account for—grief for something she had never seen whole, a sky she had never stood under, a fire she had only ever known as a rumor and a ruin. It was like mourning a grandmother she'd never met. Perhaps that was what a broken covenant was, she thought. A grief inherited by people who never got to choose it.

"Tamsin." She made herself turn and face the woman squarely. Made herself unclench her fists, split knuckles and all, and hold her hands up between them, palms open, letting the faint warmth show itself without shame. "I stole a book tonight. The Ember Codex. The real terms of the old covenant—the ones the temple burned out of every history in Vellamark. The crown's hunters burned my name into their rolls doing it. By noon there will be Inquisitors in the undercity asking about me, and they pay informants in ways you can't imagine. So I'll give you my reason now, upfront, and you can price it however you like." She drew a breath. "I'm going to climb the Spire. I'm going to give the fire back what was stolen from it. And I need to live long enough to get there."

Tamsin looked at her hands for a long time. Then at the bundle. Then, finally, at her face.

"You know what happens to people who make speeches in the Low Roads?" she said. "Nothing. Nobody's ever started a revolution down here. We're too busy staying alive to be noble." She shouldered her pole and turned back toward the passage. "But I owe the temple nothing, and the crown less, and I haven't seen a dying star up close since I was a girl. So. You can ride with my next shipment as far as the Ashfall Terraces. After that you're on your own, and if the Inquisitors peel you, you never met me, and I never met you, and this conversation happened to someone else entirely."

"That's not trust," Paloma said.

"No," Tamsin agreed, already walking. "Trust is expensive. This is interest. Payable on delivery."

Paloma stood a moment longer at the ledge, looking out at the guttering flame—watching it pulse, watching it falter, watching it stubbornly, impossibly, burn. *Hold on,* she thought at it, absurdly, the way you'd think at a sick animal. *I'm coming.*

Then she followed Tamsin into the dark, and for the first time in two days—in two years, if she was honest, in her whole careful hidden life—she was not walking alone.

She was three steps into the passage when Tamsin stopped, held up a fist, and went very still.

Above them, faint through miles of stone, a bell began to ring. Not the drowned bell of the Row. A sharper sound, repeated, spreading—bell after bell taking it up through the undercity like fire through a fuse.

"The wards," Tamsin said quietly. "Someone's tripped the deep gates." She glanced back at Paloma, and her expression, for the first time, was not a fishmonger's. It was afraid. "They're not hunting the undercity, little mouse. They're sealing it. Every exit. Including mine."

Somewhere far above, dawn was breaking over Vellamark, and somewhere far closer, boots were beginning to descend.
