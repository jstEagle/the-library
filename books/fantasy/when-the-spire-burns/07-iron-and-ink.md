## 7. Iron and Ink

The temple archive smelled of dust and tallow and something older beneath both—old paper, old vows, the ghost of incense burned by hands that had been dead for a century. Dain Corvane stood in the doorway of the Scriptorium Hall and let the smell settle over him like a second set of clothes. He had spent six years of his boyhood in rooms like this one, back when he had still been allowed to hold a book without an officer watching him do it.

Behind him, his men moved through the stacks with lanterns and list-sticks, pulling volumes at random, shaking them for loose pages, checking spines against the crown's index of proscribed texts. The sound was soft and constant: the whisper of paper, the click of iron-shod boots on stone. It sounded almost gentle. That was the trick of it, Corvane had learned. The work of the Inquisition was mostly quiet. Screaming was rare, and when it came it was usually brief.

"Found the debt rolls, sir," said Lieutenant Hask. She was a broad, competent woman with a voice like a drawer closing. "Temple ledgers going back forty years. There's a scribe here—Sterling, Paloma—indebted to the order for two hundred marks and eleven shillings."

"Eleven shillings," Corvane repeated.

"The interest compounds quarterly, sir. They keep careful accounts."

"They would." He took the ledger from her and ran his thumb down the column of figures. Two hundred marks was a fortune dressed as a bookkeeping entry—a sum designed never to be paid, only serviced, so that the debtor remained the temple's property in all but name. He had seen the same arithmetic applied to villages, to guilds, to whole provinces. The crown did not need chains anymore. It needed only patience.

"Where is the Mother Superior?" he asked.

"Sefu? In her cell, sir. The healers are with her." Hask paused. "She's alive. Barely. Whatever happened in the reliquary—it wasn't kind to her."

Corvane closed the ledger. "I'll see her alone."

* * *

Mother Sefu lay on a narrow bed in a cell barely wider than the bed itself, and for a moment Corvane thought the healers had been wrong—that she was already dead and simply had not finished the business of lying down. She was a big woman; he remembered that from the hunt rolls, from the descriptions given after the theft. A woman built like a load-bearing wall, who had held up the temple's southern archive through two floods and one fire. Now she seemed hollowed, as if something inside her had been scooped out and only the shape of her remained to say there had once been more.

Her eyes opened when he entered. They were clear. That was worse than if they hadn't been.

"Inquisitor," she said. Her voice was gravel poured slow. "Come to read me my rights?"

"You have none," Corvane said. "You know that. The gifted forfeit them at manifestation."

"Ah. Yes." Something moved across her face—not quite a smile. "Then come to hear me confess?"

He drew the room's single stool to the bedside and sat, setting his lantern where its light would fall across her hands rather than her face. He had learned long ago that hands confessed faster than mouths. Hers were folded atop the blanket like a closed book, and they were trembling—not with fear, he thought, but with cold. As though whatever had been taken from her in that reliquary room was still leaving, still draining out through her fingertips into the dark.

"I'm not required to confess to you," Sefu said.

"No."

"But you're required to write down whatever I do say." Her gaze drifted past him, to the wall. "So write, Inquisitor. You'll want this verbatim."

He took out his notebook. The gesture was automatic, and he hated how automatic it was—the little leather book, the little pencil, the posture of a man preparing to receive truth the way a funnel receives wine. His cuffs shifted against his wrists as he wrote the date. Iron, plain and unadorned, blessed by no priest and cooled in no holy water. Just metal, machined to sit snug against skin that had once done things metal could not explain. He had worn them for nineteen years. Some mornings he woke reaching for warmth that wasn't there.

"The girl's name is Paloma Sterling," Sefu said.

"We know."

"She has a gift for charm-crafting. Small things. Warm things." Sefu's mouth curved. "Buttons and ribbons, Inquisitor. That's what the great hunt of Vellamark has come down to. A woman who made buttons warm."

"Hiding a gift is itself a crime."

"Yes. So is being poor. So is being born on the wrong side of the river. Your law has always had a generous definition of guilt." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him directly for the first time. Her eyes were dark and very tired and, he realized with a small cold shock, amused. "You want to know where she went."

"I am required to ask."

"And I am required—" she coughed, wet and deep, "—to answer. Very well. She went south. Down the river road, toward Ferrow's Crossing. She'll be moving at night, sleeping in culverts. She's frightened and she's alone and she doesn't know these lowlands. If you ride hard you can have her inside three days."

Corvane wrote it down. Every word. He wrote it because he was required to, and because writing gave him time, and because somewhere beneath the writing a thought had begun to move that he was not yet ready to look at directly: *she is telling me exactly what she wants me to believe.*

It was too clean. A woman who had held a reliquary door against an alarm-writhe long enough for a thief to escape—who had paid, by the look of her, nearly everything she had—did not then hand over her fugitive's route like a hostess giving directions. Either Sefu was broken past cunning, or she was spending her last coin on a lie, and buying it with whatever the wardens' extraction had left of her.

He looked at her hands again. Still trembling. Still cold.

"Why?" he asked.

Sefu raised an eyebrow.

"Why give her up?" he said. "If this is true—if she's yours, your student, your charge—why send me down the river road after her?"

For a long moment the Mother Superior said nothing. The healers' poultice on her chest rose and fell, rose and fell, shallow as a tide pool. Then she laughed—or made a sound adjacent to laughing—and it cost her visibly, folding her forward until Corvane half-rose to steady her and she waved him back with two fingers.

"Oh, you're one of the careful ones," she wheezed. "Good. The careless ones don't last. Listen to me, Inquisitor, because I'm going to say a thing now, and you may write it or not, as your conscience allows."

"I'm listening."

"The covenant was ours to break."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Corvane waited for the rest of the sentence—for the *and we broke it thus*, for the heresy or the justification or the mad rambling of a dying woman. It didn't come. Sefu lay back against her pillows and watched him with those tired, amused eyes, and let the sentence stand there, complete and incomplete at once, seven words with a whole country buried underneath them.

"That's not an answer to my question," he said finally.

"It's the only answer there is. Everything else—the Spire, the burning, the hunt, your pretty cuffs—" her gaze flicked to his wrists, and Corvane kept his face still, "—everything else is furniture. Arrangement. A story told by people who needed the story more than they needed the truth." She breathed, and the breath rattled. "We were given something, Inquisitor. Long ago. And we were asked to give something back. And the first king decided that taking was the same as owning, and here we all are, three generations later, hunting button-makers through the night markets."

"You're describing treason."

"I'm describing history. The treason was committed before either of us was born—I merely lack the courtesy to pretend otherwise." She closed her eyes. "Go south, Inquisitor. Ride hard. Have her inside three days."

* * *

He rode south. Of course he rode south. He put twelve riders on the river road and sent four more to sweep the culverts, and he did it efficiently and thoroughly and with the particular care of a man searching a place he has already decided is empty.

They found nothing, naturally. Culverts full of standing water, a smuggler's landing stripped clean, a fisherman who remembered nothing and a ferryman who remembered less. By the third day Corvane stood on the muddy bank at Ferrow's Crossing watching the river slide gray toward the sea, and let himself think the thought he had been carrying unexamined since Mother Sefu's cell:

*She lied to me. And I knew she was lying. And I searched anyway.*

There was a word for what he had just done. He turned it over, examined it, put it away. It would keep.

*The covenant was ours to break.*

Seven words. He had copied them into his notebook twice—once in the cell, verbatim, as evidence, and once later, alone, in his own smaller script, in the margin of a page about culverts and ferry crossings, as though the sentence had followed him off the page and wanted traveling money. He caught himself turning it over at night in the officers' tent while Hask slept and the river talked to itself outside. *Ours.* Not *theirs*. Not *the dragons'* or *the first king's*, even—though surely that was what she meant, surely it was the old king's greed she named. But she had said *ours*. Ours the way you say *our debt*. Ours the way a family says *our shame*.

His superiors would call it sedition. His superiors would be right, by their lights. That was the problem with the Inquisition—you were never permitted the comfort of doing wrong knowingly. You did wrong correctly, by procedure, with documentation.

On the fourth day the orders came.

They arrived by courier, sealed with the triple flame of the High Office, and Corvane read them standing in the mud at Ferrow's Crossing while his horse steamed beside him. The language was spare. It was always spare. *All materials recovered from the Vellamark temple archive pertaining to pre-Burning history, covenant records, and Spire-era documents are to be destroyed by fire under direct supervision of the ranking Inquisitor. No copies. No extracts. No exceptions.*

Burn the archive.

He had known it would come to this. Everyone who worked the hunt knew, in the way you know a wound will scar. The crown did not seize archives to study them. It seized archives the way a man seizes a rival's letters—read quickly, burn quickly, leave no evidence that any other version of events had ever been written down. Three days ago he might have signed the destruction certificate with a steady hand and a clean conscience, if conscience was the right word for the numbness that passed for one in his line of work.

But three days ago he had not heard a dying woman say *ours*, and meant it.

* * *

The archive at dusk looked different than it had that first morning. Emptier. The stacks stood in their rows like ribs, and the light from the high windows came down amber and slow, catching the dust his men had stirred up and never quite settled. Two braziers waited in the central aisle, unlit, flanked by barrels of lamp-oil. Hask had the manifests ready. Forty-one crates of condemned material, itemized and certified, awaiting his signature.

"Where do you want the burning, sir?" she asked. "Courtyard's traditional. Better draft."

"Here is fine."

He walked the stacks first. This, too, was procedure—the supervising officer's walk, cataloguing condition and count. He performed it properly, touching nothing, noting everything. And then, at the far end of the third row, in the section reserved for liturgical commentaries nobody had opened in fifty years, he stopped.

There was no reason to stop. That was what he would tell himself afterward, and it would even be true, in the way that all the truest things are unreasonable. He stopped because the light fell a certain way, and because a thin volume had been shelved spine-inward, which archivists did only with items too fragile for display or too sensitive for browsing, and because his hand—his own hand, the one that had done things metal could not explain—rose before his judgment could convene and drew it out.

A commentary on the Burning, dated ninety years past. Anonymous authorship. He opened it expecting piety.

What he found instead was marginalia. Dense, crabbed, furious marginalia in at least three different hands, layered over decades like sediment. Scholars of the temple, writing in secret, correcting the record in the only place left to correct it—in the margins of the lie itself. *False,* one hand wrote beside the official account of the Spire's destruction. *The Codex terms were mutual. See folio 12, sealed reliquary inventory.* Another hand, older: *The fire was payment, not plunder.* A third, later, shakier: *We remember what we were made to forget. Whoever finds this—we were sorry. We were so sorry.*

Corvane stood in the amber light with the thin volume open in his cuffed hands and felt the hairline crack—the one Mother Sefu's seven words had started—run quietly deeper.

*See folio 12, sealed reliquary inventory.*

The reliquary. Where the alarm had writhed. Where a scribe had stolen a book called the Ember Codex. Where Mother Sefu had spent herself to buy the thief's escape.

It was not scattered data. It was not coincidence stacking on coincidence. It was a single thread, running ninety years deep through the margins of forbidden books and the cells of dying women and the sealed doors of reliquaries, and Paloma Sterling had pulled that thread and run, and every instrument of the crown—including Corvane himself—was strung along that thread like beads on a wire, vibrating with purpose, knowing nothing.

"Sir?" Hask stood at the end of the aisle, manifest in hand. "Braziers are ready when you are."

Corvane looked down at the commentary in his hands. Ninety years of secret sorrow and correction, bound in calfskin thin enough to feel the words through. In ten minutes it would be ash, and the ash would certify that no other version of events had ever existed.

"Give me a moment with the final count," he said.

Hask nodded and withdrew. Her boots faded down the aisle.

Corvane sat on the floor of the stack, back against the shelf, and did the fastest, ugliest arithmetic of his life. To copy was treason. Not the gray treason of a misdirected search party—real treason, ink-and-paper treason, the kind that ended with a tribunal and a short rope. To not copy was to feed ninety years of testimony into a brazier and sign his name beneath it.

His cuffs pressed cold against his wrists. He had worn them so long he no longer felt them, the way a man stops feeling a tooth that aches only sometimes. But he felt them now. He felt them the way you feel a hand on your shoulder in an empty room.

He took out his notebook—the small one, the private one—and began.

He could not copy the volume entire; there was no time and no cover for it. So he chose. He copied the oldest marginalium, the one in the faded brown ink: *The fire was payment, not plunder.* He copied the citation to folio 12. He copied the last message, the shaky one, the apology from the dead. And at the bottom of the page, in his own hand, in his own ink, he wrote the seven words a dying woman had given him, because they belonged with the others—they were all pieces of the same drowned ship.

*The covenant was ours to break.*

Then he closed the notebook, returned the volume to its spine-inward slot for whatever few minutes it had left, and went to light the fire.

It burned quickly, as paper does. The braziers ate forty-one crates in under an hour, and the smoke went up through the high windows in a black column visible from the courtyard, and his men stood around it in the gathering dusk with the satisfaction of men completing a task. Corvane signed the destruction certificate with a steady hand. *No copies. No extracts. No exceptions.* His signature sat beneath the words like a small, neat lie, and he found that he could not look at it for longer than necessary.

Afterward, alone in his quarters, he unwrapped the bandage on his left palm where a splinter of crate-wood had gashed him during loading, and sat looking at the blood beading there, and at the iron cuff above it, and at the notebook lying closed on the table between them.

One page. Out of forty-one crates. One page, hidden in the margin of an officer's field log, among culvert soundings and ferry schedules and the mundane debris of a manhunt. It weighed nothing. It changed nothing. The archive was ash; the thread should have been severed.

And yet.

He thought of Ivet—the scribe from the hunt rolls, dragged screaming a name that wasn't hers to scream—and the way terror had looked on her face like something he recognized from a mirror hung very deep in his past. He thought of Mother Sefu's cold, trembling hands, spending themselves on a lie for a runaway girl. He thought of the marginalia, *whoever finds this—we were sorry,* written by people who had known exactly what he knew now, and had chosen ink over safety too.

There was a whole lineage of them, apparently. A quiet, scattered congregation of the unwilling, passing one page at a time down through the decades, each of them certain they were only delaying the fire.

Corvane picked up the notebook and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay flat and light and heavier than his irons.

Outside, the smoke was still rising over Vellamark. Somewhere south of the river, a woman with warm fingers was carrying a stolen covenant through the dark, and the crown's best hounds—including him—were cast on a false scent. He should feel frustration. He should feel duty reasserting itself, the clean pull of the hunt resumed.

Instead he felt the crack, hairline-thin, running through the foundation of everything he had believed himself to be. And beneath it, faint as heat through iron, something stirring that he had spent nineteen years convincing himself was dead.

He blew out his lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the city breathe, and did not sleep.
