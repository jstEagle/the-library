## 8. The Ember Codex

The smuggler's hold smelled of tar, brine, and the particular sourness of a man who did not trust his cargo to stay put. Tamsin had left her a stub of tallow candle, a blanket that smelled of fish and iron, and a warning delivered with the flat economy of someone who had buried too many promises: *Nobody comes down here. Nobody knocks. If you hear boots on the stairs above, you douse that light and you get in the bilge channel and you don't come out until I say.*

Then the hatch closed, and Paloma Sterling was alone with the most dangerous object in Vellamark.

She set it on an upturned crate and simply looked at it for a long time.

The Ember Codex was smaller than she had imagined. In the archive it had been spoken of the way sailors speak of storms—a thing with weight beyond its size, a name that made senior scribes lower their voices. She had expected something vast, chained, armored in brass. Instead it was a book scarcely larger than her two hands cupped together, bound in leather gone the color of old blood, stitched with thread that caught the candlelight like embers refusing to die. There was no lock. There was no seal. The first king's censors had burned libraries for less than what lay inside these covers, and yet here it sat, unguarded as an egg, waiting.

Her fingers trembled. Not from fear—or not only from fear—but from the warmth gathering in her fingertips again, the warmth she had spent nine years pressing flat beneath ice water and numbness and prayer. The gift knew what this was before she did. It leaned toward the book the way a cold hand leans toward a hearth.

*Not yet,* she told herself, and lit the candle properly, and opened the cover.

The first pages were script she recognized—the high temple hand, formal and lovely, the letters she had spent a decade copying psalters in. But the language beneath was older, a dialect of Old Vellish that only third-tier archivists were permitted to study, and Paloma had studied it anyway, secretly, late at night, because forbidden grammar was the one luxury debt could not take from her. She read slowly. She read the way a person walks across ice.

---

*In the year of the First Frost, when the realms lay open to one another as two hands laid palm to palm, there was a covenant made at the root of the Spire.*

*It was not made by kings.*

*It was made by keepers: those of the dragon-kind who tend the fire of memory, and those of humankind who tend the memory of fire.*

Paloma stopped. She read the lines again. Then aloud, though there was no one to hear, because saying things aloud made them harder to dismiss as misreading.

"It was not made by kings."

Every child in Vellamark could recite the founding verse. *King Aldric the Uniter stood upon the Spire and bargained with the fire-drakes, that men might have warmth and dragons might have worship.* It was carved over the temple doors. It was stitched into altar cloths. It was the first thing she had ever copied as a novice scribe, her young hand wobbling through the sacred lie under Mother Sefu's approving eye.

The Codex said otherwise. The Codex said the covenant was older than the crown by four hundred years, and that its terms were simple in the way that living things are simple—brutally, elegantly, without ornament.

She turned the page.

---

*The terms are thus:*

*From humankind, memory. For the fire of Ashkarat is fed by remembrance; it burns on what mortals loved and will not let go. Each keeper who gives fire must be given memory in return—true names, true griefs, true joys—lest the flame starve into mere heat.*

*From dragon-kind, fire. Not the fire of burning but the fire of keeping: the flame that holds a city's stories whole, that keeps the dead remembered and the living known to themselves. Without it, memory rots. Names blur. Faces smooth over like water closing after a stone.*

*This is the balance. Memory given freely for fire given freely. Neither realm may take. What is taken curdles. What is offered endures.*

Paloma's mouth had gone dry somewhere around *the fire of keeping*. She looked up from the page, half expecting the candle flame to look different now—to seem thinner, or grayer, or somehow aware of its own ancestry. It just flickered. Candles didn't know what they were made of.

But she thought of the night market. She thought of old Marette, who sold roasted chestnuts and could no longer remember her son's face, only that she'd had a son, and how everyone said it was age, just age, nothing strange. She thought of how the city's oldest songs came down to them with verses missing, and how the archivists always blamed damp paper. She thought of her mother's voice, which she could still hear perfectly, and felt a sudden animal terror that someday she wouldn't—and no one would be able to tell her why.

*Without it, memory rots.*

The Spire had been burning for a generation. Not burning bright—burning *down*, guttering like the last candle in a house where everyone has fallen asleep. And everything Paloma had attributed to time, to poverty, to the ordinary erosion of hard years, was perhaps not erosion at all.

Perhaps it was theft.

---

She found the account of Aldric forty pages deep, in a section written in a different hand entirely—sharper, angrier, the strokes of a scribe who was writing fast because they believed they would be killed for writing at all.

*Hear what the histories will never print.*

*Aldric came to the Spire not as a pilgrim but as a debtor. His war had emptied his coffers; his people froze; his enemies circled. The keepers of the fire received him with courtesy, as they receive all who climb honestly. They explained the terms. They asked what he would offer.*

*He offered nothing.*

*He had prepared, instead, a chain of cold-iron and a company of men whose gifts had been bound with the same. While the eldest keeper spoke the words of offering, Aldric's men drove the chains into the Spire's root and held the keepers fast, and Aldric reached into the flame itself and took—*

Here the ink smeared, as if water had fallen on it, or tears.

*—took the fire of keeping whole, tore it from its source, and bore it back to his city in an iron vessel. He told his people he had won it fairly. He told them the dragons demanded worship and he had refused to pay it. He built the temples to house the stolen flame and called the temples holy, and the people, warm at last, chose to believe him.*

*The keepers who survived the chaining crawled back to Ashkarat carrying the wound of the taking. The covenant did not die that day. Covenants do not die. But it broke, and a broken covenant does not stop demanding its price—it only stops being paid by those who owe it.*

Paloma set the book down because her hands were shaking too badly to hold it steady.

Fury rose in her slowly, the way floodwater rises—first around the ankles, then the knees, deceptively gentle, and then all at once at your throat. Because it wasn't abstract. It wasn't history. It was Ivet, dragged screaming out of the scriptorium three days ago for the crime of warmth in her fingertips while the actual grand larceny sat enthroned in every temple in the city, gilded and worshipped and called *the grace of Aldric*. It was the hunt rolls with her own name burned into them. It was Mother Sefu counting coins against Paloma's soul and calling it devotion.

They had outlawed magic. The crown had outlawed magic, and then kept the largest working of magic in the realm locked in a reliquary and called it a relic. They burned gifted children out of their homes while the entire city ran on a stolen flame that needed—*demanded*—what the crown forbade anyone to give.

*What is taken curdles.*

And oh, it had curdled. She could see it everywhere now that she knew to look. The city's forgetting. The missing verses. The way the dragon-fire visible from the low roads had seemed to her, only nights ago, like a dying star—and she had thought *how sad*, the way you think *how sad* about weather, without understanding that she was looking at a wound that human hands had made and human law was holding open.

The realms weren't dying of enmity. That was the final obscenity of it. All the border garrisons, all the sermons about drake-malice, all the wardens and inquisitors and hunt rolls—all of it defended against an enemy that had never attacked. The dragons hadn't broken faith with humanity. Humanity had broken faith with the fire, and the fire was dying of it, and the city's memory was dying with the fire, and nobody knew, because the ones who knew were the ones who had done the stealing.

Awe and fury braided together in her chest until she couldn't tell one strand from the other. Awe, because the covenant itself was beautiful—memory for fire, freely given, freely returned, a bond between kinds that required not worship but *honesty*. Fury, because something that beautiful had been treated as a purse to be cut.

---

Near dawn, she found the last pages. The handwriting changed once more—older, wearier, the letters of someone writing against failing light—and the tone shifted from accusation to instruction, as if the writer had raged themselves empty and arrived at purpose.

*To whoever holds this book when we cannot:*

*The covenant can be reforged. The fire remembers its terms even when men forget them. But fire cannot climb to a man; a man must climb to the fire.*

*Know the price before you hope. The flame of keeping feeds on memory freely surrendered—not memory lost, not memory stolen, but memory carried up in love and given away in love. The climber will lose what they carry. Wholly. The flame does not borrow; it keeps.*

*We do not write this to frighten you. We write it so that if you choose to climb, you choose with open eyes, as the keepers chose, as the first climbers chose. The imbalance grows. Each year the fire gutters lower and the city forgets more deeply, and the day will come when no one remains who remembers that anything was forgotten at all. On that day the choice will have been made for you.*

*Choose before then.*

*Choose while you still remember what remembering means.*

Paloma closed the book.

The candle had burned down to a knuckle of tallow. Through the seams of the hatch above her, gray light was leaking—the false, colorless dawn of the undercity, where the sun arrived secondhand. Somewhere overhead, Tamsin's boots crossed the deck, paused, moved on.

Paloma sat with the Codex in her lap and understood, with terrible clarity, why the crown hunted the gifted and burned archives and kept this book behind sealed doors. Not because the truth was dangerous to the powerful.

Because the truth was *dangerous, period*. Because somewhere in Vellamark, right now, some other scribe or smuggler or chestnut-seller might be reading these exact words and feeling exactly what Paloma felt—that vertiginous, furious, holy certainty that someone had to climb.

She pressed her palms against the cover. Her gift answered, warmth rising to meet the ember-stitched leather, and for the first time since the night market, since Ivet's scream, since the ice water, she did not try to press it flat.

"Someone has to," she whispered to the empty hold. "Don't they."

Above her, boots on the stairs—fast, heavy, more than one pair.

Tamsin's voice, low and sharp, cutting through the wood: "Scribe. Kill the light. We have company."
