## 9. A Market of the Marked

Tamsin had a rule about bringing strangers below: never bring them twice.

Once was necessity. Twice was habit, and habit was how the crown found people. She'd watched whole cells unravel because someone got comfortable, started taking the same route, started trusting the same faces. Comfort was a luxury the flooded city didn't sell. So when she led Paloma Sterling down through the third lockgate and into the dripping dark of the undercity's oldest drainage galleries, she did it with her jaw set and her hand never far from the knife at her hip, telling herself this was once, only once, and that the scribe's wide eyes and ink-stained fingers were not, in fact, beginning to feel like something other than a problem.

The water here came to their ankles, black and slow-moving, carrying the smell of the river and something older beneath it—mineral, faintly sweet, like struck flint. That was the dragon-fire residue. Tamsin had grown up breathing it without knowing what it was. Everyone in Vellamark had. The city sat on its leavings like a drunk sleeping on a fortune he'd already spent.

"You're quiet," Paloma said behind her. The scribe's voice echoed wrong in the gallery—too flat, as if the stone swallowed sound on purpose.

"I'm counting," Tamsin said.

"Counting what?"

"Reasons to turn around." She held up a lantern, and its light caught the ceiling: ribbed vaults furred with pale fungus, and carved into the keystone of every arch, so worn most folk never noticed them, the old sigils. Covenant-marks. The city's founding fathers had built the undercity with the same hands that raised the Spire, back when magic ran through mortar like blood through skin. "I've found four so far. You being useful is one of them. It's the smallest."

Paloma made a sound that was almost a laugh. "And the others?"

"The Codex you stole. And the fact that Corvane didn't kill you at the ferry crossing when he easily could have." Tamsin glanced back. In the lantern light the scribe looked hollowed out, three days of running carved into the lines of her face. "An inquisitor who lets a thief go isn't an inquisitor anymore. He's a question. And questions are dangerous for everyone standing near them."

"He didn't let me go," Paloma said. "We fought together. There were wardens—"

"There are always wardens." Tamsin waded on. "The point is what he chose when it mattered. I've been running cargoes past the crown's nose since I was fourteen, and I can tell you the one thing I've learned about men like him: they don't break all at once. They crack. And a cracked man is either going to shatter or become something new, and you don't get to pick which. He does." She stopped at a wall that looked like every other wall—wet brick, weeping mortar—and pressed her palm against a sigil half-buried in slime. "So before we go through this door, I need you to understand something. What's on the other side of it isn't a hideout. It's not a rebellion. It's people. Forty-one of them, last count, including six children. They're not soldiers. They're cooks and cobblers and a midwife and a man who used to make chimes for temple bells. They're hiding because hiding is the only thing left that works, and every person I bring through this door is a risk I'm choosing to take. So whatever you're carrying—" she turned, and let the lantern light fall full on the scribe's face, "—you'd better be sure it's worth forty-one lives."

Paloma didn't answer right away. She stood in the black water, and Tamsin watched her do something she'd seen desperate people do in dockside chapels: press her thumb hard against two fingers, one after another, like checking coins.

"I have a book," Paloma said finally, "that proves everything the city believes is a lie. I have hands that can warm a sick child better than any physician's tonic. And I have nowhere else to go." Her chin lifted a fraction. "Is that worth it?"

"It'll do," Tamsin said, and pushed the sigil.

* * *

The door opened on warmth.

Not fire-warmth—the enclave had no more fuel than anyone else—but the particular warmth of many bodies in a small space, of soup long simmered, of tallow candles burning down to pools. The gallery beyond the false wall opened into a series of connected chambers that had once been cisterns, their ceilings lost in shadow, their floors dry here where the drainage had been rerouted decades ago by hands that knew stone better than the city ever would. Hammocks hung in tiers along the walls. A rope line sagged with washing. Children's chalk drawings climbed one curved wall—a sun, improbably large; a stick-figure family holding hands; a spire, crossed out, then drawn again uncrossed.

Tamsin heard the moment the room noticed them. Conversation thinned. Someone's ladle stopped scraping. A child peered down from a hammock tier with the frank, unblinking stare of the very young, and an older woman pulled the child back with one hand without looking away from the newcomers.

"Marn!" Tamsin called. "It's me. And I brought trouble."

A man detached himself from a group near the far brazier. He was heavyset, gray-bearded, with the rolled sleeves and scarred forearms of a smith—which was exactly what he had been, before the hunt, before his forge was seized and his name went on the rolls. Marn Kettleby. He'd made horseshoes for thirty years and then, in one terrible autumn, made something else entirely, and now there were warrants with his name spelled out in iron-gall ink in three prefectures.

He looked at Paloma for a long moment. Then at Tamsin. "You said you'd bring word of the ferry routes. Not a stranger."

"The stranger is the word." Tamsin jerked her chin at Paloma. "Temple scribe. On the hunt rolls. Carrying something the Inquisitor himself wants badly enough that he's tearing the low roads apart looking for her."

That moved the room. Tamsin felt it move—heard breath catch, saw a woman near the washing line go still with a shirt half-folded in her hands. Fear traveled through the enclave like current through water, finding every body at once.

"She led them here?" Marn asked quietly.

"They followed her. I chose to bring her ahead of them." Tamsin met his eyes steadily. "There's a difference, and you know it. We've been waiting years for something worth the risk of staying put. She might be it. Or she might be the thing that kills us all. I don't know which yet. Neither does she."

"That's a hell of a recommendation, Tam."

"It's the honest one." Tamsin stepped aside, leaving Paloma standing alone in the doorway between the wet dark and the warm light, and watched what the scribe would do with it.

What Paloma did was bow. Deeply, formally, with the precise economy of someone who'd been trained in temple etiquette since childhood—an old gesture, out of place among hammocks and washing lines, and somehow, because of that, disarming.

"My name is Paloma Sterling," she said to the room. "I was a scribe of the Scriptorium of Saint Aldhelm's for eleven years. Three days ago I stole the Ember Codex from its reliquary, and my mentor burned herself doing it so I could run. I can't undo that. I know some of you paid for the raid that followed—I passed the burned-out tenement on Tanner's Row." Her voice caught, steadied. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking for a chance to be worth trusting. It's all I have."

Silence. Then, from the hammock tiers, the child spoke up—six or seven, gap-toothed, legs swinging. "Are you really marked? Can I see?"

"Jessa," the older woman hissed.

But Paloma smiled—a small, tired smile, the first Tamsin had seen fully reach her eyes—and held out her hand, palm up. "Come see, if your mother permits it."

Permission granted with a reluctant nod, the child scrambled down, crossed the room with the fearless gait of the very small, and laid her own small palm against the scribe's. Nothing visible happened. But Jessa's whole face changed—her brows rose, her mouth rounded—and she spun toward the room. "She's *warm*. Like a fresh roll. Like sitting by the fire but it's coming *out of her*."

The room's fear didn't vanish. But it changed texture, the way ice changes just before it becomes water. Tamsin had seen that shift enough times to know it wouldn't hold forever. Warmth was charming. Warmth was almost innocent. But the enclave had learned, one lost member at a time, that innocence bought nothing from the crown.

It was Marn who said what everyone was thinking. "Warmth's a pretty trick, scribe. But we're not a charity. Everyone here works. Everyone here gives. The midwife pulls her weight with birthing-hands. Ferro keeps our lungs clear with air-charms when the mold blooms. If you eat at our table, you bring your gift to the table too." He folded his arms. "And we'll want to see the book. The Codex. Whatever it is you say you stole."

"The book isn't mine to show casually," Paloma said. "It shows things that will frighten you."

"Frighten us?" A bitter laugh from somewhere in the tiers. "Woman, we sleep in a drain."

But it wasn't Marn who ended the argument. It was the coughing.

It came from the innermost chamber—a deep, wet, racking sound, the kind of cough that has furniture in it, that has weeks and months in it. The woman with the shirt dropped it. The midwife, a spare, sharp-eyed woman named Odile, was already moving. And Tamsin saw Paloma turn toward the sound with an expression she recognized from her own mirror, years ago, in the first month after her own mark came in: the helpless, hungry look of a gifted person hearing suffering their gift might answer.

"No," Tamsin said, before she'd decided to say it.

Paloma looked at her.

"You don't even know what's being asked," Tamsin said. "You haven't eaten properly in three days. You look like a wind could take your purse."

"What's wrong with her?" Paloma asked Odile, ignoring Tamsin entirely, which took a certain courage given that Tamsin was the one holding the door she'd come through.

"Lung-rot," Odile said shortly. "Third stage. She was a dyer—worked the vats in Copperkettle Ward until the fumes finished what her father's lungs started. Name's Brey. She's been dying for a month. We keep the pain down with poppy and prayer, and the prayer's the stronger of the two lately." Her sharp eyes flicked to Paloma's outstretched hand, to the child still holding it. "You're a pain-worker?"

"I don't know what I am," Paloma said. "I make charms. Small ones. Warmth, easing, luck-turning. I've never—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I've never done anything like what you're thinking."

"Then don't," Tamsin said. "Odile, tell her."

But Odile was looking at Paloma with the particular hunger of the exhausted. "Child," she said, "Brey has maybe a week. The poppy's nearly out and the caravan that brings it won't run again till the thaw. I can manage her pain tonight. Tomorrow night. Maybe the night after. And then she'll die screaming, in front of the children, because sound carries in stone and I can't stop it." She spread her hands. "If you can ease her—even for a day, even for an hour—it buys her dignity. That's not nothing. That's the whole coin."

The room had gone quiet again. Even the brazier seemed to burn lower.

Paloma looked at the inner chamber's curtain. Looked at her hands. And Tamsin watched her weigh it—not quickly, not carelessly, but honestly, the way you weigh something you understand the true price of. She'd known the scribe three days. She'd expected piety, or panic, or the brittle nobility of the educated poor. She hadn't quite expected this: a woman standing in a room of strangers, calculating exactly what she could afford to lose, and finding that the ledger came out in favor of a dying dyer she'd never met.

"All right," Paloma said. "Show me."

* * *

Brey lay on a pallet bed in the innermost cistern, propped on rolled blankets, her skin the color of candle-tallow gone gray, her breath rattling in and out of her like water through a failing lock. Up close she was younger than her body suggested—forty, maybe, though the rot had aged her. Her eyes found Paloma and sharpened with something that wasn't quite hope. Hope was expensive. Dying women learned to budget it.

"So you're the thief," Brey rasped.

"Everyone's very well informed," Paloma said.

"Tamsin informs." A ghost of a grin cracked Brey's dry lips. "Sit. If you're going to do whatever it is, sit where I can see you. I don't hold with having my pain worked from behind my back. Feels like theft."

Paloma sat on the stool Odile placed. "Tell me what it feels like. All of it. Don't soften it."

"It feels like drowning slowly, in front of everyone, while they pretend it's fine." Brey's laugh turned into a cough that bent her double, and Odile was there with a cloth, and when it passed Brey lay back gray-lipped and furious with weakness. "There. That's the soft version. Now what?"

"Now," Paloma said, "you tell me if it gets worse, and you don't be brave about it." She flexed her fingers. Tamsin, watching from the curtain, saw her close her eyes, and saw her lips move—not in prayer, or not only in prayer—in *counting*, the way she counted coins, the way she counted everything. The scribe measured the world in numbers. Tamsin wondered what number she'd arrived at for this.

Then Paloma laid both palms flat on Brey's sternum, and began.

Tamsin had seen gift-work before. Ferro's air-charms looked like nothing at all. The midwife's birthing-hands looked like massage. Most working-craft in the enclave was small, careful, deliberately unimpressive—because impressive drew the crown, and subtle kept children fed. She had never seen anything like this.

Light came off Paloma's hands. Not metaphorically. Not a trick of the candle. A low amber glow, like lamplight through honey, spreading from her palms into Brey's chest, and where it spread the rattle in the dying woman's lungs *changed*—not vanished, nothing vanished, but eased, loosened, became breath instead of drowning. Brey arched. Her hands flew up and gripped Paloma's wrists, and she made a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh, and said, "Oh. Oh, it's *warm*, oh, you said fresh rolls, the child said—" and then she was crying, silently, tears running sideways into her pillow, while the amber light worked its way down through her like dawn through fog.

And Tamsin watched Paloma pay for it.

It started in the shoulders. A tremor, then a shake. Then the color left the scribe's face the way tide leaves a flat—not gradually but all at once, as if something had opened a valve. Her breath went ragged. Sweat sprang up along her hairline despite the cold of the cistern. By the time the light guttered and died—minutes, it couldn't have been more than minutes—she was swaying on the stool, and when Odile reached to steady her she discovered the scribe's hands were ice, genuinely ice, cold as the floodwater outside, cold as a dead woman's kitchen.

"Stop," Tamsin said, and stepped forward. "That's enough."

"It helped." Paloma's voice came out slurred, distant. "Did it help? Tell me it helped."

"It helped," Brey whispered. She had pushed herself up on one elbow, and she was looking at Paloma the way people look at a miracle they intend to remember precisely, because miracles don't issue receipts. "Gods above. Girl. What did it cost you?"

"A few days' warmth, maybe." Paloma tried to smile and mostly managed it. "I'll eat. I'll be fine."

"She won't," Odile said flatly, pressing the scribe's frozen hands between her own practiced ones. "I've seen gift-drain. She's given away body-heat she doesn't have. She'll shiver for two days and sleep like the dead and eat everything we own, and if she does it twice more without recovering between, it starts taking from deeper places. Nerves. Memory, sometimes. I've seen a pain-worker on Copperkettle Row forget her own daughter's face after a winter of work like that." She looked up at Paloma, and her voice gentled by a degree. "You didn't know that, did you."

"No," Paloma said. "I didn't know that."

"Memory," Tamsin repeated. The word landed strangely in her chest, heavier than it should have been. She thought of the Codex wrapped in oilcloth inside Paloma's coat. She thought of what little she'd pried out of the scribe about its contents—that it held terms, balances, prices. Human something for dragon something. She hadn't asked for details. Details were dangerous. But the word *memory* sat in her now like a swallowed hook.

"Memory," Odile confirmed. "The body keeps accounts, child. Everything's taken from somewhere. That's the first law of craft, and no temple ever taught it because temples don't want you knowing the gifts have teeth."

Brey reached out and took one of Paloma's chilled hands—the one not captive in Odile's grip—and held it against her cheek, which was, for the first time in a month, warm. "Foolish girl," she said, with enormous tenderness. "You gave a dying woman a summer day. Do you know what that's worth down here? Everything. It's worth everything."

* * *

They fed Paloma until she nearly wept—barley soup with real salt pork in it, and dark bread, and tea brewed from pine needles because actual tea was a memory itself. She fell asleep in a hammock within minutes of finishing, mid-sentence, one hand still curled around the shape of the hidden Codex through her coat, and Tamsin stood over her for a long moment with a lantern, watching her shiver and sleep and look, for once, young.

"She'll kill herself for us if we let her," Odile said quietly, appearing at Tamsin's shoulder.

"Then we don't let her."

"You saw what she is. Word travels in the low roads faster than boats. By week's end every hunted soul in the undercity will know there's a scribe below who can pull pain out of the dying. They'll come in droves. Desperate people always do." Odile rubbed her eyes. "Can you stand over her every hour of every day and ration her out like medicine?"

"I can try."

"And the book?"

Tamsin looked at the sleeping scribe. "Tomorrow," she said. "Tomorrow I ask her to open it."

She doused the lantern and stood a while longer in the dark, listening to the enclave breathe—children turning in hammocks, Brey's eased breathing, faint and even, from the inner room, the drip of the old water finding its old channels. Forty-one souls, she thought. Forty-two now, counting the thief.

Somewhere above, muffled through fathoms of stone, she heard the first bells of the night watch, and beneath the bells—so faint she might have imagined it, except she knew she hadn't, because she'd heard it growing for years, the way you hear a fire dying in another room—a long, thin note of wrongness. The dragon-fire under the Spire, guttering. The city's heart slowing.

And threaded through the pipes and culverts, carried on water from the direction of the temple district, a sound she recognized with a lurch: boots. Many boots. Moving with purpose through the upper levels of the undercity, where no lawful patrol had any business being.

Corvane, she thought. He's found the low roads.

She went to wake Paloma, and found the scribe already awake, sitting up in the dark with the Codex in her lap, unbound, open, her eyes wide and fixed on Tamsin with an expression Tamsin had last seen on a woman reading her own death warrant.

"Tamsin," Paloma whispered. "You need to read this. Tonight."
