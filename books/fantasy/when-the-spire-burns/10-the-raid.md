## 10. The Raid

The order came at dusk, written in the Inquisition's red ink on grey paper, and Dain Corvane read it three times before the meaning would settle into him like a stone sinking through water.

*Enclave suspected beneath the Tanner's Reach district. Gifted in residence, number unknown. Sweep and burn. You will lead.*

Burn. Not *clear*. Not *detain*. The word had been chosen carefully, as all words were chosen in the Chancery, and it told him everything about what was expected when his boots came down those stairs.

He sat in the warden's barracks with the paper flat on his knee and listened to his own heartbeat, which was steady, which had always been steady, which he had spent twenty years teaching to stay steady no matter what moved behind his ribs. The iron cuffs at his wrists—worn openly now, the way an honest man wears a wedding band—pressed their familiar cold against his pulse points. Cold that kept nothing down anymore so much as it reminded him what he was keeping down.

Eleven days since Mother Sefu had looked at him across a broken table with her hands shattered in her lap and said, *the covenant was ours to break*, and given him a false trail south along the river road. Eleven days since he had copied one page from the archive by lamplight, folding it into his coat like a thief, like a boy, like the frightened apprentice he had once been before the cuffs and the oaths. Eleven days of following the false trail just slowly enough, reporting just enough, letting the machinery of the hunt grind forward a half-step behind the truth.

And now the machinery had found the truth without him.

He should have felt surprise. He felt instead a terrible, clarifying calm—the calm of a man who has been walking toward a cliff in the dark and finally feels the ground tilt beneath him and knows, at last, exactly where he is going.

"Ser Corvane." Lieutenant Brassa Vell stood in the doorway of the barracks, lantern in hand, her face half-lit and half-lost to shadow. She was thirty, competent, ambitious in the quiet grinding way of people who had nothing else, and she had followed him through four years of hunts without once asking why they never seemed to catch anyone who mattered. "Wardens are marshaling. Forty picked men. The Chancery wants the sweep begun before full dark."

"Before dark," Corvane repeated. "So the gifted can't see us coming."

"So there's nowhere for them to run *to*," Brassa corrected, and something in her voice made him look up. She smiled, thin and professional. "You taught me that, ser. First month I served under you. *Never give them a direction, only a wall.*"

He had taught her that. He remembered the lesson; he could not remember believing it.

"Mount up, then," he said, and heard how level his voice came out, and hated how much practice that levelness had taken.

* * *

Tanner's Reach crouched at the bottom of Vellamark like something the city had swallowed and never digested—a warren of tannery yards long abandoned after the trade moved downriver, its lime pits gone green with scum, its drying sheds sagging into the mudflats. The crown had condemned it forty years ago. Condemned districts made excellent hiding places, because no one official ever looked at them twice, and because everyone unofficial knew exactly where to find each other.

Corvane rode at the head of the column with the rain starting, fine and cold, beading on the iron of his cuffs until they gleamed like something newly forged. Behind him: forty wardens, crossbows slung, fire-oil canisters clinking softly in their saddlebags with every stride of the horses. That sound. He would hear that sound in his sleep for the rest of his life, whichever length of life he was granted. The little glass-and-iron music of a thing not yet burned.

They dismounted at the district's edge and went in on foot, spreading through the lanes in pairs the way he had trained them, closing like a hand closing. And Corvane walked the center of the net with his senses straining against the iron's dampening, trying to feel what he had spent two decades refusing to feel: the faint warmth of other gifts, banked and hidden, somewhere below.

He found it at the mouth of the old drainage gallery—a seam in the world, subtle as a change in air pressure before a storm. A warding. Woven tight and low and desperate, the charm-work of someone skilled and someone terrified, layered over a hidden door in the tannery's flooded sublevel. His own gift stirred against the suppression like a dog hearing its name through a wall.

*There,* something in him said. *That is where they are. Now decide what you are.*

"Ser?" Brassa beside him, close enough to touch, her crossbow raised toward the gallery mouth. "Something there?"

"A dead ward," he said. "Old temple work, decayed. Nothing living past it."

She looked at him. Just for a moment—one breath, maybe two—in the grey rain-light, and he watched her eyes move over his face with the careful thoroughness of a woman cataloguing evidence she did not yet know she was collecting.

"You're certain."

"The wards don't lie, Lieutenant," Corvane said. "People do. I've built my career on knowing the difference."

It was the truest sentence he had spoken aloud in eleven days, and it was a confession, and she heard it as praise.

* * *

The descent into the enclave took an hour, because the enclave had been built by people who understood precisely what was hunting them.

Down through the tannery's rotted floors, down ladders slick with mold, along a drainage tunnel where the water ran shin-deep and black and smelled of lime and old blood, and then—abruptly, impossibly—into dry stone and lamplight. A vaulted cellar from some older city, pre-Spire or early-Spire, its walls covered in chalk marks: names, tallies, small drawings of hands. Dozens of sleeping pallets. A cook-fire vented through a clever flue that fed smoke into the tannery chimneys above. Shelves of dried food, bundles of herbs, a child's wooden horse sitting on a barrel as if waiting to be claimed.

And people. Twenty-three of them by Corvane's count, rising from their places as the first wardens came through the far entrance, pressing back against the walls. An old man with a smith's forearms. Two young women holding hands so tightly their knuckles had gone white. A boy of perhaps ten clutching the wooden horse, his eyes enormous. A heavyset woman in an apron standing between the crowd and the wardens' crossbows with a ladle still in her fist, as if she had been interrupted mid-stirring and intended to finish.

Corvane counted them the way he counted everything now, with a ledger open in his chest that he could not close. Twenty-three. And the order said *burn*.

"Inquisitor Corvane," the aproned woman said, and her voice shook but did not break. "I know your name. Everyone here knows your name. They say you're the one who reads the charges yourself. They say you look people in the eye."

"I do," Corvane said.

"Then look at mine and tell me what crime you see."

He looked. He saw a woman whose gift was probably nothing more dramatic than bread that never spoiled, or fevers drawn out through the palms—he had learned to read the shapes of suppressed gifts in the way people stood—and he saw twenty-two others behind her, and behind all of them, faint and patient as tide, the truth he had been carrying since the archive: *the covenant was ours to break. Ours. We broke it. Everything after that is the sound of us refusing to say so.*

"By authority of the Crown and the Office of Inquiry," he said, and his voice came out steady, gods help him, steady as a hangman's rope, "you are commanded to surrender yourselves for assessment and—"

He stopped.

It was a small thing that stopped him. The boy with the wooden horse had turned his face into the aproned woman's side—not hiding from the crossbows, Corvane realized. Hiding from *him*. From the cuffs. From the story the cuffs told. As if Corvane himself were the monster in this room, and perhaps he was, and perhaps he had always been, and the only question left was whether tonight was the night he stopped being it.

"—and," Corvane said again, and the word hung in the lamplight like a moth beating itself against glass.

Behind him, at the tunnel mouths, he heard the wardens shifting. He heard Brassa's boots on wet stone, moving closer. He had perhaps ten heartbeats of authority left in his body and he knew it with the same certainty he had once known the shape of his own suppressed gift: that warmth in his fingertips, that small impossible heat he had ironed flat at fifteen years old when the testers came to his village, when he had watched a girl with a similar warmth be taken away screaming and had thanked whatever gods there were that it hadn't been him.

Ten heartbeats. He had spent twenty years buying safety with silence. Ten heartbeats to spend it all.

"Sweep the eastern galleries first," Corvane said, turning, his voice snapping into command like a whip finding its crack. "There are storage chambers off the main drain—I want every crate opened, every sack cut. If they've got codex fragments or relic-work hidden, it'll be there. Take six men. The rest hold this chamber. Nobody moves until I say."

Brassa frowned. "The orders say—"

"*I* have the orders, Lieutenant. I wrote the tactical annex myself." He hadn't; there was no tactical annex. But she hesitated, and hesitation was all he needed. "Go. Now."

She went. Six wardens splashed away down the eastern tunnel, their lanterns dwindling. And Corvane stood alone in the middle of the enclave with seventeen remaining wardens at his back and twenty-three fugitives in front of him and the whole architecture of his life balanced on the point of the next thing he said.

The western tunnel—the one behind the aproned woman, the one that sloped up toward the old lime pits and daylight—was guarded by a single young warden named Pell, nineteen years old, sick-looking, new to the hunt. Corvane had noticed him earlier because the boy kept swallowing, kept touching the stock of his crossbow like a talisman.

"Pell," Corvane called. "With me. There's a sealed door at the north end I need checked—warded, probably trapped. I need someone quick and someone who follows instruction *precisely*."

Pell came at a half-run, visibly relieved to be given something concrete.

Which left the western tunnel unguarded.

Corvane walked to its mouth as if inspecting it, turned his back on it, and began loudly directing the placement of lanterns—"higher, there, we want the shadows gone from the far wall"—and while his voice filled the chamber with the ordinary music of procedure, the aproned woman looked at him.

She understood. He watched understanding arrive in her face the way dawn arrives: not all at once, but completely. Her eyes went from his face to the unguarded tunnel to the boy with the wooden horse and back. She did not nod. Nodding was evidence. She simply turned, quietly, and began gathering her people with the small efficient gestures of a woman who had organized a household under threat her entire life—hand on a shoulder here, a tilt of the head there—and they began, one and two and three, slipping into the western dark.

The old smith went first, then the two women holding hands, then the others, folding themselves into the tunnel's throat with a silence that cost them everything. The boy with the wooden horse paused at the tunnel mouth and looked back at Corvane—at the cuffs, at his face—with an expression no child should have had occasion to learn, and then he was gone too.

Eleven of them, Corvane counted, counting the way he counted everything, the ledger in his chest taking its entries. Eleven souls passing three feet behind the back of the Inquisitor of Vellamark while he arranged lanterns.

Twelve remained. Twelve he could not save without dying uselessly, and twelve who would be safer, later, with him alive and compromised rather than dead and replaced. That was the arithmetic he gave himself. He knew it was also an excuse. Both things were true. He was learning that both things could be true at once and a man could survive it, barely, the way a tree survives lightning—standing, but marked forever down its whole length.

"Ser Corvane." Brassa's voice, from the eastern tunnel. "Nothing here. Empty chambers, old straw. Whatever stores they had, they're gone."

"Then we take the twelve and search the rest of the district," he said, turning—

And stopped.

Because Brassa was standing in the center of the chamber, and she was not looking at the twelve fugitives huddled against the wall. She was looking at the western tunnel. At its empty mouth. At the absence of the warden who should have been standing in it.

Her lantern was very steady in her hand.

"Where's Pell?" she asked.

"Checking the north door with me. He must have come back around—" 

"There's no door at the north end, ser." Her voice stayed flat, professional, terribly polite. "I walked that gallery twice. It's solid stone." She let the silence sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "Eleven came down here ahead of us. My count from the watchers above. Eleven. I count nine in front of me now."

The lamplight flickered. Somewhere above, rain drummed on the tannery roof, soft as fingers.

"Nine," Corvane said.

"Two missing, then. Or five. Or more, depending on how long that door stood open." Brassa set her lantern down on a barrel with great care, the way one sets down a thing one intends to pick up again, and looked at him with no hatred at all—which was somehow worse than hatred, because hatred would have meant she'd already decided, and she hadn't decided yet. She was a careful woman. She was checking her arithmetic. "Ser, I've served under you four years. I've watched you interrogate a grandmother until she cried and then buy her bread on the way home. I've watched you lose prisoners you had cornered. I've watched you follow trails that went cold exactly when they warmed. I've told myself it was competence. I've told myself it was mercy, even, and that mercy in an Inquisitor is a kind of discipline." She drew a slow breath. "But I've never watched you stand in front of an open door with your back to it before."

The twelve fugitives had gone absolutely still, listening, understanding that the shape of their lives was being decided in a language they couldn't quite follow.

Corvane looked at his lieutenant—at the ambitious, grinding, careful woman who had followed him loyally for four years on the strength of a version of him that had never existed—and found, strangely, that he was tired. Tired all the way down, tired in the marrow, tired of arithmetic and lanterns and the sound of fire-oil canisters clinking.

"No," he agreed quietly. "You haven't."

He reached down and unlocked the iron cuff on his right wrist.

The sound it made was very small. A click, a whisper of metal, a bracelet of twenty years falling open. But the chamber changed around it—the air itself changed, thickened, warmed—and every gifted person against that wall felt it, and every one of them flinched, and then, one by one, stopped flinching, because what poured off him was not power in any shape they'd been taught to fear. It was only warmth. Only the small human heat he had spent his life smothering, rising off his bare wrist like breath on a winter morning.

Brassa stared at his wrist. At the pale band of skin beneath, unmarked, unburned, alive.

"You're one of them," she whispered.

"I'm one of *us*," Corvane said. "All of us. Every person in this room. That's the secret at the bottom of everything, Lieutenant—it's the only secret, and the Chancery has killed thousands of people to keep it, and I am done helping them." He held her gaze. "Now you have a choice, and I won't insult you by pretending it's an easy one. Report me, and you'll be decorated, and you'll spend the rest of your career leading raids like this one and telling yourself the same stories you've been telling yourself about me. Or you walk out of here and report that the raid succeeded, that the enclave was cleared, that Inquisitor Corvane is pursuing the remainder along the river road—and you buy these twelve people the night they need to scatter."

"And which story do I tell myself afterward?"

"The true one," he said. "It's heavier. But it holds."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and the small wet drip of water somewhere in the dark tunnels, and Corvane understood that he was standing at the exact border of his old life and whatever came next, and that the crossing would not feel like triumph. It would feel like this: quiet, terrified, irrevocable.

Brassa bent and picked up her lantern.

"River road," she said. "You pursued them along the river road. I lost you in the reeds at mile-marker seven." Her voice did not shake. "God forgive me, Corvane. I hope you know what you're doing."

"I don't," he said honestly. "That's how I know it's right."

She turned and shouted orders—*chamber cleared, form up, move out*—and the wardens splashed away toward the light, and the twelve fugitives watched them go with the expressions of people who had been reprieved by a verdict they didn't understand, and Corvane stood alone in the lamplight with one cuff hanging open and one still locked, half-free, half-chained, which was, he supposed, the most honest description of him that anyone would ever manage.

He was reaching to close the second cuff—to keep the disguise intact a few hours longer, just until he reached the river road—when Brassa appeared in the tunnel mouth again, alone, her face drained white in the lantern-glow.

"Ser," she said, and for the first time all night her voice did shake. "The dispatch rider just came down from the Chancery. There's a warrant." She swallowed. "A second name's been added to the hunt rolls tonight. Written in red ink, same as the gifted. They're calling it irregular. They're calling it unprecedented."

She looked at him the way one looks at a man already dead, and spoke the name anyway:

"Yours."
