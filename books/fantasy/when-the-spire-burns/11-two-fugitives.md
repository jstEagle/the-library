## 11. Two Fugitives

Corvane had been tracking her for six days, and in those six days he had learned more about Paloma Sterling than the hunt rolls contained.

He knew she slept little and ate less. He knew she doubled back through her own trail like a fox circling its earth, that she had a smuggler's instinct for water and a scribe's compulsion for ink—he'd found the charred stub of a candle beside a scrap of parchment where she'd copied something from the Codex, her hand small and precise even under pressure. He knew she was heading east, toward the Spire, though he could not yet prove it, and he suspected she would deny it even to herself.

He followed alone. That was the part that would have puzzled his lieutenant, had Lieutenant Harsk still been speaking to him. An Inquisitor of the Crown did not pursue fugitives alone; he brought wardens, iron nets, a writ with enough seals on it to stop a river. But Corvane had spent eleven days since the enclave raid being watched by his own people, and he had come to understand that the writ he carried was no longer worth the vellum it was written on. Somewhere between Vellamark and the eastern hills, his authority had quietly died, and what remained was just a man with iron cuffs and a horse he didn't trust, following a woman who should have been in chains three provinces ago.

The cuffs sat heavy beneath his coat. He felt them the way a man feels a tooth gone bad—constantly, and most when he tried to forget them.

On the sixth evening, he found her fire.

It was a careful fire, banked low in a hollow beneath an overhang of grey stone, smokeless by any craft he recognized—and then he did recognize it, and his stomach turned over slowly, like a man reading his own name in a list of the dead. Charm-work. She was using charm-work to hide her fire, folding the heat back on itself so the smoke never rose. A small thing. A crime, once. The kind of thing he had burned men for.

He dismounted at the lip of the hollow and stood there in the failing light with his hand on his sword hilt, and said, "Scribe Sterling."

She came up off the ground faster than he'd expected, a knife already in her fist—the knife was new, Tamsin's work presumably—and the Ember Codex strapped against her ribs where he could see the outline of it through her coat. Her face in the firelight was thinner than it had been in the temple archives. There was ash in her hair.

"Inquisitor Corvane." Her voice was steady. He gave her credit for that. "Come alone?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters whether there are eleven men behind you or none."

"There are none," he said. And then, because lying to her had begun to taste wrong somewhere around the enclave raid: "There were supposed to be."

* * *

Paloma had known he was coming for two days.

Tamsin's people had taught her to read a trail backward—to see where a horse had grazed, where a man had slept, how long ago, how tired. Corvane rode hard and slept badly and left a trail like a signature: disciplined, legible, increasingly desperate. She could have run east faster. She hadn't. She told herself it was because running blind in the dark broke ankles. She told herself a great many things.

So when his voice came down into her hollow, she was almost relieved. Fear, held too long, curdles into something worse than fear. At least now it had a shape and a name and a face she'd seen across an interrogation table, a man who had looked at Ivet's screaming and flinched before he mastered it. She had remembered that flinch. She had hated herself for remembering it kindly.

"You're under arrest," he said, and descended into the hollow, and even as he said it his eyes went to her hands—not to the knife, to her hands, to the faint warmth-glow that rose in her fingertips when she was afraid. She saw him see it. She saw him know that she saw.

"Am I," she said.

"Scribe Sterling, you are charged with theft of a crown relic, unlawful practice of—" His voice caught on the liturgy of it. She watched him hear himself, watch the words come out of him sounding exactly like the words he must have heard spoken over him once, in some room like this one, when he was young and terrified and hiding the same warmth in his own fingertips behind iron. "Unlawful practice of charm-craft," he finished, flatly. "And arson of the temple reliquary alarm."

"The alarm set itself on fire. I take no responsibility for its temperament."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. She catalogued it the way she catalogued everything now, hoarding small true things against the great false history she carried strapped to her ribs.

"Give me the Codex," he said. "And I'll speak for you. It won't save you. But it will make the difference between a trial and a pyre."

"And if I don't?"

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

That was when the arrows came.

* * *

They came out of the dark above the hollow without horns, without challenge, without writs—which was how Paloma knew, even as she threw herself flat behind the stone, that these were not lawful men. Crown wardens announced themselves. That was the whole theater of it, the seals and the shouting; the law wanted witnesses. Whatever fell upon them now wanted only silence.

An arrow buried itself in the earth where Corvane had stood. He was moving before the second shaft landed, dragging her by the arm deeper into the shadow of the overhang, and she had one absurd instant of noticing that his grip on her wrist avoided the fingertips entirely, avoided the gift-marked skin, as though even now he would not touch her magic uninvited.

"Wardens?" she gasped.

"Not wardens." His face was pale and set. "Rogue ones. Harsk's. Or what's left of his company after the enclave—they've been following *me*, waiting to see—" He cut himself off as a boot scraped the rim of the hollow above. "They think I'm a traitor."

"Are you?"

"I don't have time to answer that."

Four of them came down, maybe five, moving with the ugly economy of soldiers who had stopped believing in anything except each other. Paloma pressed herself into the rock and felt the old terror rise, the temple-terror, the testing-day terror—and felt, rising underneath it, something else. Warmth. Her gift answering fear the way it always had, the heat crawling up from her palms, begging to be used.

She had spent her whole life not using it.

She used it.

The darkness itself seemed to lean. She reached for the nearest warden's torch-flame and pulled—gently, gently, the way Mother Sefu had taught her to coax a stubborn candle—and the flame bent toward her like a child called home, leapt the gap between wick and air, and wrapped the man's sleeve in fire that burned nothing but his courage. He screamed and dropped his bow and beat at a fire that was not burning him, because it wasn't burning anything, it was only *fear made visible*, and men do not stand in fear made visible for long.

Beside her, Corvane went very still.

Then he tore the iron cuff from his left wrist.

She heard the chain snap rather than unlock—he'd weakened it, gods knew how long ago, all those nights riding alone—and the sound it made was small and enormous, a birdcage door swinging open in an empty house. What came out of him was not fire. It was colder than that and stranger: a stillness that spread from him like frost forming on glass, and the second warden, mid-lunge with a blade raised, simply *stopped*, frozen mid-motion, held as if the world had forgotten him. Corvane's gift, she understood dimly, was not ice but suspension—the halting of things. He had worn iron all his life to keep from stopping the world.

The third warden fled. The fourth lay groaning where Corvane's sword had found him, and the fifth—there had been a fifth—was running east into the dark, and would carry word to whoever was left to carry word to.

Silence came back into the hollow slowly, like water filling a footprint.

Paloma sat down abruptly on a stone. Her hands were shaking. The warmth drained out of them and left the ordinary ache behind, the cost, always the cost. Across the dead fire, Corvane stood looking at his bare wrists as if they belonged to a stranger—as if, she thought, a man might wear chains so long that freedom feels like a wound.

"You froze him," she said. Her voice sounded far away to herself.

"Yes."

"That's your gift. That's what the cuffs were for."

He said nothing. He knelt and began, methodically, to gather the dropped weapons, snapping bows, scattering arrowheads into the grass. Housekeeping for violence. She recognized the ritual; she did the same with her own guilt.

"They'll report it," she said. "Both of us. Your gift, my—everything. There's no going back to them now."

"No," he said. "There isn't." He said it the way a man reads his own sentence, and then he looked up at her across the ruined campfire, this hunted, exhausted, dangerous man, and asked her the question she would turn over for many nights afterward: "Is there anywhere to go *forward*?"

* * *

They rebuilt the fire together, which was its own strange liturgy—she coaxing the flame awake with fingers that still trembled, he feeding it wood with his bare, unbound hands, both of them moving carefully around each other like animals deciding, provisionally, not to fight.

The truce, when it finally took shape, took shape in fragments.

"You could have run," he said. "I tracked you badly on purpose for the first two days. You'd have seen it. You're better at trails than I am."

"I know," she said. "I stayed anyway."

"Why?"

She fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Because running from you has started to feel like running from the only other person alive who knows what the inside of these chains is like." She nodded at his wrists. "You wore iron for twenty years."

"Nineteen."

"Nineteen years." She shook her head slowly. "I numbed my hands in ice water twice a year and thought I was carrying something heavy. You carried yours on your body every day of your life. Every meal. Every prayer. Every—" She stopped, because his face had closed like a door.

"Don't," he said quietly.

"Why not?"

"Because if you say it kindly I will believe you, and I can't afford—" He stopped too. The fire cracked and settled. Above the hollow, the sky was doing its enormous indifferent work, stars wheeling, clouds passing, and somewhere far to the east, invisible from here but visible from the low roads, from Tamsin's flooded tunnels, from every high window in Vellamark, the dragon-fire guttered above the ruined Spire like a dying star.

"The Codex says the covenant needed memory," Paloma said at last, because someone had to break the silence and she was tired of being brave in interesting ways. "Human memory, freely given, for dragon-fire. The first king took the fire and paid nothing. Everything since—the outlawry, the hunts, the burnings—it's all one long theft, passed down like a crown." She looked at him. "Your superiors ordered you to burn the archive. Did you?"

"One page survived." He stared into the fire. "I copied it first. Then I burned the rest. Then I lied about it in my report, and the lie felt exactly like putting the cuff back on."

"What was on the page?"

"A list of names. Gifted people, registered in the first king's time. Not criminals." His jaw worked. "*Contributors*. They gave their memories to keep the flame lit. Voluntarily. It was an honor, once. It was a covenant, and we made it a crime, and I enforced the making." He laughed, short and terrible. "Do you know what I felt when I read that page? Relief. Relief, that I wasn't the only monster in the archive. The first king got there before me."

"You weren't a monster," Paloma said. "You were a man in irons enforcing irons. Those aren't the same thing, whatever the Inquisition taught you."

"And what does that make me tonight? I let eleven people out a door. I broke my own chain. I froze a crown warden mid-strike like a—" His voice frayed. "Every step away from them, I tell myself it's toward something. Justice. Truth. The covenant reforged. And every step, some quiet clerk in the back of my skull writes it down as desertion."

"Let him write it," Paloma said. "Clerks are wrong about important things all the time. I was one."

That startled a real laugh out of him—brief, rusty, gone almost before it arrived, but real. She found she wanted to hear it again, which was a dangerous want, and she filed it away with all the others.

* * *

Later, when the fire had settled to coals and neither of them slept, he spoke again from his side of the hollow.

"The fifth one ran east," he said. "By morning, Harsk will know where we are. And he won't come with four men this time."

"Then we move before dawn."

"Together?" He said it carefully, the word balanced on his palm like something that might bite.

Paloma looked at him across the coals—at the raw, pale bands of skin where nineteen years of iron had lived, at the man who had flinched at Ivet's screaming and copied one page before burning the rest and let eleven strangers out a door. Enemies discovered a shared wound; that was the whole of it, and it was not nothing, and it was perhaps the beginning of everything.

"Together," she said. "But I keep the Codex."

"I wouldn't have it any other way." He paused. "Scribe Sterling. When we reach the Spire—if we reach the Spire—the Codex will name its price. Have you read that far?"

She had. She had read that far alone, by candlelight, three nights ago, and had lain awake inventorying everything she loved with the terrible diligence of a debtor listing assets. Her mother's voice. Ivet's laugh. The night market. Her own name.

"I've read that far," she said.

And far to the east, above the black line of the hills, the dying star of dragon-fire flickered—once, twice—and for a single heartbeat, burned brighter than it had in a generation. As if it had heard. As if it was waiting.
