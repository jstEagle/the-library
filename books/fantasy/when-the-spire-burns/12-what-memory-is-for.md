## 12. What Memory Is For

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width of wax by the time Paloma found the passage, and she did not notice until the flame guttered and nearly died against the damp air of Tamsin's smuggler hold. She cupped her hands around it—hands that still remembered how to coax a spark into loyalty—and fed it a drop from the second bottle of tallow oil, the one Tamsin had grudgingly surrendered with the words *waste it and you owe me*.

The Ember Codex lay open on the crate before her, its pages warm in a way that had nothing to do with firelight. She had stopped trying to explain that warmth to herself three nights ago. The vellum was not merely old; it was *alive* in some slow, vegetal way, breathing out a heat like a sleeping animal, and when she read aloud—as she had begun doing without deciding to—the letters seemed to lean toward her voice like plants toward sun.

She had been reading for hours. Her eyes ached. The hold around her smelled of bilgewater, tar, dried fish, and beneath it all a faint mineral scent she now recognized as dragon-fire's residue—the same smell that clung to the ruined Spire on windy days, the smell every child in Vellamark was taught to wrinkle their nose at. She no longer wrinkled her nose at it. It smelled, she thought, like truth. Like something that had never learned how to lie.

And then she came to the passage.

It was written in the older hand—the first hand, the covenant-hand, the script the temple called *First Tongue* and taught only to scribes who would never need it. Paloma had needed it. Debt had made her learn everything in the archive worth learning, because knowledge was the only currency the poor were permitted to hoard.

*Let the terms be thus,* the Codex said, in letters that shifted like embers settling.

*When the realms were sundered and the bridge between them was raised, the fire of Ashkarat flowed into the world of men, and by that fire the memory of the city was kept: every name, every grief, every kindness done in small rooms. In exchange, the realm of men gave up to the flame that which fire cannot hold alone—a willing bearer, who shall carry into the Spire the sum of his dearest remembering, and give it freely to the burning.*

*Fire without memory is mere destruction. Memory without fire is mere forgetting. Bound together, they are what mortals call a soul.*

Paloma read it again. Then again. Then she read it the way she read debt contracts, line by line, hunting for the escape clause, the asterisk, the footnote in smaller script that would let her off.

There was none.

*A willing bearer.* Not a strong one. Not a gifted one. Not a chosen one. A *willing* one—who must climb the Spire carrying the dearest of what they are, and hand it over.

She sat back on the crate and the hold swayed gently beneath her, the tide turning somewhere below the floorboards, and she thought, with a strange floating calm: *I already knew this.*

Because she had known it the way you know a thing in a dream and wake having lost it. Somewhere in the archive, in the ledger margins, in the forged histories she had spent weeks unpicking, there had been a shape she hadn't let herself see. A price mentioned once and then sanded smooth by a generation of careful temple hands, the way you sand down a splintered banister so no one cuts themselves on the truth.

The first king had taken the fire and given nothing. That was the theft. That was the wound the whole world was bleeding from. And the cure was—

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with detached interest, as though they belonged to another scribe across a reading desk.

The cure was exactly the thing the first king had refused to do. Someone had to give.

---

She did not sleep. She told herself she would rest, and instead she took the stub of charcoal Tamsin kept for marking crates, and a sheet of packing paper, and she began to write.

Not the covenant terms. Not yet. First, something else—an inventory. Because if she was going to pay a price, she refused, absolutely refused, to pay it blindly. Mother Sefu had taught her that much and it was the one lesson that had never curdled: *never sign what you have not read to the last line, little wren. Debts hide in the margins.*

So she wrote at the top of the page, in her neat temple hand: **WHAT I WOULD LOSE.**

And beneath it, the list began, and did not stop.

**My mother's voice.**

That was the first thing, and it came out of her pen before she had decided anything, the way blood comes before thought. Her mother, dead nine years, who sang while kneading bread—badly, cheerfully, sliding off the tune and climbing back on like a woman mounting a stubborn horse. Paloma could still hear it. She could hear it *now*, in the dark hold, the off-key rise of it, the flour-dusted hum between verses. Nine years since she'd heard it aloud, and it lived in her anyway, self-sustaining, a lamp that had never gone out.

If she gave it to the flame, the lamp went out. Not dimmed. *Out*. She would stand in a kitchen someday—if there were kitchens anymore, if there were somedays—and watch a woman kneading bread, and feel nothing but mild recognition that bread required kneading.

She wrote: *I will lose the song. I will lose knowing that she sang. I may lose knowing that she existed at all.*

The charcoal trembled on that last word. She forced herself to finish it. If she flinched here, she'd flinch on the Spire, and the Codex had been very clear about willingness. A gift given with the weight still attached was perhaps no gift at all.

**Ivet.**

Her pen hesitated, then moved.

Ivet, who had failed the ward-test and screamed Paloma's name as they dragged her through the temple gates—Ivet, whose laugh had been the loudest thing in the scriptorium, a laugh that started in her belly and scandalized the novices and once made old Brother Callum drop an entire tray of ink pots. Ivet, who was either dead or in the Inquisition's cells, and whom Paloma had not been able to save, and whose name she carried now like a stone sewn into her coat lining.

*I will lose her laugh,* Paloma wrote. *I will lose the sound of my name in her mouth. I will lose the guilt too—which frightens me more than losing the laugh, because the guilt is the last piece of her I still hold, and I am not sure I deserve to put it down even if the fire asks nicely.*

She stared at that line a long time. Grief rehearsed in advance has a strange texture: you weep for a thing that hasn't happened, and the tears are real, and the thing remains un-happened, and you must do it all again tomorrow, and the day after, right up until the day it stops being rehearsal.

**The night market.**

The button-child. The smell of fried dough and wet wool. The lamplighter working his way down Rope Street with his long taper, lighting the city one flame at a time, never knowing he was performing the same office as the dragons. The old man who sold chipped teacups and swore each one was lucky. Her own stall, her own small charms sold under the counter like contraband, her fingertips warming when she worked them, warmth she had hidden since childhood the way other children hid stolen fruit.

*I will lose the feeling of being good at something small,* she wrote. *Anyone can save the world, maybe. But I was good at charms. At easing a headache, at making a lock trust me, at keeping soup warm an extra hour. If the memories go, does the skill go? Does the wanting?*

She didn't know. The Codex didn't say. That was the cruelty of covenants written by people who assumed the reader would be noble enough not to ask about refunds.

**Mother Sefu.**

Here her pen actually stopped. She set it down. She got up and walked the length of the hold twice, past the stacked crates and the coiled rope and the shuttered lantern, and came back, and picked the pen up again.

Because Mother Sefu was not a simple grief. Mother Sefu had covered her escape at terrible cost and was now—Tamsin's runners said—alive but broken in an Inquisitor's keeping. Mother Sefu had also, Paloma had begun to suspect with a sickness she couldn't yet look at directly, known more about the Codex than she had ever said. Had perhaps helped write the lie Paloma was now risking her life to correct.

Could you love someone and be furious with them in the same breath? Yes. Paloma was living proof. And if she climbed the Spire and gave her dearest memories, she would lose both at once—the love and the fury, the betrayal and the forgiveness she might someday have managed. She would lose the *problem* of Mother Sefu, and problems, once lost, cannot be resolved. They simply evaporate, taking their answers with them.

*I don't know what to write here,* she wrote finally, which was the most honest entry on the page.

**My own name.**

She wrote it last, and writing it felt like standing at the edge of a high place. Paloma Sterling. Temple scribe, third register, debt outstanding. Daughter of Maren Sterling, baker, deceased. Friend of Ivet Vasska, whereabouts unknown. Charm-seller of the night market, unlicensed, unwelcome, unbowed.

Who was she without any of it? The Codex had an answer, and it was the answer of fires and ancient bargains: whoever remained after the giving was the part that had always been hers—not inherited, not owed, not borrowed. The ember under the ash.

But the Codex had also said, two pages earlier, in a passage she'd skimmed and now returned to with cold fingers:

*Let no bearer believe the flame takes only what is named. Memory is a braid; pull one strand and the others loosen. The bearer gives her dearest remembering, and finds that the dear things were holding up the ordinary ones. She may keep her name and lose everyone who ever spoke it. This is the nature of the price, and it cannot be bargained down.*

She read that paragraph four times.

Then she blew out the candle, because she could not bear to see the page clearly anymore, and sat in the dark with the Codex breathing its slow animal warmth beside her, and let herself do the thing she had forbidden herself to do until now.

She imagined it done.

---

In the imagining, she stood at the summit of the Spire, and the flame was not cruel—that surprised her—it was almost gentle, the way a mother's hand is gentle when it takes a splinter. And it asked, and she gave, and her mother's song went first, unraveling out of her like thread from a spool, and she felt it leave and felt the hole where it had been, smooth-edged already, already healing wrong.

Then Ivet. Then the market. Then—

And here the imagination failed her, as it was supposed to, as perhaps the Codex intended. Because she reached for what remained and could not find it. Who stood on the summit if not the daughter, the friend, the charm-seller, the debtor? Some bare creature. Some ember. Was an ember enough? Was an ember *anyone*?

A floorboard creaked above her. Tamsin, pacing, or one of the enclave's sentries walking the roofline. The hold settled around her like a large patient animal.

Paloma lit the candle again. Her eyes were wet; she let them stay that way for exactly ten breaths, a discipline Mother Sefu had drilled into every novice—*grief is permitted, wren, but it keeps office hours*—and then she bent back over the page.

At the bottom of her inventory, beneath **MY OWN NAME**, she added a final heading, and her hand was steadier than it had been all night, and she did not examine why:

**WHAT THE CITY LOSES IF NO ONE CLIMBS.**

And the list wrote itself, because she had read the ledgers, she had seen the margins, she knew.

*The names of the dead, fading from gravestones faster than weather can explain.*
*Old women forgetting their own weddings mid-ceremony of telling them.*
*The recipe for plague-bread from the Famine Years, lost last winter, no one knows where.*
*Children born unable to remember their mothers' faces by their third year.*
*The city's memory guttering like the dragon-fire itself—because they are the same fire, and it is dying of what the first king stole, and every year it burns lower, and every year Vellamark forgets a little more, and calls it age, and calls it sorrow, and builds no pyre.*

She looked at the two lists side by side. What she would lose. What everyone would lose.

They were not equal. Nothing in the covenant had ever pretended they were equal. But one of them was hers to choose, and the other was happening whether she chose or not, and that difference—that single, unbearable difference—was the whole argument, and it was over.

Paloma folded the paper into quarters and slid it inside the front cover of the Ember Codex, where it fit as if the book had been waiting for it, the pages sighing shut around it with something that sounded horribly like satisfaction.

"You're a greedy old thing," she whispered to it.

The vellum warmed under her palm, which was either agreement or comfort, and she honestly could not tell the difference, and suspected that was the point.

She slept at last, two hours before dawn, and dreamed of nothing—which frightened her more than any nightmare, a small dry rehearsal of the silence to come.

---

She woke to boots on the ladder and Tamsin's face appearing through the hatch, grey in the early light, wearing an expression Paloma had learned to dread in the weeks since the theft: the expression of a woman carrying news she has already decided not to soften.

"Two things," Tamsin said, dropping onto the crate opposite, uninvited, smelling of river mud and gunpowder-adjacent things Paloma didn't want to identify. "One: your Inquisitor's been spotted on the north bank. Corvane. Alone, no escort, iron cuffs still on, asking after a scribe in a way that says he doesn't want to find you so much as he wants to *not* report finding you. Make of that what you like."

Paloma's heart performed a complicated maneuver. "And two?"

Tamsin reached into her coat and produced a folded scrap of paper, cheap stock, temple watermark. "Runner brought it from a contact in the wardens' barracks. Says it's meant for you by name." She held it out but didn't release it when Paloma took hold, and their eyes met over the paper. "Says it came from the temple cells. From someone dying."

The hold went very quiet. Even the Codex seemed to hold its breath, its warmth pressing against Paloma's hip through her coat like a hand.

"Mother Sefu," Paloma said. It wasn't a question. There was only one person in those cells whose death would be considered newsworthy, only one person whose dying would be arranged into a message.

"She doesn't have long," Tamsin said. "And the person who carried this says the guards change at dawn, and dawn," she glanced at the grey light leaking through the hatch, "was twenty minutes ago."

Paloma unfolded the note. Three words, in a hand she had known since she was seven years old, shaky now, pressed hard into the paper as though the writer feared running out of strength mid-stroke:

*Come. Before forgetting.*

She looked at the Codex. She thought of her inventory folded inside it, the lists side by side, the price already counted and accepted. She thought of a broken old woman in a cell who might be the only person alive who could tell her what the giving actually *did*—who might have seen it, or caused it, or barely survived it once before.

"I need a way across the river," Paloma said, standing, "and I need it fast."

Tamsin smiled without a scrap of humor in it. "Funny," she said. "That's the second time today someone's asked me for the impossible before breakfast."
