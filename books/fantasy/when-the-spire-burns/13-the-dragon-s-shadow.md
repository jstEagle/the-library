## 13. The Dragon's Shadow

The Spire's base was nothing like Corvane had imagined in the years he had walked past it on official business, head bowed, cuffs cold at his wrists. From the streets above it was a stump, a monument to a victory the crown never let anyone forget—charred stone rising from a plaza of polished marble where tourists and penitents alike came to look at what human will had done to dragon-kind. He had stood there a hundred times as an Inquisitor, keeping order among the crowds, feeling nothing but the weight of his office.

From below—from the flooded root-chambers where Tamsin's smugglers had ferried them in a leaking skiff through water that glowed faintly, like milk touched by lightning—it was something else entirely. It was a wound that had never been allowed to close.

The stone here was not stone. It had been flesh once, or something like flesh: the Spire's root was a vast organic lattice, blackened and cracked, veins of dead ember running through it like frozen lightning. Where Paloma's candle threw its small brave light, the walls seemed to breathe—or to remember breathing. The air tasted of cinders and old rain and something older still, something that made the back of his teeth ache, the way a tuning fork aches when its note is sounded somewhere out of sight.

Paloma stood close beside him, close enough that he could feel the warmth she carried—the gift she had spent a lifetime hiding, radiating off her now without shame. She had stopped hiding it somewhere on the road between the enclave and this place. He envied her that, and hated himself for envying it.

"Do you feel that?" she whispered.

"I've felt it my whole life," he said. "I just never knew what I was feeling."

The chamber opened around them into a hollow vast enough to swallow the temple entire. And in the center of it, coiled around the Spire's root like a moth around a guttering lamp, lay the last dragon of Ashkarat.

* * *

Vessharran was dying. That was the first thing Corvane understood, though no one had said it aloud—not Tamsin with her smuggler's bluntness, not the enclave elders with their careful warnings. You simply knew it, the way you know a dying man across a sickroom, by some animal arithmetic of breath and stillness.

The dragon was enormous beyond sense—his body filled the chamber the way grief fills a house—and yet he seemed thin, diminished, his scales gone dull as old pewter, half of them flaked away to reveal hide beneath that looked like cooled lava, gray and fissured. His wings were folded tight against his flanks, and they trembled slightly, continuously, like a bird's wings in rain. One eye was clouded white. The other opened as they approached, and it was the color of the Ember Codex's pages when Paloma held them near flame: gold shot through with red, ancient fire banked down to embers.

Corvane had spent eleven years telling himself dragons were beasts. Clever beasts, dangerous beasts, but beasts—creatures of appetite and territory, worthy of caution and unworthy of grief. He had built his career on that foundation. The crown's whole architecture rested on it.

Then Vessharran looked at him, and Corvane understood that everything he had ever believed about dragons was a story told by thieves about their victims.

"You bring iron into my house," the dragon said.

His voice did not come through the air so much as through the stone, through the water underfoot, through the bones of Corvane's own skull—a voice felt rather than heard, patient and vast and very tired.

Corvane's hand went instinctively to his sword hilt. Paloma's hand went to his arm.

"He doesn't mean harm," she said. "Vessharran—Inquisitor Dain Corvane. He—" She faltered, searching for how to say what Corvane was, and Corvane found he could not help her, because he did not know anymore either.

"Iron," the dragon repeated, and the white eye turned toward the cuffs at Corvane's wrists. "Eleven years of iron. Do you know what iron does, little warden? It does not silence a gift. Nothing can silence a gift. Iron only teaches it to scream inward."

Corvane's mouth had gone dry. "You know me."

"I know your shape." The gold eye regarded him with an expression Corvane could only call sorrowful. "I have watched you since you were a boy kneeling in this plaza while they pinned the cuffs on you. I watched you learn to fold yourself smaller every year. I have watched many such ones. You are the crown's favorite kind of weapon—the kind that cuts itself."

Paloma made a small sound beside him, half protest, half recognition. Corvane did not look at her. Looking at her would mean admitting how precisely the words had landed.

"We came to ask," Paloma said, steadier now. "The Codex says the covenant can be reforged. It says someone has to climb. We came to ask if it's true—if it can work. If it's worth—"

"Ask?" Something moved through the great body, a shudder like distant thunder. "Child, I have waited thirty years for someone to ask. But asking is not enough. The flame does not take questions up the Spire. It takes payment." The gold eye fixed on Paloma. "And you—you have already counted your price. I see it behind your eyes like coins laid out on a table."

Paloma's face went carefully blank. Corvane recognized the expression; he wore it himself in every interrogation that cost him something. She had inventoried her losses already. Her mother's voice. Ivet's laugh. He had heard her recite them once, in the dark after the wardens' ambush, thinking he slept—as though naming them could make them lighter.

"And him?" she asked. "What does he pay?"

"That is not mine to decide," said Vessharran. "That is his. But first—he must see."

* * *

"See what?" Corvane asked, and the word had barely left his mouth before the world dissolved.

There was no transition. One moment he stood ankle-deep in glowing water beside a woman he had betrayed his oath for; the next he stood in sunlight, in a palace he had only seen in portraits and official processions—the first king's palace, new-built, its marble still bright, its banners stiff with dye. And he was not himself. He was looking through eyes that were not his own, standing in a body that moved with a confidence Corvane had never possessed, and he understood with sickening clarity whose memory this was.

He was inside the first king.

He felt the king's hunger as if it were his own—which was the horror of it, because it fit him too well. The king stood at a window overlooking the young city of Vellamark and felt not gratitude but arithmetic. The dragons had given the realm fire that healed, fire that remembered, fire that kept the harvests true and the rivers clean and the minds of men unclouded by rot or forgetting. In exchange the covenant demanded mutuality: each year, a human offering of memory freely given, poured into the flame at the Spire's summit, so that the dragons might know human hearts and the fire might stay fed.

And the king—Corvane felt it rising in the man's chest like nausea, like greed, like a fever he recognized from every interrogation room where he had ever watched power devour a weaker thing—the king had decided that giving was for subjects. Kings took.

The memory moved. Corvane was carried along inside it, powerless, a passenger in a thief's body. He saw the king climb the Spire with a company of mages bound by oaths later expunged from every record. He saw the summit chamber, saw the flame—alive then, towering, warm as a hearth, welcoming, trusting. The flame had spoken to the king in the voice of an old friend. *What do you bring?*

The king brought chains. Crafted in secret, forged with the same iron that would one day be clamped on a boy's wrists in a plaza—iron that taught gifts to scream inward. The mages cast them over the flame itself, and the flame cried out—a sound Corvane felt in his own chest, a sound like every voice he had ever silenced in an interrogation cell, all at once—and the king reached into the fire and took.

He took the fire's heart. He drew it down the Spire in vessels of glass and lead, and he distributed it through the city in pipes and wards and hidden channels, feeding the temple's lamps, the crown's protections, the quiet magic that kept Vellamark's records true and its people's memories intact—all of it stolen, all of it running on a heart that had been ripped from a living flame and never once paid for.

And the king gave nothing back. Not memory. Not offering. Not truth. Instead he wrote the lie—Corvane watched the man's own hand do it, ink flowing smooth, the forged history that every child learned: *the dragons grew proud, the dragons demanded worship, the dragons were driven back in righteous war.* He wrote it in a fair hand and sealed it with the royal signet, and he slept well that night, and Corvane, trapped inside the sleeping thief's mind, wanted to scream.

But the memory was not finished. It saved its cruelest part for last, because the dragon showing it was dying and had no time left for mercy.

The king returned to the summit once more, alone, years later. The chained flame had shrunk to something small and dim, and it spoke to him with what remained of its strength, and it did not curse him. That was the worst part. It asked him a question:

*When you give nothing, what are you?*

And the king—who had taken a realm's worth of fire and written himself a righteous history and never once lost sleep—felt, for a single instant, a flicker of the answer. Felt the hollowness where a self should be. Felt what Corvane had felt every morning for eleven years, waking with iron on his wrists and a badge on his chest and a voice in his head whispering the same question in different words: *when you suppress everything you are, what remains?*

The king answered the question the way cowards answer it. He climbed down, and he ordered the Spire burned, and he told the realm it was a triumph.

* * *

Corvane came back to himself on his knees in the water, gasping, his vision swimming. Paloma's hands were on his shoulders. When had he fallen? How long—

"How long was I gone?"

"A moment," Vessharran said. "A lifetime. They are often the same length."

Corvane pushed himself upright. His whole body shook. He had interrogated murderers and seen them weep; he had always told himself their tears were weakness exposed, guilt surfacing. Now he understood that sometimes tears were simply the sound a man makes when he finally sees clearly.

"You showed me the theft," he rasped. "Why show me? I'm the crown's hound. I burned archives. I dragged a scribe named Ivet—" His voice caught. "I've done their work. Why waste your dying strength on me?"

"Because you are the only kind of person who can carry this truth up the Spire," the dragon said, "and survive carrying it. A believer would break. An unbeliever would not bother to climb. But you—" the gold eye narrowed, and there was something almost like humor in it, grim and old, "—you have spent your whole life doing the wrong thing for reasons you could not name. Guilt is a compass, little warden. Yours points true, even if you have spent years walking against the needle."

Paloma was watching him with an expression he couldn't bear—pity, and underneath the pity, something warmer, more dangerous. "Dain," she said quietly. It was the first time she had used his given name. "The cuffs."

"What?"

"Take them off."

His hands went to the iron reflexively, the way they always did—to check, to confirm, to hold the scream inside. Eleven years of habit. "If I take them off and the gift comes back full force, untrained, after all this time—"

"You will not die," Vessharran said. "I am old, but I am not cruel, and you stand in my house under truce. But I will tell you what will happen, so that you choose with open eyes." The dragon's voice softened, which should not have been possible for a voice that size, and yet it did, becoming almost gentle. "The iron comes off, and you will feel everything you have suppressed since you were nine years old. All of it at once. Every feeling you ever numbed. It will feel like falling. It has felt like falling for everyone I have ever watched do it. And the ones who survive the falling—they do not land. They learn that they were never falling at all. They were only standing still for the first time."

Corvane looked at the cuffs. Plain iron, government-stamped, worn smooth at the edges by his own skin. He had hated them for eleven years and defended them for eleven years, because defending them was easier than asking what it meant that he needed them.

He thought of Mother Sefu, broken in her cell, saying the one true sentence she had left: *the covenant was ours to break.* He thought of the page he had copied from the archive, folded now against his heart, smuggled like contraband. He thought of the eleven people who had walked free through a door he had failed to guard, and of his lieutenant's face as she watched him fail.

"When you give nothing," the flame had asked the king, "what are you?"

"I'll tell you what I am," Corvane said, and his voice came out low and steady, surprising him. "I'm a man who enforced a lie for eleven years because the lie was safer than the truth. That's what I am. And I'm done being it."

He unlocked the cuffs.

They fell from his wrists and struck the stone, and the sound they made was small—absurdly small, two pieces of metal on wet rock—but Corvane heard it echo through the root-chamber like a bell, like a verdict, like the first drop of rain after a drought that has lasted a generation.

And then the falling began.

It came up through him like floodwater through a cellar—everything at once, everything he had ever ironed flat. Grief for his mother, dead these twelve years, whom he had not allowed himself to mourn because mourning felt like disobedience. Terror from his ninth year, the day the testers came to his village school and his gift had flared visible and his father had looked at him afterward the way one looks at a stranger. Shame—oceans of it, every interrogation, every screaming scribe, Ivet's face, Ivet's voice crying his title as they dragged her. Joy, too, which was somehow worse: joy he had refused for a decade, the taste of rain, laughter in taverns he had entered only on duty, the warmth of another person's hand. All of it rose in him at once and he fell to both knees again and he wept—not the controlled weeping of confession, but the ugly, helpless weeping of a dam breaking, and Paloma knelt with him and held on, and he let her, because he had forgotten how to refuse.

He did not die. He had half expected to die. Instead, something in him that had been clenched for eleven years slowly, painfully, began to unclench, like a fist remembering it was also a hand.

When he could speak again, the chamber was dimmer—the candle lower, the ember-veins in the walls pulsing faintly, as if the Spire's root had quickened at the sound of the cuffs hitting stone. Vessharran's gold eye watched him with something that might have been approval.

"Now," the dragon said, "you are heavier and lighter at once. That is what a soul weighs when it is honest." The great head shifted, and the white eye and the gold eye together turned toward the dark throat of the Spire above them, ascending into shadow beyond the reach of any light. "She must climb to give. You must climb to return. What was stolen through the summit must be restored through the summit—and the crown knows it. They will not let you reach it easily."

As if summoned by the words, far above—muffled by stone and distance but unmistakable—came a sound Corvane knew intimately, professionally, terribly: the rhythmic shock of a ram against a gate. Then voices, many voices, orders barked in the clipped cadence of crown wardens. Then light, harsh and white, spilling down through the upper galleries, sweeping the root-chamber's edges.

"They're in the undercity," Paloma breathed. "Tamsin's people were supposed to hold them at the—"

"They held," Corvane said grimly, rising, his legs unsteady but holding, his hands—for the first time in eleven years—warm. "Long enough. That's what holding means." He looked at the dragon, and made the only vow that had ever come easily off his tongue. "We'll finish it. Whatever the flame asks. Whatever it costs."

"I know," Vessharran said, settling his vast head back onto his folded wings, closing the gold eye. "Go, then. Climb, little warden. Climb, little scribe. And when the fire asks you what you bring—" the voice faded, growing softer, wearier, sinking toward whatever sleep was for a creature this old, "—tell it the debt is acknowledged. Tell it the thief's heir has come to pay."

Above them, the white light swept closer, and boots rang on galleries that had not heard footsteps in thirty years, and the Spire's dead veins pulsed once, twice, like a heart remembering its rhythm—as though the whole ruined structure knew what was coming, and had been waiting thirty years to be climbed.
