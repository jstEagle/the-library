## 14. The City Turns Out

The news came to Tamsin the way all important news came to her: sideways, in pieces, wrapped in cargo manifests and half-drunk gossip.

She was down in the Saltcellar—the flooded counting-house that served as her office, her armory, and on bad nights her bed—when Bracko came wading through the ankle-deep water with a lantern held high, which meant it was either very good or very bad. Bracko only carried his lantern high when he wanted to be seen coming.

"Word from the Spire district," he said. "Your scribe. The one with the soft hands and the hard eyes. They say she's going to climb."

Tamsin set down the tally-slate she'd been pretending to study. "Climb what?"

"The Spire." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *the plague's back* or *the levee broke*. "They say the dragon told her to. They say the Inquisitor's going with her. They say—" he hesitated, which Bracko never did, "—they say the fire's dying, and if she doesn't give it something to eat, everything the city remembers goes with it."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Water dripped somewhere in the dark behind the counting-cages. A rat crossed the beam above them with the unhurried confidence of a creature who had never once been taxed.

"Well," she said at last. "That's the stupidest thing I've heard since the harbor tax."

"You don't believe it?"

"I believe it *exactly*." She pushed off from the desk and started pulling crates toward herself, opening them, taking inventory of things she'd spent ten years hoping never to need: hooks, line, lamp-oil, the good knives. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

By dawn, the proclamation was nailed to every door in the Warrens, and by mid-morning it had been read aloud so many times that even the illiterate could recite it in their sleep.

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN: MARTIAL LAW IS DECLARED THROUGHOUT VELLAMARK AND ITS PRECINCTS. ALL STREETS TO BE CLEARED BY SUNDOWN. ALL ASSEMBLY FORBIDDEN. ANY PERSON AIDING THE FUGITIVE KNOWN AS PALOMA STERLING, OR THE TRAITOR DAIN CORVANE, WILL BE HANGED FROM HIS OWN DOORPOST.*

"They got Corvane's name right," Tamsin observed, reading over the shoulder of a fishwife who couldn't read. "Somebody in the Chancellery has a good memory for traitors."

"And ours?" asked Wren, the youngest of her runners, a girl of perhaps fourteen with a burn scar down one forearm that Tamsin had never asked about. "Do they have our names?"

"Not yet." Tamsin folded the proclamation into a neat square and tucked it into her coat, because paper was paper and paper sold. "But they will, if we're careless. So we won't be careless."

Sundown came, and the streets did not clear.

Tamsin had expected fear to do the crown's work for it—fear was cheap labor, always had been—but standing on the Ropewalk as the light went amber and then gray, she watched something else happen instead. Shopkeepers put out their lamps but left their doors open. The fishwives stayed at their stalls with unsold herring going rank in the sun. Old men brought stools out onto their stoops and sat there like furniture that had opinions. Nobody shouted anything. Nobody sang. They just *stayed*, filling the streets with the one thing a garrison could not arrest without turning the whole city against it: their ordinary, stubborn, unremarkable bodies.

"They're not here for the scribe," said Bracko beside her, chewing something that might once have been tobacco. "Half of them don't know her name. They're here because they were told to leave, and they're tired of being told."

"Good," Tamsin said. "Angry is useful. Sentimental gets people killed."

She said it like she believed it. Maybe she did, a little.

* * *

The enclave met that night in the tannery cellar, and it was not a meeting Tamsin called—it was a meeting that happened to her, the way weather happens. Word had gone out through channels even she didn't fully control, and by midnight the cellar held more people than it had walls to lean against: charm-makers and hedge-witches, a former temple acolyte with ink-stained cuffs, two of Corvane's own wardens out of uniform and looking like dogs that had slipped their chains, smugglers from three rival crews who hadn't shared a table since the Oyster War, and old Mother Anselm, ninety if she was a day, who'd been hiding a gift for finding lost things since before magic was outlawed and who had outlived four Inquisitors through the simple strategy of being beneath everyone's notice.

"We hold the streets," Tamsin told them, because someone had to say it plainly and it was apparently going to be her. "Not win them. Not keep them. We hold them long enough for the climbers to reach the top of that tower. After that, the crown can have every cobblestone back with my blessing."

"How long is long enough?" asked Ferro, the Oyster War veteran, arms crossed over a chest like a beer barrel.

"I don't know. Neither does anyone else. That's what makes this fun."

Nobody laughed. Good, Tamsin thought. If they were laughing, they weren't listening.

"There are four ways up into the Spire district," she went on, drawing in the dust with her boot heel. "The Bridgegate, the Ropewalk stair, the Undertow tunnel—and the river itself, if you're mad or drowning. The garrison knows this too. Their plan is simple: seal all four, wait us out, starve the district quiet. Our plan is simpler. We make sure that sealing any one of those roads costs them more than leaving it open."

"With what?" Ferro again. Always Ferro. "We've got knives and they've got pikes and crossbows and the whole treasury of the crown behind them."

"We've got the streets," Tamsin said. "Every door that locks from the inside. Every window a stone can come out of. Every rooftop they have to climb while we're already standing on it. You don't fight a garrison, Ferro. You *annoy* it. You annoy it until its lines stretch thin and its temper frays and its captain starts making mistakes, and then you make sure the mistakes happen exactly where you want them." She looked around the cellar—at the scarred faces, the frightened ones, the ones trying to look neither. "And when they come through—and they will come through—we fall back, and we fall back, and we keep falling back until the climbing party doesn't need us to fall back anymore. Then we scatter, and we live, and we drink to the dead, and we start over. That's the whole glorious plan. Any questions?"

Mother Anselm raised a hand like a schoolgirl. "Who dies first?"

Tamsin met the old woman's milky eyes and didn't flinch, though she wanted to.

"Probably me," she said honestly. "I'm the loudest."

* * *

For three days, Vellamark practiced being ungovernable.

It did not look like war. It looked like nothing at all—that was the beauty of it. A cartload of cabbages overturned at the Bridgegate at the exact hour the garrison changed shifts, and took forty minutes to right because the wheels were mysteriously jammed. Every rope ladder on the Ropewalk went missing overnight, along with every boat under twelve feet in the harbor basin. The wardens' posted orders kept disappearing and reappearing with crucial words altered, so that no squad was ever entirely sure whether it was supposed to hold the stair or patrol the stair, and the resulting arguments were louder and longer than any riot Tamsin could have bought.

Wren proved to be a natural. The girl could walk through a checkpoint carrying seditious pamphlets sewn into her petticoat and come out the other side having also stolen the sergeant's whistle, and Tamsin found herself both proud and afraid in equal measure, the way you feel watching a child handle your sharpest knife.

On the third night, the garrison stopped being patient.

Tamsin heard the drums before she understood them—a deep, doubled beat rolling up from the citadel, and then the sound she'd been dreading: boots. Hundreds of boots, in step, moving into the Warrens with torches and pikes and that particular grim silence that soldiers get when they've been told tonight is not a drill.

"They're clearing by force," Bracko reported, sliding down the tannery stairs two at a time. "House to house. Bridgegate's sealed with chain. They're arresting anyone on the street."

"Then everyone should be off the street," Tamsin said, "and indoors, and every door locked, and every window full of something heavy to throw. Get the word out."

"It'll take an hour."

"You have twenty minutes."

He went. Tamsin stood alone in the tannery cellar among the ghost-smell of lye and old blood, and let herself have ten seconds—ten full seconds—to be afraid. She counted them off against her heartbeat like coins. Then she picked up her coat, checked her knives, and went out into the burning streets to spend the rest.

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have fronts and lines and honors. This was something older and meaner and quieter: a city refusing, house by house, window by window, hour by hour.

At the Bridgegate, the chain held, but the buildings flanking it did not belong to the crown, and by midnight there were three separate fires burning in three separate warehouses—not big fires, Tamsin had been very specific about *small* fires—each one positioned to make the gate approach untenable for anything larger than a patrol. At the Ropewalk stair, the garrison advanced four times and was pushed back four times by nothing more than roof-tiles, boiling water, and the sheer mathematical insult of defending soldiers who could retreat through doors their attackers couldn't open. On Copper Lane an old man named Pell stood in his doorway with a poker and would not move, and when a young warden raised his pike, forty neighbors appeared in forty windows, and the young warden looked at the pike, and looked at the windows, and lowered it, and walked away, and nobody ever learned his name, and that was fine. That was exactly fine. The city didn't need names tonight. It needed doors.

Tamsin moved through all of it like a needle through cloth—in and out, stitch after stitch, tying crews together, rerouting runners, putting out the one fire that got too greedy and starting the rumor that the Spire itself was already alight, which wasn't true yet, but which made the garrison commanders waste two precious hours confirming that it wasn't.

Twice she nearly died. Once to a crossbow bolt that took out a shutter an inch from her ear; once to a warden sergeant who recognized her from a bounty poster and chased her across three rooftops before she dropped him into a cistern and felt genuinely bad about it for almost four seconds.

And through all of it, she kept looking up.

The Spire rose over the district like a black finger accusing the sky, and at its broken summit, where dragon-fire had guttered for a generation like the last coal in a cold hearth, Tamsin could swear—could almost *swear*—that the light was different tonight. Brighter. Or maybe that was just the city burning below it, throwing everything upward, giving the old ruin back some of its color.

"Hold on, scribe," she muttered, crouched behind a chimney pot while bolts hissed past below. "You'd better be worth this."

* * *

Near dawn on the fourth day, the center failed.

It was Ferro's crew at the Undertow tunnel—they'd held it beautifully for three days, collapsing the entrance twice, and then the crown brought up the siege-engineers and simply dug around them, and Ferro fell back exactly as planned, exactly on order, bleeding from a pike-gash along his ribs and furious about the falling back more than the bleeding.

"We can still take it back," he growled, while Wren bound him with more enthusiasm than skill.

"No, we can't. And we don't need to." Tamsin spread the map of the district on a barrelhead and studied it with eyes gritty from three sleepless nights. The picture wasn't good. It wasn't supposed to be good. But the shape of it—the way the garrison's strength had pooled at the tunnel, the way the Bridgegate fires had finally drawn their reserves west, the way the Ropewalk, battered and half-burned, stood suddenly, accidentally, almost empty—

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you beautiful idiots. You gave me the stair."

Bracko leaned over her shoulder. "Is that a plan or a prayer?"

"Both. Same thing tonight." She straightened, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, scraped raw by smoke and shouting. "Everything we have left goes to the Ropewalk. One hour. One hour is all I'm asking. One hour where that stair belongs to us, and then whatever's climbing it climbs it clean, and then the rest of us run like cowards and live to be ashamed of ourselves."

"And if the climbers aren't there in an hour?"

Tamsin looked up at the Spire one more time. And this time there was no almost about it. At the summit, faint as a struck match, steady as a promise, something was burning.

"Then we hold two hours," she said. "Get everyone moving."

* * *

They held the Ropewalk stair for six hours.

History, if anyone wrote it down—and Tamsin intended to make damn sure someone did—would record that morning as a rout, a last stand, a doomed gallantry. What it actually was, from inside, was a hundred small stubbornnesses stacked end to end. The fishwives who chained their stall-shutters across the stair mouth and dared the pikemen to cut through herring barrels. Ferro, ribs bound, standing in the narrowest turn with a boat-hook and a vocabulary that made hardened wardens blush. Mother Anselm, who could find lost things, walking calmly through the melee retrieving dropped weapons and handing them to whichever side she liked better. Wren on the rooftops with a sling and perfect aim and a face that never changed at all, which worried Tamsin more than tears would have.

And when it ended—when the reserves finally came, too many, too fast, when the order went down the line to scatter and save yourselves—Tamsin stood on the smoke-hazed stair with her back against a wall and watched the survivors melt into doorways and cellars and drains, ordinary courage dissolving back into ordinary lives, leaving nothing behind but broken pikes and a stair slick with blood and herring brine.

Above her, impossibly, the Spire burned brighter than it had in thirty years.

Below her, boots were coming up the stair, and these boots were not hers.

Bracko grabbed her arm. "Back ways. Now."

"In a minute." Tamsin pulled free, dug in her coat, and came out with the folded proclamation she'd taken off a door four days ago. She unfolded it, smoothed it against the wall, and nailed it up with a knife, dead center, where every soldier ascending would have to read it. Beneath the crown's words, in chalk, in letters a foot high, she wrote:

*HELD SIX HOURS. HOW LONG CAN YOU HOLD?*

Then she ran.

Behind her the garrison poured onto the stair, and above her the new fire climbed, and somewhere between the two—between the hammering boots and the rising flame—Tamsin allowed herself, for exactly one stride, one breath, one heartbeat, to hope.

Then the shout went up from the sentries at the stair head—"They're coming down! Someone's coming *down*!"—and Tamsin turned in the doorway, and squinted through the smoke, and saw a figure staggering toward her out of the burning light.

It was not Paloma Sterling.

It was not Corvane either.

It was Wren, scorched, sobbing, sprinting, and the words the girl was screaming tore through the din of the street like a blade through wet canvas:

"Tamsin—the gates—the crown's taken the gates—*they're inside the Spire!*"
