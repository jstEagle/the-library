## 15. Mother Sefu's Confession

They brought Mother Sefu to the enclave on the third night of the riots, carried through the smugglers' tunnels on a plank door torn from its hinges, and Paloma knew before she saw her face that it was over.

The old woman had always been a mountain to her—broad-shouldered, immovable, her voice the kind that could quiet a hall of three hundred novices with a single word. Now she lay on a cot in the back of Tamsin's safehouse, among barrels of lamp oil and coils of rope, so shrunken inside her grey temple robes that she seemed less like a mountain than like the shadow one casts at dusk. Her breathing made a wet, laboring sound, as if each breath had to be pulled up from somewhere very deep by hands that were growing tired.

The wound was not visible. That was the cruelty of it. The wardens' iron bolts did not bleed much; they burned what they touched, searing the gift out of the flesh along with everything else, leaving behind a blackened starburst no bigger than a coin and a poison that worked inward, patiently, toward the heart. The enclave's healers had done what they could. What they could was not enough. Everyone in the room knew it, and everyone pretended otherwise, because pretending was a kindness and there were so few left to give.

Paloma sat beside the cot and held Sefu's hand.

It had been eleven days since the reliquary alarm had burned her name into the hunt rolls. Eleven days since Mother Sefu had stood in the archive doorway with her arms spread wide, blocking the corridor with nothing but her body and her rank, shouting *she went east, you fool, seal the east gate* while Paloma ran west with the Ember Codex pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Eleven days, and Paloma had heard nothing—had assumed the worst, then assumed something worse than the worst—and now here the worst was, delivered by smugglers at midnight, breathing wetly in a room that smelled of tallow and river water.

"You came," Paloma said, because she could not think of anything else to say. "You shouldn't have come. The city—" The city was under martial law. Every gate watched. Every street a trap. A woman of Sefu's rank, moving at night, would be taken within a hundred paces of the temple doors.

Sefu's eyes opened. They were clouded now, filmed with pain, but they found Paloma's face with the unerring accuracy of a woman who had spent forty years reading the smallest tremors in other people's handwriting. "Child," she said, and her voice was still her voice, that low bell-tone that Paloma had followed through ten years of dawn services and copying duties and cold dormitory nights. "Hush. I am dying. I intend to do it efficiently, and I cannot do it efficiently if you are weeping at me."

"I'm not weeping."

"You are. You always did. Even as a novice, when I corrected your penmanship." A breath, drawn up from the depths. "Terrible penmanship. Like a spider walking through ink."

Paloma laughed—a broken, awful sound—and pressed her forehead to their joined hands. "Ivet said the same thing."

"Ivet said it better." Sefu's fingers tightened, weak but deliberate. "Send the others out. Tamsin and her people. The healer can wait outside the door. What I have to say is for you alone, and I will not say it twice, because I will not have the strength twice."

* * *

When the room emptied—the smugglers withdrawing without argument, Tamsin pausing at the door long enough to catch Paloma's eye and mouth *call if you need me*, which was more tenderness than Tamsin would ever admit to aloud—Mother Sefu tried to sit up. Paloma eased pillows beneath her shoulders, hating how little substance moved beneath her hands, how the bones seemed to have gone soft as driftwood.

"Water?"

"Later." Sefu settled back. For a long moment she looked at the ceiling, at the water-stained beams where someone had once hung drying fish, and Paloma watched her gather herself the way she had seen her gather herself before difficult sermons: visibly, deliberately, a mountain deciding to move.

"The Codex," Sefu said at last. "You have read it."

"Some of it. Enough."

"Then you know what it says. Mutual offering. Memory for fire. The first king took and did not give." Her eyes closed. "And you know what the temple teaches instead. That the dragons grew proud. That they demanded worship beyond their due. That King Alderic the First, may his name be blessed, burned the Spire to free humanity from bondage."

"It's in every primer. It's carved on the Spire's own foundation stones."

"Yes." The word fell flat as a dropped stone. "Carved. By masons working from drawings my own hand approved."

The room seemed to tilt. Paloma gripped the edge of the cot. "What?"

Sefu opened her eyes again, and this time she did not look away. That was the thing about Mother Sefu—she had never once, in ten years, flinched from Paloma's gaze, not when doling out discipline, not when delivering bad news about debts or duties. She did not flinch now. Whatever courage it cost her, she paid it in full.

"I helped forge it," she said. "The false history. Not the whole of it—it was already old when I came to the temple, already worn smooth by repetition, the way any lie goes smooth when enough generations handle it. But sixty years ago, when I was young and newly raised to the scriptorium, the High Mother of that day found fragments. Original documents. Correspondence from Alderic's own reign. Proof of what the covenant truly required, proof that the king had taken the fire and offered nothing." She drew a breath that caught halfway and had to be finished in two stages. "And she ordered them destroyed. And she ordered a new account written. And she chose me to write it, because I had the finest hand in the temple, and because I was ambitious, and because she knew—I see that now, though I did not know it then—that I would do it."

"Why?" The question came out raw. "Why would anyone—"

"Because the alternative was ruin." Sefu's voice gained a fraction of its old iron, and Paloma recognized, sickeningly, the tone she used for justifying hard rules to novices: *we rise before dawn because discipline is love; we copy only what is sanctioned because order is safety.* "Understand what those years were, child. The burning was fresh. The city had watched its greatest wonder go up in smoke on royal orders, and half of them had cheered and half of them had wept, and both halves were looking for someone to blame. If the truth came out—if it was proven that the crown itself had broken the covenant, stolen the fire, doomed the realms to slow death—there would have been civil war. Or worse. There would have been panic. The dragons were already failing, already going blind and mad in Ashkarat, and a desperate people learns quickly to burn witches. The gifted were tolerated, barely, in those days. The High Mother believed—she made us all believe—that silence was mercy. That a stable lie was kinder than a truth that would set neighbor against neighbor until the streets ran red."

"And you believed her."

"At first? Yes. God forgive me, yes. I was nineteen and she was the holiest woman I had ever met, and she wept while she gave the order, and I thought: *this is what greatness costs, this is what duty looks like.*" Sefu's hand turned in Paloma's grip, so that their palms pressed together, and her grip was surprisingly strong, as if the confession itself were feeding her. "And then years passed, and I rose, and the lie became mine. My hand was on every copy. My corrections were in every margin. Do you understand what that means, Paloma? Every child who learned to read in Vellamark learned from pages I wrote. Every priest who preached the dragon's pride preached words I composed at a desk by candlelight, telling myself each night that tomorrow I would speak, that next year I would speak, that surely the time would come when the truth could be borne. And the time never came. Because I would not make it come. Because I had a position, and a name, and eventually a debt of my own—not coin, never coin, but a debt of silence owed to every mother superior before me who had kept the same secret, so that exposing the lie would have meant burying us all together."

Her breath hitched. Paloma realized she was crying, and did not wipe her face.

"The debt," Paloma said. "My debt. The ledger. Was that even real?"

"Oh, the coin was real. Your father borrowed real coin from real lenders, and the temple bought the paper honestly enough." Sefu's mouth twisted. "But why do you imagine the temple holds so many such papers? Why do you imagine we bind our scribes with debt rather than hiring freely? A bound scribe does not ask questions. A bound scribe copies what she is told and is grateful for her supper. We built an entire institution on the principle that gratitude is safer than curiosity. You were never an accident, child. You were the design."

Something broke open in Paloma's chest—not anger exactly, or not only anger, but something adjacent to it, something that shared a wall with anger the way grief shares a wall with relief. Ten years. Ten years of counting coins against interest calculated to keep her forever short. Ten years of numbing her fingers in ice water, hiding her small warm gift, terrified of the hunt rolls. And the whole apparatus—the debt, the fear, the careful smallness of her life—had been load-bearing architecture for a lie written in her mentor's beautiful hand.

"Ivet," Paloma said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "She was dragged out screaming my name. Because of wards your temple installed. Testing for gifts your lie made criminal."

"Yes."

"You knew the hunt was coming. The criers, the testing—you must have known before the rest of us. Did you warn anyone? Did you try to stop it?"

"No." No hesitation, no softening. Sefu gave her the word whole and unadorned, like a merchant handing over exact change. "I negotiated. I bargained with Corvane's superiors for exemptions—for senior staff, for proven loyalists. I traded names for names. I did what I have always done, child: I protected the institution, and I called it protection of the innocent, and some of the time I even believed it." Her eyes glistened, the film over them thickening, whether with tears or with approaching death Paloma could not tell. "Ivet's name was not among those I traded away. But it was not among those I saved, either. I spent my capital on people whose silence I needed, and she was young and poor and loud about her opinions, and I let the arithmetic decide. So no. Do not forgive me for Ivet. There is nothing there to forgive. Only to mourn, and the mourning belongs to you, not to me."

* * *

Outside, distantly, the riots went on. Tamsin's people holding the gates, holding the streets, ordinary men and women with iron bars and smuggled charms standing between the wardens and the rumor of a girl with a book. Paloma could hear it even here, deep in the undercity: a low rolling murmur like surf, punctuated by the crack of some distant charge. The city turning out. The city that Sefu's lie had ruled for two hundred years, finally refusing to be ruled by it.

"Corvane has the archive," Sefu said. "Or he soon will. His superiors ordered it burned—all of it, every record, every ledger, every original document that survived the last purge. He sent word to me through channels I thought were dead." Her lips curved faintly. "He asked permission to copy one page first. He did not say which page. I suspect I know."

"He copied the covenant terms. He showed me." Paloma hesitated. "He's—he's not what I expected. He took off his cuffs at the Spire's base. Vessharran showed him the truth and he didn't burn. He's climbing beside me, Mother. An Inquisitor, climbing beside me."

"I know." Something moved across Sefu's face that might have been hope, arriving late and unwelcome like a guest after the funeral. "That is partly why I came. Not only for the confession—though the confession I owed you, and owed Ivet, and owed every soul my hand lied to. But also because of what comes after. Child, listen to me. When the flame takes your memories—when you give it your mother's voice and Ivet's laugh and every morning of this life you have loved and endured—someone must carry the account of it. Someone must write you back. I had hoped—" The sentence failed. She tried again. "I am a forger, Paloma. It is the only craft I have truly mastered. But I know what writing can hold. I know that ink outlives intention. Whatever you lose on that summit, put it into him first. Make him your ledger. Let him owe *you* the debt for once."

"He's writing already. On the climb. Everything I say aloud, he writes it down. I thought it was evidence-gathering." Paloma laughed wetly. "It's the most romantic thing anyone has ever done to me and I don't think he knows it."

"Men rarely do. It is why the temple keeps better records than they do." The joke cost Sefu visibly; afterward her breathing worsened, and Paloma fetched the water after all, lifting the cup to her mentor's mouth the way she had once been taught to serve at table during her novitiate. Sefu drank a little. Declined more. Lay back against the pillows and regarded Paloma with an expression of terrible gentleness.

"Ask it," Sefu said.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever you came in here carrying. The accusation. The verdict. I have confessed, and you have wept, and neither of those things is what you actually feel. Say what you feel. I am dying; I can bear almost anything, and briefly."

So Paloma said it. She said it low, so the walls wouldn't carry it to the smugglers in the outer room, though perhaps she wanted them to hear, perhaps some part of her wanted the whole city to hear:

"I loved you. You were the closest thing to a mother I had, and my whole life you taught me that the temple was good, that the debt was fair, that the law was just, and it was all built on your handwriting. And I can't—I look at you and I love you, and I hate you, and I don't know how both of those fit in one body, and you're asking me for forgiveness and I—" Her voice gave out. She steadied it. "I can't give it. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Forgiveness is a thing you earn back and you won't be alive to earn it back, and that isn't fair either, and I'm so tired of things not being fair."

Silence. The distant surf of the riots. Sefu's labored breath.

"Good," the old woman said at last.

Paloma stared. "*Good?*"

"Good. If you had forgiven me tonight—quickly, kindly, the way you charm a beggar-child's button—I would know my confession had failed. Cheap forgiveness is another forgery, child. I have made enough of those for one lifetime; I'll not accept one as my last sacrament." She lifted her free hand, trembling, and laid it against Paloma's cheek, and her palm was fever-hot and dry as old paper. "Withhold it. Hold it like a coin you refuse to spend. And if some part of you finds, years from now, that it wants to give it anyway—to my grave, to the wind, to whatever gods bother listening—then give it then. Freely. Not for my sake. For yours. Unspent coins grow heavy. I know that better than anyone."

"I don't want you to die."

"I know." Her thumb brushed the tear from Paloma's jaw. "I have watched many die and said the same foolish sentence to every one of them. It means: *I am not ready to love you only in memory.* But memory is where we keep our dead, Paloma. It is the whole of the country they live in. And you—" the ghost of her old sternness surfaced, "—of all people, you know what memory costs now. You are about to pay more than I ever did. So take this one with you when you climb: remember that I loved you badly and too late and mostly through institutions, which is the worst way, and I loved you nonetheless. Remember it if you can. And if the flame takes it—then let Corvane's ink keep it, and trust that somewhere past the forgetting, it stays true."

Paloma bent and pressed her brow to her mentor's brow, the way novices greeted the mother superior at dawn, ten thousand mornings ago, in another life.

"Your penmanship really was terrible," Sefu whispered.

"I know. You told me every day for ten years."

"It was the only criticism you ever believed. Everything else you argued with." A pause. A breath, shallower now. "Argue with the world, child. Argue it into better shape. That was always your true gift—not the charms. Never the charms."

* * *

Sefu died an hour before dawn, quietly, between one wet breath and the next, while Paloma held her hand and recited the old office for the dead—the true version, the pre-burning version that Sefu herself had shown her once, secretly, in a locked carrel, saying *if you ever find the originals, child, you will know the words*. Outside, the riots swelled and receded. Somewhere above the rooftops, invisible from this depth, the guttering dragon-fire pulsed its slow dying light over Vellamark.

Paloma sat with the body for a long time. Then she straightened the grey robes, folded the hands, and did what she had been born to do before any of this began: she took out paper and ink, and by the light of a single candle she wrote the confession down. All of it. Word for word. Signed it, dated it, sealed it with wax and the impression of her thumb.

Evidence. Leverage. Truth. Whatever it needed to be, for whoever came after.

Tamsin appeared in the doorway, silent as her profession required, and read the situation in one glance. "We can bury her below the waterline. Smugglers' ground. No wardens look there."

"Below the waterline," Paloma agreed, rising, folding the sealed letter into her coat beside the Ember Codex. Her body felt hollowed and strangely light, the way she imagined a house feels after the furniture is sold. "Tamsin—how long until the streets fail? Until the wardens break through to the Spire?"

Tamsin's scarred face was grim in the candlelight. "We've got maybe a day. Less, if they bring the siege-wards up from the harbor." She jerked her chin toward the stairs, toward the dark, toward the ruined tower at the city's heart. "If you're climbing, little scribe, you climb tomorrow. At first light. While my people are still bleeding to buy you the door."

Paloma looked back once at the still form on the cot—at the mountain, fallen, forgiven-not-yet—and followed her up toward the fire.
