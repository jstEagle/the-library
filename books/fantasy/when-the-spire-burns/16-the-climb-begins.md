## 16. The Climb Begins

The riots began at dusk, as Tamsin had promised they would.

From the shadow of the tannery row, Paloma watched Vellamark catch fire—not with flame, but with noise. A thousand voices rose from the Fishmarket Steps, from the Chandler's Walk, from the tenement warrens where the debt-crippled and the charm-marked had spent a generation learning to be quiet. Now they were loud. Now they were throwing stones at the warden posts and singing the old market songs that the temple had ruled seditious, and the crown's soldiers, stretched thin along the Spire's approaches, turned their faces toward the din like iron filings toward a magnet.

"They'll hold the gates for an hour," Tamsin said. The smuggler stood with her arms crossed, her burned cheek catching the orange light of the distant barricade fires. "Two if we're lucky. After that, Corvane's former friends come through whether the streets are held or not." She looked at Paloma, and then—longer, harder—at Corvane. "You understand what happens up there. Nobody comes down the same."

"I know," Paloma said.

"You don't. But you will." Tamsin spat into the gutter, a benediction or a curse. "The door to the root is behind the old salt warehouse. The ward on it is dead—it died with the covenant—but the stone remembers being guarded, so it will make you *feel* guarded. Don't let it turn you around. And Sterling—" she caught Paloma's sleeve, "—the woman who healed Maren in the enclave. If you see her up there, in the fire, in your head—that's not her ghost. That's the Spire showing you your own debts. Everything up there is a mirror. Break nothing you aren't prepared to see yourself in."

Then she pressed something into Paloma's hand: a stub of chalk-white candle, wrapped in oilcloth. "Dragon-tallow. It burns without air. You'll want light that doesn't need the world's permission."

Paloma closed her fingers around it. It was warm, though it had been cold in Tamsin's pocket. Everything touched by dragon-fire was warm now; she could feel it even through the bandages on her palms, even through the ache where her gift sat coiled and waiting.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me. Come back and I'll charge you for it." But Tamsin's grip tightened once before she let go, and then she was gone, swallowed by the crowd-flow toward the gates, a small scarred figure walking into the roar of ordinary courage.

* * *

The salt warehouse smelled of a century of brine and abandonment. Behind it, half-hidden by collapsed scaffolding, a door stood in the Spire's root: black stone veined with seams of dull red, like a heart that had stopped beating but not yet begun to rot.

Corvane reached it first. He had removed his iron cuffs three days ago, at the dragon's shadow, and his bare wrists still looked wrong to Paloma—pale bands of skin where the metal had lived for years, tender as new scars. His gift moved in him now, unhidden, and she could feel it the way one feels weather: a low pressure of perception, attention made almost solid. He noticed everything. It was exhausting to be near him. It was also, she admitted in the privacy of her own skull, safer than anything else in her life.

"The stone remembers being guarded," he said quietly. "She wasn't lying. I can feel it watching us."

"Can it tell I'm afraid?"

"It can't tell anything anymore. That's why it only makes us feel it." He set his hand against the door. "It shows us our own fear and lets us call it judgment. That's all a dead god can do."

He pushed, and the door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, which was worse than any scream.

Inside, the dark was total until Paloma unwrapped the candle. Dragon-tallow lit without flint—a breath of warmth across the wick and it bloomed, throwing light the color of banked embers, red-gold and restless. By that light she saw the first gallery of the Spire's root: a vast circular chamber, its walls carved floor to ceiling with names.

Hundreds of thousands of names. Human names climbing beside dragon-names, paired two by two, each pair joined by a line of script she now recognized from the Codex: *I give this, and receive this.* The covenant written not in ink but in stone, in commitment after commitment, memory offered against fire given, the whole architecture of two realms holding hands.

And over everything, the burn marks. Great black blooms of scorched stone where the royal fires had eaten through the carvings, leaving gaps in the pairs—human names surviving with their dragon-partners erased, or vice versa, amputations rendered permanent in rock. Here and there, in the deepest scorch-shadow, something still smoldered. Not flame. A kind of heat that had no color, that Paloma felt against her skin like standing too near an oven door. Burned covenant-magic, refusing to finish dying.

"It's still alive," she whispered. "All these years. It never finished dying."

"No," Corvane said. "It's been waiting to be asked whether it should."

They began to climb.

* * *

The galleries went up in spirals, and the spiral was not merciful.

By the second gallery the stairs began to fail—steps cracked through, whole flights sagging inward like ribs of a drowned ship—and they climbed hand over hand through wreckage that shifted under them with groans of settling stone. The smoldering heat thickened. Paloma's sweat dried on her skin before it could cool. The candle burned steady regardless, needing nothing, asking nothing.

In the third gallery, the fire tested him first.

He didn't announce it. He simply stopped, halfway through a broken archway, and went rigid, and his eyes lost focus as though he were reading something very far away. Paloma knew the signs now. She had felt the first touch of it herself on the stairs below: a warmth sliding into the mind like a hand into a glove, gentle as a mother, patient as a debt collector.

She stood guard while he suffered, and watched him suffer, and learned things about Dain Corvane that he would never have chosen to teach her.

His lips moved. Fragments came out. "*I told them she was in the east rooms—*" A flinch. "*Eleven people. I counted them out. I counted them out because counting is what I do—*" Another flinch, deeper, and his voice dropped to something young and ruined: "*Father, I didn't mean to—the lamp—I didn't know the curtains would—*"

So there it was. The origin wound. A child whose gift had flared uncontrolled, a fire, someone hurt or dead, and then the cuffs, and then the Inquisition—which took gifted children and taught them that their natures were crimes, and then armed them and aimed them at others of their kind. Paloma understood the architecture of him now, all the way down. He had become the hunt because he had been hunted first. Every person he had dragged away screaming Ivet's name had been, in some chamber of his heart, himself.

The vision released him slowly. He came back to his body by degrees, gasping, gripping the broken archway hard enough to split stone dust from it.

"You saw," he said. Not a question.

"Some."

"Good." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Then you know what I am. No lies left between us on this staircase."

"Dain." She used his given name for the second time ever, and watched it land on him like a blow. "What did the fire show you just now? Not the boy. The worst choice. The one it kept returning to?"

He was silent a long moment. The ember-light moved on his face.

"A woman named Sera Kell," he said finally. "Three years ago. She was a charm-maker in the Weavers' Quarter. My orders were to bring her in for testing. She ran. I reported that she resisted and was killed attempting escape." His jaw worked. "She didn't resist. I gave her a head start. Ten seconds. It was mercy, I thought. Then the wardens caught her at the canal, and my report covered me, and her name went into the rolls as a criminal, and I slept that night. I slept *well*. That's what the fire showed me. Not the killing. The sleeping."

Paloma reached out and put her hand flat against his chest, over the sternum, where the guilt lived. He flinched but did not pull away.

"The fire isn't punishing you," she said. "It's making sure you climb awake. There's a difference."

"Is there?" His voice cracked on it. "Up here, is there a difference?"

"Yes. Punishment wants you to stop. Judgment wants you to continue. Keep walking, Dain."

* * *

Her test came in the fifth gallery.

She had been warned. Tamsin's words, the Codex's cold instruction, Mother Sefu's dying face—all of it had prepared her for visions, for mirrors, for the Spire reaching into her chest with its patient burning fingers. None of it prepared her for her mother's kitchen.

It arrived whole. The smell of cardamom and woodsmoke, the chipped blue bowl on the table, rain on the window, and her mother turning from the stove with flour on her hands, saying, "There you are, little spark. I was starting to worry." Her voice. Her actual voice, which Paloma had not heard in eleven years, since the fever took her, and it was exactly right, every grain and warmth of it, and Paloma fell to her knees on the broken stair and wept like a child.

"Little spark. What have they done to you? Look at your hands."

"Mama. Mama, I have to go up. I have to give them something, and they might take you—"

"Take me where?" Her mother smiled, and knelt, and took Paloma's burned hands in her flour-dusted ones. "Sweetheart. Listen to me. I've been dead eleven years. Whatever they take from you up here, they can't take me anywhere I haven't already gone. But you—" she touched Paloma's face, and her hands were impossibly real, impossibly warm, "—you're going somewhere I can't follow, and you're frightened, and you think being frightened means you love them less. It doesn't. Fear is just love with nowhere to put itself."

"That's not how it works—"

"Little spark. I held you when you charmed your first button. Did I tell you that? You were four. You made it glow like a tiny moon, and you looked at me, terrified, and do you know what I felt? Not fear. Pride. Terrible, inconvenient pride." She kissed Paloma's forehead, and the kiss tasted of flour and cardamom. "Whatever you lose climbing this thing—you will always have been loved. That doesn't live in your memory. It lives deeper than memory. They can't reach it."

The kitchen dissolved. The stair came back, hard and real under her knees, and the ember-light, and Corvane crouched before her with his hand hovering an inch from her shoulder, not touching, waiting.

"How long?" she managed.

"An hour. Maybe more. You spoke to someone."

"My mother."

"I know." He helped her up. His grip was steady. "Mine was there too. At the edge of mine. The father I burned the house for. He just stood in the doorway and didn't say anything. That was worse than anything the fire could invent."

They climbed on. The sixth gallery was a hall of falling masonry they crossed one stone at a time, hearts in their mouths, dust rising around them like gray snow. The seventh was flooded to the waist with water that the smoldering heat had warmed to blood temperature, and they waded through it in silence, and somewhere in that silence Paloma realized she had stopped thinking of this as an ascent and started thinking of it as a reckoning—as though every gallery stripped something false off her, and what remained underneath was smaller and truer and more afraid than the woman who had walked in.

The eighth gallery showed her Ivet.

Not a vision this time—or maybe a vision, but one so precise it made no difference. Ivet at the testing table, palms down on the ice-cold stone, looking up at Paloma with an expression of absolute betrayal, mouthing her name as the wardens dragged her out: *you knew, you passed, you knew and you passed.* And Paloma had known. Had iced her hands in the washroom while Ivet failed two doors down. Had survived by a trick and called it luck.

"Ivet," she said aloud, to the empty gallery, "I'm doing this for you too. Whatever that's worth. Whatever it buys you, wherever you are."

The smoldering heat pulsed once, like a heartbeat acknowledging a debt.

* * *

They came out of the flooded gallery onto a landing unlike the rest: a wide platform where the Spire opened like a throat, and above them—for the first time—they could see upward. Shafts of night sky between broken floors, and stars through them, and higher still, faint but unmistakable, a red glow. The summit. The place where the flame waited, hungry and patient, for what they carried.

Corvane leaned against the wall, breathing hard. Blood streaked his forearm where a falling stone had opened it. Paloma bound it with strips of her sleeve without asking, and he let her, and neither of them commented on the intimacy of it, because commenting would have required a vocabulary they hadn't built yet.

"How much farther?" she asked.

"Three galleries. Maybe four." He flexed his bare wrist, staring at the pale skin. "Paloma. When we get there. When it asks you for the price." He stopped. Started again. "I need you to know that whatever you give it—I'll keep the rest. Whatever the fire leaves behind, whatever falls out of you when the memories go—I'll gather it. All of it. I've been writing since the enclave. Everything you've said about your mother, about Ivet, about the button child. It's in my book. Ink survives fire. Someone has to survive being forgotten."

She stared at him. "You've been writing me down?"

"Someone has to," he said simply. "You're about to erase yourself to save everyone. The least I can do is refuse to let it be complete."

Above them the red glow brightened—just slightly, just enough—and beneath their feet the whole ruined Spire shuddered, a long rolling tremor, and far below, muffled by a hundred floors of stone, they heard it: shouting. Steel. The crack of warden-fire against barricades.

Tamsin's hour was up.

Corvane straightened, wincing, and looked up the dark spiral toward the burning summit, and Paloma saw the moment the last hesitation left him—not courage arriving, but courage having already been spent, and the man remaining standing anyway.

"They're through the gates," he said. "We have until dawn. Maybe less." He held out his hand. "Come on, little spark. Let's go give a god back its fire."

She took it.

Together they stepped onto the ninth gallery's broken stair—and the smoldering heat rose around them both at once, and the Spire, waking fully for the first time in a generation, began to show them everything.
