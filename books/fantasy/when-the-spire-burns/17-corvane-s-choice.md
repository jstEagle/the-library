## 17. Corvane's Choice

The fire spoke to him in his mother's voice.

Corvane knew it was a lie before the words finished forming—knew it the way a man knows the difference between rain and a bucket of water poured over his head—but knowing did not blunt the blow. The gallery they had climbed into was narrow and circular, its walls fused glass from some ancient burning, and in that glass his reflection moved wrong: a boy of eleven, thin-shouldered, standing in a kitchen that had been ash for twenty years.

*You could have saved me,* the voice said, gentle as bread dough being kneaded, as a lullaby hummed off-key. *You felt it coming. You always felt things coming. You were gifted, my darling, and you said nothing, because you were afraid, and I burned.*

"I know," Corvane whispered.

The walls drank the words and gave them back warmer.

Behind him, Paloma Sterling picked her way over a ridge of half-melted stone, her satchel—the Codex wrapped in oilcloth against her chest—clutched like an infant. Her face was streaked with soot and something worse: the residue of whatever vision the fire had shown her one gallery below. He had heard her crying without sound, the way people cry when they have learned that sound invites punishment. She hadn't spoken of it. He hadn't asked. There was a courtesy between them now, hard-won on the road and sealed in blood two nights past when the rogue wardens came for them both, and part of that courtesy was silence about the fire's cruelties.

"Corvane." She stopped beside him, close enough that he could feel the heat radiating off her—her gift, that small warm charm-craft, running high with fear. "The walls are doing it again. Don't listen."

"It's not listening that's dangerous," he said. "It's answering."

But he answered anyway. That was the trap of this place. Every gallery of the ruined Spire asked a question, and every question required a reply, and every reply opened you a little wider, like a door taken off its hinges.

Above them—three galleries up, perhaps four—the light changed. It had been changing all night, deepening from the dull ember-glow of dead magic into something quicker, hungrier, a gold that moved like water finding its level. The covenant-fire, waking as they climbed toward it. Below, muffled by a hundred tons of stone, the sounds of Vellamark's streets still reached them: bells, screams, the particular roar of a crowd that has decided to stop running and start pushing. Tamsin's people. Holding the gates. Buying them time with their bodies.

Corvane tried not to count how much time.

He failed.

* * *

They rested in an alcove where the floor had buckled into a natural seat, and Paloma unwrapped the Codex just enough to read by the glow of her own cupped hands. She read the way she did everything—thoroughly, hungrily, her lips moving around the old covenant-tongue—and watching her, Corvane felt the familiar double ache that had been living in his chest since the campfire: admiration and shame, sharing one ribcage.

"The terms are clearer here," she murmured. "Higher up. The pages near the end—they're legible again. The fire's waking is restoring the text." She looked up at him. In the ember-light her eyes were nearly the color of the walls. "It wants us to understand what we're asking it to do. Reforging isn't a ritual, Corvane. It's a negotiation. And the other party intends to make sure we know exactly what we're trading."

"And what does it want from me?" He meant it as a deflection. It came out raw.

Paloma studied him a moment too long. "Whatever you'd least like to give," she said finally, and wrapped the Codex away, and stood, and held out her hand to help him up.

He took it. Her palm was fever-warm. Twenty years of iron cuffs had taught him to flinch from warmth like that; now he held on a beat longer than balance required, and let go, and they climbed.

* * *

The eighth gallery was where the Spire made its offer.

Later, Corvane would try to describe it to himself and find the description sliding through his fingers like wet rope, but the shape of it was this: the stairs ended. Not collapsed—ended, in a doorway of clean black stone, and beyond the doorway there was a room that should not have existed inside a ruin, a room intact and lit and furnished with terrible precision.

His childhood kitchen.

Not the burned version. The whole one. Flour on the counter. His mother's shawl hooked on the peg by the door. A kettle just beginning its low note before the whistle, and the smell of rosemary bread, and through the window—the window that had looked out on the tanner's yard, which had never once in his life been beautiful—a view of the sea.

"You're dead," Corvane said to the room. "All of you. This is ash and memory, and memory is the one thing in this city that lies more fluently than the crown."

The room said nothing. The kettle sang softly on.

He understood then that the fire had learned its persuasion from the humans it had bargained with, long ago. It offered what kings had always offered. Comfort first. Terms after.

Paloma touched his elbow. "We can go around. There's a gap in the outer wall—I saw it—"

"No." His own voice surprised him. Steady. Almost calm. "No. It built this for me. It would be rude to leave it standing empty."

She gave him a look—he was beginning to be able to read her looks, another thing that terrified him more than wardens ever had—and stepped back to give him the doorway. Whatever she saw in his face made her say, quietly, "I'll be right here. However long."

"That," Corvane said, stepping through, "is precisely the problem."

* * *

Inside, the kitchen resolved around him with a completeness that stole his breath. Grain in the wood. A hairline crack in the fourth tile, left of the hearth. His mother turned from the counter with flour to her wrists, and she was not quite right—the eyes too steady, the blink a half-second slow, a portrait animated by something that loved him enough to try—and Corvane's heart broke along the old seam anyway, because grief does not check its facts.

"Sit down, Dain," she said. "You've been climbing all night. Sit. Eat. You don't have to do this."

"Do what?" he asked, though he knew.

"This." She gestured with the wooden spoon, and the gesture took in the kitchen, the Spire, the world. "You've carried it long enough. All those years with the iron on your wrists, all those people you dragged to the pyres because the alternative was joining them—do you think any loving god wanted that for you? Sit down. Put it down. We'll take it from you. That's what mothers are for."

And the fire, speaking through his dead mother in a dead kitchen inside a dying tower, laid out its terms the way the temple had once laid out scripture: gently, and with no room left in them for anything but yes.

Restoration. His gift returned—not the starved, flickering ember-sense the cuffs had permitted him, but the whole of it, the true current, the thing he had been born carrying and had spent his life pretending not to carry. He would feel the world again as he had felt it once, briefly, gloriously, at eleven years old: every charm's warmth, every lie's cold draft, every heart within a hundred paces beating out its small honest music.

And with the gift, absolution. The guilt erased. Not forgiven—erased. He would climb the rest of the Spire feeling what he had felt before the arrests began, before Ivet's screaming name, before Mother Sefu's broken body, before the archive smoke and the eleven fugitives and the lieutenant's seeing eyes: clean. Unburdened. The man he might have been if the first king had kept his bargain and the crown had never needed hunters.

There was only one price, and the fire named it with his mother's softest voice:

*Leave her.*

*Let the scribe carry her memories alone. Let her pay the whole weight of a realm's rebirth while you walk free and full. She chose her burden; choose yours. Climb behind her, restored, unguilty, and when she comes down hollowed and forgetting, you will be whole enough to love her for both of you. Isn't that kinder? Isn't that mercy? What use is a man who empties himself alongside her? Two hollow people cannot hold each other up, Dain. One of you must remain. Be the one who remains. Sit down. Eat. The bread is almost ready.*

The kettle reached its whistle.

Corvane stood in his mother's kitchen and let himself want it—honestly, completely, the way he had learned on the long road that wanting a thing and lying about wanting it were two different wounds and he was done inflicting the second. He wanted his gift back the way a blind man wants color. He wanted forgiveness more than breath. He wanted, most obscenely of all, to sit down, to rest, to stop climbing, to let someone else be the sacrifice for once in his coward's life.

The bread smelled like every good day he had ever lost.

"No," Corvane said.

His mother's face flickered—grief or static, impossible to tell.

"She doesn't need me whole," he said. "That's the lie in it. That's the poison under the honey. She needs me *witnessing*. If I come down that mountain clean and full while she's emptied herself to the last memory of her mother's voice, then everything she gave becomes a transaction between unequal parties, and I will have purchased my peace with her past. I've spent twenty years purchasing peace with other people's suffering. I know the currency. I won't spend it here."

The kitchen darkened at its edges. The flour dust hung motionless in air that had stopped pretending to move.

"You want absolution?" Corvane went on, and his voice cracked, and he let it crack, because the crack was the honest part. "Then take it the only way it's ever truly given. Not erased. Named. So write this down, fire, you ancient starving thing, write it into your glass and your stone and whatever passes for your conscience: My name is Dain Corvane, and I hunted the gifted for nine years. I put the irons on children who reminded me of myself, and I told myself it was law, and law was the word I used instead of fear. I interrogated Ivet of the Low Ward until she broke, and I watched them take her, and I slept that night. I slept. I burned an archive on orders and grieved it like a man who hadn't struck the match. I guarded a door and called it failing to guard it, and I have hated myself for the mercy and loved myself for the mercy, and both of those are true, and I am done trying to burn one of them out of me."

He pulled the iron cuffs from beneath his sleeves—the second pair, forged in the enclave, that he had worn since removing the first at the Spire's base. He had told himself he wore them out of caution. He knew better now. They were penance he could touch.

He set them on the kitchen table, beside the flour, beside the bread he would never eat, and the iron rang against wood with the small clear sound of a verdict.

"My guilt stays," he said. "It climbs with me. It sits with her memories at the summit if it must. And if your bargain requires one of us empty and one of us full—" he was moving now, walking toward the door, toward Paloma, toward the stairs that waited above the black threshold, "—then your bargain is just the first king's theft wearing a kinder face, and I have spent my whole life serving men who made that trade. Never again. Not even for my mother's kitchen."

The room exhaled.

It happened slowly, the way frost leaves a window: the flour graying, the bread flattening to a disc of char, the sea going out of the window and the tanner's yard coming back, the walls sweating soot, his mother's face resolving not into nothing but into something older—a vast attention, patient and wounded and terribly tired, regarding him from every surface at once.

*Few refuse,* it said, and the voice was no longer hers. It was the voice of coals settling, of a banked hearth deciding whether to die. *Fewer refuse kindly. You named yourself rather than letting us name you. That has not happened in a hundred years.*

"Is that approval?"

*It is acknowledgment.* A pause, vast as weather. *Climb, Dain Corvane. Carry it. See what the top asks of a man who insists on remaining heavy.*

The kitchen folded away like a hand closing. The stairs stood open above them, rising into gold.

* * *

Paloma caught his arm as he came through the doorway. She was pale, and her hands were shaking, and he realized she had heard all of it—every word he'd flung at a tower, every crime he'd confessed aloud for the first time in his life.

"Well," she managed. "Now the walls know too. No pressure."

A laugh escaped him—cracked, disbelieving, real. "No," he agreed. "Only the weight of history."

"Between friends." She squeezed his arm and let go, and turned toward the stairs, and Corvane followed her up out of the eighth gallery lighter and heavier than he had ever been in his life, the guilt still on him like a coat he had finally chosen to wear instead of one nailed to his shoulders.

They had gained perhaps two flights when the sound reached them.

Not from above. From below—from far below, down through the root of the Spire, through the galleries they had already survived: iron on stone, many boots in step, and beneath it the thin bright shriek of warden-forged binding chains being swung loose from their hooks.

Paloma froze, head cocked. "Tamsin's holding the gates. Tamsin swore—"

"They got through anyway." Corvane was already counting voices, the old hunter's habit he could not kill. Thirty, maybe forty. Too many for the enclave's remnant to have merely stumbled. Someone had given them the breach point—or sold it. "They're in the Spire's root. They'll climb fast, and they'll climb angry, and they know these stairs better than we do. Half of them trained on siege drills in this very tower."

"How long?"

He listened once more, to the rhythm of the advance, the pauses where officers regrouped.

"Dawn," he said. "Perhaps less."

Above them, through the shattered vaulting, the covenant-light pulsed once—slow, expectant, hungry—like a great heart preparing to receive them, or like a door about to close.

Paloma tightened the straps of the Codex against her chest and started to run.
