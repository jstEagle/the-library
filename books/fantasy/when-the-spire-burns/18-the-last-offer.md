## 18. The Last Offer

The summit was smaller than Paloma had imagined.

All her life she had pictured the Spire's crown as a vast platform, a stage fit for kings and dragons, the kind of place where history was made loudly. Instead there was only a broken circle of fused stone no wider than her kitchen had been, ringed by stumps of columns melted like candle wax, and at its center—a hollow. A bowl of black glass where the flame had once lived, holding nothing now but ash and a heat that came from somewhere deeper than the stone.

The wind up here did not howl. It *waited*.

Paloma stood at the edge of the hollow with the Ember Codex pressed against her chest, its leather cover warm as a sleeping animal, and felt the whole city spread out below her—Vellamark in its smoke and lamplight, the temple spires, the night market's scattered embers of color, the rivers of people Tamsin had set moving through the streets like blood through a body. Somewhere down there, Corvane was still climbing. She had heard his boots on the stairs behind her for hours, steady as a heartbeat she refused to rely on.

The Codex opened itself in her hands. She hadn't touched it. The pages turned on their own, slow and deliberate, until they found what they had been waiting all this way to show her: not terms, not law, but an invitation written in a hand that trembled.

*A willing heart ascends. A willing heart gives what it loves best remembered. The fire does not take. The fire receives.*

"I know," Paloma whispered. "I know what you want."

She had rehearsed this. That was the terrible thing—she had sat by a smuggler's fire two nights ago with the Codex open on her knees and made herself say the names aloud, one by one, so that when the moment came she would not falter. Her mother's voice, low and off-key, singing harvest songs while kneading bread. Ivet's laugh, sharp and sudden, the way she'd snorted when Paloma told a joke badly. Mother Sefu's hands over hers, teaching her the first letter-sigils. The smell of the archive. The weight of a button in a child's palm. The night market, all of it, every lantern and haggled coin and lie about the weather in her fingertips' warmth.

She had inventoried her life like a scribe counting stock, because that was the only way she knew how to face a price.

But rehearsal, she understood now, standing before the hollow, had been a kindness her mind had done her without permission. You could list your memories the way you listed debts. You could not feel them in advance. Feeling was going to happen here, in real time, one theft at a time—and she would have to stand still for every one of them.

The hollow breathed.

It was not fire yet. It was the memory of fire, the shape fire leaves behind, and it looked at her—not with eyes, nothing so simple, but with attention, vast and old and unbearably patient. She felt it sort through her the way she sorted through the archive's shelves, gentle and thorough, looking for the first page.

"Wait." Her voice cracked on it. "Please—I need to say something first. While I still—" She swallowed. "While I'm still me."

The attention held, and did not press.

Paloma knelt at the rim of the hollow. Below her, far below, the city was screaming—not with terror but with defiance; she could hear it even here, even through the wind, the sound of Tamsin's people holding streets that had never belonged to them, buying minutes with their bodies. She thought of Tamsin saying, in that flat smuggler's drawl: *Don't make it holy. Holy is what kings use to get other people killed.* 

"All right," Paloma said. "I'll try not to make it holy."

* * *

The first memory went gently, which was the cruelest part.

It took the market first—the whole of it, entire, lifted out of her like a splinter drawn clean. One moment she was kneeling in the warm dark remembering lanterns strung between stalls like captive stars, the smell of fried dough and wet wool, Old Marta shouting prices over the crowd—and then the remembering *ended*, not faded, ended, cut off mid-image the way a door closes on a lit room, and Paloma gasped because she could feel the hole where it had been. Not pain. Absence. A tooth missing from the tongue's map of the mouth.

She reached instinctively for the detail that should have followed—the button, the child, the small charm she'd made anyway—and found smooth blankness, like a page torn out of a ledger.

"That one," she said, laughing, or something near laughing, tears running freely down her face. "You started with the market? I thought you'd start with my mother."

The flame received without judgment. And then, softly, almost apologetically, it gave her back the feeling without the pictures: a warmth in the chest, a tenderness with nothing attached, love pointing at a door that no longer existed. She knew she had loved something. She would always know that. She just would never know what.

Below, distantly, through the stone, she felt the gates shudder.

* * *

Tamsin stood on the barricade at the Spire's root with a stolen warden's pike in both hands and blood in one eye, and counted her people the way you count coins you already know are short.

The crown had brought everything. Martial law had emptied the barracks into the streets, and now the square around the Spire's base was a grinding crush of shields and torchlight, and the enclave's line—the smugglers, the marked, the ones who owed nothing to anyone and had chosen this anyway—was bending like green wood. They were not winning. Winning had never been the plan. The plan was minutes. Every minute the wardens spent breaking against the barricade was a minute Paloma climbed.

"Hold!" Tamsin screamed, voice gone to gravel. "Hold the line, you beautiful bastards!"

Beside her, a boy named Fitch—sixteen, charm-scars up both arms, who'd followed her out of the undercity because she'd once fed him—went down under a shield-blow and didn't get up. She dragged him back by his collar with one hand and kept the pike up with the other, and did not let herself look at his face, because looking would cost her ten seconds and ten seconds was a life up the Spire.

A woman beside her—a dyer from the enclave whose name Tamsin shamefully couldn't recall in the moment, and hated herself for it—took a crossbow bolt through the shoulder and kept swinging. "Go help the left flank," the dyer gritted. Tamsin went. When she glanced back three breaths later, the woman was down and someone else had taken her place, and there was no time to learn anyone's name anymore. There was only the arithmetic of it: bodies traded for stairs, stairs bought for a girl none of them had known a season ago.

"Don't make it holy," Tamsin muttered, parrying, slashing, bleeding. But it didn't feel unholy. It felt like the most honest work she'd ever done.

* * *

On the summit, the second memory fought her.

Her mother came up out of her like a root coming out of soil—all of it tangled through everything else. The flame pulled and Paloma's whole inner landscape shifted; she saw, in one last flood, the small stone kitchen, the flour-dusted table, her mother's forearms bare and strong, the off-key humming that had been the sound of safety itself. She clawed at it. Not deliberately—her body simply refused, the way a drowning person refuses water, some animal core of her thrashing against the giving.

"No—wait—I'm not ready, I haven't said goodbye properly, I never—"

The flame paused. Waited. It would not take unwillingly; that was written in the Codex, that was the whole covenant, willingness was the entire architecture of it. Which meant the choice was real, every single time. There was no current to sweep her along. She had to open her hand for each thing, individually, forever.

And that, she realized, was why it had to be memories of the loved. Not suffering, not penance—love. Because only love was heavy enough to matter, and only a gift given freely, piece by piece, was a covenant and not a robbery. The first king had robbed the fire. She was going to be the opposite of the first king, one memory at a time.

Paloma closed her eyes. Opened her hand.

"Mama," she whispered. "Sing it wrong one more time."

The kitchen dissolved. The humming stopped mid-note. And Paloma knelt in the ash-hollow's light knowing only that a woman had existed whom she had loved beyond language, whose voice she would spend the rest of her life hearing as warmth-with-no-source, and wept without stopping, and did not withdraw her consent.

* * *

Corvane broke onto the last flight of stairs with blood sheeting down his arm and his iron cuffs gone—gone since the dragon's shadow at the Spire's base, since Vessharran had shown him the truth in shared memory and he had chosen to be burned rather than blind. His own gift rose in him unrestrained now, wild and half-trained, and the Spire's smoldering galleries had tested him with visions of his worst choices all the way up: Ivet's face. Mother Sefu's broken hands. Eleven shadows slipping through a door he'd failed to guard.

He crested the summit and stopped.

Paloma knelt at the hollow's edge, haloed in ember-light, and she was *unraveling*. He could see it—he could see the memories leaving her as faint gold threads, streaming from her temples and her chest and dissolving into the dark glass below, each one a small bright ribbon of everything she was. Her face was wrecked with crying and utterly calm at once, the calm of deep water under storm.

"Paloma."

Her head turned. For a moment—two heartbeats, three—she looked at him with polite incomprehension, the way you look at a stranger, and his heart stopped dead in his chest.

Then something reassembled behind her eyes. "Corvane." Relief and grief in one syllable. "You're supposed to be holding the stair."

"The stair's holding." He crossed the broken circle, dropped to his knees beside her, close enough to touch. The heat from the hollow pressed against his face like an opening furnace door. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't. You know there isn't. We read the same book." She smiled, trembling. "Mutual offering. Human memory for dragon-fire. Someone has to pay the family debt, Dain. Turns out it's me."

"You don't even know what you'll lose next."

"No." She looked down into the hollow, and her expression softened into something he'd seen on her a hundred times in the night market, bent over a child's button: weary tenderness. "But I know what I'm losing it *for*. That has to be enough. It has to be, or no one could ever do this."

He grabbed her wrist. "Then give it slowly. As slowly as you can. Talk to me while you do it. Say the names. Say everything—if it's spoken, I can keep it. I've been writing since the dragon's shadow. Give me the words and I will put them in ink, and ink survives, Paloma. Ink is what I have instead of faith."

She stared at him. Understanding moved across her ruined face like weather.

"You're going to write me," she said.

"I already started."

Something in her broke open—not the way things break, the way things *bloom*. She laughed, wet and ragged, and squeezed his hand hard enough to hurt. "Then listen. Listen to all of it. My mother's name was Odile. She sang harvest songs and got every note wrong. Ivet laughed like a sneeze and stole my hair ribbons and screamed my name when they took her, and I did nothing, and I have regretted it every day since, and I forgive myself for it exactly none percent—write that down, write down that forgiveness isn't owed just because it's deserved—"

The flame drew the third thread from her. Ivet went. Paloma staggered, caught herself on Corvane's arm, and kept talking, faster now, pouring herself out in words as fast as the fire poured her out in light, racing it, cheating it, feeding him her life one sentence ahead of the taking.

"My name is Paloma Sterling. Temple scribe. Debtor. Charm-crafter of no talent whatsoever except the talent that mattered. I liked winter best. I liked the archive at closing time. I was afraid of deep water. I loved—" she faltered, groped, found it— "I loved a man with iron cuffs who copied one page before he burned an archive, and that page saved the world, tell him so, tell him I said so—"

"Paloma—"

"—and the fourth thing is coming now," she whispered, and her grip on his hand went slack, and her eyes were filling with a stranger's gentleness, "and I can't remember what the fourth thing is, Dain, what's the fourth thing, please, you read it, you read it to me—"

The hollow roared.

Light came up out of the black glass like dawn reversed—not consuming but *receiving*, gold flooding the broken circle, flooding the stairwell, pouring down the Spire's throat toward the city and the realm beyond, and the dying star below the world flared once, twice, and caught. The covenant turning over in its long sleep. Dragon-fire waking after a generation of famine, rising to remember the city for it.

Paloma swayed at the rim of the blaze, hollowed, radiant, holding nothing of herself but the present moment and the hand of a man she no longer knew.

She turned to him with a brow furrowed, sweet and lost, and asked:

"Why are you crying?"

And Corvane—half-blinded by the light, his arm burning, his pockets full of pages—pulled the little book from his coat, held it between them like a lantern, and said:

"Because I'm the only one left who knows."
