## 20. The Written Woman

The healers of the enclave said she would wake when the fire was done with her, and not before.

Corvane did not believe them, exactly. He believed in ledgers, in testimony, in the slow arithmetic of evidence—and so he sat at her bedside in the smugglers' safehouse above the tanneries and did the only accounting left to him. Paloma Sterling breathed. Her chest rose beneath a blanket that smelled of tallow and river salt. Her hands, which had once carried a warmth he had spent his career hunting out of others, lay open on the coverlet like two emptied purses.

Three days since the summit. Three days since the Spire had stood up out of its own ashes like a struck bell, and the fire had come down through the city not as destruction but as rain—memory falling back into streets that had forgotten what they were mourning. He had watched an old woman in the Cordwainer's Row stop mid-step and begin to weep, clutching her own face, saying her sister's name over and over, a sister dead forty years whose face she could suddenly see again. He had felt it himself: the smell of his mother's kitchen, gone since he was nine years old, arriving all at once behind his eyes with the force of a blow.

Paloma had given all of hers. And none of it came back to her.

* * *

She woke on the fourth morning, and the first thing she said was "Who are you?"—not with fear, but with the flat politeness of a scribe addressing a stranger across a counter.

He had rehearsed an answer for three days. He found he could not use any of it.

"Someone who owes you," he said finally. "My name is Dain Corvane."

"Corvane." She tested it the way she must have tested everything now—turning it over, looking for a seam, finding nothing inside. "That name feels like a document I've read. An arrest warrant, maybe." A pause. "Should I be frightened of you?"

"You were," he said. "Once. You stopped."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

They regarded each other across the narrow room. She looked smaller than the woman from the Spire—the one who had climbed burning galleries with soot in her hair and fury in her spine—but her eyes were the same, dark and level, cataloguing him. Except that was wrong too, he realized. The cataloguing was different now. Before, she had looked at him as though reading a page she distrusted. Now she looked at him the way one looks at a page written in a language almost, but not quite, remembered.

"I don't know anything," she said quietly. It was not self-pity. It was inventory. "I know how to read. I know Vellamark. I know—" her hand moved, an unconscious gesture, thumb pressed to fingertips, and a faint warmth shimmered in the air between them, a charm half-formed and abandoned. She stared at her own hand. "I know this. But I don't know why I know it. I don't know my mother's name. I don't know if I had a mother." Her voice did not break. That was somehow worse. "I reached for her and there is nothing there. Not even an absence shaped like something. Just—smooth stone, all the way down."

"The flame took them," Corvane said. "What you gave it."

"And what I gave it?"

"Everything you loved." He swallowed. "You told me some of it. On the climb."

Her eyes sharpened. "Told you what?"

And there it was—the question he had been turning over for three days like a coin bitten for gold. He rose, went to his satchel by the wall, and brought back the bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He set it on the bed between them without ceremony, because ceremony would have been a lie; he was not giving her a gift, he was returning stolen property. Property he had stolen from the fire itself.

* * *

He had started writing on the first night of the climb, in the gallery of drowned bells, and he had not understood at first why his hand was moving.

She had been talking. That was the thing about her under pressure—she narrated. He had noticed it during their ambush at the ford, during the long cold walk after: fear made her talkative, and grief made her precise. As the Spire's fire had shown her its visions, testing her, she had fought back the only way a scribe fights—with recitation. She had spoken her memories aloud to keep them, naming them like entries in a ledger while the fire tried to blur them, and Corvane, walking behind her with ink freezing in his nib, had begun to write them down.

Not all of them. No one could have taken down all of them. But the ones she repeated. The ones she returned to like hands returning to a wound.

*A blue dress. My mother wore a blue dress to the harvest fair and sang off-key on purpose so I would laugh. Her voice was low. When she hummed the windows fogged.*

*Ivet. My friend Ivet, dragged out of the scriptorium screaming my name. If I live, I live carrying that.*

*A button. A child paid me a button for a charm and I charmed it anyway, because some things shouldn't be priced.*

He had filled forty pages by the summit. Sixty by the time the fire took her. He had written with his burned hand shaking, with the Spire coming apart around them, with Tamsin's people dying somewhere below in the streets they'd sworn to hold—he had written because it was the single act available to him that was not watching. And when the flame rose up around her and began to drink her down, memory by memory, he had shouted into it—not prayers, he didn't pray, never had—but names. Her mother's name, which she'd given him at the ninth gallery. Ivet's name. *Paloma.* His own handwriting, he understood dimly even then, was a bucket thrown at a wildfire. He wrote anyway.

Now he unwrapped the oilcloth, and the candlelight fell on pages warped with heat and stained with soot and, here and there, spattered with blood that was probably his.

"What is it?" she asked.

"It's yours," he said. "Or—it was going to be lost. I wrote down what you told me. Everything you spoke aloud, from the moment we entered the root to—" He stopped. "This is what the fire took. What survives of it exists in ink."

She looked at the bundle for a long time. Then she drew it toward her, and her hands—those deft, warm hands, now strangers to themselves—began to unwrap it with the care of someone handling a relic.

* * *

She read aloud. He did not ask her to; she simply did, perhaps because reading silently felt impossible, or perhaps because some instinct older than memory told her these words needed air.

"A blue dress," she read. Her brow creased. "Blue dress. Harvest fair." Nothing moved behind her eyes. She turned the page. "*When she hummed the windows fogged.*" She frowned harder. "Fogged windows. I can see—I can almost—" She pressed her palm flat against her sternum. "There's something. Not a memory. More like the shape where a memory would sit. Like a footprint in snow after the snow has melted. Do you understand? I know that something happened there. I can feel the edges of it."

"Some things return," Corvane said. "Vessharran warned—the dragon said the covenant's reforging would leave echoes. Fire remembers fire. Some memories may regrow, like a burn healing." He hesitated. "Some won't."

"Which are which?"

"No one knows. Not even the dragon."

She kept reading. Page by page, hour by hour, while the tannery light shifted from amber to grey outside the shutters. She read about her debt to the temple, and her face went hard with a recognition that had no feeling attached to it, the way one recognizes a law one has never broken. She read about Ivet, and stopped, and read it twice, and her eyes were dry but her jaw was white.

"She screamed my name," Paloma said. "Your notes say that twice. You wrote it down twice."

"You said it twice."

"Why would I say it twice?"

"Because you blamed yourself," he said, before he could decide whether to spare her. He was learning something about this new Paloma: she did not want sparing. She wanted data, even when the data cut. "Because you passed your test by numbing your hands in ice water, and she failed hers, and she screamed your name as they took her away. You carried it up the whole Spire."

Paloma set the page down. She looked at her hands for a long moment. Then she said, "Good," in a voice that shook only slightly, and he understood that she was relieved—not to have the guilt, but to have *anything*, even guilt, even pain, because pain was proof of continuity, pain meant there was still a her underneath the smooth stone.

"Read me more," she said. "The button. Read me the button."

* * *

By evening, Tamsin came, bringing broth and news and her usual economy of sentiment. She stood in the doorway looking at the two of them—the inquisitor and the thief, heads bent together over a bundle of scorched pages like novices over scripture—and her scarred face did something complicated that she flattened immediately into a scowl.

"Holdings on the gates held," she reported. "Mostly. We lost nine. Marta's people are calling it the Night of Small Fires, which is stupid, and will stick anyway. The crown's council dissolved itself this morning. Half ran, half are begging parley." She set down the broth. "There's a delegation coming from Ashkarat within the week. Actual dragons, or their people, whatever dragons send instead of people. They want to talk terms. Real ones, this time, both sides giving." Her eyes flicked to Paloma. "They asked for you by name. The dragon told them your name."

"My name," Paloma repeated. She touched her own mouth, briefly, as if checking it was still there.

"They'll want the story," Tamsin said. "Of what you did. Of what it cost. The city's already making up versions—half of them have you ascending in glory with wings of flame, half have you as a martyr the Inquisition murdered. Neither's true. Someone's going to have to tell them what actually happened." She jerked her chin at the pages. "Sounds like he's been doing that job already."

After she left, Paloma was quiet for a long time. The candle had burned down to a stub. Corvane gathered his pens and ink to give her darkness and rest, and her voice stopped him at the door.

"Inquisitor Corvane. Dain." She stumbled slightly on the given name, trying it as one tries a key in a lock. "Why did you write it down?"

He considered lying, and found he had run out of the taste for it somewhere on the eleventh gallery of the Spire, with his iron cuffs in his pocket and his gift singing in his veins for the first time in twenty years.

"Because you were giving yourself away," he said. "Piece by piece, to save everyone—including me, including a city that would have burned you a month ago. And it seemed—" His throat tightened. He was not a man accustomed to the architecture of his own feelings being exposed; he had built iron walls around them for decades and called the building duty. "It seemed intolerable that the price should be total. That you should pay everything and no record should remain of what was paid. I am a keeper of records. It is the only prayer I know."

"That's not love," she said, "but it might be adjacent to it."

His breath caught. "You don't remember—"

"No," she said. "I don't remember anything about you except what's in those pages. And what's in those pages is a man who walked into a fire beside a stranger and wrote her life down while it was being taken from her." She looked at him across the darkening room, and there was something in her gaze that was not memory, and was not quite new either—an echo, a footprint in melted snow. "So tell me honestly, Dain Corvane. Is that who you are? Or is that who I made you, in the telling?"

He thought about Mother Sefu, who had forged a history to protect a lie. He thought about the archive he had refused to burn, the door he had failed to guard, the cuffs he had removed. He thought about how a man becomes what he does, and how a story, told truly enough, becomes a self.

"I think," he said slowly, "that I became that man somewhere on the climb, and that you were holding the pen as much as I was. I think identity is collaborative. I think yours was just—taken by force, and mine was surrendered voluntarily, and the difference matters less than I used to believe."

She nodded, as if he had answered correctly. Then: "Then we should finish the book."

"Book?"

"Those are loose pages. Notes. Testimony." A ghost of the market-seller's practicality crossed her face. "A person deserves a book. Start at the beginning. My mother's name. The blue dress. Write it properly, with chapters. And when we reach the parts I don't have—" she tapped her temple, "—you'll have to fill the gaps from the outside. What I looked like. What I said. What I was, to other people. Because right now, Dain Corvane, you are the only author I have."

Outside, far to the north, the new Spire caught the last light of the sun and threw it back in a slow pulse, like a heartbeat seen through skin. Somewhere in the city, a hundred thousand returned memories were settling into their owners like rain into drought-cracked earth. Somewhere beyond the horizon, dragons were gathering.

And in a small room above the tanneries, a hollowed woman opened a fresh page, and handed a man a pen.

"Begin," she said, "with her voice."
