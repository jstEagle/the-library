## 21. A New Covenant

The summons came on a morning so clean it felt like an accusation.

Tamsin Vey had been mending a net behind her warehouse on the Salt Quay when the herald's boat bumped against her pilings, all gilt trim and nervous rowers, and a man in livery too fine for the water climbed down uninvited. He held out a scroll sealed in white wax, and Tamsin looked at it the way she looked at anything that glittered: with suspicion and a certain professional assessment of how much it might fence for.

"They want you at the Assembly Hall," the herald said. "There is to be a ceremony."

"There's to be no such thing," Tamsin said, and went back to her net.

The herald stood there long enough for gulls to judge him, then left the scroll weighted under a stone on her dock. She read it that evening by lamplight, mostly out of boredom, and learned that the Provisional Council of Vellamark wished to honor *Tamsin Vey, smuggler, of the Salt Quay*, for "extraordinary service to the city in its hour of need," and that she was invited—*invited*—to receive a commendation, a pardon for all prior offenses, and a seat among the honored guests at the first open negotiations between Vellamark and the realm of Ashkarat in a hundred years.

She used the scroll to patch a hole in the net. It was good paper. It held better than expected.

* * *

The trouble with heroes, Tamsin thought, was that everyone wanted one, and nobody wanted to feed one.

The city had spent six months learning to say words it had outlawed a generation ago. *Magic.* *Gift.* *Covenant.* They came off tongues carefully at first, like coins suspected of being clipped, then easier, then everywhere—the word *gifted* chalked on doorways where once it would have summoned the Inquisition, now meaning nothing worse than *we know, and we're not ashamed*. The temple's great doors stayed shut since the night Mother Sefu's confession had been read aloud in three markets and nailed to the cathedral itself. Scribes had scattered like startled pigeons. Some had come down to the quays asking after work, and Tamsin, to her own lasting irritation, had hired two of them, because they could keep books and she could not.

And above it all, visible from every street and rooftop and gutter in the city, the Spire burned.

Not destroyed—*burning*, which was different, which was alive. The flame that ran up its ruined length was not the hungry orange of ordinary fire but something older, a gold shot through with red, and when the wind came off Ashkarat you could smell it faintly: hot stone and rain and something sweet, like the memory of honey. People had stopped flinching from it. Children played a game called spire-light, holding their hands up to catch the glow. And every night, when the flame pulsed slow and steady like a sleeping heart, the whole city seemed to breathe with it, though if you asked anyone what exactly had come back to them in those first days after the reforging—the memories, the stolen years, the names of streets and grandmothers and old songs—they only shook their heads, half-smiling, unable to say what was new because it all felt like it had always been there.

That was the thing about dragon-fire, Tamsin had decided. It didn't give you back your past like a parcel delivered. It made your past *true* again, and truth, once settled, forgets it was ever missing.

She hadn't gotten much herself. A few flashes—a mother's hands braiding rope, a doorway in the Low Roads that no longer existed, the taste of some stew she'd been chasing ever since without success. Tamsin figured the fire gave according to need, and she'd never needed much beyond what she could carry. But she'd watched other people weep in the streets over returned faces, watched old men suddenly remember why they'd hated each other, watched the whole city stagger upright like a patient waking from a fever, and she supposed that counted for something.

What she couldn't abide was the gratitude industry that had sprung up around it.

* * *

They found her anyway, of course. Honor always did; it was like the tide that way.

The Assembly Hall had been re-roofed since the riots—Tamsin's people had done half of it, charging fair rates and pocketing a third of the materials, because a rebuilt city was the greatest smuggling opportunity in living memory and she'd have been a fool to waste it—and inside, the hall smelled of fresh timber and new paint laid thick over old smoke. The Council sat in a crescent on a dais, seven of them, wearing chains of office that had belonged to men now fled or dead. And beside them, unmistakable even seated, even robed in grey instead of iron, sat Inquisitor Dain Corvane.

He wasn't an Inquisitor anymore. There wasn't an Inquisition anymore. He was something the paperwork kept calling *Envoy to Ashkarat*, which Tamsin privately translated as *the man who talks to dragons because nobody else has the nerve*. He looked thinner than he had at the Spire's base, and there was scarring along his jaw and neck that the robe didn't quite hide, burn-marks in the shape of climbing. His hands rested on his knees, ink-stained, and he watched the room the way he'd once watched fugitives: completely.

Tamsin stopped at the foot of the dais and did not bow.

"Tamsin Vey." The senior councilor, a soft-bellied man named Ostric who had spent the occupation safely invisible and emerged afterward full of principles, spread his arms. "Vellamark owes you a debt beyond measure. The gates of the Spire were held by ordinary people that day. You held them longest."

"I paid people to hold them," Tamsin said. "It's not the same thing, whatever the ballads say."

"The ballads," Ostric continued smoothly, "are evidence of the city's love."

"The ballads are evidence that two fiddlers survived the fighting and needed work." She crossed her arms. "What do you actually want?"

A flicker of distaste crossed Ostric's face, quickly mastered. Beside him, Corvane's mouth did something that in a less disciplined man would have been a smile.

"We wish," Ostric said, "to offer you formal thanks. A pardon, as stated. And a position. The harbor watch requires rebuilding from the keel up, and there is no one in this city who knows the water lanes, the hidden passages, and the character of every man and woman who works below as you do. We would make you Harbormaster."

The word dropped into the silence like a coin into a well. Tamsin heard it hit bottom and echo.

"Harbormaster," she repeated.

"With a house. On the Hill."

"A house on the Hill." She let that sit a moment, savoring it. "You want to put me, a convicted smuggler with eleven outstanding warrants—you'll find them in the archive, assuming you haven't burned that yet—in charge of policing the waterfront I've spent twenty years teaching to lie to people exactly like you."

"The warrants are pardoned," Ostric said stiffly.

"The *knowledge* isn't," Tamsin said. "That's what you're buying. Not me. My maps. My people. My ability to walk into any den on the Salt Quay and walk out again with my throat intact. You can't buy that with a title, councilor, because a title is worth exactly as much as the next council that decides to hang me with it. I've seen what happens when the crown offers trust. I helped bury the last lot who took it."

Ostric's face had gone the color of old candle wax. One of the younger councilors, a woman with ink-stained fingers and the look of someone who'd actually worked for a living, leaned forward. "Then what *do* you want, Mistress Vey? Everyone wants something."

"Timber at cost," Tamsin said promptly. "Tar. Rope. Lantern oil. Grain shipments inspected quickly and honestly, because my people are hauling relief cargo upriver and your customs men are robbing them blind at the toll bridges. And—" she glanced at Corvane, "—whatever arrangement the envoy here is building with Ashkarat, I want my ships listed as neutral. Both realms' ships. If there's going to be trade across the water, someone's going to have to carry it, and it may as well be people who already know how to move goods nobody official wants to ask about."

"That's—" the young councilor began.

"That's a business proposal," Tamsin said. "Write it down. I'll wait."

* * *

They wrote it down. It took three hours, most of which Tamsin spent eating the Assembly Hall's refreshments and distributing the best of them into her pockets for the dock boys outside.

Corvane walked her out himself.

They went down the great steps together, past the new memorial—unfinished, a slab of black stone waiting for names, though whose names and how many had already started the fights that would outlast everyone present—and out into the square where the market stalls had crept back like grass through cobblestones. The Spire's light lay over everything, gold-red, steady.

"You could have taken it," Corvane said. "The position. Half the city expected you to."

"Half the city doesn't know me." Tamsin rolled her shoulders, working out the ache the cold always put there. The scars from the gate-fighting had healed ugly across her back and shoulder; some mornings she couldn't lift her arm past her ribs. She didn't mention it. "You take their title, you start caring what they think. You start wanting the next one. I've seen honest people turn into councilors. It's not pretty, and it's usually quick."

"And the pardon?"

"Paper burns." She shrugged. "I keep my own records."

Corvane almost smiled. Up close, in daylight, the burn-scars looked worse and the tiredness looked worse too. There was a book-shaped bulge under his robe, and Tamsin, who noticed such things professionally, guessed it never left him.

"How is she?" she asked, before she could decide not to.

He was quiet for a few steps. Down in the square, a charm-seller had set up a stall openly—openly!—little woven knots and warmed stones and bits of dragon-glass, and a crowd of children stood around her with the solemnity of scholars.

"She remembers the climb," Corvane said at last. "She remembers the choice. Everything after—" He stopped. Started again. "I write it down for her. Every day. What she told me on the way up, what the others tell me, what I saw. Some mornings she reads a page and says, *that sounds like someone I'd have liked.*" His voice held steady, which must have cost him. "But yesterday she charmed a button for a child in the market. Without knowing why. Her hands remembered when nothing else did."

Tamsin looked at the charm-seller's stall, and understood, and said nothing about it, because some things weren't hers to carry.

"The dragons," she said instead. "How's the talking going?"

"Slowly. They live a very long time and hold grudges longer. Vessharran has agreed to send an emissary to the Council." A pause, dry as ash. "The emissary arrives tomorrow. By air. Over the harbor. I have tried to explain to the harbormaster's office that panic will be counterproductive."

"Your harbormaster's office," Tamsin said, "is a pack of fools who've never seen anything bigger than a barge. They'll sound alarm bells. They'll loose arrows. Someone will try to tax it."

"Which is why," Corvane said, turning to face her fully, and there it was, the look she remembered from a flooded tunnel and a burning Spire, patient and utterly unsurprised, "the Council has authorized me to offer you a commission. Not a title. A task. Tomorrow, when the emissary comes down over the water, we need eyes on the quays that the quays actually listen to. Keep your people calm, keep the customs men sober, and keep anyone with a crossbow from doing something unforgivable." He paused. "There's pay."

"There's always pay," Tamsin said. "That's what makes it work."

She looked out at the harbor—at her harbor, whatever papers said—where the evening light was coming down gold and the fishing boats were running in ahead of the dark, and beyond them, far off over the water, the faint smudge of mountains that was the edge of Ashkarat, closer than it had been in a hundred years. The Spire's flame pulsed overhead, slow, patient, keeping the city's memory like a lamp in a window.

"All right," she said. "One day. Eyes on the quays, customs men sober, crossbows holstered. And envoy—if your dragon scares my dock boys, you're paying for the drinks to settle them."

"It's agreed," Corvane said gravely, and Tamsin had known him long enough now to hear the laughter underneath.

She turned toward home along the quay, past the mended nets and the new-built cranes, past the memorial stone still waiting for its names, and allowed herself—one time, briefly, where nobody could see—the smallest smile. Rebuilding, it turned out, was just smuggling with better manners. And tomorrow the sky over her harbor would hold a dragon, and she intended to be standing on her own roof with her arms crossed and her terms ready.

Above her, the flame burned on, and the tide came in, and the work waited, as it always did.
