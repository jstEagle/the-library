## 22. Small Charms, Again

The night market smelled the same as it always had—or so people told her. Woodsmoke and frying dough, wet stone from the canal-side stalls, the green bite of fresh herbs crushed between a herbalist's fingers as she haggled over bundles of feverleaf. Paloma could not verify any of it from memory. She knew these things the way she knew the alphabet: because someone had written them down for her, in a careful hand, on pages she now carried everywhere like a second skin.

But knowing, she was learning, was not nothing.

She set up her stall an hour before dusk, as the lamplighters made their rounds along the Vellamark quays with long poles tipped in ordinary fire—the kind that came from oil and wick, though even that felt different now, felt *attended*, since the Spire had burned bright again. Her table was a plank across two barrels, same as before. Her wares were laid out on a square of undyed cloth: small charms in rows, each tagged with a scrap of paper bearing her own cramped, unmistakable scribe's hand.

A charm against chilblains. A charm for safe crossing of the ferry at high water. A charm to keep milk sweet through a warm night. A charm for courage, which sold best and which she priced honestly, having once needed it herself more than anyone could guess.

None of them were hidden now. That was the strangest thing—not the magic itself, which lived in her fingertips the way warmth lives in a held cup, but the *openness*. For most of her life she had worked with her sleeves pulled down, her hands curled shut, the gift a coal banked under ash lest one spark betray her. Now her hands lay bare on the plank in the lantern light, palms up while she waited, and no one flinched, and no crier called her name into the dark, and the only iron in sight was the rusted old lock on the temple's abandoned debt-ledger, which sat at the corner of her table as a kind of joke between herself and Tamsin, who had pried it out of the archive rubble and insisted it was "good luck, or at least good irony."

The debt was gone. All of it. The crown's reckoning after the fall of the old order had dissolved the temple's claims along with the temple's authority, and Paloma had watched her name struck from the ledger by a clerk with ink-stained fingers and an expression of profound relief, as if striking names were the only mercy his office had ever been allowed. She remembered none of the years the debt had ruled her. She knew them the way she knew the market smells: secondhand, written down, read aloud to her on slow evenings by a man who had once hunted her and now read to her like it was the most natural work in the world.

*You owed nine hundred crowns,* Corvane had told her once, turning a page. *You counted coins in your sleep. You hated the smell of sealing wax because it meant another notice nailed to the scribes' door.*

*Do I still hate it?*

He had smiled—that slow, unaccustomed smile, like a door opening in a wall everyone believed was solid. *You bought sealing wax last week. For the letters you write to the Ashkarat envoys. So I'd say the hatred didn't survive.*

She did not remember writing those letters either. But they existed, in her hand, full of her own turns of phrase, and reading them gave her the peculiar vertigo of meeting a self she trusted completely without being able to recall becoming her.

The first customer arrived while the light was still going amber over the rooftops: a fishwife wanting a charm against gulls, paid for properly with three coppers and a grin. Then a ferryman's wife, then two apprentices off shift, pooling their coins for one shared charm of good dreams. Word had spread, the way word does, that the charms sold at the third stall past the canal arch were honest ones—some made by a human hand with a human gift, and some, marked with a tiny pressed scale at the base, given freely by a dragon older than the city itself.

Vessharran sent gifts downriver twice a season now. The old dragon could not fly far anymore; half-blind, heavy-winged, he kept to the hot springs at the edge of the Ashkarat shallows where the covenant-fire pooled and steamed. But he remembered Paloma—or rather, he remembered *for* her, which was its own strange tenderness. He would press a claw-tip to cooling stone and leave behind small things: a bead of glass fused from ash and river sand that hummed faintly when held by someone grieving; a sliver of scale polished smooth, warm as a held hand no matter the weather; a pebble that glowed ember-red in complete darkness, which mothers bought for children afraid of the night.

Paloma sold those too. She never marked up the price. It seemed wrong to profit from a gift she couldn't remember receiving, though Vessharran, when she had told him this—shouting politely across the steam, because the old dragon's hearing had gone selective—had rumbled something she had asked Corvane to transcribe:

*Tell her the memory is not the gift. Tell her the giving is the gift. She will understand when she forgets me too.*

She understood now. Or she understood the shape of it, the way you understand the shape of a room in the dark, by walking its edges.

Dusk settled. The lanterns took over from the sky. The market filled the way markets fill—not all at once but by accretion, a body of noise and warmth assembling itself out of separate hungers. Paloma worked steadily, murmuring over each charm, letting the warmth gather in her fingertips and pass into thread or bone or knot. It cost her less than it used to. In the enclave, in the bad months, healing the dying woman's pain had nearly unmade her—Tamsin had told her that story, grimly proud, and Paloma had listened to her own sacrifice like a tale about a stranger she admired. Since the reforging, since the fire came back into the world and memory began returning to the city *like rain*, the gift ran cleaner. As if the imbalance itself had been a tax levied on every working charm, all those generations, and no one had known why the price kept rising.

A woman stopped at the stall with a child on her hip—a boy, maybe four, red-cheeked from the cold, wearing a coat with a mended elbow and an expression of tremendous seriousness. The woman wanted a charm against nightmares. The boy, she explained, had been waking screaming since the fires in the low quarter during the martial-law winter, and nothing she sang him helped.

"Since the winter," Paloma said gently. "I'm sorry. That was a hard season."

"You'd know," the woman said, not unkindly. "Everyone says you were there."

"I'm told I was," Paloma said, and smiled, and let it stand. It was the answer she always gave. It had taken her a year to stop apologizing for it.

She chose a charm—a knot of river-reed and a thread dyed the deep red of dragon-fire, bound with a whisper of warmth—and pressed it into the boy's small fist. He looked at it, then up at her, with the frank appraisal of the very young, and then, with the gravity of a magistrate closing a case, dug into his coat pocket.

What he produced was a button.

It was brass, or had been brass once, worn nearly smooth, with a design stamped on it that time had erased. It was worth nothing. It was, by any measure of the world, worthless—and the boy held it out on his open palm the way kings hold out scepters, certain of its value, certain of the transaction, certain that this was how things were done: a charm for a button, a fair trade between two serious people.

Something moved through Paloma that had no name in any language she knew.

It wasn't memory. She checked, the way she always checked, reaching back along the corridors of herself that Corvane's pages had mapped—the mother's voice, gone; Ivet's laugh, gone; the flooded undercity, the campfire, the climb, the flame at the summit taking her name syllable by syllable—all of it gone, all of it existing now only in ink and in other people's retellings. Nothing surfaced. No image, no scene, no reason.

And yet her eyes stung. And yet her throat closed around something enormous and wordless. And yet her hand, moving without consulting her, reached out and accepted the button with both palms cupped, reverent, the way one accepts a relic.

"Thank you," she heard herself say, and her voice cracked on the second word.

The boy nodded once, satisfied, business concluded, and buried his face in his mother's shoulder. The woman fumbled for coins, but Paloma waved her off. "Paid," she said. "Paid in full."

They went. Paloma stood a moment in the lantern light with the button in her hands, breathing carefully.

This had happened before. Tamsin had told her—gruffly, looking away, the way Tamsin delivered anything soft—that on the first night, the old night, years ago, a child had paid for a charm with a button and Paloma had charmed it anyway, had knelt down and strung it and told the girl to wear it always. *You cried about it later,* Tamsin said. *I don't know why. You wouldn't say. But you cried.* And Corvane's pages confirmed it, in his precise, restrained hand: *She keeps the first button somewhere. She has never told me where. I suspect she no longer knows herself.*

Somewhere. Perhaps in a box. Perhaps lost in the flight, in the burning, in the year of upheaval. Paloma had searched her few belongings and found nothing, and had decided—deliberately, the way she decided everything now—to let it be lost. Some things could exist in the story without needing to exist in the hand.

But this new button. This boy. This trade repeated across years by strangers who had no idea they were reenacting anything at all.

She turned the little disc over in her fingers. Worn smooth. Meaning erased. And yet her whole body insisted on its weight, the way the body insists on the shape of a stair in the dark long after the house has changed.

*Memory outlived by meaning.* Someone had written that to her once—one of the enclave folk, in a letter she kept folded in the Codex's new binding. Or perhaps she had written it herself and forgotten. It hardly mattered. It was true either way, and truth, she had learned, did not require a witness with a perfect recollection. Truth required only that it keep working. That the charm still warmed. That the button still mattered.

She threaded the button onto a cord with fingers that trembled slightly, and tied it off with a knot that was also a small charm—protection, or luck, or simply love, the categories having long since blurred—and hung it at the corner of her stall where the lamplight caught it. Not for sale. A sign, though of what, she could not have said. Other people seemed to know. They touched it sometimes, passing, the way pilgrims touch a doorpost, and some of them smiled at her with wet eyes, and she smiled back, sharing a feeling whose origin belonged entirely to them.

* * *

Corvane came near midnight, as he usually did, when the market had thinned to embers and the last stall-keepers were banking their coals. He came down the quay with his collar up against the river damp, carrying bread and a flask of spiced wine and, tucked under his arm as always, the satchel with the pages. He had stopped wearing the iron cuffs years ago. There was a pale band of skin at each wrist where they had sat, and Paloma had kissed both bands exactly once, deliberately, and told him they looked like the marks left by a shackle that had finally been outgrown.

He set the bread down on her table without asking, the way of people who have done a thing ten thousand times, and glanced at the button hanging in its place of honor.

"The boy again?" he said. "Third one this month."

"Fourth." She poured wine into two tin cups. "There must have been a whole fashion of it. Children paying with buttons. Did I start that? Is it in the pages?"

"It's in the pages," he said. "But I think the children started it themselves. Children are the only ones who never needed convincing that a thing can be worth what you decide it's worth." He lowered himself onto the barrel beside her stall with the careful movements of a man whose body had been burned and rebuilt and burned again, and who regarded each painless evening as a small personal victory. Half-burned at the summit, the stories said. He never spoke of it. His hands, though scarred, wrote beautifully. He had spent the entire climb of the Spire writing—she knew this the way she knew the market smells, from the record, from his own testimony given calmly to the reconciliation courts while officials leaned forward in disbelief: *I wrote down everything she said as she climbed. Every memory she named as she gave it away. It was the only thing I could do. It was the only thing that felt like refusing.* 

Now he opened the satchel, out of habit, and she saw the edges of the pages—hundreds of them, boxed and ordered and copied twice over, one set in the new archive, one set here, in the chest under her bed. Her life, in his handwriting. Her mother's voice described so precisely that once, reading his account of it—*a low voice, quick to laugh, given to humming when she sewed, and she hummed the same three notes over and over, a little descending run like steps going down into a garden*—Paloma had dreamed of humming, had woken with the three notes in her mouth, had sung them until noon.

Some things returned. The healers said the covenant-fire carried memory back into the world like weather, and whatever the flame had taken from her had not been destroyed but *scattered*, poured back into the rain and the river and the city's stones, and now and then a piece of her drifted home and settled. Her mother's humming. The color of Ivet's scarf. The exact ache of cold hands in ice water, waiting for a test she would pass by cheating. These came back unbidden, unprompted, arriving like birds at a window.

Some things only existed in ink. The sound of Mother Sefu's confession. The look on Tamsin's face the day the gates held. What it felt like to give her own name to the fire. Those she read, and reread, and they were hers the way scripture is hers—true, treasured, and slightly holy in their distance.

"Read to me," she said. "Something from the middle. From the bad part."

Corvane looked at her for a moment. He always did that—checked, quietly, whether she meant it tonight, whether the grief was bearable-shaped this evening or the other kind. Then he found the page. He knew the ordering better than she did; he had written it.

*"She is counting coins against her debt,"* he read, and the words fell into the night like stones into still water. *"Nine coppers and a bent nail. She is tired in a way that sleep will not fix. A child comes to her stall with a button in her fist, and Paloma Sterling—who cannot afford generosity, who has hidden the warmth in her hands for eleven years—takes the button and charms it anyway, and tells the child to wear it always. Later she will not be able to explain why this mattered so much. I am writing it down so that it is never fully lost."*

The market noise washed around them. Somewhere a fiddle. Somewhere laughter. Overhead, far to the east, above the springs at the edge of the Ashkarat shallows, a faint red glow breathed against the underside of the clouds—the Spire's sister-light, the covenant-flame, steady now, attended, fed by offerings freely given instead of stolen. On clear nights you could see dragons crossing it, small and distant as migrating birds.

Paloma drank her wine. The button turned slowly on its cord in the breath of the river wind, catching the lantern light, worn smooth, meaningless, precious.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked. "The version of me who cried about a button and couldn't say why?"

Corvane considered this with the seriousness he brought to all her questions, as though each one were a page to be gotten exactly right.

"No," he said at last. "Because she isn't gone. She's just—" he gestured, searching, at the stall, the charms, the button, the whole small glowing economy of the night market—"distributed. She's in the ledger they burned and the ones we saved. She's in what Tamsin built. She's in every child who pays with a button now. You're not missing, Paloma. You're *everywhere*. I've checked. I wrote it all down."

She laughed—the laugh he said she'd always had, though neither of them could source it, and neither of them minded.

The lanterns guttered low. The last customers drifted home. And in the morning there would be letters to write to Ashkarat, and charms to make, and a city still learning to tell itself the truth. But that was morning's work.

Tonight there was only the stall, the wine, the man with the satchel, the button turning on its cord—and the warmth in her hands, which had never once, in all the years of losing and finding, gone cold.
