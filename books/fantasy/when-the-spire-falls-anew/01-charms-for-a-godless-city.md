## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The fog came off the Sorrowdeep canal at dusk, and with it came Gideon Delacroix's customers.

They always arrived half-swallowed by that gray, hooded, hurrying — a fishwife with salt-cracked knuckles, a lamplighter missing two fingers to frostbite, once, memorably, a minor guild-lord wearing a beggar's cloak over silk. They came to the crook of stalls beneath the collapsed aqueduct where the lantern-light didn't quite reach, and they bought what she sold: charms for a godless city.

Not real charms, of course. There was no such thing anymore. Hadn't been for ten years, since the night the sky over the Spire went dark and every prayer in the realm fell like a stone into deep water. What Gideon sold was the memory of magic — knotted red thread to ward off fever, iron nails bent at the new moon against ill luck, dried vervain in a pouch stitched with the sigil of Veyra, the Silent Mother, whose silence had swallowed all the others. Placebos. Comfort cut into shapes small enough to hold. She charged fairly and lied gently, and in ten years she had never once let herself believe her own merchandise.

"Thread again," said Maren the fishwife, laying three coppers on the stained wool of the counter. "For my boy. He's got the wet-cough again, worse than winter."

"The same knot as before," Gideon said, working the cord without looking down. Her hands remembered; her mind was elsewhere, on the fog and the cold and the way the canal water had smelled wrong all day — scorched, faintly, like a doused fire. "Tie it at his left wrist. Say nothing while you tie it."

"And I say nothing, and he sleeps, and the cough breaks." Maren smiled with the weary faith of someone who knew it was theater and paid for it anyway. "You're good at this, priestess."

"I'm not a priestess."

"You were. It's in how you knot it. The old ones did it just so — thumb over, twice around." Maren took the charm and pressed her lips to it, an old habit from older days. "Veyra keep him."

"Veyra keeps no one," Gideon said, and hated how flat her voice had become, how practiced. Ten years of saying it had worn the words smooth as river glass.

Maren left. The next hour brought four more buyers, and Gideon sold fever-wards and luck-knots and one expensive false relic — a splinter of "true ash" from the Spire's fall, actually oak charred in her own hearth — to a merchant who wanted it for his daughter's wedding dowry. The fog thickened. Somewhere across the city a bell rang the hour, its bronze voice cracked and dull, like everything else in Corvane.

She was packing up when she heard the child.

A girl, six or seven perhaps, standing at the edge of her stall with the particular desperation of the very poor — barefoot despite the cold, hair cropped short against lice, holding out a chipped clay figure of a woman with no face. A hearth-god, one of the little household idols people still kept out of stubbornness, though the hearths themselves had long gone cold of anything but ordinary flame.

"It doesn't work," the girl said. "My gran prays and prays and the fire won't come. We're hungry. Please make it work."

Gideon crouched, meaning to give the child a copper and send her home. That was all. That was the whole of her intention.

And then the girl closed her eyes, and prayed.

*Hearth-mother, warm us. Fire for the pot. Fire for the cold. Please.*

Gideon had heard ten thousand prayers exactly like it. She had mouthed versions of it herself through the first black year, kneeling in ruins, until her knees bled and her throat gave out and she understood that silence was simply what the world was now. She felt nothing. She expected to feel nothing.

Instead, she felt a *pull*.

It began behind her sternum — a sensation like a hook set gently in flesh and drawn taut toward something vast and far away. Her breath stopped. The fog seemed to lean inward. And in the girl's cupped hands, above the faceless clay idol, a flame *bloomed*.

Small. Steady. Wrong-colored — gold shot through with veins of deep violet, a color Gideon had seen only once before, ten years ago, in a sanctuary full of dying light. Real fire. Answered fire. The girl shrieked with delight and terror and clapped her hands around it, and the flame sat there in her palms like a tame bird, warming her dirty fingers, casting dancing shadows up the underside of the aqueduct.

Gideon could not move. The hook behind her sternum pulled harder, and beneath the numbness of a decade, something enormous turned over in the dark like a leviathan waking under ice.

*Veyra.*

The name rose in her unbidden, and with it a pressure at the edge of hearing — not a word, not yet, but the *shape* of a word, the way silence itself seemed to gather and lean—

The flame went out.

The girl blinked at her empty hands, then at Gideon, radiant and frightened. "Did you do that?"

"No," Gideon whispered. It was the truest thing she'd said in ten years.

Then she saw the torches.

Three of them, bobbing fast along the canal path, white-liveried figures breaking into a run — and above their torchlight, the pale gleam of the inquisitors' masks, featureless porcelain with a single vertical slit, the faces of the Order of the Unlit Flame. They had been watching. Of course they had been watching. A answered prayer in Corvane was not a miracle to them; it was contraband of the worst kind, a spark looking for tinder.

"Run," Gideon breathed, and pushed the girl toward the alley behind the stall. "Don't look back. Don't tell anyone. *Go.*"

She swept her wares into the hidden satchel beneath the counter, heart slamming, and was reaching for the latch when the first torchlight broke over the stalls.

"GIDEON DELACROIX." The voice was amplified by a speaker-mask, hollow and genderless. "BY ORDER OF THE ARCHON OF THE UNLIT FLAME, YOU ARE DETAINED FOR TRAFFICKING IN FORBIDDEN RITES."

*Trafficking in forbidden rites.* Not smuggling, not fraud — those were the charges of ten years ago, the ones she'd served out in the salt mines of Vhal. This was new language. This meant they knew something. This meant they were afraid.

She ran.

The alleys behind the aqueduct were a maze she'd mapped in her bones over five years of selling in Corvane — left past the tannery reeks, right through the gap where the wall had settled away from the butcher's roof, down the stair into the flooded lower lane where the water came to mid-shin and no mounted man could follow. Torches flared and died behind her. Boots slapped stone. Once, a crossbow bolt buried itself in a doorframe a hand's width from her ear, and she felt rather than heard the second bolt loosed and miss.

Her lungs burned. Her satchel thumped against her spine. And through all of it, absurdly, terribly, the *hook* behind her sternum never slackened — pulling, pulling, northwest, toward the mountains, toward the place she had sworn never to go again, and with each stride the shapeless pressure at the edge of hearing grew a fraction more distinct, like a voice rising slowly through deep water.

She made the tanner's gate as it was being barred for the night, and would have been caught there entirely except that a hand shot out of the shadow of the arch and hauled her inside, and a familiar gravel voice hissed, "Shut up and don't be stupid, it's me."

Old Tam Vetch, fence, drunkard, and once — briefly, disastrously — her lover, held the gate bar with one arm and a cudgel in the other, his remaining eye wide with fear. Behind him, the tannery yard stank of lime and hides, safe and dim.

"They're raiding everyone tonight," Tam panted, dropping the bar as the torches swept past outside. "Whole quarter. They took Widow Sarelle and her candles. Took the mendicant brothers off the steps of the almshouse. Gid—" He gripped her shoulder, and his eye was wild. "It ain't just here. Caravan came in from the coast roads this evening. They say prayers are being answered all down the seaboard. Crops coming up green in a night. Dead men sitting up at their own funerals. Fires lighting in empty hearths from here to the Thornmark. The gods are *waking*, girl, and the Archon's burning every town that says so."

The hook behind her sternum pulled, northwest, patient as tide.

Ten years of grief and numbness, and in one evening a child's answered prayer had cracked them open like river ice in spring — and underneath, terrifyingly, was not despair. It was hope. Wary, half-drowned, furious hope, the most dangerous thing she had ever carried.

"They'll come back," Tam said. "They know your name. You can't stay in Corvane."

"I know." Gideon looked north, where the fog thinned and the first peaks of the Ashenveil range stood black against the stars — toward the ruined Spire she had fled from a decade ago, toward whatever was calling her with a dead god's voice.

She shouldered her satchel.

"I have to go home."
