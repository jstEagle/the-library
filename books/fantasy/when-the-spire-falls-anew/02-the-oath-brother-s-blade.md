## 2. The Oath-Brother's Blade

The mountains came up out of the mist like the shoulders of something sleeping.

Gideon had walked three days since Corvane, following the old pilgrim road that wound north through pine and scree, and for three days the god in her chest had been whispering. Not words — Veyra did not give words, not anymore — but a pressure, a warmth behind her ribs like a coal wrapped in wool. It had been silent for ten years. Ten years of ash and numbness, of selling charms that were only carved bone and wishful thinking. Now every mile toward the Spire made the warmth burn brighter, and Gideon could not decide whether she was walking toward salvation or into the mouth of a trap.

The rain began at dusk on the third day, thin and needling, turning the road to grease. She took shelter beneath an overhang where the rock folded back like a cupped hand, and she was wringing water from her braid when she heard the sound that stopped her heart: the soft ring of a blade being drawn from a scabbard, deliberate, unhurried.

She knew that draw. She had trained beside it for six years.

"Turn around slowly," Cassian said.

* * *

She turned. He stood at the mouth of the overhang with the rain streaming off his hood, and he looked exactly as he had in her nightmares and nothing like her memories. The boy who had shared her novitiate bunk, who had laughed until he wheezed when she imitated the high priest's sermons — he was gone. In his place stood a tall man in inquisitor's grey, silvered at the temples, with the pale brand of the Order burned into his left cheek like a tear of molten wax. His sword was out. Of course it was. Inquisitors did not come to talk.

"Cassian." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You've put on weight."

Something flickered across his face — pain, or its ghost — and was smothered. "Gideon Delacroix. By order of the Archon's Court, you are under writ of erasure."

"Writ of *what?*"

"You know what you saw in Corvane." He took one step forward, boots grinding on wet stone. "The child. The fire. You know what it means, and you know who else knows it. The Order cannot permit—"

"The Order cannot permit what?" She laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "That prayers are being answered again? That after ten years of your masters burning healers and hanging charm-sellers, the old gods woke up anyway?" She spread her arms, letting him see she held no weapon. "Kill me, then. But you'll have to explain to Veyra why her oath-brother's hands are bloody."

"Do not speak her name." His voice dropped, low and rough, and for one heartbeat the inquisitor was gone and only the boy remained, terrified. "You don't understand what they've ordered. Gideon. *Listen to me.*"

He raised the sword.

And hesitated.

It was only a hesitation — half a breath, a tremor in the wrist — but Gideon had spent six years learning every language of his body, and she read it fluently. His elbow locked wrong. His weight stayed on his heel. Every drilled killing stroke the Order had taught him, his body was refusing.

"They didn't send you to arrest me," she said quietly. "They sent you to erase me. And you can't do it."

"I *can*." The words came out strangled.

"No." She stepped toward him, close enough that the point of his sword pressed against her sternum, dimpling her soaked shirt over her heart. "Do it, then. Here. Right here. If you can."

The rain filled the silence between them. His hand shook. The steel against her breastbone trembled like a plucked string.

"They gave us a list," he said at last, barely above the rain. "Three nights ago, in the Archon's chamber. Witnesses to the old covenant — everyone still living who stood present when it was sealed. Priests. Chroniclers. One child who was carried out of the Spire before it fell." His eyes lifted to hers, and she watched the truth arrive in them even as it arrived in her own. "*One child.* Gideon — I thought you died with the rest. I prayed you had. Because the ones on that list don't get trials. They get unmade. Their names struck from every record. Their families given ashes and lies. There is no warrant for your arrest because there is no *you*, as far as the law is concerned. There never will be again."

The world tilted. Ten years of running, of hiding, of selling false charms under false names — it rearranged itself around this one fact like iron filings around a lodestone. Not hunted for smuggling. Hunted for remembering.

"Why tell me this?" she whispered. "Why not finish it?"

He looked down at the sword against her chest. Slowly — she watched the war move through his whole body, muscle by muscle — he turned the blade aside and let the point fall.

"Because ten years ago I swore an oath beside you at Veyra's altar," he said, "and the Order burned the altar but they couldn't burn the oath. Because I have signed eleven erasure writs and slept badly after every one. And because—" His voice caught. He mastered it. "Because when they read your name off that list, Gideon, I felt the same thing I felt the day we buried our masters. That if the world has to lose you twice, I would rather be damned with you than promoted without you."

* * *

Later — much later, when the rain had thickened to a grey curtain and neither of them could pretend the decision hadn't been made — they sat beneath the overhang sharing hardtack from his pack and heatless fire from a smuggled inquisitor's stone.

"The pass closes with snow in nine days," Cassian said. "After that, the Spire is unreachable until spring, and the Archon's riders will find us long before spring. We go now, together, or not at all."

"Together." She tested the word like a bad tooth. "You realize I spent ten years rehearsing what I'd say if I ever found you again. Most versions ended with me hitting you."

"Only most?"

"In some of them I forgave you first. Those were the worst ones." But she found, examining herself, that the anger had gone strange and soft, like a scar finally itching as it healed. "Why did you stay with them, Cassian? All these years?"

He was quiet a long time. The inquisitor's stone pulsed faintly between them, giving light but no warmth.

"At first, survival," he said. "Then usefulness — I told myself I could soften their writs, lose names off lists, save a few. And then one day I realized I'd stopped saving anyone and started just... enduring. That's how they win, Gideon. Not with fire. With patience. They make the world so cold you're grateful for any small warmth they allow you." He looked at her. "And then a child in Corvane lit a candle with a prayer, and the whole city smelled like woodsmoke and divinity, and I remembered what warmth actually was."

She reached across the space between them — slowly, giving him time to pull away — and closed her hand over his. His fingers were cold and scarred and they curled around hers like they had never learned otherwise.

"If we reach the Spire," she said, "and if what I think is happening is really happening — the gods waking, the dragon dying, whatever covenant they're so afraid of — there may be no coming back from it. For either of us."

"I know."

"They'll send others. Better hunters than you."

"I know."

"And you'll be a traitor forever. Erased yourself."

A ghost of the old smile, the bunk-room smile, crossed his ruined face. "I was erased the moment I lowered my sword. Everything after this is bonus."

She laughed — really laughed, for the first time in ten years — and the sound startled birds out of the pines above them, and somewhere far to the north, deep beneath the mountain's roots, Gideon felt the coal behind her ribs flare hot enough to hurt.

Veyra knew they were coming.

* * *

They walked through the night and all the next day, climbing as the road narrowed and the pines gave way to bare black stone. On the second evening they crested a ridge and saw it for the first time: the Spire, broken-topped against a bruised sky, its shattered crown bleeding a faint red light into the clouds. Dragon-light. Dying-light.

Cassian stopped dead. "Gods' blood," he breathed. "I can *feel* it from here."

"So can I." Gideon's hand drifted to the charm at her throat — the one true thing she owned, carved bone worn smooth by a decade of touching. It was warm. Warmer than it had ever been. "Cassian — look at the light. It's flickering. Like a candle in a draft."

"Like a candle about to go out," he said.

And then, from the ridge below them, thin on the wind, came the sound that froze them both where they stood: the creak of harness, the snort of horses, and voices — many voices — chanting the flat, grey cadence of the Order's evening rite.

Riders. Already on the mountain. Between them and the Spire.

Cassian's hand found hers in the dark, and his grip was iron. "There's another way up," he said. "The pilgrims' stair. It's fallen, it's deadly, and it's the only path they won't watch."

Gideon looked at the broken crown of the Spire, burning its last red light into the dark, and stepped off the edge of the known world after him.
