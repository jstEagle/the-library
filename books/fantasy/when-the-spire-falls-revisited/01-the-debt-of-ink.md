## 1. The Debt of Ink

The ink came first. It always did.

Before the bells, before the grey light crept through the scriptorium's high windows and found the frost on the inside of the glass, there was the smell of it—oak-gall and iron, the faint sweetness of gum arabic, the ghost of a hundred thousand sins recorded in the ledgers of Vess. Oscar Amberdale knew that smell the way other people knew their mothers' voices. She had been breathing it since she was nine years old, when her father sold her pen to the temple to cover what he owed, and in eleven years she had learned that ink outlasts almost everything: paper yellows, candles gutter, flesh fails—but a line written in good ink endures.

She was copying penance-ledgers now, as she copied them every morning, her breath fogging in the cold because Abbot Corvel said heat made the hand lazy. *Item: one widow Maren Cole, three days' fasting for the sin of grumbling against the harvest tithe.* Her nib whispered across the vellum. *Item: one boy Tam Ostler, seven lashes, confessed to theft of a candle-end.* Somewhere below, the temple of Vess was waking—brothers shuffling to matins, the great bronze bell tolling the hour in its cracked voice, the sound worn smooth as a river stone from centuries of striking.

Oscar wrote, and kept her head down, and did not think about how many years remained on her indenture. Fourteen. She thought instead about nothing at all, which was a skill, and like all skills it required practice.

Thornfell sat in the crook of two mountains like a stone dropped in a cupped palm, and the temple stood at its center the way a spider sits at the center of a web. Everything that moved through the town moved through Vess eventually: tithes, confessions, debts, deaths. Oscar had mapped every road around the temple's reach—the smuggler paths through the scree fields, the goat trails over the shoulder of Mount Carness, the old quarry cut where no brother ever walked because the wind up there made them nervous. She had never used those roads herself. She only knew them the way a caged bird knows the shape of the sky: precisely, hungrily, and without any intention of testing them.

Her nib scratched. Outside, snow ticked softly against the window. The world was small, and cold, and orderly, and hers.

Then the door opened, and everything changed.

* * *

Brother Anselm had gone down to the reliquary, so the scriptorium belonged to Oscar alone—which was why the child chose that moment, Oscar would think later. Children know things. They read rooms the way scholars read texts, with an attention adults lose.

She was small, six or seven perhaps, though starvation makes age impossible to guess. She wore a coat that had been someone's coat long before it became hers, sleeves rolled four times at the wrists, hem dragging. Her hair was dark and matted with burrs, her lips cracked, her eyes enormous—not the enormous eyes of fear, Oscar noticed, but of something stranger. Focus. Purpose. A child who had come here on purpose, through the snow, past the gate-brothers, past the dogs.

And she was speaking.

Not crying, not begging—speaking. Words fell out of her in a steady, urgent stream, low and rhythmic, and they were words Oscar had never heard in her life and yet—

*Hala veneth. Hala veneth, sorra keth-illun.*

Oscar's nib stopped mid-stroke. A blot of ink bloomed on the ledger like a wound.

She understood none of it. That was the first thing she told herself, standing very still behind the desk while the child crossed the cold flagstones toward her, leaving wet footprints. She understood *nothing*. And then, with a sensation like ice water finding a crack in a wall, she understood *some of it*. Not the words themselves—she could not have translated a single syllable if asked—but something underneath them, some architecture of meaning her ear recognized the way a body recognizes a stair in the dark even when the candle is out. The words rose and fell in a particular way. There were pauses where a person would pause. There was a shape to it, a grammar of grief or urgency, and Oscar's blood seemed to lean toward it like a plant toward light.

*Sorra keth-illun,* the child said again, and touched her own chest, and then pointed—outward, downward, toward the mountains east of town, toward the ruin everyone in Thornfell called the Spire and never spoke of above a whisper.

"I don't understand you," Oscar said. Her voice came out strange.

The child looked at her with those vast dark eyes and repeated the phrase, patient as a teacher, insistent as a bell.

Behind her, footsteps in the corridor. Brother Anselm returning, his keys jangling. Oscar's heart performed a quick, traitorous calculation she hadn't asked it to make: the gate-brothers would turn this child out into the snow; the abbot would ask questions; questions were dangerous; this child was dangerous; and Oscar Amberdale, whose entire survival strategy for eleven years had been *be small, be quiet, be nothing*, felt her hands close the ledger and slide it beneath a stack of finished copies as though hiding evidence.

"Here," she heard herself say. "Under the desk. Quickly."

The child went under the desk as though she'd been told to a thousand times.

Anselm filled the doorway, round and red-nosed, smelling of candle wax. "Cold enough to crack stone," he said cheerfully. Then he paused. "You look pale, girl."

"The ink fumes," Oscar said. "It's the ink."

"Mm." He squinted at the window, at the frost, at nothing in particular. "Abbot wants the harvest ledgers by noon. Don't dawdle." He left. His footsteps faded. Under the desk, the child breathed—a small, controlled sound, the breathing of someone practiced at being hidden, which was its own terrible kind of knowledge.

Oscar knelt. Up close, the child smelled of woodsmoke and pine and something older underneath, mineral and cold, like air off a glacier. Snow had frozen in her lashes.

"Who sent you?" Oscar whispered.

The child tilted her head. Then she spoke again—that same phrase, *hala veneth*, and this time she took Oscar's hand, turned it palm-up in both of her small ones, and pressed one finger into the center of it, hard, twice, like a seal being stamped.

Oscar stared at her own palm. Nothing marked it. Nothing needed to. Because the moment the finger struck, the words rearranged themselves in her mind—not translated, nothing so kind as translation—but *recognized*, half-understood the way you half-understand a dream upon waking, the sense already dissolving as you reach for it:

*A word for the falling place. A word for the thirteenth moon.*

The Spire had fallen two hundred years ago. Everyone knew that. Everyone knew the war ended when the fortress broke and whatever burned inside it burned, and the old tongue died with the people who'd held it, and the temple of Vess wrote the whole thing into a single careful paragraph in its histories and locked the rest away. No living scholar claimed to speak it. No living scholar claimed anything about the Spire except that it was over.

So why did the dead language live in the mouth of a starving child? And why—this was the question Oscar sat with, kneeling on cold flagstones with her heart hammering like the bell overhead—why did it live, faintly, terribly, in *her*?

Her father had come from somewhere east, from villages no one visited anymore, and he had never spoken of his mother's people, and he had died owing money, and that was supposed to be the whole of the inheritance: debt. Debt of ink. Debt of years.

The child watched her with those patient, purposeful eyes, and outside the snow thickened against the windows, and somewhere beyond the mountains the thirteenth moon was already climbing toward its fullness.

Oscar got up, fetched the heel of yesterday's bread she kept hidden in her desk drawer, and put it in the child's hands.

"Eat," she said. "Then we're going to talk about what you just showed me. And then—" she glanced at the door, at the corridor, at the whole listening weight of the temple around them, and felt her small, carefully built life shift on its foundations like a house in floodwater— "then we may need to leave."
