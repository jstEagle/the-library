## 2. Thirteen Moons

The child slept in the scriptorium's ink-closet for three nights before Oscar understood what she was saying.

Not sleeping—Oscar did not sleep either. She sat at her desk with a stolen lamp turned low, the penance-ledgers shoved aside, and spread before her the scraps of vellum on which she had transcribed the child's words as fast as her memory could hold them. The language came out of the girl in fragments, in the small hours, half-delirium and half-song: *senneth-vara. keth-illune. senneth-vara.* The same phrases, over and over, like water wearing a groove in stone.

Every scholar in Thornfell would have said the tongue was dead. Every scholar in Thornfell was wrong, because every scholar in Thornfell had learned their dead languages from books, and Oscar had learned hers from her mother's lullabies—half-remembered, shamefaced, sung with the windows shuttered because Spire-blood was not something you admitted to in a town that had burned its share of Spire sympathizers. She had spent twenty-three years pretending those songs meant nothing. Now they meant everything.

*Senneth-vara* — the falling place. *Keth-illune* — she turned it over and over in her mind until the translation surfaced like a drowned body: the thirteenth moon. And between them, threaded through the child's fevered repetitions, a pattern that was not language at all but geography. Ridges. Rivers. A road that forked where no honest road forked—a smuggler's path, one Oscar had walked twice herself, high in the passes above Thornfell, where the cairns were stacked by men who asked no names.

The child was reciting a map. And the map ended at the heart of the Spire.

Oscar set down her pen. Her hands were shaking, and the lamplight made the ink look wet and black as a wound.

Thirteen moons. She counted backward through the temple calendar pinned to the wall, and the arithmetic closed around her throat like a hand. The thirteenth moon rose in nineteen days.

* * *

Captain Rell stood in the burnt shell of the waystation at Cold Ford and let the ash settle on his shoulders like a blessing.

His men had found nothing. Again. The child had passed through here—two days ahead of them now, traveling with someone who knew the old roads, which meant the scribe, which meant the trail was still warm—but knowing and catching were different creatures, and Rell was patient the way fire is patient under a roof it has already eaten into.

He picked up a child's shoe from the ashes. Too big for his quarry; some other family's loss. He set it down gently.

"The abbot thinks this is an errand," he said to Brother Cassel, who hovered at the doorway wringing his hands the way churchmen wrung hands instead of doing work. "A retrieval. Evidence to be catalogued." He smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "But the Vessians have had nine hundred years to dig up everything the Spire buried, and they never found the heart. A mute child walks out of the mountains speaking the defender-tongue, and suddenly the abbot wants her *examined*. He knows more than he preaches."

"You believe the words are genuine?"

"I believe," Rell said, turning toward the door, toward the mountains standing blue and cold against the dying light, "that the war did not end. It only paused, the way a held breath is not the same as peace. And I believe that when the thirteenth moon rises, someone is going to exhale." He looked back at Cassel, mild as milk. "We will find the child first. Whatever the abbot wants her for—we have better uses for a key than locking it in a reliquary."

Behind them, the waystation's charred beams groaned in the wind, remembering.

* * *

On the fourth night, the abbot sent for them both.

Oscar knew what the summons meant before she finished reading it—the careful, courteous phrasing that was how the temple said *I know you have something and I am being generous for exactly one more day.* She folded the letter, fed it to the lamp, and watched the abbot's courtesy curl and blacken.

The child stood in the closet doorway, watching her with eyes too old for a face maybe six years old. Mute when the temple physicians examined her—not mute, Oscar corrected herself, simply *unwilling*, saving all her speech for a dead language and a living scribe—and watching now with the terrible patience of someone who had already been carried a long way by worse people than Oscar Amberdale.

"They're going to take you," Oscar told her quietly. "Put you in a cell. Ask you questions until the answers fit what they want." She knelt, so their eyes were level. "I'm supposed to bring you upstairs tonight."

The child reached out and touched Oscar's wrist, right over the pulse. Then she spoke—one word, soft, in the old tongue—and Oscar's whole body went cold, because the word was *stay*, and it was spoken the exact cadence her mother had used, the exact cadence of every lullaby she'd spent two decades burying.

"No," Oscar said. Her voice cracked on it. "No, we're not staying. We're leaving."

She took almost nothing. Her mother's ring, worn smooth. A knife. The false-bottom drawer's contents: three travel writs in forged hands, salt pork wrapped in oilcloth, a vial of poppy syrup, and the little book of her grandmother's songs that she had told herself for years was merely a keepsake. Under it, the ledger page bearing her debt—she left that deliberately on her desk, face-up, a message in the only language the abbot respected: *the debt stays; the debtor does not.*

They went out through the scriptorium's window onto the chapel roof, down the ivy that generations of truant novices had trained into a ladder, across the graveyard where Oscar paused just long enough to press her palm flat against her father's headstone without reading it. Then the drainage culvert beneath the tannery wall, which stank of rot and freedom in equal measure, and out.

Out, and up, into the hills where the honest roads gave up and the dishonest ones began.

* * *

They walked the smuggler roads for six days, and Oscar remembered why she had survived Thornfell: because she knew them.

She knew which trails were watched and which were merely rumored to be watched. She knew the hollow under the split oak where a sympathetic carter left bread in exchange for silence about his cargo. She knew that the bridge at Greywater was rotten on its western third, that the shrine at the second pass had been desecrated by zealots and should be given a wide berth, that the shepherd at Lark's Hollow could be trusted with coin but never with questions.

The child—she had begun calling her Wren, because the girl made small sharp sounds in her sleep like a bird dreaming—walked when she could and was carried when she couldn't, growing heavier and warmer against Oscar's back as the mountains grew colder. At night Oscar sang to her. Not the temple hymns. The other ones. The buried ones. And each time, Wren's breathing eased, as though the songs were a country the child recognized.

It was on the sixth night, huddled in a shepherd's bothy with rain hammering the turf roof, that Oscar finally understood the shape of what she was carrying.

She laid out her transcriptions by firelight and traced the route one more time—and saw it plainly, the way you see a face in a crowd once you've stopped looking for features. The map wasn't a map of roads. It was a map of *bloodlines*. Every waypoint matched a family name from her grandmother's songs: the fell at Greywater where the Amberdales had kept watch. The ford named for the Vessic martyrs. The passes held by houses that no longer existed except as place-names and, apparently, as blood running quiet in the veins of scribes and shepherds who didn't know what they were.

The defenders hadn't all died when the Spire fell. They had scattered, married, forgotten—deliberately, perhaps, or mercifully. And the child's words weren't instructions for strangers. They were a summons home.

"Who are you?" Oscar whispered to the sleeping girl. "What are you?"

Wren opened her eyes. In the firelight they were the color of deep water. She pressed her small hand flat against Oscar's sternum, over her heart, and said, clearly, in the old tongue:

"*Senneth-vara.*"

Then, after a moment, with the gravity of someone handing over something breakable: "*Amberdale.*"

* * *

They heard the horses at dawn.

Not temple riders—temple riders traveled loud and righteous, bells on the harness. These came quiet, single file along the ridge above the bothy, and Oscar knew before she lifted her head that the zealots had found the trail. Rell's people. The ones who wanted the thirteenth moon to mean what they needed it to mean.

She woke Wren with a hand over the child's mouth, gentle, and packed in ninety seconds what it had taken six days to gather. Through the bothy's smoke-hole she watched the riders descend toward the valley floor—nine of them, moving with the unhurried certainty of hunters who believed the net was already drawn.

There was one road east from Lark's Hollow, and they were on it.

But there was another way. There was always another way, if you were willing to pay for it in terror. Oscar swung Wren onto her back, cinched the straps, and turned west, toward Greywater Bridge and the rotten third and the long drop into the gorge below—the crossing she had sworn since childhood she would never take at speed, in fog, carrying anything she could not bear to lose.

The thirteenth moon was eleven days away. Behind her, faint on the wind, she heard a rider's horn sound once, clean and cold as ice cracking.

Wren's arms tightened around her neck.

"Hold on," Oscar said, and ran.
