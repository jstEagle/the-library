## 3. What the Mountain Remembers

The passes above Thornfell did not forgive, and they did not forget. Oscar had learned both lessons young, running contraband ink up the goat-trails for a man who paid in coin and never asked her name. Now she climbed them again with nothing to sell and everything to lose: a child on her back, three days' bread in her pack, and the thirteenth moon swelling overhead like a wound that would not close.

The cold came down off the peaks in long grey sheets. It found every seam of Oscar's coat, every gap in the child's wrappings, and it carried the smell of old snow and older stone—the smell, Oscar thought unwillingly, of the Spire itself, though she had never stood within ten leagues of the thing. She knew its scent anyway. That was the trouble. That was the whole ruinous trouble.

The girl—Wren, because she would not say her name, or perhaps had none to say—rode silent against Oscar's spine, her breath a small hot rhythm at the nape of Oscar's neck. For six days she had spoken only in that dead tongue, the syllables falling out of her like water from a cracked jar, and for six days Oscar had pretended not to understand more than fragments. Pretense was a skill. She had been indentured since she was nine; pretense was the first thing the temple taught you, before even your letters.

But the mountain stripped pretense away the way wind strips paint.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a fallen watchstone—one of the boundary markers the old defenders had raised when the Spire still had a country, not just a crater. The stone leaned like a tired soldier. Its carvings had been worn to suggestions: here a hand, there a flame, everywhere the same looping script that the temple's scholars called *grave-tongue* and refused to teach on pain of penance. Oscar set Wren down, built a fire no larger than two cupped hands, and rationed out bread and melted snow.

It was then that the child looked at her—not at the fire, not at the dark, but at her, straight through the eyes as if reading something written on the inside of Oscar's skull—and said, clear as a bell over frozen water:

"*Oskara Vess-dottir. Amberdale-line. Keeper-of-small-things.*"

Oscar's hands went still around the bread.

Not the whole sentence—she could not have rendered it word for word into trade-speech if her life depended on it, and it might. But she understood it the way you understand a song from childhood, without ever having studied the notes. *Oskara* was her name, the name her grandmother had whispered over her cradle and been scolded for. *Vess-dottir* meant daughter of Vess, which was absurd, because Vess was the god the temple served, except that it wasn't, except that the temple had taken the name the way it took everything, and—

"Where did you learn that," Oscar said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice.

Wren tilted her head. Then she spoke again, slower, patient, the way one speaks to someone wading into deep water: "*Your blood remembers what your mouth has forgotten.*"

Oscar sat back against the watchstone and felt the mountain lean on her.

She had known, in the way one knows a scar—there but unexamined—that her family was wrong. Her father's debts were real, but so were the questions the abbot never asked aloud: why the Amberdales kept a box of black-bound books they could not read; why her grandmother sang lullabies in grave-tongue and called it "the old counting"; why the temple had accepted an indenture rather than let the family leave Thornfell entirely. Debt, she had always told herself. Just debt. Small lives are built on small lies, and hers had been load-bearing.

Now the lie lay in pieces at her feet, and under it: blood. Defender's blood. The Spire's people had not all burned when the fortress fell; some had scattered south, married, renamed themselves, learned to keep their heads down so well that four generations later their own daughters believed the keeping-down was all there was.

Oscar laughed—a short, broken sound that startled a bird off the watchstone—and pressed her wrist against her eyes. She grieved then, briefly and fiercely, for the woman she had planned to be: the scribe who served out her term, bought back her freedom with twenty years of careful copies, took a room above a chandler's shop, and grew old over ledgers that balanced. A small life. A survivable life. She had loved that life the way you love a wall that keeps the weather off, without ever admitting it was also keeping *you* in.

That woman had died somewhere on the smuggler roads, six days ago, the moment Oscar lifted a mute child through a scriptorium window instead of handing her to the abbot.

"*You weep,*" Wren observed, in the tone of someone cataloguing weather.

"I'm not weeping." Oscar wiped her face. "I'm paying a toll."

The child considered this, then nodded, as if tolls were a language she too spoke.

* * *

Rell caught them on the fourth day, at the rope bridge over Greywater Gorge.

Oscar smelled the zealots before she saw them—tallow and hot iron, the stink of consecrated armor—and went cold from the boots up. Below the trail, the gorge dropped three hundred feet into white noise; the bridge was a smuggler's construction, hemp and pine slats, good for fifty years or one careless moment, depending on who was crossing and how much they wanted to live.

Six figures in grey-and-gold came up the switchback behind them at a pace that wasted nothing. Rell led them, tall and unhurried, his voice carrying across the rock with terrible mildness.

"Scribe Amberdale. The temple forgives. The temple always forgives—it is the temple's single greatest expense." He stopped thirty paces off, hands open, sword sheathed. "Give me the vessel, and you'll be copying penance-ledgers by spring. You have my oath."

"You gave an oath at Thornfell," Oscar said. "To the ferryman whose boat you burned with him in it."

"The ferryman was a heretic." Rell smiled the way frost forms. "Everything is, eventually."

Wren's small fingers dug into Oscar's shoulder. And then the child did the last thing Oscar expected: she slid down, walked to the edge of the bridge, and began to speak—not to Rell, not to Oscar, but to the gorge itself, to the falling water, her voice rising into that dead tongue until the words rang off the canyon walls. Oscar understood only the shape of it: a warning, or a summons. Perhaps both. On this mountain, she was learning, the two were often the same.

"She's calling it," Oscar breathed. Not the zealots. Something else. Something that slept below.

Rell moved.

He was fast for a man in consecrated iron—faster than the smugglers' tales promised—and Oscar had perhaps four seconds, maybe five, in which the whole rest of her life compressed into a single choice. There was no winning. There was only the bridge, and the blade, and the child standing at the edge of the deep with her arms spread like a small dark prophet.

Oscar drew her knife—the cheap scribe's knife, good for quills and bad for war—and cut the anchor rope.

The hemp parted with a sound like a gasp. The bridge sagged, swung, and tore free along its length, slats raining into the gorge, and Oscar threw herself forward because Wren was still on the wrong side of the collapse—still on *her* side, the far side, the side where Rell was already lunging—and caught the child around the waist as the last span bucked beneath them like a living thing trying to shed them both.

For one suspended instant they hung between sky and water, and Oscar saw it all with perfect clarity: Rell's hand closing on empty air; the pack with their bread and flint tumbling end over end into the white; the moon, impossibly, impossibly close, full thirteen days early in her mind if nowhere else.

Then the far ledge took them, hard, and the world became stone and pain and the roar of the falls.

When she could think again, they were lying in a drift of pine needles forty feet below the trailhead, bruised, breathless, alive. Above them, across a gap no living thing could jump, Rell stood at the broken bridge-stub. He did not rage. He simply looked down at them for a long moment, and then he turned away, unhurried as ever, to find another road.

He would find one. Men like Rell always found one. But he would need days, and days were the only currency Oscar had left.

Their supplies were gone. Their route was gone. Ahead lay the high passes proper—two days of climbing with no food, no fire, no shelter but stone—and somewhere beyond them, the Falling Place, where an heir she'd never met guarded a heart she half-carried in her own blood.

Wren tugged her sleeve. Pointed up, past the tree line, past the snow, to where the thirteenth moon hung pale against the afternoon sky like an eye beginning to open.

"*Hurry,*" the child said, in the old tongue and then, for the first time, in words Oscar's mother might have used: "*She is waiting.*"

Oscar gathered the child onto her back, tested her bruised ribs, and started to climb.
