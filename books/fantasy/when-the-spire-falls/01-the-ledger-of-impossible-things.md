## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with cataloguing bottled echoes, Paloma had learned in his six years as keeper of the Vellum & Thorn's ledgers, was that they talked back.

Not aloud. Not in any way the guild's other clerks would have noticed. But when he uncorked one to verify its contents against the entry—*Item 4471: echo of a wedding vow, coastal dialect, faint brine undertone*—the voice inside drifted up like smoke and settled against his ear, and he heard it the way he heard everything: too well.

*"Again,"* the vow whispered. *"Say it again. I had it right the first time and she laughed and I want—"*

Paloma stoppered the bottle. Wrote *contents verified, stable* in his neat clerk's hand. Did not write: *sounds heartbroken.*

That was the rule. Six years, one rule. **Hear everything. Write nothing.**

He worked by lamplight in the counting room at the back of the guildhouse, a narrow space that smelled of ink, tallow, and the particular dust that accumulated around objects that were not entirely of this world. Shelves climbed the walls to the ceiling beams. Beyond the door, the guildhouse settled into its night sounds—the tick of cooling hearthstones, the creak of old timber, Brannic's snores rolling down from the loft above the map room like distant surf.

And beneath all of it, the raven.

Quillfeather was roosting on her perch in the front gallery, where she spent her days judging customers and stealing buttons, and she was talking to herself the way ravens did after sundown—a low, rambling mutter that most people would mistake for birdsong.

*"—clerk again, lamp still burning, always the lamp burning, tidy little clerk, folds his cuffs twice, who folds his cuffs twice? Nobody honest, that's who. Left cuff first then right cuff first then left cuff, rotation, clever, hiding something, everybody hides something, Sef hides letters under her mattress, Brannic hides the good brandy in the atlas, G for Ghent, ha, but the clerk—"* a rattle of feathers, the sound of her shifting her grip on the perch— *"the clerk hears me. Hears me right now. Doesn't move. Good little statue. Clever little statue."*

Paloma's pen did not falter. He finished the line he was writing, blotted it, and turned to the next item on the tray.

She'd been testing him for years, Quillfeather. Little probes—a dropped comment about his mother, a remark about the way he flinched at thunder before it came. He had never once reacted. It was, he suspected, the longest game either of them had ever played, and neither of them knew what winning looked like.

He lifted Item 4472 from the tray: a jar the size of two fists, wrapped in wool despite the summer heat. The ledger described it as *a dream, captive, provenance unknown, acquired from a drowning sailor off the Graywater coast.* Inside the glass, instead of smoke or light, there was weather. A slow grey swirl, like a sky deciding whether to rain.

When he held it near, the dream brushed against his mind the way a cat brushes a leg—casual, proprietary, checking if he still belonged to it.

He saw, for half a heartbeat, a kitchen he didn't recognize. A woman's hands kneading bread. A window with thirteen small panes. Someone humming.

His own throat hummed along, one note, before he caught it.

The dream swirled faster, interested.

"No," Paloma said quietly, to the jar, to himself. "We're not doing this."

The weather inside went sullen. He wrote *contents verified, dormant* —a generous description—and set it back in its wool nest.

*"—talked to it,"* Quillfeather muttered from the gallery, conversational as a neighbor over a fence. *"'We're not doing this,' says the clerk, to a jar. Talks to jars. Talks to the moths in the archive, told the moth 'left side only, eat the left side,' and the moth did it, saw it myself, ate every left page-edge and never once the right. Who notices which side a moth eats? A man who talks to moths. Tidy little liar of a clerk."*

Paloma turned the page. His handwriting remained immaculate. Somewhere in six years of practice he had achieved a penmanship so controlled it could survive anything, including an audience of one very smug bird.

It wasn't that he feared being thrown out. That was the strange part, the part he examined sometimes at night like a stone with an odd shape. Odessa Vell ran a guild that traded in bottled echoes and captive dreams and—one item, deep in Vault Three, entry redacted even from him—one stolen star. She employed a cartographer who drank, an apprentice who lied charmingly, a cook who had fled two marriages and one warrant. The Vellum & Thorn was, by any reasonable measure, an asylum for people who didn't fit anywhere else.

He would fit fine. That was the problem. He would fit *so well*, and then he would belong to them, and belonging was the one thing his mother's last instructions forbade.

*Keep your ears shut and your mouth shut and your name small,* she had written, in a letter he had memorized and burned. *A man no one sees is a man no one uses.*

He was eleven when she died. He was twelve when he presented himself at the guild's door with forged references and a gift for arithmetic, and Odessa Vell had looked at him for a long, uncomfortable moment—not through him, which was the effect he cultivated, but *at* him, which was dangerous—and said, "You'll do. Don't touch the inventory."

Six years of don't-touch-the-inventory. He touched all of it. He had to; it was his job. He simply never let it know he understood.

* * *

At midnight he allowed himself the ritual. He capped the ink, squared the day's ledger with its brothers on the shelf—all thirty-one volumes, one per year since the guild's founding, spines stamped in gold that had gone brown—and took the lamp down the corridor to do the rounds.

This was the part of the night he would have denied enjoying, if anyone had ever thought to ask, which no one ever did.

The guildhouse at midnight belonged to him alone. He checked the front bolts out of habit though nothing in six years had ever come for them. He paused in the gallery, where Quillfeather watched him with one round eye and performed, ostentatiously, a yawn.

"Good evening," Paloma said, because a clerk who spoke to no one was stranger than a clerk who greeted the raven. Everyone talked to the raven. It was practically policy.

"Evening," Quillfeather said back, in the flat tone of a bird pretending it couldn't talk, which fooled no one, which was the point. Then, under her breath, in the murmur she clearly believed no one heard: *"Says evening. Polite. Polite like a locked drawer."*

In the kitchen he found evidence of Sef's night raid: crumbs, a knife left wrong-side-up on the board, and a note in her looping hand propped against the cheese wheel. *Apprentice tax. Tell Odessa and I'll tell her who really broke the blue bottle.* The blue bottle had been broken three weeks ago. Paloma knew exactly who had broken it—he'd heard the whole event through the floor, including Brannic's elaborate attempt to frame the cat they didn't have—but he re-inked his pen and added, in his capacity as keeper, a formal deduction of two coppers from Sef's wages, which he would quietly reverse at month's end.

The system worked. The system had always worked.

In the map room, Brannic's snores changed pitch, and Paloma stood a moment in the doorway watching the big man sleep across his own charts, one arm dangling, a half-finished coastline bleeding off the edge of the table onto his cheek in ink. Brannic had fought in the border wars before the wars stopped being spoken of, and he mapped the frontier now with a tenderness he showed no living person. He called Paloma "clerk" the way other men said *son*—gruff, unexamined, safe precisely because it asked nothing.

Paloma adjusted the blanket over him without waking him and did not let himself think about how easy it would be to stay.

Vault check. Cellar check. He ended, as he always ended, in the front hall, where the guild's charter hung behind glass and the wall beside it carried the mural.

He didn't look at the mural. He made a point of not looking at it, the way you make a point of not looking at a loose tooth. Thirteen moons in a ring, painted by some founder's hand two hundred years ago, and the thirteenth scratched out so violently the plaster had scarred. Children dared each other to count them. Clerks ignored them.

Tonight, though—tonight the building was doing the thing again. The low hum in the stones, below hearing, below sense for anyone else, like a cellar full of bees thinking unhappy thoughts. It had been doing it for a week. He'd told himself it was the river running high, the frost leaving the foundations, ordinary geology.

But buildings didn't grieve. Buildings weren't alive, and the hum wasn't sound anyway, not really—it was the same register the bottled vows lived in, the register where the caged dream pressed its face to the glass.

The guildhouse was uneasy. And things that were uneasy usually knew why.

"Superstition," Paloma said aloud, to the empty hall, in his flat clerk's voice.

From the gallery, soft as falling dust: *"...says to nobody. Answers nobody. Talks to houses now."* A pause, feathers settling. *"House answers back."*

He stood very still in the dark hall, lamp raised, listening to the hum that wasn't there, and for the first time in six years the rule—*hear everything, write nothing*—felt less like armor and more like a debt coming due.

Above the city, through the high window, the stars burned on, indifferent and numerous, and Paloma Dravenmoor blew out his lamp and went up to bed, taking care, as always, to fold his cuffs twice.
