## 2. Thirteen Moons on the Wall

The trouble with a building that trades in impossible things, Paloma had learned in his nine years as keeper of the Vellum & Thorn's ledgers, was that it was never quiet.

Not audibly. To anyone without his particular affliction, the guildhouse on Candlemere Row was as silent as any respectable building: creaking timbers, settling stone, the ordinary complaints of old mortar. But Paloma heard it the way other people heard music through a wall—muffled, constant, and impossible to fully ignore. The guildhouse hummed. It hummed when the bottled echoes on their shelves resonated against one another at odd hours. It hummed when the caged dream shifted in its brass-and-glass enclosure and pressed itself against the bars like a child testing a fence. And lately—it had been humming wrong.

Uneasy. That was the word. A low, fretful vibration in the foundations, like a dog that heard something coming down the road long before its masters did.

Paloma told himself it was the weather. He was very good at telling himself things. He'd had practice.

"Ledgers," said Brannic, appearing in the doorway of the counting room with the particular gravity he reserved for matters that were not actually urgent but which he wished to be treated so. "You'll want to see this."

Brannic was the guild's cartographer, which meant he drew maps of places that were difficult to map—the shifting fens where the drowned villages walked, the trade roads that only existed on alternating Thursdays—and he brought to all human interaction the same careful, unsentimental precision. Broad shoulders gone soft with desk work, ink stains to the second knuckle, a face like a closed ledger himself. Paloma liked him enormously and had never once told him so.

"If this is about the fen-road moving again," Paloma said, "I've already adjusted the third-quarter projections."

"It's about the east stairwell." Brannic's jaw worked, as though chewing the sentence before swallowing it. "We're doing repairs. Water damage from the storm-month. Sef pulled the paneling off, and—" He stopped. Started again, which for Brannic was the equivalent of visible distress. "There's a mural behind the paneling. Old one. Older than the guild, maybe. I thought you should see it before Odessa decides what to do with it."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the one who notices things," Brannic said simply, and turned, and Paloma had no choice but to follow.

* * *

The east stairwell smelled of wet plaster and dust disturbed for the first time in decades. Sef had already stripped away a great rectangle of water-stained paneling, and she stood back from it now with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of someone who had found something she didn't want to have found. She was nineteen, quick-fingered, perpetually broke, and currently feeding an addiction to tangle-leaf she claimed she'd quit twice. She glanced at Paloma and then away, which was her way of greeting nearly everyone.

"Well," she said. "That's not creepy at all."

It covered the entire wall above the first landing: thirteen moons, rendered in pigments that had somehow kept their colors while everything around them faded. They marched across the plaster in a wide arc—a calendar, or a procession, each moon painted in a different phase and a different hue. Silver. White. The deep red of an eclipse. One that seemed to be made of honeycomb. One that was simply an open eye.

Twelve moons, whole and vivid.

And the thirteenth—

The thirteenth moon had been scratched out. Not painted over; scratched, gouged out of the plaster itself with something sharp and deliberate, over and over, until nothing remained but a ragged wound in the wall. Someone had wanted it gone. Not hidden. *Erased.* And they had wanted whoever came after to know it had been erased, because they hadn't even plastered over the damage. They'd just put up paneling and let the secret sit there under the surface, waiting.

"Huh," said Paloma, because it was expected of him.

He kept his voice level. He kept his face arranged into the mild, competent blankness of a guild clerk confronted with mildly interesting historical trivia. Inside, the humming in his skull had risen to a whine.

Because the building was singing about it.

He couldn't have explained how he knew what the guildhouse was saying—he didn't speak buildings the way he spoke beasts; there were no words, just pressure and tone—but the message was unmistakable anyway. Distress. An old distress, worn smooth by decades, the way a path is worn into stone by feet that stopped walking it generations ago. The building remembered that wall. Remembered the night the thirteenth moon was scratched away, remembered the hands that did it, and it had been holding that memory in its stones ever since, the way a bruise holds the shape of the blow.

*Something happened here,* the walls sang. *Something happened here, and we are still not allowed to say.*

"You feel it too, then," Sef said.

Paloma's stomach dropped. "Feel what?"

"The room." She shrugged, defensive. "Everyone says this stairwell's cold. Brannic won't take the shortcut past it anymore. I always figured it was drafts." She looked back at the mural. "Thirteen moons. Everyone knows there's supposed to be twelve."

"Do they?" Paloma asked, keeping his tone light, curious, clerkish. "I thought the count varied."

"Nope. Twelve. Always twelve." Sef ticked them off on her fingers, quick and practiced, a street kid's catechism. "Waxing, waning, blood, honey, eye, veil, ash, pearl, storm, green, iron, and the last one nobody agrees on. Twelve. So what's that?"

"A mistake," Paloma said. "Or a decoration. Guilds commission murals the way merchants commission portraits—flattery first, accuracy somewhere after."

Sef looked at him with the flat, unimpressed stare of someone who had grown up reading faces for survival and had never once been fooled by a guild clerk. Then she shrugged and went back to scraping paneling, and Paloma let out a breath he hadn't realized he was holding.

Brannic hadn't moved. He stood at the base of the stairs with his arms folded, studying the gouged-out moon the way he studied contested borders—with the patient attention of a man who believed every mark left on a surface was testimony.

"There's writing," he said.

"What?"

"Beneath the arc. Small. Pigment's flaked, but—" He stepped up onto the landing, close enough to touch the wall, and traced a line of ghostly letters with one thick finger. His lips moved as he read. Paloma watched him and felt the hum in the walls sharpen to a point.

"Can you make it out?" he asked.

"Some." Brannic frowned. "It's a dialect. Old trade-tongue, pre-Concord. 'When the... thirteenth turns...' Something about turning. 'The seal...'" He squinted. "'...the seal remembers.'" He straightened. "That's all I can get. The rest is dust."

*A seal,* thought Paloma, and the word landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He listened for the splash and heard nothing, and told himself that meant nothing.

"A seal of what?" Sef asked, from below.

"Could mean anything," Brannic said. "Trade contracts used seals. Guild charters. Wax, mostly." He said it reasonably, but he didn't look away from the wall, and Paloma understood suddenly that Brannic didn't believe it either. Brannic was offering the explanation the way you offer a coat to someone shivering—not because it will fix anything, but because standing still and admitting the cold helps no one.

"We should tell Odessa," Paloma said.

"I already sent word." Brannic finally turned. His eyes, pale gray as a winter harbor, settled on Paloma and stayed there a moment too long. "You went white. Just now. When I read it."

"The dust," Paloma said. "I'm allergic to dust."

"That's not true," said Sef, without looking up. "You sneezed in the archive for two hours last month and you're fine right now."

"Sef."

She raised her hands in surrender, grinning a little, and Paloma made a mental note to audit her expense claims with unusual thoroughness. It was the closest thing to retaliation available to a man whose only weapons were arithmetic.

* * *

He meant to go straight back to the ledgers. He meant to lose himself in the comfortable grammar of inventory—bottled echoes, lot forty-one, provenance disputed; captive dream, one, condition restless—and let the morning's strangeness settle into the sediment of ordinary days, where all strange things went if you ignored them long enough.

But the guildhouse would not stop humming.

It followed him through the corridors, up through the floorboards, a thread of unease woven through the stone. By mid-afternoon he had taken refuge in the archive, the deepest room in the guildhouse, three floors down, where the oldest ledgers lived behind locked doors that only he and Odessa could open. If the building had a heart, it was here. And the heart was pounding.

Paloma sat among the shelves with a lamp lit and a ledger open on his knee—one of the ancient ones, bound in vellum that had gone the color of weak tea—and tried to work, and could not. Because here was the thing he had built his entire life upon, the load-bearing wall of his existence, and the morning had put the first crack in it:

Secrets can stay buried.

His own secret had stayed buried for thirty-one years. Buried so deep that even he sometimes doubted it—that was the genius of the hiding, that the hider began to doubt the thing hidden. He was Paloma Dravenmoor, guild clerk, keeper of accounts, a man of no particular distinction. He was not a beast-speaker. Beast-speakers were storybook creatures, war relics, things that had been hunted out of the world so thoroughly that respectable people could pretend they'd never existed at all. He had spent three decades making himself invisible—no close friends, no lovers, no drunken nights where loose talk might slip out, just competence and courtesy and a life as carefully balanced as a column of figures that must never, ever fail to sum.

And it had worked. That was the unbearable part. It had worked for thirty-one years, and the proof was that he was still alive, still unnoticed, still here.

Except.

Except that a wall in his own building had been keeping a secret too—for longer than his, sealed behind paneling, and the moment the wood came off, there it was. Thirteen moons. Scratched out. *The seal remembers.*

Buildings kept secrets badly. People kept secrets badly. Everything kept secrets badly, given enough time. Stone wore through. Paneling warped. Ink faded until the words beneath showed. The world was full of buried things, and buried things had one universal property, which was that they did not stay buried.

He looked down at the ledger in his lap and realized he had read the same line eleven times. *Received of the widow Anselm, one echo (maternal), condition weeping.*

Condition weeping.

Paloma closed the ledger gently, as though it were asleep.

He should tell Odessa about the mural. That was procedure. Odessa Vell ran the guild with a firm hand and a longer memory than anyone gave her credit for, and she would want to know about a pre-Concord inscription on her own walls. He should walk upstairs and say: *Guildmaster, Brannic found a mural of thirteen moons with the last one destroyed, and words beneath it about a seal, and the building has been humming about it all day, and I would like very much to know why.*

He should. And yet some instinct older than procedure held him in the archive chair, the same instinct that had kept his mouth shut for thirty-one years. Because there was a question he could not ask out loud, and it sat in his chest now like a swallowed coal:

*Why does a guild that trades in impossible things keep a scratched-out moon hidden behind paneling?*

And beneath it, quieter, in a voice he barely permitted himself to hear:

*What else in this building is hiding?*

The lamp guttered. In the dark of the archive, the old ledgers stood in their ranks like sleeping congregations, and Paloma felt—absurdly, undeniably—the sensation of being watched by them. Of being known. The building's hum had changed key again, lower now, almost gentle. Almost pitying. As if the guildhouse were saying: *You think you're the only one down here pretending?*

"Superstition," he said aloud, to the empty room, and was comforted by how steady his voice sounded, and disturbed by how loud.

He climbed back up through the floors as evening came on. Passed the east stairwell, where Sef had draped a canvas over the mural—Odessa's orders, pending inspection. The cloth hung crooked, and one corner had slipped, and through the gap the ragged wound where the thirteenth moon had been watched him descend the stairs with its scratched-out eye.

That night, Paloma entered the day's transactions in the master ledger with perfect penmanship. Bottled echo, sold, one. Tangle-leaf, purchased, four ounces, against Sef's future wages. Nothing about murals. Nothing about moons. When he blew out his lamp he lay in his narrow bed on the top floor and listened to the guildhouse hum beneath him, uneasy, patient, keeping its time like a clock counting toward something.

In the morning, Odessa sent for him. There was a buyer downstairs, she said, who had come asking after a very particular piece of inventory.

A voice, she said, that speaks to beasts.
