## 3. A Buyer for Silence

The buyer arrived on the last market day of autumn, when the fog off the canal was thick enough to bottle and sell.

Paloma knew the Vellum & Thorn was expecting someone important because Odessa Vell had spent the morning polishing. She never polished. The guildmaster of a house that traded in bottled echoes and caged dreams considered cleanliness a form of vanity, and vanity a form of lying, and she had built her reputation on never lying about anything except the location of her vaults. But that morning she'd scrubbed the front counter until the old oak showed its grain, and she'd made Sef beat the dust out of the welcome rug, and she'd put on the gray coat with the silver thread at the cuffs, which meant: we want this person's money and we want them to leave happy.

Which meant money. Real money. The kind the guild hadn't seen since the year they sold a thunderclap to a desert prince.

"Front room," Odessa said, passing Paloma's desk without slowing. "You're taking minutes."

"I always take minutes."

"You're taking minutes *today*." She said it like a warning wrapped in an instruction, which was very like her.

Paloma pulled the ledger toward him—the good one, vellum-bound, with margins wide enough for his small precise hand—and dipped his pen. Around him the shop settled into its morning noises. Brannic arguing with the map case in the back, because the maps had been humid overnight and were sticking together like lovers. Sef stacking inventory crates with more enthusiasm than care. Quillfeather on her perch by the window, preening with the theatrical slowness of a bird who knows she's being watched and intends to be worth watching.

*Fat one comes up the street,* she said, conversationally, to no one. *Fat purse, thin patience. Smells of lilies over something dead.*

Paloma did not look up. This was the discipline of his life: he never looked up when the raven talked. Eleven years in this building and he had perfected the art of hearing everything while appearing to hear nothing—pen moving, eyes down, the very portrait of a clerk whose mind contained nothing but columns and sums.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole trick. Everyone in the guild thought Paloma Dravenmoor was the dullest man ever to hold a pen, and he had worked very hard, one careful unremarkable day at a time, to make them right.

*He's stopped,* Quillfeather reported. *Looking at the spire. Nobody looks at the spire unless they're counting windows or planning something.*

The spire rose behind the guild like a grey finger accusing the sky—one of seven left in the city from the old war, all of them sealed, all of them silent, all of them officially nothing but monuments to a history nobody wanted to discuss. Paloma counted windows in it sometimes himself, late at night, when the ledgers were done and the loneliness got loud. He'd told himself it was just insomnia. He'd told himself a lot of things.

The knock came at nine exactly. Three knocks, spaced like a heartbeat.

Odessa opened the door herself, which she almost never did.

The stranger who entered was tall in the way that suggested either nobility or a very expensive tailor, wrapped in dove-grey wool despite the season, face hidden behind a veil of black silk mesh that turned their features into a suggestion rather than a fact. Lilies and something underneath. Something dead. Quillfeather shifted on her perch, feathers flattening, and Paloma felt the raven's unease crawl across the room like cold water.

"Guildmaster Vell." The voice was neither young nor old, neither high nor low. It had been sanded down until nothing personal remained. "Thank you for receiving me."

"We open at ten," Odessa said. "You're early. Early buyers are either desperate or dangerous, and I charge extra for both. Sit."

The stranger sat. Paloma took minutes.

"The guild has a reputation," the stranger began, gloved hands folded on the counter between them, "for discretion regarding unusual acquisitions. Bottled echoes. Captive dreams. I'm told you once moved a captured star without waking it."

"We move inventory," Odessa said flatly. "Get to what you want."

"A voice." The veiled head tilted slightly. "A specific voice. One that speaks to beasts."

The pen in Paloma's hand kept moving. That was the miracle of it, the small daily miracle of his existence: the hand wrote *Item under negotiation, buyer unknown, terms pending,* while every other part of him turned to ice water.

There was no such item. There was no such inventory. There was Paloma, sitting six feet away, writing minutes.

"There is no such thing as a voice that speaks to beasts," Odessa said. Her voice had dropped into the register Paloma privately called the cellar—that low, cold place where she stored things too heavy for the upper shelves. "Beast-speakers are a story mothers tell children so they'll behave near the woods. The war ended two hundred years ago. Whatever magic made such creatures died with it."

"The war ended," the stranger agreed pleasantly. "The magic didn't. It went somewhere. Things that go somewhere can be found. Things that live—" the gloved hands spread, magnanimous, "—can be purchased."

"If you've come to buy a fairy tale, I know a man in the Lowmarket who sells bottled ones. Cheaper than me and twice as honest."

"Two hundred thousand crowns."

The number landed in the room like a dropped anvil. Sef, passing the doorway with a crate, actually stopped walking. Brannic's map-argument died mid-syllable. Even Quillfeather went still.

Paloma's pen scratched on. *Two hundred thousand crowns.* His handwriting stayed beautiful. He would remember that later, how beautiful his handwriting stayed, and it would frighten him more than anything else that happened that day.

"For a story," Odessa said.

"For a *person*," the stranger said softly. "We both know the difference. The guild has had a keeper of your ledgers for eleven years now. Diligent. Quiet. Invisible. Remarkable, the things an invisible man can hide in plain sight, in a city full of people who never look at clerks."

The pen stopped.

One drop of ink fell from the nib onto the page, spreading through the fibers of the vellum like a dark bloom, like a wound, like the exact shape of everything Paloma had spent his life preventing.

He did not look up. Looking up was confirmation. Looking up was the end of eleven years of careful, patient, invisible work, and so instead Paloma Dravenmoor did the only thing he could do: he blotted the ink, ruled a fresh line, and wrote, in his beautiful clerk's hand, *Buyer declined. Session concluded.*

But his heart was hammering against his ribs like something trying to get out of a cage, and across the room, on her perch by the window, Quillfeather had gone absolutely rigid, her black eyes fixed on him with an expression he'd never let himself read before because reading it would mean admitting what it said.

*I knew,* she'd said once, years ago, apropos of nothing, while he cleaned her cage. *I've always known, little clerk. Your secret's safe with me. Ravens keep secrets better than graves.*

He'd laughed then, nervously, and changed the subject.

Now the stranger stood, smoothing their coat, entirely unhurried. "Consider my offer. The sum is not final. The sum is whatever it needs to be." They produced a card from somewhere in the dove-grey wool—heavy stock, black-edged, blank except for a single symbol pressed into it in silver foil: a circle of thirteen marks, twelve full and one struck through. "When you're ready to sell, send word. We pay delivery costs."

"We will never be ready," Odessa said.

"All houses say that." The stranger paused at the door, veil catching the grey light. "All houses fall. Good day, Guildmaster."

Then the fog swallowed them, and the door closed, and the shop exhaled.

* * *

For three hours afterward, nobody spoke about it, which in the Vellum & Thorn was itself a kind of roar.

Sef restacked the same crate four times. Brannic rolled and unrolled a map of the northern coast that nobody had asked about. Odessa disappeared into her office and did not come out, and through the door came the sound of a drawer opening, and paper, and the scratch of a pen much less calm than Paloma's own.

It was Brannic who finally broke, stomping into the front room where Paloma still sat, ledger open, having written nothing new for three hours.

"They knew," Brannic said. No preamble. Brannic didn't believe in preambles; he said maps needed no introduction and neither did he. "Whoever that was, they knew. Not guessed. *Knew*. Eleven years, and somebody walks in off the street and names you like a price."

"I don't know what you mean," Paloma said. "They asked after some story. Odessa told them there's no such thing."

Brannic looked at him. Brannic had a cartographer's eyes—eyes that measured, that noticed where rivers bent wrong and coastlines had been redrawn—and when those eyes settled on Paloma they did the same thing they did to falsified charts. Found the alteration. Measured it. Filed it away.

"Right," Brannic said slowly. "Some story."

He walked back to his maps. Paloma stared at the blot on the page.

The blot had dried into something almost deliberate-looking. A mark. If you squinted—if you were the sort of person who saw omens in ink—it might have been a bird. It might have been an eye. Paloma closed the ledger on it before he could decide.

* * *

Quillfeather found him at dusk on the back stairs, where he'd gone to breathe air that didn't smell like lilies.

She landed on the rail beside him and said nothing, which from Quillfeather was louder than shouting. For a long moment the only sounds were the evening bells and the foghorn on the river and, beneath it all, faint as a held breath, the hum of the guildhouse itself—the sound only he heard, the building's old bones murmuring in their sleep. Tonight the murmur sounded uneasy. Tonight the walls felt like a man trying not to cough during a funeral.

*They'll come back,* Quillfeather said at last.

"I know."

*Not for Odessa's answer. Odessa's answer was never the point. You don't make an offer that size to buy something. You make it to tell the seller you already know where the thing is.*

"I know that too."

*And?*

"And I don't know what to do." The admission came out of him before he could stop it, quiet and cracked, and he hated how it sounded—eleven years of control unraveling on a stairwell because a raven asked a question. "I have run the numbers, Quillfeather. Every scenario. Someone talked. Someone always talks eventually, that's the arithmetic of secrets—the longer you keep one, the more hands touch it, the greater the chance of leakage. I should have left five years ago. I should have left last spring. I stayed because—" He stopped. Because the guild was the first place that had ever felt warm. Because Sef saved him the burnt ends of the honey bread and pretended not to. Because Brannic had named a river after him on a map once, jokingly, *the Draven*, and Paloma had gone home and been quietly ridiculous about it for a week. Because leaving meant starting over alone, again, and he was so tired, and the tiredness had started to feel like a country he lived in.

*Because you love them,* Quillfeather said. *Yes. I know. I have eyes.*

"That makes me stupid."

*It makes you a bad accountant of yourself. Different disease.* She hopped closer along the rail, close enough that he could see the oil-sheen of purple in her black feathers where the lamplight caught them. *Listen to me, little clerk. Birds understand hiding. Hiding is half of what feathers are for. And I will tell you what the sparrows told me today, what the whole street's been saying since that stranger walked out of here: there are others asking. Not just the one. A fat purse in the spice quarter. A veiled woman at the ferry docks. Same question, same words—a voice that speaks to beasts. Same symbol on their cards.* She tapped the rail with her beak, once, hard. *Twelve circles and one crossed out. The sparrows think it's a religion.*

"A religion."

*Everything people do in groups is a religion. Sparrows are very clear on this.* 

Paloma looked out at the city. From the back stairs you could see the spire rising above the rooftops, black against the deepening violet sky, and above it—the first stars coming out, indifferent and ancient, and the moon rising fat and pale over the eastern wall. He counted without meaning to. Old habit. Thirteen moons in the cycle the old calendars used, though only twelve hung in the sky these days; the thirteenth was a superstition, a scribal error, a myth. Everyone knew that.

Everyone official, anyway.

"Odessa knows something," he said. "She didn't even flinch at the price. You don't refuse two hundred thousand crowns without flinching unless you already knew someone would offer it someday."

*The guildmaster keeps her own ledgers,* Quillfeather agreed. *Ones she doesn't show the clerk.*

"Then I need to read them."

*Now that,* said Quillfeather, *is the first interesting plan you've had in eleven years.*

He should laugh. He didn't. Down in the street, a figure in dove-grey stood at the corner opposite the guildhouse, motionless in the fog, face turned upward toward the spire. Watching. Counting windows, maybe. Or waiting for a light to go out.

Paloma stood, and the unease in the walls rose around him like water, and he realized with terrible clarity that the life he'd built—the careful, invisible, beloved life—had a hole in it now, shaped exactly like a veiled stranger's voice saying *a person, we both know the difference.*

That night, Odessa called the family to the long table and fed them stew and said, "Business as usual tomorrow. Nobody speaks of the visitor. Anyone asks, we sell echoes and dreams and nothing stranger, and we've never heard of beast-speakers, and neither has anyone's mother." She looked at each of them in turn, and her gaze rested on Paloma one heartbeat longer than the others—not accusation, he told himself, just bookkeeping, just the guildmaster counting her assets—but he couldn't be sure, and being unable to be sure was its own kind of drowning.

Later, in his narrow room under the eaves, Paloma lay awake and listened to the house settle, and did not tell anyone anything.

He thought about going to Odessa. *Someone knows what I am. Someone offered a fortune for me. I think we should talk about it.* He composed the speech four times. Each time it died in his throat, because confession was a door and doors swing both ways, and if Odessa already knew—if she had known for eleven years, if the guild itself was some kind of cage built soft—then telling her would change nothing except that he would finally know he'd never been safe. And if she didn't know, then telling her would put her in danger, and Brannic, and Sef, and Quillfeather, and every warm careless kindness in the building would become leverage with a price tag.

So he confessed nothing. He lay in the dark and guarded his silence the way he'd guarded it all his life, and hated how practiced it felt.

Somewhere below, the guildhouse hummed its uneasy hum. Somewhere out in the fog, the watcher at the corner had not moved in four hours. And somewhere overhead, past the smoke and cloud, the stars were burning down toward the night—the last ordinary night, though no one alive knew it yet—when every star in the sky would still be where it belonged.

Paloma slept badly. In his dream, a voice like sand and lilies counted to thirteen, and on the thirteenth number, all the lights went out.
