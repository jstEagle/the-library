## 4. The Night the Stars Went Out

The auction ledger for the autumn gala ran to eleven pages, and Paloma had copied it twice because Odessa's handwriting in the margins looked like a spider arguing with a fountain pen.

*Lot 14: bottled echo, widow of a drowned sailor, weeping quality. Reserve: forty crowns. Do not shake.*

*Lot 22: captive dream, lucid, mildly vengeful. Cage reinforced. Bidders advised to keep hands inside the viewing radius.*

*Lot 31: one (1) stolen star. Withdrawn from sale per Guildmaster's order. No bids will be taken. Anyone who asks about Lot 31 gets shown the door and then the street.*

He had inked that last line himself, at Odessa's dictation, three days ago, and he had not asked why a star could be owned but not sold. That was the arrangement that kept him employed and alive: the Vellum & Thorn traded in impossible things, and Paloma Dravenmoor asked no questions that couldn't be answered with numbers. He was good with numbers. He was very good at being nobody — a thin, gray man behind a high desk, spectacles he didn't need, a stoop he practiced. Twenty-six years old and he had spent all of them learning to be furniture.

Furniture did not flinch when Quillfeather landed on his shoulder.

"You're doing the thing," the raven said, into his ear only, because that was another part of the arrangement — the one no ledger recorded, the one that would get him killed if anyone ever heard him answer. "The breathing-in-counts thing. You do it when you're nervous."

"I'm counting lots," Paloma said, barely moving his lips, eyes on the page. "It's arithmetic."

"It's *brooding*. There were forty-two bidders at the door and you counted them twice."

"Forty-one."

"Forty-one," she conceded, ruffling her feathers so they brushed his jaw like a hand. "One of them is wearing her veil indoors and carrying a scent I don't like. Iron and church-candle. The moon-people smell like that. I told Brannic and he said I was being dramatic, which is rich coming from a man who irons his maps."

"Ravens are dramatic by nature," Paloma murmured. "It's in your bones. Like hoarding shiny things and holding grudges."

"I hold *records*," Quillfeather said with dignity. Then, quieter, with the terrible precision ravens reserved for the truths they dropped like stones down wells: "You should tell Odessa about the veiled one."

"And say what? 'Your clerk overheard the furniture gossiping'?" He turned the page with unnecessary firmness. "No one knows what I am. That's the whole point of me."

Quillfeather said nothing to that, which was how he knew she disagreed. She was very good at disagreeing silently. She'd had years of practice.

Below the gallery, the auction hall of the Vellum & Thorn had been dressed for its biggest night of the season. Chandeliers of bottled light — harmless, legal, boring — hung over rows of velvet chairs filled with the kind of money that wore jewelry as armor. Sef moved through the crowd with a tray of cordials, seventeen and quick and pretending hard not to look at the price tags on people's collars. Brannic stood at the map table by the wall, showing a fat merchant a survey of the borderlands with the patient air of a man explaining rain to a fish. And at the podium, Guildmaster Odessa Vell held the room the way she held everything: lightly, and completely.

"Lot fourteen," her voice carried, warm as banked coals. "A bottled echo, provenance verified. The widow of Captain Ilves, who went down off the Greywind coast. Handle it gently, friends. Grief keeps."

The echo passed from hand to hand under glass, a small grey swirl that wept faintly when tilted. Paloma watched the bidding climb and entered each raise without looking up. This was the shape of his life and he had made peace with its shape: the guild traded in other people's impossible things, and he kept the accounts of them, and at the end of every night he climbed the stairs to his narrow room above the vaults and listened to the building settle and the city murmur through the walls, and he was alone in a way that had long since stopped hurting and started merely being, like weather.

That was the lie he told himself, anyway. Loneliness doesn't stop hurting. It just learns your name.

*Lot nineteen,* he wrote. *Caged dream, lucid. Passed in at reserve. Note: cage scratched from the inside again. Recommend thicker bars or an apology.*

The dream had been muttering to itself all evening from its cage on the side table — not words, exactly; dreams didn't use words — more a pressure, a sulky heat against the edge of his hearing. Most of the guild thought the dream cage was empty. Only Paloma heard it pacing.

Only Paloma heard, at half past nine, every dog in Amberfall begin to howl at once.

* * *

It started small, the way disasters do. A single hound somewhere near the rivergate, one long note of alarm. Then a second voice joining, then ten, then the whole ragged choir of the city — dogs first, then cats yowling from rooftops in voices stripped of their usual self-importance, then the horses in the livery districts screaming like things being unmade. Underneath it, lower than hearing, the pigeons rose off every roof in Amberfall in one motion, a single vast wingbeat, and fled east.

Paloma's pen stopped mid-stroke.

Because it wasn't alarm. He knew alarm; alarm was sharp and directional and faded. This was something he had never heard before, something that poured through the stone of the guildhall and up through the soles of his boots and into the bones of his inner ear where his forbidden gift lived: grief. Enormous, city-wide, animal grief. Every creature in Amberfall was mourning something, all at once, with total conviction.

And they were mourning *upward*.

"Master Dravenmoor." One of the auction stewards, frowning toward the tall windows. "Are the kennels—"

"No," Paloma said. His own voice sounded far away. He was already standing, which he never did during auctions, which furniture does not do, and half the room was turning now, the money-people sensing disturbance the way sharks sense blood. "Excuse me. Ledger matter."

He walked to the windows with his heart going like a hammer in a bottle, and he looked up at the sky over Amberfall.

The stars were going out.

Not clouding — there wasn't a cloud from here to the horizon, the night was clean and cold. Not eclipsing — this was no slow shadow eating the moon. They were going out the way candles go out when someone walks past and breathes: in order, in rows, in *waves*, a darkness rolling across the sky from the north like a tide of black water, and each star it touched simply stopped. Not dimmed. *Stopped.* As if someone up there were pinching wicks between enormous fingers, methodically, one constellation at a time.

Beside him, a merchant's wife gasped. Down in the hall, a hundred conversations died mid-word. Someone dropped a cordial glass and nobody bent to pick it up. Through the open window, above the howling, Paloma could hear human voices now too, spilling into the streets, and beneath the humans the animals went on grieving, and their grief had a shape he could almost read, a single word repeated across every throat and burrow and nest in the city:

*Gone. Gone. Gone. They're gone. Who took them? Who took them?*

"They didn't die," Paloma whispered. He didn't know he was saying it aloud until Quillfeather's claws tightened on his shoulder. "They were—"

Then the wards fell.

* * *

People would argue about the sound afterward. Some swore it was thunder; some, the tolling of a cracked bell; the priests of the Ninefold said it was the voice of heaven clearing its throat. But everyone in Amberfall heard it, and everyone described it the same way in the end, when they finally stopped pretending to be calm: like glass breaking. All the glass in the world, breaking at once, very far away and very close, a sound that came not through the ears but through the teeth and the breastbone and the water in every cup.

Paloma felt it go through him like a hook drawn out of flesh. For one blinding instant he *heard* the border wards — truly heard them, for the first time in his life, though he'd lived beside them all his days the way you live beside your own heartbeat. They had been singing. All along, all those years, a vast low chord laid over the world, and he had never noticed because he had never been without it. Now the chord snapped, and the silence it left behind rang worse than the breaking.

Every hair on his arms stood up. Somewhere in the marrow-deep place where his gift lived, something whispered, in a voice like his mother might have had, in a language older than the guild, older than the city:

*Now they can hear you too.*

"Everyone stay calm," Odessa's voice cut through the hall, and it was remarkable, it was masterful, it did not tremble even slightly, and Paloma — who knew her ledgers better than anyone, who knew the exact weight of her handwriting — heard the tremble anyway, buried where only an accountant would find it. "The wards are a municipal concern. The Guild of Vellum & Thorn deals in rare goods, not omens. Sef, bar the doors. Brannic, the lamps — all of them, now."

"But the bidding—" began the fat merchant from the map table.

"The auction," Odessa said, "is concluded."

In the sudden lamplight, faces floated pale as drowned things. The veiled stranger stood perfectly still amid the stirring crowd, and though Paloma could see nothing of her face, he felt — distinctly, horribly — her head turn, tracking him at the window, the way a compass needle finds north. Quillfeather felt it too; she flattened herself against his neck and went silent in the way birds go silent when the hawk's shadow crosses.

The animals outside were still crying. Dogs, horses, the cattle in the stockyards, the rats in the walls — the rats most terribly of all, because rats never stop talking and now their chatter had collapsed into one keening thread that ran through the guildhall's foundations like a live wire: *the lights are gone, the lights are gone, our mothers told us the lights would always be there, the lights are gone.*

And under it, deeper, older — from beyond the border, from the wild country where the wards had stood for three hundred years, something else was moving through the new silence. Something waking up. Paloma couldn't have said how he knew. He knew the way he knew which lot was mispriced. It was simply there, suddenly, in the opened dark: attention. Vast and patient and turning toward the city like a head turning in bed.

"Odessa." Brannic's voice, low, at the podium. He had a survey chart in his hands and his knuckles were white around it. "My instruments. Every needle I own is spinning. The wardlines aren't degraded, they're *absent*. All of them. North, south, east, west. It's as if they were never—"

"I know what it's as if." Odessa was already moving, stripping off her auction gloves finger by finger, and Paloma watched her do it and understood that she was arming herself. "Get the strongroom. Wake the vault-wards. And somebody find my clerk."

"I'm here," Paloma said.

She stopped. Looked at him — really looked, for the first time in six years of employment, the full weight of her attention like a hand laid flat on his chest — and whatever she saw in his face made her expression change by a fraction, some door opening a crack behind her eyes.

"Yes," she said slowly. "I rather think you are."

Outside, the last of the howling faded as the animals fell silent — not calmed, Paloma knew, but listening, every creature in the dark city cocking its head toward the north, toward the fallen wards, toward the thing that was waking. Even the rats had gone quiet. In twelve years of listening to the secret speech of beasts, Paloma had never once heard the world hold its breath.

Above the guildhall, where the stars had been, the sky was a smooth and perfect black, like the cover of a ledger waiting to be written in.

Quillfeather pressed her beak to his ear and spoke the only prayer either of them knew.

"*Paloma,*" she said. "*What did we just lose?*"
