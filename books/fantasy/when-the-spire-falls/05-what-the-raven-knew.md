## 5. What the Raven Knew

The guild slept badly the night the stars went out, and so did I.

Not that anyone would have guessed it from the ledgers. By dawn I had reconciled three weeks of entries, corrected Brannic's ink-smudged figures on the bottled echo shipments, and drafted a contingency inventory in case the wards' collapse triggered a run on protective charms. The Vellum & Thorn had survived guild wars, a fire, and one memorable incident involving a tax assessor and a jar of captive lightning. It would survive a dark sky. Numbers were good for that. Numbers didn't care what was missing above them.

I was at my desk on the mezzanine, where I could see the whole shop below—the shelves of impossible things, the auction floor with its chairs still stacked from the interrupted sale, the great iron door with its new crossbars—and I was pretending, as I always did, that this was enough.

It wasn't working.

Every animal in Coppervein had been screaming since midnight. Not with their throats—with something underneath their throats, a frequency I'd spent thirty years learning to ignore, and now it wouldn't be ignored. The dogs were keening in the streets. The horses in the livery down the lane had kicked their stalls to splinters. And the rats—there were always rats, in a building like ours, in a city like ours—were moving through the walls in slow processions, and their voices carried a word I hadn't heard them use before.

*Mourning.*

They said it over and over. *Mourning, mourning,* like a bell tolling, like they were walking a funeral for the sky itself.

I pressed my palms against my ears until my head rang. It didn't help. The voices weren't in my ears. They never had been.

"You look terrible," said Quillfeather.

She landed on the ledger stand with the particular insolence of a bird who has never once been told no. She was a raven the size of a loaf of bread, black feathers shot through with an oil-slick sheen of violet, and officially she was the guild's messenger and mascot. Unofficially she was the most dangerous gossip in Coppervein, and I had spent eleven years performing an elaborate pantomime of *not understanding her* every time she perched near me and said something cutting about Odessa's love affairs or Sef's study habits.

Eleven years. That's how long I'd known her. Eleven years of careful, practiced deafness.

But the sky was dark, and the wards had fallen, and somewhere out there a wolf-pack was screaming words only I could hear, and I was so tired.

"Terrible," Quillfeather repeated, tilting her head. Her eye was black and wet and ancient. "Worse than usual. You've got the look of a man doing arithmetic to keep from screaming."

"I don't know what you mean," I said. "Birds say things. I've been over this."

"Oh, *birds say things.*" She hopped closer, claws clicking on the ledger board, right across my neat columns. "Yes. Birds say things. Dogs say things. Rats say things. Horses, cats, the fat carp in Odessa's courtyard pond who holds grudges longer than most marriages—all of them say things, all day, every day, and you have stood there with your little clerk face nodding along like a man listening to weather reports in a language he definitely doesn't speak."

My pen stopped moving.

"Your left eyebrow," she said, "does a thing. When I talk. Just the left one. It twitches up like it's trying to escape your skull. Eleven years, Paloma Dravenmoor. Eleven years of watching that eyebrow, and every year I thought: *this is the year he stops pretending.*"

"Quillfeather." My voice came out strange. Flat. "You should go back to the roost."

"The roost smells like Sef's potions homework. No thank you." She sidestepped, dragging one foot across the margin where I'd totaled the echo inventory, leaving a faint scratch. "Here's what's going to happen instead. The sky is empty, the wards are ash, the whole city's animals have lost their minds with grief, and you are sitting up here adding numbers because it's the only prayer you know. So we're going to talk. Properly. For once."

"There's nothing to talk about."

"*Paloma.*"

And there it was. My name, in her voice—a raven's croak reshaped into human syllables, rough and precise, and she said it the way my mother must have said it, the way nobody living had ever said it, with the emphasis on the second syllable, the way it was meant—

I looked at her. Really looked. And she looked back, unblinking, patient as stone.

"How long," I whispered.

"How long have I known?" She fluffed her feathers, settling in like a merchant about to name an outrageous price. "Since the week I arrived. You fed me dried fish behind the counting room and asked me—not the other keepers, *me*—whether the road east was safe for travelers with carts. Because you wanted to know if the *horses* thought it was safe. You were cleverer than you thought, and less clever than you needed to be." A dry clicking sound, raven laughter. "After that I watched. The way you listen at stable doors. The way you always know which delivery mule is going to bite before it does. The way you go gray when the rat colonies get loud. I've known for eleven years, clerk. And in eleven years I have told exactly no one."

"Why?"

That stopped her. She turned her head away, toward the dark windows, and for a moment she was just a bird looking at an empty sky, and there was nothing funny in her at all.

"Because I know what it is," she said quietly, "to be the only one of your kind in a room full of people who'd sell you if they knew what you were. Ravens remember, Paloma. We remember being hunted for our feathers. Being blamed for the plague-years. Being called omens by men who then made us their omens, their tools, their messengers, and thanked us by caging us." She looked back at me. "A secret like yours isn't a weight you carry alone. It's a weight someone has to help hold. I decided I could hold one end. Quietly. Without asking your permission, because you'd have panicked and denied everything, and your eyebrow would have fallen off from twitching."

I couldn't speak. There was a pressure behind my sternum, huge and formless, and I understood with sudden, humiliating clarity that I had been lonely for thirty years and hadn't let myself know it, and now a raven was telling me she'd been standing beside me the whole time.

"You knew," I managed. "All these years. You watched me pretend, and you let me."

"I let you *survive*," she said. "There's a difference. Pretending kept you safe. It also kept you small, but safety first, that's my philosophy. Small and alive beats large and dead in every ledger I've ever read." She hopped onto the back of my chair, close enough that her feathers brushed my ear. "Besides. Watching you was the best theater in the guild. The eyebrow alone."

Something escaped me that might have been a laugh. It came out cracked and half-sobbed, and I covered my mouth, and Quillfeather politely pretended not to notice, which is the highest courtesy one creature can pay another.

* * *

We sat in the graying light while the city grieved below us. Eventually I asked the question I'd been circling since midnight.

"The animals. What are they saying? All this mourning—I don't understand it. Stars burn out. Stars die. It's sad, but—why do they sound like this? Why do the wolves sound like they're at a *funeral*?"

Quillfeather went very still. Ravens are never still; stillness in a raven is like silence in a struck bell. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped into a register I'd never heard her use—no mockery, no performance. Just the plain truth, delivered flat.

"Because stars don't die like fires die, Paloma. Ask any beast. We've always known more about the sky than you featherless folk—we navigate by it, we mate by it, we migrate ten thousand miles on patterns your astronomers haven't charted. And every animal that ever lived knows the difference between a thing ending and a thing *leaving*." Her claw tightened on the chair back. "Last night, the sky didn't end. It was *emptied*. Do you understand? A star dying leaves a corpse—a dimming, a falling, a last light going red. There was none of that. One heartbeat the stars were there, singing—that hum, that old hum under everything, the one the wards used to drink—and the next heartbeat they were gone, all together, clean as a picked pocket. No corpses. No falling. Taken."

"Taken," I repeated. "By what?"

"That's the word coming up from everywhere. From the wolves in the hills, from the whales off the coast—the harbor seals passed it upriver two days ago, they felt it in the water first. *Taken. Hidden. Sealed.*" She paused. "And here's the part you're really not going to like."

"I doubt anything else today can surprise me."

"They say it was done by hands. Beast-hands or man-hands, the stories disagree—but hands, and a place, and a purpose. The animals aren't mourning a death, Paloma. They're mourning a *theft*. Somewhere out there, someone is holding the stars the way your guild holds bottled echoes. In jars. In cages." Her black eye fixed on mine, and I felt it like a hook behind my ribs. "In a vault."

The ledger swam in front of me. Thirteen moons scratched off a mural. A veiled stranger offering obscene money for a voice like mine. Wards that fell the same hour the sky emptied, wards that everyone said drank from something, fed on something—

Fed on *voices*.

"Odessa knows," I heard myself say. "Or the founder did. This guild—Quillfeather, what does this guild actually *do*?"

"Now that," said Quillfeather, "is an excellent question, and here's a piece of the answer for free: last night, after the sky went out, Odessa went down into the vault levels. The deep ones. The ones that aren't on any ledger you keep." She clicked her beak. "She took a lamp and the founder's key, and she stood in front of the sealed door down there for a long time. And before she touched it, she said a word to the door. Softly. Like a password, or an apology." Her head tilted. "And the door, Paloma—the door *answered* her. In a voice. Not a voice like mine or yours. A voice like every animal in Coppervein screaming at once, folded down small enough to fit behind iron."

The pressure in my chest had become a cold, spreading thing.

"She talked to it," I said. "And it answered."

"And then she locked it again and came upstairs and started organizing crossbars on the doors like a woman buying time." Quillfeather spread her wings, resettled them. "The animals have one more thing to say, and then I'll let you do your arithmetic. They say the theft isn't finished. Whatever was taken—the stars, the hum, the voice—they say the takers aren't done, that something is still *open* down under the city, something that was supposed to stay shut until—" she stumbled over the shape of it, a phrase passed beak to beak through a thousand species, "—until the moon turns thirteen times. Until the thirteenth turn completes. And when it does, whatever's behind that door won't need a keeper anymore. It will open itself."

Outside the window, far away in the western hills, a wolf began to howl. Then another. Then a dozen, a long rising chord of grief that rolled across the dark city, and beneath it, in the register only I could hear, they weren't howling at the empty sky at all.

They were howling a warning. The same word, over and over, passed from pack to pack, hill to hill, all the way to the walls:

*Thirteen. Thirteen. Thirteen.*

Quillfeather watched my face do whatever it did.

"So," she said gently. "Still want to reconcile those ledgers?"

Down on the auction floor, boots on stairs. Odessa, climbing from the vault levels, lamp in hand, her face pale as paper—and she was heading straight for my mezzanine, straight for me, and the founder's key was still hanging from the cord around her neck.

"Paloma," she called up, and her voice was wrong—careful, weighted, a voice carrying something it wasn't sure I could hold. "Come downstairs. There are things you need to see. Things about the guild. And about—" she hesitated, and I saw her choose the word, "—about *voices*."

Quillfeather hopped off my shoulder and onto the dark windowsill, silhouetted against a starless sky.

"Told you," she murmured. "Best theater in the guild."
