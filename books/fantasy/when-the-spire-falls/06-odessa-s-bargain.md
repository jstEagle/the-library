## 6. Odessa's Bargain

Odessa Vell did not knock.

She never knocked. She owned the building; knocking, she said once, was for people who feared what they'd find on the other side of the door. So when I heard her boots on the stairs at an hour when even the raven had gone quiet—third bell past midnight, the ledger's ink still tacky under my pen—I had exactly the time it took her to cross the landing to decide whether to close the book in front of me or leave it open.

I closed it. Then, because that looked like guilt, I opened it again to a boring page. Bottled echoes, lot forty-one. Nothing to see here. Just a clerk and his columns.

She came in anyway.

"Paloma." She said my name the way she appraised inventory: weighing it, checking for cracks. She was not in her nightclothes. She was dressed as if for a negotiation, which meant she'd been waiting for me to finish pretending to sleep before she came down, which meant she knew I didn't sleep much, which meant—

"You've been counting my footsteps again," she said.

"I count everything. It's a clerk thing."

"Mm." She took the chair across from my desk without being offered it, and set something on the wood between us with a click that made my stomach drop through three floors into the cellar.

A key. Old iron, black with age, its teeth worn almost smooth. I recognized the shape of it from the oldest ledgers—the ones chained shut in the vault, the ones Brannic wasn't allowed to touch and I was only allowed to touch because nobody thought about me enough to forbid it. Invisibility had its uses. This was one of the few nights I wished it worked better.

"Do you know what this opens?" she asked.

"No."

"Liar." No heat in it. Almost fond. "You're a bad liar, Paloma. You always have been. It's why I trust you with money—you can't bring yourself to fudge a column even when it would spare you a scolding." She leaned back. "But you lie beautifully about yourself. That's a different skill entirely. Where did you learn it?"

The candle between us guttered. Upstairs, faintly, Quillfeather shifted on her roost, and I heard her mutter something in raven-speech that translated roughly to *here it comes*.

"The guild takes in strays," I said carefully. "That's the whole business model. Strays are cheap and loyal."

"And where do strays learn to hear ravens?"

The world went very still and very loud at once. The candle flame stood up straight like a soldier at attention. Somewhere below us, in the vaults, water dripped from a pipe that hadn't dripped in years—I heard it now, clearly, the way I'd been hearing things all my life and hiding it: the building's small sounds, the mice in the walls discussing the weather, the caged dream upstairs turning over in its sleep and murmuring in a language made of colors.

I could deny it. I'd denied it for thirty-one years. I had a whole architecture of denial built up around this exact moment, load-bearing walls and all.

But Odessa had put a key on the table instead of calling the wardens, and that told me more than any confession could.

"How long have you known?" I asked. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of that.

"Since your second week." She smiled, thin and tired. "You corrected the inventory of the aviary. We were missing two finches, and everyone else assumed they'd escaped. You said, 'They didn't escape, they were bought by the perfumer on Cordwainer Lane, he wanted their song for his new blend.' And then you went white as vellum and insisted you'd deduced it from the cage latch." She tapped the desk. "Nobody deduces anything from a cage latch, Paloma. But the perfumer *had* bought them, so I let it go, and I watched, and over the years I collected maybe nine hundred other moments just like it. You're very good. You're not that good."

I sat there and let thirty-one years of careful invisibility collapse quietly around me like a tent with a snapped pole.

"Why keep me on?" I asked finally. "If you knew. A beast-speaker is—" I reached for the word the world used. "Dangerous. Illegal in three provinces. Worth a fortune to the wrong buyers."

"A beast-speaker is a person who works cheap and stays late," Odessa said. "Everything else is politics." Her face did something complicated then, something I'd never seen on it in twelve years of employment: it aged. Not the surface aging of wrinkles but the deep settling of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body. "And tonight, keeping you on stopped being a courtesy and became a necessity. Which brings us to the key."

She turned it over with one finger. The candlelight slid off the black iron.

"When I say 'the guild,' what do you picture?"

"Bottled echoes. Caged dreams. The stolen star. Auctions on quarter-days. Ledgers."

"All true. All the shop window." She stood, and I realized she expected me to follow her, and my legs obeyed before my fear finished filing its objection. "The guild has two businesses, Paloma. One trades in impossible things. The other exists to make sure one particular impossible thing is never traded, never found, never *used*. We are not merchants who guard a secret. We are guards who sell trinkets to pay for the guarding. It's been that way since the founding. Vellum and Thorn weren't lovers, whatever the songs say. They were a scribe and a warden, and they built this place as a lock with a gift shop attached."

We went down. Past the auction hall with its dust-sheeted podium, past the vault door with its three locks, down a stair I had catalogued in my head years ago but never actually walked—a stair behind a wall that swung open at Odessa's touch, breathing out cold air that smelled of stone and old rain and, beneath it, something I had no name for until I did:

Voice.

Not sound. Not yet. A pressure, like standing near a waterfall you can't see, like the moment before a flock lifts off all at once. Every hair on my arms rose. The mice in the walls above us went silent. Even the building itself—that low hum I'd been pretending not to hear since chapter one of my life here—hushed, the way a crowd hushes.

At the bottom of the stair was a door. Black iron. The key fit it the way my secret had just failed to fit inside me anymore: perfectly, and too late.

Beyond it, a chamber. And in the chamber—

I don't know how to write what was in the chamber, and I'm a man whose entire trade is writing things down. There was no object. There was an absence shaped like presence, a stillness at the center of the room that was somehow louder than the rest of the world combined. The stone floor around it was worn smooth in concentric rings, as if people had circled it for centuries, never approaching, never leaving.

"The First Voice," Odessa said quietly. "Before the old war, before the wards, before the provinces drew their tidy borders—there was one language, and every living thing spoke it. Beasts. Birds. The rivers, if the oldest accounts are believed. Humanity lost it in the war—or most of humanity did—but the war's last working of magic didn't destroy the Voice. It drove it underground. Sealed it here. And every beast-speaker born since, every child who can talk to a raven or calm a wolf, is..." She hesitated, choosing the word like choosing a coin from a purse.

"A leak," I said.

She nodded slowly. "A crack in the seal. The Voice finds a throat now and then and speaks through it. The border wards—the great ones, the ones that fell three nights ago—they weren't built to keep enemies out. That's the story we tell. They were built to *press down* on this chamber. To smother the Voice so thoroughly that no crack could form. They fed on voices to do it. On yours. On your mother's. On every speaker the wards ever touched." She looked at me, and her eyes in the dark were older than her face. "You felt it, didn't you? Your whole life. Like singing into a pillow."

I had. God help me, I had. Every time I spoke to an animal I'd felt the words come out flattened, muffled, half-strangled on the way to the ear that needed them—and I'd assumed that was my flaw. My brokenness. Thirty-one years of thinking my gift was defective when it was being *taxed*.

"They fell," I said. "The wards. The stars went dark the same hour. What does that mean for this?"

"It means the press is lifting." Odessa's voice was flat, which is how I knew she was terrified; Odessa was only ever flat when the alternative was screaming. "It means the Voice is getting louder every day, and somewhere out there is a moon-cult that spent decades preparing for exactly this night, and they want what's under this door. And I am the last warden of a guild that's forgotten it's a garrison, and I cannot work the sealing rites alone."

She turned to face me fully.

"The rites require two speakers. One to command, one to consent. Thorn wrote them that way deliberately—a seal laid by force won't hold. The Voice must be *asked*, and someone must answer for it." She paused, and here she did the hardest thing I ever saw her do: she asked. "Help me reseal it, Paloma. Before the thirteenth moon turns. Before the cult finds the door."

* * *

We climbed back up in silence. The wall sealed behind us. The building resumed its hum.

In my office, she poured two fingers of plum brandy from the bottle I kept for bribing inspectors and pushed one glass at me. I drank mine without tasting it.

"You should know," she said, "that I'm not asking you to do this blind. If you say yes, I'll teach you everything. The rites, the history, the ledgers Thorn kept. All of it. A speaker who consents to a seal deserves to know what he's consenting to." She gathered her coat. At the door she stopped, her back to me. "And Paloma. Whatever you're not telling me—and you're not telling me several somethings, I can see them stacked behind your eyes like unsold stock—you should know that withholding them will cost you later. Not because I'll punish you. Because secrets are heavy, and you'll be carrying enough weight soon."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with the brandy and the ledger and the enormous fact that my life had changed shape while I wasn't looking.

* * *

I sat until the candle burned down to a coin of wax. Then I did what clerks do. I opened a fresh page and I thought.

Because here is the truth I could not give her: I already knew about the First Voice. Not the name—she gave me that—but the *fact* of it, the way you know a scar's shape without remembering the wound. I'd known since I was six years old, since my mother pressed a bottled echo into my hands and said words I've carried longer than I've carried my own name: *When they come asking, tell them nothing. When the guildmaster asks, say yes.*

Say yes. To Odessa. Twelve years ago, a dying woman scripted this exact conversation, and I'd walked into it tonight like a mark walking into a fixed game, and the cruelest part—the part I couldn't stop turning over—was that I couldn't tell whether I'd been played or *protected*. Maybe both. Maybe those were the same thing, in my family.

So I said nothing. I would say nothing. I would learn her rites and read her ledgers and stand beside her at the seal, and I would do it for reasons of my own that sat coiled under my ribs like a second heartbeat: because my mother had told me to, because the wards had been strangling my voice since birth and I wanted—God, I *wanted*—to see the thing that had done it, and because if the Voice remembered the war the way the ghost-stories said it did, then somewhere in that absence-shaped silence was a memory of a woman with my eyes and her hands full of stars.

I lied to Odessa Vell with my whole honest face. I said yes.

She studied me for a long moment, and I would swear on every ledger I've ever balanced that she saw straight through me—saw the mother, the bottle, the buried orders—and chose to accept the lie anyway, because she had withholdings of her own stacked behind her flat grey eyes, weights she'd carried so long they'd grown into her spine.

"Good," she said. "Training begins tomorrow. Dawn. Don't be late."

She left. I blew out the candle.

In the dark, Quillfeather dropped from her roost to the windowsill and regarded me with one round eye. *"Well?"* she said in raven-speech, which needs no translation when the question is that obvious.

"She knows," I told her. "About me. About the Voice. Everything except—"

*"Except why you really said yes."*

I didn't answer. The raven clicked her beak, satisfied, and settled on my shoulder like a black epaulet.

Below us, faint as breath, something in the deepest cellar hummed a note I had never heard before. And I realized, with a clerk's cold clarity, that I recognized the tune.

It was a lullaby. My mother's lullaby.

The First Voice was humming my mother's song—and tomorrow at dawn, Odessa intended to teach me how to make it stop.
