## 7. The Cult of the Open Sky

Brannic had mapped every sewer in Vhalen's Reach before he was twenty, which was why he was the one sent to find out where the cult's preachers were sleeping.

It wasn't a glamorous assignment. Odessa had put it plainly at the morning table: "Someone's been chalk-mooning our doorways. Find out who and where, don't engage." Chalk moons — the open circle with the slash through it, the sigil that had started appearing on shutters and bridge pylons the week after the stars went out. Brannic had scraped four off the guild's outer wall alone. The fifth had been drawn inside his own map room, on the back of a chart he'd finished the night before the sky went dark.

That one bothered him more than he let on.

So: the sewers. He traveled light — crowbar, knife, a stub of charcoal, and a folded copy of his own survey of the Undercity, because if he was going to get lost anywhere, it would be down there, and he refused to be lost in a place he'd personally measured. He wore his oldest coat. Cartographers made bad heroes; they were built for crouching in drains and arguing about scale.

The chalk led him east. Fresh marks under the Tallowbridge, fresher ones near the old tannery outfall, and then a stretch where the marks stopped being scrawled and started being *painted* — careful white crescents, waist-high, evenly spaced, like a procession route. Someone with patience and paint money had moved into his sewers.

He followed the crescents to a maintenance gallery beneath the Saltmarket, a wide brick throat where three tunnels met, and heard voices ahead. Not many. A dozen, maybe. He did what any sensible man would do and turned around.

The man standing behind him in the dark said, "Evening, friend," and put a hand the size of a dinner plate on Brannic's shoulder.

* * *

They took his crowbar and his knife and, insultingly, his maps. They marched him through two junctions he'd never surveyed — which stung worse than the hands on his arms — and into a chamber that had been a cistern once and was now something closer to a chapel.

Candles everywhere. Real candles, dozens of them, burning in iron sconces along the curved walls, and above them, painted across the entire vault of the ceiling, the night sky as it used to be. Every star. Constellations he knew by their trade names — the Plowman, the Sisters, the Long Serpent that sailors steered by — rendered in silver leaf gone dull with damp, each one slashed through with black paint.

Under a sky full of murdered stars, twelve people knelt on wet stone facing a woman in grey.

She was older than Brannic expected. Cult leaders in the penny dreadfuls were always young and beautiful, all cheekbones and madness. This one had a grandmother's face and a grandmother's hands, folded neatly in her lap, and when she turned to look at him her eyes were dry and terribly patient.

"You're not one of ours," she said.

"He's a mapmaker," said the big man who'd caught him. "Had charts of the Undercity."

"Then you're useful, and I'm sorry for the rough handling." She gestured, and the hands left his shoulders. "Sit, if you like. We won't hurt you. We've had enough hurting."

Brannic didn't sit. Standing men could leave faster. "I'm just passing through."

"Nobody passes through my church." She said it without heat. "You came following the moons. Everyone who comes follows the moons. Sit or stand, but listen. It costs you nothing but time, and time is the only thing we have too much of now."

He stood. But he listened, because he was a professional, and information was the whole point.

"The sky is better empty," the woman said. "That's the first thing, and the hardest thing, so I'll say it plain. You've spent your life under those lights. They're pretty. They're comforting. They are also the bars of a cage, and we are all inside it, and the thing that built the cage is still awake underneath this city."

Murmurs from the kneeling twelve. Brannic kept his face still and thought: *the spire.* His hand itched for the charcoal stub they hadn't found in his boot.

"The old war didn't end," the woman went on. "It was *filed away*. Sealed. The founders of your precious guilds, your wards, your tidy little border lines — they didn't stop the war, they shelved it, and they paid for the shelf with stolen voices. Do you know what the wards ate? Do you know what fed them, century after century?"

Silence. One of the kneeling figures began quietly to rock.

"They fed on speakers," the woman said. "Beast-speakers. People born with the old magic in their throats, the last gift of the war's final hour. Every ward-stone in this country is soaked in what those people gave up without knowing they'd given it. And when the seal grew fat enough, it reached up and swallowed the stars too, so no one would look up and start asking questions."

"That's a story," Brannic said. His voice came out flatter than he intended.

"It's a ledger entry," the woman said. "I've read it. Have you?"

He hadn't. Nobody had. The guild's deep archive was sealed even to Odessa's inner circle, and Brannic had always assumed that was guild paranoia, the same instinct that kept bottled echoes behind triple locks. Now he filed the assumption away next to a growing cold feeling in his stomach.

"So you put out the candles," he said instead. "You dropped the wards. To free these speakers."

"To wake the world up," the woman corrected gently. "The wards fell because the thirteenth moon turned its face to us. We didn't break anything. We opened a door that was always going to open. The question — the only question — is who walks through it first." She rose, finally, and her voice changed, took on the cadence of recitation, and the twelve knelt lower.

"When the dark moon finishes her thirteenth turn, the seal dissolves, and the First Voice rises from beneath the spire. And there will be one born who can answer it — one Speaker whose blood remembers the founding, whose voice can unmake what was made. The Speaker of the Open Sky. And when the Speaker speaks, the war ends — truly ends — and the sky stays dark and honest forever, and no voice is ever stolen again."

Brannic's skin crawled, and not because of the doctrine. Doctrine was cheap; he'd heard end-of-the-world salesmen on every corner since the stars died. What crawled was the specificity. *One born whose blood remembers the founding.*

"And do you know who this Speaker is?" he asked. "Or do you just wait for a voice to fall out of the ceiling?"

Something moved through the chamber then — a ripple among the twelve, heads lifting, glances exchanged. The woman looked at him for a long moment, and her grandmother's face did something complicated.

"We know the line," she said.

"The line."

"The keepers' line. The guild's own." She stepped toward him, and the candlelight made her shadow climb the murdered constellations. "The Vellum & Thorn has kept a secret keeper for thirty years. Hidden in plain sight, doing sums. Raised by the last keeper, hidden in the ledgers, taught to be small and quiet and invisible." Her voice softened into something almost tender, which was infinitely worse. "Her son. Her name was Maren Dravenmoor, and her son walks your halls tonight, friend mapmaker, and when the thirteenth turn completes, we will ask him — politely, gently, with all the love this poor world has left — to open the door."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the rocker stopped rocking.

Brannic discovered he had stopped breathing. He made himself breathe. Made his face stay bored, bored, a bored cartographer in a wet basement listening to nonsense — while inside him something enormous and furious stood up and began pacing.

*Maren.* He knew the name. Everyone who'd worked the deep archive knew the name; it was inked inside the cover of the oldest ledger in the guild's possession, faded brown script, *property of Maren Dravenmoor, keeper,* and Paloma Dravenmoor signed his weekly tallies in a hand that was — Brannic had noticed it years ago and never let himself think about why — the same hand. The same slant. The same strange little crossbar on the D.

Paloma, who catalogued impossible things and never touched them. Paloma, who flinched when the ravens talked. Paloma, who had been placed in that guild like a coin in a hiding spot by a mother who knew exactly what was coming.

*Hidden by someone who loved him.* And found anyway.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Brannic said. "I inventory maps. I don't know anyone named Dravenmoor."

"No," the woman agreed, watching him with those dry patient eyes. "You wouldn't."

* * *

They didn't kill him. That was the interesting part. They gave him back his crowbar and his knife at the mouth of the Saltmarket tunnel and told him to go home and think about whether he wanted to be on the right side of the ending. The big man even shook his hand.

Which meant they weren't afraid of him reporting back. Which meant either they were fools — they weren't; the woman had eyes like a person who'd done arithmetic with lives — or they *wanted* word carried to the spire. A message. An opening bid. *We know where your Speaker sleeps. We're being polite. For now.*

He walked fast. He walked the long way, doubled twice through the fish-market shambles, and came up through the guild's coal cellar rather than the front door, and by the time he pushed into the kitchen his heart was going like a trip-hammer and his mind had already been working the problem for forty minutes and hated every solution it found.

Odessa was at the table over the accounts. Sef was scrubbing pots. Quillfeather sat on the window bar doing her evening preen, feathers looking, as ever, deliberately disreputable.

"Brannic." Odessa looked up, and whatever she saw in his face made her set down the pen. "Report."

He closed the kitchen door first. Then he stood there with the words lined up and ready and found he couldn't fire them, because firing them would change everything, would put a target on a clerk who sorted bottles and apologized to furniture, and some animal part of Brannic wanted to carry this secret into the ground the way the guild carried everything else.

But the thirteenth moon didn't care what he wanted.

"They have a name," he said. "For their Speaker. For the one they think opens the spire." He made himself say it steady, made it sound like data, because data was survivable. "Paloma. They know about his mother. Maren Dravenmoor. They know she hid him here. They called him—" and here his voice did crack, just slightly, protective fury rising up through the cartographer's calm like floodwater over a levee, "—they called him *ours*. Like he already belonged to them. Like they're being *patient*."

Sef's scrubbing brush stopped. Quillfeather's feathers flattened flat against her body, every one.

Odessa had gone very still in the way she went still before decisions. "How sure are you?"

"I'm sure they knew I'd run straight back and tell you. I'm sure that was the point. And I'm sure—" Brannic dragged a hand down his jaw. "I'm sure that when I denied knowing him, she smiled at me like I was confirming it. Odessa. They're not hunting him. They're waiting for him. There's a difference, and the difference is worse."

The kitchen held its silence. Somewhere overhead the dark moon would be climbing, invisible, thirteenth of its cursed kind, counting down turns nobody could see.

"Does he know?" Sef asked quietly. "About his mother?"

"No," Brannic said. "And now we know why she hid him. And now *they* know we know they know." He exhaled. "Every secret in this building just got a countdown on it."

Quillfeather launched off the window bar without a sound and was gone through the gap under the eaves — and Brannic, watching the space where she'd been, understood exactly where the raven was flying, and said nothing, because some messages were hers to deliver.

Odessa was already pulling the deep-archive key from around her neck.

"Get your maps," she said. "Every way in and out of that cistern. Tonight, before they move their congregation." She paused at the door, and for one unguarded moment the Guildmaster looked simply like a tired woman guarding a house full of people she loved. "And Brannic. When you draw them — draw fast. I don't think we have until the thirteenth turn anymore. I think they're coming early."

Above the city, where the stars should have been, the sky stayed black and honest and full of waiting.
