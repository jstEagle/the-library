## 9. The Dream in the Cage

The caged dream had been restless for three nights.

Paloma noticed it the way he noticed everything in the Vellum & Thorn—not through sight, but through sound. The dream hung in its leaded-glass cage on the vault shelf like a jar of storm-light, and it made a noise only he could hear: a low, wet thrumming, like a bird beating its wings against a window it couldn't understand. Dreams in captivity were supposed to sleep. This one was awake. This one was *angry*.

"You should have sold it months ago," he muttered to himself, running a finger along the ledger's margin where he'd recorded the acquisition two years back. *Item 447: One dream, lucid, provenance unknown. Do not open while dreaming. Do not open while grieving. Do not open at all.*

The guild had a talent for acquiring things with instructions nobody followed.

He was halfway through reconciling the quarterly figures—bottled echoes were up, captive dreams embarrassingly stagnant, and someone (Sef, almost certainly Sef) had logged three feet of ghost-rope as missing again—when Quillfeather dropped through the skylight gap like a piece of falling night and landed on his ledger.

"Hey!" He shooed her. She didn't move. Ravens rarely did anything they didn't decide to do first.

*"Company,"* she said, in that voice that lived behind his teeth, the one no one else could hear. *"Wrong company. Fast company. They smell of tallow and moon-oil."*

Paloma went still. "How many?"

*"Six. Coming down Tallow Lane. Two more on the roof across the way. They've been circling since dusk like they're afraid to land."* Her black eye fixed on him, bright as a wet stone. *"They're not afraid to land. They're waiting for something."*

For you, Paloma thought, and his hands went cold on the ledger. Since the veiled stranger and his obscene offer—the offer Odessa had refused so coldly the man's candle-flame had guttered—Paloma had been waiting for this the way you wait for a tooth to finally break. Six days of jumping at footsteps. Six days of saying nothing to anyone.

He stood, and his chair scraped loud in the empty counting room, and that was when the first window came in.

Glass has a particular sound when it breaks with intent—not the bright accident of a dropped tumbler but a flat, professional crunch. Then another, from the showroom floor below. Then light: not lamplight, not moonlight, which was all the sky offered anymore, but the ugly green-white flare of cult-fire, the kind that burned without heat because it wasn't really fire, it was just absence wearing fire's clothes.

"Odessa!" he shouted, already moving. "Brannic! We have—"

"I know." Odessa Vell came up the stairs two at a time, and Paloma would remember later how strange she looked: not armored, not armed, but in her nightclothes with a carving knife in one hand and the guild seal-ring already glowing dull gold on the other. Fifty years old, iron-haired, barefoot on her own stairs. "Back door. Sef's getting Tammo down the cellar. Brannic—"

"On the stairs." Brannic's voice floated up from below, calm as a man reading a shipping manifest, accompanied by the unmistakable scrape of a half-barrel being dragged into a barricading position. The cartographer fought the way he drew maps: slowly, thoroughly, with an assumption that geography would eventually cooperate.

Paloma should have run. Every instinct he'd spent thirty-one years sharpening said run—invisibility was a craft, and the first rule of the craft was *be elsewhere*. Instead his eyes went to the vault shelf.

To Item 447.

The dream was screaming now, a sound like wind through a drowned cave, slamming itself against the leaded glass from inside. And the glass—Paloma saw it with horrible clarity—was cracked. Had been cracked, maybe, since the wards fell six nights ago. Everything sealed in this city was coming unsealed, star by star, ward by ward, pane by pane.

"The cage," he said. "Odessa, the dream—"

"Forget the inventory."

"It's going to—"

Cult-fire arced through the broken showroom window and caught the curtain of bottled echoes on the west wall. Forty jars of other people's voices ignited at once, and the sound they made releasing was a chord—a vast, discordant, grieving chord, forty strangers screaming their last words in harmony—and the dream heard it, and answered, and the leaded glass of its cage burst outward like ice on a pond in spring.

What came out was not light. Paloma had expected light. What came out was *remembering*—a pressure, a bloom, a tide of it, rolling through the shop like floodwater through a ground-floor flat, and everywhere it touched, people stopped being where they were and became where they had worst been.

The last thing Paloma saw before it took him was Odessa's face, turning toward him, mouth forming his name.

Then the dream closed over his head, and he was eleven years old, kneeling in the reeds behind his mother's house, and the marsh-wolf was dying, and he was telling it *you are not alone, you are not alone*, in words no boy should have, and behind him he heard the footsteps of the men who had come because a neighbor's dog had told a neighbor's wife what it heard the strange boy say to animals, and his mother's hand was pressing a ledger into his arms, and her voice was saying *run, don't look back, don't ever let them hear you*—

The dream moved on. It always moved on. That was the mercy and the horror of it; it gave each of them only a moment of their own worst before flowing to the next.

But a moment was enough. A moment was everything.

* *

Paloma surfaced on the counting room floor with Brannic's knee under his shoulder and the taste of marsh water in his mouth.

Around him, the guild lay scattered like pieces after a game nobody wanted to finish. Sef was curled against the cellar hatch, both hands over her ears, whispering a name—her brother's, he realized, over and over, an anchor-line of a name. Tammo, the old paper-maker, sat propped against his own desk with tears cutting tracks through the dust on his face, staring at nothing, at something sixty years gone. Odessa was on her feet already, which figured, though she was gripping the desk hard enough that her knuckles had gone the color of bone.

And the raiders were down.

All eight of them, folded onto the showroom floor among the wreckage of the echo-curtain, weeping or laughing or simply emptied out. Cult-fires had guttered green and dead. Whatever worst things the dream had found in eight zealots, it had shown them all at once, and none of them were holding weapons anymore. One was kissing the floorboards. Another was calling for a mother who was, by the look of his face, long past answering.

"The cage," Odessa said hoarsely. "Is it—"

"Empty." Paloma's voice came out sanded raw. "It's spent. Shared dreams burn fast when there's this many minds close together. It poured itself out."

"How would you know that?"

The question landed exactly where she aimed it.

Paloma looked up at his guildmaster. Around the room, the others were surfacing too—Sef lifting her head, Tammo blinking, Brannic rising off his knee with the deliberate care of a large man deciding whether to be a wall or not. Outside, somewhere, the cultists were stumbling away into the dark, and the city was quiet in the way cities are quiet after a scream.

Because I've read every ledger in this building, he could say. Because it's in the acquisition notes. Because anyone would know.

Because I've been listening to your raven for nine years and she's been telling me your secrets and keeping mine, and right now the dream just handed all of mine to you on a plate.

"That wasn't random," Sef said suddenly. Her voice was thin but steady; she was sixteen and had survived worse than this, apparently, though nobody had known that either until tonight. "They came for something specific. The leader—the tall one—he went straight for the vault shelf. He knew where the dream was in the dark."

"They wanted the dream?" Tammo rubbed his face. "Why steal grief?"

"Not to keep," Brannic said quietly. "To use. Point it at a city gate and the guards open it themselves just to make it stop." He looked at Odessa. "They'll come back with more of them. And they'll come back for—" He stopped.

Everyone looked at Paloma.

He had spent his whole life becoming furniture. It was a genuine skill, a masterwork of a skill: the clerk at the corner desk, the man who balanced ledgers and fetched ink and was never once described in anyone's letters home. He knew precisely how to arrange his face so that eyes slid off it. He began arranging it now.

And then he stopped.

Because Odessa was still looking at him, and her face held not suspicion but recognition—the look of a woman assembling a picture from pieces she'd been finding for years and had never laid side by side until a broken dream forced her to. The wolf, dying in the reeds. The ledger pressed into a child's arms. Nine years of a clerk who flinched at nothing except birdsong, who could find any record in the archive blindfolded, who had once, memorably, calmed the carter's runaway horses by standing very still in the road and doing something with his hands that everyone had agreed afterwards not to think about too closely.

"Paloma," Odessa said. Not unkindly. Worse than unkindly: gently.

"Don't," he said.

"The wolves' tongues," she said. "Last winter. When the shipment came in and the pack-master's dogs went mad—those tongues weren't cut out, were they. You talked them down. All the way down."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above, Quillfeather shifted on the shattered skylight frame, feathers rustling, saying nothing—which for a raven was a roar.

"I don't know what you mean," Paloma said, and it came out cracked down the middle, and everyone heard the crack, and he understood with a strange floating clarity that the hiding was over, that it had ended the moment the cage broke, that the last six days of silence had just been the long echo of something already finished.

He was so tired. He was tired the way the blighted land outside the wards must be tired, holding shapes it couldn't sustain.

"My mother," he said, and then had to stop and start again. "My mother hid me here. Twenty years ago. She was—she kept your ledgers before me. I found her handwriting last week, in the oldest volumes, and I haven't—I haven't told anyone, I haven't told any of you anything, ever, because the last time people knew what I was, men came with knives, and my mother put me on a road with a book in my arms and I never saw her again."

The words fell into the room and lay there.

"In the reeds," Tammo said softly, to no one. "I saw it too. The dream showed us all the same thing, didn't it. Your bit of it."

"You talk to animals," Sef said. She said it flatly, testing whether the sentence could hold weight. Her eyes were enormous. "That's real. That's actually real, and you've been doing our books."

"Someone has to," Paloma said, absurdly, and Sef made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't been so close to a sob.

Brannic crossed the room. He was the largest thing in the guild by a comfortable margin, a man built like a border-marker, and when he stopped in front of Paloma, Paloma braced for—for what? Pity. Fear. The polite recalibration of a stranger into a specimen.

Brannic put one huge hand on top of Paloma's head, rumpled his hair like a boy's, and said, "Nine years. You could have told us nine years ago. Think of the card games we'd have won."

Paloma stared at him. "That's—you're not—that's it? That's all any of you are going to say?"

"What else is there to say?" Odessa had released the desk. She came around it and stood looking down at him with fifty years of impossible inventory in her face, and for the first time since he'd known her she looked, briefly, exactly as tired as he felt. "You've been living under my roof a ninth of a century, keeping my accounts, feeding my raven—" a pointed glance upward; Quillfeather examined the ceiling with tremendous dignity— "and you thought the part of you that talks to beasts outweighed the rest. It doesn't. It never did. Sit down. Have something to drink. Then help me figure out why the moon-burners want my dream badly enough to die for it, because I promise you, they wanted more than a weapon tonight. They wanted to see what we'd show each other."

Paloma sat down. Someone—Tammo, bless him—put a cup in his hand. The brandy in it was terrible and ancient and tasted like the guild smelled: vellum, thorn-wood, dust, home.

Nobody left. That was the thing he kept returning to, turning it over in his mind like a coin he suspected was counterfeit and couldn't find the flaw in. Nobody left. Nobody edged toward the door. Sef had dragged her chair next to his and was leaning against his shoulder as though nothing whatsoever had changed, as though the dream hadn't shown her whatever it showed her about herself while showing him the reeds and the wolf and the knife-men. Brannic was already sketching on the back of a shipping list, mapping sight-lines for a defense of a building he now knew was guarding a beast-speaker, and the map had no exits marked *for Paloma*—just positions, all of them together.

Exposure, he thought dizzily. So this is what it buys.

Above them, wings beat once against the broken skylight, and Quillfeather dropped through, landing on Paloma's shoulder in full view of the entire guild—bold, brazen, claiming him publicly the way ravens claim shiny things.

*"Told you,"* she murmured, only for him. *"Less alone than you believed. Took you long enough."*

"Hush," Paloma whispered back, and Sef turned to look at his lips moving, and smiled, and asked nothing.

Odessa raised her cup. "To the Vellum & Thorn," she said. "Still standing. Still stubborn. Still full of secrets—" her eyes swept them, one by one, and Paloma saw that she meant it as a promise, not a threat— "though perhaps fewer than yesterday."

"To the guild," they echoed, raggedly, and drank.

* *

Later—much later, when Sef had fallen asleep against the ledger pile and Tammo had shuffled off mumbling about reinforcing the cellar door, when Brannic had barricaded the showroom with the thoroughness of a man building a monument—Paloma climbed to the counting room with a lamp and sat down at his desk because his desk was where he went when the world needed to become arithmetic.

The ledgers were safe. The ledgers were always safe. He opened the current volume and found Quillfeather's earlier landing had left one grey feather pressed into the margin like a bookmark, and he did not remove it.

Below the window, the city lay dark under a sky with no stars, only the thirteenth moon climbing its slow arc, thin and wrong and patient. He thought about his mother's handwriting in the oldest ledgers. He thought about the tall cultist reaching for the dream-shelf in the dark, knowing exactly where it hung—which meant they had a source inside the guild's secrets, or access to them, and that thought sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He was still turning it when Quillfeather roused on the sill, feathers flattening, head snapping toward the south window.

*"Paloma."* Her voice had changed. All the gossip gone out of it, all the mischief, leaving something old and hard. *"Come here. Quickly. Look south."*

He joined her at the glass. Beyond the rooftops, beyond the dead wardline where the border stones stood pale as teeth, the horizon was moving.

Not clouds. Shapes. Four-legged ones, dozens of them, then hundreds, pouring out of the hills and down the old trade roads in a river of silver-grey eyes, flowing toward the city walls—wolves, deer, cattle, things with too many joints and too much shadow, the wild beasts of the hollow country fleeing something behind them that Paloma could not see and did not want to.

And over it all, rising through the night like smoke, he could hear them calling. Not to each other.

To him.

*"They're asking for the speaker,"* Quillfeather said quietly. *"They're asking for you by name."*
