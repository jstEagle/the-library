## 10. Wolves at the Wardline

*Quillfeather*

The wind changed before the wolves did.

I was riding the thermals above the eastern wall—doing what I do best, which is seeing things before anyone else does and pretending I don't care about them—when I caught it: a smell rolling off the broken country like a spilled inkwell. Wild magic. It has a taste, if you're a bird. Old and green and wrong, like a forest that grew in a single night and never learned to be patient.

And under that smell, another one. Fur. Fear. A hundred animals moving together with no plan but *away*.

I banked east and saw them coming up the old trade road: wolves first, a whole pack of them, gray backs dusted white with road ash, running in that terrible loose formation of creatures who have forgotten why they run but not how. Behind them, deer with their ears torn. Foxes carrying fox kits by the scruff. Things I didn't have names for—beasts from past the border wards, from countries where the maps just give up and draw sea monsters instead. All of them flowing toward Vellamark like water finding a crack in a wall.

Because we were the crack. The city still had walls, still had lamps, still had the last scraps of working wardlight glowing along its rim like embers someone had forgotten to stamp out. Everything else had gone dark when the stars went out. The beasts weren't coming to attack us. They were coming because we were the only lit thing left.

I wheeled back toward the guildhouse, and I'll admit—I flew fast. Not because I was scared. Because Paloma would want to know, and because there is a small, embarrassing part of me that has been bringing him news since the day he fed me dried fish off his own ration without being asked, and apparently eight years of dried fish buys a raven's loyalty forever. Birds are cheap that way. Don't tell anyone.

He was in the counting room, of course. Where else. Bent over the ledger with his lamp turned low, writing numbers in that small careful hand of his, wearing his clerk face—the one he thinks hides everything and actually hides nothing, if you know where to look. Which I do. I've been looking for eight years.

"Raven at the window," I said, tapping the glass. He flinched, then opened it, and cold air and my feathers tumbled onto his desk together.

"They're coming," I told him. "Beasts. Hundreds. Maybe more behind them. Wolves in the lead."

His pen stopped. "How far?"

"You'd be able to see them from the roof by the time you finished asking me questions about it."

He was already moving. That's the thing about Paloma Dravenmoor that nobody in this city knows—not Odessa with her sharp eyes, not Brannic with his murals, not even Sef, who notices everything except the obvious—he doesn't hesitate. When it's an animal hurting, or an animal afraid, he moves before his own fear can catch him. He's spent his whole life building walls around what he is, but every wall has a door, and the door opens outward, toward anything with fur or feathers in trouble.

I rode his shoulder down the stairs, which he pretends to hate.

The guild gathered on the roof: Odessa with her arms crossed and her jaw set like she was already negotiating with the horizon; Brannic with his spyglass; Sef pale and gripping the parapet; the others crowding behind. And beyond the wall, out on the dark plain, you could see them now—a river of eyes, hundreds of pairs, catching the city light and throwing it back.

"They'll hit the gates within the hour," Brannic said. "They won't stop. They're blind with panic. The gate garrison will loose arrows."

"The gate garrison will loose arrows," Odessa repeated flatly, "and then we will have a massacre on our doorstep, and every beast past the wards will learn that cities mean death." She looked at Paloma. Just looked. And I saw something pass between them that I filed away in the place where I keep things that might matter later—which is most places in my head, because ravens hoard information the way magpies hoard buttons.

Paloma went over the wall.

Not literally. Almost literally. He climbed down the inside of the gate tower while everyone else argued about petitions and protocols, and I went with him because somebody sensible had to. Below us, the wolf-pack had reached the moat-road, and they were circling, snapping at each other, the panic spreading through them like fire through dry grass. On the wall above, soldiers were stringing bows. I could hear the bowstrings creaking in the wind.

"Paloma," I said quietly. "Whatever you're going to do, do it small. There are three hundred people on that wall and every one of them has eyes."

"I know," he said.

He didn't do it small.

But that's his story to tell, not mine. I'm just the bird who watched him stand in the open gate with the whole city behind him and every wolf in the broken country in front of him, and thought: *there goes the most impossible thing in a guild full of impossible things, and he still thinks he's ordinary.*

Ravens remember everything. Someone has to.

* * *

*Paloma*

The wolves came up the moat-road like floodwater, and the soldiers on the wall came apart like a dam.

"Hold!" someone shouted, and nobody held. Arrows nocked. The pack's lead bitch—a huge gray scarred across one eye—threw her head back and loosed a sound that wasn't quite a howl. Grief, I'd have called it, if grief could be given teeth.

I heard her clearly. That was the problem. Over the fallen wards the wild magic ran thick as fog, and my gift, which I had spent thirty-one years muzzling, was off its leash whether I liked it or not. Every animal on that road was speaking at once, and I understood all of it.

*The sky fell.* The lead wolf's voice was smoke and old blood. *The sky fell and the ground forgot us. The den-scent is gone. The den-scent is gone.*

They didn't know where home was anymore. That was the blight leaking through the broken wards—not just magic, but unmaking. Beasts whose territories had been erased from beneath their paws, herds whose migration songs had gone silent mid-verse. They were running toward the last lit thing, and the last lit thing was about to shoot them.

"Archers stand down!" Odessa's voice cracked across the wall from behind me—she'd followed me down, of course she had—but the garrison captain was shouting back about orders, about rabies, about the horde, and the moment was slipping the way moments do.

So I did the stupid thing. I walked out the postern gate.

The night air hit me like cold water. Ahead, the pack wheeled at the movement—one man, unarmed, lamplit from behind—and the lead wolf lowered her head, hackles rising, and I heard her think, clear as a bell across a lake: *A door-thing. Meat that opens doors.*

"I'm not meat," I said.

Out loud. In words. In front of three hundred witnesses on the wall behind me.

The pack stopped.

It's hard to describe what it looks like when two hundred animals go still at once, unless you've seen it. Like the world holding its breath. The lead wolf stared at me, and I stared back, and somewhere in the middle of the staring I realized exactly how badly I had just miscalculated—because she hadn't been startled by a human voice. She'd been startled by the *other voice underneath it*, the one I hadn't meant to use, the one shaped like wolf-language wearing a man's throat.

*You speak,* she said. Her good eye fixed on me. *Small-soft thing. You speak True.*

"I—" My mouth was dry. Behind me, on the wall, I could feel the silence of three hundred people, and it was worse than any shouting. "I'm asking you to hold your pack back from the gate. No one here wants to hurt you. There's water past the moat, and the fields south of the wall haven't been touched by the blight yet. You can den there."

*Why should a door-thing help den-kind?*

"Because—" And here is where I should have lied. Should have said *because the guild commands it*, or *because mercy is policy*. Instead what came out of me, in front of everyone, was the truth, in a voice that cracked straight down the middle: "Because I know what it's like when the thing that kept you safe stops working. And I know what it's like when no one speaks your language."

The lead wolf held my gaze for a long time. Wind moved through two hundred coats of fur. Somewhere on the wall, a bowstring creaked and was, slowly, deliberately, relaxed.

*You are hiding,* she said at last. Not cruelly. The way you'd note the weather. *Hiding is a kind of den. But dens are for sleeping, small-soft thing. Not for living in.*

Then she turned, and the pack turned with her, flowing away toward the southern fields like a river changing course, and I stood alone on the moat-road with my heart slamming against my ribs and the knowledge settling over me that I had just spoken beast-speech—openly, audibly, undeniably—before the entire Vellamark garrison.

I made myself turn around.

Faces lined the wall. Soldiers, guildsmen, Sef with both hands over her mouth. Brannic, expressionless in a way that meant he was screaming internally. Odessa, arms folded, watching me with an unreadable look that I would spend the rest of the night failing to interpret.

Quillfeather dropped onto my shoulder, feather-light, and murmured into my ear, pitched so only I could hear: "Well. You wanted to stay invisible. Perhaps next time try not to negotiate a peace treaty with the entire canine nation."

"Shut up," I whispered.

"Eight years of dried fish," she said, "and *this* is the gratitude."

The walk back through the gate felt like walking through deep water. The crowd parted. Nobody spoke. I catalogued their faces the way I catalogue inventory—automatically, desperately—looking for the thing I feared most, which wasn't hatred. Hatred I knew how to survive. It was recognition. The look of people realizing that the quiet clerk who tallied their bottled echoes had never once been one of them.

I found it in maybe half the faces. And in the other half—Sef's, wide-eyed; Brannic's, steady; the stable-hand Marta's, fierce and wet-eyed—something else entirely.

Odessa dismissed the garrison with a word, then jerked her chin toward the guildhouse. "My office. Now."

The office smelled of ink and candlewax and the particular dust of ledgers older than the city. She closed the door, sat down behind her desk, and looked at me for a long, terrible moment.

"How long?" she said.

"Odessa—"

"*How long?*"

I thought about lying. I had been lying professionally for thirty-one years; I was very good at it. But the wolves' voices were still ringing in my ears, and the lead bitch's parting words had lodged under my skin like a splinter. *Dens are for sleeping. Not for living in.*

"Since I was six years old," I said. "The first thing I ever heard was the guild's cellar rats arguing about cheese."

Something flickered across her face—too fast to name. She reached into her desk drawer and drew out a ledger. Not one of ours. Older. Its spine was cracked, its cover stamped with a sigil I'd never seen: a spire beneath thirteen moons.

"Then you'll understand," Odessa Vell said quietly, "why your mother kept these."

The floor tilted. The room went very far away and very close at the same time.

"My mother," I said, "was a laundress in the Low Quarter. She died when I was nine."

"Sit down, Paloma."

I didn't sit down. I stood there holding the edge of her desk while she opened the ledger to its first page, and turned it toward me, and by the light of her lamp I read the handwriting on the first line—

—and knew it instantly, the way you know your own reflection. Slanted right. A little hook on every *a*. Handwriting I had seen a thousand times on the labels of jam jars, on birthday notes slipped under a cellar door, and never once questioned, because I had been taught not to question, because someone had built my whole invisible life brick by brick and then burned the plans.

The handwriting was my mother's.

And the entry read: *Received into keeping, one First Voice. To remain sealed until such time as the wards can bear no more. Keeper's note: my son must never know what he is, or what the guild owes him.*

Outside, far to the south, the wolves began to sing in the dark—two hundred voices, mourning and grateful in equal measure—and for the first time in my life, I couldn't hear them.

I could only hear my own heart, breaking open like a ledger falling from a great height.
