## 11. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The astronomers arrived in a rain of apologies and brass instruments.

There were three of them, sent down from the Observatory of the Nine Spires by a provost who had, apparently, run out of explanations for the sky. They stood dripping in the guild's front hall like drowned scholars, clutching telescopes wrapped in oilcloth, and Odessa Vell looked at them the way she looked at anyone who entered her guild without an appointment.

"You'll wipe your feet," she said. "Then you'll tell me something I can't already guess."

The eldest astronomer—a stooped woman with ink-stained fingers and the particular exhaustion of someone whose life's work had stopped working—unwrapped her telescope as if it were a body she was preparing for burial.

"Guildmaster," she said, "we've been tracking the dark moon since the night the stars went out. It has no name in any catalogue. It wasn't there before." She swallowed. "It completes its thirteenth turn in nineteen days."

I felt the words land in my chest before my mind caught up with them. Nineteen days. I thought of Brannic's mural, the thirteen moons with the last one scratched out by some long-dead hand, and how I'd called it superstition to his face. The building hummed under my feet, that low uneasy note it had carried for weeks now, and for once I didn't try to convince myself it was settling foundations.

"Thirteen turns," Odessa said quietly. "And then?"

"And then the moon reaches apogee directly above this city," the astronomer said. "Directly above—" she checked a scrap of paper, though I suspected she knew it by heart "—the spire."

* * *

They set up their instruments on the guild's roof, which meant the guild's roof became a place where people gathered, which meant I could no longer take my evening ledger-work up there in peace. I resented this more than I wanted to admit. The roof had been mine—not officially, but in the way that a quiet corner belongs to whoever keeps it. Now it belonged to brass rings and star charts and Sef asking the astronomers questions they couldn't answer, and Brannic sketching the dark moon from every angle like he could draw it out of the sky.

It hung low over the city each night, a coin of absolute black ringed by a faint grey corona, eating the light around it. On clear nights you could see the place where stars should have been and weren't—a wound in the sky shaped like everything missing.

On the fourth night after the astronomers arrived, I climbed up anyway, ledgers under one arm, because competence is the only prayer I know. Quillfeather came with me, riding my shoulder, and if any of the astronomers found it strange that a guild clerk brought a raven to work, none of them said so.

"She's getting faster," Quillfeather said, meaning the dark moon. Ravens measure time in food and weather, so when one talks about astronomy, it means something.

"Faster than what?"

"Than the stories said. The old crows used to sing about a black turn that came at the end of things. Thirteenth turn. When it closes, the seal closes—or opens. Depends which crow you believe." She preened my collar flat with her beak, proprietary. "Your mother believed it closed."

I went still. "You knew her."

"I told you I did." Quillfeather's voice gentled into that rasp she used when she was being careful with me, which was rare enough that I noticed it every time. "She sat on this same roof, little clerk. Same ledgers. Same hunched shoulders. She used to say the thirteenth turn was a door, and doors don't care whether you're ready—they only care whether they're locked."

Below us, through the skylight, I could see lamplight moving in the guild hall. Odessa pacing. Brannic's broad silhouette at his map table. Sef's smaller shadow darting between them. My family, arranged in warm geometry beneath my feet, and I sat on the cold roof counting days I didn't have.

Nineteen, then fifteen. The numbers fell away like pages torn from a ledger no one wanted balanced.

* * *

Odessa came for me on the twelfth day before the turn.

Not summoned—I was never summoned; I was simply always at my desk, and she simply always knew where to find me—but she came anyway, which was different, and the difference frightened me. She carried two cups of chicory tea and set one on my ledger without asking permission to touch the sacred paperwork, which was also different.

"Walk with me," she said.

We walked the length of the guild's cellar corridor, past the vaults, past the shelf where the caged dream had lived before it escaped, all the way to the door at the end that I had catalogued eleven years ago as *Door, iron, purpose unclear, do not open (per founder's instruction)* and had never once touched. It was humming now. Audibly. Anyone could hear it.

"The rites are old," Odessa said. "Older than the guild. The founder didn't invent them; she transcribed them from the First Voice itself, back when it still consented to be sealed." She pressed her palm flat against the iron door, and the humming changed pitch, almost leaning into her hand. "There are nine of them. A sealing requires a speaker at each stage—voice, breath, blood, name, memory. You can't fake it. The Voice knows the difference between someone reciting and someone giving."

"Giving what, exactly?" I asked, though the word *memory* was already sitting in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Everything," Odessa said. "That's the price. That's why the founder did it herself, and why she never spoke of it after. A sealing doesn't take your life. It takes your self. Whoever performs the ninth rite walks out of the spire with nothing left of who walked in." She turned to look at me, and her eyes were steady in the way of a woman who had decided not to flinch on someone else's behalf. "I'm telling you this because you deserve to know what I'm training you for. Not because I'm asking you to do it. There may still be another way."

"There isn't another way," I said. "You wouldn't be showing me the door otherwise."

"No," she admitted. "There probably isn't."

We stood there together in the humming dark, two keepers of other people's secrets, and I understood that she had known what I was far longer than the veiled stranger in the market ever had. Maybe since the day she hired me. Maybe since the day my mother left me on her doorstep with a ledger in my arms.

"When do we start?" I asked.

"Tomorrow," said Odessa Vell. "At dawn. Bring your voice."

* * *

The first rite took place in the guild's map room, which Odessa had cleared of maps.

In their place she'd drawn a circle on the floorboards in chalk and ash and something silver that moved when I wasn't looking directly at it. Brannic stood watch at the door—he insisted, and Odessa let him, and I understood that this was less about guarding against intruders and more about giving him something to do with his fear. Sef had been sent to bed. Quillfeather refused to be sent anywhere.

"Rite of Naming," Odessa said. "The Voice must know who addresses it. Speak your name into the circle—and mean it. Every part of it."

"My name is Paloma Dravenmoor," I said.

The circle drank the words and gave back silence.

"That's the name you were given," Odessa said. "Speak the name you made."

I looked at the floor. At the chalk. At Brannic's shadow in the doorway, solid as furniture, as certain.

"Paloma of the Vellum & Thorn," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and the circle flared silver, and somewhere below us the iron door rang like a struck bell.

Odessa nodded slowly. "Good. It heard you." She began sweeping the circle clean with a broom of bundled feathers—raven feathers, I realized, and decided not to ask. "Six more rites. Each one costs more than the last. By the eighth you'll understand exactly what the ninth takes, and you'll have to choose it anyway, awake and unforced. That's the rule. The Voice won't accept a sacrifice that doesn't know itself."

"What happens if I fail? If I stop halfway?"

"Then the seal fails, the Voice wakes unheld, and the war remembers itself." She met my eyes. "So we don't stop halfway."

* * *

The rites ate the days.

Breath: I exhaled my whole lungful of air into a clay vessel until I saw spots, and the vessel glowed faintly blue, and Odessa sealed it with wax and told me I'd get it back only if the sealing failed. Blood: three drops on a mirror of polished obsidian, and the mirror showed me not my face but my mother's, young, laughing at something off-frame, and I had to walk away from it twice before I could finish. Name: done. Memory—the fifth rite—was the one that broke me open.

For the fifth rite, Odessa had me lie in the center of the circle while she burned a twist of dried mothwing and rosemary, and she said, *Give it one true memory. Only one. The rest comes later or not at all.*

I gave it the night of the escaped dream.

I hadn't known I was choosing that one until it left me—until I lay gasping on the floorboards and reached for the memory of my family standing around me in the flooded shoplight, having seen the worst of me, having stayed anyway—and found the shape of it smooth and blank as a river stone. I remembered that it happened. I remembered that I had been happy. The happiness itself was gone, scooped out, and something in the circle held it now, keeping it safe, keeping it *for* me, the way a pawnshop holds a coat.

"It comes back if the sealing fails," Odessa said softly, kneeling beside me. "Every rite is reversible except the ninth. Remember that. Hold onto it."

But lying there with a hole in my chest shaped like belonging, I already knew what I would choose. I think she knew too. That was the terrible courtesy of her: she never once asked me to promise. She just taught me the rites perfectly, completely, holding nothing back, so that whatever I chose, I would choose it knowing.

* * *

Seven days before the turn, I couldn't sleep.

I went up to the roof, because the astronomers had finally gone home and the roof was mine again, and the dark moon hung over the city like an eye closing. Below me the guild slept. Brannic snored in a rhythm I could have charted. Sef talked in her sleep about water. Quillfeather roosted in the rafters above the hall, one foot tucked up, dreaming raven dreams.

*I will not remember this,* I thought. *In seven days I will stand at this window and feel nothing. I will see these rooftops and these sleeping people and they will mean no more to me than strangers in a market. And they will remember me. That's the trade. They carry me; I go light.*

Quillfeather landed beside me without a sound.

"You're doing it wrong," she said.

"Doing what?"

"Grieving in advance. It wastes the days you've got left." She hopped closer, head tilted, one black eye catching the moonless dark. "The old crows had a word for a speaker who walks into the ninth rite. *Vess.* It means both *vessel* and *empty*. Your mother argued with the elders about it for a whole winter. She said a person isn't a jar—you can't pour them out and leave the shape standing. She said if the price was truly paid, then someone owed the payer, and debts of honor outlive seals."

"She sounds like she'd have hated this plan," I said.

"She did hate this plan. She built the guild to make sure you'd never have to—" Quillfeather stopped. Shifted her weight. "There's a thing I haven't told you. About the night she left."

The wind moved along the roofline. Below us, Brannic snored on, faithful as a tide.

"She didn't just hide you here," Quillfeather said. "She paid something too. Small, early payments, the way you pay a debt down. Every year on the thirteenth of Frostmonth, she gave the seal one memory of you—one hour, one afternoon, one ordinary moment—so that when the final turn came, the Voice would already know the taste of what it was asking for. She spent nineteen years making sure that if it ever came to this, the price would be *familiar*. Negotiated. Not a theft."

I stared at her. "Are you saying she pre-paid my ransom?"

"I'm saying she loved you in installments," Quillfeather said, "and the Voice kept every receipt."

I put my face in my hands. Somewhere behind my ribs, in the hollow where the fifth rite had taken its tithe, something shifted—an ache with a direction, pointing down, toward the iron door, toward the Voice that remembered wars and kept accounts and had, perhaps, been waiting nineteen years not for a sacrifice but for a settlement.

"Five days," I said.

"Four," said Quillfeather. "The astronomers rounded up. Crows don't."

* * *

On the last night, Odessa gathered everyone in the hall without telling them why.

She lit every lamp in the building—all of them, extravagantly, wastefully, the guild burning bright as a signal fire—and set out food nobody cooked and wine nobody usually allowed, and she stood at the head of the table and said, "Tomorrow we do what the guild was founded for. Some of you will hold the doors. Some of you will hold each other. Paloma holds the hardest thing, and I won't insult him by describing it in front of everyone."

Brannic pushed back his chair and stood, and for a moment I thought he was going to argue—big, gentle Brannic, who had chosen family over safety once already and would choose it again with his fists if the world made him. But instead he crossed the room, took my shoulders in hands like dock-cleats, and pressed his forehead to mine.

"Nineteen days ago I showed you a scratched-out moon," he said. "You told me it was superstition." His voice shook. "Tell me again. I want to hear you be wrong one more time."

"It was superstition," I said, and he laughed, wet-eyed, and the hall laughed with him, and Sef was crying openly into a cup of wine she was too young for, and Quillfeather dropped from the rafters onto my shoulder and said nothing at all, which from her was a eulogy.

Later—much later, when they'd all gone up to bed and the lamps had burned down to a row of patient flames—I sat alone at the table with the final ledger open in front of me.

I didn't write an entry. I just wrote my name at the top of a fresh page, the way the founder must have done, the way my mother must have done, and underneath it I wrote: *Whoever balances this book next, be kind to them. They were loved. Check the margins.*

Because that's where my mother had hidden hers. That's where I'd hidden mine. Every impossible thing I'd ever catalogued, I'd annotated in the margins with small private notes—*the bottled echo in row three laughs at night; the caged dream likes lullabies; Brannic hums when he draws coastlines*—a secret second inventory of everything I cared about, invisible unless you knew to look.

If the ninth rite took me clean, the margins would remain. Someone would find them eventually. Someone would read them aloud.

The iron door, three floors down, hummed like a held breath.

Dawn was coming, and with it the descent, and with it the Voice that waited at the bottom of everything, keeping its receipts. I blew out the lamps one by one, and the dark that came in behind them was almost gentle, and on my shoulder Quillfeather slept on, trusting me with her dreams for a few hours more.

Four days, the crows said.

I intended to use every hour.
