## 12. A Stolen Star

Brannic had mapped every inch of the Vellum & Thorn twice over—once in ink for the guild records, once in his head for the day someone came to take it. So when he walked the vault stairs that morning and felt the wrongness before he saw it, he trusted the feeling the way other men trusted their eyes.

The vault sat three floors beneath the counting room, carved from the same granite as the spire's foundations. It was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Odessa called it *old quiet*, the silence of things that had been shut away so long they'd forgotten how to make noise. Brannic had always found it restful. A place with no opinions.

This morning the old quiet was broken.

The door stood open. Not forced—the lock was pristine, the hinges unwounded—but open, and swinging slightly on its iron arm like something embarrassed to have been caught.

Brannic did not go in. He stood on the stair and counted backward from ten, because Tammas the quartermaster had taught him that a man who rushed into a wrongness became part of it. At four, he smelled it: scorched air, sharp and sweet, like lightning bottled too long.

At zero, he went in anyway.

The vault's inventory lived in forty-one niches cut into the walls, each sealed behind leaded glass. Bottled echoes, rows of them, murmuring to themselves in the dark. The caged dream, its brass prison humming. The reliquary of teeth from beasts that had never been named. And in the central alcove, raised on a plinth of black stone under its own ward-glass dome—

Empty.

The star was gone.

* * *

Odessa arrived in ninety seconds, which meant she'd felt it too, wherever she'd been. She came down the stairs still buttoning her coat, hair unbound, and stopped beside Brannic without a word. They looked at the empty plinth together for a long moment.

"Tell me," she said.

"No breach marks. No scorch on the glass except inside the dome." He pointed. "Whoever took it opened the case from within the ward-circle. They knew the sequence."

"The sequence is written nowhere."

"It's sung," Brannic said. "You taught it to three people. Me, Tammas, and Sef."

He watched her face do what her face always did—not much, but enough. A tightening at the jaw. The flicker of a woman running sums she didn't want to finish.

"Sef is sixteen," Odessa said.

"She is. And she has the voice of an angel and the pockets of a pickpocket, and last month she asked me how the vault wards were keyed." He hadn't thought much of it then. Apprentices asked questions. That was what apprentices were for. "I answered her. I drew her the diagram."

"You what?"

"I teach her cartography, Odessa. Wards are cartography. Lines and thresholds and where the doors are." He heard his own voice going flat and defensive and hated it. "She asked good questions."

Odessa crossed to the plinth. She didn't touch it. Her hand hovered above the black stone the way a physician's hovers over a wound. "There's residue," she said quietly. "Cult-work. Moon-salts. This wasn't a theft of opportunity. It was a delivery."

Outside the vault, somewhere far above, the guild was waking. Brannic could hear the floorboards talking—Paloma's even clerk-step crossing the counting room, Quillfeather's talons on the stair rail, Sef's quick light feet moving toward the kitchen. Moving fast. Moving early.

He'd known her feet for two years. He'd never noticed they moved differently when she was guilty.

"We don't say anything yet," Odessa said. "We watch. We—"

"No."

She looked at him.

"I've spent my whole life drawing maps of places I can't protect," Brannic said. "Borders move. Cities fall. I accepted that a long time ago—it's why I draw, instead of fight. But this house—" He stopped, started again. "I chose these people. That was the one border I got to draw myself. And if there's a hole in it, I want to look at the hole. Not wait politely while it gets bigger."

For a moment he thought she'd pull rank. Guildmaster outranked cartographer; that was the shape of the world. But Odessa Vell had built the Vellum & Thorn out of strays, and strays only followed you if you let them be dogs sometimes.

"All right," she said. "Watch. Say nothing until we know."

They both already knew. That was the cruelty of it. Knowing and saying were different countries, and neither of them wanted to cross.

* * *

Brannic spent the day being a cartographer, which was to say: being invisible. He corrected charts at the long table in the map room with the window at his back, and he watched the guild through the reflection in the glass, the way he'd learned to watch borders—by pretending to look elsewhere.

Tammas inventorying tinned peaches, complaining about the price of tin. Paloma at his ledger, pen scratching, raven on his shoulder like a second shadow. Odessa upstairs, writing letters no one would send. And Sef.

Sef, scrubbing the auction floor. Sef, hauling crates. Sef laughing at Tammas's jokes, feeding Quillfeather crusts she probably shouldn't have been giving a bird that gossiped. Sef doing everything exactly right, exactly normally, and checking the front window every eleven minutes. Brannic counted. He couldn't help counting. Every eleven minutes, her eyes went to the street, to the corner of Gildersleeve Lane where the lamplight didn't reach, and then away again, quick as a card trick.

At noon she left the guild. Said she was fetching thread for the display cases. Brannic gave her sixty seconds, then took his coat and followed.

Gildersleeve Lane ran along the guild's east wall, narrow as an apology. Sef walked it fast, not looking back, which told him she was either innocent or very good—and nobody who checks a window eleven minutes apart is very good. She turned south into the Ropewalks, past the tar-shops, past the shuttered chandler's, and ducked into the mouth of an alley between a dye-house and a derelict granary.

Brannic didn't follow into the alley. He climbed instead, up the dye-house fire stair, onto the low roof, and lay flat behind a rotted parapet with a view straight down. Old habits. In the border wars, the men who survived were the men who got high first.

Below, a figure waited in the alley's throat. Hooded, veiled, gray-cloaked—the cut of the moon-cult, unmistakable now that Brannic had seen it once and couldn't stop seeing it everywhere. The veiled stranger from the auction night. He recognized the posture more than the face: patient, coiled, a person used to standing in shadows waiting for the world to come to them.

Sef handed over something wrapped in oilcloth. Small. Even from the roof, Brannic's stomach dropped through the city's foundations, because he knew the size and weight of that bundle. He'd helped crate it. He'd drawn the diagram of its case.

Not the star. Something else—a ledger page, maybe, or a rubbing of the ward-sequence. Smaller sin, same disease.

The veiled figure passed back a phial. Glass, stoppered, glowing faintly blue-white like a captured piece of sky. Sef held it in both hands the way you hold water in a drought, or a heart in your palms, and said something too quiet for the roof to catch. Then she turned and ran for home, clutching the light against her chest like a child.

Brannic stayed on the roof long after the alley emptied. Below him the city went about its business—carts, cries, a dog barking itself hoarse at nothing. Somewhere in it all, a sick boy was drinking poisoned water, and a sixteen-year-old girl was selling pieces of the only family she had to buy him one clean month.

He thought about maps again. About the little conventions of the trade—how you marked a ford that drowned travelers, a pass that avalanched. You didn't leave those off the map out of mercy. Mercy was telling people the truth about dangerous places.

Sef was a dangerous place. And he loved her anyway, which was the worst map he'd ever had to read.

* * *

He didn't tell Odessa immediately. That was the thing he would turn over later, in the dark, examining it like a flawed chart: the hours he sat on what he'd seen, and why. Because telling made it real? Because some part of him hoped the phial was medicine and the bundle was lies? Because he remembered being fifteen in a burned-out border town, trading a soldier his father's compass for bread, and knowing even then that hunger made liars of everyone and the lie wasn't really yours?

He told himself all of it. None of it held.

In the end it was Quillfeather who forced the issue. The raven landed on his windowsill at dusk, head tilted, one eye fixed on him with the flat gold appraisal she reserved for people hiding something.

"You smell like rooftops," she said. Ravens always sounded like they were filing a complaint.

"I smell like everything. You live over a shop that sells bottled echoes."

"Mm." She hopped closer. "The mice say the girl met a ghost in the Ropewalks. The mice say she gave the ghost paper and got back moonlight."

"The mice talk too much."

"The mice keep this city alive, fat man. Show some respect." She preened once, sharply. "Paloma knows."

Brannic went cold. "How?"

"He listens. It's his whole personality." Quillfeather's feathers rustled in something like a shrug. "He hasn't told Odessa. He's been sitting in the ledger room since supper looking like a man deciding which debt to default on first. I thought you should compare notes. Misery likes a ledger partner."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the one who follows people." She fixed him with the gold eye. "And because whatever you decide to do, you'll do it with a map. The rest of them just feel things and break stuff. Someone in this madhouse has to draw lines."

She flew off before he could answer, which was her way of ending conversations she'd already won.

* * *

He found Paloma in the ledger room with a single lamp and the guild's oldest registry open in front of him—not the working ledgers, the historical ones, the ones with vellum gone brown as tea. Brannic had never seen Paloma read them. Paloma read everything, but quietly, the way he did everything, filing himself into the guild's corners so thoroughly you forgot he was furniture.

"You followed her," Paloma said, without looking up.

"You listened."

"I listened." He turned the page. His hands weren't quite steady. Brannic noted it the way he noted everything: elevation, bearing, distance. "The dye-house roof. The alley. The phial."

"You knew all that and let me climb around in the dark?"

"You needed to see it yourself. Some truths don't transplant well." Paloma finally looked up, and in the lamplight his face had the terrible gentleness of a man who has already done his grieving and is waiting for you to do yours. "Her brother's name is Tobin. He's nine. The ward-water gave him the graying sickness—his blood's forgetting how to be blood. The cult promised her a cure. One page of the vault sequence per dose."

Brannic sat down across from him, hard, the chair complaining. "How do you know all this?"

"Quillfeather talks to the rats. The rats talk to the pigeons. The pigeons sit on Sef's windowsill." He closed the old registry. "I've known for six days."

Six days. Brannic stared at him. Six days the guild had been bleeding through a hole the size of a girl's love for her brother, and their own clerk had sat on it, keeping Sef's secret the way he kept everything—invisible, filed, safe.

"You should have told Odessa."

"Yes," Paloma said simply. "I should have. I'm going to, tonight. But I wanted—" He stopped. Restarted, which he almost never did; Paloma's sentences usually arrived finished. "I wanted someone else to know before it became an accusation. So it could be a problem instead of a betrayal. There's a difference. Accusations end things. Problems get solved."

"That's a very clerk way to look at treason."

"It's the only way I know how to look at anything."

And there it was, Brannic thought—the thing underneath the thing. Paloma wasn't protecting Sef. Or not only. He was protecting the idea of the guild, the fiction they'd all agreed to live inside: that chosen family was safer than blood, that the Vellum & Thorn was a border that couldn't be breached because everyone inside it had already been breached once and knew better. If Sef could sell them out for a phial, the fiction died, and they were just another band of strays waiting for the world to finish them.

Brannic understood. He'd drawn enough borders to know that every line on a map was a prayer somebody had written down because they couldn't bear to say it aloud.

"Then we solve it," Brannic said. "Together. Before Odessa finds the empty niche and starts solving it with rope."

"There isn't going to be rope." Paloma almost smiled. Almost. "But there is going to be a conversation, and we're all going to hate it, and then we're going to get the star back."

"How? We don't even know where the cult keeps it."

Paloma pulled the old registry back toward him and turned it to a page Brannic hadn't seen, marked in faded brown ink in a hand small and precise. "I think we do. And I think the reason the cult wanted our star specifically is written right here, in my mother's handwriting, next to an entry that says the vault's captive star came from the Hollow Country—and that it remembers the way home."

* * *

They told Odessa together, that night, in the vault itself, standing around the empty plinth like mourners at a grave with no body. Odessa listened without interrupting, which was worse than shouting, and when Paloma finished she was silent long enough that the bottled echoes in their niches began to murmur, nervous, picking up the mood of the room.

"Sef stole from this guild," she said finally. "Sold us to the people who put out the sky. To buy medicine." She looked at the plinth. Looked at Brannic. "Say something, cartographer. You've had all day to draft it."

Brannic had. He'd drafted six versions on the walk down, and thrown all of them away, because maps taught you one thing above all others: terrain doesn't care what you wish it were.

"She's a traitor," he said. "And she's ours. Both things are true, and I'm tired of pretending truth comes one at a time. We bring her in. We hear her whole. And then we point her at the cult and let her burn them down for what they made her do—because that girl will do anything for her brother, and right now the cult owns that. I want it back."

Odessa studied him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—approval, maybe, or grief wearing approval's coat.

Above them, faint through three floors of stone, the guild bell rang the midnight hour. And beneath it, so soft that only Brannic—who had spent a lifetime listening to buildings—caught it, came another sound: the front door, easing shut. Small feet on the stairs, going up, quick and light, carrying a phial of stolen moonlight home to a dying boy.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow we burn the whole rotten sky down.

But tonight, the thirteenth moon turned one turn closer, and somewhere in the dark the stolen star was remembering its way home.
