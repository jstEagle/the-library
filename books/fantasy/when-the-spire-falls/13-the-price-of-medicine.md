## 13. The Price of Medicine

Sef counted the coins again. It didn't help. Eleven silver marks, three coppers, and a guild scrip worth two more. The cult's price had been one stolen star.

She put the money back in the tin under her cot and pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw constellations that weren't there anymore. Nobody's were. That was the whole problem.

Her brother lay in the narrow bed across the room, breathing in that wet, careful way he'd learned in the last week, like each breath was a coin he couldn't afford to spend twice. Tam. Fourteen years old and already learning to ration his own lungs.

"Sef." His voice came out thin as paper. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing."

"The face. The one where you're counting something."

She laughed, because if she didn't she was going to cry, and Tam hated when people cried at him. He said it made him feel like a funeral with a pulse. "Go back to sleep."

"I've been asleep all day. I'm so bored I memorized the water stains on the ceiling. There are forty-one. One of them looks like Odessa Vell if she were melting."

"Everything looks like Odessa Vell if it's disapproving enough."

He smiled. Then the smile cost him, and he turned his face into the pillow and coughed, long and deep, and Sef sat very still on the edge of her cot and did not go to him because going to him would mean touching him would mean feeling how hot he still was, five days after the fever should have broken.

The water did this. Everyone knew it now, though nobody official would say it. When the wards fell, something in the old aquifers woke up angry—wild magic leaking down through the broken borders and settling in the wells like sediment you couldn't see. Half of Lowmarket was sick. The physicians' guild called it *wardtaint* and charged a fortune for tonics that only slowed it. The moon-cult called it *the world's honest blood* and gave theirs away.

That was the trap. That had always been the trap. They didn't want her soul; they wanted her too tired to defend it.

The star was in the third vault. She knew the rotation of the locks, knew which nights Odessa took the long ledger home with her, knew the gap between the night watch's rounds because she'd helped Brannic set the schedule himself. She'd told them about the vault weeks ago, before Tam got sick, back when it was just information, just words, just a woman in a gray hood saying *we only want what was never theirs to keep* and meaning it kindly, somehow, which was the worst part.

Now they wanted her to open the door herself.

* * *

The Vellum & Thorn smelled different since the stars went out. Not worse—Sef had spent two years learning its smells, ink and beeswax and the iron tang of bottled echoes, and those were all still there—but thinner, like the building was holding its breath. Even the raven had gone quiet on her perch above the front counter, feathers sleeked down, watching everything with one eye and then the other, as if she could catch the dark sky out by surprise.

Sef catalogued returns at the front desk and tried not to think about vaults. This lasted four minutes.

"You're doing the thing," said Paloma Dravenmoor, from behind her.

She jumped hard enough to knock over an inkwell. He caught it—of course he caught it, Paloma caught everything, silently, without ever seeming to move—and set it upright, blotting the spill with a rag before it reached the ledger.

"What thing?" she said.

"The face where you're counting something."

"That's Tam's line."

"It's a good line. I stole it from him weeks ago." Paloma slid the repaired ledger back toward her. His hands were inkstained to the second knuckle, always. He had the kind of face you forgot while looking at it, which Sef had once thought was a flaw and now suspected was armor. "You've checked the clock eleven times in the last hour. You don't have an appointment. So either you're late for something that doesn't exist, or you're early for something that does."

The shop was empty. Evening light came through the front windows the color of weak tea—no stars outside yet, but the sun still worked, mostly. Quillfeather shuffled along her perch and made a sound that Sef would have sworn, in any sane month, was a sigh.

"I didn't tell them anything important," Sef said. It came out fast and ugly, the way confessions do when you've been rehearsing them in the wrong order. "I told them the star existed. Where it was kept. I haven't—I haven't opened anything. But they know I can. And my brother is dying, Paloma, and their physician actually *cures* people, I've seen it, I watched a woman stand up out of a wardtaint fever like she'd been paid to do it, and they said—" Her voice cracked. She hated that. She was the steady one. She was the apprentice who never spilled ink. "They said the price went up."

Paloma didn't say anything for a moment. He had a way of not saying things that felt louder than most people's speeches.

"They asked you to steal," he said finally. "Or to let them in."

"To take the star out through the garden gate Thursday night. They'd handle the rest." She wrapped her arms around herself. "And here's the part you're supposed to hate me for: I was going to do it. Last night I stood in the yard with the gate key in my hand for half an hour. The only reason I didn't is that Tam started coughing blood and I couldn't leave him long enough to walk twenty feet."

"Then you didn't betray us. You stood in a yard holding a key."

"Don't be generous at me. I can't afford it."

Something moved behind Paloma's eyes—not anger, not pity. Calculation. Sef had spent two years assuming the guild's quiet clerk was exactly what he appeared: a man made of ledgers. But lately, since the sky broke, she'd started noticing the other things. How the rats in the storeroom never touched the grain near him. How stray cats followed him down Tallow Street at a respectful distance, like an honor guard that had been sworn to secrecy. How, the night the stars vanished, every animal within a mile had screamed, and Paloma had gone white as bone and said, quietly, "they're mourning," before anyone else had even understood the sound was language.

Quillfeather dropped off her perch and landed on the counter between them, close enough that Sef could hear her breathing.

"Tell me about the cure," Paloma said.

* * *

The cult's clinic operated out of a defunct bathhouse on Candlewick Row, and it wasn't hidden at all, which was somehow more frightening than if it had been. People lined up outside it in the gray morning—dockworkers, laundresses, a whole family from Tanner's Close, all of them carrying the same look Sef saw in her own mirror: the specific exhaustion of people who had stopped believing the city would save them.

Sef walked past it twice with Paloma beside her, both of them anonymous in work cloaks, and described what she'd seen inside. The gray-hooded woman—*Sister Merren*, she said her name was—who mixed the draughts. The copper kettle that never seemed to boil but never went cold. The patients drinking it and improving within hours.

"And you believe it works," Paloma said.

"I watched it work. On six people."

"Mm." He stopped in the shadow of a shuttered chandler's shop, out of sightline of the queue. "Do you know what's in it?"

"Merren says springwater blessed under the dark moon. Says the wards were poisoning the water for centuries and the stars were the poison. Says the taint is just the truth arriving."

"Do you believe her?"

Sef thought about Tam's ceiling stains. Forty-one. "I believe my brother drinks it Thursday or he doesn't see winter."

Paloma nodded slowly. Then he crouched down—right there, in the gutter mud of Candlewick Row, in full view of anyone who cared to look—and did something with his hands. A small motion, fingers curled, almost like he was offering something.

A rat came out from under the chandler's door.

It crossed the street in broad daylight, trotted up to Paloma's shoe, and sat back on its haunches and looked at him with the frank, unbothered attention of a colleague waiting for instructions.

Sef's mouth went dry. "Paloma."

"Hm."

"There's a rat."

"There are nine rats," he said mildly. "Two under your left foot. Don't move."

She looked down. There were, in fact, two rats sitting patiently by her boot, and more emerging from the storm drain at the corner, whiskered heads rising out of the dark like a slow tide. Every hair on her body stood up. Every story her grandmother had ever told about plague-years and gnawed cradles stood up with it.

But the rats weren't swarming. They were *waiting*. Arranged, almost. The nearest one groomed its paw and then fixed Paloma with its black eyes again, attentive as a clerk awaiting dictation.

Paloma made a low sound—not quite speech, not quite whistle, something in between, a syllable that lived in the throat of some older language. The lead rat answered with three sharp chirps and a chittering run of sounds that meant nothing to Sef and clearly everything to him. His brow furrowed. He made the sound again, differently, and got a different answer, and then a rapid-fire exchange that reminded her horribly of two accountants arguing over a discrepancy.

"What are they saying?" she whispered.

"Water," Paloma said absently. "There's a lot of arguing about water. Rats are territorial about sources—it's the whole architecture of their politics." He listened, head tilted. "They're telling me the wells in Lowmarket went bad first because the taint follows the old ley-channels, and the channels all bend toward the spire, like roots bending toward—" He stopped. Started again. "The bathhouse. The cult's clinic. Their well is clean. Genuinely clean. The rats drink from it."

"So Merren was right? The water really—"

"Their well is fed by a sealed cistern under Candlewick Hill, dug before the wards ever went up. Pre-ward water. Untouched by any of it." He glanced up at her, and for a second the ledger-clerk mask slipped entirely, and underneath it Sef saw someone young and frightened and ferociously kind. "The cure isn't magic, Sef. Or it's only the magic of digging a well in the right place three hundred years ago. The draught might be real medicine. But the water is the cure, and the water was free the whole time, and they're charging desperate people their loyalty for it."

The rats had begun to argue among themselves—a scurrying, squeaking debate. Paloma held up one finger and they subsided.

"Ask them," Sef said. Her voice shook. "Ask them if there are others. Other clean sources."

"I was getting to that." He crouched lower, and the sound he made this time was longer, a running ripple of clicks and soft notes, and the answer came back from a dozen throats at once, a chorus in the gutters, and Paloma closed his eyes to listen the way other men listened to music.

When he opened them, he was smiling. It transformed his forgettable face into something she'd remember for the rest of her life.

"There are eleven," he said. "Clean springs, sealed cisterns, one brewery cellar whose owner will be insufferable about it. The rats know every one. And they'll show us."

* * *

Tam drank his first cup of true clean water that evening, hauled up from a forgotten cistern beneath the ruins of the old bellfoundry, in buckets, guided through rat-warrens and collapsed cellars by a boy-shaped shadow of whiskers who seemed to regard the entire enterprise as beneath his dignity but above his standards.

Sef boiled it anyway. Old habits. Then she sat on the edge of Tam's bed and made him drink until he complained, which was the best sound she'd heard in a month.

"It tastes like nothing," he croaked.

"It tastes like nothing *bad*. There's a difference."

By midnight his fever broke—not dramatically, not like the cult's theatrical recoveries, just a slow tide going out, his breathing easing breath by breath until somewhere around the second bell he fell into the heavy, graceless sleep of a boy who was going to live. Sef sat on the floor beside the bed and cried into her knees, silently, because he hated funerals with a pulse.

She was still there at dawn when the knock came. Three soft raps. Guild rhythm—Brannic's pattern, except Brannic never knocked soft.

It was Odessa Vell. The guildmaster stood in the stairwell with her arms folded and her face arranged into its usual expression of a woman perpetually disappointed by reality's paperwork. Behind her, half-hidden, stood Paloma, looking like a man who had argued all night and lost gracefully.

"Apprentice Sef," Odessa said. "We need to discuss the third vault."

Here it was, then. Here was the end of everything—the expulsion, maybe the watch, maybe worse, and the terrible thing was that standing in the doorway of her brother's sickroom, hearing him breathe easy behind her, she found she couldn't summon a single regret.

"I know what I did," Sef said. "I'll take whatever—"

"You'll take a map." Odessa thrust a rolled parchment at her. "Eleven clean water sources in this city, verified last night by means I'm choosing not to ask about. The guild is going to start hauling water to Lowmarket by cart, publicly, tomorrow, and we need someone who knows the streets and the faces and can organize distribution before the cult spins it into a miracle of the dark moon. That's you. Congratulations on your promotion. It comes with no raise, because we're a guild, not a charity."

Sef stared at the map. Then at Paloma. He shrugged, faintly, apologetically, ink-stained hands in his pockets.

"You're not turning me in."

"Turning you in accomplishes nothing. The cult keeps its recruit, my guild loses its best apprentice, and your brother buries you instead of the reverse." Odessa's voice was flat as a ledger line, but her eyes weren't. "Mercy isn't the absence of judgment, girl. It's judgment aimed at the actual problem. The actual problem is that half this city is dying of thirst next to a locked cistern. Fix that, and we'll call the rest even."

Behind her, Paloma mouthed something. Sef read his lips: *forty-one stains.*

She laughed, wet and cracked, and took the map.

It wasn't until Odessa turned to go that the guildmaster paused, hand on the rail, and spoke without looking back.

"One more thing. The rats' report last night—they mentioned something odd. A twelfth source, far out past the wardline, in the hollow country. A spring the beasts guard and won't drink from themselves. They say it remembers names." Odessa's jaw tightened. "And this morning, Brannic's bed is empty and his survey kit is gone. He's gone after it alone."

Outside the window, over a city with no stars, the first pale light of dawn found the dark shape of the spire—and beneath it, on the eastern road, a lone cartographer walking toward a country that ate memories.

Sef looked at the map in her hands. Then at Paloma, who had already begun, quietly, packing ink.
