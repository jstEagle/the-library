## 14. Into the Hollow Country

The wardline announced itself before we reached it.

I'd imagined a wall, maybe—something with architecture, something you could point at. Instead it was a seam in the air, faint as a scratch on glass, running across the moor like a sentence someone had started and abandoned. On our side, the grass moved when the wind moved. On the other side, the grass held still, as if it were listening.

Quillfeather landed on my pack and dug her claws in deep enough to hurt.

*The birds won't go past it,* she said. *The birds say there's nothing past it to go to.*

"That's not how places work," I muttered.

*The birds disagree.* She ruffled, feathers up, looking less like a guild member and more like an offended cushion. *Which is why the birds are staying here, and you're walking into nothing. I'm coming anyway. Someone has to remember you.*

Odessa Vell stood at the seam with her hands folded behind her back, studying it the way she studied a contract before refusing it. Behind her, Brannic adjusted the straps of an pack that could have carried a pony, and Sef checked her knife twice, then a third time, which was one more than usual.

"The beasts said two days' walk," Odessa said without turning around. "Then the Moonrise Court."

"Three," I corrected. "The wolves were specific. Three days, and don't drink from standing water, and whatever you do—" I heard the wolf's voice again in my head, all gravel and old grief, *don't answer anyone who calls your name wrong.* "Don't answer anyone who calls your name wrong."

"Why?" Sef asked.

"Because out there," I said, "your name is the first thing to go."

Nobody laughed. That was the trouble with a family made of guild misfits: they took impossible information the way other people took weather reports, nodded, packed accordingly. Brannic hefted his enormous pack. Odessa stepped through the seam.

The world on the other side didn't change. That was the horror of it. Same moor, same gray sky, same wind—except the wind had no sound now, and neither did our footsteps. The fallen wards had fed on voices for a hundred years, and this land had been inside their mouth the longest. It came out of the wards' collapse hollowed, quiet as a room after the argument ends.

We walked into the Hollow Country, and by the second mile, I'd already forgotten what joke Brannic had told at breakfast.

---

The guides found us at dusk.

They came down off a ridge in single file—a lean grey she-wolf with a torn ear, followed by two yearlings and, absurdly, a badger who kept pace like a sergeant. The she-wolf looked at me for a long moment, nostrils working, and then bowed her head in the shallow way wolves do when they mean it.

*You are the one who spoke for the pack at the wardline,* she said. Her voice was winter. *The river-otter told the marsh-birds, and the marsh-birds told everyone. You owe nothing and are owed much. We will walk you to the Court's shadow.*

"I'm grateful," I said aloud, because manners mattered even when only one person present understood the answer.

"What did she say?" Brannic asked. He'd taken to asking constantly, hungry for it, the way a man who can't read asks about every signpost.

"She says we walk at night. The blight moves slower in the cold."

Sef pulled her coat tighter. "And the names? When do we start losing them?"

The she-wolf's ears flicked. *She asks when the forgetting starts,* I translated, and the wolf made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could bite.

*It already started. Ask her what her mother's face looks like.*

I asked. Sef stared at me, then at the darkening moor, and I watched her reach back and come up empty—not empty, worse, *blurred*, the way a ledger entry smudges when a thumb drags across wet ink. Her face did something complicated and then went very still.

"It's fine," she said. "It's—it's foggy. That's normal. Everyone's mother gets foggy."

"Everyone's mother doesn't get foggy," Brannic said gently. "That's why we wrote everything down." He patted his pack, where he'd spent three days before departure copying every memory anyone would give him: birthdays, quarrels, the exact recipe for Odessa's terrible stew, in his blocky cartographer's hand. He'd written down mine too. I hadn't asked him to. He'd done it while I slept, which was either sweet or terrifying, and with Brannic it was usually both.

We walked at night, as instructed. The badger scouted ahead and reported in short gruff bursts: *road washed out; stone bridge holds; something wearing a traveler's coat sitting by the fire we left last week, still sitting, don't look at its face.*

I didn't ask what it meant by *still sitting.* In the Hollow Country I was learning not to ask.

---

Here is what the blight does, as best I understood it from the wolves and the crows that perched at the edge of it, refusing to cross:

Memory is a thing the land carries. Every road remembers its walkers; every hearth remembers its families; every name spoken over a cradle roots itself into the floorboards. The wards fed on voice for a century, and when they fell, they didn't die cleanly—they drained. They drank the remembering out of the ground between the border and the city, and what's left is a country where the past runs downhill like water through cracked clay.

Travelers forget their names as they walk, because a name needs somewhere to live, and out here nothing will hold it. The land won't carry it. So it sits on top of you, light as a hat, and the first good gust takes it.

The wolves survive because wolves never needed the land to remember for them. Their memory lives in blood and scent and the long story told nose-to-ear down generations. The blight slides off them like rain off oil.

Humans are the ones who drown.

We passed three shrines in the first night—all recent, all wrong. Little cairns of stones with scraps of paper under them, and on the paper, in shaking hands: *my name is*. Some just began the sentence. One had written a name, then crossed it out, then written it again, then crossed it out again, as if the writer couldn't trust their own record. The she-wolf wouldn't let us stop at any of them.

*The dead who forget they're dead stay by their shrines,* she said. *The living who forget they're living wander. Neither kind likes visitors.*

I catalogued them anyway. Old habit. Somewhere in my head, an inventory line opened itself: *Found: three shrines, contents grief, condition deteriorating.* I hated myself a little for it and did it regardless, because the ledger was the only part of me the blight couldn't drink. Facts written in my own hand stayed written. I'd taken to reciting entries under my breath as we walked—*Paloma Dravenmoor, clerk of the Vellum & Thorn, born in the lower city, keeper of ledgers, friend of a raven named Quillfeather who is currently riding my pack and complaining about the mud*—and Quillfeather would mutter *that last part's true, keep going* whenever I faltered.

---

We camped at dawn in the shell of a coaching inn. It was the emptiest building I've ever stood in, and I've stood in the guild vaults. Not ruined—intact, mostly. Tables. A cold hearth. A child's wooden horse by the stairs. But the inn had been drunk dry of itself. Stand in the doorway of any house and you feel the family in it; stand in this doorway and you felt a hole shaped exactly like everyone who had ever lived here. The wood remembered nothing. The blight had eaten down to the grain.

Sef built a fire. Brannic unpacked his memory-pack and read us our own names like a bedtime story, and Odessa sat apart, sharpening a blade she never used, watching the moor.

I went out to speak with the wolves.

The she-wolf lay at the perimeter with her torn ear cocked toward the horizon. Her yearlings slept in a heap. Up close her smell was juniper and old blood, and beneath both, something like smoke.

*Ask me,* she said, before I'd said anything. *You've been circling the question since the ridge.*

"How do you know the Court?" I asked. "You guide people there but you hate it. Your whole pack hates it. I can smell the hatred."

She was quiet a long time. Out on the moor, the sun came up colorless, the way everything here was colorless, drained toward gray.

*Before the wards fell, there was a valley east of here where my kind spoke to the shepherds,* she said finally. *Not the way you speak. Older. Both sides knew each other's young by name. When the wards went up—the founders' wards, the great sealing—they needed voices to feed them. Beast-voices. They took the valley's voices first, because we were closest, and because no one asked questions about animals gone silent.*

Her yearling stirred and pressed against her flank. She licked its head absently, an entire history in one gesture.

*My grandmother's grandmother remembered the valley. She taught us the names so we would not be the kind of creature the land forgets. That is all a wolf is, clerk: a name carried a very long way.* She looked at me with yellow eyes. *Your guild's founder built the cage. The cult wants to open it. And you—you smell like the lock.*

I didn't answer. There wasn't an honest answer that didn't end with me confessing things I'd sworn to confess only at the spire's foot or not at all.

*Sleep, Speaker,* she said, mercifully letting it lie. *Tomorrow we cross the white field. That is where the land takes the most.*

---

The white field was salt. Nothing grows there; nothing decays. It stretches flat to the horizon like a page no one has written on yet, and crossing it is the closest I ever want to come to being unwritten.

Halfway across, Brannic stopped walking.

He didn't say anything. He just stood there, this enormous gentle man with a pack full of everyone's recorded lives, staring at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who'd handed him luggage. Then he pulled out his ledger, opened it with fingers that had forgotten how to be careful, and read, aloud, in a voice gone thin: *"Brannic Ostrew, cartographer, third-best cook in the guild, afraid of deep water, loves deep water, once fought a customs officer for insulting Odessa's hat—"*

"Fought a *what?*" Sef said, and then her face changed as the sentence landed, as the memory reattached itself, and she laughed the ragged laugh of someone pulled back from a ledge.

That was the rhythm of the crossing. Two hundred paces of blankness, then a scrap of paper, a recited fact, a name spoken by a friend—and the self would catch, like flint, like a spark finding tinder. We towed each other across the salt the way climbers rope together on ice.

I lost my name four times on that field.

The fourth time was the worst, because I didn't notice. I just kept walking, unbothered, a body with a pack, and it was Quillfeather who shrieked in my ear—aloud, actual sound, which she almost never used—*"PALOMA. Say your name. SAY IT."* And I looked at her, this black bird I loved with a love whose object I couldn't locate, and honestly tried, and produced nothing.

So she said it instead. Over and over, in her rusted-hinge voice, hopping along the salt beside me: *Paloma, Paloma, Paloma, you tedious beautiful man, Paloma*—until the word snagged on something in my chest and I heard myself say it back, and then I was kneeling on the salt with a raven pressed against my cheek and the others crowded around me, shouting my name like it was the answer to a riddle, which I suppose it was.

Afterward Odessa walked beside me and didn't pretend it hadn't happened.

"When we get the star back," she said quietly, "when this is over—you'll tell me what you're carrying. What you've been carrying since the buyer in the veil."

It wasn't a question. Guildmasters don't ask questions; they set terms.

"Yes," I said. Because after the white field, lying to these people had become a physical difficulty, like breathing through cloth. I just hadn't decided *when* yes would happen. There was a difference between confession and timing.

She nodded once, satisfied, and moved up the line. Quillfeather rode my shoulder the rest of the day, and I let her, and neither of us mentioned it.

---

We saw the Moonrise Court at dusk on the third day, and my first thought—not brave, not strategic, purely human—was *that's where the stars went.*

The cult had raised their stronghold in a natural bowl of hills, and the whole bowl glowed. Not with starlight—with absence. They'd hung thousands of mirrors, polished discs of tin and silvered glass, on frames and poles and strung wires, and every mirror faced upward, reflecting a sky that had nothing left to reflect. The effect was a dome of dead light, a ceiling of small gray moons, and beneath it the stronghold itself: a ring of white walls, a central tower, and banners so dark they looked cut out of the sky.

At the heart of it, visible even at this distance, something pale turned slowly above the tower. The stolen star, in its cage of glass and iron, spinning like a specimen in a jar.

*There are dogs there,* the she-wolf said, flat with disgust. *Cult dogs. They have forgotten they were wolves. They guard the gates and do not smell anything anymore.*

"Can you get us closer unseen?"

*To the walls, yes. Inside, no. Inside is yours.* She turned her head and regarded me with that yellow patience. *One more thing, Speaker. The ones in white—they sing at moonset. All of them, the same song, every night. My scouts say it is a mourning song. Do you know what they mourn?*

"No," I said.

*Neither do they,* she said. *That's what frightens me. They forgot, and they kept singing anyway.*

We made camp in the last fold of hills before the bowl, close enough to count watch-fires. Brannic spread his maps. Odessa laid out the plan in her contract-negotiating voice—gates, walls, rotations, the tower—and I half-listened, because most of the plan's success rested on the part she couldn't plan: the beasts who owed me favors, the otters and marsh-birds and one very large and ill-tempered boar who had reasons of his own to hate the people who shot arrows at talking things.

I was checking the ink on my notes when the wind shifted, coming up out of the bowl, carrying sound for once—the Hollow Country rarely gave sound freely, but it gave this.

Singing. Hundreds of voices in unison, rising with the rising thirteenth moon, thin and clear and terribly sad. A mourning song for something the singers couldn't name.

And underneath the song, woven through it, a thread I recognized in my bones before my mind caught up—a voice like winter, like smoke, like every wolf I'd ever refused to hear:

*speaker. speaker. come to the gate, speaker. we know your name even if you lose it. come and see who sings your mother's name.*
