## 15. The Speaker Before Me

The blight ate names the way rust ate iron—slowly, then all at once.

We had been walking for two days when I caught myself mouthing my own name to keep it. *Paloma Dravenmoor.* Two words, like a ledger entry. I repeated them under my breath every hundred paces, and by the third day I had added a third line: *keeper of the Vellum & Thorn,* because a name alone felt too thin a rope to hold onto.

Brannic had it worse. The cartographer who could draw any coastline from memory had woken on the second morning unable to remember his mother's face, and by noon he'd taken to writing *BRANNIC* on his own forearm in ink, renewing it whenever sweat smeared it. Sef, walking beside him with her hood up and her guilt worn as openly as her exhaustion, had started saying his name aloud every time she thought of it—a tic, an offering. "Brannic." "Pass me the waterskin, Brannic." "You're doing that thing with your jaw, Brannic."

He never asked why she said it so often. I think he knew. I think that was her penance, and his mercy, and neither of them spoke of it.

Quillfeather rode my shoulder in silence, which for Quillfeather was its own kind of catastrophe. A raven who narrates everything—who has opinions on my posture, my diet, my taste in boots—had gone quiet somewhere past the second ward-marker. When I finally asked her about it, she said only: *"Names are heavier out here. I'm carrying yours for you. Don't make me carry conversation too."*

So we walked in silence through a country that had forgotten itself.

The Hollow Country didn't look blighted, at first glance. That was the horror of it. The hills rolled green. Streams ran clear over pale stones. There were birds—I heard them, faint threads of speech in the animal tongue, thin and strange—but their talk was wrong. Not the rich gossip of city sparrows or the long formal courtesies of wolves, but fragments. Loops. A thrush repeating the same three syllables over and over like a prayer with no god behind it: *home, home, home.*

"They've forgotten what home means," I told Odessa quietly. "They remember there was something. They can't find the shape of it anymore."

Odessa walked ahead of us all, her guildmaster's coat swapped for road-plain wool, her cane tapping a rhythm on the packed earth. She hadn't slept since the wardline. I don't think she trusted sleep in a place where dreams leaked. "Keep talking," she said now, without turning. "All of you. To each other, to yourselves. Tell stories. Boring ones especially. The blight takes what you don't use."

So we told boring stories. Brannic described, in exhaustive detail, the proper way to stretch vellum. Sef recited inventory codes from memory until they blurred together into nonsense-syllables and made Quillfeather laugh for the first time in a day. I talked about bookkeeping. About ledgers. About the particular satisfaction of a balanced column.

I did not talk about my mother. Some things I kept off the page entirely.

* * *

We found the cult's looting camp at dusk on the third day—or rather, the crows found it, and told me, because even here some part of the old speech still ran under the ground like water under stone. *Two-legs gone, two-legs gone, shiny-things left behind, come and see, come and see.*

The camp sat in the crook of a dry riverbed: a scatter of cold fire-rings, broken crates, straw packing strewn like entrails. The cult had moved on toward the Moonrise Court—they were always moving toward it now, the thirteenth turn tightening like a knot—and they had left behind what they couldn't carry or didn't value.

What they didn't value filled me with a rage so quiet and complete it frightened me.

Bottled echoes lay scattered in the dirt. Dozens of them. Little glass teardrops on silver chains, each one holding a scrap of someone: a lullaby, an argument, a death-rattle, a wedding vow. In the guild we catalogued these reverently. We charged obscene prices for them, yes—Odessa would be the first to admit the Vellum & Thorn traded in grief as readily as anyone—but we *handled* them. We wrapped them in wool. We wrote their contents in the ledger in careful script so that no echo would ever become anonymous.

The cult had used three of them as sling-stones. One was shattered against a rock, its echo bleeding out into the air on an endless loop—a woman's voice, saying *I forgive you, I forgive you, I forgive you* to no one, wearing thinner each repetition until the words were just breath shaped like forgiveness.

Sef knelt and began gathering them without being told. Brannic stood watch, hand on his knife, muttering his own name like a rosary. And I walked the camp's edge, listening, because that is what I do, what I have always done: I listen for the thing everyone else has missed.

That's how I heard her.

Not with my ears. With the other listening—the one I'd spent thirty years pretending I didn't possess. It came from a crate half-buried in straw, and it came as a hum, low and familiar, a melody I knew before I knew I knew it. The building-hum of the Vellum & Thorn had a cousin, and its cousin was singing in this broken crate in the middle of nowhere.

I dug with my hands. Splinters, straw, dirt under my nails. And there, wrapped in oilcloth with a care that contradicted everything else the cult had touched, was a single bottled echo. Larger than standard. Older. The glass was dark, almost black, and etched around its base in a spidery antique hand:

*ECHO No. 001 — DO NOT OPEN — DO NOT SELL — DO NOT FORGET HER*

My hands stopped working. My whole body stopped working. Because I knew that handwriting. I had spent the last week learning it, line by line, in the oldest ledgers of the Vellum & Thorn—the entries in the margins, the corrections, the little marginalia jokes in a keeper's hand from twenty-five years ago.

It was my mother's handwriting.

And it was *mine*. Or near enough that seeing them side by side would settle any question of whose blood had learned to hold a pen.

"Paloma?" Odessa's voice, behind me. Careful. She'd seen my face. "What is it?"

"She's in here," I said. My voice came out wrong, scraped. "Odessa. My mother is in this bottle."

The silence behind me stretched. Then Odessa crossed the camp, took the bottle from my hands with the reverence she otherwise reserved for the stolen star, and turned it in the last grey light. "Echo number one," she read. "The first thing the guild ever captured. I wondered—" She stopped herself. Looked at me. And for once in our long, guarded acquaintance, the guildmaster of the Vellum & Thorn chose not to withhold. "Your mother sealed it herself. It's been in the vault since before I was guildmaster. The night the wards fell, the vault—" she exhaled through her teeth, "—the vault shook. Things moved. I inventoried it after the auction. This was missing. I told myself I'd imagined it."

"The cult took it."

"And abandoned it here." Her jaw tightened. "Because they opened it, and it wasn't what they wanted. It isn't a weapon. It's just—" she weighed the bottle gently, "—a woman."

"Give it to me," I said. "Please."

She gave it to me.

* * *

I waited until camp was made. Until Brannic had the watch and Sef had finally, guiltily slept, and Quillfeather sat on my knee in the lee of a fallen wall, and the small fire threw more shadow than light. Then I held the bottle in both hands and worked the stopper the way the old ledgers taught: a quarter-turn counterclockwise, then stillness, then an invitation. Echoes open to invitation. That's what makes them echoes and not prisons.

The glass warmed. Darkened further. Then the darkness gathered itself, rose out of the bottle like smoke deciding to be a person, and stood before me in the firelight.

She looked the way I remembered and nothing like I remembered. Small. Dark-haired, with my same stubborn hairline. Hands that had held pens and held me. She was translucent at the edges, and where her eyes should have held light they held instead a kind of patient attention, the look of someone who has been waiting a very long time and has made peace with the waiting.

"Mama," I said. Thirty years old, guild clerk, keeper of impossible things, and the word came out of me like I was six.

"Hello, little bird," said the ghost of Wren Dravenmoor. "You got tall. I hate that I missed it."

"You—you knew. You put yourself in the bottle. You hid it in the vault. Why?"

"Because dead women can't keep secrets, but echoes can." She smiled, and it cracked something in my chest. "Sit down, Paloma. You'll want to sit for this. Your legs always went first when the truth arrived."

I sat. Quillfeather pressed against my thigh, warm and solid, and did not speak, because she understood this was a moment that belonged to the living and the nearly-living alike.

"You found the ledgers," my mother said. "I know you did; I wrote them so you would. Every entry, every margin note—it was a trail of breadcrumbs, and you followed it exactly as stubbornly as I knew you would." She knelt across the fire from me, translucent knees on translucent earth. "So you know the guild was built to guard the First Voice. You know the stars were hidden to feed the wards, and the wards were fed on speaker-voices, and that your gift—my gift—isn't a blessing. It's a scar. The war's last magic needed translators between the species it had wounded, and it made us, and then the founders sealed the war away and let the world believe we were myth."

"I know," I whispered.

"You know the *what*." She leaned forward. "But you don't know the *why*, and the why is the only thing that matters now. Listen to me, little bird. Everyone—your guildmaster, the moon-cult, the founders' ghosts, maybe even you—all of you believe the First Voice is dangerous because it *starts* wars. That it's a weapon. That if it wakes, it will command armies of beasts, shatter kingdoms, burn the map." She shook her head slowly. "That's wrong. The First Voice doesn't start wars. It *remembers* them."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a bird repeated its broken loop: *home, home, home.*

"It was the world's first memory," she went on. "Before writing, before ledgers, before stone. When the old races went to war, the Voice heard everything—every order given, every scream, every act of mercy in the mud. It remembers all of it, perfectly, forever. And here is the terrible secret, Paloma: whoever holds the Voice chooses which memories the world keeps. Not remembers. *Keeps.* The founders didn't seal it to hide a weapon. They sealed it because they couldn't agree on which version of the war should survive. Each faction wanted its own grief enshrined. So they buried the argument along with the Voice, and the forgetting spread outward from the seal for a thousand years, and that forgetting—that slow leak—is what hollowed this country." She gestured at the dark hills, the looping birds, the name-eating air. "This is what a world looks like when nobody chooses what to remember. It doesn't stay neutral. It rots."

My mouth was dry. "And the moon-cult?"

"Wants the world to forget the war entirely. Wipe it clean. Which sounds like mercy until you understand that beast-speakers *are* the war's memory, living and walking. Erase the memory completely and you erase us—not kill us, worse: unmake us, retroactively, until no one ever loved us." Her voice gentled unbearably. "Until no one ever loved *you*, little bird. That's what their victory means. Not your death. Your never-having-been."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the night.

"But the founders' way is no better," she continued. "Reseal the Voice, keep the argument buried, let the forgetting spread another thousand years until the Hollow Country is everywhere and every star stays dark and every speaker's voice feeds a ward until there are no speakers left either. That's the other extinction, slower and politer." She reached out—her hand passed through mine like cool water—and her eyes held mine. "There's a third door, Paloma. There always was. The Voice doesn't demand a seal or a surrender. It demands a *choice*. Someone must stand before it and decide which memory the world keeps. The beginning of the war, or the ending of it. The founders couldn't agree. The cult won't choose anything but erasure. But you—" and here she smiled, that smile, the one I had carried through thirty years of pretending to be ordinary, "—you were raised in a house of ledgers. You know better than anyone alive that a record is never neutral. Every entry is a choice about what matters. You've been training for this your whole life without knowing it."

"I'm a clerk," I said stupidly.

"You are a *keeper*," she said. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

I looked at her—at the smoke-edge of her, at the bottle in my lap, at thirty years of her handwriting teaching me mine. "Why didn't you tell me? Before you died. Why hide all of it?"

"Because hiding you was the only way to keep you," she said simply. "And because I hoped you'd never need to know. And because—" the ghost-voice frayed, and she pressed on, "—because I was a coward about one thing only, Paloma, and it was this: I couldn't bear to tell you that someday you might stand where I stood and have to choose, and that whatever you chose would cost. I wanted you to have one life without a ledger entry pending against it. Forgive me that. It was the only lie I ever told you."

"It wasn't a lie," I heard myself say. "It was a mercy. I know the difference. I keep books."

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and bright, and for one moment she was not a ghost or a recording or a relic but simply my mother, laughing at her son across a fire. Then the laugh faded, and the edges of her dimmed with it, and I understood with a lurch that echoes are finite. Bottled grief runs out.

"Mama—"

"Listen fast, then. The Moonrise Court sits on the spire's roots; you know this. The ritual begins on the thirteenth turn's eve, not the day itself—they'll want the Voice awake but unclaimed when the moon completes. You'll have one window, one moment, and it will feel exactly like standing at a crossroads with no signs. Remember this: the Voice will offer you anything. It is not lying. It never lies. It simply does not understand that some prices are too high, because no one ever taught it love—only memory." She was fading faster now, smoke thinning to mist. "And Paloma—one more thing. When you speak to it, speak as a keeper, not a soldier. Soldiers pick sides. Keepers write the true record. Write it *true*."

"What was your choice?" I burst out. "When you stood before it—did you—"

"I never got my chance," she said, and there was no bitterness in it, only a vast tired tenderness. "I died owing the world one decision. Consider this my repayment, and consider it *yours*: not inherited, little bird. Offered. That's the whole secret of inheritance—it's never a debt. It's only ever an offer. You may refuse it. But I don't think you will. I think you'll do what you've always done with impossible things."

"What's that?"

"You'll catalogue it," she said, smiling, dissolving, becoming evening mist above a fire in a forgotten country, "and then you'll take care of it."

And she was gone, and the bottle in my hands was only glass, warm as a cheek, empty as a finished page.

* * *

I sat by the fire a long time. Quillfeather eventually hopped up onto my shoulder, and for once she didn't editorialize. She just leaned her warm weight against my jaw, the way she did on bad nights at the guild, and let the silence be a shared thing.

Finally she said, softly: *"You're crying."*

"Bookkeepers don't cry over closed accounts," I said.

*"No,"* she agreed. *"But sons do."*

In the morning we broke camp and walked north, toward the Moonrise Court, toward the thirteenth turn. Brannic had rewritten his name on his arm twice. Sef walked closer to him than she needed to. Odessa studied me across the miles with the look of a woman counting costs she hadn't yet disclosed.

None of them asked what my mother's echo had said. But at midday, when the blight's silence pressed hardest, I heard Sef say "Brannic," and Brannic answer "here," and I understood that all of us were doing the same arithmetic: what we would pay, what we would keep, what we would refuse to lose.

I mouthed my own name to the wind. *Paloma Dravenmoor. Keeper.*

Then, quieter, testing it like new ice: *chooser.*

Ahead, on the horizon, the Moonrise Court had begun to glow—white and hungry, a crown of light around the darkest moon in history—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the ground itself had started to hum in ten thousand animal tongues at once.

The First Voice was waking.
