## 16. Storming the Moonrise Court

### Brannic

The Moonrise Court sat in a bowl of dead hills like a cupped hand holding something it shouldn't. Brannic had mapped worse places—had mapped the Hollow Country itself, back when the names of travelers still survived the walk—but he had never mapped a place that *hated* being seen. The cult's stronghold was built from pale stone that drank the starlight, which meant it drank nothing now, because there was no starlight left to drink. It glowed faintly anyway, from within, lit by the stolen star they'd pulled out of the Vellum & Thorn's vaults.

"That's our star," Sef had said when they'd first crested the ridge, and her voice had gone flat and hard in a way Brannic had never heard from her before. "That's ours. I carried the inventory key for two years. I know its weight by the sound the case makes."

He'd believed her. That was the strange part—the part he kept turning over in his mind like a coin he suspected was counterfeit and kept finding true. She had betrayed them. She had fed the cult their routes, their schedules, the location of the star. And she had done it to buy medicine for a dying boy, and Paloma had found the boy clean water instead of turning her in, and now here she was, crouched in the gorse beside him, pointing out the guard rotations she herself had helped design.

Family, Odessa liked to say, is not a thing you're given. It's a thing you keep deciding.

Brannic decided again, there in the gorse, with his knees aching and his old surveying knife gone dull from cutting brush.

"Ritual starts at moonrise," Sef whispered. "Which is meaningless now, since there's no moon anyone can see, but they keep the schedule anyway. Fanatics love a schedule. The courtyard fills—all of them, maybe two hundred—and the leader stands on the western stair. The star goes into a basin at the top of the spire-stump. After that, I don't know. I was never allowed to see past the third door."

"The third door," Quillfeather said from Brannic's shoulder, tasting the word. The raven's feathers were oiled and dark and she looked, as always, like she knew something funny that everyone else was too slow to get.

"Past the third door," Sef said, "is where they took me when they wanted to remind me what happens to people who stop being useful."

Nobody said anything to that. Down in the bowl, the pale stone pulsed once, twice, like a heart remembering how.

Brannic unrolled his last map against a flat rock and weighted the corners with pebbles. He'd drawn it from memory, from Sef's descriptions, from three days of watching, and it was honest work: one bridge over the dry moat, one gatehouse, one courtyard shaped like a wound. "We come in during the ritual," he said. "Everyone's eyes on the star. That's when the bridge is weakest guarded and strongest needed."

"Weakest guarded," Quillfeather repeated. "Strongest needed. You always pick the sentence where you're standing in the middle, cartographer."

"I'm a cartographer," Brannic said. "I don't stand in the middle. I stand exactly where the lines cross."

* * *

The ritual began without a moon, which somehow made it worse. Two hundred white-robed figures filled the courtyard, and every one of them lifted their face to an empty sky, mouths open, waiting for light that wasn't coming. Their voices rose in a low drone that Brannic felt in his teeth. At the top of the broken spire, a woman he couldn't see clearly held the stolen star above a stone basin, and the whole court glowed with its borrowed light, and the hills around them threw long shadows toward the horizon as if even the land was trying to look away.

They crossed the moat-bridge at a run, all six of them—Odessa in front with a guild-cutlass she hadn't unsheathed in twenty years, Paloma pale and silent with his coat pockets full of bottled echoes, Sef leading because she alone knew the doors, Brannic and the twins Wren and Cobb flanking, Quillfeather overhead as a black scrap against blacker sky.

The gate guards were expecting no one. Fanatics, Brannic thought grimly, love a schedule, and the schedule did not include a bookseller's war party coming up the service ramp with a dream-cage on a handcart as a diversion. The caged dream—recaptured, re-corked, and rigged by Sef to burst open on impact—went through the gatehouse window and detonated into a bloom of other people's worst memories. Three guards dropped their weapons and started screaming about fires they'd set as children. One simply sat down in the doorway and wept.

"Third door," Sef shouted, and kicked it open, and it was not locked, because she had been the one who locked it, once, and she had never given them the new key.

Then everything went wrong in the ordinary way things go wrong: an alarm bell nobody had planned for, a squad of robed swordsmen who turned out to be stationed *inside*, and the courtyard of two hundred faces turning, all at once, away from the sky and toward the intruders.

"Bridge," Odessa said calmly, in the voice she used for closing auctions. "Hold the bridge."

Brannic turned around. Behind them, across the dry moat, came the second wave—the ones who'd been posted outside, forty strong, running for the only crossing. If they got across, the guild would be crushed between two walls of believers.

He planted himself at the bridge's narrow throat and drew his surveying knife, and behind him he heard Odessa say, not unkindly, "Cartographer. Exactly where the lines cross."

"Yes," Brannic said, and held.

* * *

He would remember it later in pieces, the way he remembered all his maps: not as continuous time but as accurate fragments.

The first man came at him with a sickle and a hymn in his mouth, and Brannic broke his wrist with the flat of the blade and put him over the edge into the moat. The second and third came together, and Brannic learned quickly that faith makes fighters stupid—they didn't flank him, they *charged*, because charging was what the doctrine said, and doctrine was easier than thinking. He let the bridge's narrowness do his fighting for him. One at a time. Always one at a time. A bridge is just a map with weather.

Something opened a gash along his forearm. He didn't look at it. Looking at wounds was how you decided you'd lost.

"Cartographer!" Quillfeather's voice from somewhere above, ragged. "You're bleeding on my flight path!"

"Redraw around it," Brannic gasped, and laughed, and hurt two more of them.

They came in waves and the waves broke on him and he counted them the way he counted contour lines, methodically, because counting kept the fear outside where it belonged. Twelve. Fifteen. His arms went heavy as felled timber. Somewhere behind him he could hear Odessa's cutlass singing, hear Sef shouting directions through the doors she'd once locked, hear the low drone of the ritual stuttering, faltering—

And then, from the top of the broken spire, a sound like a struck bell made of night, and every robed figure below stopped moving at once, and Brannic, swaying over the moat with his knife red to the hilt and his breath coming in torn pieces, understood that whatever was happening at the top of that tower had just changed shape entirely.

The last wave hesitated at the far end of the bridge.

"Hold," Brannic told them, in a voice that was mostly blood and gravel, and to his own astonishment, they did.

### Sef

Sef had spent two years learning the Moonrise Court's architecture from the inside, and in two years she had never once been allowed through the fourth door. Now she went through it with Odessa Vell's cutlass at her shoulder and her own guilt burning in her chest like swallowed coal, and the fourth door was not locked either, because the cult had never imagined that a girl who sold her family for medicine would sell them back again.

Good, she thought. Underestimate me. Everyone does. It's the only gift I've ever had that works reliably.

The stairwell beyond spiraled up inside the broken spire, past landings where the cult stored the things they'd looted from the fallen wards: bottled echoes racked like wine, dreams in cages stacked to the ceiling, a shelf of small brass instruments whose purpose Sef didn't want to guess at. Her hands remembered the inventory codes. Her hands had written half of them. There was a special horror in robbing your own ledger.

"They'll have moved the star to the basin already," she said. "The ritual has a rhythm. Star into the basin, then the chanting changes key, then—" she faltered, because the next part was the part that had made her stop sleeping. "Then they feed it. Voices. They've been collecting beast-speakers' voices for years. Bottled. The star eats them, and the eating keeps the seal from—"

She stopped, because Paloma had gone very still on the stair behind her, and his face had a look she recognized from mirrors: the look of someone doing arithmetic with terrible numbers.

"How many bottles," he said quietly.

"At least thirty," Sef said. "Maybe fifty. I inventoried them myself."

Above them, the chanting changed key.

* * *

The chamber at the top of the spire was open to the dead sky, a crown of pale stone with the basin at its center, and the cult's leader stood over it with the stolen star suspended in her hands. Up close, Sef could see that the woman was older than she'd seemed from below—older than grief, almost—and that the light of the star turned her face silver and terrible.

Around the basin, on iron stands, hung the bottles. Dozens of them. Each one glowed faintly with trapped sound, and Sef—who had catalogued them without ever listening, because listening was forbidden—suddenly heard them now, all together, a choir of animal voices screaming inside glass.

Paloma made a sound she had never heard a person make.

It was not a word. Later she would understand that it was worse than a word—it was an answer, in some language older than any she knew, called out to the bottled voices like a shepherd calling to sheep penned in the dark. And the bottles answered. All of them. A shiver ran through the rack, glass ringing glass, and the nearest bottle cracked clean down the middle, and something like a wolf's howl poured out of it and dissolved into the air, free.

"Take the stands down," Odessa snapped. "Every bottle. Now."

The cultists between them and the racks had weapons, but they also had two hundred years of certainty, and certainty is slow. Sef went under a spear-thrust she had watched drilled a hundred times—left foot drags, thrust comes high—and drove her shoulder into the driller's knee. Odessa's cutlass sang. And Paloma walked through the middle of it untouched, because every beast-blooded guard in the room flinched away from him as though he were the storm itself, and reached the rack, and began tearing bottles down and cracking them one after another against the stone.

Each freed voice rose, wheeled once around his head like a bird coming home, and vanished upward into the empty sky. Sef saw the leader of the Moonrise Court watch this happen, and saw—not fanaticism on her face, not rage, but something rawer and sadder, the look of a woman watching her life's purpose fly away in pieces.

The star flared in her hands.

"It knows," the woman said, to no one, to the sky. "It remembers where it was taken from."

And then the tower shook, because below them, at the bridge, something enormous had finally finished happening, and the stolen star tore itself free of the leader's grip and shot upward like a fleeing bird, straight into the dark.

Straight toward a black scrap of wings that had been circling the spire all night, waiting.

"No," Sef breathed. "Quillfeather, no—"

### Quillfeather

Quillfeather had flown through arrow-fire before. She wished people would stop acting like it was a novel experience. It was merely unpleasant, the way tax season was unpleasant, the way Paloma pretending he couldn't hear her gossip was unpleasant. You adjusted your line, you respected the wind, and you did not, under any circumstances, think about the arrows as personal.

The star came up out of the tower trailing light like a wound trails blood, and it was hot, hotter than anything she had ever carried, and it was screaming. Not in raven. Not in wolf or rat or any of the hundred tongues she'd picked up eavesdropping on Paloma's conversations with the city's animals. It screamed in a language underneath all languages, and Quillfeather, who had spent her whole sharp clever life pretending feelings were for other birds, understood it perfectly.

*Put me back,* the star said. *Put me back where I was taken from.*

Below, the cultists had recovered from their trance enough to remember they owned bows.

"Typical," Quillfeather muttered, and folded, and dropped.

Falling is just flying with commitment. She fell past the first volley, felt the wind of a shaft part the air where her tail had been, banked hard off a thermal rising from the heated stone, and caught the star against her breast with both feet. It burned. Of course it burned. Everything worth carrying did; she'd learned that from watching Paloma carry his secret for thirty years.

*Clever bird,* the star said, in the language under languages. *Little thief. Take me home.*

"You're not going home," she told it through gritted beak, climbing, climbing, the tower falling away beneath her, arrows stitching the dark below. "Home burned down. Home is a guildhall with bad plumbing and a ledger nobody balances. You're going to the man who hears everyone, and you're going to behave."

The star pulsed against her claws, and for a moment she felt it looking at her—not with eyes, but the way a fire looks at a moth—and she felt it find the small hoarded things she kept at the bottom of herself: the memory of the egg she'd never had, the warmth of Paloma's shoulder on cold nights, the particular smell of the guild's back room where she was allowed to sleep and pretend she wasn't nesting.

*I could give those back to you doubled,* the star murmured. *I remember warmth. I remember every warm thing the world ever lost.*

"Everyone wants to make a bargain tonight," Quillfeather said, and beat her wings harder. "I've seen what bargains cost in this family. Fly."

An arrow took her through the outer edge of her left wing—a line of cold fire, feathers spinning away into the dark—and the star lurched in her grip and nearly tore free, and for one long terrible second she and the star and the empty sky were all falling together.

Then a shape rose out of the darkness to meet her. Not another bird. A voice—a chorus of them, dozens of freed voices that had wheeled upward from the shattered bottles and never left, hovering in the dead air above the tower like a congregation with nowhere to go. They caught her. They bore her up, wolf-howl and hawk-scream and the low patient murmur of oxen, and they carried her and the burning star up over the rim of the world while the arrows fell short and the Moonrise Court shrank beneath her to a pale scar in the dark hills.

Below, tiny with distance, the guild spilled out of the tower door onto the spire's crown—Odessa limping, Brannic leaning on Sef, his arm bound and soaked, Paloma with his face turned up, tracking her flight the way he tracked everything: silently, completely.

*To the guildhall,* the freed voices said together. *To the keeper. Before the thirteenth turn.*

And Quillfeather, bleeding, burning, carrying a stolen star home through the ruined dark, thought: *Yes. But someone had better tell him what it's going to ask for when it gets there.*

Because she had heard the star's bargain, whispered against her breastbone in the language under languages, and it had not offered her warmth for free.

Nothing in this story was ever free.
