## 17. What the Cult Wanted

They took my knife, my boots, and my dignity, in that order.

The knife I expected. The boots were a courtesy—*you will not run*, the moon-cult's silence said—and the dignity went somewhere between the bridge where Brannic had held the line alone and this chamber at the heart of the Moonrise Court, where they made me kneel on a floor of pale stone that had been polished by centuries of feet and no amount of scrubbing would ever make clean again.

The chamber was round. Of course it was round. Everything here was built around circles: the ritual rings, the moon-phases inlaid in silver, the thirteen alcoves cut into the walls, twelve of them empty and one of them—the thirteenth—bricked over with stones so old the mortar had gone black. Above me, where a ceiling should have been, there was only open sky. Starless sky. The whole court was a bowl turned up to a darkness that had once been full of light.

I knelt in the center of it like a coin dropped into a well.

*Don't talk,* I told myself. *You've spent thirty years not talking. This is just one more room where you don't talk.*

But my hands were shaking, and Quillfeather wasn't on my shoulder, and the last thing I'd heard before the cult's net-mages brought me down was Sef screaming my name from the far side of the wall, which meant she'd seen, which meant the family knew exactly what they'd lost, and none of that helped me kneel any straighter.

The doors opened without a sound.

She came in alone.

I had imagined the leader of the Cult of the Open Sky a hundred ways since Brannic came home with her name carved into his memory: a zealot in bone-white robes, an old soldier, a thing barely human anymore, worn down to appetite and moonlight. What walked through the doors instead was a woman perhaps sixty, gray-haired, plain-faced, dressed in travel-stained wool with no ornament except a single silver band at her throat. She moved like someone who had carried heavy things for a long time and had stopped noticing the weight.

She looked at me kneeling there, and something crossed her face that I did not expect and was not prepared for.

Grief. Old grief, settled in like weather.

"You're smaller than the prophecies made you," she said.

"The prophecies never met me."

Something almost like a smile. She gestured, and I felt the pressure lift off my tongue—the cult's silencing, a woven thing, cold as a hand around the throat. I worked my jaw. My voice came out rusty.

"That's a beast-binding," I said. "Old craft. Where did a woman who hates speakers learn to weave one?"

"From your people," she said simply. "Before we killed them."

The words landed like a dropped stone in still water. I kept my face still because I had thirty years of practice keeping my face still, but inside, everything tilted.

"Sit," she said, "if you're going to glare at me. Kneeling is for rituals, and this isn't one. Not yet." She lowered herself onto a stone bench at the circle's edge with the care of someone whose knees had opinions, and waited until I'd risen stiffly and found a bench across from her. The space between us was maybe ten feet of consecrated moonlight. It might as well have been ten miles or ten inches; I genuinely could not tell which.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said. "You'll want to interrupt. Don't. I've waited forty years to tell it, and if I stop, I won't start again."

I said nothing. In the rafters above the open sky—or where rafters should have been—I thought I heard wings, and told myself it was wind.

*Quillfeather,* I thought, deliberately, the way you throw a rope to someone across a gap. *If you can hear me. Get to the others.*

"My people were called the Vessari," she began. "You won't find the name in any ledger. Your guild is very good at making things vanish from records—I know, I've looked. We lived east of the Hollow Country, in the river valleys, before the war. We weren't warriors. We were rememberers. That was our magic—not fire, not flight. Memory. We could hold a thing that happened and keep it whole, keep it *true*, generations downstream. A village could lose its harvest and its children and its name, and as long as one Vessar lived, the loss itself would be remembered rightly. We were the world's witnesses." Her hands folded in her lap. "Every war needs someone to remember why it started. We were that someone. For both sides. That was our crime—to be useful to everyone."

The sky above us was utterly dark. I found I couldn't look away from it.

"When the war came," she said, "both armies wanted us silenced first. Isn't that always the way? Not the enemy's soldiers. The witnesses." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "There was a working, in the last year of the war. Both sides together, which should tell you how frightened they were. They didn't kill us—they were afraid killing us would leave the memories loose, unheld, hungry. Instead they—" her breath caught, and she mastered it, "—they unmade our holding. Every Vessar woke one morning unable to remember their own names. Our magic didn't die; it was *turned around*. Made to eat inward. My grandmother forgot my mother. My mother forgot me while I stood in front of her. And when the forgetting reached the bottom of each of us, we ended. Quietly. Like lamps running out of oil."

"How many survived?" I asked, though I already knew.

"One," she said. "Me. I was four years old. A Vessari child holds memory differently—we're born with the vessel already open. Whatever they did couldn't reach all the way into me. So I remember all of it. Every valley. Every name. Forty thousand people, and I am the only ledger they were ever written in." She looked up at the starless sky. "And then, twenty years ago, I learned that the stars themselves had been sealed away. That the guilds had hidden the war's ending behind wards and darkness so that no one—not even the sky—would ever look at it again."

My mouth was dry. "That's not what the sealing was."

"No?" She smiled, and there it was, the fanatic I'd been promised—except the fanaticism was made entirely of sorrow, and sorrow is much harder to fight than hate. "Then tell me what it was, clerk. Tell me what your founder was hiding under the spire, if not the truth of what was done to end the war. Tell me why the wards fed on voices—on *your* voice—if not to drown out everything the First Voice remembered."

I had no answer. Odessa had told me half-truths and I had told her half-lies back, and between us we had built a structure with nothing load-bearing in it, and this woman had put one finger against it.

"They call me a fanatic," she said. "In the lowlands, in the cities, they say the Open Sky wants to snuff out the light forever, that we worship darkness. Let them. Darkness is only what's left when the lie is removed." She leaned forward. "Here is what I want, beast-speaker. I want the war forgotten. Truly forgotten—not sealed, not hidden, *gone*. The First Voice remembers everything: every beginning, every atrocity, every reason. As long as it remembers, the war can wake again—as long as anyone alive carries the shape of it, some fool can rebuild it. But if the Voice forgets—" she spread her hands, "—the wound closes. Permanently. No more seals to fail. No more thirteenth moons counting down. Peace, real peace, bought once and paid for forever."

"And the price," I said quietly, "is my people."

Her expression didn't change. "Your people are the war's echo. Beast-speakers were made by the last workings of the old magic—voices shaped to speak for creatures dragged into a war they never chose. If the war is forgotten, the echoes go with it. Yes. I know." She held up a hand before I could speak. "And I know what you'll say next, because I've said it to myself every night for forty years, lying awake in this beautiful terrible circle: *how is that different from what was done to mine?*"

"Is it?"

"I've decided it is," she said, "and I've never once believed my own decision. But forty thousand dead, clerk. Forty thousand, and the world didn't even keep their names. I will not let the same thing happen twice. If the cost of the world remembering nothing is that it also forgets the ones who suffered most—" her voice finally cracked, just slightly, along an old fault line, "—then at least the suffering ends everywhere, equally, in the dark. At least no one is left carrying it alone."

I sat with that. The sky pressed down, starless, patient.

Because here was the thing—the thing I would carry out of that chamber like a splinter too deep to draw: I understood her. Completely. I had spent my whole life erasing myself so that other people wouldn't have to hold the truth of what I was. She had spent hers holding a truth so heavy that she wanted to burn the very capacity for holding out of the world. We were both trying to solve the same problem—the unbearable weight of memory—and we had chosen opposite answers, and neither answer was mercy.

"You sound like me," I said at last. "That's the worst part. You sound like me on my worst nights."

"Good," she said softly. "Then you understand why I can't stop."

"The stars," I said. "When you take the First Voice—when it forgets—what happens to them? Do they come back?"

She hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but I had spent decades reading hesitation in merchants and clerks and guildmasters, and I caught it the way you catch a false coin—by weight.

"They may," she said. "Some lights return when the lie is lifted. Some were only ever reflections." 

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." She rose, joints protesting, and looked down at me with that settled grief. "At moonturn, the seal dissolves. The Voice wakes. You will stand before it—you were always going to; the prophecies your guild wrote in secret are quite specific about that—and you will choose. Silence it forever, as Odessa trained you to do, and the war stays buried but the wound stays open, and in a hundred years or five hundred, someone finds it again. Or give it to me, and the world forgets, and your people go with it, gently, in the dark, and there is no more hunger and no more fear and no more *anything*." She moved toward the doors. "Or," she added, without turning around, "you find a third answer. The Vessari believed in third answers. It's what got us killed."

The doors closed behind her.

I stayed on the bench a long time, in the circle of that starless sky, doing sums in my head the way I always did when the world broke—because numbers were the one language that had never lied to me. Two answers, and a third one hiding somewhere between them like a figure that wouldn't balance. An enemy whose grief was my grief wearing a different coat. A woman who had offered me, with perfect sincerity, the annihilation of everything I was, and had wept—almost wept—doing it.

*Forty thousand names,* I thought. *She's the only ledger.*

And then, because I am what I am, because I could no more stop listening than stop breathing, I heard it—faint, from beyond the chamber walls, from the cells and corridors of the Moonrise Court: the animals the cult kept. Caged messenger-hawks. A blind mole used for tunnel-sensing. Dozens of small frightened hearts.

And among them, sharp and furious and unmistakably hers:

*Paloma. PALOMA. You absolute featherless idiot, I have been screaming for an hour—*

Quillfeather.

*I'm here,* I thought back at her, hard enough to hurt. *I'm alive. Listen carefully—*

Above the court, past the rim of that open sky, something vast was beginning to move. The dark moon, climbing toward the top of its thirteenth turn, weeks early—because the stolen star in their vault was doing exactly what a stolen star does to a seal.

The whole night shivered, like glass under strain.

*Get to the others,* I told her. *Tell Odessa: we don't have weeks.*
