## 20. A Stranger at the Table

*Brannic*

The first thing Brannic did when Paloma woke was nothing. That was the hardest thing he'd ever done.

He'd carried the man up from the spire himself—had felt how light he'd gotten, all bone and stubbornness under the clerk's coat—and laid him in the bed above the shop that still smelled of ink and lamp oil and the particular dust of ledgers nobody had touched in weeks. Odessa had checked his breathing. Sef had held a candle to his eyes. Quillfeather had screamed at him from the windowsill for six hours, which everyone agreed was either a terrible sign or a very good one.

And then Paloma opened his eyes, looked around the room like a man waking in a stranger's house, and said, politely:

"Where am I? And who are all of you?"

Brannic's hands remembered holding a bridge alone against thirty cultists while the world's last star burned through Quillfeather's feathers above him. His hands did not know what to do now. He folded them.

"I'm Brannic," he said finally. "I draw maps."

"Maps," Paloma repeated, tasting the word. His gaze drifted to the wall where three of Brannic's charts hung pinned—the Hollow Country, the wardlines before they fell, the city with its thirteen moons rendered in careful ink. "Those are good. The coastlines are right." A pause. "I don't know why I'd know that."

"You were our ledger-keeper," Sef said from the doorway. She'd been crying somewhere out of sight; her voice hadn't caught up yet. "You kept accounts of impossible things. Bottled echoes. Dreams. You were—"

"Sef." Odessa's voice, quiet, stopping the flood before it drowned him. "He doesn't need it all at once."

"He needs *something*," Sef said, and it broke in the middle. She turned and left, and Brannic heard her boots on the stairs going down too fast.

Paloma watched the empty doorway for a long moment. "Did I do something to her?"

"No," Brannic said. "She did something to us. We'll get to it."

Paloma looked down at his own hands as if checking they were his. "I remember stars," he said slowly. "I remember a word I don't know anymore. It's like—" He made a shape in the air, frustrated. "Like a room where everything's been moved two inches. I can tell something lived here. I can't find any of it."

Quillfeather dropped from the windowsill onto the bed's footboard and stood there, head cocked, one black eye fixed on him with the particular patience of a bird who has decided you are a problem she intends to solve personally.

"Do I know that bird?" Paloma asked.

"She knows you," Brannic said. "That's the important direction, right now."

* * *

*Sef*

Sef couldn't stay downstairs. Her brother was asleep in the back room—breathing easy for the first time since the water went bad, thanks to rats and a man who couldn't remember doing them the favor—and she couldn't sit still while Paloma lay up there being polite to strangers. Being polite to *them*. Paloma had never once been polite to them, not really; he'd been dry, and exacting, and secretly kind in ways he thought no one noticed, which was the whole problem, because now the noticing was all anyone could do.

So she went back up, past Odessa's warning look, and sat on the edge of his bed, and said, "I'm going to show you something, and it's going to be strange, and you're allowed to hate it."

"That's a promising opening."

"It's the only kind I've got." She unstoppered the little glass vial she'd kept since the raid—a single drop of the escaped dream, recaptured and diluted, which Odessa said should have been destroyed and Sef had hidden instead because she was, per Paloma's own ledgers, "a liability with excellent instincts."

The vision rose between them like steam.

Sef gave him hers first: her brother coughing blood into a rag, the cultist's smooth voice promising medicine, her own hand signing her name in their book. Paloma flinched at the end—not at her betrayal but at what came after, at the image Sef carried of him crouched in an alley asking rats for clean water, his voice making sounds no human throat should make, his face lit up like someone coming home.

"That's me," Paloma whispered. "That's—I did that?"

"You've done it your whole life. You just hid it."

The dream shifted, because dreams always shift, and showed him other things. Brannic as a boy, burning a map rather than let an army read it, weeping over the smoke. Odessa young, standing over a grave with a spade, deciding to become someone harder. Quillfeather's memory, featherless and falling, caught by a hand that smelled of ink.

And then—because the dream was old and greedy and had already tasted him once—it found the seam in Paloma where his memories used to be, and it poured itself into the gap.

Sef saw him see it: the guild hall full of light, the auction interrupted by falling stars, wolves calming under his voice in the square, his mother's handwriting in a ledger, the word leaving his mouth in the dark under the world. He went rigid. Tears ran down his face without his permission, fast, confused.

"I don't *know* these," he gasped. "I can see them but I don't—they're pictures of someone else's life—"

"They're yours," Sef said fiercely, gripping his hand. "They're yours and we're keeping them until you're ready. That's how this works. That's how *we* work."

When the dream faded he stared at the ceiling for a long time.

"The woman in the visions," he said. "Odessa. She looks at me like I'm her son."

"She looks at everyone like that. She just pretends she doesn't."

"And the raven—"

"Is currently eating your hair tie. Yes."

* * *

*Quillfeather*

Quillfeather had buried two humans before this one, and both times the ground had been the problem, the final indignity, the closing of a door. This was worse than burial. This was a man walking around inside an empty house calling hello into rooms he'd furnished himself.

She stayed on his shoulder. That was the whole of her strategy and she committed to it completely.

He kept trying to work out what she was. She could see it behind his eyes—the flicker toward her, the almost-listening, the way his head tilted when she said *food* or *stupid* or *stay*. The gift wasn't gone. She'd known animals who'd been trapped, drowned, starved back to themselves, and the self always sank before it surfaced. He was sunk. He would surface. In the meantime, someone had to maintain standards.

So she screamed at him when he tried to leave the shop alone. She stole his pen when he picked up a ledger and started reading it like an accountant instead of like a man reading his own love letters, which is what those books were, though none of the fools downstairs had figured that out yet. She pulled his hair when his voice went flat and polite, because flat and polite was the voice of the man who'd spent thirty years hiding, and that man was dead, and she refused to host his ghost.

"Why do you keep doing that?" he demanded on the third day, rubbing his scalp.

"*Stay,*" she told him.

"Stay," he repeated. And then, softer, testing something: "I understood that."

She preened. Of course he did. Debt of honor, the Voice had said, in the way it said things, which meant it had already decided, and Quillfeather had never once bet against a creature that remembered endings.

* * *

*Brannic*

On the fourth night, Brannic brought the ledgers down to the common table where Paloma sat picking at bread, and stacked them in front of him, and said, "Read me your favorite entries."

"I don't have favorites. I don't remember writing these."

"Then find some." Brannic sat across from him, arms crossed. "Start anywhere. Out loud."

Paloma opened the nearest volume at random. Brannic had chosen the oldest ones deliberately—his mother's years first, then the long blank span, then his own neat clerk's hand taking over, entry after entry, years of them. Paloma read:

"'Item 441: One echo, bottled, provenance unknown. Contents: laughter, three voices, a door slamming, someone saying *not tonight, love.* Value: sentimental. Keeper's note: do not sell to the widow on Candle Lane. She thinks it's her husband. It isn't. But she sleeps better when she visits it, so we charge her nothing and call it storage.'"

He stopped. Looked up. "I wrote that?"

"You wrote four thousand of those."

"'Item 512,'" Paloma continued, slower now, "'one dream, caged, hostile, escaped twice. Recommend better cage. Also recommend someone apologize to it. Dreams hold grudges.'" A breath of laughter escaped him, startled out. Then his finger found the next line and stopped moving.

Brannic knew which line it was. He'd marked the page.

"'Item 513: One apprentice, stolen, age nine, name Sef. Value: not for sale. Not ever. Entered into inventory so she stops running. Note: inventory means we keep track of things. Keeping track means we keep them.'"

Paloma's hand was shaking. "This is—" He looked around the table, at Odessa pretending to review accounts by the fire, at Sef frozen in the stairwell where she'd been eavesdropping badly, at Quillfeather on his shoulder. "These aren't ledgers. These are—you wrote down every person—"

"We're an inventory of impossible things," Brannic said. "You included yourself once. Page 300. Read page 300."

Paloma flipped pages with fingers that weren't steady. Found it. Read silently first, lips moving, and then aloud, barely above a whisper:

"'Item 900: One keeper, beast-speaker, thirty-one years hiding in plain sight. Value: beyond audit. Status: found. Note to whoever reads this after I'm gone—he was loved the whole time. He just never checked the books.'"

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the city murmured its nightly murmur, stars restored overhead through the window, all of them burning.

"I don't remember any of this," Paloma said. His voice cracked down the middle. "I can see the handwriting and I know it's mine and I feel nothing where it should be. It's like reading a stranger's mail."

"Then we'll introduce you," Brannic said. "Stranger to stranger. It worked for the rest of us. None of us started out family—we got inventoried in, same as everyone."

* * *

*Odessa*

Odessa Vell had buried a husband, outlived a founder, and sealed a truth beneath a spire for twenty years, and none of it had prepared her for the particular torture of being looked at kindly by Paloma Dravenmoor. Kindly was how he looked at strangers. Kindly was the whole tragedy, worn openly on his face.

But she was Guildmaster, and Guildmasters fed people. So she cooked—the big pot, the one that came out only for funerals and victories, and this was somehow both—and set the table for seven, and put Paloma in his chair, the one at her right hand that he'd claimed years ago and defended against all comers.

He sat in it like a guest.

They told stories anyway. That was the rule Odessa set, and she set it loudly: one story each, every night, about Paloma, until he was sick of himself. Brannic told the story of the wolf-pack in the square, and how Paloma had stepped in front of a hundred witnesses and chosen mercy over safety. Sef told the story of the rats and the well-water, and her brother toddled in halfway through to drop a crumpled flower on Paloma's plate, having been informed this was the man to thank. Quillfeather told hers in Raven, which nobody else spoke, but everyone agreed the tone was sufficiently insulting to be accurate.

And Odessa told the story she'd sworn she wouldn't: the night she'd found him, twenty-two years ago, a boy left at the door with a note in a dead woman's hand. *Keep the books. Keep the boy. Keep both secret.* She'd done two of the three for too long, and the third had nearly cost them everything.

"You were built into this guild before you could write," she finished. "The rest was just paperwork."

Paloma sat very still, staring into his bowl.

"I believe you," he said at last. "All of you. That's the strange part. I believe every word, and there's nothing in me answering to it. It's like being handed keys to a house I've never seen, and everyone insisting I grew up there."

"Then grow up here again," Odessa said. "We have time. The stars aren't going anywhere. Neither are we."

He looked up then, and for half a heartbeat something moved behind his eyes—recognition reaching for a name and missing. Quillfeather tightened her grip on his shoulder, and the moment passed.

"One thing," he said. "Tonight, at the edge of sleep, I heard the mice in the walls. Arguing about cheese." He laughed, uncertain, waiting for them to laugh too.

Nobody laughed. Everybody grinned.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing," said Odessa, and refilled his bowl, and let him figure it out.

* * *

*Sef*

It was Sef who found the hook, weeks later, in the newest ledger—the one he'd started the morning before the auction, the last day of his old life. The final entry was unfinished, ink trailing off mid-word where he'd run to answer the alarm. But beneath it, in fresher ink, dated three days after the spire, in a hand that shook:

*'Item 901: One keeper, memories surrendered, status unknown. If he returns even partly, start a new book. Title it:* When the Spire Falls. *Note to self: you paid for their belonging. Let them pay for yours. Fair trade. — P.D.*

Sef carried the book upstairs to where Paloma sat mending a cage for a dream that had come back voluntarily—which everyone agreed was unprecedented and slightly suspicious—and put it in his lap.

"You wrote us instructions," she said.

Paloma read it twice. His thumb pressed flat against the signature, as if trying to warm it.

"I don't remember writing this either," he said quietly. "But it sounds like advice from a man who knew what he was doing."

"So?" Sef said. "Are you going to take it?"

Outside the window, the thirteenth moon hung thin and silver over the rooftops, and the stars wheeled behind it, all of them burning, all of them accounted for. On Paloma's shoulder, Quillfeather leaned close and murmured one word in the old tongue, soft as a seed:

"*Begin.*"

Paloma reached for a fresh pen, opened a new book to its first blank page, and wrote:

*"Item 1."*

He paused, listening—to the mice, to the raven, to something vast and patient turning over beneath the city like a sleeper remembering its own name.

"To be continued," he said, and began.
