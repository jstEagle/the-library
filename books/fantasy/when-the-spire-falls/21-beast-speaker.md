## 21. Beast-Speaker

The first thing I hear is the raven, and she is complaining about me.

"—three days of moping and he hasn't once opened the window, which is an insult to the weather, which has been *immaculate*—"

I drop the ledger. It hits the floor of my room above the Vellum & Thorn with a slap like a struck bell, and Quillfeather's voice cuts off mid-syllable.

Silence. The kind of silence that has a shape, that leans in close to see what you'll do next.

I stand very still at the edge of my bed, bare feet on cold boards, and I listen past it. Past the creak of the shop settling. Past Sef arguing with Brannic downstairs about whether a bridge is a load-bearing insult or a structural compliment. Past all the ordinary noise of the guild that has been, for eleven days now, just noise—flat, mute, human, closed.

"*Immaculate*," I say out loud, testing the word like a coin bitten for gold. "You called the weather immaculate."

The silence stretches.

Then, cautiously, from the windowsill where she has been sitting on my shutters for three days without my knowing: "*He hears.* Oh, thank every feathered god, he hears. Do you know how exhausting it is being eloquent with no audience?"

I cross the room in four steps and throw open the window. Morning pours in, cold and clean, smelling of river-smoke and bread from the bakery two streets over. Quillfeather hops back on the sill and regards me with one bright eye, her head tilted at the exact angle of someone deciding whether to be smug or merciful.

"You knew," I say. "Before the stars went out. You always knew."

"I keep telling everyone that and no one listens. Ravens are famously ignored." She fluffs herself into a ball of affronted dignity. "The Voice gave it back, clerk. Whatever bargain you struck, whatever you paid—it took your memories and left the rest sleeping. And then it woke up and decided you'd been robbed."

"It was a debt of honor," I say slowly, because I can feel the truth of it arriving in me like water finding a channel. "It owed me. For—" My throat tightens. For speaking the word. For ending the war with a memory instead of a blade. For choosing, when every other choice was taken from me.

"For being the first thing in nine hundred years to tell it the truth," Quillfeather finishes. "Yes. We've all heard. It won't stop talking about it. The wolves are sick of it. The rats have formed opinions."

I laugh—a cracked, rusty sound, the first real laugh in eleven days—and press my forehead against the cold frame of the window. Eleven days ago I woke up at a table full of strangers who loved me, holding a ledger in my own handwriting that said *Paloma Dravenmoor, keeper, friend, family* in entries spanning years. Eleven days ago I had no gift, no past, no name I recognized as mine.

And the gift hadn't been taken after all. Only sleeping. Curled somewhere behind the hole in me like a fox in winter, waiting.

"Why now?" I ask. "Why not the day I woke?"

"Debts settle in their own time." Quillfeather preens her wing. "Also, honestly? I think it wanted to see if they'd keep you. If strangers would spend eleven days retelling a man his own life just so he wouldn't be alone in it." Her eye glints. "They did. It noticed. The Voice is sentimental. Don't tell it I said that."

Downstairs, something crashes—Sef's voice rises, indignant; Brannic's follows, unrepentant—and the ordinary music of my impossible family plays on without me, and I realize my hands are shaking.

Not from fear.

* * *

I go down.

That's the whole of the decision, really. Eleven days of being a stranger at my own table, of letting them feed me stories like medicine, of watching Brannic's face crumple slightly each time I failed to remember the bridge—he held alone, against forty cultists, because we were coming home—of hearing Sef replay the escaped dream's visions again and again, hoping this time one would stick. Eleven days of being a guest in my own belonging.

But now the gift is awake, and the gift remembers what I cannot, and I am done hiding in my own house.

The common room smells of ink and toasted oats and the particular dust of old vellum that Odessa Vell swears is medicinal. Brannic has knocked over the map chest—again, apparently, based on Sef's volume—and is down on his knees among a spill of charts, enormous hands moving with absurd gentleness over parchment older than the city.

"The eastern fold," he's saying, "goes *under* the western fold, that's cartography, that's basic—"

"That's chaos," Sef says. She's perched on the counter, nineteen years old and sharp as a paper cut, and when she sees me on the stairs her whole face changes. "Paloma. You're up early."

"He's up at all," Odessa murmurs from her ledger desk by the fire. She doesn't look up, but her pen has stopped. In eleven days I have learned that Odessa's pen stopping is equivalent to another person dropping everything and running across the room.

Quillfeather sweeps in over my shoulder and lands on the map chest, which is the wrong place to land, judging by Brannic's groan.

"Announcement," the raven says.

Everyone freezes. They don't understand her words—but they know her tones by now, the way you learn the moods of weather. Announcement means something is coming.

"She talks too much," I say. My voice comes out strange. "Always has. Called the weather immaculate this morning. Insulted me for three days of moping."

Brannic goes still on the floor. Sef slides off the counter. And Odessa Vell sets down her pen, very carefully, the way a woman sets down a blade before an embrace.

"Your gift," she says quietly.

"Came back. Or stopped pretending to leave." I look around the room—at these four people who rebuilt me from ledgers and loyalty, who never once made me feel like a debt they were servicing. "It wasn't erased. It was sleeping. The Voice returned it. As a—" My voice catches. "As a debt of honor. Apparently I told it the truth once, and it found the experience novel."

Nobody laughs. That's fine. I'm barely managing it myself.

Then Brannic stands up—slowly, unfolding like a siege engine—and crosses the room, and wraps me in a hug that lifts my feet off the floor. He doesn't say anything. He doesn't have to. His heartbeat against my ear says *you came back, you came back, you came back*, in the language underneath all languages.

Sef punches my arm when he puts me down. Hard. "Don't you ever pay mysterious magical prices again," she orders. "We talked about this."

"We did not talk about this."

"We're talking about it now."

Odessa simply nods once, and picks her pen back up, and that—from her—is thunderous applause.

* * *

The wolves come at dusk.

I hear them before anyone else does—the guild's wards don't chime anymore since the spire fell, and anyway, I don't need wards. Their voices arrive down the river valley like a change in pressure, six or seven animals moving through the outer districts, and the city's dogs answer them in a ragged chorus of alarm.

"They're not hunting," I say, standing in the doorway, and the whole common room turns. "They're lost. The pack from the wardline—the one I calmed during the raid. The blight broke their territory-memory. They can't find their denning grounds."

"Can you—" Sef starts.

"Yeah." I pull my coat off its hook. "I can."

Brannic falls in beside me at the door, a mountain volunteering as escort. Odessa says only, "Ledger entry tomorrow. Full account." Which is Odessa for *be careful, come home*.

We find them in the tannery quarter, in a yard full of abandoned frames, seven wolves pressed together in the dark, hackles up, eyes reflecting the lantern light like coins at the bottom of a well. The city watch has formed a line at the street's mouth with pikes and fear in equal measure. Somewhere a child is crying. Somewhere a dog is screaming itself hoarse.

Eleven days ago—or rather, a lifetime ago, in the person I no longer remember being—I stood before a panicked pack and nearly revealed myself before hundreds of witnesses, and the cost of hiding rose up against the instinct to help.

I walk forward alone.

"Watchman," I call, without turning. "Lower the points. They'll hold if I ask them to."

"And who in the dark moon's name are you?" someone demands.

I stop between the two lines—human steel on one side, wolf hunger-and-grief on the other—and I take a breath, and I do the thing I have spent my entire concealed, careful, invisible life not doing.

I speak so everyone can hear both sides of it.

"You crossed the blight to find your dens," I say to the wolves, in their own tongue, clear and carrying, while the watchmen stare. "Your ground-memory is broken but your nose works. Follow the river-scent north past the third weir—the old willow stand still smells like home. Your den-mother marked it before the dark came. Go. You're safe. No one here will loose a shaft at you tonight."

The lead wolf—gray-muzzled, scarred, the one whose terror I once caught like a falling stone—stares at me for a long moment. Then she tips her head back and *sings*, one long rising note, and the pack flows past me like water, like gratitude, like forgiveness, and is gone into the dark toward the willows.

The watch line stands frozen. A pike clatters to the cobblestones.

"He spoke to them," someone whispers. "In wolf. Like the old stories. Like the war-stories."

I turn and face them, and I do not lower my eyes, and I do not soften what I am.

"My name is Paloma Dravenmoor," I say. "I'm the keeper of the Vellum & Thorn. And I'm a beast-speaker. Yes. Like the ones the wards fed on. Like the ones the old war made." I let that sit in the cold air. "The spire is down. The seals are broken. There's nothing left to hide from you, and there never should have been anything to hide. Ask your questions. I'll answer what I can."

Silence. Then, from the back of the watch line, an older woman pushes through—watch-sergeant's knots on her cord, gray in her braid—and looks at me with an expression I can't read.

"My grandmother said her mother could talk to goats," she says slowly. "We thought it was a story."

"It's not a story," I say. "It never was."

She looks at me a long moment more. Then she turns to her line and says, "Stand down. All of you. Stand *down*." And to me, gruffly, almost gently: "The tannery quarter's been afraid of something in the dark for a week. Maybe it was only ever wolves. Maybe it was worse. Either way—" she jerks her chin at Brannic, looming behind me like a friendly cliff, "—your guild takes beast-refugees at the door, or that's the rumor. The rumor's about to get bigger."

"The rumor's true," I say. "Tell it loudly."

* * *

Word travels faster than wolves.

By the time Brannic and I walk back through the Vellum & Thorn's doors, there are already people waiting outside them—not angry, not armed. A fishwife holding a hen that, I can hear plainly, has been trying for *years* to explain about the fox problem and been dismissed as a stupid bird. Two children with a limping cat. A stablehand whose horses have been screaming warnings about a rotten beam for a season nobody believed.

They don't want miracles. They want translators. They want the thousand small voices of the world taken seriously at last.

Sef takes one look at the crowd and starts organizing them into a queue, because Sef would impose order on the apocalypse. Odessa appears in the doorway, assesses the situation with one sweeping glance, and says the most terrifying words in commerce: "Book appointments."

And Quillfeather lands on my shoulder—my shoulder, where she has decided she lives now, apparently forever—and murmurs, just for me:

"There's more. Listen."

I listen.

Past the hen, past the cat, past the horses three streets away. Down through the cobbles, into the roots of things, into the deep grain of the city itself—the oldest thing here, older than the spire, older than the war. Stone and timber and river-clay, the slow patient voice of ten thousand buildings leaning on each other, holding each other up.

And beneath it all, faint as a pulse, unmistakable as a bell:

*Paloma.*

The city saying my name.

*Paloma. Paloma Dravenmoor. Keeper.*

Not the way beasts speak—quick, hot, present-tense. This is slower, vaster, a voice that measures time in centuries. It sounds the way Odessa's pen sounded when it stopped. It sounds like being known.

"What does it say?" Brannic rumbles beside me. He can't hear it. But he can hear *me*, and something in my face must be extraordinary.

I stand in the doorway of my guild, my family at my back, my city opening its ancient throat to say my name, and I think about a boy who spent thirty years making himself small enough to pass through the world unnoticed. A clerk. An absence. A careful blank space in every ledger but his own.

That boy is done.

"It says my name," I tell him, and I'm smiling, and I don't try to hide that either. "The whole city knows my name."

"Good," Brannic says simply. "Took it long enough."

Quillfeather laughs—a rasping, crowing sound—and the first person in the queue steps forward with her ridiculous, eloquent hen, and I roll up my sleeves, and get to work.

Because that's what a keeper does.

* * *

Late that night, after the last appointment (the cat; the diagnosis: splinter, dramatic temperament), after Odessa's ledger entry written in her own hand—*Item: one gift, restored. Value: beyond cataloguing*—after Sef has fallen asleep mid-argument about whether the hen counts as a client or a complainant, I climb to my room and open the window.

The sky is thick with stars. Real ones, burning, restored—every one of them back in its socket, the whole spilled treasury of heaven accounted for. Somewhere out there is the star we stole back from the Moonrise Court. Somewhere out there is everything the old war tried to bury.

Below me, the city murmurs. Above me, Quillfeather settles on the sill.

"So," she says. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," I agree.

"The spire," she says. "They're really going to do it. Take it apart. Stone by stone. No more seals, no more hidden things." She ruffles her feathers against the night chill. "You know what that makes you, clerk? The last secret the old world ever kept. And you just spent today giving yourself away for free."

I lean on the sill and look at the stars, and at the dark shape of the spire against them—soon to fall, soon to become something new—and I feel no grief at all.

"Not the last," I say. "Listen."

Quillfeather listens. And far below us, in the bones of the city, in the roots of the hills, in the deep places where the old magic pooled like rainwater—something answers. Not one voice. Hundreds. Small, frightened, hidden speakers like the boy I used to be, hearing that one of us walked into a square and refused to hide.

"They'll come," I say softly. "All of them. And when the spire falls, there'll be a school where they can learn what they are without learning to be ashamed of it."

"Mm." Quillfeather tucks her head under her wing. "Sentimental."

"Says the raven who sat on my shutters for three days."

"Someone had to guard you." Her voice is already half-asleep. "Family business."

I stay at the window a long time, listening to the world talk—the wolves singing at the willows, the city humming my name, the small hidden voices gathering courage like sparks—and for the first time in either of my lives, the one I remember and the one I don't, I am not listening in secret.

I am part of the conversation.

Tomorrow we start taking the spire apart.

Tonight, the whole world says my name, and I say it back.
