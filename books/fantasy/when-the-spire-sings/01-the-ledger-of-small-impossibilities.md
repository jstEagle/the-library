## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The key that opens yesterday was lying in the ditch behind Maren's cottage, and it had been there, by Beatrix's best estimation, for eleven years.

He knew this because he could see its thread. Everyone else who passed the ditch — the tinker with his rattling cart, the miller's boys hunting frogs, even old Tam Wicker who claimed to have known every stone in the parish since before his knees went — walked past it without a glance. To them it was a bent scrap of greenish metal, the kind of thing a plough turns up and a sensible person leaves where it lies. But to Beatrix Dunmore, crouched in the wet bracken with his notebook balanced on one knee, the key glowed.

Not with light, exactly. With *attention*. A fine silver filament ran from the key's bow — an ornate loop worked into the shape of a door seen from the side — up through the bracken, through the hedge, through the cottage wall itself, and vanished somewhere into the past. That was how threads of that kind always looked: they did not connect a thing to another *place*, but to another *when*. Most objects carried only short threads. A spoon remembered the hand that first held it. A boot remembered the road. The threads faded after a season or two, fraying into nothing, and the object became simply what it appeared to be.

This key's thread was thick as a ship's cable, and it hummed when Beatrix looked at it too long, the way a plucked string hums, and if he let his eyes unfocus and his gift come forward — that sliding, falling sensation, like stepping off a stair that wasn't there — he could read along it.

*Eleven years ago*, the thread said. *A girl of perhaps nine, standing at this ditch at dusk, weeping because her father had sold the family's ferry rights and meant to move them inland, away from the river she loved. She wished, with the whole furious weight of a child's wishing, to go back to yesterday morning, when none of it had happened yet. And something in the world — some patient, listening something — heard her.*

The key had not granted her wish. Keys never do; they are only keys. It had simply *become possible*, which is a different and more terrible thing. Somewhere in the world there was now a lock that would open onto yesterday, and this was the only key that fit it, and the girl had grown up, married a cooper in Millbrook, and forgotten she had ever wished at all. But the wish remained in the metal, coiled tight as a spring, waiting.

Beatrix wrote all of this down. His pencil moved quickly across the page, in the cramped, slanted script Maren called *chicken-tracks* and which he privately considered the only honest handwriting in the county. He recorded the key's dimensions, its weight (heavier than it should be, as though it carried more than one day inside it), the color and behavior of its thread, and then, in the section Maren had ruled off at the bottom of each entry with three careful lines, he wrote the classification:

**IMPOSSIBILITY, SMALL. CLASS: TEMPORAL. STATUS: UNCLAIMED. DANGER: MODERATE. NOTE — DO NOT LET IT OPEN ANYTHING.**

He hesitated, then added beneath, in smaller letters: *I wonder what yesterday it opens. I wonder if yesterdays are all the same to a key, or if each one is different, like doors.*

Then he heard the gate, and his heart dropped through the wet grass like a stone.

* * *

Maren Ashcottle, hedge-witch of Lower Fenwick, did not walk so much as arrive — a process involving much rustling, several muttered words to herself, and a general sense that the vegetation was making way for her under protest. Beatrix had four seconds' warning, which was three more than usual, and he used them badly. He stuffed the notebook into his shirt, snatched the key from the ditch with his bare hand — a mistake, he realized at once, because touching an unclaimed impossibility directly was like pressing your ear to a struck bell — and flung himself flat behind the rain barrel just as the cottage door banged open above him.

"Beatrix." Her voice carried across the yard the way frost carries across a field. "I know you're out here. Your boots are by the door and you are nowhere in the house, which means you're either in a tree or in a ditch, and you haven't the head for trees."

He pressed his palm against the mud and breathed through his mouth. The key sat in his other fist, humming faintly, and through the thread-sight that never entirely left him — even closed-eyed, even asleep, he saw the world as a loom, everything crosshatched with silver and gold and occasional sickly green filaments binding thing to story, story to place, place to memory — he watched Maren's own thread come toward him across the yard. Hers was the strangest he'd ever catalogued: not one color but many, braided, like a rope made of different decades all twisted together. There were knots in it he didn't have names for yet. She'd been a witch longer than he'd been alive, and she had *done things*, whole seasons of doing, that she had never told him about.

"Beatrix Dunmore." Closer now. "If I have to find you myself, it'll be the worse for you."

It occurred to him, as it often did, that hiding from a hedge-witch was fundamentally a foolish enterprise. Maren couldn't see threads the way he could, but she knew the yard the way a spider knows its web, and sure enough he felt a prickle along his spine, a sensation like being counted. He stayed still anyway. Pride, his mother had once said — no. Not his mother. Someone had once said that pride was a luxury of people who had someone to be proud *of*. He couldn't remember who. The thought slid away from him the way thoughts sometimes did, leaving behind a small smooth hole, and Beatrix filed the hole away with the others, in the drawer at the back of his mind he tried never to open.

"Fine," Maren said, to the yard in general. "Sulk out here. See if I care. Supper's at six, and if you miss it again I'm giving your portion to the goats, and they've better manners than you."

The door banged shut. The prickle withdrew.

Beatrix lay behind the barrel a while longer, watching the sky go the color of a bruise healing, and opened his fist.

The key lay in his palm, warm now, though the evening was cold. Its thread stretched away eastward, over the fen, over the hills beyond the fen, toward a horizon he had never crossed and could not, quite, imagine. He had read once — in the one book Maren owned that wasn't a recipe book or a book of wards, a water-stained volume called *A True Account of the Guild of Impossible Things* — that the guild kept a hall where such objects were brought, and logged, and studied, and kept safe. That there were people whose whole work was this: walking the country, finding the small cracks where wishing had gotten into the world, and writing them down so they could be mended.

The book had been vague about where the hall stood. Books about impossible things usually were; it was practically a rule. But it had included one detail Beatrix had turned over in his mind every night since he was twelve, the way you turn a stone in your pocket: *the ledgers of the guild*, it said, *are written in a hand that must be learned, for a wrongly-recorded impossibility is worse than an unrecorded one.*

A hand that must be learned. As though record-keeping itself were a craft, a magic, a *belonging*.

He wanted it the way he supposed the girl in the thread had wanted her yesterday: with his whole furious weight.

* * *

Supper was barley soup with nettles in it, eaten across a table from Maren in a silence broken only by the fire and her occasional disapproving glances at the mud on his sleeves. She was a narrow woman, brown and tough as a hazel switch, with grey hair she hacked off herself each spring equinox and eyes the pale yellow of something that hunts at dusk. He had lived with her for seven years, since she'd found him — he genuinely did not remember the details; another smooth hole — half-starved on the north road, and taken him in, and taught him herbs and wards and the reading of omens, and never once, in seven years, asked him to call her anything warmer than *Maren*.

"You touched it," she said suddenly. "Whatever you found today. You touched it with your skin."

He kept his eyes on his soup. "How could you possibly—"

"Because you're sitting funny. Because you keep your left hand under the table like a boy caught with stolen plums. And because," she said, setting down her spoon with a click, "you've got the look. The far look. Like you're reading a letter nobody else can see."

Beatrix said nothing. Under the table, his left hand curled around the key, and its hum traveled up his arm and settled somewhere behind his sternum, a small persistent warmth, like swallowing a coal from a fire that hadn't gone out.

"You can't keep doing this," Maren said, and her voice had changed — quieter now, and tired, and underneath the tiredness something he had never heard from her before and could not name. Something almost like fear. "Finding these things. Writing them in that little book of yours. Cataloguing the world's mistakes like a clerk of — like a *clerk*."

"Like a clerk of what?" he said. "You were going to say something. Clerk of what?"

"Eat your soup."

"Maren." He set down his spoon. "There's a guild. I've read the book. The Guild of Impossible Things. They write things down, properly, in ledgers, and they mend the cracks, and I could — I could learn the hand. I could be useful *somewhere*, to *someone*, instead of—" He stopped, because the sentence was going somewhere he didn't want to follow it in company.

Instead of living in a ditch-side cottage with a woman who taught him wards but wouldn't tell him why. Instead of being the strange boy the village tolerated because the witch kept him. Instead of lying awake at night counting the holes in his own memory and wondering what kind of person loses his mother's face and doesn't even know when he lost it.

Maren looked at him for a long moment across the table, and the firelight did something complicated in her pale eyes.

"The guild is a story," she said finally. "Older than either of us. Stories about guilds are how people avoid doing the real work, which is here, in the mud, with your hands." She stood, gathered the bowls, and paused at the doorway with her back to him. "And Beatrix — the world isn't a ledger. Some things aren't meant to be written down. Some things get *worse* when they're written down. Remember that."

She went out to the woodshed. He heard her splitting logs with unnecessary violence, and understood that the conversation was over.

* * *

That night he sat up by candlelight and added the key to the master list at the front of his notebook, entry number forty-one in a catalogue spanning three worn volumes. Forty-one impossible things in seven years. A spoon that stirred tea into memories of whoever had last washed it. A mirror that showed rooms instead of faces. A coin that was always a penny short of the fare. All of them small. All of them humming quietly in the loom of the world, their threads mostly short and fading, most of them harmless if left alone.

But lately — and this he had written in no ledger, because he had no words for it yet — the threads had been *changing*. Over the past month, the great background weave of the country itself, the vast slow warp of rivers and roads and borders that he usually barely noticed because noticing it was like noticing the sky, had developed a sound. A wrongness. When he let his gift slide forward and really listened, the whole fabric hummed faintly out of tune, like a harp left out in the damp.

Three nights ago he had looked east, hard, following the warp-thread of the border road as far as his sight could stretch, and the thread had simply — stopped. Not faded. Faded threads tapered, thinned, dissolved gently into nothing. This had been cut. Snapped clean, the severed end curling back on itself like a burned wick, and beyond it there was not darkness but *absence*, a place his sight refused to report on, the way the eye refuses to see the back of its own socket.

He had told himself it was a flaw in him, a trick of tiredness. He had not told Maren. He suspected, from the way she had been burning ward-candles two at a time all month, and muttering to herself in the old fen-tongue, that she had noticed something too, and was also not telling him.

Beatrix blew out the candle and lay in the dark with the key on his chest, rising and falling with his breath. Its thread stretched away eastward, past the fen, past the hills, past the border where the world's weaving stopped, and he wondered — not for the first time — whether an impossibility could point the way home to a home he'd never had.

Somewhere out there, he thought drowsily, there is a hall full of ledgers, and a chair at a long table, and a hand I could learn.

Somewhere out there, the loom is coming apart at the edges, and nobody is writing it down.

He slept. And in the night, without his knowing it, the key turned over in his sleep-warmed palm like a thing settling in, and its long thread trembled all the way to the horizon — where, forty miles east, on the oldest wall of a certain guildhall, a thirteenth moon was quietly rising in a mural that had shown only twelve for three hundred years.
