## 2. Thirteen Moons on the Wall

Oren Vey had counted the moons on the guildhall wall every morning for forty years, the way other men counted their pulse or their coins. Twelve of them, arranged in a slow spiral above the great hearth, painted by hands long dead in pigments mixed with ash and egg and, if the oldest rumors were true, a little ground bone. The mural was called *The Turning Year*, though no one living knew which year it turned. It was older than the guild's charter. Older, Oren suspected, than the country itself, though he would have been flayed before saying so aloud.

Twelve moons. He had checked them yesterday at dusk, as he did every dusk. He could have drawn them from memory: the pale first moon at the spiral's center, small as a thumbnail; the swollen harvest moon near its outer edge; the cracked moon that some apprentice centuries ago had painted with a hairline fracture running pole to pole, so that it seemed perpetually about to come apart — a joke, perhaps, or a prophecy; and all the rest between, each distinct, each known to him the way his own knuckles were known to him.

This morning there were thirteen.

He stood in the empty hall with the fire burned down to grey lace and did not move for a long time. The thirteenth moon hung just beyond the outermost curve of the spiral, where the plaster met bare stone, as if the painter had run out of wall and left it unfinished — except it was not unfinished. It was new. Its surface was still faintly damp-looking, a wet sheen across the paint, and its color was one Oren had never seen on any wall or sky: a deep bruised silver, like moonlight drowned in water. Around its rim, in letters too small to read without climbing, ran a thin ring of script.

Oren knew script when he saw it, even script that small, even script that wrong. Ledger-script. The hand of accountants. The hand of bargains.

His heart was behaving badly. He noted this the way he noted everything — calmly, precisely, in the ledger-keeper's habit of mind he had worn so long it had grown into his bones — and then he made himself breathe until it behaved again. Forty years of practiced calm sat on him like a well-tailored coat. No one watching him now, not even the sharpest-eyed apprentice, would have seen anything but an old man considering a painting on a cold morning.

That was the trick of it. That had always been the trick of it. A Guildmaster's true work was not the trades or the ledgers or the impossible inventory. It was standing between the guild and the truth, holding the door of his own face shut.

He climbed the ladder slowly, joints complaining, and read the ring of writing around the thirteenth moon.

*One turn borrowed. Twelve remain.*

The words were in the founder's hand.

Oren sat down on the top of the ladder, quite suddenly, because his legs had made the decision without consulting him. He gripped the rail. Below him the hearth ticked as it cooled, and somewhere overhead a beam settled with a groan, ordinary sounds, the sounds of a building minding its own business, and he clung to their ordinariness like a drowning man clinging to driftwood.

*One turn borrowed.* He had known, of course. He had helped. Forty years ago — no. Longer. Longer than that, though the arithmetic went strange whenever he approached it, the way distances went strange near the border wards, stretching and folding. He had stood in this hall as a young man with ink still under his fingernails and watched the founder write numbers into the air, and he had said yes. He had said yes because the alternative had been written on the founder's face, and the founder's face had been the only map of despair Oren had ever needed.

But knowing a thing and seeing it written on your own wall in your own dead friend's hand were different animals entirely. Knowing was a ledger entry. Seeing was a bill come due.

*Twelve remain.*

He descended the ladder. He fetched the long dust-cloth from the cleaning cupboard — the one Bramble used for the high shelves — and he covered the mural. All of it. Twelve moons, thirteen moons, the whole turning year, hidden behind grey linen that smelled of lye and old smoke. If the apprentices asked, and they would ask, he would tell them the plaster was being treated for damp. Damp was dull. Damp was believable. No one had ever lost sleep over damp.

It was the first lie he had told the guild in eleven years, and it went down smooth as oil, which frightened him more than the moon did.

* * *

By the time the hall woke — Bramble first, always, her tread heavy on the kitchen stairs, followed by the smell of porridge and scorched honey; then Ilex drifting down with charcoal stains already on her cheek despite the hour; then the trickle of journeymen and apprentices, yawning, bickering, alive — Oren Vey had arranged himself behind his desk in the counting room with three ledgers open before him and his expression set to its morning default: mild, attentive, faintly bored. A man whose largest concern today was the state of the candle inventory.

"Guildmaster." Sable appeared in the doorway without knocking, because Sable had never once knocked in the six months since she'd arrived, bloodied and half-frozen, asking for sanctuary with a ward-breaker's tools still in her pack. She looked at him the way she always did, with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing, and Oren felt the familiar prickle of being weighed. "You're up early."

"I'm always up early."

"You're never up early. You're up exactly on time. There's a difference." Her gaze slid past him toward the hall, toward the covered wall, and something flickered across her face — curiosity, or its ward-breaker cousin, suspicion. "What's with the sheet?"

"Damp," Oren said. "Plaster treatment."

"Mm." She let the syllable hang there, unconvinced, and he understood that she had filed it away somewhere in that relentless head of hers, next to everything else she'd filed away about the guildhall and its master. "The boy logged something last night. Beatrix. Entry four hundred and six. A key."

Oren's stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn, which he permitted himself to feel for exactly two seconds before filing it away as well. "A key."

"A key that opens yesterday. His phrasing." She leaned against the doorframe. "Ilex is beside herself. Wants to take rubbings. Wants to know what it opens and whether yesterday means literal-yesterday or metaphorical-yesterday and whether we can get it to open last Tuesday because she mislaid a map. The boy himself won't come out of the archive. He's sitting in there with it wrapped in his scarf like it's a wounded bird."

"Has anyone touched it?"

"We're not idiots, Guildmaster."

"No," Oren agreed. "You are many things, but not that." He closed the candle ledger with unnecessary gentleness. "Bring me the log. Not the key. Just the log."

Sable pushed off the doorframe and paused, one hand on the jamb. "You know," she said, conversational, dangerous, "in my former line of work, when a man covers a wall overnight and won't say why, that's usually the most interesting thing in the building." And she went to fetch the log before he could compose an answer, which was, he suspected, exactly why she'd said it.

* * *

Entry four hundred and six, in Beatrix Dunmore's small, precise, slanting hand:

*Logged at dusk, 11th of Emberfall. Object: key, iron, unremarkable in form save that its bit is cut in the shape of a question mark. Provenance: found beneath the floorboard of my old room at Maren's cottage, wrapped in oilcloth. Function, per test (see marginal note): opens yesterday. Marginal note: I opened yesterday. It was raining. I do not recommend it. Threads observed: seven, all leading backward, none leading anywhere I wish to follow. Filed under: Things That Should Have Stayed Under Floorboards.*

Oren read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting the dread rise through him like groundwater through a cellar floor.

*Found beneath the floorboard of my old room at Maren's cottage.*

Maren. The hedge-witch out past the fen road who took in strays and taught them too much and charged them nothing but their company, and who had sent the guild her runaway apprentice with a note reading, in her crabbed hand, *he sees threads, keep him fed, don't ask him what else he sees.* Oren had kept him fed. Oren had mostly kept to the not-asking, though it had cost him; the boy saw things, all right. Saw the way objects were stitched to their stories, saw the seams. Saw, Oren had begun to suspect lately, more than any thread-seer ought to see and stay sane.

And now the boy had gone back to Maren's cottage — or dreamed he had, or threaded his way there somehow, and with this boy the distinction mattered less than it should — and dug up a key that opened *yesterday*, and logged it in his neat little hand with a marginal note of dry complaint, and filed it under *Things That Should Have Stayed Under Floorboards*, and thought nothing more of it. Because to Beatrix Dunmore, an impossible thing was simply Tuesday.

To Oren Vey, it was a signpost.

Keys that opened time did not surface by accident. Nothing surfaced by accident anymore; that was the whole sickness of the age, the whole secret rot at the center of the guild's cheerful trade in marvels. Every impossible thing the guild catalogued, traded, shelved, and dusted was load-bearing. Every one of them held a corner of the world up, and Oren had spent forty years pretending otherwise because the founder had asked him to and because pretending was easier than the truth.

But a key that opened yesterday, appearing the same night a thirteenth moon bloomed on the oldest wall in the country? The world did not deal in coincidence. The world dealt in *installments*.

"Send the boy to me," he said to the empty room, and then, because the empty room deserved honesty if no one else did: "God forgive me. Send the boy to me."

* * *

Beatrix Dunmore came into the counting room like a cat entering a room full of rocking chairs — low, watchful, ready to bolt. He was seventeen, maybe, all elbows and hollow cheeks, with ink on his fingers and his collar buttoned to the chin against a world he clearly didn't trust. He carried the key wrapped in his scarf, and he set it on the desk between them with the exaggerated care of someone handling something both precious and furious.

"You wanted the log reviewed, Guildmaster?"

"I wanted you," Oren said. "Sit."

Beatrix sat on the edge of the chair, weight forward, a boy built for leaving. Oren had seen that posture a thousand times in forty years of collecting strays. It was the posture of someone rehearsing the doorway.

"The key," Oren began, keeping his voice level, unhurried, the voice he used for candle inventories. "Tell me how you found it."

The boy's eyes flickered. "It's in the log."

"Logs record what happened. I'm asking what it *was* like. There's a difference, and the difference matters." He leaned back. "Humor an old man."

A silence. Then, grudgingly, the story came out in pieces: the dream of the cottage, so vivid he'd woken with fen-water smell in his nose; the floorboard loose in a way it hadn't been loose when he lived there; the oilcloth packet with his own name stitched into it in thread so fine he'd needed his gift to see it. "Someone put it there for me," he said. "Recently. The stitches were fresh. Weeks old, maybe less."

"Did you see who?"

"No one was there. But—" Beatrix hesitated, and Oren watched him wrestle with something. "There were threads. From the key. Seven of them, going backward. And one of them—" His jaw worked. "One of them didn't go backward. It came out of the key and went *up*. Through the roof. Toward the capital. Toward the spire." He looked up, and his eyes were too old for his face. "Guildmaster, why does a key that opens yesterday have a thread tied to the spire?"

Because the world is mortgaged, Oren thought. Because everything you've ever catalogued is a stitch in a bargain you know nothing about, and the thing you dug out of your old life is humming with the debt. Because I helped make it so, and I am an old coward, and you are going to hate me someday, and you will be right to.

What he said was: "Objects of this kind often anchor to large structures. The spire is the largest thing we have. It's nothing to trouble yourself over."

It was the second lie of the day, and Beatrix looked at him for a long moment with those thread-sighted eyes, and Oren had the horrible certainty that the boy could see the lie itself — see the seam of it, the stitching, the way a false statement hangs slightly wrong on the frame of a face. But the boy said nothing. He had learned, somewhere before the guild, that pressing adults for truth got you locked rooms and worse. Oren recognized the lesson because he'd taught versions of it himself, gently, to protect him.

Protection. That was the word cowards used.

"One more question," Oren said. "The key. Where is it kept now?"

"The strongbox in the archive. Third shelf. Wrapped—"

"In your scarf, yes, Sable mentioned." He smiled, and hated how easily the smile came. "Leave it there. Don't test it again. And Beatrix — if you notice anything strange, anything at all, in the days ahead, you'll bring it to me directly. Not to Ilex, not to Sable. To me."

"Strange how?"

*Strange* like a thirteenth moon, Oren thought. Strange like a countdown written in a dead man's hand above my hearth. Strange like the horizon feeling, lately, when I look at it too long, as if it were painted on cloth and someone has begun rolling it up from the edges.

"Strange," he said, "in any way that makes the hairs on your arms stand up."

The boy almost smiled. "That happens around here daily."

"Then we shall have a busy season." Oren dismissed him with a nod, and watched him go — watchful, light-footed, carrying his impossible key and his impossible eyes back down the corridor, unaware that he had just handed his Guildmaster the second worst piece of news Oren had received in forty years.

The first was on the wall.

* * *

Night came early that evening, as it had been coming early all season, and Oren waited until the hall emptied, until Bramble's kitchen went dark, until Ilex's lamp behind the map room door finally guttered out, until even Sable's restless pacing upstairs stilled. Then he took a lamp to the great hearth and pulled the dust-cloth from the mural.

The thirteenth moon had changed.

The ring of script around its rim had grown longer. New words had joined the old ones, written smaller, crowded, urgent — and Oren climbed the ladder with his heart hammering its bad rhythm and held the lamp close and read:

*One turn borrowed. Twelve remain.*
*Payment deferred is payment doubled.*
*The keeper remembers. The keeper forgets.*
*Ask the boy about the seventh thread.*

The lamp shook in Oren's hand. Wax spat onto the ladder rail.

*Ask the boy about the seventh thread.*

The founder's voice, after all these years, reaching out of a wall to give him instructions. Or warnings. With the founder it had always been impossible to tell the difference, right up to the end — especially at the end, when the numbers were being written and Oren had stood there young and terrified and agreeing, agreeing, agreeing.

He looked at the thirteenth moon, at its drowned-silver face, and felt the practiced calm settle over him one more time, smooth as the dust-cloth, hiding everything.

Tomorrow, he would ask the boy about the seventh thread.

Tonight, he covered the mural, trimmed the lamp, and sat alone in the dark hall listening to the building breathe around him, counting — twelve, twelve, twelve — like a man praying in reverse.

Outside the window, above the sleeping guildhall, the real moon rose fat and pale over the rooftops.

And far to the north, where the border wards stood in their ancient patient lines, something Oren could not yet name had begun, very quietly, to sing.
