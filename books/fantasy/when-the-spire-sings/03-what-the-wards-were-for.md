## 3. What the Wards Were For

The summons came at dawn, folded into a pigeon's beak like a knife between its teeth.

Beatrix found the note pinned to his cot by a hatpin — Oren Vey's handwriting, which always looked as though it were trying to escape itself: *Ward-stone on the Marchline, east of Hollowmere. Cracked. Take the kit. Take the coat. Don't take all day.* Beneath it, in smaller script, almost an afterthought: *And boy — if anything speaks to you out there, don't answer it.*

A cheerful way to start a morning.

He dressed in the grey apprentice coat with the guild's sigil stitched at the shoulder — a spire inside a circle of twelve moons — and packed the repair kit from the supply closet: trowel, mortar of crushed slate and grave-dust, a spool of silver thread that was not silver, and the ledger. Always the ledger. He had taken to carrying it against his chest lately, under the coat, the way other men carried lockets of their mothers' hair. His own copy of his own life, page by page, impossibility by impossibility.

The key that opens yesterday sat in its entry still, drawn in careful ink, and every time he looked at the little sketch he felt something in the drawing look back.

Hollowmere lay two days east by the mail coach, if the coach ran, and the coach did not run, so it took him three by a farmer's cart, one by his own blistered feet, and half a day by argument with a ferryman who claimed the river had changed its mind overnight and now flowed the wrong direction entirely.

"Been like this since Tuesday," the ferryman said, poling upstream against a current that ran downstream, his face set in the grim expression of a man who has decided not to be surprised by anything ever again. "River knows something. Animals know things before weather. Rivers know things before worse."

"Before what?" Beatrix asked, and paid him double not to answer, because he was beginning to suspect that answers in the world outside the guildhall came with interest rates he could not afford.

The Marchline revealed itself the way old scars reveal themselves: gradually, then all at once. First the hedgerows thinned. Then the birdsong thinned. Then the land simply stopped pretending to be interested in itself — fields going fallow without complaint, fences leaning like tired sentries, and ahead, where the map said the country ended and the next country began, there was nothing dramatic at all. Just a low ridge of hills, and along their spine, at half-mile intervals, the ward-stones: waist-high pillars of blue-grey rock, each carved top to bottom with script so old the letters had begun to dream of being something else.

Twelve stones visible from the ridge. Beatrix counted them twice, and both times, before his eyes could settle, he thought he saw the suggestion of a thirteenth far down the line, wrapped in haze. When he looked directly at it, it declined to exist.

*Thirteen moons on the wall,* Oren had muttered once, thinking himself alone with the mural. Beatrix had not asked. Some questions were doors you didn't want to find unlocked.

The cracked stone was the fourth from the north end, and it was cracked the way an egg is cracked — not broken, but opened. A fissure ran from crown to base, thin as a hair, and through the fissure seeped a light that was no color Beatrix could name: the light of a thing remembered incorrectly. It hummed, very faintly, a note he felt in his back teeth.

He knelt, unpacked the kit, and got to work the way Maren had taught him to work: measure first, judge never, and never touch the story until you've touched the object. He laid his palm flat against the stone's flank.

The threads rose to meet him.

This was his gift, the one Maren had tried to sell and he had run from — though when he tried to remember the running, the memory came to him oddly, like a letter addressed in someone else's hand. The threads were always there for him: the lines that bound every object to its story, every story to its teller, every teller to the ground they stood on. A hammer was a hammer because of ten thousand hammers before it, all the way back to the first stone struck in anger or hunger; thread-sight let him see the line running back through all of them.

The ward-stone's threads should have run into the ground, into the bedrock, into the deep slow story of the land itself.

Instead they ran *outward*. All of them. Thousands upon thousands of filaments of meaning, pouring from the stone and away over the border-lands behind him, into farms and churches and graveyards and kitchens, threading through cradles and chronicles and the names of lanes that only three people alive still remembered — and anchoring there. Holding. Pulling taut.

The stone was not facing outward, toward enemies.

It was holding the country *down*, the way a paperweight holds down a manuscript in wind.

"That's not right," Beatrix said aloud, and a voice behind him said:

"Now you're starting to see it."

He came off his knees and around with the trowel raised like a sword, which would have been humiliating if anyone from the guild had witnessed it, and found himself facing a woman sitting on the next ward-stone down the line as though it were a garden wall.

She was perhaps forty, dressed in road-leather the color of wet slate, boots built for walking away from things. Her black hair was cut brutally short, and across her left cheek ran a scar in the shape of a sigil — an actual sigil, burned into the skin, a ward-sign of severing, the kind written on the doors of places that wanted never to be opened again. She had been using herself as a door. He understood that immediately, the way he understood all objects: she was a woman who had been *used*, in the technical sense, as an instrument, and had survived the application.

"You're sitting on an active ward," Beatrix said, because his mouth often chose precision over survival.

"I'm sitting on a dead ward," the woman said. "Number seven. Broke it myself eleven years ago. Paid well for it, too." She turned her head and spat, with ceremony, into the grass on the foreign side. "That's the joke nobody tells. You can break us, but you can't unbreak yourself after."

"You're a ward-breaker."

"Sable." She swung down off the stone and landed soft, a big cat in bad boots. "Formerly of the respectable profession of tearing holes in my own country for money, currently of the respectable profession of sitting on stones watching them crack and feeling sick about it."

"Why are you watching them crack?"

"Because I broke one," Sable said, "and eleven years later, the whole line is failing like rotten teeth, and I have done the arithmetic on whose fault that is more times than is good for a person." She nodded at his hands. "You're guild. Thread-sight?"

Beatrix said nothing. Maren's voice in his memory: *a gift named is a gift priced.*

Sable shrugged. "Fine. Keep your secrets. Here's mine, free of charge, since you're going to kneel back down and put your hand on that stone anyway, and you'll see it yourself in about a minute." She walked closer, and up close her eyes were the pale grey of frost on a window, and very tired. "You think these wards keep enemies out. Everyone thinks that. That's what they're *for*, officially. Defense. Border. Big scary rocks keeping the neighbors polite." She crouched, laid her own scarred palm against the base of the cracked stone, and Beatrix watched her flinch — watched her feel, even without the sight, some echo of what poured through it. "They don't keep anything out, boy. They hold everything *in*. Every ward-stone on this line is a stitch in a seam, and the seam is holding the whole country's story shut against the dark. Names. Boundaries. The fact of Hollowmere. Your mother's maiden name. The taste of bread in the east vs. the west. All of it, pinned in place, like—"

"Like a manuscript under a paperweight," Beatrix said quietly.

Something flickered across Sable's face. Respect, maybe, or grief for him. "Yeah. Like that. And I spent eleven years telling myself I broke a *wall*. One wall. Walls get rebuilt. But I didn't break a wall. I unpicked a stitch. And a seam with one stitch gone doesn't fall apart right away — it just starts to *run*, slow, like a ladder in stocking, and everyone keeps wearing the stocking because who looks at their stockings, right?" She laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Then the cracks start. Then the rivers get confused. And then one night soon, the wind comes up under the whole manuscript, and—"

She stopped. Looked at the sky. Looked back at him.

"How many moons does your guild's sigil have?" she asked.

"Twelve," Beatrix said.

"When did you last count them?"

He thought of the mural. Of Oren Vey's face in the dark of the hall, lit by candlelight, doing sums no man should have to do.

"I haven't," he said.

"Count them tonight," Sable said. "Count them, and then come find me, because I'll want to watch your face when you do."

* * *

He repaired the stone anyway. It was what he had come to do, and besides — Sable's story might be a story. Ward-breakers lied; that was presumably most of the job description. So he mixed the mortar of slate and grave-dust, and he fed the silver-that-was-not-silver thread down the fissure while speaking the closing words Maren had drilled into him, the ones that made an object agree to be whole again, and the stone sealed, and the humming steadied, and he sat back on his heels sweating and told himself firmly that the threads he'd seen had simply been the ordinary anchoring-threads of any great working, that of course a border ward's threads would extend inward, that defense required reach—

But the threads hadn't reached outward like a hand pushing away.

They had reached inward like fingers *gripping*.

And now that he had seen it, he could not stop seeing it. He looked south, toward home, and for one vertiginous heartbeat he saw the whole country as the stone saw it: a vast page of writing, dense and beautiful and teeming — every lane a sentence, every family a paragraph, every song a footnote — held flat and still and *legible* by twelve hundred stitches driven into its edges.

Pinned. Like a specimen. Like something that would fly apart, or fade, or wake up and leave, if ever the pins came out.

*What are we,* Beatrix thought, with the first real crack opening in him, quiet and cold as the fissure in the stone, *that we have to be held here?*

"First time?" Sable said gently, from behind him. She had a fire going now, absurdly domestic, and was heating water for tea in a dented tin pot. "Seeing the country like that. It's a bad first time. No lube, no warning."

"How long have you known?"

"Known? Eleven years. *Understood?*" She poured tea into two tin cups and handed him one, and her hand was steady but her voice wasn't, quite. "Since about four days ago. When the stars started dimming at the edges. You've seen it — don't lie, guild-boy, everyone's seen it, they just call it weather." She sipped. "There's a debt coming due. There's always a debt coming due; that's what a country *is*, a debt everyone agrees to keep paying. Only this one—" she tapped the healed fissure with one boot, softly, the way you'd knock at a sick friend's door— "this one somebody else signed for. A long time ago. And whoever signed it used the whole nation as collateral, and used these stones as the seal, and now the creditor's come to collect."

"You don't know that."

"I broke one stitch and watched eleven years of ladder run," Sable said. "I know the shape of the thing that's unraveling, even if I don't know its name." She looked at him over the steam. "Your Guildmaster knows. I saw his face once, years ago, at a guild function — the way a man looks at a bill he's been expecting. You want to know the truth of your pretty guild, apprentice? Ask Oren Vey what the twelfth moon was hiding behind."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the line of stones, faint as breath, the seventh ward — her ward, the dead one — gave a low, grinding sigh, like a seam settling before it splits.

And Beatrix, holding his tea, looked east past the border into the gathering dusk of the neighboring country, and saw — or thought he saw, and would spend the rest of his life being sure — the horizon *end*. Not fog, not distance. End. A straight edge, clean as a page torn out, where the world simply stopped having been written.

"Boy," Sable said quietly, following his gaze, and for the first time she sounded afraid. "How fast can you walk?"
