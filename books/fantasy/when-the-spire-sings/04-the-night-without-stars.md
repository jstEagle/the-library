## 4. The Night Without Stars

*Beatrix*

The key that opens yesterday had been humming in his satchel all evening, and Beatrix had learned by now that when an impossible thing hummed, it was not singing. It was warning.

He sat in the loft above the guildhall's counting room, cross-legged on a pallet he had never quite managed to think of as his own, and copied the day's entries into the master ledger in his careful, slanted hand. *One brass compass that points at doors. One jar of thunder, half-empty. One key — provenance unknown — which opens the day before itself.* The ink dried slowly in the cold. Below him, the guildhall murmured: Bramble arguing with her ovens, Ilex scratching at a map of somewhere that had stopped existing on Tuesday, Oren Vey's boots pacing the length of the great room in a rhythm Beatrix had come to think of as the building's second heartbeat.

He should have been asleep. Instead he was watching the window.

There was no reason to watch the window. The border wards were three days' ride away, and whatever Sable had told him about them — *they don't keep things out, boy, they hold the story in* — had been rattling around his skull like a die in a cup ever since. He kept telling himself she was a ward-breaker, and ward-breakers said dramatic things; it was practically their trade. But he had touched that ward-stone with his thread-sight, and he had felt what she meant. The country was a page, and the wards were its margins, and margins are only lines until someone draws outside them.

The key hummed.

Then it *sang* — one high, thin note, like a wet finger on a glass rim — and every impossible thing in the guildhall sang back. The jar of thunder rolled against its shelf. The compass needle spun and spun and would not settle. Downstairs, the ovens went silent all at once, and Bramble's voice cut off mid-word, and the silence after was the loudest thing Beatrix had ever heard.

He was on his feet without deciding to be. He was down the stairs without feeling the steps.

"Oren—" he started, and then the sky happened.

It did not go dark. It had been dark for hours. But in one breath — one shared, stolen breath, the kind of breath a drowning man takes — every star in the sky went out. Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Ended*, the way a candle ends when pinched, all at once, everywhere, as if some vast hand had closed over the whole bowl of heaven. And where the stars had been there was nothing, and the nothing was not black but *absent*, a color that hurt to look at because it was not a color at all, it was the place where color would have gone if there had been any left.

Beatrix heard Ilex scream from the map room. He heard Bramble drop something ceramic. He heard Oren Vey say, very quietly, in the tone of a man reading a ledger entry he has dreaded for years: "So. It's begun."

But Beatrix was already running for the door, because through the thread-sight he could see what the others could not: the threads. Every thread binding the city together — street to street, lamplight to lamplight, name to name — had gone slack, and out past the eastern wall they were simply *gone*, snipped clean, and beyond the snipping there was no horizon. There was no edge of the world. There was just the moment where the country used to continue, and then a nothing so complete that his eyes slid off it like rain off glass.

"The streets," he heard himself say. "Oren, the streets near the wall—"

He did not wait for permission. He never had, and perhaps that was why the guild had kept him.

* * *

*Sable*

Sable felt the wards fall the way a man feels a tooth leave his jaw: a wrongness, a gap, a tongue that keeps returning to the absence. She had been drinking alone in the Drowned Lantern, three streets from the guildhall, pretending she hadn't spent two days trying to decide whether to warn anyone about anything, when the lamps along the bar guttered in unison and the tavern-keeper's face went briefly, horribly smooth — features present but uncommitted, like a sketch abandoned halfway.

Then the face came back, and the tavern-keeper blinked and said, "Anyone want the last of the stew?" as though nothing had happened, and maybe for him nothing had. That was the worst part. The unwriting didn't announce itself. It just quietly took the seams out of things and let them hold their shapes out of habit.

Sable paid for a drink she hadn't finished and went out into the street.

The sky was empty. She had broken wards before — small ones, crooked ones, the kind hedge-towns threw up against tax-collectors and worse — and she knew what a failing ward looked like from the inside: a shiver, a sag, a slow leak. This was not that. This was a dam going all at once, and she stood in the middle of Copperlane with her heart hammering and thought, *I helped build this. Not this ward. But ones like it. I took coin to unpick the stitches of places, and I told myself it was just work.*

Out past the rooftops, where the eastern road should have run silver under the moon toward the river towns, there was nothing. Not fog. Fog has texture. This was the visual equivalent of a word you've forgotten mid-sentence.

And people were walking toward it.

Not deliberately — nobody walks into nothing on purpose — but the night had turned everyone's feet confused, and a knot of late travelers coming up from the docks were following the eastern road out of habit, and the road was forgetting itself ahead of them faster than they could walk. Sable saw a carter take a step onto cobbles that were no longer cobbles, onto ground that was no longer anything, and his boot found no purchase because there was no purchase to find, and he pitched forward into the absent dark—

—and a boy got there first.

She would remember it afterward in fragments, the way you remember lightning. A slight figure in a patched apprentice coat sprinting down Copperlane with a satchel banging against his hip. A hand shooting out and catching the carter's collar an inch from the edge. The boy hauling backward with more strength than his frame suggested, both of them sprawling onto cobbles that still existed, still remembered being cobbles. And past the boy's shoulder, where the carter's cart had been standing, there was now nothing at all — cart, mule, the stretch of road beneath them, all of it gone between one heartbeat and the next, unwritten so cleanly that even the memory of it seemed secondhand.

The carter lay gasping. The boy knelt over him, gripping his shoulders, saying something Sable couldn't hear — *you're here, you're here, stay here* — and his eyes, when he lifted them, were doing something she had only seen once before, years ago, on the face of a founder's agent: seeing the threads. Seeing exactly how close the man had come to being a sentence that trailed off.

"Well," Sable said to the empty sky, to the dead stars, to the whole unwinding mess of it. "Damn."

She had come to the city to disappear. Instead she grabbed the carter's other arm and helped drag him back toward the guildhall, because apparently that was who she was now.

* * *

*Oren Vey*

Oren had known this night would come for thirteen years, and knowing had prepared him not at all.

He stood in the great hall of the Guild of Impossible Things with the founder's oldest ledger open on the table before him, and he read the entry he had read a hundred times and understood fully for the first time:

*Borrowed against the whole: thirteen turns of the moon. Collateral: everything. When the last turn wanes, the debt comes due, and the creditor collects the country.*

Thirteen turns. Twelve had passed since the bargain was struck — he had counted them himself, had marked each waning on the mural wall with a scratch hidden beneath a layer of paint, a coward's calendar. Tonight the thirteenth moon had risen, swollen and wrong-colored, hanging low over the capital like a coin held too close to the eye. And the wards had fallen, and the stars had gone out, and the horizon had ended, and the guild members were crowding into the great hall with torches lit against a darkness that torches had no business comforting anyone from.

"Guildmaster." Ilex stood in the doorway of the map room, chalk dust on her fingers, her face gray. "The eastern districts. My maps of them are—" She swallowed. "They're blanking. Ink doesn't hold. I drew Marrowgate an hour ago and it's fading while I watch."

"Then we redraw it," Oren said. "As often as it takes."

"It won't matter if—"

"Ilex." He made his voice the calm one, the practiced one, the one that had talked frightened apprentices down from ledges literal and otherwise for thirty years. "Everything matters. That is rather the point of our entire profession."

Bramble came through from the kitchens carrying bread and hard cheese and a kettle of something hot, because Bramble's answer to the end of the world had always been to feed it, and Oren loved her for it in a way he had never found adequate words for. She set the food down among the ledgers and instruments and said, "People are coming. From the east side. They're standing in the square looking at where the horizon used to be, and they're not screaming, which frightens me more than screaming would."

"Let them in," Oren said. "All of them. Open the undercroft."

And then Beatrix came through the doors with a half-drowned-looking carter and, behind them, a woman Oren recognized from a wanted sketch eleven years old — Sable, the ward-breaker, the one his apprentice had met at the border stones and somehow not gotten killed by — and the boy's face was white as paper and set like iron, and he said:

"There are people still out there. On the roads. If the streets forget while they're standing on them—"

"I know," Oren said.

"We have to ring the bell. The guild bell. People know the guild bell — it's been ringing at dusk for two hundred years, it's *in* the city's story, it's one of the threads. If we ring it, they'll come to it. Things that are written into a place hold better than things that aren't. You taught me that."

Had he? Oren tried to remember the lesson and found, to his quiet horror, that the memory had a softness at its edges, a blur, like a page left too long in the rain. He covered it by nodding. "Ring it, then."

Beatrix ran for the bell tower, and Oren watched him go, and thought: *the founder said someone with thread-sight might someday unpick the bargain. He never said whether unpicking it would save us or finish us.*

Outside, the thirteenth moon climbed higher, and the nothing at the edge of the world crept closer, patient as interest accruing.

* * *

*Beatrix*

The bell rope was cold and familiar in his hands, and Beatrix pulled it the way he had pulled it a hundred dusks before, except tonight he pulled it like a man throwing a line to someone drowning.

*Boom.* The sound went out over the rooftops, and through the thread-sight he could see it do what he'd hoped: the sound was a thread, an old thick golden-brown thread stitched through two centuries of evenings, and where it passed over the city the fraying edges pulled tight again, just slightly, just for a moment. Streets remembered themselves under its shadow. Lamps steadied. Somewhere a dog stopped howling at the empty sky.

*Boom.* In the square below, faces turned upward toward the guildhall. Some of them began to move toward the sound, slowly, the way sleepwalkers move, drawn by something older than decision.

*Boom.* And then he saw the child.

A girl, seven or eight perhaps, in a nightgown, barefoot on Copperlane — separated from whoever had been holding her hand when the stars died, standing frozen in the middle of the street while adults streamed past her toward the bell. And beyond her, up the lane, the unwriting was coming. Beatrix could see it with his own eyes now, no sight needed: the far end of Copperlane simply ceasing, housefronts dissolving not into rubble but into *never-having-been*, and the cessation rolling toward the girl like a tide, unhurried, certain.

He did not think. Thinking was for afterward. He dropped the rope mid-swing, took the tower stairs three at a time, hit the street level running, and sprinted down Copperlane toward a child who was about to stand in a place that had forgotten how to be a place.

The air changed as he ran. Behind him, the city. Ahead of him, the *absence* — and it wasn't cold, that was the terrible thing, it wasn't wind or void or any sensation at all, it was the sensory equivalent of a blank page, and his body understood before his mind did that to step into it was to become blank too.

"Hey!" His voice cracked. "Hey! Look at me!"

The girl turned. Her face was the ordinary miracle of a child's face at night — tear-streaked, chin trembling, utterly alive — and Beatrix felt the threads of her, bright and new-minted and barely knotted into the world yet, and something in his chest tore open. *Not her. Anything but her.*

He reached her as the unwriting took the house beside them. He didn't watch it happen; he caught her up, swung her onto his hip the way he vaguely remembered — *did* he remember? the memory flickered, softened, slipped — being carried once, long ago, and turned and ran.

The street fought him. Cobbles vanished under his feet and he stumbled on nothing, on *unbeing*, and the girl screamed and buried her face in his shoulder, and he shouted at the street — actually shouted words, ledger-words, the way he catalogued impossibilities: *"Copperlane! You are Copperlane, you run from the guildhall to the east gate, you have been walked for six hundred years, you are WRITTEN—"*

And whether it was the bell still tolling above, or the words, or the sheer stubborn weight of two lives insisting upon themselves, the cobbles rose up to meet his next stride. Solid. Remembered. He ran on them, and ran, and ran, and burst out of the dying end of the lane into the square, into torchlight, into the crowd flowing toward the guildhall doors, and collapsed to his knees with the girl clutched against him, both of them shaking.

Sable was there. He didn't remember her arriving; she was simply kneeling beside him, one hand on his back, saying nothing, which was somehow exactly right.

"She's all right," Beatrix managed. "She's — check her, make sure she's—"

"I've got her." Sable lifted the girl, who clung to her neck with the ferocity of the rescued. "And you?"

Beatrix opened his mouth to say *fine*, and discovered he didn't know. Something was wrong with his hands; they wouldn't stop trembling. Something was wrong with the night; the stars were still gone, all of them, and the thirteenth moon hung overhead like an unpaid bill. And something was wrong inside him, deeper than either — a faint, fresh hollowness, as though in reaching across the edge of the unwritten he had brushed it, and it had taken a toll. Small. Tiny. A single coin from a full purse.

He couldn't remember, suddenly, what his mother's laugh sounded like.

He was sure he had known it this morning.

Above the square, the guild bell tolled on and on, holding the city together stitch by golden stitch, and Beatrix knelt in the torchlight among strangers and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *It isn't just the borders. It isn't just the villages. It's taking payment from all of us, everywhere, all the time.*

From the top of the tower, Oren Vey was watching the empty sky, and his face, when Beatrix found it, was the face of a man reading the last page of a ledger he had hoped never to balance.

"Inside," the guildmaster called down, and his voice carried strangely in the starless dark. "All of you. There is something you must see — and something I must confess."
