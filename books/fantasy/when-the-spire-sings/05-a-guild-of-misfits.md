## 5. A Guild of Misfits

Sable had broken into better halls than this one, and she had never once been invited into any of them. That was the first strange thing about the night: she climbed the guildhall steps among survivors and refugees, mud to her knees, a child's dried tears on her shoulder where the boy had clung to her during the walk from the square, and no one asked for a password, a toll, or an explanation. The doors simply stood open, golden light spilling down the stone like the hall had been waiting all along for exactly this crowd.

She hated it immediately. Open doors in a night like this were either stupidity or a trap, and Sable had survived forty years of hard living by assuming both.

"Inside," Oren Vey said again, holding the door himself, which was the second strange thing. Guildmasters did not hold doors. Guildmasters sat behind tables stacked with the paperwork of their own importance. This one looked like a man holding shut a wound.

The crowd pressed in — dozens of them, maybe a hundred, the dazed and the weeping and the stubbornly practical, carrying what they'd carried when the sky went dark. A woman with a hatbox she would not set down. Two brothers arguing in whispers about whether their street still existed. The child Beatrix had pulled from the dissolving lane, who had not spoken since and would not let go of Sable's sleeve.

She let him hold it. She told herself it meant nothing.

Inside, the guildhall was nothing like she'd imagined, and she had imagined it often, professionally, over the years — usually while casing its windows. She'd expected marble and guildly pomp, the gilded self-satisfaction of people who traded in wonders. Instead the great hall was worn and warm and cluttered almost past navigation: shelves bowing under ledgers whose spines had been mended and mended again, lamps hung on hooks that had clearly held stranger things, a stuffed crow wearing a tiny hat presiding over the mantel with an air of having earned it. Every surface bore marks of use. Not a museum. A workshop. A kitchen. A home somebody had actually lived in, hard, for a long time.

And everywhere, everywhere, ledgers.

"They're real," muttered a voice at her elbow, and Sable nearly put a knife through the hand attached to it before she recognized Beatrix, pale as tallow, his ledger clutched to his chest the way the woman held her hatbox. He was staring at the shelves. "I thought — I assumed half of it was theater. Guilds are mostly theater. But he keeps *everything*."

"You've been here two years and you didn't know what your own guild kept in its own hall?"

"I was never allowed past the reading room." He said it without accusation, but Sable heard the thing underneath it, the old ache, and looked away because looking at it too long might make her admit she recognized it.

A bell rang somewhere deep in the hall — not the tower bell, a small hand-bell, bright as a struck spoon — and a mountain of a woman leaned over the balcony rail above them, flour to her elbows, hair escaping its scarf in every direction at once.

"Bramble says: sit down, all of you," the woman bellowed, in the tone of someone accustomed to being obeyed by weather. "Bramble says there is soup, and Bramble says nobody starves on the night the stars go out, Bramble's grandmother didn't survive three famines to watch it happen now. Sit! Bowls will find you!"

"Bowls will find you?" Sable said.

And they did. From the kitchens came a procession of bowls — walking, sliding, one abominably whistling — drifting down the stairs and out along the benches like ducks to a pond, steaming, each one settling itself in front of a person with a decisive little thunk. The child beside Sable stared at his bowl. His bowl stared back, if a bowl could be said to stare, and then nudged an inch closer to him in unmistakable encouragement.

He picked up the spoon. He ate. It was, Sable would later reflect, the first miracle of the night that mattered.

* * *

She found Oren Vey at the far end of the hall, doing what guildmasters apparently did instead of holding court: inventorying faces. He stood against a pillar with a lamp turned low, watching the crowd come apart and re-form around the soup, cataloging who was missing, probably, or who was too quiet. Sable had seen that exact stance on smugglers counting their crew after a bad crossing.

"Ward-breaker," he said, without turning. "You came."

"Your apprentice dragged me." She took a position against the same pillar, angled so she could see both doors. Old habits. "Don't flatter yourself into a rescue."

"I wouldn't dare." He turned his lamp up a fraction. In the light his face was worse than it had been on the tower — calmer, which was the problem. Calm like a frozen river. "You told him something at the border stone. Something that made a ward-breaker walk a runaway apprentice back toward the disaster instead of away from it."

"I told him the truth. It was a moment of weakness."

"And the truth was?"

Sable watched the bowls making their rounds. Watched the child laugh, wetly, horribly, at a bowl that bobbed for his attention like a gull. "That the wards don't keep anything *out*, Guildmaster. You know that, don't you? You must. That's what tonight was."

Something moved behind Oren's eyes — not surprise. Confirmation arriving where he'd been praying for contradiction. "Say it plainly."

"The border stones aren't walls. They're stitches. You stitch a hem to keep the cloth from unraveling, yes? Same work. The wards don't hold out enemies. They hold the country *in place*. Its shape. Its story. Whatever word you like." She kept her voice flat, because flatness was the only honest way she knew to carry it. "And tonight somebody pulled the thread."

Oren closed his eyes. For a moment he looked every year of his age and several he had been saving. "Twelve years I've been guildmaster," he said quietly. "Before that, thirty under the one before. And in all that time I told myself the wards were about enemies. Raiders, rival powers, the old wars. It's a comfortable story. We tell comfortable stories to apprentices so they can sleep." He opened his eyes. "What else did you see, at the stone?"

"Gaps." She chose each word like footing on scree. "Roads that loop. A river running backward. People on the far road with no faces yet — just smooth, waiting to be decided. I've seen unwritten land before, Guildmaster; I've made a little of it myself, in my way. This wasn't neglect. This was *appetite*."

* * *

It was past midnight when the crowd finally thinned — the many settled into corners and pews and any horizontal surface, the hatbox woman asleep sitting up with her arms around the box — and the core of the hall showed itself. Sable counted them without meaning to: Oren. Beatrix, curled in a window seat with his ledger open though he hadn't turned a page in an hour. The mountain woman, Bramble, who turned out to be the cook and also, apparently, the mage, though of what school Sable couldn't guess, unless the school was Soup. And a fourth, whom Sable had been actively trying not to look at for two hours, because looking at her felt like trespassing.

She sat at the big worktable under a hanging lamp, drawing. She was older than the others, silver-haired, ink to her wrists, and around her the table was buried in maps — hundreds of them, layered like leaves, and every single one depicting places Sable did not recognize. Coastlines she had never sailed. Cities with names in no alphabet she could read, some labeled in careful script: *gone since spring*. *gone since the frost fair*. *gone; nobody left who says the name*.

"That's Ilex," Beatrix said softly, appearing at Sable's shoulder. "The cartographer. She maps—" He stopped, restarted. "She maps places that don't exist anymore."

"Places that *never* existed, more likely."

"No." Beatrix's voice was very quiet. "Look closer. Look at the dates."

Sable looked, because he asked in the tone of a man handing you something breakable. The nearest map showed a river town, lovingly rendered down to the weathervanes — and dated last autumn. Sable had been through that country last autumn. There was no such town. There was a bend in the river with some stones in it, locals said there'd always just been stones.

"She draws them after," Beatrix said. "From whatever's left. From what people remember, and what the stones remember, and — she says the ground holds a shape for a while after the story leaves it. Like warmth in a bed." He swallowed. "I used to think her maps were the guild's most useless trade. Maps to nowhere."

"And now?"

"Now I think they might be the only honest maps in the world."

Across the room, Ilex looked up from her work as if she'd heard — perhaps she had; perhaps cartographers hear everything — and met Sable's eyes. She didn't smile. She gave a single nod, the nod of one professional acknowledging another's presence in her workspace, and returned to a coastline only she had ever seen.

* * *

It was Bramble who broke the silence that follows soup, by slamming a second pot onto the table and announcing, "Right. Since none of you sleeping soldiers are going to ask, I'll say it plain: the boy figured it out on the tower stair, and he's been chewing it alone ever since, and Bramble did not raise him to chew alone." She pointed a wooden spoon at Beatrix like a marshal's baton. "Out with it."

"I'm not—" Beatrix began.

"You are. Spoon's waiting."

There was laughter in the hall then, thin and startled, the first laughter since darkfall — brief, quickly smothered, but real. And Sable watched Beatrix absorb it the way a starving man absorbs bread: carefully, suspiciously, as though it might be taken back.

He opened his ledger instead of answering. Turned to a page near the middle. Set it on the table and spun it to face them all.

"Two years of entries," he said. "I logged what the guild traded in, because that was my job, and I logged it honestly, and I thought honesty was enough. A key that opens yesterday. A ladder for a widow that reaches her son's window in a city she's banned from. Rain sold by the barrel to a drought parish at a fair price. A song returned to a man who'd lost it." His finger moved down the page. "I thought these were oddities. Charities. Curiosities. The guild's little vanities." He looked up, and Sable saw how much it cost him to say the next part, saw the young man dismantling his own understanding brick by brick. "But the entries have a pattern, and I'm a ledger-keeper, patterns are my whole trade. Every impossible thing this guild has ever traded does *work*. Maintenance work. The key that opens yesterday — yesterday is where erased things go first, before they're gone entirely. The rain — weather is one of the easiest stories to lose; parishes that forget rain stop having it. The song—" his voice caught, steadied— "a song is a memory that learned to carry itself. Every entry. Every one. We weren't trading curiosities. We were *patching*. Patching holes in the story before they spread. All of it. The whole absurd guild catalogue. It was repair."

Silence held the hall. The stuffed crow on the mantel seemed, if possible, to listen.

"Well," said Bramble, into the hush. "He sees it."

"He was always going to," said Oren. "It's why we took him."

"Why *did* you take him?" Sable asked sharply, and heard her own voice come out harder than intended, because something in her had been circling that question all night. "A hedge-witch's runaway with a talent for bookkeeping. Two years and you kept him below stairs, logging trinkets. If he's so vital to the pattern, why hide him from the work?"

The lamplight moved. Nobody answered fast enough, and Sable had spent a lifetime reading rooms where everyone was choosing between truths, and this room was drowning in them.

"That," Oren said at last, slowly, "is part of what I have to confess. And I'll confess it — before this hall and everyone in it, tomorrow, when there's daylight enough to see faces. Tonight isn't for my sins. Tonight, the question is simpler." He looked around at the four of them, at the sleeping strangers, at the child. "The country is coming undone. The wards are down. Whatever bought us the time we've lived inside is calling in its debts. So: the guild has always been misfits. Ward-breakers, map-makers, cooks with the wrong kind of grandmother, apprentices nobody wanted. People who fit nowhere else. It seems fitting — it seems *cruelly* fitting — that the last job falls to us." He straightened, and for a moment Sable saw the guildmaster the legends described. "Stay, and fight, if you will. The door stands open for anyone who'd rather run. No ledger records shame for those who choose the road."

Nobody moved. Sable noted, with professional irritation, that neither did she.

Bramble snorted and began stacking bowls with a crash that ended all speeches. Ilex turned her lamp brighter and kept drawing. And Beatrix sat with his ledger open, staring at his own entries as if seeing, for the first time, the shape of the hands that had written around his life before he arrived.

Sable pushed off the pillar, meaning to find a wall to sleep against, and found the child standing in front of her instead, bowl in hand, looking up.

"You're staying?" he asked. First words since the square.

"Don't know yet," Sable said.

"Oh." He considered this with terrible seriousness. "The bowl likes you."

She looked down at the bowl. The bowl, traitorously, gave a small hopeful wobble.

* * *

Later — much later, in the smallest hours when even grief sleeps badly — Sable gave up on sleep and wandered the hall's upper gallery, checking exits, because that was who she was, and stopped at the sound of voices below.

Ilex and Beatrix, at the worktable. The cartographer had laid a fresh sheet before the boy, and was speaking low and steady, the tone of a teacher who has decided the curriculum can wait no longer.

"—the oldest maps, the ones under everything. Your founder drew them. And in the margins, every time, the same notation. Thirteen strokes, and a word in cipher I cracked twenty years ago." Ilex's ink-stained finger rested on a margin dense with tiny characters. "It says *installments*, boy. It says the country has been paid out in installments, and someone has been making the payments."

Beatrix went white in the lamplight. "Paid to *whom*?"

"That," said Ilex, "is the wrong question. The right one is written here too." She turned the oldest map fully into the light, and Sable, leaning over the gallery rail, felt her breath stop.

For drawn beneath the founding lines of every settlement, every road, every ward-stone in the country — beneath the entire nation, sketched in the founder's own hand — ran a single line of script, repeated like a refrain:

*A debt is a story told twice.*

And beneath it, fresher, in ink that could not have been more than a day old, though the map was centuries old, a new line had appeared:

*Final payment due at the turn of the thirteenth moon.*
