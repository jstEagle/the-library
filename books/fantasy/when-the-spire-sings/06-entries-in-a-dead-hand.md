## 6. Entries in a Dead Hand

The guildhall slept the way a wounded animal sleeps: fitfully, one ear cocked toward the dark. Beatrix lay in the narrow cot they had given him — third door past the kitchens, a room that still existed, which felt like an extravagance these days — and listened to the building breathe around him.

It did breathe. He had learned that much in his weeks under its roof. The guildhall was older than any of them and stitched through with threads so old they had gone grey with use, like a beloved shawl worn thin at every fold. At night those threads hummed faintly, and if he let his thread-sight open just a crack, he could see them: thousands of filaments running up through the foundations, out through the walls, into the country beyond. Each one anchored to something the guild had once recorded. A key that opened yesterday. A bell that rang only for liars. A jar of last summer's rain that never emptied because someone, somewhere, still remembered the storm it fell in.

Maintenance work on reality. That was what Sable had called it, and Beatrix had laughed at first, because surely that was too large a thing for a hall full of misfits who argued about supper and kept a goat that could only be seen by people who owed it money. But then the stars had gone out, and the horizon had learned to end, and no one laughed about anything anymore.

He could not sleep. His mind kept turning over the ledger entries, his own neat handwriting from years of apprenticeship, each impossible thing catalogued with a date and a thread-count and a small note on where it bound into the world. He had thought he was keeping records. He had thought he was a clerk.

Now, in the dark, he admitted what he had been circling since the night the wards fell: he wanted to know *why*. Oren Vey knew more than he said. The Guildmaster's calm was not calm; it was a coat worn over something that flinched. And the mural on the eastern wall of the great chamber — twelve moons carved by hands centuries dead — now showed thirteen, pale as a scar, appearing overnight like frost on glass. Beatrix had seen Oren stand before it three mornings running, saying nothing to anyone, his thumb pressed against the thirteenth moon the way a man presses a bruise to confirm it is real.

So Beatrix did what he always did when the world frightened him. He went to find the paperwork.

* * *

The guild's archive occupied the cellar levels, and the cellars went deeper than the building above them had any right to be deep. Bramble swore the lower galleries had been excavated rather than built — "dug down into the mountain's memory," she said, "and you can taste it in the dust" — and she was right; even without opening his sight fully, Beatrix could feel the weight of the stone around him, dense with old bindings, old bargains, old names.

He carried a lamp and the master key ring, which he was technically not permitted to remove from Oren's study but which Oren, in his distraction, had left lying beside a cold teacup. Guilt sat in Beatrix's chest like a swallowed stone. He told himself he would return everything by morning. He told himself he was doing this for all of them.

The archive proper was a cathedral of shelving: iron racks rising four stories into the gloom, ladders on brass rails, drawers upon drawers upon drawers, each labeled in scripts spanning five centuries. The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things, complete. Every entry Beatrix had ever made lived here, copied fair into volumes bound in green leather, alongside the work of every apprentice back to the founding.

He started where any honest researcher would start: at the beginning.

The founder's name was given only as **A. Merrow, First Keeper**. The earliest ledger was a thin thing, water-stained, its spine cracked like an old man's knuckles. The handwriting inside was beautiful — a flowing, confident hand that slanted slightly leftward, as though the writer leaned perpetually away from the page, away from whatever they were setting down. Beatrix read the first entries with a scholar's hunger.

*A key that opens yesterday. Bound: the east gate of Harrowmere. Thread-count: nine. Note: the gate remembers being closed.*

*A bell that rings only for liars. Bound: the assize court at Candleford. Thread-count: eleven. Note: it has rung twice this year already.*

Ordinary miracles. The guild's bread and butter, before bread became complicated. He turned pages, following the founder's hand forward through decades, watching it change — growing smaller, tighter, the leftward lean becoming a hunch. Somewhere around the two-hundredth leaf, the ink changed color. It went from brown to a black so absolute it seemed to drink the lamplight rather than reflect it.

And the entries stopped being about keys and bells.

Beatrix stood very still in the aisle, the lamp trembling in his grip, and read:

*Paid: the memory of my mother's kitchen. Received: one turn of the moon. Balance outstanding: twelve.*

*Paid: the memory of my brother's laugh. Received: one turn of the moon. Balance outstanding: eleven.*

*Paid: the sound of rain on the roof of the house I was born in. Received: one turn. Outstanding: ten.*

His first thought was that someone had hidden fiction inside the archive — a novel, a madman's fantasy, slipped between true ledgers as a joke. But his eyes were not ordinary eyes. He let his thread-sight widen, carefully, the way Sable had taught him to breathe through it, and looked at the book itself.

The pages were threaded. Every single page ran through with a filament of the same grey-black as the threads anchoring the country, and each filament ran *out* of the book — out through the shelf, down through the floor, into the deep roots of the world — and along those filaments, faint and terrible, moved something. Not information. *Absence.* Packaged absence, parcel after parcel after parcel, traveling outward to some recipient he could not see, traded away page by page.

One memory per page. One turn of the moon per payment.

Thirteen turns. A thirteenth moon on a wall that had shown twelve for six hundred years.

"Oh," Beatrix said aloud, and his voice sounded very young in the vaulted dark. "Oh, no."

* * *

He did not remember climbing the ladders or crossing the gallery. He found himself two floors up, in the section marked **FOUNDING DOCUMENTS — RESTRICTED**, with the dead man's black-inked ledger hugged against his chest like something rescued from a fire. His heart was going hard. The lamp threw his shadow enormous across the shelves.

There had to be more. A trade like this — a *country* traded, because that was what the arithmetic meant, wasn't it? Thirteen moons of borrowed time purchased with the nation's own memories, its own story paid out in installments until there was nothing left to pay with — such a bargain would have a contract. Contracts had counterparts. Counterparts were filed.

The restricted case stood at the gallery's end, behind a grille of wrought iron worked into the shapes of interlocking moons — twelve of them, Beatrix noted, twelve, the smith who'd forged it had known nothing of a thirteenth. The lock was old and proud and expected to be picked by force. Beatrix was not strong. But among the impossible things he had catalogued in his first month of apprenticeship was a skeleton key that opened any lock *which believed itself deserved*, and he had written in his notes, in his own careful hand: *thread-count fourteen; note: the lock must want to be honest.* He had carried that key ever since, half-superstitiously, in his breast pocket, because it was the most beautiful thing he had ever recorded.

The grille's lock saw the key and sighed open like a door admitting family.

Inside, the case held perhaps thirty volumes, and every one of them was wrong. Not damaged — *wrong*, the way the horizon was wrong now, the way a street that forgot itself mid-step was wrong. Their spines bore titles in the founder's beautiful hand: *Terms of Continuance. Schedule of Payments, Revised. On the Nature of the Creditor. Final Provisions.*

Beatrix opened *Terms of Continuance*, and the lamplight bent away from the pages as though the book were colder than the air around it.

It was a contract. Of course it was. Everything was contracts; the whole world was contracts; he had simply never understood that the ground itself might be signatory. The language was archaic and precise, and Beatrix, who had spent years learning to read thread-script off the singing spire and ledger-marks off ward-stones, parsed it line by line while his blood went slow and cold.

**Whereas** the realm called [here the word was not blanked but *unwritten*, a smooth absence in the vellum where a name should be, and Beatrix's throat closed, because he realized he could not supply it, could not remember what the country was called, could not recall ever having known] stands in peril of dissolution;

**and whereas** the undersigned First Keeper has found a creditor willing to extend continuance;

**let it be agreed**: that the realm's continuance shall be secured for the span of thirteen lunar turns; that payment shall be rendered in the coin of memory, drawn first from the undersigned, thereafter from the realm entire, in order and proportion as the creditor shall determine; that upon exhaustion of the realm's remembered self, the debt shall be satisfied by dissolution, the realm's story passing wholly to the creditor; and that no amendment shall hold except one made in the creditor's presence, in the heart of that which sings, by a hand able to see the threads thereof.

A hand able to see the threads.

Beatrix read that clause four times. His lips moved on the fifth.

Thread-sight. The gift Maren had found in him when he was a child pulling loose strings out of her hedge-charms and reweaving them better than she'd tied them. The gift she had guarded jealously, hidden, lied about to everyone who came asking. *You don't show that kind of thing to strangers, boy,* she used to say, gripping his shoulder hard enough to leave marks. *People who know what you can do will want to use you, and I'm the only one allowed to do that.*

He had run away from her because she wanted to sell him. That was why. Wasn't it? The memory should have been right there — the argument, the door, the road — but when he reached for it he found it strangely smooth, like a river stone, like something handled so often the details had worn away. He pushed the feeling down. Later. There would be time to grieve his childhood later, if there was a later.

Because here was the terrible shape of it, assembling itself in the dark of the archive like a figure walking out of fog: the founder had bought thirteen turns with memories, and when his own ran out, the contract drew from the realm. From everyone. From grandmothers and children and the woman who sold apples at the Candleford market. One memory per page. The black-inked ledger was not a record of the payments.

It was the payments. The book *was* the debt, threaded through with the realm's stolen past, and somewhere a creditor was receiving it installment by installment, and the installments had been coming due for six hundred years, and the thirteenth moon on the wall meant the account was nearly settled.

Nearly. Not yet. One clause remained unspent: *no amendment shall hold except one made in the creditor's presence, in the heart of that which sings, by a hand able to see the threads thereof.*

Amendment. The word sat in him like a lit coal. You could not break this contract — the founder had surely tried; the final provisions volume would tell him how badly that had gone. But it could be amended. Rewritten. If someone reached the heart of the spire, and if the creditor could be brought there, and if that someone could see the threads well enough to unpick a signature and tie a new one —

That someone would need thread-sight. Strong thread-sight. The strongest anyone knew of.

The kind of sight that people had once tried to buy.

* * *

He found the apprenticeship entry an hour later, near dawn, because he could not stop looking, because betrayal is a wound that keeps asking to be touched.

It was in the roster volume — the plainest book in the case, a simple register of every soul the guild had ever taken in. He almost skipped it. But his eye caught on a date he recognized, and he turned back, and there it was.

*Received into the Guild this day: Beatrix Dunmore, apprentice, aged eleven years, gifted in thread-perception. Sponsor: Maren Ostler, hedge-witch of the fenmarch. Consideration received: one memory, drawn per schedule.*

Below, in the founder's beautiful leftward-slanting hand, long dead these three centuries, long dead and somehow still signing:

*A. Merrow, First Keeper.*

Beatrix sat down on the floor of the archive, among the shelves of the unmade, and held the open book, and did not cry, because crying required knowing what to weep for and his grief had lost its address.

Maren had signed him over. His own entry — the beginning of his life at the guild, the first place he had ever felt he might belong — was a *payment*. He was not an apprentice who happened to work in an archive. He was an asset, entered into the ledger like the key that opened yesterday, bound and threaded and filed. The founder had taken him as consideration, part of the schedule, a hand able to see the threads, held in trust until the final turn came due.

And Oren. Oren, who pressed his thumb against the thirteenth moon. Oren, who had taken Beatrix in with such particular gentleness, who taught him ledger-script and never asked why he sometimes woke screaming about doors. Had Oren known what the boy in his care actually was? Had he fed him breakfast for eight years while counting down to the moment the contract collected?

The lamp guttered. Above him, faintly, through six floors of stone, the spire began its dawn tone — the low, sweet, sorrowful note it had sung every morning since the stars went out. Lately Beatrix had begun to fancy he could hear words in it, or nearly hear them, the way you can nearly remember a dream.

Now he knew why. The spire sang in ledger-script. The spire was reading the accounts aloud.

Beatrix closed the founder's ledger and pressed it flat against his chest and felt, beneath the binding, the slow pulse of a country's stolen memories moving along their threads — moving outward, page by page, toward a creditor waiting at the end of the count.

One turn of the moon left. Perhaps less.

He stood, snuffed the lamp, and climbed toward the light with the dead man's handwriting warm against his heart, carrying the worst knowledge of his life: that he had been sold before he could walk, that his belonging had always been a line item, and that the only hand in the world able to rewrite the bargain was the one the bargain had bought.

He would make it to the spire. He would find the creditor. And he would put a pen through every line of that contract if it was the last thing his remembering self ever did.

In the great chamber above, the thirteenth moon waited on the wall, and beneath it, unaware of footsteps approaching, Guildmaster Oren Vey stood with his back to the door, whispering a name over and over — as if rehearsing it — a name that was Beatrix's.
