## 7. The Spire Begins to Sing

Ilex had mapped every street of Ashfall twice before she was twenty — once in ink, once in memory, because her grandmother taught her that a map was only as honest as the hand that could redraw it from nothing. She thought of that lesson now, standing in the guildhall's eastern window with her charcoal hovering over fresh vellum, because the spire had begun to sing and no one had told her what to do when the world started reading itself aloud.

It began, as most terrible things did in the guildhall, with Bramble.

"The bread's gone strange," the cook-mage announced from the kitchen doorway, flour to his elbows, his voice pitched in that careful register he used when he wanted to seem calm and fooled no one. "Not spoiled-strange. *Forgotten*-strange. I went to make the honey loaves my mother made, and I couldn't — Iles, I couldn't remember if she'd used rye or wheat. I could see her hands. I could see the kitchen. But the grain just... wasn't there anymore."

Ilex set down her charcoal. Her hands were always steady; that was the curse and craft of her. "Your mother," she said slowly. "What was her name?"

Bramble opened his mouth.

The silence that followed lasted four full seconds. Ilex counted them the way she counted everything, because counting was a kind of wall you could hide behind, and behind that wall something cold was already moving through her chest like water finding the cracks in a cellar floor.

"I'll write it down," she said. "Everything you can still reach. Write it all down before it goes."

And then, as though her words had been an invitation rather than a warning, the spire sang.

* * *

No one who heard it ever managed to agree on what the first note sounded like. Oren swore it was a bell struck underwater. Sable said it was the sound a ward-stone makes in the last instant before it breaks — which Ilex privately suspected said more about Sable's past than about the spire. Beatrix, pale and sleepless at the long table where he kept the guild ledgers, said only: "It sounds like a pen."

But Ilex was a cartographer, and cartographers do not hear the world the way other people do. They hear it as lines. And what came down from the spire that morning was not music at all.

It was script.

She felt it before she understood it — that familiar itch behind the eyes, the one that meant information was trying to become geometry. The tone poured through the open windows and into the room, and where it touched surfaces it left faint traceries, silver-pale, like frost forming in summer: along the table's edge, across the flagstones, up the doorframes. And the traceries were letters. Not letters in any alphabet she knew, but she knew them anyway, the way she knew north without a compass — because they were ledger-script, the same looping notation that filled the columns Beatrix tended, the same characters the guild had used for three hundred years to record things that could not otherwise be recorded.

The spire was reading aloud. It was reciting the country's accounts.

"Beatrix," she said, and her voice came out flat and strange, the voice she used for coordinates. "Can you read this?"

He was already standing. He crossed the hall to the window ledge where the frost-script lay thickest, and Ilex watched his face while he read — watched the color leave it in stages, like a tide going out.

"'Item,'" he read, in a whisper. "'One village, called Harrowmere. Population ninety-one souls. One mill. One well of sweet water. One bell, cracked but beloved.'" He stopped. His throat worked. "'Status: unmade.'"

"Harrowmere," Bramble repeated blankly. "Where's Harrowmere?"

Ilex turned to her maps.

This was her part now. This was why the guild kept a cartographer who could hold whole cities in her head like other people held songs. She spread the great survey chart of the realm across the long table, weighting its corners with inkpots, and ran her finger west-northwest from the city, past the river bends, into the low green hills where — where there should have been a village. There was a village. She had mapped it herself, eleven years ago, a scatter of roofs around a millpond, and she had drunk cider in its tavern and argued with its reeve about the true course of a footpath.

Her finger found the hills.

The hills were empty. Not blank — worse than blank. The parchment there showed the faint ghost of erased ink, a shadow where writing had been lifted away, and around the absence the neighboring features had subtly rearranged themselves, roads curving to close the gap, the river sliding eastward to fill the space where the millpond had been. The map was healing over the wound. The map did not want to remember either.

"It was here," she said. Her finger pressed against the ghost until the paper dimpled. "Harrowmere was *here*. Ninety-one souls, Bramble. A mill. A well. A cracked bell someone loved."

"There's never been a village there," Sable said from the doorway, arms crossed, in the tone of someone reporting weather. "I've walked those hills."

"You've walked them since," Ilex snapped, with a fierceness that surprised even her. "You've walked them after."

* * *

By midday they had established the pattern, because the spire did not sing once. It sang in entries — a tone, a pause, a tone — each phrase blooming somewhere new in the guildhall's stone, and each entry naming another thing the country had lost. Ilex stationed herself at the table with fresh vellum and began the work that would define the rest of her life, though she did not know it yet: she transcribed.

Beatrix read the script aloud as it formed. Oren stood at the far end of the hall with his hands clasped behind his back, wearing the expression of a man listening to a debt collector itemize his household goods, and said nothing at all. Sable prowled the perimeter. Bramble cooked, because Bramble always cooked when the world ended — "people grieve better fed," he said, and shoved bowls of stew into their hands whether they wanted them or not — and between servings he called out names of dishes he could no longer source, ingredients whose names dissolved on his tongue even as he reached for them.

By evening the list had grown long enough that Ilex stopped writing it as a list and started writing it as a map, because a list of the dead is unbearable and a map of absences is — not bearable, exactly, but *workable*. You could work with a map. You could stand at a map.

She marked Harrowmere with a small hollow circle. Then Thistlefen, a market town of six hundred. Then the bridge at Coldwether, which she had crossed in spring. Then the whole of the Vale of Alders, forty square miles of orchards and hamlets, gone so completely that the mountains beyond it had shifted southward on her chart by half a degree, and she only caught the error because she had memorized that half-degree the way other women memorized lullabies.

"Say the names back to me," Beatrix said at some point. He had pulled his chair beside hers and was staring at the growing archipelago of hollow circles. "The villages. Say them out loud. If we say them, they're still —"

"Thistlefen," Ilex said.

"Thistlefen," Beatrix echoed. "Coldwether. Harrowmere."

"Harrowmere," said Bramble from the kitchen, uncertainly, like a man testing ice.

Outside, the spire sang the next entry, and it was not a village.

* * *

"'Item,'" Beatrix read, and his voice had begun to fray at the edges, worn thin as old thread. "'One song, sung at harvest, called *The Reaping Bell*. Words in the old tongue. Melody carried between villages for nine hundred years. Status: unmade.'"

He paused. In the pause, Ilex became aware of a terrible emptiness in her own head — a shape where something should have been, the way a missing tooth leaves a hole the tongue keeps returning to. She tried to hum. She had known that song. Every child raised within a hundred miles of Ashfall knew that song; it was the song you sang while the last fields were cut, and her grandmother had sung it off-key on purpose because she claimed the old tune was too pretty for such honest work.

Ilex could remember her grandmother singing. She could remember the off-key-ness. The tune itself was simply not there, and when she reached for it, her mind slid off into smooth blankness, like a boot finding no foothold on wet rock.

"It took the song," she said. "Not the singers. The *song*." Her voice did something humiliating halfway through the sentence, a crack, a wobble, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eye until it behaved. "Do you understand what that means? It isn't taking places and letting the people go free. It takes them *together*. And then it takes the words out of our mouths afterward. It's not erasing the country, Beatrix. It's erasing our ability to mourn it properly. You can't grieve what you can't name."

"That's the design," Oren said.

Everyone turned. The Guildmaster had not spoken in hours. He stood beneath the great mural on the western wall — twelve moons in a ring, painted by some founder-era hand, and beneath it, the thirteenth moon that had appeared overnight, still raw and wrong, its paint seeming to darken slightly whenever Ilex looked directly at it, like a bruise remembering itself.

"The trade," Oren continued quietly, "was paid in memories. I told you that much. What I did not tell you — what perhaps I did not let myself understand — is that the payment does not come only from the payer." His hands, clasped behind him, were white-knuckled. "A country is a shared possession. Its story lives in ten million heads at once. When you sell a piece of a story that is held by everyone..." He closed his eyes briefly. "Everyone pays. The ledger balances across the whole population. That is why the names go from our mouths. We are all of us guarantors of the debt."

"You *knew*," Sable said. It was not a question and it was not shouted; it was delivered with the flat precision of a knife laid on a table. "You knew it would eat outward. From the borders in. From the books into the mouths."

"I knew the terms," Oren said. "I have spent thirty years learning what the terms *meant*." For one moment the practiced calm slipped, and underneath it Ilex saw something that frightened her more than the empty hills on her map: an old man looking at the wreckage of a choice he had helped make when he was young enough to believe desperation was the same as necessity. "I am sorry," he said, to no one, to everyone. "I am so deeply sorry."

Then he straightened, and the calm returned like a lid on a pot, and he said, "Continue the transcription. All of it. Every entry. If the spire is keeping accounts, then we keep them too. Two ledgers are harder to lose than one."

* * *

They worked through the night. Ilex did not remember deciding to work through the night; she simply looked up at some point and the windows were black and the candles had burned down to pools and her right hand ached in a way that would linger for days, and the vellum in front of her was dense with hollow circles and hollow squares and, near the end, hollow stars — because the entries had changed again sometime past midnight.

"'Item,'" Beatrix read, hoarse now, wrapped in a blanket Bramble had forced on him hours ago. "'One constellation, called the Shepherd's Crook. Seven stars. Status: unmade.'"

Ilex put down her pen.

She went to the window. Above the guildhall's roof, the autumn sky wheeled, and she found the region of heaven where the Shepherd's Crook should have hung, that modest crook of seven stars that farmers had steered by since before there were farmers. The sky there was whole. No gap, no wound. Just stars — different arrangements, unfamiliar patterns flowing smoothly into one another, a sky with no crook in it anywhere, a sky that had never needed one.

The sky had healed too. The sky did not want to remember either.

"They're gone," she said. "Even the light. Even the *light* is gone, and starlight travels centuries to get here. Whatever ate it ate it backward through time as well as space." She laughed, once, a small horrible sound. "I mapped that constellation when I was nine years old. My first chart. My grandmother pinned it above the hearth. I could draw every star in my sleep."

"Draw it now," Beatrix said suddenly, urgently, turning in his chair. "Ilex. Draw it anyway. If the sky won't hold it, your vellum can. That's what a map *is* — it's a place where forgetting doesn't get a vote."

So she drew the Shepherd's Crook from memory, seven stars, the slight bend at the handle that no one else had ever bothered to render correctly, and she labeled it in ledger-script copied from the spire's own frost-writing, and she wrote beneath it: *Status: remembered.* It was the smallest act of rebellion imaginable, a woman with aching fingers marking a circle of charcoal on a scrap of sheepskin. It was also, Ilex understood with sudden total clarity, the most important work being done anywhere in the country tonight.

Because the spire sang the truth. The spire sang the losses, faithfully, terribly, entry after entry — and grief, she realized, grief was not the opposite of the erasure. Grief was the *record* of it. To hurt this much was proof that something had been here. The pain in Bramble's face when he forgot his mother's grain was itself a kind of monument. The hollow circles on her map were not absences. They were gravestones, and gravestones insisted: *someone was here.*

"We say the names," she said, standing, her voice steadier than it had any right to be. "Every entry, we say the names back. Thistlefen. Coldwether. Harrowmere. The Reaping Bell. The Shepherd's Crook. We say them until our throats give out, and then we write them, and then we say them again tomorrow. Let the spire keep its accounts. We'll keep ours."

Bramble, flour-dusted and red-eyed, raised his ladle like a toast. "Harrowmere," he said firmly. "Ninety-one souls. Good well water."

"Harrowmere," said Sable, surprising everyone including, visibly, herself.

"Harrowmere," whispered Oren Vey, and the word seemed to cost him more than it cost any of them, though none of them yet knew why.

* * *

Near dawn, Beatrix stopped reading mid-entry.

Ilex looked up from her charts and saw him frozen at the window, one hand pressed flat against the frost-script crawling along the sill, his lips moving, his face draining toward grey. The latest entry must have been long, because the tone sustained and sustained, and the script kept coming, line after line after line, far longer than any village or song or constellation had warranted.

"What is it?" she asked. "What does it say?"

Beatrix did not answer at first. When he finally spoke, his voice was barely there at all.

"It's a name," he said. "A single name. It's been repeating it for — I don't know how long. Over and over. Same entry, again and again, like it's stuck." He swallowed. "It says: '*Item: one apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things. Status:*'" He stopped. Started again. "*'Status pending.'*"

The tone faded. The frost-script on the sill shimmered, held, and did not fade with it. The entry hung there in the pre-dawn light, unfinished, waiting — the first entry the spire had ever sung that was not yet true.

Pending.

Ilex met Beatrix's eyes across the ruined candlelight and the grave-map of a disappearing country, and saw her own fear reflected there, sharpened to a point.

The spire had begun listing a name it hadn't finished erasing. Which meant somewhere in the unwritten dark, the pen had been lowered — and was hovering.
