## 8. Bargaining with the Moon

Oren Vey had spent sixty years learning how to lie with furniture.

It was a guild skill, though no one ever wrote it down in the ledgers: the way you arranged a room told people what to believe about it. A cluttered desk said *busy, don't disturb me*. A swept hearth said *nothing has gone wrong here*. So on the morning after the stars went out, Oren rose before anyone else and did exactly what he always did — he banked the fire, filled the kettle, set six cups on the long table instead of five, because the seventh had been unmade along with everything east of the Harrow Road and he could not bear to look at the empty place where it used to sit.

Then he took down the key Beatrix Dunmore had logged three weeks ago — the one that opened yesterday — and sat with it in his hands while the guildhall slept, and let himself feel, for the length of one kettle's worth of steam, the full weight of what he had done.

The key was small. Brass, or something that remembered being brass. It was warm the way a sleeping bird is warm, and when he turned it over in his palm he could feel the faint tick of it, like a heart counting backward. He had interrogated it, in the way of guildsmen: held it to candlelight, weighed it against standard measures of impossible, asked it politely who had forged it and when. The key answered the only way such objects ever answer. It showed him yesterday.

He had seen himself, forty years younger, standing in a cellar beneath this very hall, holding a lantern while an old man with ink-stained fingers wrote in a ledger that drank light.

Oren closed his fist around the key until the ticking stopped.

*You knew,* he told himself. *You have always known. You simply never called it by its name.*

* * *

Beatrix found him there at first light, which was no longer a reliable phrase, since nothing outside had reliably been light or dark for two days now. The boy came down the stairs the way he came everywhere — quietly, apologetically, like someone perpetually asking permission to occupy space. He had ink on his knuckles and shadows under his eyes, and he paused at the bottom step with that look Oren knew too well: the look of an apprentice deciding whether the Guildmaster's stillness was an invitation or a wall.

"You're up early," Beatrix said.

"I'm up late," Oren said. "There's a difference. Sit."

The boy sat. He looked at the key on the table between them and did not touch it, because Beatrix Dunmore had better manners around impossible things than most guildmasters Oren had trained, and that included Oren himself, if he was honest, which he was trying very hard not to be this morning.

"The thirteenth moon," Beatrix said. Not a question. The boy had seen it too, then. Everyone had, eventually — the pale extra disc that hung low over the western hills like a coin someone had nailed to the sky, visible even through cloud, even through walls if you stared hard enough at the backs of your own eyelids.

"Yes," Oren said.

"It wasn't there before."

"No."

"And the wards fell the same night it appeared."

"Yes."

Beatrix turned his cup slowly on the table, once, twice. Oren watched him do the arithmetic. The boy couldn't help it; it was how his gift expressed itself. Where other people saw objects, Beatrix saw threads — the fine bright lines that bound every thing to every story ever told about it. He would be seeing them now, Oren thought. Seeing the threads that ran from the key on the table up through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the mural in the north hall, through Oren's own chest, and out into the dark where a thirteenth moon hung like an unpaid bill.

"Guildmaster," Beatrix said carefully, "when I logged the key, you told me some trades were maintenance work. Keeping things mended." He hesitated. "What were we maintaining?"

And there it was. Sixty years of lying with furniture, undone by one apprentice asking one honest question.

Oren poured the tea. His hands were steadier than they deserved to be.

"I'm going to tell you a story," he said, "and I want you to understand that I have told it to no one alive. Not because it's secret. Because it's shameful, and shame is heavier than secrecy, and I am a coward in the specific way that clever old men are cowards — I hide things so well I forget where I've put them."

He set the kettle down.

"The founder's name was Alveric Dune. You'll have seen the portrait in the north hall, the one where he looks stern and slightly damp. That painting is a lie; he laughed constantly and hated rain. He founded this guild two hundred years ago, after the Sundering War, when the country was — " Oren searched for the word and hated every candidate. "*Dying.* Not conquered. Not burned. Dying the way a language dies when its last speaker falls asleep. Borders that wouldn't hold their shape. Rivers forgetting which way was downhill. People waking up unable to remember their children's faces, and worse — the children unable to remember they'd ever had parents at all."

Beatrix had gone very still. It was the stillness of thread-sight, Oren realized; the boy was watching something none of them could see.

"He tried everything," Oren went on. "Wards, songs, monuments, ten thousand small impossibilities hammered into the fabric of the land like nails into a splitting hull. And it bought time, but time was all it bought. So Alveric went looking for something larger to bargain with. And he found it. Or it found him. There's a difference, at that scale, and it doesn't matter anymore."

"The thirteenth moon," Beatrix whispered.

"The thirteenth moon," Oren agreed. "Or whatever wears its shape. It lent us thirteen turns. Thirteen months, boy, that's all — a year and a month of borrowed existence. In exchange, it takes payment. Do you know what currency a thing like that deals in?"

The boy's face had gone grey. "Memories," he said. "One per page."

Oren nodded slowly. "Alveric signed for the whole country. Every soul in it became collateral — every memory, every name, every story, held as surety against the debt. And when the thirteen turns run out, the loan is called, and the country is unwritten, and no one will ever have grieved it because no one will remember there was anything to grieve." He lifted his tea and found he didn't want it. "That was the founding bargain. Everything this guild has ever done — every ward repaired, every impossible thing catalogued and kept — has been installment payments. Maintenance work. We have been paying down the interest for two hundred years."

"But the principal," Beatrix said.

"Has never been touched." Oren met his eyes. "No. That's not true either. Some of us have been paying more than interest."

* * *

He took Beatrix down to the cellar himself.

Not the cellar from the key's yesterday — that one was sealed, and had been for forty years, and would stay sealed until Oren was dust. This was the wine cellar, which was also the memory cellar, which was also, in the way of guild buildings, several cellars depending on who was asking. Bramble's preserves lined one wall. Sable's confiscated ward-breaking tools hung on another like instruments of a surgery nobody wanted performed. And in the back, behind a rack of elderberry cordial that Ilex insisted was actually excellent, stood a door of plain black oak with no lock.

"You don't lock it?" Beatrix asked.

"Locks keep things out," Oren said. "This door keeps me honest. Anyone can open it. I just have to choose to walk through knowing what's inside."

He pushed it open.

Inside was a small room, and in the small room was a chair, and on the chair was folded a coat Oren recognized with a lurch — his own coat, from fifty years ago, brown wool, brass buttons, the left cuff worn thin where he used to rub it when he was thinking. He had not seen that coat in decades. He had not known it existed until this moment, and the seeing hurt like pressing on a bruise he hadn't known he had.

"There's a ledger on the shelf," Oren said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Mine. Look at the entries."

Beatrix found it by touch — the room had no windows and the single lamp made more shadow than light. He opened the ledger and read aloud, slowly, the way one reads something written in a language one is only half-fluent in.

"*Paid: the smell of my mother's kitchen. Received: one turn extended for the eastern marches.*" A pause. "*Paid: my brother Wren's face as he looked at seventeen. Received: the southern wards hold another week.*"

"Stop," Oren said, because the boy's voice was starting to shake and Oren could not afford, yet, to hear it shake.

"Guildmaster — Wren — "

"I don't know who Wren is," Oren said flatly. "That's rather the point. I paid for him. Fifty years ago, when the southern wards were failing and there was no one else, I sold the memory of my brother's face to buy them a week. And then, over the years, other installments. My mother's kitchen. My wedding day — I know I was married; there's a ring in a box upstairs and a widow in Lowmarsh who presumably remembers me fondly, and I cannot recall her name, and I have learned to live with that the way you learn to live with a stone in your boot, which is to say badly, and quietly, and forever." He gestured at the coat. "I kept the things I'd lost, physically, where I could see them. As evidence. As penance. The mind forgets, but the hands can hold proof."

Beatrix was staring at him. Behind the boy's eyes, Oren could see the arithmetic running again, terrible and fast — the wards, the moons, the pages, the years. Watching a young man watch his future take shape.

"How many turns are left?" Beatrix asked.

"One," Oren said. "After this moon, twelve have been spent. Twelve turns of maintenance, of patches and prayers and the occasional — " he tapped the ledger — "personal contribution. One turn remains. And when it ends, the debt comes due in full, and no amount of ward-mending will stop it."

"Then we pay the principal somehow. We find a way to — "

"We?" Oren said, sharper than he intended. "Boy, listen to me. You have been in this guild three months. You owe this bargain nothing. When I was your age I thought the same thing — *we'll fix it, we'll find the flaw in the contract, there's always a flaw* — and I spent fifty years finding flaws, and every flaw I found cost me a piece of myself to exploit, and the debt didn't shrink by so much as a page." He took a breath. Made his voice gentle again, because the boy deserved gentleness and had gotten almost none of it lately. "I am telling you this so you can walk away. That's the one inheritance I can give you clean: the option of leaving."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. Then, quietly: "You gave me the same speech about the hedge-witch, didn't you? When I first arrived. *You can always leave, lad.*" A ghost of a smile. "You say it to everyone you're afraid of losing."

Oren had no answer for that. Sixty years of words, and the boy disarmed him with three sentences and tired eyes.

* * *

They climbed back up into the grey false-morning. Sable was in the hall, sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, which was her way of thinking. Ilex was at her maps, drawing and redrawing the coastline of a county that had stopped existing on Tuesday. Bramble was making breakfast with the aggressive cheerfulness of a cook-mage whose spells required belief to work and who therefore refused, on principle, to stop believing.

Oren crossed to the tall cabinet beside the hearth — the one everyone assumed held candles — and took from it a small object wrapped in oilcloth.

The compass had belonged to Alveric Dune. It was a plain thing, silver-cased, smaller than a plum, with a needle of dark glass that did not point north. It had never pointed north. It pointed toward doors — toward thresholds, openings, ways through and out and in. Oren had watched it swing toward the guildhall's front entrance, toward the cellar door, toward the gap in a hedgerow where a runaway boy had once squeezed through at midnight with stolen ledgers under his arm.

He had never understood why it sometimes trembled toward Beatrix Dunmore himself.

"Alveric carried this when he made the bargain," Oren said, pressing it into the boy's hands. "It's the only tool he ever trusted afterward. It doesn't show you the way forward. It shows you the way *through* — and there is a difference, and you, of all people, will understand it." He closed Beatrix's fingers over the case. "If you're going to ignore my advice and go looking for answers, then go armed."

"Guildmaster — Oren — " The boy stumbled over the name, and something in Oren's chest pulled tight, because he suddenly could not remember whether he had ever heard Beatrix say it before, or whether that was one of the things already slipping. "Why give it to me? Why not keep paying yourself? You've been carrying this alone for fifty years."

"Because I am nearly out of past, lad," Oren said, and heard his own voice crack on it, and let it crack, because the boy had earned honesty and honesty was the last luxury he had left. "And because guilt is a heavy thing to carry alone and I have carried it long enough to know it does not get lighter. It gets *heavier*, and then one day you set it down and discover it has grown roots through you." He stepped back. "So I'm passing it on. Consider it your apprenticeship completed early. Guilt, one compass, one impossible country. Mind the step."

Beatrix laughed — a wet, startled sound — and clutched the compass, and the dark glass needle swung once, twice, and settled.

Pointing directly at the boy's own chest.

Then, slowly, dragging, as though moving through honey, it swung away — south and east, toward the capital, toward the drowned archive, toward something waiting.

"That," Beatrix whispered, "can't be good."

"No," Oren agreed. "But it may be true, and those aren't the same thing, and in my experience the second one is rarer and more useful." He picked up the cold tea and poured it out, and began, out of habit, to arrange the cups on the table — six of them, seven places, the arithmetic of loss — and stopped himself, and set out seven anyway.

Outside the windows, the thirteenth moon hung pale and patient over the hills, and somewhere far to the south-east, in a city that still believed itself whole, a door that opened only for the belonging-nowhere was dreaming of a boy with ink on his knuckles.

"Pack lightly," Oren said. "And take Sable. She breaks things professionally, and you're going to need something broken."
