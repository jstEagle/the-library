## 9. The Road That Forgets

The road out of Millbrook had been a road yesterday. Beatrix was almost certain of that.

He kept checking the ledger he carried against his chest — the small one, the working one, the one with the key-that-opens-yesterday pressed between its pages like a flower someone had forgotten to dry. The road's entry was there in his own handwriting: *Old King's Way, running east from the mill town, cobbled, bordered by hawthorn, three days' walk to the river ferry at Caudlemouth.* Three days. He had written it himself, not two seasons past, when a traveling tinker had asked him to log the impossible fact that her cart wheel turned without turning.

But the road under his boots now had no memory of cobbles. It was packed earth, then it was grass, then it was nothing at all — just a suggestion of passage, like a sentence someone had started and abandoned mid-word. Twice since dawn they had walked in a wide circle without knowing it, passing the same leaning fencepost with the same crow on it, and the second time the crow had looked at Beatrix with an expression he could only describe as embarrassment.

"Stop counting your steps," Sable said, without turning around.

"I wasn't."

"You were. You do it when you're frightened. Left foot every time you're scared, right foot when you're only worried." She walked the way she did everything — like the ground owed her money. "A looping road eats rhythm. If you count, it learns your pattern and feeds it back to you. Walk crooked."

Beatrix stopped counting. It was harder than it sounded. His whole life was counting — ledgers, entries, threads, the number of impossible things catalogued per season, the number of times Oren Vey had said *well done* this year (four, and Beatrix could name each one). Counting was how he held the world still. And the world, lately, would not hold still for anything so polite as arithmetic.

Behind them, Millbrook was already softening. He'd looked back once, only once, and seen the chimney smoke rising wrong — straight up, unbothered by wind, as if the village had forgotten what wind was for. The hedge-witch Maren was somewhere behind them too, or ahead of them, or erased entirely; he couldn't decide which possibility hurt most, so he filed all three under *later* and walked east, or what the compass said was east.

Oren's compass didn't point north. It pointed toward doors — that was what he'd told Beatrix in the guildhall, pressing the brass instrument into his hands with the guilt of forty years sitting on his shoulders like wet wool. Now it sat in Beatrix's palm, needle trembling, always trembling, pointing not at a direction but at a *somewhere*, the way a dog points at birds. Today it pointed slightly south of east, which meant either the capital had moved or they had.

"Tell me about the ward," Beatrix said, because the silence had begun filling up with the sound of the land forgetting — a faint friction, like pages being turned very far away. "The one you broke. You've been chewing on it for two days. I can hear you chewing."

Sable snorted. Then she was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer, and he had learned that about her: she answered questions the way rivers answered bridges, eventually, and only if you didn't make a fuss.

"There was a woman," she said finally. "In Harrowfen. Pregnant, husband dead in the marsh fever. No family. The ward-stone at the edge of her field hummed all night, kept her awake, and the local magistrate said wards were guild business and guild business cost more than she had." Sable kicked a stone off the ghost of the road. It flew too far, as though the distance between things had gotten loose. "I broke it for six silver marks and a hot meal. Took me an hour. I was good."

"What happened?"

"The stone wasn't humming because it was broken. It was humming because it was *holding*. Holding her story in place — the widow-with-child-of-Harrowfen, the grief, the name, all of it stitched to that corner of the country so the country wouldn't lose track of her. I thought wards kept things out." Her jaw worked. "Nobody tells you wards keep things *in*. Not soldiers. Stories. I unpicked her thread and walked away with my six marks, and two years later I came back through Harrowfen and nobody knew her. Not her neighbors. Not the midwife. Not the priest. There was a house with a cradle in it and no one who remembered why."

The crow on the fencepost had followed them somehow. Beatrix chose not to log this.

"You've been paying for it ever since," he said quietly.

"Everyone pays. Question is whether you pay forward or backward." She glanced at him, quick and sharp as a knife drawn and resheathed. "Your turn, ledger-keeper. Why does a boy who can see threads run away from the one person who taught him to see them?"

The question landed where she'd aimed it, which was directly into the gap — the hole in him he'd been circling for weeks, the missing room in the house of his own history. He knew Maren had trained him. He knew he had loved her the way you love weather, helplessly and with complaints. He knew he had run. But the *why* of it was gone, scooped out clean, and when he reached for it his mind slid off the edges like a foot off ice.

"I don't remember," he said. It was the first time he'd admitted it aloud, and saying it made it heavier, made it real, made it a thing that had weight and could fall on him.

Sable stopped walking. She looked at him properly for the first time that morning — not at the ledger, not at the compass, but at him, at whatever was left after the erasure had taken its cut.

"How much?" she asked. Not *what*. How much.

"My mother's face. My reason for leaving. The smell of Maren's kitchen, which I know I knew because I have entries describing it — rosemary and burnt honey — but the description is all I have now, like reading a letter from someone I invented." His voice stayed level through sheer professional habit. Ledger-keepers learned that. "Some days I think it's just what happens when you leave home. Home fades. Everyone says so."

"Everyone is wrong," Sable said. "Home doesn't fade. It waits. What's eating you isn't distance." She resumed walking, crooked, off the centerline of the dead road. "It's got your scent."

* * *

They crossed the river at noon, or where the river should have been.

Caudlemouth's ferry was gone — not sunk, not moored elsewhere, but *ungone*, the way a word is gone from the tip of your tongue. The river itself remained, more or less, except that it was running backward, sliding uphill toward its own source with a look of quiet determination, carrying leaves and one indignant duck in the wrong direction. On the far bank, the hawthorn hedgerows that should have lined the Old King's Way grew in perfect geometric spirals, as if someone had described hedgerows to a stranger over a bad connection.

"We wade," Sable said.

"It's running backward."

"Then it'll push us along. First useful thing it's done all week."

The water was cold in a way water shouldn't be — cold like a cellar, cold like the space between stars, cold with the specific chill of something that had forgotten warmth was part of its job. Beatrix held the ledger above his head as they crossed, and halfway over, the current stuttered. For three heartbeats the river simply *stopped*, hanging around their waists like glass, and in that stillness Beatrix saw the threads.

He always saw them, but usually faintly, usually politely — silver filaments running from objects to their stories, from the ferry-post to the idea of ferries, from a loaf of bread to the hands that baked it. Here in the stopped water the threads were visible as they died. He watched the river's thread fray strand by strand above his head, a rope unraveling into nothing, and where each strand parted there was not darkness but *absence*, a place his eyes refused to report on, a hole shaped exactly like a piece of the world.

Then the current lurched back to life and shoved them both stumbling onto the southern bank, and Beatrix knelt in the mud and was quietly, thoroughly sick.

"Thread-sight," Sable said, crouching beside him. Not a question.

"You knew?"

"Oren talks in his sleep. Also you stared at the river like it owed you an apology." She handed him her flask. It contained something that tasted like pine tar and forgiveness. "What did you see?"

"Its story is coming apart. From the ends inward. Like — like a book losing its last chapters first." He wiped his mouth. "Sable, the capital is east of here. Everything between us and it is older than this river. Older stories, thicker threads. When those go—"

"Then we walk faster."

They walked faster.

* * *

They met the travelers at dusk, in a hollow where the road briefly remembered itself enough to be a road.

There were five of them, walking west in a tight, careful cluster, the way people walk when the ground has lied to them before. Farmers by their coats. Beatrix raised a hand in greeting and called a hello, and the nearest figure turned toward him, and Beatrix felt the greeting die in his throat.

The traveler had no face yet.

Not wounded. Not masked. *Not yet finished* — the skin smooth as an egg where features should be, the head tilted with an attentive, patient blankness, as if the face were a room awaiting furniture. The other four were the same. Five faceless people standing in the last gold light, waiting to be written.

"They passed us an hour ago going east," Sable breathed. "We saw them from the ridge. They had faces then."

"No," Beatrix said. His voice came out strange. "We saw faces because we assumed faces. I logged them as farmers because that's what the shape suggested." He was doing it even now — his gift reaching out, greedy for meaning, trying to stitch *farmer, father, tired, going home* onto these blank heads. And the threads were there, he could feel them, but they ran the wrong way. These threads didn't anchor the travelers to stories. The travelers were anchored to the *absence* of stories. They were placeholders. Draft copies of people, circulating while the originals were being recalled.

One of them — the tall one, who might have been a woman, might have been a scarecrow given a coat — extended a hand toward Beatrix. In the palm lay a small object: a wooden button, ordinary, worn smooth.

When Beatrix took it, sensation flooded him like water through a broken dike. *Larch wood, carved with a pocketknife by a girl named—* the name arrived and vanished, arrived and vanished, a fish surfacing and diving. A coat it belonged to. A winter. A hand sewing it back on by firelight. All of it real, all of it *recently* real, all of it now belonging to no one.

"She's holding what's left of herself," Sable whispered. "That's why they cluster. They're carrying pieces. Waiting for—" She stopped. Waiting for someone who could write them back.

Beatrix closed his fingers around the button and did the only thing he knew how to do. He opened the ledger against his chest, right there in the road, and wrote:

*Entry 4,412. One wooden button, larch, worn smooth. Belonged to a coat mended often and well, by someone who was loved. Owner currently unrecorded. Status: recoverable.*

He spoke the words aloud as he wrote them — he didn't know why, except that the spire sang in script, and script wanted voices — and the button warmed in his palm. The tall traveler's blank head turned slowly toward the sound of his pen, the way flowers turn toward light, or the way drowning people turn toward shore.

And across the smoothness of that unfinished face, something appeared. Faint, provisional, like breath on glass: the suggestion of a mouth. Of gratitude, or hope, or simply the shape a mouth makes before it remembers how to speak.

"We can't fix them," Sable said gently. "Not here. Not with one ledger."

"No." Beatrix tucked the button safely into the ledger's spine, next to the key that opened yesterday. Two impossibilities, pressed together like flowers. "But I can keep their pieces. And somewhere in the capital there's a contract, and contracts can be amended. Every debt has terms, Sable. Even this one."

The faceless ones stood watching them go, five blank moons in the dusk, and Beatrix did not look back until the dark took them, because looking back was starting to feel like a luxury purchased on credit.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a milestone that read CAUDLEMOUTH — 12 in letters that were fading even as Beatrix read them, the numerals thinning like ink in rain. No fire. Sable said fires attracted attention now, though she declined to specify whose.

They ate hard bread and harder cheese, and Beatrix got out the compass to check their bearing, and that was when Sable said it.

"When I broke the Harrowfen ward," she said, staring into the fireless dark, "I told you it was six marks and a meal. That's true. But it wasn't the first one."

Beatrix went still.

"I broke four. Maybe five — I stopped keeping count, which should have told me something, since counting was the only conscience I had left." Her laugh had no floor under it. "Small wards, outlying stones, places no one important would miss. I told myself they were redundant. I told myself a lot of sentences that began with *I told myself*. And then the thirteenth moon showed up on Oren's wall, and I understood what all my little breakages had been. Not theft. *Withdrawals.* I'd been helping empty the account."

She pulled something from inside her coat and held it out to him: a ward-breaker's pick, black iron, worn bright at the grip. "I've carried this for nine years waiting for a reason to throw it in a river. Never found one deep enough. So instead—" she pressed it into his hand, closing his fingers over it the way he'd closed his own over the button, "—you carry it. Guild property now. Logged."

"Sable—"

"Log it, Beatrix. *Entry:* one instrument of harm, surrendered. *Status:* repurposed." Her voice cracked precisely once, and she talked over the crack as if it belonged to someone else. "That's how it works, isn't it? That's what you do. Things stop being what they were when somebody writes down what they'll be instead."

Beatrix looked down at the pick in his hand — an ugly little tool that had unmade a widow's place in the world — and then at Sable, who had handed him her worst self like a confession, like a wound shown to a healer, trusting him with the sight of it.

He opened the ledger. Wrote the entry. Spoke it aloud into the dark, and heard the words settle over the iron like snow.

"Entry 4,413. One ward-breaker's pick, black iron. Former instrument of erasure. Current purpose: to be carried by someone who intends, before this is over, to use every tool he has to put things *back*." He paused. "Status: repurposed."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Sable exhaled — nine years leaving her body in a single breath — and lay back against her pack, and something between them was different, some load redistributed, some weight now shared. Trust, Beatrix thought, wasn't built out of grand vows. It was built out of buttons and picks and entries in a dying hand, small objects passed palm to palm, each one saying *I believe you will hold this better than I could.*

He was still warm with it when the compass woke.

It had been trembling since Millbrook, needle quivering toward doors. Now the trembling stopped — snapped still as a struck bell going silent — and the needle swung hard, hard, hard, straining against its pivot toward the east, and the brass casing grew warm in Beatrix's hand, and from somewhere inside the instrument, from somewhere inside the metal itself, came a sound.

A tone. Single, low, patient.

Not a sound the compass was making. A sound the compass was *receiving* — carried on no air, arriving through the ground, through the bones of his hand, through whatever thread connected every door in the world to every other door. Beatrix knew the register of it instantly. He had heard its cousin every day of his apprenticeship, drifting from the distant spire: ledger-script, sung.

But the spire was fifty miles east, in the capital, and this tone was close. This tone was *nearby*. This tone was coming from the dark hills to the northeast, where no spire stood, where no door should be, and as Beatrix lifted his head he saw that the stars — the few stubborn stars that had survived the great vanishing, scattered like coins left behind in a hurry — were going out above those hills. One by one. In a line.

As if something were walking beneath them, opening doors, and each opened door let the dark pour through.

"Sable," Beatrix whispered.

She was already sitting up. Already looking. Already, he realized, reaching for the black iron pick in his pack.

"That song," she said. "It's not singing to us, is it."

Beatrix checked the compass, and the needle confirmed what he already knew, pulling toward the extinguishing stars like a heart yanking on its own string.

"No," he said. "It's counting us."
