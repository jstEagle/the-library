## 10. The Archive of Unmade Things

The capital had drowned a hundred years before Ilex was born, and no one had thought it strange, because that was the kind of thing the country did without noticing: it lost things and kept walking.

She stood at the water's edge where Corrow Street used to begin — she knew it had been Corrow Street because her grandfather's maps said so, and because the drowned lampposts still leaned at the angle streets lean when they are trying to remember which way they go — and she watched the floodwater lie flat as poured glass. Not murky. Not moving. The whole sunken city lay beneath it in perfect detail, rooftops like the scales of sleeping fish, bell towers with their bells still hanging, everything sharp and clear and utterly unreachable. The water didn't distort. It preserved. That was the worst part. You could see everything you had lost, down to the moss on the weathervanes, if only you were willing to drown for it.

"The archive's under the Chancellery," Sable said beside her. She had her hood up against a wind that wasn't blowing, which Ilex had learned was a ward-breaker's habit — testing, always testing, whether the world still answered her hands. "Third sublevel. The flood never got below the second."

"Then how do we get to the third?"

Sable smiled the way she did when she was about to do something that would have gotten her hanged, once, when there had still been laws worth hanging people under. "We don't get to it. We let it come up to us. Wards are just doors that forgot they're doors." She knelt at the waterline and pressed two fingers to the flooded cobbles. "I spent ten years breaking things like this. Give me a minute to apologize first."

Ilex left her to it. Apologizing to locks was a Sable ritual and not one to be interrupted; the last time Bramble had joked about it mid-apology, the lock had taken offense and eaten his spare apron strings.

Behind them, Beatrix sat on a fallen lintel with the founder's compass open on his knee. He'd barely spoken since the road — since the faceless travelers, since the river that ran backward through the valley of Tarnish, carrying its water uphill toward mountains that were no longer entirely sure they existed. Oren walked the perimeter of their little camp each night reciting the names of towns aloud, and this morning he had stumbled on Harrowmere, and Beatrix had watched him stumble, and something in the boy had gone very quiet and very careful, the way a person goes careful when they've realized the floor is thinner than they thought.

Ilex sat down next to him. She kept her satchel in her lap, the way she always did, with her thumb hooked through the strap. Inside were forty-one years of maps. She did not think about what drawing cost her now. Thinking about it made the blankness come faster, like scratching at a bruise.

"You're going to ask me about the compass," Beatrix said, without looking up.

"I'm going to sit here," Ilex said. "Asking is your department. You're the ledger-keeper."

"It doesn't point anywhere anymore." He turned it over in his long fingers. The needle swung in slow, drunken circles, hunting. "It pointed true the whole road here. Door after door after door. Now—" He caught it in his palm. "Now it just spins."

"Maybe you're standing in front of all of them at once," Ilex said. "A city is mostly doors, when you think about it."

Beatrix almost smiled. Almost. "You think everything's a map."

"Everything *is* a map. People are just bad readers." She nodded at the drowned city. "Look at it properly. Every rooftop is a line someone drew. Every street is a sentence. A city is an argument a civilization had with itself about how to live, written in stone so it couldn't be taken back." Her throat tightened. She hadn't meant to say that much. "Wasn't. Couldn't be taken back."

Below them, the water shivered.

* * *

It came up in a column, silent as held breath.

Not a geyser — a lifting. The entire surface of the flood rose three feet, four, five, and hung there, and beneath it the drowned streets went dark and then lit from within, lamp by lamp by lamp, until the sunken capital glowed like a lantern seen through frosted glass. A stairway surfaced where none had been, stone steps ascending out of the deep in a spiral, water sheeting off them and falling upward back into the suspended flood, because even gravity in this place had been archived rather than abolished.

"Well," said Bramble, who had insisted on coming despite having no head for heights or depths or anything in between. He adjusted the pack of cooking gear on his back and looked at the impossible stairway with the expression of a man being asked to review a restaurant inside a whale. "That's either an invitation or a mouth."

"They're often the same thing," Sable said, shaking water off her fingers.

Oren went first. Of course he went first. Guildmasters went first; that was half of what the title meant. But Ilex noticed he paused at the top step, hand pressed to the wet stone, lips moving — counting something, or reciting, or perhaps simply reminding himself of his own name. His memory had been leaking for weeks now, little losses disguised as pauses. She had started keeping a list of them in her map-margins without telling anyone. It was eleven items long. She hated every entry.

The stairway descended into the dark of the third sublevel, and the water hung above their heads the whole way down, an ocean balanced on good manners.

* * *

The Archive of Unmade Things did not look like an archive.

Ilex had imagined shelves. She had imagined catalogues, index cards, the smell of vellum and dust — the honest smells of remembering. Instead the great chamber opened around them like the inside of a bell: circular, resonant, dim, with a floor of black glass that gave back their reflections a half-second late, as though the room had to think about whether they still existed before agreeing to show them.

And the things. The unmade things were everywhere.

They hung in the air at the height of the heart, drifting slowly on currents Ilex couldn't feel. A child's wooden horse, worn smooth by love, rotating gently. A wedding ring with a hairline crack through the band. An entire door — just a door, blue-painted, with a brass number nine screwed onto it — floating six feet off the ground with nothing behind it but more darkness. A single boot. A music box whose lid was frozen mid-open, its song visible as a faint golden shimmer above it, playing to no one, forever unfinished.

"What is this place?" Bramble whispered, and his whisper echoed wrong, arriving before him.

"When the country forgets something," Ilex said slowly, reading the room the way she read coastlines, "it has to go somewhere. Nothing truly vanishes. Erasure is just... filing." She looked up at the drifting horse. "This is the file room."

Beatrix had gone pale. He was staring at the floating objects with his thread-sight wide open — she could tell because his eyes had that faraway, over-bright look, the look of a man watching invisible seams — and whatever he saw, it frightened him badly enough that he'd stopped breathing through his nose.

"There are threads," he said. "On everything. All of them cut. Not broken — *cut*. Clean. Scissored." He reached toward the wooden horse and stopped an inch away. "Something didn't lose these things. Something *edited* them."

"The trade," Sable said quietly.

Oren said nothing at all. He was looking at a small object drifting near the wall, and Ilex followed his gaze and saw that it was a tin whistle, dented, the kind sold at harvest fairs for a copper. And she saw Oren's face do the thing faces do when grief arrives too fast for dignity — the collapse held off by maybe a quarter-second of pure will.

He had told them once, drunk on Bramble's plum wine, about learning to whistle on a fair-day whistle his sister won for him. He had also mentioned, in passing, some other night, that he'd had no sister.

Both memories could not be true. One of them had already been paid out. And here hung the whistle, filed among the unmade, proof of which story had survived and which had been spent.

"We're not here for a museum tour," Sable said, and her voice was gentler than her words. "Contract. Sublevel three. Where?"

Ilex unslung her satchel. Her hands knew the motion: strap, buckle, roll of maps, find the one she needed. She had drawn the Chancellery's sublevels from a pre-flood survey found in a guild strongbox — the only surviving plan of the drowned floors. She spread it on the black glass floor and weighted the corners with stones from her pocket, and the room's strange light came up through the paper and made her linework glow like veins.

"Here." Her finger found the chamber. "We're standing in the old records hall. The contract vault should be directly beneath us. There'll be a mechanism — a reader's desk, probably, with a seal. Archives like this don't hide their treasures. They make you *qualify* for them."

"And how does one qualify?" Bramble asked.

"By belonging," Ilex said, "or by not."

She didn't know why she said it. The words came out of her the way map-labels sometimes did, arriving from somewhere beneath knowledge — from the country itself, maybe, whispering through whatever channels were still open. Sable looked at her sharply. Beatrix looked at the compass in his hand, where the needle had stopped spinning.

It pointed straight down.

* * *

The reader's desk was exactly where the map said it would be, which frightened Ilex more than any impossibility had. Maps were supposed to describe the past. Hers were starting to describe the future, and she decided firmly not to think about what that meant, and added it silently to the growing list of things she was not thinking about.

The desk stood alone in a smaller chamber beneath the bell-room, carved from a single block of black glass, its surface holding a shallow depression shaped like an open book. Above it, suspended in nothing, hung a seal the size of a dinner plate: white wax, unbroken, marked with twelve moons arranged in a ring.

Twelve. Not thirteen.

Oren approached it the way penitents approach altars. "I've seen this mark before," he said. "On the founding charter. In the guildhall. Twelve moons — everyone assumed it was decorative. Calendar imagery." His voice had gone thin. "It was a signature. It was always a signature."

"How do we break it?" Sable asked.

"We don't." Oren looked at Beatrix. And Ilex watched him look, and saw the terrible arithmetic happening behind the guildmaster's eyes — the same arithmetic, she realized, that must have happened behind the founder's eyes, centuries ago, when he chose which debts to pay with other people's pasts. "The desk decides who may read. I don't know how. But I know it isn't me. I've already spent too much of myself near this bargain. If I touch it—"

"If you touch it, it might take the rest," Sable finished. "Yes. We understand how installments work."

Bramble cleared his throat. "Then who touches it? Because I want it noted that I volunteer for nothing, and my mother didn't raise a fool, and also I'm holding everyone's supper, which gives me leverage."

Everyone looked at Beatrix.

He was staring at the depression in the desk, and the needle of the compass in his fist was trembling against the glass like a trapped moth, pointing down, down, down, and his thread-sight was open so wide that Ilex could almost see the light of it through his skin.

"I can see the threads on the seal," he whispered. "All the way through it. Into the vault. They're..." He swallowed. "They're ledger-script. The whole thing is written in ledger-script. It's an entry. The contract is just — an entry in a book. Someone wrote the country down as a debit."

"Can you open it?" Oren asked.

Beatrix laid his palm flat on the black glass.

For a moment, nothing. Then the seal above the desk began, very slowly, to turn — twelve moons wheeling around its center like a clock deciding — and the wax split along a seam Ilex hadn't seen, and the chamber filled with a sound like a page turning somewhere enormous, and the floor of the depression filled not with ink but with light, and in the light, writing.

Ilex read it upside down, cartographer's habit, and her blood went cold with every line.

*Let it be entered,* the contract began, *that the nation called [the name was smudged, erased, unreadable — the country had forgotten its own name first] , finding itself beyond saving, is hereby pledged...*

She read the terms. One memory per page, per household, per turn of the moon. Thirteen turns purchased. Collateral: the country entire. And then the final clause, and she read it twice because the first time she refused to believe it:

*A single exception is granted. One door shall remain, opening outward, that the maker might not seal himself in with his sin. This door shall answer only to one who belongs nowhere: no house holds him, no history claims him, no name fits him wholly. To such a one, and to such a one alone, the door will open.*

The light faded. The chamber was dark again except for the compass needle, burning green in Beatrix's fist, pointing at the floor with the insistence of a drowning man pointing at the surface.

Nobody spoke.

Ilex became aware, gradually, horribly, of the silence arranging itself around Beatrix like furniture. Of the way Sable had shifted her weight, angling slightly between Beatrix and Oren — protective instinct, or guilt, or both. Of the way Bramble had stopped fidgeting. Of the way Oren had closed his eyes.

Because they all understood the clause. They all understood it the way you understand a diagnosis.

An orphan raised by a hedge-witch who was never quite family. A runaway with gaps already eating through his childhood — no mother's face, no reason for leaving, holes where the anchors of a life should be. A boy whose own apprenticeship entry had been signed in another man's hand. No house held him. No history claimed him. His name fit him like a borrowed coat.

The door out existed. It had always existed. It was waiting beneath their feet, patient as wax, and it would open for exactly one person in all the world.

It would open for Beatrix.

"That's why the compass brought us here," Beatrix said at last. His voice was very steady, which was somehow worse than screaming. "Not to the contract. To the door. It's been pointing at the door this whole time." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "The founder didn't leave an escape hatch by accident. He built it for whoever came after him. He built it for—" He stopped.

"For someone like you," Oren said softly. "Someone unwritten enough to slip through."

"No." Beatrix shook his head. "No, listen — don't you see? It's not an escape hatch. It's a *debt collection*. The clause says the door opens for someone who belongs nowhere. It doesn't say they get to choose to walk through it. What if it opens and *takes*?" He looked around the chamber, at each of them, and Ilex saw the hope in his face curdle into fear in real time, hope sharpening on the whetstone of that final clause. "Or worse. What if it opens, and the country finally finishes forgetting, and the only reason any of you are still standing here is because I haven't left yet?"

"That's not—" Bramble started.

"The clause says *alone*, Bramble." Beatrix's voice cracked on the last word. "To such a one, and to such a one alone. I walk through that door, and whatever's on the other side, none of you follow. None of you *can*." He looked down at the compass, and his knuckles were white around it, and the needle burned steady and green and merciless, pointing down at the door that wanted him. "The founder didn't build a way out. He built a way to make the loss *tidy*. One person leaves. Everyone else gets erased cleanly behind him. That's not mercy. That's bookkeeping."

Ilex's chest hurt. She thought about maps — about borders, which were just lines drawn by people who needed to believe the other side was other. About how the cruelest thing a border could do was pass straight through the middle of a person, dividing them from themselves.

Beatrix was a border now. The country on one side, the door on the other, and the whole bargain balanced on where he chose to stand.

"We should rest," Sable said finally, into the ringing quiet. "Read it again at dawn. Contracts have loopholes; I've broken enough wards to know every lock has a seam. We'll find the seam."

There was no seam. Ilex could see that in the geometry of the clause, in its perfect closed symmetry, the way she could see that a coastline had no shortcuts. But she said nothing, because dawn was hours away, and hope deferred was still hope, and they would need all of it for what came next.

They climbed back up through the bell-room, past the drifting relics of the forgotten — the horse, the whistle, the door numbered nine — and as they passed, Ilex noticed something she hadn't noticed before.

The blue door with the brass nine was no longer empty behind it.

There was a faint outline in the darkness of its frame now. A shape. A figure, perhaps, standing on the other side, waiting with the patience of wax and centuries. Too indistinct to be a person. Too deliberate to be nothing.

And as Beatrix passed it, the compass needle in his fist swung — just once — away from the floor, and pointed at the figure behind the door, and held.

Beatrix hadn't seen. He was ahead of her on the stairs, shoulders bowed under the weight of a clause, asking Sable something about seals and seams.

But Ilex had spent her whole life reading maps, and she knew what she had just seen: a route, drawn on the one chart no one had ever surveyed, leading out of the country and through the blue door and into wherever the founder's voice was still speaking.

She memorized it. That was what she did. She put it in the atlas of her mind, on the page where she kept the things she was afraid to look at directly, and she followed the others up into the air, while below them, in the dark of the Archive of Unmade Things, the figure behind the blue door waited for the boy who belonged nowhere —

and, Ilex was almost certain, began to hum a tune that the music box upstairs had been trying, for a hundred years, to finish.
