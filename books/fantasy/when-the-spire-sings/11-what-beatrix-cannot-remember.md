## 11. What Beatrix Cannot Remember

The guildhall had a way of holding silence the way other buildings held dust — in every corner, in every seam, waiting to be disturbed. Beatrix had learned its textures over the weeks since the stars went out: the creak of the third stair from the landing, which sounded like a word in a language no one spoke anymore; the way Bramble's kitchen exhaled warmth and rosemary at odd hours, as if the cook-mage fed the building as well as the people in it; the particular hush of the ledger room after midnight, when the candles burned low and the spire's distant song thinned to something almost like breathing.

It was there, in that hush, that he made his discovery.

He had not meant to. He was working, because work was the one thing that still felt solid — ink on paper, columns of figures, entries in his own careful hand. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers even now, especially now, because Oren Vey had said what Beatrix already believed in his bones: *if we stop writing it down, we agree it never happened.* So Beatrix catalogued. A spoon that stirred itself toward grief. A window in Ilex's map room that showed weather from before the erasure. A child's shoe found on a road with no road around it. Small impossibilities, each one a thread he could still see, still knot, still hold.

He was copying an entry from three weeks back — the night the wards fell — when his pen stopped.

Not because of anything on the page. Because of something behind his eyes.

He had written, in his own hand, in the margin of that terrible night's record: *Mother would have hated this.*

And he could not see her face.

* * *

At first it was like reaching for a candle in a dark room and finding the table bare. He knew — he *knew* — that he had a mother. The knowledge sat in him the way a scar sits in skin: proof of a wound, shape of a history, but no longer any feeling in it. He knew she had hands. He knew she had a voice. He knew, with a certainty that arrived from nowhere and attached itself to nothing, that she had smelled of woodsmoke and tansy.

But her face —

He closed his eyes and reached for it the way you reach for a name on the tip of your tongue, and what came back was not blankness exactly. Blankness would have been honest. What came back was worse: a face that shifted. Brown eyes, then grey. A long jaw, then a soft one. Every time he thought he had her, she moved, like a reflection in water disturbed by rain. His mind was offering him composites, guesses stitched together from every woman he'd ever passed in a market, and presenting them as memory.

"That's not how remembering works," he said aloud, to the empty room, and his voice sounded strange to him — thinner than he expected, younger.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above him, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the spire sang its ledger-script song. Lately Beatrix had begun to understand it without trying, the way he understood the threads binding objects to their stories. The spire was reading the country aloud, entry by entry, and the entries were getting shorter.

*Hollowmere. Population two hundred and eleven. Known for its apple orchards and its bell-ringer who —*

The song always stopped somewhere. It never finished Hollowmere anymore. There wasn't enough of Hollowmere left to finish.

Beatrix sat very still, hands flat on the ledger, and did the thing he was best at in all the world: he looked for threads.

Every object had them. Every person. A thread ran from a thing to its story, from its story to the people who told it, from those people to the places that held them. He had seen Sable's threads first as frayed rope, knotted and reknotted where she'd tied herself back together after breaking the border ward. He'd seen Oren's as silver wire gone brittle, snapping strand by strand — though he'd told himself that was age, that mentors were allowed to fray. He'd seen his own threads only once, early on, and had been too busy being frightened of everything else to look closely.

He looked now.

He turned his thread-sight inward, which was not easy; it was like asking an eye to see itself. But he managed it, the way he managed everything with the sight — by relaxing into it, by letting the world go soft and luminous — and there he was, Beatrix Dunmore, rendered in filaments of pale gold, and the filaments were beautiful, and they were wrong.

There were gaps in him.

Not thin places. Not worn places. Gaps — clean-edged absences, like pages cut from a book with a razor, like the horizon where the country used to continue. Three of them that he could count. One sat where his childhood should have been, a hollow roughly the shape of a kitchen, roughly the shape of a mother, roughly the shape of a hedge-witch named Maren whose cottage he could describe perfectly and whose reason for taking him in he could not recall at all. One sat over whatever had happened in the last days before he ran away — he remembered running, remembered the road, remembered arriving at the guildhall door soaked with rain and lying through his teeth about why — but the *why* was a hole with scorched edges, as if someone had burned the answer out of him. And the third gap was small and recent and it terrified him more than the others, because he could not tell what it had once contained. Only that something was missing, the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue.

The erasure hadn't started when the stars went out.

It had started with him.

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He tested instead, methodically, cruelly, the way he tested any impossible thing brought to the guild: list what is claimed, list what can be verified, note the discrepancy.

Claimed: he had grown up in Maren's cottage at the edge of Thistlefen. Verified: he could draw the cottage floor plan from memory, every room, the loose flagstone where he'd hidden things. Claimed: he had a mother before Maren, or perhaps Maren had been something other than a mother — the words had gotten tangled somewhere, another small wrongness he'd never questioned. Verified: nothing. No face. No voice except a tone he invented and then distrusted. No single scene, not one, not breakfast or bedtime or a scolding or a kindness. Twenty years of a life and the archive was empty.

He went to the ledgers next. His own hand was in them from his first week at the guild — neat, small, slightly left-slanted — and he read backward through his own recorded life looking for the man who'd written them. There were references. *Maren says patience is a tool like any other.* *Wrote to Maren; no reply yet; she never replies.* *Dreamed of the fen again. Woke up crying and don't know why.*

That last one stopped him cold. *Don't know why.* He had written it without alarm, the way you write down a headache. As if the not-knowing had been ordinary. As if it had been going on so long it had become furniture.

How long? He paged forward, back toward the beginning of his apprenticeship, hunting for the earliest entry, and found it dated four years past, in a hand nearly identical to his own but not quite — rounder, less sure of itself. And beside it, in the founder's dead hand that he had learned to recognize in the hidden ledger, a notation he had not been able to read before and could read now, because the spire's song had been teaching him ledger-script in his sleep:

*Subject retains thread-sight. Memory excision proceeding per schedule.*

Per schedule.

Beatrix set the ledger down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and walked to the window, and looked out at the starless dark, and discovered that his legs would not entirely hold him, so that he ended up sitting on the floor beneath the sill with his knees drawn up like a child's, in a guildhall full of people who would have come if he called, calling nothing.

They had been eating him slowly. All this time. While he catalogued impossible keys and mended ward-stones and learned to love — because he had, he realized, he had let himself love them, Oren and Ilex and Bramble and even prickly, guarded Sable — while he did all that, the same hunger that was unwriting Hollowmere and the stars and the eastern provinces had been sitting down to meals inside his skull. And it had not taken randomly. It had taken precisely, *per schedule*, starting with the things that anchored him hardest: his mother's face, his home, his reasons. Leaving intact everything that made him useful. Leaving his gift untouched, his diligence, his obedience, his habit of writing things down for other people to keep.

Leaving him a perfect instrument with a hollow where a boy used to be.

*Why leave me the sight?* he asked the dark. And some cold, patient part of him that sounded uncomfortably like the founder's voice in the hidden ledger answered: *Because the sight is the point. You don't burn a field you're planning to harvest.*

* * *

Dawn came grey and sourceless, the way it always did now, light with no sun behind it. Beatrix was still on the floor when he heard the stairs, and then a knock, and then Sable's voice, rough with sleep and concern. "Bramble says you didn't eat. Bramble saying that is basically a fire bell."

"I'm fine," he said, and heard immediately that it was a lie, and heard her hear it.

The door opened anyway. She came in with bread and honey and the wary grace of a woman who had spent years picking locks and picking fights and had recently, reluctantly, learned to pick her way into people's confidence instead. She set the food down, looked at him — really looked, the way she did, like reading a lock's mechanism — and sat down on the floor across from him without being asked.

"You found something."

He wanted to say no. He opened his mouth to say no. What came out, quietly, horribly, was: "Sable, what does my mother look like?"

She blinked. "I've never met her. You've never spoken of her. Or—" she paused, recalibrating, "—you have, maybe, but never the parts that mattered. I assumed it hurt."

"It doesn't hurt," Beatrix said. "That's what frightens me. It should hurt and it doesn't. It's like — do you know what it's like when a ward-stone dies? Not broken. Dead. The stone's still there. You can hold it. But the story's gone out of it, and touching it feels like touching a stranger wearing a friend's clothes." He wrapped his arms tighter around his knees. "I'm doing that from the inside, Sable. I'm the stone."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then, in the flat practical voice she used for hard truths: "Show me."

So he showed her — the gaps, the marginalia, the founder's notation, *proceeding per schedule*. He watched her face as she read it, watched the color leave it, watched her arrive where he had arrived hours ago and stand at the edge of it the way you stand at the edge of the place where the horizon simply ends.

"They took your memories to pay for the moon-turns," she said at last. "One page at a time. Your pages."

"Someone's pages. Maybe everyone's. Maybe every person who ever signed a guild ledger paid without knowing." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I keep thinking — if it's still happening, if it's *per schedule*, then right now, while we sit here, I might be losing something else. Something I won't miss until it's gone. That's the trick of it, isn't it? You can't mourn what you can't remember. It's the perfect theft. The victim thanks the thief for tidying up."

"Then we fight it the way you fight everything," Sable said. "You write it down."

"I *am* the thing that gets written down—"

"No." She took his wrist, hard, her ward-breaker's grip, and pressed his own pen into his hand. "You write yourself down. Everything. Today. What you remember, what you half-remember, what you're afraid of forgetting tomorrow. Names. Faces you can't see — describe the holes where they should be. If it eats you, it eats the ledger too, but I'll be here, and Ilex will be here, and Bramble will feed the thing that's left of you until it remembers how to be a person. That's what a guild is, Beatrix. That's the whole impossible trade."

He stared at her. Something in his chest that had been clenched since midnight loosened by a single degree — not hope, not yet, but the space where hope might be installed later, like a window in a wall.

"And if there's nothing left?" he asked. "If I get to the end and I'm just — a function. A pair of eyes that sees threads, walking around in a coat."

"Then we'll teach the eyes to belong to us," Sable said, "and the rest we'll rebuild. I broke a country's borders for money. Believe me when I tell you I know a thing or two about what can be mended and what has to be remade instead."

He wrote all that day. He filled eleven pages. He described the hole where his mother's face should be until his hand shook. He wrote *Maren* forty times like a charm against forgetting, and noticed, on the thirty-ninth, that he could no longer quite remember why he had run from her — only that the not-remembering had scorched edges, and that under the scorch, faint as ash-drawn lines, something waited that felt less like absence than like a door someone had locked from the outside.

He was staring at that word — *door*, underlined twice without his meaning to — when the knock came at the guildhall's front gate. Heavy. Imperious. Familiar in a way that made the hair on his arms rise before his mind caught up.

Bramble answered it. Beatrix heard the door open, heard the wind of the unwritten lands push into the hall smelling of nothing at all, and heard a voice he had not heard in four years and could not, he realized with a lurch, have described if you paid him — a voice that nonetheless knew his name.

"Where is my apprentice?" said Maren the hedge-witch. "We need to talk about what they took from him. And what he took from me."
