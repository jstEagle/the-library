## 12. The Hedge-Witch Returns

Beatrix did not go down to the hall. He stayed on the stairs with his back against the wall and his heart going like a trapped bird, listening.

Four years. Four years since he had slipped out of Maren's cottage at Greyhollow with a stolen satchel, two apples, and the ledger he had not yet known was holy, and in all that time he had never once been able to call her face to mind — not her face, not the crooked line of her fence, not the smell of the cottage where he'd slept from age six to age seventeen. He had told himself it was ordinary forgetting, the way everyone forgets, the slow erosion of distance. Now, standing in a guildhall full of people who could map vanished places but not explain why their own apprentice's childhood was fog, he understood: the erasure had been eating him since long before the stars went out. It had started small. It had started with him.

And yet the voice below knew him. Knew his name the way a hook knows a fish.

"Where is my apprentice?" Maren said again, louder, and beneath the demand Beatrix heard something he had never heard in her voice before, because he could not remember her voice well enough to compare it: fear. "I walked nine days out of the unwritten lands. Nine days walking through country that couldn't be bothered to remember itself, following the one thread left that still pointed anywhere. Do not make me climb these stairs myself."

Bramble's rumble, low and placating. Oren Vey's voice, thinner than Beatrix had ever heard it: "Madam, if you'll state your business—"

"My business," she said, "is the boy who can see the seams of the world, and the men who came to buy him out of it."

The word *seams* dropped through Beatrix like a stone through still water. He hadn't told anyone that phrase. He had barely told himself. Thread-sight, the guild called it — the gift of seeing what bound objects to stories, keys to yesterdays, wards to the land they held. But privately, late at night, when the ledger-light was low and no one watched, he thought of them as seams. The stitch-lines under the skin of things. And he had never spoken that word aloud to anyone living.

He went down the stairs anyway. His legs made the decision before his courage caught up, which was, he would reflect later, how most of the important decisions of his life got made.

* * *

She stood in the middle of the guildhall floor looking smaller than the voice that carried her name up stairwells. Small and brown and weathered as a fence post, wrapped in a traveling cloak gone grey with road-dust, her boots crusted with something that was not mud — it was the pale, featureless residue of ground that had forgotten being ground. Her hair, which Beatrix's memory insisted should have been iron-grey, was white now, all of it, as though the unwritten lands had bleached her on the way through like a bone left out in weather. She held a hazel switch in one hand, worn smooth. Her eyes found him on the stairs and fixed there, and Beatrix felt seen in the old terrible way, the way only she had ever managed: as though she were reading the ledger of him, entry by entry, and finding errors.

"There you are," she said. Not softly. But not unkindly either, and that undid him more than cruelty would have.

"You're supposed to be dead," Beatrix said. His voice came out flat. "Greyhollow is gone. The whole valley. Ilex mapped it last week — or tried to. There wasn't anything to map."

"I know." She looked down at her boots. "I was standing in my garden when it went. I felt the story come out from under me like a rug pulled sideways, and I did the only thing a hedge-witch knows how to do." She raised the hazel switch. "I held onto my thread and I walked. You don't get erased while you're gripping your own thread, boy. That's hedge-craft, first lesson. Everything else is commentary."

"You're lying," Sable said from the doorway of the map room, arms crossed, ward-breaker's suspicion plain on her scarred face. "Nobody walks out of there. The roads loop. The rivers run backward. We've been through it — you walk three days toward anywhere and end up where you started, minus a memory for toll."

"I didn't follow the roads," Maren said. "Roads are written things; they've already been unwritten here. I followed him." She pointed the switch at Beatrix, and the whole hall turned to look. "Every thread has two ends. One's knotted into whatever it binds. The other trails back to whoever first tied the knot. His threads trail back to Greyhollow. To my cottage. To me. I just put my hand on the line and hauled myself along it like a fisherman hauling rope. Took nine days. Cost me—" she paused, and Beatrix saw her count silently, lips moving, and come up short, "—some things. I'll find out which ones as we go."

Oren Vey stepped forward, and Beatrix saw the Guildmaster do the thing he did so well and at such cost now: arrange his face into calm. "You speak of thread-sight. Of agents who came to buy. Madam, those are dangerous words in this hall, this week of all weeks. If you know something of the founder's bargain—"

"The bargain?" For the first time Maren laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as kindling. "Guildmaster. I helped pay for it."

The silence that followed had weight. Bramble stopped stirring the pot he had instinctively put on the moment the gate opened, because grief and shock were, in his cosmology, best treated with soup. Ilex looked up from a half-finished map with the expression of a woman watching two disasters converge. Sable's hand drifted to the knife at her belt.

"What," Oren said quietly, "do you mean?"

Maren lowered herself onto the bench by the cold hearth as though her legs had finally submitted their resignation. She looked very old suddenly, older than the white hair suggested — old in the marrow, old in the ledger of her.

"Eleven years ago," she said. "Maybe twelve. Time runs strange when you spend it. Two men came to Greyhollow. Fine coats, soft voices, faces you forgot the moment you looked away — I thought then that they were merely forgettable, and I have since learned better. They said they represented interests in the capital. They said they were collecting certain rare talents. And they offered me more coin than Greyhollow sees in a generation, for one thing." Her eyes came up and met Beatrix's across the room, and did not flinch, and he hated her for that, and understood later that the not-flinching was its own confession. "For the boy's sight. Not borrowed. Sold outright. They wanted him delivered to the capital by the turn of the leaf-fall, papers signed, apprenticeship transferred, and I was to take the coin and tell myself I'd done right by him — that a hedge-witch's cottage was no place for talent like his anyway."

* * *

The rage did not arrive all at once. It arrived the way floods do — a sound first, far off, then water over the tops of everything.

"You sold me," Beatrix said.

"I refused," Maren said.

"You SOLD me—"

"I REFUSED." She was on her feet, the hazel switch cracking against the hearthstone hard enough to split it, and for one moment the cottage-witch Beatrix had followed through hedgerows learning which mushrooms lied and which stars kept secrets stood inside her weathered frame like fire inside a lantern. "Listen to me, boy, because I have walked nine days through nothing to say this and I will only say it once. I refused them. And then I sat awake for three nights doing sums, because refusal is cheap when it costs you nothing, and I asked myself what refusing would cost, and whether I had any right to refuse on your behalf, and whether a life in the capital with fine patrons might not be worth more than mushrooms and frostbite. And on the fourth night I wrote back and said yes."

The flood crested. Beatrix heard himself make a sound he did not recognize as coming from his own chest.

"And then," Maren went on, quieter, relentless, "on the morning they came to collect you, I burned their contract in my own hearth, packed you a satchel with two apples, and told you to run to the city and lose yourself among people who'd never heard of Greyhollow. And I told you never to write, and I burned your letters unread, and I let you believe—" her voice caught, actually caught, the first crack in her, "—that I'd tried to sell you. Because if the agents thought I'd gone back on the deal out of love rather than greed, they'd have taken us both, and they'd have taken the sight out of you with instruments I did not want to learn the names of. Hatred keeps a boy away better than love does. So I made sure you hated me. It's the one piece of craft I ever regret learning."

The hall was very still. Beatrix became aware distantly that Sable had moved to stand near him — not touching, just near, the way she had learned to be near him on the road that forgets — and that Bramble had set a bowl of soup on the table beside Maren without comment, because even betrayal, in his cosmology, deserved soup.

"You let me hate you," Beatrix said. "For four years. I built my whole self out of running from you. I joined the guild because—"

He stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, a shape in fog.

Because why? He had always told himself he'd fled to the city and simply fallen into the guild's orbit, luck and coincidence. But he remembered now — a new memory, surfacing clean as a fish breaking water — the night he ran. Maren pressing a scrap of paper into his hand at the gate, whispering: *If it goes wrong, find the Guild of Impossible Things. Tell them Maren sent you. Tell them the hedge-witch pays her debts.* He had lost the paper somewhere on the north road. He had found the guild anyway, drawn to it like water finding a drain, and had never once questioned why a runaway's feet had known exactly where to go.

"She sent me here," he said aloud. "You sent me to the guild."

"The guild is the only institution in this country the agents feared," Maren said. "Older than the crown. Deeper than the archives. If anyone could keep you safe, it was madmen who trade in impossibilities." Her gaze slid to Oren Vey, and something passed between the two of them that Beatrix could not read — recognition, guilt, an accounting deferred. "Though I begin to wonder how much safety money bought here, too."

Oren did not answer at once. When he did, his voice was careful as a man crossing ice. "What did the agents look like, Madam Witch? Precisely."

"Forgettable," Maren repeated. "But their hands — I remember their hands. Ink-stained to the wrist. And each of them wore a ring with thirteen stones."

Beside Beatrix, Sable breathed in sharply. Across the hall, Ilex laid down her pen. And Oren Vey closed his eyes as though the weight of twelve moon-turns of guilt had just acquired a thirteenth.

"Thirteen stones," Oren said. "Yes. I know the ring." He opened his eyes and looked at Beatrix, and for once the practiced calm was gone entirely, and underneath it was something raw and exhausted and almost young. "Then it's worse than we thought, boy. They weren't buyers. They were clerks. The founder's own collectors, still making rounds, still gathering thread-sight wherever it grows — because someone with your eyes is the one thing in all the world that could unpick what he stitched. Which means the founder didn't just fear you, Beatrix Dunmore. He's been paying attention to you your entire life."

The hearth ticked. Somewhere above them, faint and patient, the spire began — so softly it might have been imagination — to sing.

And Beatrix, standing between the woman who had lied to save him and the man who had lied to buy time, felt the last comfortable lie of his life come loose: that he had ever been a bystander in this story. He had been its subject since before he could write his own name.

"Maren," he said slowly, "the night I ran. The paper you gave me. You said the hedge-witch pays her debts. What debt? Owed to whom?"

Maren looked at the fireless hearth for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone quiet enough that the whole hall leaned in to catch it.

"Not owed by me, boy. Owed *to* me. Your mother's family held the freehold on Greyhollow's thread-line — every ward-stone in that valley was tied to her blood. When the agents came sniffing after your sight, I told them the truth: the thread they wanted runs through a bloodline, and bloodlines can't be sold, only inherited or extinguished." She met his eyes. "They took the second option under advisement. So I need you to understand something before you decide what to feel about me. Those men didn't want your gift for the guild's sake or the crown's. They wanted it because the founder needs a thread-seer's hands to amend his bargain — and he needs them before the thirteenth moon comes full."

The spire's song swelled, one clear note, unmistakable now, threading through the guildhall like light through a keyhole.

"And if the thirteenth moon finds no heir to amend it," Maren finished, "it collects the whole country. Every page. Every name." She picked up the bowl of soup Bramble had left her, as though the world were a reasonable place. "So. Shall we talk about how we're going to save it?"
