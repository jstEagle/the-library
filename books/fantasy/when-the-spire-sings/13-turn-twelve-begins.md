## 13. Turn Twelve Begins

The moon came up wrong.

Oren Vey stood in the guildhall's eastern gallery with a cup of cold chicory going bitter in his hand, watching it clear the rooftops of the Shambles. He had watched twelve hundred moons rise from this window — no, he corrected himself, not twelve hundred, that was absurd, perhaps four hundred, perhaps fewer; the number slid away from him like water through cupped fingers — and never once had one risen *crooked*. But this one climbed the sky at an angle, leaning eastward as though it were trying to look back at something behind itself, and its light fell across the city in long slanted stripes that made every shadow point the same direction, like iron filings obeying a magnet no one could see.

Twelfth turn. One left.

Behind him, in the hall below, the guild was arguing.

They had been arguing since sundown, and Oren had let them, because an argument was a living thing and the guildhall needed all the living things it could hold. The great table had been pushed against the wall to make room, and the guildsmen stood in loose clusters on the mosaic floor — the mosaic that showed the guild's founding, the founder's outstretched hands, the words worn to illegibility by two centuries of boots. Ilex stood near the hearth with her arms crossed over her ink-stained apron, her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was winning an argument she hadn't yet been allowed to have. Bramble had planted herself in front of the kitchen doorway like a siege fortification, wooden spoon in hand as if she intended to beat back whichever faction displeased her. And scattered among them, the newer members, the refugees who had drifted in since the Night Without Stars — a bell-ringer whose tower no longer existed, a tax assessor who kept trying to assess things that had stopped existing, three sisters who had arrived holding hands and now could not remember each other's names, only the shape of the holding.

And Beatrix. Standing apart, as he always stood slightly apart, near the ledger shelf, one hand resting on the spines as though the books might anchor him. The boy had a way of listening with his whole body, head tilted, eyes moving from speaker to speaker like a man reading a page too quickly. His thread-sight, Oren thought. Even now, even exhausted, the boy was probably seeing the argument itself as threads — the bright strands of loyalty, the fraying ones of fear, the knot where Sable's voice pulled tightest.

Sable stood in the center of the floor, and it was Sable the room bent toward, because Sable had been to the edge of the unwritten lands and come back with her boots still on.

"—and I'm telling you what I saw," she was saying, in that flat, hard voice she used when feeling threatened the softness of her own heart. "I walked the border after the wards fell. I walked it for six days. You don't fight erasure with swords. There is nothing to strike. You swing at it and your arm comes down through empty air where a hill used to be, and then you start forgetting why you swung." She turned slowly, addressing the whole room. "There is a door. The archive proved it. It opens for someone who belongs nowhere, and we *have* someone—"

"You want to spend him," said the bell-ringer, Harl, whose voice still carried the resonance of bells even though his tower was a hole in the world now. "That's what you're saying. Spend him like coin."

"I want to *save* someone," Sable snapped. "Thirty of us walked into this hall. Do you want to save thirty, or do you want to be noble and save none?"

"And go where?" This from one of the nameless sisters, her voice thin. "Through the door into what? A country we've forgotten? We'd be refugees from a place that no longer remembers it ever held us."

"Better refugees than ghosts," said Harl.

"Better ghosts than cowards," said Bramble, and the spoon came up like a marshal's baton. "You want my opinion? Nobody's asked, but you'll have it anyway. The founder traded this country away once because he thought there was nothing worth fighting for except running. Look how well that turned out. Thirteen turns of borrowed time, and now the bill's come due and everyone wants to skip town before the collector arrives." She jabbed the spoon toward the eastern window, toward the crooked moon. "We are the Guild of Impossible Things. Since when has *impossible* meant *unwise*?"

A rumble of assent, and a counter-rumble, and the room threatened to boil over again. Oren took a breath, set down his chicory, and started down the stairs.

His legs felt strange on the stairs. That was new. Not weak, exactly — but unfamiliar, as though he were descending steps in a house he'd visited often but never lived in. He counted them without meaning to. Eleven. There should be fourteen. Or was it nine? He gripped the rail and kept his face composed, because a Guildmaster's face was the last wall standing when everything else fell.

*You have been paying installments,* he reminded himself. *Small ones. A childhood here, a face there, the name of the woman who taught you your first cantrip. You knew the cost when you signed.* But knowing a cost and paying it were different trades entirely, and lately the collector had stopped being polite about the difference.

He reached the bottom step and the room turned to him, the way rooms always did, and he felt the weight of every gaze settle on his shoulders like a mantle cut from lead.

"The Guildmaster speaks," said Harl, half-mocking, half-hopeful. "Let's hear wisdom."

Oren crossed to the hearth. He had given a thousand speeches from this spot — he was almost sure it was a thousand; it might have been forty — and he knew the trick of it: begin low, let the firelight do the work, speak to the fear rather than the argument.

"My friends," he began, "when I was a young man, younger than some of you—" he gestured vaguely toward Beatrix, and found, with a lurch like a missing stair in the dark, that the vague gesture was all he had. The name would not come. He reached for it and his mind presented him instead with an image: a hedge-witch's cottage, rain on the roof, a boy hunched over a ledger by candlelight writing in a small precise hand. The image was vivid. The name was gone.

"—younger than our apprentice," he finished smoothly, and heard his own voice perform the smoothness, and hated it.

But Beatrix had looked up sharply. Of course he had. The boy saw threads; he must have seen the thread between Oren's intention and Oren's word go suddenly slack, a snapped stitch in an otherwise sound seam. Their eyes met across the room, and Beatrix's expression did something terrible and gentle: it did not correct him. It simply *held* him, the way you hold a stumbling elder's elbow while pretending to admire the view.

"The door is real," Oren said aloud, because the argument deserved honesty. "Ilex's map-work confirms it. The compass confirms it. And it is true that it will open for one of us and not for others." He let the silence sit. "So here is what a Guildmaster knows that a frightened man forgets: a door is only a door. What matters is which side of it you stand on when you decide."

"You're saying stay," said Sable flatly.

"I'm saying that fleeing is also a trade," Oren said. "And I have seen the price list. We flee, and the country dies unwitnessed. Everything it was — every song, every grudge, every recipe Bramble guards like scripture — goes out like a candle in an open window. And whatever lies beyond that door receives thirty ghosts carrying fragments of a nation nobody there will know existed." He shook his head slowly. "Or we stand. And we find the impossible thing. Because finding impossible things is, I am reliably informed, the entire point of this establishment."

A laugh moved through the room — small, tired, real. Even Sable's mouth twitched.

It was Ilex who broke the fragile peace, stepping forward with a rolled map in her fist. "Then we stop debating whether and start planning how. The spire sings louder every dusk. Its script lists what's been erased — but lately—" her voice caught, and Oren watched this woman who had mapped drowned cities struggle with the sentence — "lately it has begun listing things that haven't been erased *yet*. Pre-erasure. It's singing ahead of the dark. If we can read the order, we can know where it strikes next, and stand there first."

"Stand where?" asked Harl. "With what?"

"With story," said Ilex simply. "With memory. With weight. Every place we fill with remembered things holds longer. My map slowed the erosion around the guildhall by a full day. A full day, bought with paper and recollection." She unrolled the map across a trestle table, pinning its corners with Bramble's salt cellars, and the room crowded in.

Oren crowded in too, and that was when it happened properly.

He was looking down at Ilex's lines — the river, the capital, the road they had named twice because its first name had been eaten — and Beatrix leaned in beside him, close enough that Oren could smell the ledger-ink on the boy's sleeves, and Oren turned to say something to him. Something encouraging. Something a mentor says.

"Beatrix, I want you to take the compass tomorrow and—" No. Wait. That wasn't— He frowned. "Bexley. I want you—" The name came out wrong and he felt it come out wrong, felt the whole room feel it come out wrong. "Bexley. Bex—"

The hall went quiet. Not shocked-quiet. Worse. Careful-quiet. The quiet of people deciding together, wordlessly, not to look at each other.

"It's Beatrix," the boy said softly. "Guildmaster. It's fine. It's Beatrix."

"*Beatrix*." Oren said it like a man setting a bone, and the relief of it nearly buckled him. He straightened his spine. He raised his voice into the register he had used for decades to make halls believe in him. "Beatrix will take the compass. Sable goes with him. Ilex gives them the route. And the rest of us hold this hall, and hold the festival Ilex proposes, and we feed that spire until it sings so loud the dark has to shout over us." He looked around the room, meeting eyes, daring anyone to see the crack in the mortar. "Questions?"

No questions. Only the terrible tenderness again, moving through the guildsmen like a current — he could see them doing it, arranging themselves around his gaps the way water arranges itself around stones. Bramble refilling his cup without being asked, because he had forgotten he wanted it. Harl positioning himself at Oren's right hand, where a steadying grip could be disguised as deference.

And Beatrix watching him with those ledger-keeper's eyes, cataloguing, no doubt, the exact dimensions of what was being lost. Writing it down somewhere behind them, in that tireless internal book.

*He will remember me,* Oren thought, standing at his own hearth while the meeting dissolved into work-teams and whispered routes. *When I cannot remember myself, the boy will carry the entry. That is what ledgers are for.*

The thought should have comforted him. Instead it felt like reading his own obituary in a stranger's handwriting.

* * *

Past midnight, when the hall had gone quiet and only the coals spoke, Oren sat alone in the eastern gallery with the founder's ledger open on his knees — the dead-hand ledger, the damning one, recovered from the hidden drawer weeks ago and read so many times the pages had gone soft as cloth.

*One memory per page,* he thought, turning a leaf. *Thirteen turns of the moon. A fair rate, said the thing in the dark, for a nation entire.*

He had helped. He wanted that written plainly somewhere, even if only in the private court of his own dissolving mind. He had been young and the country had been dying and the founder had wept when he signed, actual tears, and Oren had believed — still believed, on his good days — that grief could sanctify a monstrous arithmetic. On his bad days he knew better. On his bad days he understood that he had not paid much, early on. The collector had been patient with him. Patient, and strategic. It had taken his memories of the trade first, so that he would forget the weight of what he carried. Then the faces of the negotiators, so he could not testify. Then, gently, gradually, the past — his mother's kitchen, his first snow, the color of the sea at Carrowmere — each one lifted like a coin from a sleeping man's purse.

Now the collector had grown impatient. Now it took in daylight. Now it had reached his tongue and taken a boy's name out of his mouth mid-sentence, in front of everyone, because the thirteenth turn was close and debts called in last hours grow loud.

Footsteps on the stairs. He knew the rhythm before the figure appeared — quick, light, hesitant at the top landing. The apprentice. Beatrix. He said the name silently to himself, twice, three times, hammering it into the boards of his mind like nails into a roof before a storm.

"You should be sleeping," Oren said. "Tomorrow you walk toward the capital."

"So should you." Beatrix came and sat on the bench across from him, uninvited, which was one of the things Oren privately loved about the boy — the way his manners stopped exactly where his care began. In his hands he carried a slim volume. The guild's active ledger. "I've been making entries tonight. Festival preparations, supply counts, Ilex's route notes." He hesitated. "And one more."

He opened the ledger and turned it around. By the light of the guttering lamp Oren read the fresh ink:

*Entry 447. An old man keeps forgetting a name. The name refuses to be forgotten. Logged as an impossibility in progress. Witnessed: the whole guild, who have agreed — all of them, without being asked — to say it often.*

Below it, in different hands, already: Harl had signed as witness. Bramble. Ilex. Three wobbly signatures from the nameless sisters. Even Sable, whose signature was just an aggressive slash.

Oren stared at the page for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far off, faint as a pulse behind a wall, the spire sang its inventory of losses.

"This is a maintenance log," he managed at last. "You've entered me as a thing to be maintained."

"I enter everything the guild won't let break," Beatrix said. "That's most of it, these days." He closed the book gently on his own hands. "Say it back to me. Once. So it's in your mouth and not just mine."

"Beatrix," Oren said. "Apprentice. Keeper of ledgers. Boy who logged a key that opens yesterday and never told me how he closed it again."

Something flickered across the young man's face — pain, maybe, or recognition, the ghost of the reason he had run from Maren's cottage, the reason Oren himself could no longer fully retrieve — and passed. "Good," he said quietly. "Keep it. Hold onto it the way you hold the rail on the stairs."

So he had noticed that too. Of course he had.

"They'll ask you to lead them," Beatrix said, standing, tucking the ledger under his arm. "After I leave tomorrow. They'll look at you because that's what they've always done. And you'll open your mouth sometimes and the words won't be there." He met Oren's eyes, and there was no pity in it, which Oren was grateful for beyond speech. Pity would have been unbearable. This was something else — an accounting. A refusal to round down. "When that happens, sir — let us finish your sentences. Let us be your margin notes. A guild is a ledger written in many hands. Yours isn't the only one anymore."

He went down the stairs — counting them, Oren noticed; eleven, and the boy didn't correct the count either — and his footsteps faded into the dormitory wing, and Oren sat alone again with the dead man's ledger and the lamp and the distant singing.

Outside the eastern window, the twelfth moon hung at its wrong angle, pouring its slanted light across a country with eleven days left in it. Twelve days. Twelve days. He gripped the number like the stair rail.

And far away, at the edge of hearing, beneath the spire's lament, he thought he heard another sound — thinner, older, patient as dust settling — the sound of something on the far side of the world turning a page.
