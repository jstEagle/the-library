## 14. The Cartographer's Last Map

Ilex had always believed that a map was a kind of promise.

Not a record — records could lie, could rot, could be burned by men who wanted the past to be tidier than it was. A map was a promise that a place would still be there when you arrived. That the river you crossed on Tuesday would still remember its banks on Friday. That the road did not end in nothing simply because no one was walking it.

She had made ten thousand promises in her fifty-one years. She had kept every one of them.

Now she sat in the guildhall's map room with a blank sheet of vellum the size of a dinner table and realized she was about to break her last.

* * *

The erasure had reached the coast road overnight.

Bramble brought the news up from the kitchen at dawn, flour still on his knuckles, his voice carefully level in the way he reserved for catastrophes. "Fisherwick's gone," he said. "The whole town. Not burned, not flooded. *Gone*. And worse—" He stopped, pressed his thumb and forefinger to the bridge of his nose. "Worse, I can't remember what we called the fish they sold there. I know we ate it. I know I liked it. The name is just... a hole."

Ilex knew the feeling intimately. She had been waking with holes for weeks now. A village whose church bell she could once describe down to the crack in its lip. A bridge she'd crossed forty times. Her mother's sister's face, which had been ordinary and beloved and was now a smooth oval where a person used to be.

The country was being unwritten, and it unwrote in order of forgettability first. Fisherwick had been small. Fisherwick had been easy to lose.

But Ilex had mapped Fisherwick when she was nineteen years old, on her first solo commission, and she remembered every street of it — not despite the erasure but somehow *through* it, because her memory of places was not stored the way other people's memories were. Other people remembered Fisherwick as an experience: the smell of brine, a particular innkeeper's laugh. She remembered it as lines. As distances. As the angle at which the harbor curved away from the lighthouse and the exact width of the causeway that flooded at spring tide.

The erasure ate stories. But a map was not a story. A map was a *structure*, and structures were harder to chew.

That was when the idea came to her — fully formed, terrible, luminous, the way her best ideas always arrived, already knowing their own price.

* * *

"You want to draw the whole country." Oren Vey stood in the doorway of the map room, and even in the grey half-light of the starless morning he managed to look like a man who had considered every possibility and found all of them wanting. His hands were clasped behind his back. They had been shaking lately; he hid them when he thought no one noticed. Ilex noticed. She noticed everything. It was the cartographer's curse. "From memory. All of it. Every shire, every river, every—"

"Every named thing." Ilex smoothed the vellum with both palms. It was good stock, calfskin, bought years ago for a commission she'd never taken. It had been waiting. Perhaps everything had been waiting for this. "Yes."

"To what end? The land is vanishing faster than any hand could draw it. You'd be painting a portrait of a man while he burns."

"I'd be making him too heavy to burn." She looked up at him. "Think about what the wards were, Oren. Think about what Sable told us. They didn't keep enemies out. They held the story *in place*. Weight. Tension. Anchorage. The contract took the country's story as payment, page by page, but a story is only as real as the number of minds insisting on it. If one mind holds enough of it — precisely, structurally, obsessively enough — then the unwriting has to work around that mind. It has to go slower. Maybe it has to stop."

"You don't know that."

"I don't know anything," Ilex said. "I'm a cartographer, not a philosopher. But I've spent my life learning how much weight a line can carry. A border drawn on paper has held off armies, Oren. Hasn't it? Men have died for ink. Ink is not nothing. Ink is a *claim*."

Oren was quiet for a long moment. Through the window behind him, where stars should have been wheeling over the rooftops of the city, there was only flat black, like paint over a window. Like the sky had been closed for renovation.

"And the cost?" he said finally.

Ilex smiled, because she had known he would ask, and because she had been composing her answer since before dawn, and because a certain kind of honesty deserved a certain kind of ceremony.

"You know what thread-sight is for Beatrix. Seeing the connections between things." She turned back to the blank vellum. "Map-sight is its cousin. When I picture a place, I don't just see it. I see it *newly*. Every time. The light is different, the shadows have shifted, a cart has moved into the square since the last time I looked. My memory doesn't store images, Oren. It stores *instructions for generating* them. And instructions can run forever."

"But?"

"But the erasure doesn't take what's written. It takes what's *writable*. What could still change." Her throat tightened, and she made herself say the rest plainly, the way she drew borders: without decoration. "If I pour every place I know into this map — fix them, final them, make them permanent claims instead of living instructions — then I will have spent my ability to imagine. Any of it. Ever again. I won't be able to picture a room I've never entered. A face I haven't met. Tomorrow." She laid her hand flat on the empty calfskin. "I'll be able to walk through a door and never know what's on the other side until I'm standing in it. For the rest of my life."

Oren crossed the room slowly. He was older than her by fifteen years and had carried guilt longer than either of them cared to count, and when he put his hand on her shoulder it weighed like an anchor dropped in shallow water.

"I can't ask that of you," he said.

"You're not asking. I'm telling." She picked up her pen. "Besides — think of it professionally. Every map is a sacrifice, if you do it honestly. You kill the territory to make the portrait. The living, breathing, changing country dies on the vellum so that the idea of it can survive. I'm just doing it more thoroughly than most."

"That's a horrible philosophy."

"It's mine," she said. "I'm rather attached to it. One of the few things left that I am."

* * *

She began in the northwest, because that was where she had begun her life's work, and because endings should echo beginnings whenever possible. It was the cartographer's superstition, and she had few enough superstitions left worth keeping.

The first line was the coast itself. She closed her eyes and let Fisherwick rise — not the memory of it, but the *generation* of it, the old machinery turning over, the streets assembling themselves from her stored geometry. The harbor curving away at eleven degrees from true north. The lighthouse on its basalt thumb. The crooked lane where the houses leaned together like conspirators. The fish market, the salt-stained steps, the bell tower with its cracked bell that rang a half-tone flat and everyone loved it anyway.

She drew it all. Every line she set down, she set down twice in her mind — once as image, once as claim. *This is real. This was real. This will remain real.*

Somewhere below the vellum, she felt it happen: a faint resistance, like a current pushing back against a dammed river. The erasure, meeting something it couldn't easily swallow.

She kept drawing.

Hours passed. Bramble brought food and she forgot to eat it. Beatrix came and went quietly, fetching her fresh pens and ink, saying nothing, watching her with those ledger-keeper's eyes that saw threads everywhere — and after a while she felt him seeing hers, the bright filaments running off the map and out through the walls of the guildhall, anchoring somewhere far away in ground that was trying to forget it existed. He didn't tell her what he saw. He just started bringing tea instead of leaving it on the stairs, and refilling her cup before she asked. That was how Beatrix spoke. In maintenance.

By dusk she had finished the northwest quarter. The coast, the fens, the hill country, the moor roads, four hundred villages she had visited or heard described by reliable witnesses, each one pinned down in ink like butterflies on a board.

Her head ached in a way she had never felt before — not pain exactly, but *absence*, a hollowness spreading outward from behind her eyes, as though rooms were being emptied in a house she still lived in.

She tested herself, quietly, while the others argued strategy in the hall below. She tried to picture the sea.

She got the sea. Of course she did — the sea was on the map, fixed, final, hers forever. Then she tried to picture the sea *at sunset*, in colors no one had ever painted, under a sky she invented — and found nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. An instruction that no longer ran.

It was beginning.

She flexed her fingers, dipped her pen, and moved to the southeast quarter.

* * *

On the third night, Maren the hedge-witch found her still working, swaying in her chair, and stood in the doorway for a long time before speaking.

"You know what you're doing to yourself," Maren said. It wasn't quite a question. Hedge-witches dealt in trades too, and recognized the shape of one from across a room.

"I know exactly what I'm doing to myself." Ilex didn't look up. Her pen was moving along the southern ranges now, the high passes she'd crossed at twenty-six with frostbite in two toes and the most beautiful sunrise of her life — a sunrise she was, even now, spending. "I'm doing what the founder wouldn't. He traded the country away. I'm trading myself *for* it. It's a better bargain. Mine comes with interest."

"He'd have hated you."

"The founder?" Ilex allowed herself a thin smile. "Good. Maps should provoke someone."

Maren came closer, looked down at the vast sheet — three-quarters filled now, a nation resurrected in iron-gall ink, dense with names, each name a nail, each nail a hold against the dark. "Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

"Will you stop?"

"No."

Maren nodded slowly, as if a bargain struck between two women who understood prices needed no further negotiation. But at the door she paused. "When this is done," she said, "when the country's saved or it isn't — come find me. There are hedge-crafts for mending minds. Old ones. Ugly ones. They might not work. But you shouldn't spend everything and then assume the bill is final. That's not cartography. That's surrender."

Ilex stared at her, and for the first time in three days, let her pen rest. "You're offering to unspend something?"

"I'm offering to try." Maren's mouth twisted. "I've cost people enough. Let me cost myself a little for once, in a good cause."

Then she was gone, and Ilex, after a moment, returned to the map — but lighter, somehow, the way a traveler feels lighter knowing there's a waystation ahead, even if the waystation might be a ruin.

* * *

She finished on the fifth day.

The last region was the center — the capital, the spire, the guildhall itself, the small circle of streets where she had lived her whole adult life. She saved it for last deliberately. A map should end where the heart is. And she drew it with more care than anything else, every alley, every gutter, every door, because these were the places the erasure would reach last and hardest, and she wanted the final nails driven deepest.

When it was done, she sat back, and her hands were steady, and the map covered the whole table and hung over the edges like a tablecloth woven from a nation.

Beatrix stood beside her. He had barely left the room in five days. Now he looked at the finished work, and then at her, and his eyes went wide and wet.

"It's singing," he whispered. "Ilex — the spire. Can't you hear it? It changed."

She couldn't hear it. She had never been able to hear the spire; that gift belonged to others. But she could feel the map under her palms, and it felt different from every map she'd ever made. It felt less like paper and more like ground. Like standing on the country itself.

"How much is left?" Beatrix asked gently. "Of your... of the imagining."

Ilex considered lying. Then she considered that lying was a kind of invention, and she wasn't sure she could manage one anymore.

"Let's find out," she said. She closed her eyes. "Describe me a color that doesn't exist yet."

Silence. Long silence. She felt him take her hand.

"I can't make new pictures," she said at last, opening her eyes. "Only the ones I've banked. Everything I'll ever see again, I've already seen. Everything I'll ever imagine is on that table." She touched the vellum one more time, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping child. "Which means, Beatrix Dunmore, that from today onward, I am entirely dependent on the world to surprise me. I can't foresee anything. I can't plan a single tomorrow in my head. Whatever happens next — at the spire, in the capital, in whatever remains of turn twelve — I will experience it blind, like everyone else."

She looked up at him, and found she was smiling, and meant it.

"So for pity's sake," she said, "make sure whatever happens next is worth not being able to picture."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, the spire began to sing — and this time, everyone in the guildhall could hear it, and this time, it was not listing what had been lost.

It was calling them south.
