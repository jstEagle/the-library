## 16. The Founder's Bargain

The winter the stars began to die, Elowen Vance still believed that grief could be outworked.

She was forty-one years old, and she had spent eleven of those years keeping the ledgers of a country that no longer believed in itself. The Kingdom of Vael had not been conquered. It had not burned. It had simply begun — slowly, politely, the way ice leaves a pond — to stop being true. First the border villages forgot their own names and answered to whatever strangers called them. Then the roads shortened overnight, as though the land between towns had grown embarrassed by its own length. Then the children were born with eyes the color of nothing in particular, because their parents could no longer remember what color eyes ought to be.

The scholars called it attenuation. The priests called it the Quiet. Elowen, who had been a hedge-witch's apprentice before she was anything else, called it what it was: the country was being unwritten.

And she was one of perhaps six people alive who could see the threads.

* * *

She saw them the way other people saw weather. Every object in the world carried a filament of story binding it to meaning — a wedding ring threaded to its vows, a hearthstone threaded to every meal ever cooked upon it, a name threaded to the mouth that first spoke it. Most people lived inside their threads without ever feeling them pull. Elowen had been born able to see the whole loom.

It was thread-sight that told her the truth about the Quiet. She stood in the market square of Carrowmere on a gray morning in the last week of autumn and watched a baker's stall come loose from its own history. The threads attaching the man to his bread, his bread to his father's recipe, his recipe to the wheat fields east of the river — she watched them thin, go gray, and part, one after another, like harp strings snapping under a slow hand. The baker did not notice. He kept selling loaves he could no longer remember how to make, and the customers kept buying bread they could no longer remember tasting, and by noon the stall was just wood and canvas and a smell of something almost like flour.

Elowen went home and opened her ledger and wrote, in her small precise hand: *Carrowmere loses its bread. We are running out of country.*

Then she did what she always did when the world broke somewhere she couldn't reach. She went looking for someone to trade with.

* * *

The moon came down to meet her at the standing stones above the Vale of Ashes, which was not a poetic name but a description; the valley had burned so long ago that even the fire's memory had gone out of the soil, and only the stones remembered being warm.

She had sent up the invitation three nights before — ink made from her own blood, written on birch bark, burned at the new moon. Old magic. Desperate magic. The kind of magic you used when you had already decided the price didn't matter, because everything that mattered was already being taken from you.

The moon did not descend in any way her eyes could properly follow. One moment the sky was empty over the stones; the next there was a presence the size of the horizon folded into the space between two monoliths, and Elowen's thread-sight screamed. Because the moon had threads too — thousands of them, silver and vast, running down to every tide, every wolf-song, every lover who had ever sworn by its light. And every single thread was taut. Held. Like a creditor's hand closed around a debt.

"You are the one who keeps accounts," the moon said. Its voice was not sound so much as gravity — a pressure behind her sternum, a weight in the roots of her teeth. "We have watched you write."

"Then you know why I've called." Elowen kept her hands flat on the cold stone. It steadied her, touching something old enough to have survived this long. "My country is dying. Not its people — its *being*. Something is unwriting us. I want to know if it can be stopped."

"It cannot be stopped," said the moon. "It can be delayed. These are different things, ledger-keeper, and I will not pretend otherwise to you, since you of all mortals will check my arithmetic."

"How long?"

"The erasure moves at the speed of forgetting. A turn of the moon buys you what your people would forget naturally in a season — no more. Thirteen turns is the most I may lend against a thing this large. After that, the debt comes due, and the country finishes dying all at once instead of slowly." A pause, and the pressure of the voice softened by a degree Elowen might have imagined. "You should know that I am not cruel. I am only old, and old things keep strict books."

"Thirteen months," Elowen said. "Thirteen turns to find another answer."

"Thirteen turns to find an answer that does not exist," the moon corrected gently. "I have watched nations end before, little scribe. There has never been an answer. There is only the question of what you will pay to postpone the ending, and who will pay it."

Elowen looked down at her hands. They were a working woman's hands, ink-stained, chapped at the knuckles, scarred where a hedge-witch had taught her knife-work on nettles and worse. Behind her, down in the dark valley, the lights of Carrowmere were going out one by one — not blown out, not extinguished, but *forgotten*, lamps burning with no one left to remember lighting them.

"What's the price?" she asked.

"For thirteen turns? A memory per page." The moon's light shifted, and she understood suddenly that it was showing her the contract — a great page written in the sky in script older than her alphabet, terms unfolding like wings. "One memory, freely surrendered, for each turn of borrowed time. Not yours alone — that would buy you a season. A nation's worth. Your people will pay in installments they will never feel, because each payment is precisely the memory of having paid. That is the mercy built into the bargain, and you may thank me for it or curse me, whichever lets you sleep."

"And at the end of thirteen turns?"

"At the end," said the moon, "the debt comes due in full, unless someone has found a way to amend the contract. Which no one has. Which no one will." The light gathered itself, patient, immense. "So. Do we have terms, Elowen Vance, keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things?"

She had named the guild four years ago, half in jest, when it had been six eccentrics in a drafty hall trading in charms and curiosities. She thought of the name now and felt it settle around her shoulders like a yoke.

"I have one condition," she said. "Written into the contract. In my hand, in my blood, unamendable by either party."

The moon waited. Old things, she was learning, could afford patience; it was only the dying who hurried.

"When I sign," Elowen said, "I want it recorded that this contract is *flawed*. Deliberately. By design. I want a flaw woven into it so deep that no power in this sky or under this earth can smooth it out — a loose thread, left loose on purpose. Because I am going to spend these thirteen turns training successors, and building a guild, and teaching children to see what I see. And someday — maybe in thirteen turns, maybe in thirteen hundred — someone with thread-sight is going to stand where I'm standing and look at this contract and *see the flaw*. And unpick it. And rewrite the founding line."

Silence, then, of the kind that has mass. The monoliths seemed to lean in.

"You wish to defraud me," the moon said at last, and there was something in the gravity of its voice that Elowen, in later years, would lie awake wondering about. Wondering if it had been anger. Wondering if it had been hope.

"I wish to leave a door open," she said. "You're lending me time. I'm telling you now, honestly, to your face: I intend to use that time to break your contract. If that makes the loan unacceptable, then let my country die honest."

The moon was quiet a long while. When it spoke again, the terms had changed — subtly, in ways she would need years to fully map. The flaw was there. Woven in. A single thread in the great page of the sky, left deliberately slack, deliberately visible to anyone born with eyes like hers.

"Sign," said the moon.

Elowen Vance drew the knife across her palm and wrote her name in the oldest ink there is.

* * *

The installments began that night, and they were gentle, exactly as promised. No one felt themselves forgetting. A grandmother in the river towns lost the lullaby her mother had sung her, and hummed a tuneless substitute without noticing the difference. A soldier lost the memory of his commissioning day and kept the medal anyway, polishing it out of habit he could no longer explain. A child lost the face of a dog that had died the summer before, and grieved a little less, and everyone said how well she was taking it.

Elowen felt her own payments too. The taste of her mother's honey-cakes went first. Then the sound of her teacher Maren-of-that-age's laugh — no relation, she would note grimly in her journal, to the hedge-witch line that would one day produce a girl named Maren, though the coincidence would itch at her for years. Then the color of the sea, which she had seen once, at nineteen, and had carried with her through every gray winter since.

She wrote each loss down as it left her. That was the discipline. That was the work. *Paid this night: the smell of rain on hot stone. Paid this night: my brother's middle name. Paid this night: why I started keeping ledgers at all.* The ledger filled, and the country held, and the stars stayed lit, and Elowen learned to measure hope in lunar increments.

But she also wrote the other book. The hidden one. The one she sewed into the spine of the guildhall itself, beneath the foundation stone, where only someone with thread-sight would ever think to look.

In it she recorded everything the official histories would be allowed to forget: the true terms of the bargain, the location of the flaw, the shape of the amendment that could undo it. And on the last page, in ink she renewed every year until her hands were too old to hold a pen, she wrote the instruction that mattered most:

*To whoever finds this: the contract can be rewritten, but only by exchange, never by refusal. The moon does not forgive debts; it only accepts different payment. Find the door. Find the one who belongs nowhere — for the contract binds citizens, and only a non-citizen's hand can amend it without tearing the page. And when you find them, tell them what I am telling you now, at the end of my strength, with the twelfth turn nearly spent and my own name beginning to slip:*

*Tell them it is not a sacrifice. Tell them it is a trade, and trades are the guild's whole business, and no one in the history of impossible things has ever gotten something for nothing. Whatever they give up, they must be given something back. Love, if you have any sense. Love, and a place at a table, and their name written in a living hand.*

*I was not able to save myself. Save them better than I saved me.*

*— E.V., founder, Guild of Impossible Things*

* * *

She died in the guildhall, in the twelfth month, with snow at the windows and Oren Vey — young then, freckled, terrified, holding her hand — asking her over and over what he was supposed to do, what the thirteenth turn would bring, whether there was anything else, *anything*, she wanted him to know.

"There's a flaw in the contract," she told him, because her memory of hiding it had been paid away years before, and she was discovering it fresh, each time, like a woman finding a letter she'd forgotten writing. "There's always been a flaw. I'm sure of it. I can almost see it. Someone will see it, someday. Someone with the sight." Her grip tightened. "Find them, Oren. Keep the guild alive until you find them. Promise me the guild outlives me."

"I promise," Oren Vey said, and meant it, and would spend centuries learning what the promise cost.

Elowen smiled, and outside the window, for the first time in twelve months, a star went dark.

Not all at once. Just one — a small blue one low over the eastern hills, the kind sailors steered by, the kind children wished on. It guttered like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the sky closed over the place where it had been, seamless, as though it had never hung there at all.

Down in the city, ten thousand people glanced up at the same moment, feeling a loss they could not name, and then forgot to feel it.

And high above the guildhall, unseen by anyone, the spire — her spire, raised in the guild's first year, stone by stone, each block inscribed with a word she hoped the country would remember — began, very softly, very faintly, to sing.

* * *

Centuries later, in a field of standing doors under a sky with no stars, a young man with thread-sight stood before the thirteenth door and heard, threading through the founder's ancient voice, the last line of a dead woman's hope:

*"I left the flaw for you. I only pray you found someone to sit with you in the dark afterward."*
