## 17. Everything We Kept

The guildhall smelled of woodsmoke and burnt sugar, and Beatrix stood in the middle of it holding a candle he had not been asked to light.

It was Sable's idea, though she would deny it later. She'd come back from the well at dusk — the well still worked, which was itself a small miracle, water remembering how to be water even when the sky above it had forgotten its stars — carrying two buckets and a look on her face that Beatrix had learned to read as the beginning of trouble.

"We're doing it wrong," she said, setting the buckets down with a thud. "All of us. Oren's in his study trying to remember the founder's name, Ilex is drawing maps until her hands bleed, Bramble's cooking like grief can be salted. And the spire's up there singing into the dark, and we're just — hiding under it."

"The spire sings what it has," Oren said from the doorway. His voice was steady, but his hands were folded too carefully, the way men fold their hands when they are counting something they cannot afford to lose. "Story. Memory. We've given it what ledgers hold. Names, dates, trades, debts."

"Ledgers," Sable repeated, with the contempt of a woman who had spent her life breaking things built by careful people. "The country isn't made of ledgers, old man. It's made of *this*." She kicked the bucket, sloshing water across the floorboards. "Of songs people sing wrong on purpose. Of the way Bramble burns the bread every fourth day because his grandmother did, and no one corrects him because the burning *is* the recipe now. You want to feed a country back to itself? Feed it dinner. Feed it the ugly parts. Feed it everything you're embarrassed to write down."

Beatrix looked down at the ledger in his arms. He had carried it for so long it had worn a groove in his hip, a groove he could feel through three layers of cloth. The pages were full of small impossibilities, catalogued and cross-referenced, each one a thread he had once believed held the world together.

He had been right about the threads. He had been wrong to think they were only his to count.

"Tomorrow night," he heard himself say. All three of them turned. "We do it tomorrow night. A festival. Everything we kept — everything we never wrote down. Songs, recipes, arguments, names of dead friends, whatever anyone has left. And we sing it at the spire until the spire has more story than the dark does."

Oren was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "That is either the bravest or the most foolish thing proposed in this hall since its founding," and Beatrix thought he saw, beneath the practiced calm, something that might have been relief.

* * *

They worked through the next day like people preparing a feast during a siege.

Bramble took over the kitchen with a ferocity that startled everyone who had only known him as the gentle cook-mage. He pulled out recipes he had never written down — his grandmother's honey-bread, which required singing to the dough in a dialect that hadn't existed since before the wards went up; a stew whose ingredients changed depending on what the eater needed rather than what the pot contained; a candied flower that tasted, he said, "like being forgiven."

"You'll all eat," he announced, stirring a pot that glowed faintly gold, "and while you eat, you'll tell me one thing your mother used to say. Anything. A scolding counts."

Ilex spread paper across the great table and began, not mapping places, but drawing *people*. Faces she remembered from villages already erased. Hands. A dog. She drew them quickly, desperately, as if each line were a rope thrown into dark water. "If I draw them," she muttered to no one, "then someone existed. That's all authorship is, really. Someone insisting."

Sable went door to door through what remained of the town below — fewer doors than there had been, and some of those opening onto rooms that flickered like bad candlelight — and collected things. She came back with a child's wooden horse, half-forgotten; a funeral hymn hummed to her by an old man whose name she couldn't learn because he no longer fully had one; a jar of river-water from a stream that had begun running backward, "because it's ours even when it's confused."

And Beatrix opened the guild ledger to its last clean page and began, for the first time, to record things that had happened rather than things that could not be.

*Bramble burned the bread today. On purpose. It was perfect.*

*Ilex laughed at Sable's impression of Oren. Oren did not laugh, but his mouth did something complicated.*

*Sable held the wooden horse for a long time before putting it down.*

He wrote until his hand cramped, and then he understood, suddenly and completely, why the founder had traded memory for time. Because memory was the only currency that mattered, and spending it felt exactly like love.

* * *

They lit every candle in the guildhall at dusk.

Then they climbed — all five of them, carrying food and instruments and a stubbornness that no erasure had yet found a way to unpick — to the base of the spire, where the stones rose up into a darkness that had once been sky.

Oren spoke first. His voice cracked twice, and both times he kept going, which Beatrix understood now was the whole art of being a guildmaster.

"This hall was founded on a lie," he said. "A beautiful, desperate lie — that impossible things could be catalogued, filed, maintained. But the truth underneath it, the truth the founder knew and feared and hoped for, is this: impossibility is not a thing you keep. It's a thing you *give*. Every meal cooked for someone else. Every name spoken aloud after death. Every stupid song. Those are the entries that matter. So tonight—" his voice broke a third time and steadied, "—tonight we make our entries loudly."

Bramble sang first. The bread-song, in the dead dialect, and the dough rising beside him seemed to lean toward his voice like a plant toward light. He taught them the chorus, and they sang it wrong in four different ways, and the wrongness made it theirs.

Ilex told stories of the drowned archive towns — not their names, which were gone, but their *characters*. The village where everyone argued about the same bridge for two hundred years. The town whose bell rang only when it rained, for reasons no one ever discovered. She gave the forgotten places back their personalities, if not their names.

Sable sang the funeral hymn badly, because she didn't know the words, and the old man's ghost-of-a-name seemed to stand a little straighter inside the melody anyway. She put the wooden horse at the spire's base. "For whoever owned this," she said. "You existed. Somebody remembers the shape of your toy."

And Beatrix read aloud from the new ledger — not the impossible things, but the ordinary ones, the ones that had happened between the misfits in the days since the stars went out. The burnt bread. The laughter. The way Oren had forgotten Beatrix's name last week and covered it with "young man" and how everyone had pretended not to notice and how much love lived inside that pretending.

The spire listened.

Beatrix felt it listening, the way you feel a sleeping animal wake — a shift in the air, a warmth in the stone under his palms. The soft tone it had held since the founder's era deepened, broadened, picked up harmonics. And threaded through the sound, unmistakable now, came words in ledger-script that Beatrix could read with his thread-sight pressed against the stone:

*Honey-bread. Dead dialect. Preserved.*

*Bridge-argument village. Character: contentious. Preserved.*

*A boy's wooden horse. Loved. Preserved.*

"More," Sable shouted, laughing and crying at once. "It wants *more*!"

So they gave it more. Bramble fed everyone stew that tasted like whatever they most needed, and the eating itself became an entry. Oren recited the guild's founding oath, stumbling, correcting himself, refusing the smooth version in favor of the true one. Ilex described faces until her voice gave out. Beatrix read until the candlelight caught the ink still wet on the last page.

And above them the spire sang louder than Beatrix had ever heard it — louder than grief, louder than the erasure's silence — a song made entirely of small kept things, and for one shining hour the dark beyond the guildhall walls seemed less like absence and more like a held breath.

* * *

It was very late when the song softened again into something almost conversational, and the others drifted down toward sleep, and Beatrix stayed with his palm flat against the stone.

The spire spoke to him alone, quietly, in script that flowed under his fingers like water:

*One turn remains.*

*I have counted what you have given me. It is much. It is not enough.*

*The contract stands in my heart, keeper. What was signed can be amended. But amendment requires payment, and the door of payment opens only from within.*

He closed his eyes. He thought of the field of standing doors. He thought of the founder's voice offering escape, and of the flaw deliberately left in the bargain, centuries old, waiting like a seed.

*"I left the flaw for you. I only pray you found someone to sit with you in the dark afterward."*

He had found them. They were asleep downstairs, smelling of woodsmoke and burnt bread, having chosen him over and over without once asking what he cost.

Below, a floorboard creaked. He turned to find Sable leaning in the doorway, arms crossed, watching him with the expression she wore when she was deciding whether to break something.

"You've figured out what you're going to do," she said. It wasn't a question.

Beatrix looked at her — at the ward-breaker who regretted, the cook who sang to dough, the cartographer giving away her own eyes, the mentor forgetting himself one memory at a time — and found that the truth was too heavy to lift alone, and too important to hide.

"Tomorrow," he said. "When the last moon rises. Stay close to me, all of you. Whatever happens at the spire — stay close."

Sable studied him for a long moment. Then she pushed off from the doorframe and came to sit beside him against the warm stone, shoulder to shoulder, saying nothing, offering the only thing she had ever been certain of:

She would not let him face the dark alone.
