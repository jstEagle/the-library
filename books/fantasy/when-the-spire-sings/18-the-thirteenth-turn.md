## 18. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon rose wrong.

Beatrix felt it before he saw it — a pressure behind his eyes, like the moment before a sneeze or a sob — and then he was standing in the guildhall's ruined courtyard with everyone else, faces tilted up, watching the thirteenth moon climb over the spire as if it had always been there and had simply been waiting for permission to be noticed.

It was pale. Not white — *pale*, the way paper is pale, the way a page is pale before anything is written on it. It had no craters, no seas, no shadows. It was blankness wearing the shape of a circle, and it rose without light, casting nothing, illuminating nothing, and yet every surface in the courtyard seemed brighter for its presence, the way a wound looks cleaner just before it bleeds.

"It has no name," Ilex said beside him. She was holding her last map against her chest like a shield, though Beatrix knew by now that the map was not for shielding. It was for feeding. Every hour since she'd drawn it she had been whispering to it — names of towns, names of rivers, names of people — and every hour the ink held a little longer against the erasure gnawing at the country's edges. "Nothing that has no name can be argued with."

"Everything can be argued with," Maren said. "That's what arguing is *for*." The hedge-witch stood at the courtyard's edge with her arms crossed and her staff planted like a flagpole, and she did not look up at the moon. She looked at Beatrix. She had been looking at him, on and off, for three days now, in the particular way of someone rehearsing an apology they haven't earned the right to give yet. "The question is whether we argue with it from inside the room or outside it."

"The door holds," Sable reported. She came around the corner of the guildhall at a jog, ward-chalk dusted to her elbows, and Beatrix watched Oren try to follow the sound of her voice and fail. The Guildmaster stood near the fountain — dry these two months now, its stone mermaid missing her left arm and, last Tuesday, her face — with his hands folded and his chin lifted in the posture of a man listening closely to something no one else could hear. He had been holding that posture more and more. His memories went out of him now like heat out of an open window, and he had taken to standing very still whenever he felt one leaving, as if stillness could fool the erasure into thinking he wasn't home.

"Beatrix," Oren said, when the silence stretched too long. "Beatrix Dunmore. Apprentice, third year. Keeper of ledgers." He said it slowly, deliberately, the way you set down something fragile. "I say it aloud so I'll know it tomorrow. Whoever I am tomorrow — write it down where I can find it."

"You wrote it down yourself this morning," Beatrix said gently. "Twice. On your wrist and on the ledger's flyleaf."

"Good lad." Oren smiled at the middle distance. "Good lad. Now — the moon is rising, which means the turn has begun, which means—" he frowned, reaching, grasping, "—there is something I was supposed to tell you. Something about the founder. Something about *doors*."

"I know," Beatrix said. "You told me. Yesterday. And the day before."

"Then I've been useful three times," Oren said, "and I intend to be useful a fourth," and he laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle like river ice, and no one pretended not to hear it.

Above them, the blank moon cleared the spire's crown.

And the spire began to sing.

* * *

They had heard it sing before. For weeks it had been a low thread of tone, readable only as script — Ilex transcribing it in the margins of her maps, translating grief into geography: *Ashenford, gone. The Vell family, gone. The name of the river at Marlow's Bend, gone.* A mourning song. An inventory of loss, sung by the last thing in the country stubborn enough to keep records.

This was not that song.

The note that came off the spire now was high and hard and getting higher, a whistle building toward a scream, and where before it had listed what was lost, now it listed what was *leaving* — and Beatrix understood, with a lurch, that the difference mattered, that the spire had changed registers the way a person changes from reporting to pleading. It was not taking notes anymore. It was calling roll. And everything it called was answering with silence.

"Ledger-script," Ilex whispered, her hands moving involuntarily, sketching the tones into the air as if she could catch them. "But fast. Faster than I can — *Hollowmere*, gone, *the word for the blue of evening*, gone, *the second verse of the miller's song*, gone—" Her voice shook. "It's reading us out, Beatrix. Line by line. The whole country is one entry long, and we're nearly at the bottom of the page."

"How long?" Sable asked.

Ilex didn't answer. She didn't have to. They all looked up at the blank moon, hanging fat and featureless at the top of the sky, and did the arithmetic together: one moon-turn of time left, and a moon that had never had a name counting down hours that had already been sold.

"We go now," Beatrix said.

He had rehearsed the speech. He'd written it out the night before in ledger-hand, neat and certain, all the arguments lined up like beads on a thread: why it had to be him, why the compass pointed at his chest and nowhere else, why a boy who belonged nowhere was the only key that fit a door out of everywhere. But standing under that screaming spire, he found he didn't need any of it. The speech fell out of him and left only the plain thing underneath.

"I'm going into the spire. I need you three to hold the door."

Maren's jaw tightened. Sable said, "Define hold."

"Hold it open. Hold it *shut*. Whatever the spire asks of it." He met each of their eyes in turn — Sable with her chalk-burned hands and her old guilt worn smooth as river stone; Maren who had sold him once and had walked out of the unwritten lands to unsell him; Oren, who was already forgetting why he was standing in a courtyard at midnight but whose feet were planted exactly where a Guildmaster's feet should be. "The contract is written into the stone at the heart. The founder made sure of it — made sure it couldn't be read except from inside, couldn't be amended except by someone with thread-sight, couldn't be reached except through one door. He built a lock and threw away the key because he thought the key would never grow up." Beatrix's voice didn't waver. He was almost proud of that. "He was wrong about the key."

"And after?" Maren asked quietly. "After you unpick his bargain and pay whatever price the paying costs — what then? Do we get you back?"

"That depends," Beatrix said, "on how good you all are at keeping records."

No one laughed. But something passed around the courtyard anyway, something that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite tears either — the look of people agreeing to a plan they hate with every part of themselves except the part that matters.

Oren stepped forward first. He took Beatrix's hand in both of his own, and his grip was strong, and his eyes were wet, and for one clear moment the fog receded and the man who had hired a runaway apprentice three years ago looked out through them whole.

"When you're in there," Oren said, "and the stone offers you the easy line — the line where you walk away and everyone else pays — remember that a ledger isn't honest because it balances. It's honest because someone signed it." He squeezed. "Sign well, Beatrix Dunmore. Third year. Keeper of ledgers."

Then the fog rolled back in, and Oren blinked, and asked pleasantly why they were all standing in the cold, and Beatrix turned away before his face could do anything unforgivable.

* * *

The spire's door was not locked. It had never been locked. That was the thing nobody had understood until now: the founder hadn't needed locks, because the door itself was the test. It opened onto a stair that spiraled downward into the spire's roots — down past the foundations, down past the bedrock, down into the place where the country kept its spine — and every step of the way, the stair showed you the life you could have had instead.

Beatrix climbed down through them.

First there was a kitchen — his mother's kitchen, though he could no longer swear to it; there was bread smell and a window with rain and a woman humming with her back turned, and he knew, the way you know a dream, that if he stepped into it the erasure would take him gently and the woman would turn around and love him and he would never again know that he had ever been anywhere else. He kept walking.

Then a workshop: hedge-witch shelves, bundled herbs, a stool sized for a child, and Maren younger in it, kinder in it, saying *stay, boy, stay, I'll teach you everything and sell none of it* — and he kept walking.

Then the guildhall as it might have been if he'd never run away at all: warm, unbroken, the fountain running, the mermaid whole, and Oren laughing at some joke with all his memories intact, and a chair pulled out at the long table with a plate set at it, waiting, patient, *his* —

Beatrix stopped.

The plate had bread on it. The bread was warm. Somewhere above him the spire screamed its inventory, and somewhere below him the contract waited in the stone, and here in between was a chair with his name on it in a world where none of this had ever needed doing.

"You're not real," he told it.

"No," said the founder's voice, from everywhere, from the walls, from the warm bread, gentle as ever, tired as ever. "But you are, and that's the tragedy. Sit down, boy. Let them save themselves. You owe this country nothing — you ran from it. I checked. It's in my ledger."

"My ledger says otherwise." Beatrix's voice came out steadier than he felt. "Entry one. *A runaway apprentice presents himself at the guildhall gate, requesting work.* Signed, witnessed, filed. I didn't run from this country. I ran *to* it. There's a difference, and you of all people should respect it — you taught me that every entry is a thread, and threads don't care why they were tied. Only that they hold."

Silence, then — the particular silence of an old man being contradicted by a fact.

The kitchen faded. The workshop faded. The guildhall held longest, the way the truest things let go slowest, and Beatrix walked out of its warmth into cold black stone, and the stair ended, and the heart of the spire opened before him like a wound.

* * *

The chamber was small. Smaller than it should have been, for the center of everything — a low round room of veined grey stone, lit by no lamp, warmed by no fire, and yet glowing faintly along every seam, because the seams were *writing*. Lines of ledger-script ran through the rock in every direction, dense as veins, bright as embers, converging on the far wall where the founding contract stood inscribed floor to ceiling in letters tall as doors.

Beatrix had imagined it a hundred times. He had imagined fire, thunder, a voice like a landslide. What he found instead was handwriting. Careful, cramped, human handwriting, crowded with amendments and marginalia and little annotations in different inks across four hundred years — a document, worked on, worried at, *maintained*, the way everything in this country worth keeping had always been maintained by tired people who refused to stop.

He found the founding line easily. It was the oldest ink, and it glowed the dimmest, worn thin by centuries:

*Let the nation of [name] stand thirteen turns, in trade for [value], rendered by [signatory], unto the creditor that waits between the stars.*

Three blanks. Three words eaten out of the world so thoroughly that even the stone had forgotten them — the country's name, the price paid, and the hand that paid it. Beatrix put his palm against the wall and let his thread-sight open, and for the first time he saw the bargain entire: not lines of text but lines of *thread*, thousands of them, running out of the stone and up through the spire and out across the whole dark country — threads binding every town to every other town, every name to every memory, every star to its socket in the sky. And beneath them all, thicker than any of them, darker, older, a single cable running up and out through the roof of the world to the blank moon, taut as a bowstring.

Thirteen turns. Twelve spent. One left, and the string drawn to the cheek.

Behind him, far above, the door at the top of the stair boomed — once, twice — and then held, groaning against a weight it was never built to bear alone.

"They're holding it," he breathed. "Sable. Maren. Oren." He thought of Oren's hands, strong around his own. *Sign well, Beatrix Dunmore.* "They're holding the door so the spire can't offer me a way out while I'm working."

The founder's voice came one last time, thinner now, coming from further away, as if the man himself had become mostly marginalia.

"There's a line in that contract," it said, "that no one has ever read to the end. Because reading it to the end means accepting what it costs. Are you certain you want to be the kind of clerk who reads to the end?"

Beatrix Dunmore, third-year apprentice, keeper of the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things, pressed his hand flat against the founding line and closed his eyes.

"I'm the kind," he said, "who writes the ending myself."

And the contract began, at last, to answer him — the ancient ink stirring under his fingers like a sleeper turning over, the threads shivering in their courses, the blank moon overhead leaning close to listen — and Beatrix felt the first thread of his own memory go slack in his chest, plucked like a harp string, as the stone asked him, in handwriting older than the country, the only question that had ever mattered:

*What will you trade?*

Above him, muffled through a hundred fathoms of singing stone, he heard Oren call his name — *Beatrix Dunmore, apprentice, third year, keeper of ledgers* — saying it aloud into the dark so that someone, somewhere, would know it tomorrow.

And Beatrix opened his eyes, and reached for the pen that was not a pen, and began to amend.
