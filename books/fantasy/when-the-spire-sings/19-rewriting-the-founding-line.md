## 19. Rewriting the Founding Line

The heart of the spire was not a room. Beatrix understood that within three steps of entering it, though his feet kept insisting on the fiction of a floor beneath them.

It was a throat, or a lung, or the inside of a bell mid-strike — a vast hollow space where the walls were not stone but script, ledger-script, column upon column of it rising into a darkness that had no ceiling and descending into a darkness that had no floor. The words were not written on anything. They *were* the wall. He could see the threads now, even here, even with his hands shaking and his eyes streaming from the song: fine silver filaments running through every character like veins through a leaf, each one anchored somewhere below in the country he could no longer see and reaching up toward something above that he did not want to name.

And at the center, suspended where a heart would hang if the spire were a body, hung the contract.

It was smaller than he expected. A single page, unrolled, its edges fluttering as though in a wind that existed nowhere else in the world. The vellum was old enough to have gone amber, and the ink had gone brown, but every word still held. Of course it held. Words like these did not get the courtesy of fading.

Behind him, at the threshold he had crossed what felt like a lifetime ago, the door stood open. Maren filled most of it, her hedge-witch bulk braced against the frame, her hands flat on either side as if she could hold the whole doorway shut by stubbornness alone. Beyond her shoulder he could see Sable, one hand raised, ward-light guttering blue around her fingers. And further back, small and grey in the song's glare, Oren Vey, who should not have been able to climb the spire stairs at all, who had forgotten his own mother's name two days past and Beatrix's twice.

"You don't have to do this," Maren said. Her voice came through the song warped, bent, as if it had to travel a long way to reach him. "Boy. Beatrix. You don't."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You called me by my name."

"I've been practicing." Her jaw worked. "In case I only got the one chance left to say it right."

The song rose around him. It was not sound anymore, not really; it had passed through sound the way a river passes through a country, touching everything, belonging to nothing. He could read it now without trying. That was the cruelty of thread-sight: once you saw the threads, you could not stop seeing them, and the spire's voice was made entirely of thread. It sang the inventory of the lost. *Harrowmere, gone. The ferryman Aldous Crane, gone. The word for the smell of rain on hot slate, gone.* Line after line after line, and each line it sang, somewhere behind him the country grew thinner, more transparent, closer to the condition of a page turned blank.

Beatrix stepped forward until the contract hung an arm's length away.

Up close he could see the founder's hand in it — the same hand that had signed his apprenticeship entry, the same confident downstrokes, the same little hook on the descenders that he had spent years unconsciously imitating without knowing why. *One nation, whole and remembered, held against the dark for thirteen turns of the moon. Payment: memory, drawn down page by page, until the account is settled.*

And there, at the bottom, the amendment clause. The founder had been thorough. The founder had always been thorough. *Should any party possess the sight of threads, they may amend this instrument, provided the amendment be paid for in kind.*

In kind. Memory for memory. Story for story.

He had known since the field of doors. He had known since Ilex read the drowned archive aloud in her careful cartographer's voice, and everyone had looked at him with such terrible gentleness that he'd wanted to scream. But knowing a thing and standing before it were different countries, and he had just crossed the border between them.

"Beatrix." Sable's voice now, pushed through Maren's shadow. "There's another way. There has to be—"

"There isn't," he said, "and we both know I'm the only one who can hold a pen here."

The pen. That was the other thing waiting for him, floating beside the contract as if it had been set out for a signing, which it had, which it had been waiting centuries to be. It was a quill cut from a feather he recognized — a spire-wren's feather, white as bone, the kind that nested in the high places and sang back whatever was sung to them. The guild kept one in a glass case in the entry hall. Beatrix had dusted that case every week for four years and never once wondered why the feather inside was warm.

He took it. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

* * *

"Tell me what you're doing," Maren demanded, when he turned the contract over and found the amendment space waiting near the seal — a blank band of vellum, patient as an empty grave. "All of it. Out loud. If you're going to do something this stupid, you'll do it where I can hear every word and hate all of them."

"All right." His voice sounded strange to himself, very calm, very far away. "The contract trades the country's memory for time. Thirteen turns. We're on the last one. When it runs out, the erasure finishes, and everything inside the borders goes — not dies, *goes*, unwritten, undone, the way Harrowmere went. No one will mourn us because no one will remember us to mourn."

"And your fix?"

"The clause says amendments are paid in kind. The country's memory was the original payment." He wet his lips. "So the amendment restores the country's memory. All of it. Every page that was sold. And the payment — the new payment, the exchange —"

"Is yours," Sable said. Not a question. Her ward-light had gone very still around her fingers, the way fire goes still before it gutters out.

"My memories of home." He said it evenly, because if he didn't say it evenly he would not say it at all. "That's what 'home' means in the contract's grammar — not a place. A belonging. Everything in me that knows where I come from. My mother's face. Maren's kitchen. The road I ran down the night I left. The reason I ran. The guildhall's smell. Oren's voice saying my name. All of it, drawn off, page by page, to buy back theirs."

The song dipped, just slightly, as if the spire itself were holding its breath.

"No," Maren said.

"Maren—"

"*No.*" She shoved off the doorframe and came three steps into the heart of the spire, and the script-walls flared around her, offended, and she glared at them until they dimmed. Four hundred pounds of hedge-witch defying the architecture of reality itself. "I sold you once already, boy. I sat across a table from those agents and let them put a price on your thread-sight while you slept upstairs, and I told myself it was for the coven, for the craft, for any lie that fit. You ran because of me. Whatever home means in their fancy grammar, I'm in it, and I am not letting you pay with it. Take my memories instead. Take all of mine. I've half given them away already, being old."

"That's not how it works." His throat ached. "I asked the compass. I asked the spire. It has to be willing payment from the amending party. From the one holding the pen." He lifted the quill. "From me."

"Then put the pen down."

"If I put the pen down, everyone goes. You. Sable. Oren. Bramble and her soup. Ilex and her maps. Every child in every village that hasn't been erased yet." He looked at her, and let her see all of it, because she deserved that much. "You tried to sell me once. This is me selling myself, freely, to buy something worth more than me. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and I need you to understand it, because you're the only one left who knew me before, and someone has to remember that I chose this."

Maren stood there. The song poured through her and she stood there, and then she did the last thing he expected: she laughed, a broken, wet sound, and wiped her face with a forearm the size of a ham.

"Stubborn as your mother," she said. "Go on, then. Go on. But I'm staying right here, and when you forget me, I'm going to introduce myself again, and again, however many times it takes, and one day you'll laugh at something I say and won't know why, and that will be enough. That will have to be enough."

* * *

He turned to the contract.

The blank amendment band seemed to widen as he approached, the way a door widens for someone it wants. He could feel the threads now, all of them, the whole web of the country laid bare to his sight: silver lines running out from the spire's heart in every direction, through stone and soil and story, anchoring bridges and birth-records and the names of hills, and beyond the borders — nothing. Threads ending in frayed darkness, like rope rotted through. Like a book whose final chapters had been torn out.

But the threads were not dead. That was the miracle buried under the horror. They were *dormant*. Sold, not destroyed. Held somewhere in the contract's deep grammar, in escrow, waiting for a payment that would release them home. The founder had built it that way — the grieving, thorough, monstrous founder — because even in his despair he had not been able to make himself destroy anything permanently. He had only ever pawned it.

Beatrix set the quill-tip to the vellum.

Thread-sight was not writing, exactly. It was unpicking and re-weaving, and the quill was only the needle. He closed his eyes and found the first thread — the great master-thread, the one labeled in the founder's hand *the memory of the nation, whole* — and he followed it down into the contract's knot, and there he began to work.

The amendment wrote itself through him rather than by him. He felt the words leave his chest in a way that had nothing to do with breath:

*Let the memory of this country be returned to it, entire, this turn and every turn after. Let the wards stand without payment. In exchange, let the amending party yield his own memory of home — its people, its places, its love — to the same account, page by page, until the balance is met.*

The contract drank the words. He felt it drink, a vast slow swallow, and then the threads began to move.

They came home like birds at dusk.

He watched — he could not help watching, thread-sight was a wound that never closed — as the sold threads caught fire with return, streaming back out through the spire's walls, down through stone, out across the dark country. He felt the wards re-knit along the borders, felt the horizon grow a far edge again. Somewhere a river remembered which way was downhill. Somewhere a village that had been a smudge of forgetting solidified into rooftops and smoke and dogs barking at nothing.

And the stars —

The first star returned directly overhead, punching through the dark like a needle through cloth. Then another, east. Another, west. Each one arrived with a faint soundless chime he felt in his teeth, and each one pulled a thread taut behind it, and the web of the country brightened strand by strand until the whole sky was filling, filling, the way a ledger fills, line by faithful line.

"It's working," Sable breathed from the doorway. "Beatrix, it's *working*—"

"Yes," he said.

And then the payment began.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the worst of it. He had braced for pain, for tearing, for some dramatic rending worthy of the sacrifice, and instead it was gentle, the way sleep is gentle, the way forgetting where you set something down is gentle. The contract drew his memory of home out of him page by page, and each page went soft and warm and weightless as it left, and he felt himself growing lighter and lighter, a lantern with its candle carried away.

His mother's face went first — no, not first; first went the *name* of his mother's face, the word-mirror where he had kept it, so that the face itself floated free in his mind, beloved and anonymous, a portrait with the plaque pried off. Then the kitchen. Then the smell of Maren's bread, which had been the smell of safety, which became simply a smell, then less than a smell, then nothing. The night he ran away unraveled thread by thread, and with it went the reason, and with the reason went the anger, and with the anger went — strangely — the forgiveness too, because forgiveness needs something to forgive.

He kept working. That was the discipline of it, the awful clerical discipline of a lifetime in ledgers: you finish the entry. His hands did not shake. The quill moved. The amendment sealed itself into the vellum in letters of light, and the founder's ancient signature shuddered and accepted the addition like an old scar accepting a new one.

Oren Vey came forward then, past Maren, past the flaring walls, moving like a man wading upstream. He stopped in front of Beatrix and looked at him with eyes that were mostly fog now, and said, in a voice worn thin as onion skin:

"I don't know your name. But I know I would be proud of you, if I could remember why."

"You named me keeper of the ledgers," Beatrix said. "Four years ago. Midwinter. You said I had the neatest hand you'd ever seen and the saddest eyes, and you hoped the work would mend one without ruining the other."

Oren's face crumpled and cleared and crumpled again, a man watching weather cross his own fields. "Did I," he whispered. "What a clever fellow I must have been."

Above them, the last star slid home.

The song changed.

It had screamed for a turn of the moon, and now, all at once, it did not. It settled into a low, round, complete chord, the sound of an account balanced, a book closed properly, a debt discharged to the final copper. The script-walls dimmed to a warm ember-glow. The contract rolled itself shut with a sigh like wind through wheat, and sank into the heart of the spire, and was gone, and the threads of the country hummed around Beatrix like a struck bell finally allowed to rest.

He lowered the quill. He looked at his hands, steady, empty, and tried to remember why his face was wet.

"Why am I crying?" he asked.

Nobody answered. Maren had turned away, her shoulders heaving. Sable had both hands pressed over her mouth. And Oren Vey, keeper of nothing, remembering nothing, reached out and took the quill gently from Beatrix's fingers and set it down, and said:

"I don't know, lad. But come away from the wall now. Come and sit by the fire, and tomorrow we'll start a fresh page, and perhaps between all of us, we'll work out who you are."

Beatrix let them lead him toward the door. Behind him the spire sang softly, sweetly, a lullaby in ledger-script, and the stars burned in their courses, every one, and he cried the whole way down without knowing for whom — while in his pocket, unnoticed, a scrap of vellum he did not remember taking bore a single line in his own neat hand:

*A key that opens yesterday. Do not let them tell you who you were. Choose.*
