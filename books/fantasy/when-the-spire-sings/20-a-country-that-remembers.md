## 20. A Country That Remembers

Sable had kept vigil over dying men before. She had sat with ward-breakers gutted by their own craft, with soldiers who bled out in the border mud, with an old woman in the unwritten lands whose name had been eaten out of her while Sable held her hand and lied and said, *I know you, I know you, I know you.* She knew the shape of a vigil: the long dark, the small sounds, the way grief made time move like honey through a cracked jar.

This was not that shape.

Beatrix Dunmore slept on the guildhall's narrow cot like a man who had simply set something down — not fallen, not broken, but *finished* — and his breathing was slow and even and entirely untroubled, as though he had not walked into the heart of a singing spire and traded away every memory of home to buy back the sky.

The stars were still there. That was the thing she could not stop checking, the thing that kept pulling her to the window every quarter-hour even though Ilex had scolded her twice about wearing a groove in the floorboards. Out past the eastern wall, past the rooftops of the town, the stars burned in their old positions, steady as nails hammered into black velvet, and the horizon ran on and on toward lands that existed again. Lands with names. Lands with rivers that remembered which way was downhill.

Dawn came up gold and ordinary, and Sable watched it happen from the window with her arms folded tight across her chest, because if she did not hold herself together she was afraid she would come apart at the seams the way the country nearly had.

* * *

By full light, the town had begun to remember how to be itself.

She heard it first as noise — cart wheels, dogs, someone shouting cheerfully about fish prices — and then as something stranger underneath the noise: the sound of a nation exhaling. People came out into the streets and stood blinking in the sun like creatures emerging from a burrow after a hard winter. A woman embraced a lamppost. An old man knelt in the middle of the market square and pressed both palms flat against the cobbles, and when Sable leaned closer she saw that his lips were moving, and she did not need thread-sight to guess what he was saying. Probably a prayer. Possibly just his own name, repeated, to be sure of it.

Nobody knew what they owed. That was the strangest part, the part that sat in Sable's chest like a swallowed stone. The country had been bought back, restored, stitched whole along every border — and the people walking around in it had no idea there had ever been anything wrong. They remembered the Night Without Stars, surely; you could not erase a night like that from ten thousand minds at once without erasing the minds. But they had already begun to smooth it over, the way memory does. Already telling each other it had been an eclipse. A strange weather. A bad dream shared by everyone at once, which happened sometimes, didn't it, in the years when the moons misbehaved?

Only the guild remembered the truth, and one of them had paid for the remembering.

Sable turned from the window. Bramble was in the kitchen below, and the smell rising through the floorboards was porridge and honey and something spiced that Sable suspected was Bramble's answer to grief — cook-mages fed people when they could not fix them, and no one in the history of cooking had ever been worse off for it. Oren Vey sat at the long table with a cup of tea going cold in front of him, staring at nothing. He had aged a decade in a single night. There were gaps behind his eyes now, soft-edged holes where installments used to be, and when Sable spoke to him he answered a half-beat too late, as if translating from a language only he could hear.

"He's still sleeping," she said.

Oren nodded slowly. "Good. Let him." He looked down into his cold tea. "Sleep is where the mind does its mending. Or so I'm told." A pause. "I believe someone told me that once. I can't recall who."

Sable's throat tightened. She crossed the room and put her hand on his shoulder, briefly, awkwardly — she was not a woman built for comfort, she had spent too many years taking things apart for coin — but she left it there until he covered it with his own.

"He'll wake up," she said. "And when he does—"

"When he does," Oren said quietly, "he won't know us. And we will have to decide, all of us, what we are to him. Strangers who claim to be family. That is a delicate trade, Sable. I've made many bad ones in my life, and I can tell you the worst are the ones where you ask someone to accept something they cannot verify."

"Then we don't ask," Sable said. "We show him. However long it takes."

Oren smiled, and it was the saddest smile she had ever seen on him. "You've changed since the road," he said. "Do you know that? When you came to my guildhall you smelled of lockpicks and regret. Now you smell of—"

He stopped. Frowned. Sable watched him grope for the word and lose it, watched the gap swallow it whole, and her heart cracked a little more.

"—of something better," he finished at last. "That will have to do."

* * *

She took the midday watch alone.

Ilex had gone up to the map room, ostensibly to redraw the borders from her recovered survey notes, though Sable suspected the real reason was that Ilex could not bear to look at Beatrix yet — not after watching him walk into the spire, not after the cost. Maren had gone home to her cottage at the edge of the unwritten lands, or what had been the edge; she'd said she needed to see whether her garden still existed, and she'd said it lightly, but her hands had shaken as she'd buckled her traveling cloak, and Sable had understood. Maren had her own debts to face. Guilt was a country too, and some people had to walk its borders alone.

So it was Sable who was sitting beside the cot when Beatrix opened his eyes.

It happened without drama. No gasp, no startle. His lashes simply lifted, and there were his eyes — brown, warm, familiar, and utterly vacant of her. He looked at her the way you look at furniture. Politely. With mild curiosity and no recognition whatsoever.

"Hello," he said. His voice was rough with sleep. "Where am I?"

"The guildhall," Sable said. "In Corrowmere. You're safe."

"Corrowmere." He tried the name like a foreign dish. "Is that... am I from here?"

"No," Sable said, and then, because lying to him felt like a second theft, "Neither am I. Not originally."

He pushed himself up against the headboard. Up close he looked thinner than she remembered, hollowed at the cheeks, and there was a fine tremor in his hands that he seemed not to notice. He looked around the little room — the cot, the washstand, the window with its view of the singing spire — with the methodical attention of a man taking inventory of a room he'd been locked in.

"There was something," he said slowly. "Before I woke up. Something I was supposed to—" He pressed his palm against his sternum, frowning. "There's a hole. Right here. Like a tooth that's been pulled. I keep putting my tongue in the space where it should be." His eyes came up to hers, and they were frightened now, properly frightened, the fear of a man who has realized the floor of himself is missing. "Did something happen to me?"

Sable had rehearsed this. All through the night she had rehearsed it, turning sentences over like stones, looking for the ones least likely to wound. And now that the moment had arrived, every rehearsed sentence crumbled, and what came out was the truth, plain and clumsy:

"You saved us. All of us. Everything. And the price was your memories of home, and we would give them back if we could, and we can't, and I'm sorry."

He stared at her.

"That's not an answer," he said at last, not unkindly. "That's a eulogy."

A sound escaped Sable that might have been a laugh or might have been something sadder. "Yes," she admitted. "It rather is."

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Sable."

"Sable." He nodded gravely, committing it. "Are we friends?"

The question went through her like a blade slipped between ribs — clean, precise, devastating. Because the honest answer was *yes*, yes, more than friends, closer than blood, the kind of friend forged on a road that forgot itself, sharing bread and watches and the confession of her worst sin under a starless sky — and he would never know any of it unless they told him, and telling him was not the same as giving it back.

"We're getting there," she said.

He considered that. Then, with the small stubborn dignity of a man refusing to be pitied, he held out his hand. "Then let's start properly. Beatrix—" He stopped. Blinked. "Beatrix. Is that right? It feels like a word I found in a drawer."

"That's your name," Sable said. Her eyes were stinging. She took his hand anyway. His grip was cool and careful. "That one's yours. They couldn't take that."

"They couldn't take that," he repeated softly, testing it. And then, unexpectedly, he smiled — a crooked, tentative thing, but a smile. "Good. I like it. It sounds like someone who writes things down."

Sable laughed then, really laughed, wet-eyed and startled by it, because of course he liked it, of course the instinct survived the erasure — the ledger-keeping heart of him, the part that saw threads binding objects to stories, the gift that had started everything and ended everything and was, apparently, indestructible.

* * *

Bramble brought up porridge with honey, and Beatrix ate it like a man who had never been fed anything so good, which broke Sable's heart further, because she remembered that Bramble had cooked for him a hundred times and he had always complained — fondly, endlessly — about the cinnamon. He complained about the cinnamon now too, and Bramble burst into tears and hugged him, and he endured it with the baffled patience of a stranger being embraced at a funeral, and Sable saw him file the moment away somewhere, carefully, the way he must once have filed ledger entries.

In the afternoon, Ilex came down with a sheet of paper and her good pens, and asked, very formally, whether he would permit her to make a map of his memories — of what remained, that is, of the territory of him.

"I don't have much territory," Beatrix said. But he closed his eyes and dictated, and Ilex drew: a hedge-witch's cottage he could not picture, only describe from fragments. A road he had run down at night, direction unknown. A feeling like candlelight. A feeling like being watched. A scrap of vellum—

He stopped.

"What vellum?" Sable asked sharply.

He reached into the pocket of the shirt he'd been dressed in — the same clothes he'd worn into the spire — and drew out the scrap. It had gone unnoticed through the night, through the vigil, through all of Bramble's ministrations. On it, in a hand that was unmistakably his own, neat and precise as everything he had ever written, was a single line:

*A key that opens yesterday. Do not let them tell you who you were. Choose.*

The room went very quiet.

"That's my writing," Beatrix whispered. He turned the scrap over twice, as though the reverse might explain the front. "When did I write this? What does it mean — a key that opens yesterday?"

Sable thought of the first entry she had ever seen in his ledgers, back when the world was whole: *a key that opens yesterday.* The impossible thing he had catalogued in a hedge-witch's cottage, hiding, yearning, belonging nowhere — and she understood, with a certainty that settled over her like snowfall, that the boy who had written that line was still in here. Not as memory. As instruction.

"It means," she said carefully, "that whoever you were trusted whoever you're going to be. He left you a message. He wanted you to choose your own history instead of inheriting ours."

Beatrix looked at the line for a long moment. Then he looked up at each of them in turn — Sable, Ilex, Bramble hovering tearfully in the doorway — strangers all, and yet gathered around his bed like sentries, like family keeping watch over something precious they refused to surrender.

"All right," he said finally. "Then help me choose well."

Outside the window, the sun climbed toward evening, and the spire began its dusk-song, soft and sweet, a lullaby in a script only one person in the room could read — and did not know yet that he could.

Sable sat down beside him, ready to begin.
