## 21. Ledgers Written in Other Hands

The morning came back to him slowly, the way light returns after a long eclipse — not all at once, but in bands.

Beatrix woke in a narrow bed on the guildhall's third floor, in a room that smelled of cedar shavings and old paper and, faintly, of Bramble's honey-bread baking somewhere below. Sunlight fell through the window in a warm stripe across the floorboards. Outside, the city was making its ordinary noises: cart wheels, a fishmonger crying the day's prices, children running somewhere with their shoes slapping stone.

Ordinary noises. He had learned, over the past weeks of his strange convalescence, that ordinary noises were worth more than gold now. Every star that had come back to the sky had come back because of something he could no longer remember doing. The country remembered; he did not. Somewhere in the great exchange — the one written into the spire's heart, amended in ledger-script by his own hand — he had paid away every memory of home: Maren's cottage with its crooked chimney, the smell of her hedge-magic, the reason he had run, the face of the woman who must have been his mother. All of it, spent like coin, so that a nation could keep its own past.

He sat up. His hands were steady. That was the strangest thing — the steadiness. A man ought to tremble when he wakes as a stranger to himself. But Beatrix had found instead a kind of clean quiet, like a page before anything is written on it.

There was a knock at the door. Two soft raps, then a pause, then one more — some rhythm that felt like it should mean something, though he couldn't say what.

"Come in."

Sable entered carrying a tray: bread, butter, tea gone slightly oversteeped. She was lean and sharp-eyed as ever, ward-breaker's calluses still ridged along her fingers, but she had taken lately to wearing her hair tied back with a blue ribbon, which he understood — without knowing how he understood — was new.

"You slept late," she said.

"I dreamed," Beatrix said. "I think I dreamed. There was a hedge. And someone laughing behind it." He frowned. "I don't know if it was mine or if it was just weather in my head."

Sable set down the tray. She looked at him for a long moment with an expression he had learned to read in these weeks: careful, watchful, tender in the way of people handling something breakable that they refused to treat as broken.

"Today might be a good day for it," she said.

"For what?"

"For the ledger."

* * *

The guildhall's great room had been rearranged for the occasion. Beatrix knew this because Oren Vey told him so twice, once on the stairs and once again at the door, as though repetition itself were a form of reassurance. The Guildmaster looked older than the man Beatrix had first met — or rather, the man whose first meeting he'd been told about, described to him in careful detail by others, since he retained no memory of it himself. Oren had lost much in the final turn, paying out his own past in installments until almost nothing remained. What was left of him was thinner, quieter, prone to standing in doorways looking puzzled at rooms he had owned for forty years. But his eyes were still kind, and kindness, Beatrix had decided, was memory enough for a beginning.

Inside, the long table had been cleared of everything except ledgers.

Dozens of them. Stacked in towers, laid open flat, fanned across the wood like the wings of sleeping birds. Beatrix recognized them by their bindings — the guild's particular green leather, the brass corners worn dull by generations of thumbs — though he could not have said where he recognized them from. It was like recognizing your own handwriting in someone else's diary.

Ilex stood beside the tallest stack, her ink-stained fingers laced together. The cartographer had paid her own price for Ilex's map — the great act of defiance that had slowed the erasure — and now she could draw only what was put in front of her, unable to picture anything new. But her eyes, when they found Beatrix's, held no bitterness. Only the patient attention of a woman who had made her peace with authorship of a different kind.

"We thought you should see them," Ilex said simply. "All of them. Whenever you're ready. Or never, if you choose never. That's yours to decide too."

Beatrix crossed the room slowly. His reflection ghosted along the polished table as he went, and he watched it pass over the open pages like a cloud crossing a field.

The first ledger lay open near the near edge, its entries in a hand he knew instantly, intimately, helplessly: his own.

*Entry 4,112. A key that opens yesterday. Provenance unknown. Found in the pocket of a coat that does not exist, sold by a man who insists he has never been born. Threads: three. One runs backward through time, fraying. One runs to a door in the capital that no longer opens. One runs to me.*

He read it twice. The words were his, the observations were his, the peculiar way the entry turned inward at the end — *one runs to me* — was his. But reading it was like watching a stranger perform a familiar gesture. He knew it was himself. He simply couldn't feel the self in it.

"This is the first thing I ever logged?" he asked.

"The first thing you logged here," Sable corrected gently. "You'd been keeping records since before you came. Hedge-witch training. She taught you to write things down so they couldn't be argued with later."

Maren. The name surfaced without any feeling attached to it, like a fish rising to touch the surface of dark water and sinking again. He had been told about Maren. He had been told what she tried to do — sell his thread-sight to the founder's agents — and why he ran, and how she had walked out of the unwritten lands alive to find him, and how she had wept in the guildhall kitchen while Bramble fed her soup she couldn't taste through her own tears.

She was downstairs now. He had seen her twice since waking, in hallways, at meals. Each time she had opened her mouth as though to speak and then closed it again, as though the apology were a heavy object she kept picking up and setting back down.

"She'll come up when she's ready," Sable said, following his gaze toward the door. "Or she won't. We don't arrange people here, Beatrix. We only keep accounts."

* * *

They gave him the morning, and he spent it reading himself.

It was the strangest act of archaeology imaginable: excavating a person from paper. Entry by entry, page by page, the shape of Beatrix Dunmore assembled itself out of evidence. He learned that he had a habit of underlining thread-counts twice when something frightened him. He learned that he had once spent four days tracking a spoon that stirred itself whenever someone nearby lied, and had grown genuinely fond of it, and had recorded its eventual disappearance with what the marginal note called — in his own hand — "an unprofessional degree of grief."

He laughed out loud at that one. It startled everyone in the room, including himself.

"There," said Bramble from the doorway, flour on her forearms, her cook-mage's rings glinting. "That's the sound. Knew we'd get it back eventually."

Bramble brought up a second kind of archive entirely: songs. From the festival — *Everything We Kept*, the night the misfits had fed the spire enough story to sing louder than the erasure — she had written down every verse, every chorus, every half-remembered ballad and bawdy song and lullaby that anyone could recall. And threaded through them, in a different ink, were songs that had no source. Songs about a boy who saw threads. Songs about a runaway apprentice who catalogued impossibilities. Songs the guild had made about *him*.

Bramble sang one, quietly, badly, happily, while Beatrix followed the words on the page:

*Oh, the ledger-keeper's eyes see the stitching of the sky,*
*Every thread that holds a hill up, every knot that keeps time dry,*
*And he wrote down all our wonders in a hand both neat and true,*
*So the country would remember all the things it never knew.*

"That last verse changed," Bramble said, tapping the page. "Sable added a line the night after you woke. Listen." She hummed it: *and the keeper who unmade him so the rest of us could stay.* Her voice cracked slightly on the last word. "We've sung it every dusk since. Spire seems to like it. Sings along, sort of. Underneath."

Beatrix touched the page where the new line was written. His throat worked. He didn't cry — he had discovered that grief without memory was a strange animal, present but deniable — but something in his chest shifted, settled, took weight.

"These are my hands' work," he said. "These songs. This festival. Whatever I did in the spire." He looked around the room, at Oren dozing lightly in his chair, at Ilex cataloguing the ledgers into some order only she understood, at Sable pretending very hard to be interested in the window. "You're giving me back a person. But he isn't me. Not exactly. He's — a portrait painted by everyone who loved him."

"Aye," said Bramble cheerfully. "And what's wrong with that? Best portraits always are."

* * *

In the afternoon, Maren came upstairs.

Beatrix heard her on the stairs before he saw her — a slow, deliberate climb, the tread of a woman making herself do something difficult one step at a time. When she appeared in the doorway, the hedge-witch looked smaller than he remembered being told she looked. Her grey hair was plaited practically, her hands were stained green to the wrist with some tincture, and her eyes were red-rimmed in a way that suggested either sleeplessness or a recent and hard-fought battle with composure.

She stood there a moment. Then she crossed the room, took the chair opposite him at the table, and sat down with the air of someone presenting themselves for judgment.

"I practiced this," she said. "Forty times walking here. Had a whole speech. Very dignified." She exhaled. "Gone. All of it. Gone."

Beatrix folded his hands on the table and waited.

"I tried to sell you," Maren said bluntly. "Your gift. Your sight. To agents of a dead man's bargain, before I even properly understood what the bargain was — I told myself it was for your good, that they could teach you, that a hedge-cottage was too small a life for what you were. And maybe I believed that. Maybe half of me did. But the other half was tired, Beatrix. Tired and poor and greedy for a better winter, and I dressed it up as love because love was the only currency I had that didn't shame me." Her voice roughened. "You caught me at it. You saw the threads of the deal right there in the parlor, didn't you — saw what they'd tie you into. And you ran. And it broke my heart that you were right to."

The room had gone very quiet. Even Oren had stopped dozing.

"I'm not asking you to remember forgiving me," Maren said. "You can't, and it wouldn't count anyway, would it — forgiveness on loan, borrowed against a debt you don't recall. I'm asking something harder. I'm asking you to forgive me fresh. Now. With nothing but what's in front of you: an old woman who did a terrible thing to a boy she loved, and who spent eleven moon-turns walking through hell to stand in front of him and say so." She slid something across the table.

It was small, wooden, worn smooth. A whistle, carved in the shape of a wren.

"You made this when you were nine," she said. "For the wrens in my hedge. You couldn't whistle it yourself — never learned — but the birds answered anyway. I kept it. I don't know why I kept it, except that throwing it away felt like throwing away the part of me that hadn't yet done the worst thing I ever did." Her jaw tightened. "It's yours. Not as proof. As a choice. If you want the boy who carved it to have existed, take it. If you'd rather start clean — leave it on the table and walk out, and I'll never trouble you again."

Beatrix picked up the whistle. It fit his palm in a way that felt less like memory than like recognition — the particular comfort of an object that had been held by the same hands many times. He raised it to his lips, experimentally, and blew.

The sound that came out was thin and wavering and completely tuneless.

But somewhere outside the window, distantly, a wren answered.

Beatrix lowered the whistle. He found that his eyes were wet, and that he had absolutely no idea which past he was crying for, and that it did not matter even slightly.

"All right," he said, voice unsteady. "Fresh. We begin fresh."

Maren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and gripped the edge of the table as though the world had tilted. Then she reached across and squeezed his hand once, hard, and let go, and stood, and left the room quickly, before dignity could fail her entirely.

At the door she paused. "You underline thread-counts twice when you're scared," she said, not turning around. "You got that from me."

Then she was gone, and Beatrix sat holding a bird of carved wood, crying for a home he could not picture, and finding — to his astonishment — that the tears were warm instead of bitter.

* * *

Evening came, and with it, the last item.

Oren Vey rose from his chair with the care of an old man handling fragile cargo, and brought forth a ledger unlike the others. Newer binding, emptier pages, and on the inside cover, in faded brown ink, the guild's founding inscription — the same words, Beatrix knew from Ilex's account, that had been written into the spire's heart, the bargain-line he had rewritten at the cost of himself:

*We trade in impossible things, that the world may keep its possible ones.*

Below it, the founder's signature. Below that, a column of apprentice signatures stretching down two centuries. And at the bottom, in a hand Beatrix knew from four thousand entries — his own — a single line:

*Beatrix Dunmore, apprentice to the Guild of Impossible Things.*

"It's still there," Oren said quietly. "Your name. The erasure never took it — couldn't. A signature freely given binds deeper than any bargain can unpick. That's guild law, and it turns out it's also the law underneath the law." He set the ledger on the table and turned it toward Beatrix. Beside the old entry, a blank space waited. "But a name signed by a boy who no longer exists is a relic. What sits at this table now is someone new. So we won't inherit you into the guild, Beatrix. We invite you. Sign again — or don't. Either answer makes you family. Only one of them makes it yours."

The room held its breath. Sable had come to stand at his shoulder. Ilex had stopped sorting. Even the spire, far off across the rooftops, seemed to hush, its dusk-song thinning to a listening note.

Beatrix looked at the blank space. Then he looked around the table — at these people who had spent months building him a self out of songs and entries and awkward apologies and blue ribbons and oversteeped tea, because they refused to let him wake up alone in a country he had saved.

He thought: *I cannot remember choosing them.*
He thought: *I can choose them now.*

He dipped the pen. His hand — the hand of four thousand entries, the hand that had rewritten reality in ledger-script — did not shake at all.

*Entry 4,113,* he wrote, in a firm and unhurried line. *One apprentice, returned. No threads attached. Provenance: chosen.*

And beneath it, he signed his name.

The moment the ink dried, far off across the city the spire sang — one clear, bright, unhurried tone, like a bell agreeing. And in the silence after it, from the hallway below, came the sound of quick footsteps on the stairs, and Bramble's voice calling up, breathless and delighted:

"Beatrix! Come quick — the compass. The founder's compass — it's moving again. And there's a new door standing in the courtyard that none of us has ever seen before."
