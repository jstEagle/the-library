## 22. When the Spire Sings

The spire sang at dusk, as it had sung every dusk for a year, and Beatrix Dunmore had learned to set his pen down when it began.

It was not a song one could hum. It was ledger-script made audible — a slow recitation of everything the country remembered about itself, rising from the spire's heart in tones that settled into the bones like warm rain. Tonight it named the bell-founders of Harrow Fen, whose names had come back to them in the spring. It counted the bridges of the Alderwater and found every one of them standing. It spoke a word in a dialect that only three people alive still knew, and somewhere across the city, an old woman laughed out loud for no reason she could name.

Beatrix listened with his eyes half-closed, the way he did each evening, letting the sound wash through the open window of his room above the guildhall. Below him, Lamplight Street was doing what Lamplight Street did at this hour: Bramble arguing cheerfully with a delivery boy about whether her sourdough starter was legally a person (the boy held it was not; the starter, perched on the counter like a smug golden cat, appeared to agree with him). Sable was rehanging the guild sign, which someone — and everyone knew who — had enchanted again so that it read *GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS: YES WE'RE OPEN* in letters that rearranged themselves whenever Oren looked away.

Oren himself sat on the front step, where he sat most evenings now, hands folded over his cane, watching the light go amber and then violet. He had stopped trying to recover what he'd lost. He said once, in the early months, that grief for a memory was like grief for a person who had never existed — you mourned the shape of the hole. But he had also said, more recently, watching Beatrix balance the day's ledger: *I don't remember teaching you your letters, lad, but I remember being proud of you while I did it. That's enough. That's more than enough.*

Beatrix opened the ledger to a fresh page.

A year ago he would have written *key that opens yesterday* or *coin that lands always heads* or *letter delivered before it is sent*, and felt the old ache underneath the ink — the yearning of a boy cataloguing other people's wonders because he had none of his own. Now the pen moved differently. He wrote the way a mason lays stone: deliberately, knowing the wall would hold.

*Entry 4,112. A key that opens tomorrow.*

He had been holding that entry for three days, turning it over, testing its weight. The client was a girl of perhaps twelve named Odile Vetch, who had come up the steps that morning with mud on her boots and her dead father's watchmaker's loupe screwed into one eye, and who had said, with the flat certainty children reserve for true things: "I want to see tomorrow before it happens. Not far. Just far enough to know it's worth walking into."

Bramble had cried into the bread dough. Sable had looked away sharply and pretended to check the wards, which were fine, which were always fine now, which sang along faintly with the spire like harmony lines under a melody.

But it was not impossible. That was the thing. After everything, after the thirteenth turn and the field of standing doors and the founder's voice like frost down the centuries — after Beatrix had traded every memory of home to buy back a country's soul — almost nothing was impossible anymore. Difficult, yes. Costly, yes. The guild's whole trade, he understood now, had never been about impossibility at all. It was about price. Every wonder had a cost, and the guild existed to make sure someone honest stood on the paying side of the scale.

So he wrote the entry properly, the way entries were meant to be written:

*A key that opens tomorrow. Client: Odile Vetch. Price: to be negotiated with care. Notes: the client does not wish to escape her life; she wishes to be certain it continues. This is not the same desire as the founder's. This is the opposite of the founder's. Proceed.*

His hand paused over that last line. He thought of the founder sometimes — the man in the flashback chapter of his own history, the dying nation in his arms, the terrible arithmetic of love. Beatrix no longer hated him. He wasn't sure he forgave him either. Some debts you didn't forgive; you just paid them forward until they changed shape.

*

Sable found him on the roof an hour later, because Sable always found him on the roof, because the roof was where he went when the dusk-song finished and the quiet afterward got too large.

She lowered herself onto the shingles beside him without ceremony, which was how Sable did everything — ward-breaker's economy of motion, nothing wasted, nothing performed. For a while they watched the lamplighters work their way up Cordwainer Row, little blooms of gold opening street by street.

"Odile Vetch," Sable said.

"What about her."

"You're going to take the commission."

"I'm going to negotiate the commission."

"She's twelve."

"She's twelve," Beatrix agreed, "and her father died in the unwriting, and she doesn't remember his face any better than I remember my mother's, and she came to us because we're the only people in this country who understand what it is to want something back that the world says is gone." He turned the pen in his fingers. "I'm not going to give her a key that opens tomorrow, Sable. I'm going to teach her why she wants one, and then I'm probably going to give her something better. That's what Oren would do."

"Oren would charge her double."

"Oren would charge her double and then lose the invoice." He smiled. The old joke, worn soft as a river stone. Somewhere below, Oren's laugh drifted up through the window, answering something Bramble had said, and Beatrix felt the familiar tug behind his ribs — the shape of the hole, mourned but no longer bleeding.

Sable was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone she used for things that mattered: "One year ago tonight."

"I know."

"You never mark it."

"I mark it every day." He tapped his own temple. "In here. There's a lot of empty shelf space. I've been putting things there on purpose. Today's entry: one year since I rewrote the founding bargain. Witnessed by: everyone who stayed."

Sable bumped her shoulder against his — the closest she came, most days, to saying the word *family* aloud. She didn't need to say it. The word had been rebuilt into him the way Ilex's map had rebuilt the roads: not from memory, but from love, laid line by line by people who refused to let him be a stranger in his own life.

Below, the guildhall windows glowed. Ilex was visible at the long table, drawing — she still drew every night, maps of places that existed now, though she could no longer picture anything new and worked entirely from description, from the words other people gave her. She called it *collaborative cartography*. She called it, privately, *the best trade I ever made*. Tonight Maren sat across from her, pointing at the map of the northern moors, correcting a river's course, and the two of them argued with the easy ferocity of people who had decided to belong to each other. Maren had stayed, after everything. Forgiveness, Beatrix had learned, was less a door than a road — long, unpaved, walked daily.

"Come inside," Sable said. "Bramble made the festival bread. The good kind. With the currants."

"The currants are a lie. There are four currants and nine hundred people want them."

"That's what makes it a festival."

*

Inside, the hall smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary and the particular sweetness of Bramble's magic, which clung to her cooking like memory clings to a house. Beatrix took his seat — his seat, third from the end, the one with the wobbly leg Oren kept promising to fix and never would, because, he suspected now, Oren had forgotten which chair it was and had committed to the promise too proudly to admit it.

They ate. They argued. Odile Vetch's commission was debated at length, with Ilex sketching possible tomorrows from the descriptions of others and declaring most of them "geographically optimistic." Bramble slid the plate with all four currants toward Beatrix when no one was looking, and he split them five ways, and nobody mentioned it, and everybody noticed.

When the plates were cleared, Oren rapped his cane on the table, and the hall went quiet the way it always did — not out of command, but out of love wearing the costume of command.

"A year ago," Oren said, "this guild was a rumor with a roof. Then it was the last lit room in an unwritten country. And now—" he gestured at the ledger, at the waiting list pinned to the board, at the impossible things stacked politely on the shelves like well-behaved guests, "—now we're a business. Gods help us all."

Laughter. Even from Maren, who was learning to laugh here, slowly, like learning a language late in life.

"So," Oren said, and his voice gentled, "the ledger-keeper has an entry to close out. Beatrix. Whenever you're ready."

Beatrix rose. He carried the great ledger to the reading stand the way he carried it every night, both arms, reverent, and he turned to Entry 4,112 and read it aloud, and then he added the closing line, the one he'd been saving:

*"Status: accepted. The key will not be made. Instead, the guild offers the following truth, free of charge, to Odile Vetch and to all future clients: tomorrow opens for everyone who walks toward it together. No key required."*

He signed his name. His own name, in his own hand, learned again from a page shown to him by people who loved him — and beneath it, the signatures of witnesses: Sable, steady and slanted. Ilex, precise as a border. Bramble, surrounded by a small doodle of a loaf. Oren, careful, formal, the letters of a man writing against forgetting. Maren, hesitant, hopeful, *his teacher*, in whatever complicated way that word meant now.

And at the bottom, added shyly, in a child's careful script: *I was here. Odile Vetch.* Because the girl had refused to leave until she'd seen it done, and because the guild had decided long ago that ledgers belonged to everyone who kept them.

The spire, outside, caught the moment. It always did. Its dusk-song shifted — Beatrix heard it even through stone — a new phrase woven in, brief and bright, the sound of a country adding one more small true thing to itself.

Home, he thought, looking around the table. Not found. Made. Made out of arguments and currants and wobbly chairs and names spoken nightly against the dark. Made out of a ledger that anyone could sign.

He dipped his pen one last time, and beneath Odile's signature, wrote the entry that would carry them into whatever came next:

*Entry 4,113. Tomorrow. Status: open.*
