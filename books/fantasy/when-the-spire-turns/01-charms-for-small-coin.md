## 1. Charms for Small Coin

The morning Vellamark decided to ruin Noor Halloran's life began, as most mornings did, with a horse complaining about its feet.

Noor heard it before they were properly awake — a low, grumbling mutter drifting up through the floorboards from the stable yard below their rented room, where the cart-horse Bramble lodged with three others. *Cold,* the horse was saying, in the way horses said everything, which was at length and with grievance. *Cold, and the straw is damp, and the boy who waters us hums off-key, and my near hind hoof aches, and nobody cares.*

*Somebody cares,* Noor muttered into their pillow. *I always care. Go back to sleep, you great grey grouch.*

The grumbling stopped, surprised into silence, and then resumed in a softer register — the equine equivalent of a pleased harrumph. Noor lay there a moment longer, savoring it the way other people savored the last bite of a honey cake. Fifteen years, and the thrill never dulled. A voice answering back. Not an echo. Not imagination. An answer.

Then the bell of Saint Osla's rang the hour, and Noor got up, because charms did not string themselves, and luck-knots did not sell themselves, and rent did not pay itself, more's the pity.

* * *

The stall in Tallowman's Row was Noor's by a lease so old the landlord had forgotten he owned it, which was the best kind of lease. By the time the sun cleared the Spire — white and terrible and lovely, the way a knife is lovely — the little table was laid out in its usual order: luck-knots in red and river-blue twine, braided while Noor hummed the tune their mother had hummed; copper bells with no clapper, sold as "quiet bells for quiet houses" and bought, always, by people who found them funny; bundles of dried lavender that were only lavender, because even a fraud needed honest stock to hide behind.

And beneath the table, in a covered basket, three eggs that had nothing whatsoever to do with charms. Those were for Marla at the pie stall, who traded breakfast for gossip, and gossip was a currency Noor could pay in without risk.

"Morning, Halloran." Marla arrived before the eggs were even properly hidden, which meant she'd been watching for them. She was a broad, flour-dusted woman with the eyes of a customs inspector. "You hear the criers yesterday?"

"No. I was braiding."

"You're always braiding." Marla took her eggs and lowered her voice to a stage whisper that half the row could hear. "They're saying there's to be a proclamation. Something from the crown. New wardens, too — I saw them measuring the gate arches for new gates. Measuring, Halloran. Nobody measures a gate unless they mean to close it."

"Probably tolls," Noor said, and kept their hands steady on the twine, because hands were easy to steady when you'd had practice, and fifteen years was a great deal of practice. "Everything's tolls now."

Marla snorted, unconvinced, and wandered off to poison the row with the news. Noor watched her go and told themselves the small, practiced lie: *nothing is coming. Nothing ever comes. The city has forgotten how to look at people like me.*

The city, obligingly, spent the rest of the morning proving them right.

* * *

Trade was good. Trade was usually good on luck-knots, because Vellamark was a port town and sailors were superstitious in direct proportion to how wet their jobs were. A bosun with a beard like tarred rope bought three blue knots for his crew and haggled the fourth free, which was traditional. A young woman bought a quiet bell for her mother's sickroom and stood holding it a moment too long, turning it over, and Noor recognized the look — the look of someone who wanted to ask a question they couldn't afford to ask.

"Ring it if you need it to," Noor said gently. "It won't. That's rather the point. But holding it helps some people."

The woman's face did something complicated and grateful, and she left, and Noor felt the small warm coal of it settle in their chest. That was the other half of the trade, the half nobody paid for: the half where you noticed people. Fifteen years of hiding taught you to see everyone else who was hiding. It was a whole invisible city, Vellamark, layered under the visible one like writing under writing.

The lame cart-horse came at midmorning.

Noor had heard him coming for a quarter hour — the uneven rhythm of three sound hooves and one that kissed the cobbles and lifted again too quick. He was hauling a cart of river gravel, driven by a carter named Odell with a face like a closed ledger, and the horse stopped level with the stall not because he chose to but because Odell stopped to argue with the fishmonger about the price of eels.

The horse was grey, going white at the muzzle, and he was in pain. Noor heard it the way you'd hear a violin played badly — the wrongness in every step, a hot wire pulled tight from hoof to hip. *Stupid,* the horse was muttering to himself, the way hurt animals did, furious at their own bodies. *Stupid stone. Stupid road. Keep walking. Don't limp where he can see, he sells lame horses to the knacker, don't limp, don't—*

*He's not looking,* Noor said, low, under the fishmonger's shouting. *Look at the eels, everyone looks at the eels. Which hoof?*

The horse's head swung around. For a moment he simply stared, ears forward, the whole gray weight of his attention landing on Noor like weather. Horses always did that, the first time. As if they'd spent their whole lives talking into a well and had finally, impossibly, heard a voice come back up.

*Near hind,* he said at last, wondering. *The stone was months ago. It healed crooked. He says I'm lazy.*

*You're not lazy. You're clever. Clever horses don't limp where it costs them.*

The horse made a sound that was half breath and half laugh, if horses laughed. *You talk strange. Like a bird that learned people-words.*

*I get that a lot,* Noor said, and then Odell turned around, and Noor became, instantly and completely, a person appraising a horse.

"Fine animal," Noor said, which was a lie so large it deserved its own stall. "Bit short in the near hind, though."

Odell's eyes narrowed to ledger-lines. "He's sound."

"Sound enough," Noor agreed pleasantly. "For gravel. I'm not after a carter's horse, mind — I want something to pull my stall cart to market and back, quarter mile each way, flat ground. I'll give you eight marks."

"Twenty."

"Ten, and I don't tell the horse-trader on Cordwainer Street that you've been hauling on a lame near hind since midsummer. He's got a long memory and a short temper."

Odell went the color of a beet and looked at his horse, and the horse — bless him, bless his crooked hoof forever — dropped his head and held the bad leg delicately, theatrically off the ground, the very portrait of equine suffering. Noor kept their face solemn as a gravestone.

"Twelve," Odell said bitterly, "and you take him now."

"Twelve," Noor agreed, "and I'll throw in a luck-knot for the cart."

They shook on it. Odell unhitched and walked away fast, not looking back, and the horse looked back for both of them — at the cart, at the road, at the whole gray geography of his old life — and then at Noor.

*Truly?* he said.

*Truly. You'll pull a light cart of twine and bells and sleep in clean straw. And I know a farrier who owes me, and she owes me enough that she'll be gentle about the hoof. It won't mend crooked things, but it can stop them aching.*

The horse considered this. *What do you want in return?* It was not a suspicious question. It was a horse's question — the honest arithmetic of the world, where nothing came free and pretending otherwise was a people-disease.

*Company,* Noor said, before they could choose a smarter answer. *Somebody to talk to on the road.*

*I'm very opinionated,* the horse warned.

*I'm counting on it.*

His name, it emerged, was Bramble, which he hated, and which Noor promised to use only in mixed company. He walked beside them to the stable yard at the end of the row, favoring the leg less now that he knew someone was watching it kindly, and Noor paid the stable-boy extra to fork fresh straw, and stood in the warm hay-smelling dark a while with a hand on the great grey neck, listening to him catalog the stable's many injustices, and felt — as they felt every time, and as they had learned to feel guilty about every time — *happy.*

That was the trouble. That was the whole rotten trouble of it. The gift didn't feel like a crime. It felt like the one true thing about them, and hiding it was like holding your breath for fifteen years and calling it a life.

* * *

The afternoon brought the wren.

It arrived in a flurry of outrage, shot through the market like a small brown thunderbolt, and landed on the corner of the stall with its feathers puffed and its whole body vibrating. Around it, the market went on. Nobody looked twice at a wren. That was the beauty of small birds; the world found them beneath notice, which was a mistake the world made about a great many useful things.

*They've taken the nest on Cooper's Lane,* the wren announced, without greeting, because wrens did not believe in greetings. *The whole wall came down. Eggs and all. I flew at the mason's head. I want it recorded that I flew at his head.*

*It's recorded,* Noor said solemnly. *The mason will bear the scar of your wrath to his grave. Were the eggs—*

*Gone. Smashed.* The wren's feathers flattened slowly, and the outrage went out of it like air from a bellows, and what was left underneath was small and cold. *Fourteen days I sat on them. Fourteen days, and a man with a cart of stones didn't even see.*

Noor looked at the little bird — the heaving chest, the bright furious eye — and did the thing they always did, the thing that was equal parts kindness and selfishness. They reached into the basket under the table and came up with a strip of suet they'd brought for exactly this, and held it out flat on their palm.

*Here. Eat. Grief is easier with something in your stomach; I speak from extensive experience.*

The wren hopped onto their hand and ate in furious little jerks, glaring at the middle distance, and Noor watched the market flow past over the bird's head — Marla's pies, the tarred bosun, the fishmonger still shouting about eels — and thought, not for the first time, about the shape of a life. A stall. A horse. A wren that visited. Eggs traded for gossip. A gift spent in whispers, coin by coin, in the gaps where nobody important was looking.

It was a good life. It was a small life, and it was built like a luck-knot: pretty on the outside, and underneath, a pattern that only held because no one ever pulled a thread.

*You've gone quiet and sad,* the wren observed, with the tact of its species, which was none. *Your face does a thing. Like the mason's.*

*My face is nothing like the mason's.*

*It's doing the thing right now.*

*Eat your suet.*

The wren ate its suet. The light went long and gold across the row, and the stalls began to fold up around them, canvas and creak and the smell of cooling pies, and Noor counted the day's take — modest, honest, enough — and let themselves believe, one more evening, one more time, that the pattern would hold.

It was Marla who broke it, coming back at dusk with her face wrong.

"Criers," she said. "Just now, at the fountain square. Crown proclamation, posted at every gate and church door by morning." She hesitated, and Noor watched her decide, visibly, whether to say the rest. Marla was a gossip, but she was not a cruel one. "They're offering silver, Halloran. For names. Names of the gifted."

The word landed in Noor's chest like a dropped stone in a well. *The gifted.* Fifteen years of euphemism, and now the crown had found a word for them, and put a price on it, and the price was silver — cheap, insultingly cheap, the price of a lame horse.

"Proclamation of what, though?" Noor heard themself ask. Their voice came out steady. Hands, voice, face — all the practiced surfaces, holding. "What does it actually say?"

"That's the thing." Marla's eyes were doing the customs-inspector thing again, and for one terrible moment Noor thought: *she knows, she's always known, she's doing sums.* But Marla only shook her head. "Nobody knows. It's sealed until the reading tomorrow at dawn. Sealed proclamations, new wardens, gates being measured." She gathered her baskets. "I'd braid extra knots tomorrow, if I were you. Times like this, people want luck."

*Times like this, people want silver,* Noor did not say.

They walked home through streets going blue with evening, past the fountain square where the proclamation board stood dark and blank under its canvas shroud, past the church doors that would wear the news by morning like a new scar. Bramble, stabled and fed, greeted them with a complaint about the water bucket that Noor answered on autopilot, half their mind climbing the stairs, lighting the lamp, looking at the little room that was theirs.

The satchel lived under the floorboard by the hearth. It had lived there for fifteen years — packed, oiled, ready, a coward's luggage. Noor knelt and lifted the board and looked at it and did not take it out. Not yet. There would be a reading at dawn, and a proclamation, and it might be nothing — tolls, taxes, a new tax on twine, anything, *anything* — and a person did not run from a sealed board. A person braided their knots and fed their horse and slept.

In the eaves above the window, the wren had roosted, as it sometimes did, and it murmured small drowsy grievances into the dark. Noor lay listening to them, and to the city settling, and to the far bell of the Spire tolling the hour across the rooftops — white and terrible and lovely, and tomorrow, perhaps, about to turn.

*Sleep,* Noor told the wren, and meant it for themself too. *Whatever comes at dawn, we'll hear it together.*

The wren, being a wren, did not answer kindly. But it tucked its head under its wing, and trusted them enough to sleep, and that — Noor thought, staring at the ceiling while the lamp guttered — that was the thing the crown had never understood, and never would.

Some things could not be bought with silver. Some things you had to be given.

Dawn was four hours away. It felt like the whole world was holding its breath.
