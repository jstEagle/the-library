## 2. The Proclamation

The wren woke them by screaming directly into their left ear.

*Up! Up! Up! There's a man in the square and he's got a BELL—*

Noor came upright with a charm-knife already in hand, which was a habit they were not proud of and had never once needed. The room was gray with early light. The wren sat on the bedpost, feathers puffed to twice its size, vibrating like a struck string.

*A bell,* it repeated, as if this were news of an invasion.

"Bells ring every morning," Noor said, voice rough with sleep. "Bakers' bells. Tanner's bells. The whole city is a conspiracy of bells."

*Not like this.* The wren hopped closer, and its voice dropped into that register Noor thought of privately as *small bird serious*, which was rare enough to make their stomach tighten. *This one walks. It has legs under it.*

No one else in Vellamark could have heard the difference between a bell rung and a bell carried, or would have cared if they had. That was the trouble with being what Noor was — half their life was spent translating things no one wanted translated. They dressed quickly, pulled on the good coat with the deep pockets, and let the wren ride on their shoulder down the stairs of the tenement, past Mrs. Aldery scrubbing her step, out into the cold gold light of Lammas Square.

The crier stood on the fountain steps with a scroll and a voice built for shipyards. He wore the crown's colors — white and iron-gray — and beside him, two wardens in matching livery stood with the particular stillness of men who had been told to be seen and nothing more.

Noor slid into the crowd near the cheese-monger's awning, anonymous among market-day shoulders.

"Hear ye," the crier boomed, and the bell — carried up the steps by a boy — rang once, flat and official. "By order of His Majesty's Regency Council, the hunt is declared for those who practice the forbidden arts. The gifted. The touched. The traffickers in unbound magic."

The word landed in the crowd like a stone in still water. Noor felt the ripples go through the bodies around them — small flinches, a held breath, someone's hand moving to a child's shoulder.

"Silver is offered," the crier continued, "ten crowns for each name given before the Warden's List is full. Names may be given at any garrison house. Informants' names shall be kept confidential." A pause, professionally timed. "The crown thanks the loyal citizens of Vellamark for their vigilance."

Ten crowns. Noor did the arithmetic without wanting to. Ten crowns was four months of rent and then some. Ten crowns was a winter of coal and bread and the good tea instead of the dust at the bottom of the tin. Ten crowns was a fortune to half the people standing in this square, and all it cost was a name.

They watched the crowd do the same arithmetic.

Most faces did nothing at all, which was its own kind of horror — the careful blankness of people deciding, right there between the fish stall and the fountain, whether they knew anyone worth ten crowns. A woman near Noor looked at her own hands. A boy mouthed the word *gifted* to himself, tasting it.

And then there was Tam Broadfoot.

Tam kept the pickle barrel two stalls down from Noor's charm-stall, a wide, easy man who laughed too loud and paid his debts late but always paid them. Three winters ago, when his plow horse Bess had gone off her feed — refusing harness, screaming at shadows — Noor had lingered after closing and spoken to her. Just a little. Just to learn that a badger had gotten under the stable floor and died there, and Bess's nose, which was better than any man's, could smell grief rotting beneath her feet all night long. They'd dug out the bones together, Noor and Tam and Bess, and Tam had never asked why the horse calmed the moment the ground was clean. He'd just bought them a pie and said, "You've a way with animals, Noor Halloran. Wish I had it."

Now Tam Broadfoot turned around in the crowd, found Noor's face across the square, and looked at them.

He didn't look away. That was the thing. People glanced; people's eyes skated past neighbors all day long. But Tam looked, and his brow creased, and behind his eyes something moved — some memory of a calm horse and a bought pie, turning over like a coin being weighed.

Noor smiled. It took everything they had to make it a market-day smile, easy and slightly bored, the smile of a person selling luck-knots, harmless as bread. Tam's face did something complicated. Then he nodded — one short nod, unreadable — and turned back toward the crier.

The wren's claws tightened on Noor's shoulder. *He knows.*

"He doesn't know anything," Noor murmured, lips barely moving. "There's nothing to know."

*Liar,* said the wren, without malice. Birds were terrible liars themselves and had no patience for it in others.

The crier was reading the legal language now, the clauses, the penalties. Noor stopped listening. They were watching the wardens instead, the way they scanned the crowd with flat professional patience, cataloguing. One of them held a slate. Writing names? Watching reactions? It was impossible to tell, and impossibility was worse than certainty.

Somewhere in the crowd, a dog began to bark — high, frantic, at nothing visible. Its owner yanked the lead and hissed at it to shut up. But Noor heard what the owner couldn't: the dog wasn't barking at nothing. It was barking at the wardens. At the smell rolling off them, sharp and chemical and wrong.

*They've been dipped in it,* the wren said quietly. *Hound-scent. That's hunter's preparation. My cousins say the palace kennels have been busy for weeks. They're making dogs that can follow a whisper.*

Noor's skin went cold despite the morning sun. Hounds trained past scent into something deeper — everyone knew the stories, though they'd been only stories, fifteen years old, from the time of the Binding. Dogs that didn't chase rabbits. Dogs that chased *magic*, whatever that smelled like to a nose.

And here Noor stood, in the open, in daylight, with a wren on their shoulder chattering beast-speech in their ear where any dipped nose could catch the shape of it.

"I'm going to buy cheese," Noor said aloud, brightly, to no one, and moved through the crowd at exactly the pace of a person buying cheese.

* * *

The rest of the day passed in the longest morning of Noor's life.

They opened the stall because not opening the stall would be strange, and strangeness was death now. They sold three luck-knots — red cord, three loops, no magic in them whatsoever except the ordinary kind — to customers who haggled harder than usual, as though silver might soon be worth less than caution. They sold a birth-charm to a young mother who chatted about the weather. They smiled until their face ached.

And underneath the smiling, they listened. The market talked, markets always talked, and today the talk had a new grain in it.

"—heard they took the tinker's daughter in Harrowgate, just dragged her out mid-performance, she was only doing puppet-voices—"

"—my cousin says it's not really about magic, it's about the taxes, they want us watching each other so we can't watch them—"

"—well I say good. My gran was hexed by a hedge-witch in '41 and no one ever paid for it—"

That last one said loudly, pointedly, by a man who caught Noor's eye as he said it, to see what Noor's face did.

Noor's face did nothing. Fifteen years of practice. Inside, though, something was curdling — fear going thick and sour, turning slowly into something heavier. Not panic. Panic was fast and clean. This was dread with weight to it, settling into the bones like wet sand.

Because here was the truth Noor kept circling and refusing to land on: the hunt would work. Not because the wardens were clever, but because of the arithmetic. Ten crowns. Everyone in Vellamark knew someone. And you didn't need proof, you only needed a name, and names could be guesses, and guessed-at gifted folk would be taken and questioned, and questioning had ways of finding things out. Sooner or later — probably sooner — someone would guess at the charm-seller with the odd way with animals.

Or someone would remember.

The cart-horse came by at midday, hauling stone for the cathedral repairs, and stopped — as she always did — at the corner by Noor's stall. She was gray-muzzled now, the mare Noor had bargained with a hundred times over the years, lame in the near hind but proud about it.

*Charm-seller,* she said, in her slow gravel way. *Your face is wrong today.*

"My face is fine."

*Your face is a door closed hard.* She shifted her weight off the bad leg. *The dogs in the yard are different. New dogs. They don't sleep right. They dream loud, and the dreams are all one dream, and the dream smells like you.*

Noor's hands went still over a half-finished knot.

"What kind of dream?"

*Running,* the mare said simply. *Everyone in the dream is running, and in the dream the runners are guilty. That's how they're teaching them. Guilt has a taste, apparently. Yours will taste like fear by the time they meet you.* She looked at Noor with one dark, patient eye. *I am telling you this because you once told me where my pain lived, when no one else would listen. I pay my debts.*

She moved off before Noor could answer, hooves dragging gently on the cobbles, and Noor stood in the noon light holding a length of red cord and understood, with total clarity, that the day of whispers was over.

* * *

They closed the stall early, which was also strange, and also unavoidable. Their fingers wouldn't tie knots anymore; they'd tried, and produced something lopsided that would probably curse whoever bought it.

Back at the tenement, the light was going amber through the window. Noor stood in the middle of the small room and looked at their life laid out around them: the lamp, the kettle, the drawer of finished charms, the blanket the wren slept under in winter. Fifteen years in this room. They had been nine years old when the Binding came down and the Spire went quiet, and they'd built this small safe life the way birds build nests — one stolen twig at a time.

And now the arithmetic again. Ten crowns. Tam's long look. New dogs dreaming of runners.

"All right," Noor said aloud. "All right."

The wren flew in through the window gap and landed on the table. *We're running, then.*

"I didn't say that."

*You're packing your satchel while saying that. Your hands are braver than your mouth. This is why I like your hands.*

Noor looked down. They were indeed packing the satchel. Some part of them had started the moment they walked through the door — the oilcloth wrap for the charms, the flint, the knife, the wool socks, the little tin of tea because a person had to draw a line somewhere and Noor drew theirs at bad tea. They added the cord, the scissors, the letters from their mother, dead eleven years now, tied in a bundle they had not opened in five.

It fit in one satchel. Fifteen years fit in one satchel. There was a lesson in that, and Noor declined to learn it just yet.

Then they stood at the window, and the last hard thing arrived, the one they'd been walking around all day.

Below, on the sill outside — because Noor had never quite been able to keep a wild bird caged, even a tame-winged one, and the wren came and went freely — no. Not the wren. Below, on the street, the rag-and-bone man's cart rattled past, and on top of it, in a wicker cage, a songbird sang its heart out to be sold.

Noor's gaze snagged on it. The bird was singing the way caged birds sing, endlessly, beautifully, madly — and Noor could hear the words in it, because they always heard the words. *Let me out. Let me out. Let me out. Sun. Sun. Let me out.*

"Oh," they said softly. "Oh, that's not fair."

*What?* said the wren.

"Nothing. Get in the satchel."

*I refuse the satchel. I will fly alongside like a dignified creature.* But the wren hopped onto the windowsill and went quiet, watching Noor the way birds watch weather.

Noor shouldered the satchel. Took one last look at the room — the lamp, the kettle, the drawer — and blew out the lamp even though they were leaving, because wasting oil was a sin their mother had died believing in.

Then they went down the stairs and out into the street, and the rag-and-bone man's cart was stopped at the corner, the man arguing cheerfully with the cheesemonger about prices, and the cage sat unattended on the tailboard, singing *let me out, let me out.*

Anyone could walk by. Anyone might notice a stranger opening a cage. In a city offering ten crowns a name, opening cages was practically a confession.

Noor did it anyway. Two seconds, latch lifted, and the songbird burst upward in a spiral of brown feathers and pure profanity-laced joy, and Noor felt something in their chest unlock along with the latch. If the gift was going to get them killed, then let it spend itself on things like this. Let the last use of it, above ground, in daylight, be a small stupid mercy no one would ever know about.

*Sentimental,* the wren observed from the gutter, taking wing.

"Yes," Noor said. "Come on."

They meant to go to the river gate. They meant to walk out of Vellamark calmly, like a person visiting an aunt, and take the north road, and figure the rest out later. That was the plan, such as it was.

They got as far as Tanner's Lane.

The lane was empty in the dusk — and then it wasn't. Two shapes came around the corner ahead: wardens, and with them a dog. Not a patrol dog. One of the *new* ones. Noor saw it in the lamplight, saw the way it moved, low and intent, nose working — and felt the animal's attention sweep the street like a lantern beam.

And stop on them.

The dog's head came up. Its nostrils flared. Noor felt the connection open unbidden, the way it always did, and heard what was inside the dog: not cruelty, nothing so simple. Just the dream, the taught dream, running and guilt and the taste of fear, layered over a good honest animal's confusion. *This one smells wrong,* the dog was thinking, in its blunt way. *Wrong-smelling things must be told to the tall ones.*

"Noor?" one of the wardens said, squinting. "Noor Halloran? Charm-seller? What are you doing out this end?"

Every answer Noor owned evaporated. The wren screamed overhead — *RUN, you enormous idiot, RUN—* — and the dog's muscles bunched, and the warden's hand drifted toward the whistle at his collar—

And Noor ran.

Not toward the river gate. Not toward anywhere. Away, boots hammering cobbles, satchel slamming their spine, up the first alley their legs chose for them while their mind stood empty as a swept hearth. Behind them the whistle shrieked — two notes, three, the signal spreading down the lanes like fire through dry grass — and other whistles answered it, farther off, and then farther than that, a whole ringing city of whistles.

*Left!* the wren cried, banking low. *LEFT, the wall's got a drain, the rats know me, the RATS—*

"The *what*—"

But there was no breath left for questions. Noor ran, and the wren flew, and below and behind the whistles multiplied, and ahead the alley ended in darkness and the smell of deep water and old stone, where something waited that Noor could not yet see, and could no longer avoid.

Down, then. Whatever was down there — it was down, or it was the whistle.

Noor chose down.
