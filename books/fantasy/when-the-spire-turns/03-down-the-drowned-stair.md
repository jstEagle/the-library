## 3. Down the Drowned Stair

The wren had gone up through the smoke-hole like a thrown stone, and Noor had watched the empty cage for a long moment afterward, feeling the particular foolishness of a person who has just done something kind and irreversible.

Then they packed.

It was not a graceful packing. It was the packing of someone who has never practiced running away because they always assumed they'd be better at staying. A spare shirt. The good luck-knots — the ones braided with river-reed that actually seemed to work, though Noor would have denied under torture that they worked at all. Their mother's bone needle, which had never sewn anything but had outlived everyone who might have explained it. Half a loaf of barley bread gone stiff as roof-slate.

And then the knock came, and there was no more time for packing at all.

* * *

Noor went out the back window of their own stall the way water goes out of a cracked cup: all at once, and in every direction at once, and none of it dignified.

The rooftops of Vellamark's Tanner's Row were built for storage and pigeons, not for people, and certainly not for people carrying satchels full of charms that jingled with every desperate stride. Noor learned this immediately and viscerally, sliding on wet slate, catching a gutter with one hand, and discovering that the charm-cord around their wrist — the little woven horse, the one they'd made on a slow afternoon because it amused them — had snagged and was now attempting to strangle them.

"Not now," Noor hissed at it, wrestling it loose. "I *made* you."

Behind them, below them, the sound that had been chasing them since the criers' bells stopped was getting closer. It was not the sound of boots. Boots they could have outrun, or hidden from, or bribed with a luck-knot and a lie. This was a different sound entirely. This was the sound of dogs.

Four of them, maybe five, voices braided together into one long baying note that rolled up the walls of the row like weather. Noor knew that note. Every child in Vellamark knew that note; it meant a runaway apprentice or a thief in the granaries, and you stayed indoors until it passed. But Noor also knew something else about that note, something the criers hadn't said and the proclamation hadn't mentioned, something that sat in Noor's chest like a swallowed stone:

They were hounds. And hounds were kin.

That was the whole rotten joke of Noor's gift, the thing they'd spent fifteen years pretending wasn't true. Not that they could *command* beasts — anyone who claimed command over another creature was selling something, and Noor had seen what happened to those sellers. No. What Noor had was worse and better: they could *hear* them. And being heard by a beast was a door that swung both ways, because beasts could hear you too, and they could tell when you were lying, and they could tell — this was the part that made the gift unbearable on nights like tonight — they could tell when you were afraid.

So the hounds behind them knew exactly how afraid Noor was. They could taste it on the wind like blood.

And Noor, crouched on a rooftop ridge with slate dust on their palms and their heart going like a drum in a parade, could hear *them*. Could hear the confusion braided through the bay. Because these weren't granary-hounds on the scent of a thief. These were hunters' hounds, big-shouldered and deep-chested, bred for stag and boar, and what they'd been given to hunt smelled wrong to them. Smelled like the kennel-master's daughter who used to sneak them bacon rinds. Smelled like the boy who'd pulled a burr out of the young one's ear last spring and gotten licked across the face for his trouble.

Smelled like *kin*, and they had been ordered to run it down anyway.

"*Up*," said one voice in the chorus, low and urgent, an older hound trying to keep the pack moving. "*Scent says up. Slate, slate, go up.*"

"*Wrong*," said another, younger, cracking with doubt. "This is Hetta's-smell. This is bacon-rind smell. We don't chase bacon-rind smell."

"We do now," said the old one, grimly. "We do now, pup. Say your sorry later."

Noor heard all of it, every word, and felt something crack open in their chest that was not entirely fear. There was grief in it, yes — real grief, the specific grief of hearing creatures you loved the way you loved rain love you back while being dragged toward you on a rope of other men's orders. But underneath the grief, horribly, inconveniently, there was *recognition*. The old hound didn't want this either. The old hound was doing what Noor had done for fifteen years: obeying the law of the leash because the alternative was teeth at your throat.

We are the same, thought Noor, sitting on a rooftop at dusk with the sky going bruise-colored above Vellamark. You and I. Both of us told to hunt what we love.

Then the younger hound's voice came again, and this time it wasn't doubt. It was something braver and more dangerous than doubt.

"Hetta's-smell," the young hound said — and Noor realized with a lurch that the dog had named *them*, had taken the scent of the bacon-rind girl and hung a name on it, the way beasts did, the way Noor themselves named things. "Hetta's-smell is *up*. Hetta's-smell is going to the water. I can hear her heart. It's going too fast. It'll stop if we make it go faster."

And then, in a voice so quiet it barely crossed the gap between rooftops, the young hound broke rank in the only way a leashed thing can break rank:

"I'm going to be slow," he said. "Somebody tell the man I'm going to be very, very slow. My paw hurts. Look at my paw. Somebody look at my paw."

Noor laughed. It was a horrible time to laugh, alone on a rooftop with hunters' hounds closing in, but laughter came anyway, hot and startled, because the young hound had just invented lameness on purpose — had just committed, in his own doggish way, the exact crime Noor had been committing for fifteen years. Feigning harmlessness. Performing injury. *Slow down, masters, nothing here but a lame old thing, nothing worth your silver.*

Kin, thought Noor. Truly kin. Running the same trick on opposite ends of the same leash.

They ran.

* * *

What followed was forty minutes of the least heroic flight in the history of Vellamark, and Noor would remember every humiliating second of it for the rest of their life.

They went north along the ridge of the dyers' sheds, where the air was so thick with madder-stink that it scrambled the hounds' noses for a blessed hundred yards. They came down a drainpipe that groaned like a ship's mast. They crossed a courtyard where a goose — a large white gander with opinions — took one look at Noor's panicked face and began to scream like a murdered soul, because geese could smell fear too, and geese, unlike hounds, had never once in recorded history kept their findings to themselves.

"NOT NOW," Noor told him, and the gander, hearing actual words aimed at him from a human in flight, paused in his screaming, tilted his great stupid head, and honked once — softly, almost conversationally — before resuming at double volume.

"You're a *traitor*," Noor panted, and meant it with their whole heart.

The body was the worst traitor of all. That was the lesson of the rooftops, the one Noor carried away bleeding and bruised: you could keep your gift secret for fifteen years, could keep your fear secret, could keep everything buttoned and folded and hidden behind a stall of harmless charms — but the body told the truth at a dead run. The body sweated it. The body panted it into the night air where anything with a nose could read it like a broadsheet. Fifteen years of whispering, and it turned out Noor had been shouting the whole time, shouting *here, here, gifted, frightened, here* — and the city had simply never had ears pointed their way until tonight.

By the time Noor dropped off the last roof into the tannery yard by the canal, their legs were shaking, their lungs were full of dye-smoke and regret, and the hounds' voices had found them again through the maze of sheds — except now the voices sounded different. Slower. Almost apologetic.

"*She's tired,*" the young one was saying. "*She's very tired. If we just... if we sort of... circle...*"

"*Good boy,*" said the old hound, and even that sounded like a prayer.

And then, from the dark underneath the tannery's plank steps, from a shadow no bigger than a loaf of bread, a voice spoke to Noor that was not a hound's voice, and not a goose's voice, and not any voice Noor had ever been brave enough to answer in daylight.

"*Down,*" said the rat.

* * *

He was a big sewer-rat, brown as wet oak, missing half an ear to some ancient argument, and he sat on the bottom step with his tail curled around his feet like a gentleman at a fire, and he looked at Noor the way no rat had ever looked at Noor before: frankly, appraisingly, as an equal.

"*You're the market one,*" he said. "*The charm-seller. The whisperer.*"

"How do you—" Noor started, and then stopped, because the answer was obvious and embarrassing. Of course he knew. The rats knew *everything* about the market. The rats had known about Noor for years. Every crumb Noor had ever swept from under their stall, every quiet word they'd ever murmured to the cart-horse at the end of the lane — the rats had heard it all, filed it all, passed it along in the long brown gossip-chain of the undercity. Noor had spent fifteen years terrified of being overheard by humans, and the entire time, a civilization of witnesses had been taking notes two feet below the cobblestones.

"*We voted,*" the rat said, and Noor's skin prickled at the word *voted*, because it implied process, deliberation, a quorum. "*About you. Years ago. Whether you were safe to know.*"

"And?" Noor managed. "What did you decide?"

"*That you fed the lame horse apples and told nobody.*" He said it plainly, without warmth, the way rats said true things. "*A human who is kind when there is nothing to gain is rare. A human who is kind in secret is rarer. We decided you were one of those. We've been waiting for you to need us.*"

Noor stared at him. Somewhere behind them, faint and far now, the hounds were still circling, still being magnificently, heroically slow. The canal lay black and flat to the east. The proclamation's silver price hung over the whole city like a second moon. And here, on a tannery step, a rat with half an ear was telling Noor Halloran that they had been *voted on*, and *approved*, and *waited for*, by a committee of sewer-dwellers, and that the wait was over.

Nobody had ever waited for Noor in their life. That was the orphan's particular wound — not the losing, but the never-being-awaited. And here it was, offered by a creature the size of a shoe, in a voice like dry leaves.

"*Down,*" the rat said again. "*There's a stair. Old. Older than the city's memory. The humans sealed it and forgot it and built a privy on top of it, which is very funny if you're a rat and merely rude if you're anyone else. It goes under the canal. It goes to the Tallow Market. You want the Tallow Market. Everyone who's anyone who isn't allowed to be anyone goes to the Tallow Market.*"

"I don't—"

"*You're running from the crown with a satchel of luck-charms and a gift you can't hide anymore. Yes you do.*"

The terrible thing, Noor thought later, was how easy it was. Not the descent itself — the descent was pitch-dark, knee-deep in places, and smelled like the inside of the city's oldest secrets. But the *decision* was easy. Easier than staying had ever been. Because the rat hadn't offered safety, exactly. He'd offered something Noor hadn't been offered since childhood: a place where the gift wasn't a crime or a secret or a burden, but simply a fact, like height, like hunger, like a name.

"*Follow my tail,*" the rat said. "*Don't touch the third wall. The third wall bites.*"

"The wall *bites*?"

"*Everything down here bites. It's how we say hello.*"

* * *

The drowned stair went down farther than any stair in Vellamark had a right to go, and it went down *wet*, each step slick with a century of seep, the walls sweating brick-blood rust that stained Noor's palms like guilt. The rat moved ahead with his tail raised like a lantern-pole, chattering the whole way — about the flood year, about the warden who'd fallen down these stairs drunk forty years ago and been robbed of his boots by three separate families of rats in a single night ("*we split it fairly; there was a whole arbitration*"), about the moths.

"*You'll like the moth-woman,*" he said. "*Or you won't. She reads futures in the patterns moths make on a lamp-glass. She's usually wrong, but she's wrong in such interesting ways that it hardly matters.*"

"What's down there? At the bottom?"

"*People.*" The rat said it the way other folk said *treasure*. "*People the city threw away. A falconer with one arm who talks to birds that aren't there anymore. A boy who hears rust eating iron and tells it to eat slower. An old woman who sells bottled thunder. And rats, obviously. Lots of rats. We're the government, more or less. Don't tell anyone. It's a soft coup.*"

Noor laughed again — the second laugh of the night, and this one less horrified, more astonished, the laugh of a person stepping out of a burning house into a garden they didn't know existed. The dark pressed close. Water dripped somewhere with the patience of centuries. And Noor Halloran, fugitive, criminal, hunted by hounds who loved them, followed a rat down a drowned stair beneath the city that had outlawed them, and found — this was the strange mercy of it, the first taste, the taste that would change everything —

they found they weren't whispering anymore.

Every word between them rang out plain in the dark, unhidden, at full voice. The gift and the listener, walking together, making all the noise they liked. It should have been terrifying. Instead it felt like taking off boots after a long march. Like the first breath after surfacing.

At the bottom of the stair, where the tunnel opened and warm air rose smelling of tallow-smoke and frying onions and animal warmth, the rat stopped and turned, and in the distant glow Noor saw his whiskers spread in what was unmistakably, absurdly, a smile.

"*Welcome to downstairs,*" he said. "*Mind the goose.*"

"The—"

But the rat had already scurried ahead into the light, tail high, and Noor, heart hammering, satchel jingling, followed him down into the Tallow Market — and the first thing they heard, echoing up out of the warm dark, was a woman's voice reading a moth's wings aloud, and beneath it, unmistakable, the low conversational honk of a large white gander who had, apparently, beaten them both down the stairs.

Traitor, thought Noor, and grinned, and went down to meet their future.
