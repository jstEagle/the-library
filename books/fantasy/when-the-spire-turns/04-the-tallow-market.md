## 4. The Tallow Market

The stair opened into a cavern that had once, Noor guessed, been a cistern — a great brick belly of a place, ribbed with arches, its floor half-covered in shallow black water that reflected the light of a hundred lamps as if the market floated among stars. The air smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone and, underneath it all, faintly and impossibly, of oranges.

And it was full of people.

Not the desperate huddle Noor had imagined when the word *undercity* lived only in their nightmares. People were *trading*. A woman with river-grey braids weighed out spoonfuls of something luminous into twist of paper. Two old men argued cheerfully over a crate of singing stones, each holding one to his ear like a shell. Children — actual children — chased each other along the walkways with paper lanterns, and nobody told them to be quiet, and nobody looked over their shoulders, and no one at all was pretending to be harmless.

Noor stood at the foot of the stair with their satchel clutched to their chest and felt something in them crack quietly open.

"*Told you,*" said the rat, who was sitting on a bollard washing his face with an air of immense self-satisfaction. "*Best market downstairs or up. Mind the goose.*"

"I heard you the first time," Noor murmured. "What's he going to do?"

"*Nothing, probably. He's a menace but a coward.*" The rat paused mid-wash. "*Mostly.*"

The moth-reader's voice carried across the water, and Noor found themself drifting toward it the way a cold hand drifts toward a fire. She'd set up her stall on a barge moored where the walkway widened — a low boat hung with nets of dried lavender, and she sat cross-legged in its center with a lamp burning blue and a fat brown moth walking slowly across her open palm.

"*This one crossed the river three nights running,*" she was telling a young man who leaned forward hungrily. "*It keeps going back to the same window on Chandler's Row. Whatever you left behind there, love, it hasn't forgotten you either. Go home. Knock. Don't bring flowers; bring soup. He's a soup sort of grief.*"

The young man laughed — a wet, surprised laugh, the kind that comes out sideways from somewhere broken — and pressed coins into her hand and went up the stair two at a time.

Noor had stopped walking. They didn't remember deciding to stop.

The moth-reader looked up. Her eyes were pale as skimmed milk, ringed all around with fine wrinkles, and they went through Noor like a fish-gutter through a herring: one clean stroke, everything laid out.

"Well," she said. "You're damp, you're starving, you've got a wren-shaped hole in your heart and a horse's worth of guilt on your back, and you're standing in my light."

"I'm sorry—" Noor stepped aside.

"No, stay in it." She waved them closer with the moth-hand, carefully, so as not to jostle her passenger. "I'm Sefa. I read wings, not minds, before you flinch like that. But wings are honest, and yours—" she nodded at the brown moth now lifting off her palm and spiraling lazily down to settle, of all places, on the clasp of Noor's satchel, "—yours have been circling this spot since sundown. You're late. Sit down before you fall down."

"I don't have coin."

"Did I ask for coin?" Sefa reached under her seat and produced, with the timing of long practice, a wrapped parcel and a clay flask. "Bread and honey wine. The bread is two days old and the wine is mostly vinegar, which is why I keep them under the seat instead of selling them. Sit."

Noor sat, at the edge of the barge with their boots nearly in the water, and ate bread that tasted like the best thing they had ever eaten, which is what two days of running does to bread.

Around them the market breathed. A boy of perhaps twelve threaded between the stalls with his sleeves rolled up, touching things — a rusted lock here, a corroded lamp-chain there — and wherever his fingers passed, metal sighed and brightened. He paused by a stall of old tools and Noor heard him murmur, tenderly, "*There, now, none of that,*" to a saw that had been seizing up, and the saw's teeth loosened and gleamed, and the tool-seller paid him in dried figs without a word passing between them, because apparently down here even commerce was conducted in the language of kindness.

"What's his gift?" Noor asked quietly.

"Pip? Rust. Speaks to corrosion." Sefa snorted. "Useless gift above. Down here it makes him rich in figs. That's the whole of the Tallow Market in one boy, if you want the lesson free: what the city threw away is exactly what we needed."

The gander chose that moment to arrive.

He came paddling grandly along the flooded channel that ran beside the walkway, white as a spilled jug of milk, and hauled himself out directly in front of Noor with the air of someone arriving at an appointment he had scheduled personally. He fixed them with one flat orange eye.

"He followed me," Noor said.

"*He follows everyone,*" said the rat, appearing on Sefa's barge rail. "*Don't feed him. It encourages opinions.*"

"His name's Aldous," said a voice behind them, "and he's already decided you belong to him, so you may as well surrender now."

* * *

The voice belonged to a mountain.

That was Noor's first impression and, in fairness, their lasting one: a man built like something quarried rather than born, grey-bearded, weathered to the color of old rope, with his left sleeve pinned and folded neatly at the elbow and a falcon's jess still knotted — Noor noticed, and caught their breath — around his right wrist. Worn soft with years. Kept, not used.

He stood at the edge of the walkway holding a crate of candles against his hip as though it weighed nothing, which it probably did, to him.

"Brannic," said Sefa, by way of introduction, "this is the one the rats brought in. Noor." She said the name deliberately, watching them both. "Fresh off the rooftops tonight. Hounds nearly had them."

"Hounds." Something moved behind Brannic's eyes — not softening; hardening, like frost forming inward. "Whose?"

"The crown's. Whose else hunts gifted folk with dogs these days?" Sefa's mouth twisted. "They ran them across half the upper city. The wardens, I mean. And their beasts with them."

Brannic set the candle-crate down very slowly, as if putting the anger somewhere safe until he needed it. Then he looked at Noor properly, and Noor braced for the look they knew — the assessing look, the *what are you worth, what are you hiding, how fast can I sell you* look that fifteen years in a market stall teaches you to see coming.

Instead Brannic looked at their hands.

"You're bleeding," he said. "Palms. Rope burns?"

"The drainpipe grating. On the way down." Noor flexed their fingers sheepishly. "It's nothing."

"It's filthy, is what it is. Every cut in this city goes bad in this water, and you've been wading through it in street boots." He picked the crate back up. "Come on, then."

"Where?"

"Shelter."

Noor did not move. Fifteen years of instinct sat on their chest like a cat: *don't follow strangers, don't show where you sleep, don't let anyone learn your name twice.*

Sefa watched them weigh it. "He's not going to hurt you," she said gently. "If he wanted to hurt people, love, he'd have to put down the candles first, and I've never seen him manage it."

"I heard that," said Brannic, already walking.

Aldous honked once, imperiously, and fell in beside Noor like a bodyguard who had not been hired but had simply shown up and would be billing them later.

They followed him along the walkway, past stalls Noor's eyes kept snagging on: a woman mending luck the way a cobbler mends shoes, stitching silver thread through worn-out charms and handing them back *better*; a tank of eels that glowed faintly when a girl hummed to them; a corner where somebody had chalked a list on the wall under the heading **TONIGHT'S TRADE**, and every item on it was small and human — *boots, size 8, barely worn. Lullabies, any tune. One (1) honest opinion, gently delivered.*

"This way," Brannic said, ducking under a low arch. "Mind your head. And the goose."

"Aldous seems determined to supervise."

"He supervises everyone. Thinks he's the warden of this whole level. We let him believe it because he's usually right about who's trouble." Brannic glanced back, just briefly. "He hasn't bitten you yet."

"Is that the test?"

"That's the test."

The passage climbed slightly and turned, and opened into what had clearly once been a guardroom — barrel-vaulted, dry, lit by a brazier and a hanging lamp, furnished with a table, mismatched stools, shelves of jars and rope and oilcloth, and a smell of beeswax and leather that reminded Noor, absurdly and painfully, of the tack-room behind Halloran's old livery, before. There was a cot in the corner. There was a second pallet being aired by the fire. There was, on the table, a chessboard mid-game with pieces carved from cork and bone.

And there was a hooded falcon perch, empty, polished, and draped with a cloth kept clean.

Noor looked at it too long. Brannic saw them looking, and his face closed like a door.

"Sit," he said. "Hands out."

"Sorry?"

"Over the brazier. Warm them. Then give me your hands and I'll bind those cuts properly, because if they fester down here you'll lose the use of them, and speakers need their hands less than most but I'll not have it on my conscience regardless." He knelt at the hearth, unhurried, pulling a roll of clean linen from a shelf without looking. "Boots off after. Wet socks. Don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue," said Noor, who had absolutely been assembling an argument.

"Everyone argues. You can do it while I work."

He cleaned the rope burns with water warmed at the brazier and salt from a jar, and his one hand was astonishingly gentle, and he wrapped the linen with the neat economy of a man who had bandaged a thousand animal injuries and never once cooed sympathy at the patient. Noor found their eyes stinging and blamed the smoke.

"Why?" they asked, when they could trust their voice. "Why help me? You don't know me."

"No."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer. I don't know you." Brannic tied off the last knot, then sat back on his heels and looked at Noor with eyes the color of a winter sky deciding whether to snow. "Down here we help first and know people later. It's the reverse of how it works up there. Up there they know you for years and help never." He rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and poured something amber into two tin cups. "Drink. It's nettle wine. It tastes like punishment. It works."

Noor drank. It tasted like punishment. It worked.

"Rats say you talk to beasts," Brannic said, settling onto the stool opposite, and there it was at last — not suspicion exactly, but weight. Attention. The specific gravity of a man deciding something. "The rat vouches. The goose has adopted you, which means little, he adopted a smuggler once who turned out to be a warden's informer, but the *rat* vouching is rarer than you'd think. Old Marrow's line doesn't spend trust cheap."

"Old Marrow?"

"The rat matriarch. You'll meet her when she decides you're ready, not before, and arguing wastes breath." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Here's what you need from me tonight, and then you should sleep, because you've been awake thirty hours by the shadows under your eyes and I won't have useful information wasted on someone who's hallucinating.

"One: you're safe in this room tonight. That's mine to give and I'm giving it.

"Two: tomorrow the market will want to know you. They'll ask what your gift is and what it costs you and whether the wardens saw your face. Answer honestly. Lies travel faster than truth below ground; there's nothing to slow them down.

"Three—" and here he paused, and his thumb found the old jess at his wrist and rubbed it, once, an unconscious motion worn smooth as river stone, "—three, don't ask me about the Spire, or the palace, or the king. Not yet."

The room went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"I wasn't going to ask," Noor said carefully.

"Everyone asks eventually. It's natural. Newcomers always want to know what's *above* them — who took what from whom, who's to blame, whether there's a plan." Brannic looked at the empty perch, and his jaw worked. "Some questions I'll answer when I'm ready. Some answers cost more than shelter, and I don't know you well enough yet to pay them."

Noor thought of the hawk — the queen's dying hawk, fifteen years ago, and the words they had swallowed — and understood, in a single cold flash, exactly what a man carries when he says *not yet.*

"All right," they said softly. "Not yet."

Something shifted in Brannic's shoulders — a fraction of tension released, like a knot eased one loop. He studied Noor for a long moment.

"You didn't push," he said, sounding faintly surprised.

"I know what 'not yet' weighs. I carry one myself."

For the first time, the mountain smiled. It changed his whole face, made him suddenly, unexpectedly handsome in the way of weathered doors and old ships.

"Then you'll do," he said. "Pallet's by the fire. Blanket smells of goose; that's Aldous's contribution and non-negotiable. If you hear scratching in the walls at night, it's friends. If you hear honking, it's Aldous with an opinion about the dark. Ignore both, sleep anyway."

Noor lay down on the pallet, under the blanket that did indeed smell of goose, and listened to the market humming distantly through stone — voices, laughter, the eel-girl's song, life going on defiantly beneath the feet of a city that had outlawed it. For fifteen years they had hidden their gift in whispers and charm-knots and the ache of being almost entirely alone.

Tonight a rat had vouched for them, a fortune-teller had fed them, a goose had adopted them, and a stranger with a falconer's knots and an unpayable grief had bound their wounds and called it shelter.

*Family*, whispered something hopeful and reckless in Noor's chest, and Noor told it firmly to be quiet, because they knew exactly what hoping cost.

They were nearly asleep when the scratching came — not in the walls, but at the door, deliberate, patient. Brannic was already up, opening it a hand's width, listening.

Then he turned back, and his face in the lamplight had gone strange.

"Marrow's sent for you," he said. "She wants to talk. Tonight. About the Spire."

Noor sat up, heart beginning, already, to pound. "You said—"

"I know what I said." Brannic pulled his coat from its peg, and his voice was grim as iron. "But Marrow doesn't send at night for questions. She sends at night for warnings. Whatever the rats have remembered, Noor — it couldn't wait until morning."
