## 5. What the Rats Remember

Noor had always assumed that if they ever met a rat worth talking to, it would be an emergency — a sewer crisis, a plague warning, something with teeth and urgency. They had not imagined tea.

But the Tallow Market kept its own hours, and one of those hours, apparently, was tea with rats.

"Sit properly," said Old Marrow, "you're slouching like a gull over a fish cart."

She was enormous — not fat, exactly, but *substantial*, in the way of things that had decided long ago to stop apologizing for the space they took up. Her fur was the grey of old pewter, her whiskers shot through with white, and she sat on a crate of tallow candles as though it were a throne that had been built specifically to her measurements. Around her, arranged in a loose crescent on upturned barrels, sat perhaps two dozen of her kin, all of them watching Noor with the patient, unblinking attention of creatures who had heard every lie a human could tell.

Noor sat properly.

"You'll have to forgive them," Marrow continued, gesturing with one small paw at her assembly. "They're young. The last human who came down here speaking beast-tongue was eleven years ago, and he was lying about having bread."

"I don't have bread either," Noor admitted.

"At least you're honest about your poverty. That's more than most kings manage." Marrow's nose twitched — Noor was learning to read those twitches, the way one learned weather. This one meant amusement. "Brannic says you want to know about the Spire."

"I want to know what's happening above us. The hunt. The wardens. Why now, after fifteen years." Noor hesitated. "The rats see everything down here. The walls, the pipes, the foundations. I thought—"

"You thought we might have seen something worth telling." A rustle went through the crescent of younger rats, whiskers twitching, and Marrow silenced them with a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces. "We might have. The question is whether you can bear hearing it. Humans ask questions all the time. Fewer of them enjoy the answers."

"I've been enjoying very few answers lately," Noor said. "I'd still rather have them than not."

Marrow considered this for a long moment. Then she said, in a different tone — quieter, the way people spoke in churches and sickrooms — "Sedge. Come forward. You saw it. Tell the charm-seller what you told me."

From the back of the crescent, a rat stepped out. He was young — young for the story he carried, Noor would think later, though rats measured age differently than people did, in seasons and shadows rather than years. His fur was brown going grey at the muzzle, and he moved with a limp that favored his left hind leg.

He climbed onto the crate beside Marrow and sat, and for a moment said nothing at all, and the whole Tallow Market seemed to lean in — the moth-reader Sefa pausing with her lantern dimmed, the rust-boy Pip frozen halfway through whatever mischief he'd been conducting with a padlock, even Brannic standing at the edge of the candlelight with his single arm folded and his face carefully blank.

"It was winter," Sedge began. His voice was high and thin, the voice of a creature built for whispers. "Seven winters past. Maybe eight. Winters blur when you live under the river pipes; the cold comes through everything the same."

* * *

"I was young then," Sedge said. "New to the deep tunnels. My mother had sent me down to the old cistern below the Spire's foundation to check the grain stores — there was a baker's cellar that leaked flour through a cracked wall, before the baker went away. We were rich that season. Fat on stolen flour and spilled ale. You forget, charm-seller, that the undercity has its own seasons, its own wealth and famine. We were rich.

"And then the pale procession came."

"Pale," Noor repeated.

"Pale as tallow," Sedge said. "Pale as the fat we render for candles. That's why we call this place the Tallow Market, though most folk think it's just because of the candles. It's because of *them*. Everything down here got named after that night.

"There were twenty of them. Perhaps thirty. Men and women both, walking in step, and every one of them dressed in white — not cloth-white, not linen-white. White like things are white when all the color's been drunk out of them. Their faces were white. Their hands were white. And they walked without sound. Charm-seller, I have lived my whole life listening to tunnels. I know the sound a boot makes on wet stone, the sound a rat makes, the sound a drip makes falling from a height. These people made no sound at all. Not their footsteps. Not their breathing. Not the rustle of their clothes. The only sound in the whole procession was a kind of humming, low, like a hive that's gone quiet for the night, and it came from above them. From the thing they carried."

"What were they carrying?" Noor asked, though some part of them already knew, the way you know the shape of a fall before it ends.

"A bed," Sedge said. "A fine bed, with posts of dark wood, carried on the shoulders of six of the pale ones. And in the bed lay a man. Big man. Grey in his beard, or he'd have been grey — his beard was white too, everything white, everything drained — and he wore a nightshirt, the kind a king sleeps in, embroidered at the collar. His eyes were closed. His chest moved. Slowly. So slowly you'd miss it if you blinked.

"And he wore the crown."

The candles flickered. Noor realized they had stopped breathing and made themselves start again.

"He was asleep," Sedge said. "Or something near enough to sleep that it makes no difference. And the pale ones carried him down — down past the cistern, down the stair that isn't on any map, down under the Spire's roots, into the dark where even we don't go, because the dark down there hums and things that hum want you to come closer and be quiet forever. And I watched them go, from behind a broken pipe, with my heart going so hard I was sure they'd hear it. And the last of them turned at the bottom of the stair, just before the dark took them, and looked straight at where I was hiding.

"And smiled."

Nobody spoke. Pip had come to stand beside Noor at some point without Noor noticing, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, the way children stand next to someone bigger when the story gets bad.

"They didn't kill me," Sedge said simply. "That's the worst part, charm-seller. They *saw* me, and they didn't care. I was nothing. A rat behind a pipe. Why would the harvesters bother with one rat? They had what they'd come for." He paused. "My mother said I dreamed wrong for a year after. Screamed at nothing. She thought I'd eaten spoiled grain. But it wasn't the grain. It was the smile."

* * *

"That was seven, eight years ago," Noor said slowly, when Sedge had climbed down and the silence had stretched long enough to ache. "And the king — King Aldous — he was seen after that. Public appearances. The balcony at Midwinter. The processions through the Merchant Quarter. I *saw* him. Everyone saw him."

Marrow's whiskers twitched — not amusement this time. Pity, maybe. Or contempt for how slow humans were, which amounted to nearly the same expression.

"Did you," she said. "You saw him. On a balcony. At a distance. Behind railings. Did you ever hear him speak?"

"He gave speeches," Noor said, but even as they said it, the memory wobbled. "There were proclamations. The binding laws, fifteen years ago — he announced them himself. From the Spire steps. He..."

They trailed off. Because what they remembered was a figure in gold and white, tall and still, and a voice that carried across the square with strange flatness, like a bell rung by someone who didn't care whether it rang. And they remembered, distantly, shamefully, that everyone around them had murmured about how quiet the king seemed, how changed, and how quickly such talk had stopped — stopped all at once, the way talk does when it becomes dangerous.

"Fifteen years ago the magic-binding started," Noor said. The pieces were rearranging themselves in their head with an almost audible click. "The laws. The registry. The seizures. Everyone said it was fear — the Hollow Fever years, the fires, the panic. Control. The crown wanted control."

"And now?" Marrow prompted.

"Now they're hunting us. Not registering us. Not fining us. *Hunting.* Silver for names. Wardens with hounds." Noor's mouth had gone dry. "If the king died seven years ago — if something else has been wearing him since — then the binding wasn't control. Binding is how you keep a thing fresh. Caged magic doesn't fade. It doesn't disperse into the ground the way wild magic does. It stays. It pools. It—" They stopped, horrified by the clarity of it. "It ripens."

"*Now you're thinking,*" said Marrow, with grim approval.

"The hunt isn't about control," Noor said. Each word came out heavier than the last. "It never was. It's a harvest. Fifteen years of binding every gifted soul in Vellamark, bottling us up like preserves, and now they're gathering the jars. Taking us somewhere. Somewhere with copper roots running down into the dark, somewhere a sleeping man in a crown has been kept alive all this time because—"

"Because dead magic fades," said Sefa softly, from beside her moths. The fortune-teller hadn't moved in minutes, but her eyes were bright and terrible in the candlelight. "Living vessels hold it. Slowly fed upon, a gifted person can yield for years. Decades, if you're patient. If you're cruel in the patient way."

"And the thing upstairs," Noor said, and their voice sounded far away to their own ears, "has been eating Vellamark one gifted soul at a time for seven years, and nobody noticed, because who looks at a king on a balcony and thinks *that's not him*?"

"I noticed," said a rat from the back of the crescent, and there was a ripple of grim laughter, brief and necessary, the kind of laughter that keeps people from screaming.

* * *

Afterward — after the crescent of rats dispersed into the walls, after Sedge limped off into the dark with Marrow's paw briefly resting on his shoulder — Noor stood alone at the edge of the Tallow Market, holding a cup of chicory tea that had gone cold without them noticing.

Brannic found them there. The falconer had a way of arriving without seeming to walk anywhere, all silence and shadow, though Noor suspected the silence was deliberate, a habit left over from a life spent moving through rooms where kings slept.

"Well," he said. "Now you know."

"Do I?" Noor laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I know a rat's story about a pale procession eight years ago. That's not knowing. That's a nightmare with witnesses."

"It's more proof than most men ever get." Brannic leaned against the tunnel wall. In the candlelight his empty sleeve hung pinned and flat, and Noor found themselves staring at it and wondering, not for the first time, what had taken the arm — and why a royal falconer was living under the city selling candle-fat and secrets. "I've heard Sedge's tale a dozen times. Every detail the same. Rats are like that. Better memories than priests, and less reason to lie."

"Why are you telling me this? Helping me? You barely know me. You offered me a corner to sleep in and no questions, and now suddenly there's a plot against the crown and I'm in the middle of it."

Brannic was quiet a long moment. When he answered, he looked at the tunnel wall instead of at Noor.

"Because you asked the rats what they remembered," he said. "Everyone else asks them what they can steal."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's not." And he pushed off the wall and walked away into the candle-smoke, leaving Noor with the strong suspicion that Brannic knew a great deal more about pale processions than he was ready to say, and that the arm and the king and the same terrible night were braided together in him like rope.

* * *

Noor couldn't sleep. They lay in the corner Brannic had lent them — a pallet, a blanket that smelled of tallow and hawk, a candle stub — and stared at the low ceiling of the tunnel, and did arithmetic with dread.

Fifteen years of bindings. Seven years of harvest. How many gifted souls had been taken in the night, quietly, their disappearances explained by fever, by flight, by debt? The neighbor who'd vanished three winters ago. The fiddle-player from the Copper Lane market whose music had made flowers open out of season, gone between one fair day and the next. The midwife. Gods, the midwife, who could soothe any pain with a touch, taken for questioning and never returned, and everyone had said *wardens, probably sent her north*.

North. There was no north. There was only down, and a stair that hummed.

Noor thought about the hounds on the rooftops, the ones ordered to hunt their own kin. Thought about the silver paid for names. Thought about a smiling white face turning at the bottom of a stair, indifferent to one small witness, because one small witness was nothing against a city full of ripening jars.

Fear had been simple. Fear said: *run, hide, survive.* Fear fit inside a satchel.

This was bigger. This was the slow understanding that the cage they'd been hiding in was itself inside a larger cage, and the thing keeping both had been feeding the whole time, patiently, politely, wearing a dead man's crown and waving from balconies at the people it was fattening for the table.

Noor got up, relit the candle stub, and dug out their satchel. The luck-knots were still there, the little harmless charms, the lies they'd told with their hands while their gift whispered underneath. They held one up — a knot of red thread, three loops, sold for a copper to a woman who'd wanted safe travels — and thought about all the knots they'd tied and all the words they'd swallowed.

Not anymore.

Somewhere in the walls, small claws skittered — Marrow's people, going about the undercity's business. Noor spoke softly toward the stone, knowing they'd hear.

"Tell your matriarch I need to speak with Brannic again. Tomorrow. Properly. About the night he lost his arm."

The claws paused. Then, faintly, amused: *He won't like that.*

"No," Noor said. "I don't imagine he will."

They blew out the candle and lay back down, and the dark of the undercity pressed close, and beneath them — far beneath, under the cisterns, under the roots, under everything — something ancient and patient hummed its hive-song, and Noor fell asleep composing, over and over, the first hard question they would ask in the morning.
