## 6. Brannic's Oath

The night the king went mad, it was raining, and Brannic had complained about his boots.

He remembered that. Fifteen years later, he still remembered that — standing in the mews corridor of the Spire, wringing rainwater out of his left bootlace and grumbling to a young gyrfalcon named Sorrel about how the crown could afford a hundred gold weathervanes but not one decent pair of waterproof boots for the man who kept its birds alive. It was the sort of complaint a man makes when everything is ordinary, and the gods, who have a cruel sense of theater, like to let a man make it right up until the moment nothing is ordinary ever again.

He had been royal falconer for nine years by then. Nine years of dawn flights and bloody knuckles and the particular, unrepeatable pleasure of a hawk coming back to your fist because she chose to — not because she was bound, never because she was bound, because binding a hawk's wing broke something in her forever, and King Aldous had understood that. That was the thing people forgot now, in the years since the outlawry: the old king had been a man who hated cages.

Brannic had loved him for it. Quietly, the way soldiers love, which is to say without ever saying so.

That night there had been a summons. Not for him — for the king himself, from the Spire's deep chambers where the court magicians kept their instruments. But the summons had come through the mews, oddly, carried by a page whose face was gray as porridge, and the king had stopped on his way past to check on Sorrel's new jesses, because that was the kind of man Aldous was, and so Brannic had been standing three feet away when the thing happened in the deep chambers below.

He had heard it before he saw anything. A sound like a bell being unmade. And every bird in the mews had screamed at once.

Not cried out. Screamed — a hundred throats, hawks and falcons and the old merlin half-blind with age, all of them at once, a sound Brannic had never heard before or since, and never wanted to hear again. It was the sound of animals feeling something arrive. Something vast and hungry and wrong, arriving underneath their feet, in the heart of the tower they lived in.

"Steady," Brannic had said, stupidly, uselessly, moving down the row with his free hand out. "Steady, steady, what is it, what —"

And then the king had screamed too.

Not the birds' scream. A man's scream, torn out of him from somewhere deeper than the lungs, and Brannic had run — run down the spiral stair toward the deep chambers with no weapon and no plan, because nine years of loving a man will make you do that — and what he found at the bottom he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe and failing.

The chamber was round, and its floor was copper. Copper veins, copper roots, running out through the walls in every direction like the roots of an inverted tree, and along those roots, in that single terrible moment, magic itself was flowing — upward, inward, drawn from every bound source in the city into the center of the room, where the king stood with his hands pressed flat against a lattice of copper wire, and something was pouring into him through the crown on his head.

Something else was pouring into him. Something wearing the crown.

Brannic never saw the entity clearly. He saw the edges of it — a darkness behind the king's eyes that did not belong to any human face, a shape in the air above the lattice that bent the torchlight wrong, the way heat bends it, if heat were patient. He heard it speak. He wished, every day after, that he hadn't. It spoke in the king's voice and in six other voices layered beneath it, voices going back further than any reign, and it said:

*This one will do. This one will do nicely.*

And King Aldous — good King Aldous, who fed his hawks from his own plate and once spent an entire afternoon helping a stable-boy look for a lost cat — turned his head and looked at Brannic with eyes that were not entirely his own, and smiled.

"Brannic," the king said pleasantly. "You saw nothing."

"I — your majesty —"

"You saw nothing," the king repeated, and the smile stayed fixed while the voice underneath the voice said, in a register that made the copper roots sing, *and neither did your arm.*

The pain came after. He remembered that much clearly — that the pain arrived late, like thunder after lightning, and by the time it did, the lattice had already taken his left arm from just below the elbow. Taken it cleanly, bloodlessly, the way a debt collector takes coin, and the copper roots had drunk whatever ran through a man that was not blood, and the king had watched with that borrowed smile, and Brannic had lain on the cold floor listening to himself make sounds he didn't recognize as his own.

The last thing the king said to him that night, crouching down, gentle as a nurse, was: "You fell in the mews. The birds spooked. Everyone knows this. Say it back."

Everyone knows this.

So Brannic had said it back. Through the fever, through the surgeon's work, through the long gray weeks of learning to do everything in the world with one hand, he had said it back. To the physicians. To the queen's steward, before the queen conveniently died of a wasting illness nobody quite named. To the councilors who asked their careful questions with their careful faces. *I fell in the mews. The birds spooked.* He said it so many times it wore a groove in him, like a rope over a stone sill, until the lie and the truth were both worn smooth and indistinguishable.

Then had come the Proclamation — magic outlawed, the gifted named enemies, the hunt begun — and Brannic had looked at the notice nailed to the Falconers' Gate and understood, finally and completely, what he had helped bury. The thing under the Spire didn't want obedience. It wanted harvest. Every law against magic was a fence around a field, and the field was full of children, and he had spent fifteen years keeping his mouth shut because he was afraid, only afraid, just afraid, and fear had made him a gardener for the thing that ate them.

He'd resigned his post within the year. Walked out through the same gate with one arm, one bird — Sorrel, smuggled out under his coat, furious about it for a week — and gone down instead of up, because down was the only direction left that the crown wasn't looking.

* * *

"Noor."

The boy — Noor was hardly a boy, but everyone under Vellamark seemed young to Brannic now, everyone except Old Marrow, who was older than the city's plumbing and twice as durable — looked up from the bowl of barley soup Sefa had thrust upon them. In the lamplight of the Tallow Market's back room, with wax stalactites dripping overhead and the smell of tallow and wet stone and somebody's endless onion stew, Noor Halloran looked exactly like what they were: exhausted, hunted, and stubborn as a burr.

"Eat," Brannic said. "Then listen."

Noor ate. Good instinct. Brannic approved of people who ate when offered food during a crisis; it showed priorities were intact.

"When you were upstairs," Brannic said, "with the rats, hearing what Marrow's lot remembered about the procession — you looked at me like a person looks at a locked door."

"I didn't mean —"

"Don't apologize. I've been a locked door fifteen years. I know the look." Brannic settled onto the crate across from them, and his missing arm ached the way it always ached before a confession, which was to say, precisely. "You deserve to know why the door's been locked. So. Sit still. This isn't a short story, and I'm only telling it once, because telling things more than once wears grooves in a man."

Noor set down the spoon.

"Fifteen years ago," Brannic began, "I was the royal falconer. Which means I was closer to the king than most men are to their own brothers. I knew his moods from the set of his shoulders. I knew he hummed off-key when he was happy and went silent when he was worried, and I knew — gods help me, I knew — that in his last year he started humming less." He turned his cup in his fingers. "There were signs. Little ones. Days he didn't come to the mews. A look in his eye like a man listening to something far away. I told myself it was the war taxes. I told myself it was his health. You can tell yourself a great deal when telling yourself the truth would cost you your place, your pension, and possibly your neck."

"And then?" Noor asked quietly.

"And then I saw it. All of it. What sits under the Spire, what the copper does, what poured itself into my king while he stood there and smiled at me with someone else's smile." Brannic's hand had gone white-knuckled on the cup without his permission. He loosened it. "It took my arm that night. Not in rage — in bookkeeping. That's the part that keeps me up nights, Noor. It wasn't angry. It was *tidying*. I was a loose entry in a ledger, and it balanced me."

Silence, except for the drip of wax somewhere above, and the far-off murmur of the market — Pip arguing with a kettle about rust, by the sound of it.

"Why tell me now?" Noor said. "You've had fifteen years to tell anyone. Why me?"

Brannic laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate that needed oiling. "Because you're the first person who asked the rats before asking me. Because you looked at the truth-shaped hole in my story and didn't pretend it wasn't there. And because—" he paused, and found that the next words came easier than he'd expected, which was either grace or exhaustion, "—because I'm tired, lad. Tired of being a locked door. A secret kept too long stops protecting anyone and starts feeding the thing it hides from. I learned that from watching a hawk I refused to bind. Keep her flightless and she sickens. Same with truth."

Noor's face did something complicated — recognition, maybe, or kinship. "I hid my gift for fifteen years too," they said. "Behind a stall of luck-knots. Whispering to horses in empty alleys like a criminal."

"Then you know the shape of it. The whispering life." Brannic nodded slowly. "Here's what I know that you don't, and it's ugly, so brace yourself. The king is alive. Whatever wears him has kept him alive — hollowed out, dreaming, but alive — because a dead king is a martyr and a living king is a *vessel*, and vessels last longer if you don't crack them. Marrow's rats saw the procession go down. I felt the moment it happened. Both of us have been carrying half a map for fifteen years." He leaned forward. "Together, we might have the whole of it. And the whole of it means there may be a way to reach him. To reach the real him, buried under all that copper and all those stolen years."

"Do you believe that?" Noor asked. "Truly?"

Brannic considered lying — briefly, out of habit — and then decided he had retired from the profession.

"I believe a hawk comes back to the fist if you haven't broken what calls her home," he said. "That's all the theology I've got. But it's held true so far."

Noor looked down at their hands — market-worn hands, charm-seller's hands, hands that had spent fifteen years pretending to be smaller than they were — and when they looked up, something had shifted in their face. The hunted look was still there, but behind it now was something with a spine in it.

"The rats said harvest," Noor said. "Sefa's moths keep turning dark. If the hunt is a harvest, then every gifted person the wardens take goes down those copper roots, same as your arm went. Same as the king went."

"Yes."

"Then we're not running anymore." It wasn't quite a question, and it wasn't quite addressed to Brannic either; it was addressed to the room, to the wax and the stone and the family assembling itself piece by piece in the dark under Vellamark. "We're going in."

Brannic felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight for fifteen years — not relief exactly, and not hope, but the sturdy working-class cousin of both. Purpose. He'd missed purpose. It fit better than the arm ever had.

"Well then," he said, and stood, and held out his one hand. "Falconer's rule, Noor Halloran: you don't fly a bird you haven't weighed, and you don't storm a Spire you haven't planned. But between weighing and planning—" his grip closed around theirs, scarred palm to charm-seller's palm, conspirator to conspirator— "there's a first step, and it's this."

From the doorway, Old Marrow's whiskers twitched in what might have been approval, and somewhere above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the Spire hummed — low and patient and utterly unaware that its ledger was about to be audited.
