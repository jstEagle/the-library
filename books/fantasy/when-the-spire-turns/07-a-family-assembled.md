## 7. A Family Assembled

### Noor

The Tallow Market smelled of candle-fat and wet stone, and by its third morning Noor had learned to stop flinching at the things that lived in it.

There was a woman at the corner stall who read fortunes in the wings of moths — *Sefa*, she was called, with rings on every finger and a laugh that arrived three seconds before anything funny was said. There was a boy of perhaps eleven who sat on an anvil no one had ever seen him hammer, whispering to a pile of old hinges until they opened and closed like mouths. His name was Pip, and he had already stolen Noor's spoon twice, "because it liked me better."

And there were the rats.

Noor had known rats all their life, from above — quick gray shadows along the market gutters, spoken to only in the smallest whispers, never answered. Here, they came and sat openly on crates like merchants waiting for their turn at the scale.

It was Old Marrow who found them first. She was enormous as rats went, gray gone silver at the muzzle, with one ear notched like a docked coin. She climbed Brannic's stool while Noor ate a breakfast of barley porridge, planted her paws on the table's edge, and said:

"You chew like a rabbit with a grudge."

Noor choked on the porridge.

"Marrow," Brannic said mildly, without looking up from the arrow shaft he was fletching, "we talked about startling guests."

"We talked about you *wanting* me to," Marrow said. "You hoped she'd choke. You've been hoping she'd leave for three days." She turned her bright black eyes on Noor. "He hasn't told you what he is yet, has he? A royal falconer. One arm short. Guilt up to the teeth."

"Marrow—"

"Fine, fine." She settled onto the crate beside Noor's elbow, tail curled neat as a written line. "I'll say nothing else. Except this: your gift isn't a whisper anymore, girl-or-otherwise. Down here it's a bell. And bells get rung."

"I'm not—" Noor began, then stopped, because arguing with a rat felt like arguing with weather.

"You are," Marrow said comfortably. "You've been talking to my people since the hour you fell down the Drowned Stair. Asking after exits. Asking whether we'd bite. Very polite, very quiet, very much the habit of someone who learned young that being overheard is dying." Her whiskers twitched. "Nobody down here will report you. That's the whole point of the place."

Noor looked around the Tallow Market — at Sefa laughing with a fishwife whose scales were actually fish scales; at Pip asleep against his anvil with a hinge curled under his chin like a cat; at the lamplight caught in forty jars of rendered fat, each one glowing faintly gold with something that wasn't quite fire.

"Then why does everyone keep saying I should leave?"

"Because Brannic loves you," Marrow said, "and loving people makes them stupid about safety." She groomed one paw, unbothered. "Also he owes me cheese."

* * *

### Brannic

Brannic had spent fifteen years perfecting the art of not caring about people, and in four days Noor Halloran had dismantled it with barley porridge and a talent for asking exactly the wrong questions.

"You're staring at your fletching like it insulted your mother," Sefa observed, dropping into the chair across from him. She dealt her moth-cards out between them out of pure habit — wings pressed flat, iridescent, dead things that still seemed to listen. "Which means you're staring at the speaker-child."

"I'm deciding how much trouble they're worth."

"Mm. And how much is that?"

Brannic didn't answer. Above them, through sixty feet of stone and old brick, Vellamark went about its lawful day: criers calling silver for names, wardens' boots on the cobbles, a city holding its breath and pretending to sing. He had heard that sound before. He had heard it the year the king changed.

"The child has a gift the wardens can use," he said finally. "That's the whole arithmetic. Speak to hounds, speak to horses, walk into any warden post in the kingdom and open every door. The crown would give a barony for them."

"And you'd rather they weren't given."

"I'd rather they weren't *caught*."

Sefa gathered her cards, tapped them square. "So make them useful instead of catchable."

"They're a market charmer. They sold luck-knots."

"So did half the gifted in this city before the Proclamation. So did you sell falcons, once upon a time." She leaned forward, rings clicking. "Brannic. The hunt won't stop at the market stalls. It'll come below eventually — it always comes below. When it does, do you want a frightened child hiding in your cellar, or someone who knows what they can do?"

From the shadows behind the grain sacks, a voice said: "I know what I can do."

Pip emerged, rubbing sleep from his eyes, hinge still in hand. "They talk to animals. Everyone knows. Even the moths know, and the moths don't gossip, which tells you how obvious it is." He climbed onto the table, entirely uninvited, and sat cross-legged among Brannic's arrowheads. "My da used to say rust remembers everything that ever touched it. Chains, locks, cage-bars. If the wardens come below, I could ask the locks to be shy."

"Locks can't be shy," Brannic said.

"These ones could. I've asked." Pip shrugged. "Mostly they sulk about it, but they do it."

Brannic looked at the boy — skinny, gap-toothed, utterly serious — and felt the old cold thing in his chest crack another degree further. This was how it started. This was how it had started last time, too: one person worth protecting becoming two, becoming five, becoming a night in the palace mews with blood on the straw and a king screaming at things only he could see.

"One plan," he heard himself say. "One careful plan, and nobody plays hero. Not the child, not the rust-boy, not the fortune-teller, and certainly not the rat."

"Rats don't play hero," said Marrow from the crate. "We play chess. Fetch a board and I'll show you."

* * *

### Noor

They assembled, though Noor hadn't understood until later that this was what was happening — that the loose, wary orbit of strangers was tightening into something with a shape.

Sefa brought her moth-cards and a kettle. Pip brought six locks of varying temperament, which he introduced by name. Marrow brought two younger rats, Fern and Cobble, who mostly served as an audience for her opinions. And Brannic — Brannic dragged a slate down from his sleeping loft, chalked a rough map of the Spire district on it, and stood before it like a man reporting to a garrison that consisted of a fortune-teller, two children, and a rodent matriarch.

"The hunt works from the top down," he said. "Wardens take names at the gates, sweep the guildhalls, then the tenements. Below-ground comes last, always — the crown's never cared what happens under its own feet." He tapped the slate. "But 'last' isn't 'never.' We have maybe a month. Less if someone above sells us."

"So we run," Noor said. It was what they'd assumed since the Drowned Stair. "Out through the flood tunnels, past the weir—"

"And live where?" Brannic asked, not unkindly. "In the hills, eating pinecones? Every road out of Vellamark has a warden toll now. Every village has a bounty board. Your face goes on one within the week." He set down the chalk. "Running keeps you alive. It doesn't keep you *safe*. There's a difference, and the difference is the rest of your life."

Silence, except for the kettle beginning its low complaint.

"What, then?" Noor asked. "Stay and be harvested?"

"That depends," Marrow said, "on what the harvest's for."

All eyes went to her. She sat up on the crate, and even Fern and Cobble went still.

"My people go everywhere, little speaker. Drains, walls, granaries, palaces — especially palaces; palaces are made of crumbs." Her voice lost its dryness. "Three years back, some of mine saw a procession in the Spire's under-halls. Night work, no torches but green ones. Men carrying a litter down and down, and on the litter a man asleep — pale as tallow, wearing a crown, and *breathing wrong*. Slow. Like a man breathing through someone else's lungs."

The kettle shrieked. Nobody moved to it.

"They carried him down," Marrow said, "and they did not carry him up. And the king who walks the palace gardens since then — the one who ordered your hunt — my people say he doesn't smell right. Doesn't smell like a man at all. Smells like cold iron and old honey." She looked at Noor. "You asked why stay. I say: because whatever wears the crown up there is eating this city, and it just started eating faster. Running from it doesn't starve it. It just leaves more room at the table."

Noor's hands had gone cold around their cup. Fifteen years of hiding, and they had thought the worst truth was simply *they will find me*. It had never occurred to them the finding might be the smallest part of it.

"Then we need to know," Sefa said quietly. All the laughter had gone out of her; her moth-cards lay untouched. "Not guess. Know. What's on the throne, what it wants, where it's weak." She looked around the table, at each of them in turn. "Moths for omens. Rats for memory. Rust for what the cages and chains have heard. Falconer's knowledge of the Spire itself." Her gaze landed last on Noor, gentle and terrible. "And a voice that every living thing in that building might answer."

"I'm not leading anything," Noor said. "I sold luck-knots."

"You freed a wren," Pip said, "and the wren told the sparrows, and the sparrows told the pigeons, and the pigeons are unbearable about it. You're famous. Well. Among birds."

Despite everything — despite the dread sitting in their chest like swallowed river-stone — Noor laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, and Pip grinned like he'd picked their pocket of it.

* * *

Later, when the others had drifted off — Sefa to her cards, Pip to bedeck his anvil with the evening's locks, Marrow to wherever matriarchs go — Noor stayed at the table with Brannic, watching the candle burn down through its jar of fat.

"You could still send me away," Noor said. "Up the stair, out the north drain. You clearly want to."

Brannic was quiet long enough that the flame guttered.

"When I lost my arm," he said at last, "it was in the Spire. In the king's own service. And I have spent fifteen years telling myself I saw nothing, because seeing something meant the king I loved was mad or worse, and I couldn't afford either." He turned the stub of his sleeve over, absently, like checking an old wound. "I'm not sending you anywhere, Halloran. I'm trying to decide if I'm brave enough to look at what I buried. You've made it harder. You keep *staying*."

"Is that bad?"

"It's the worst thing you could do to a man like me." But he almost smiled as he said it, and pushed the last of the bread across the table without being asked, and Noor understood that this, too, was a kind of answer.

They walked back to their pallet beneath the stairs, past the jars of soft-gold light, past the anvil where a lock murmured goodnight to a boy already snoring. And Noor lay in the dark with their arms crossed over their chest, guarding something new.

They had been orphaned at seven. They had been alone so long that solitude had grown into them like a splinter grows into wood — painful, familiar, part of the grain. And now there was a fortune-teller who saved them the first pour of tea. A boy who stole their spoon because "it liked them better." A rat who called them *little speaker* like it was a title. A one-armed man who fed them bread he couldn't spare.

It felt like standing on ice that was bearing weight. It felt like the moment before either cracking or holding.

*Please,* Noor thought, to no one, to the dark, to whatever listened — *don't let me lose this too.*

Above them, faint through the stone, the great bell of the Spire tolled the hour. And far off in the drains, a sound Noor had never heard before rose and faded: hundreds of rat-feet, running together, all in one direction.

Old Marrow's voice reached them last, carried up through a vent like smoke:

"Wake the speaker. Something's come up from the deep water, and it's been asking for them by name."
