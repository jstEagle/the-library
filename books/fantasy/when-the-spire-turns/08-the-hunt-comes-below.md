## 8. The Hunt Comes Below

Sefa had read moths for forty years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above the others: the small omens always arrive first.

That morning it was a dead grey miller moth on her reading cloth, wings spread like a tiny surrendered flag, and beside it — this was the part that made her hands go cold — a candle-moth that refused to land. It circled her lamp in tight, frantic rings, over and over, like a thought that would not sit down.

"Marrow," she said to the shadow beneath her stall, "what do you make of a moth that won't land?"

The rat-matriarch emerged by degrees, as she did everything: first whiskers, then eyes, then the long silvered body of her, arranging herself on Sefa's counter with the dignity of a duchess taking a throne. She looked at the circling moth. She looked at Sefa. Her tail curled into a question mark.

"Aye," Sefa said. "That's what I thought too."

The Tallow Market was already awake around them, because the Tallow Market was never properly asleep. Down here beneath Vellamark, under three layers of cellar and cistern and forgotten street, the light came from tallow candles rendered from who-knew-what, and the air smelled of wax and river water and roasting chestnuts, and everywhere there were people the city above had decided not to see. The moth-reader with her cloth of omens. The boy who spoke to rust and could tell you, by kissing a hinge, whether a door had ever been locked against anyone. Old Marrow and her parliament of rats, who kept the ledgers nobody wrote down. Brannic, one-armed and broad as a doorway, mending falcon hoods at a bench he'd dragged from somewhere and never explained. And now Noor Halloran, the charm-seller from above, five days fled from the hunt, who was learning to eat chestnuts without flinching at every footstep overhead.

Sefa watched Noor across the market lane, haggling with the tallow-renderer over a bundle of candles, and felt the old ache she'd felt watching young exiles for decades: the way they laughed too hard at small jokes, the way they checked exits mid-conversation, the way safety sat on them like an unfamiliar coat they kept waiting to be asked to give back.

Noor was laughing now, actually laughing, at something the renderer had said, and Sefa let herself hope — foolishly, she'd later think, but hope is never foolish at the time — that maybe the crown's long arm would be slow reaching down three flights of drowned stairs.

Then the candles guttered. All of them. Every flame in the Tallow Market bent sideways at once, toward the flood tunnels, as if bowing to something coming.

Marrow stood up on her hind legs, and every rat in the market stood with her, a hundred small silhouettes along the pipes and rafters, all facing the same direction.

"They're in the west tunnels," Sefa breathed, because Marrow's rats spoke to her in their own fashion, in pressures and patterns she'd spent years learning to read. "Wardens. A lot of them. With—" She swallowed. "With dogs."

* * *

The trouble with living below the law, Brannic had told them once, tapping his stump against his knee, is that you get good at leaving. Too good. You practice running so often that when the day comes you almost don't notice you've stopped building anything worth staying for.

Sefa thought of that as the market dissolved around her. It didn't panic, exactly — that was the terrible, admirable thing. The chestnut seller banked his coals in four practiced movements. The rust-boy Pip kissed the great iron gate and murmured to it, and the lock that had held fast against wardens for a decade sighed open like an old man changing his mind. People folded their lives into bundles and moved, quick and quiet, toward the flood tunnels east, where the water ran shallow and the ceiling was high enough for standing.

"East tunnel, everyone," Brannic boomed, calm as Sunday, herding with his one arm the way a shepherd moves sheep. "Sefa, take your cloth. Leave the lamp."

"The lamp is brass—"

"Leave the *lamp*, woman."

She left the lamp. She took the cloth, with its dead miller moth and its frantic candle-moth, which had finally landed — on her shoulder, wings trembling, refusing to leave her.

Noor appeared at her elbow, satchel already strapped, face pale. "Where do I go?"

"With me." Brannic's hand closed on Noor's shoulder. "You're the one they're hunting hardest. You go deepest. Marrow—"

The rat-matriarch was already flowing toward the west passage, and Sefa understood: the rats would go toward the danger, counting it, buying seconds with their bodies if they must. She caught the old creature's eye and Marrow gave her a look of such ancient, weary competence that Sefa almost smiled despite everything.

Almost.

Because that was when the sound came — boots in the west tunnel, many boots, and the particular jingle of warden chain-mail, and beneath it, worst of all, a chorus of panting. Hounds. Dozens of them, breathing as one animal.

And then light. Warden lanterns, white and merciless after the market's warm tallow glow, pouring through the western arch like spilled milk.

"By order of the Crown," a voice rang out, young and frightened itself, Sefa noted, "all persons of unbound gift will submit for assessment!"

Nobody submitted. Nobody even slowed. The Tallow Market emptied like a cup tipped on its side, and Sefa ran with the rest, cloth clutched to her chest, her poor moth riding her shoulder like a pilot who'd chosen his ship.

* * *

The flood tunnels east were built by engineers two hundred years dead who had clearly expected Vellamark's poor to drown politely. The passages branched and doubled back; the water varied between ankle and knee; and every fifty paces there was an alcove where, in theory, a person might shelter while the flood took someone else.

Sefa splashed along behind Brannic and Noor, and behind her came others — the chestnut man, two sisters who mended broken glass by singing to it, old Tomas who could find water with a bent pin and had been finding it for people free of charge since before the outlawry. A whole congregation of the quietly gifted, fleeing by routes they'd memorized the way other people memorized prayers.

They were nearly to the junction at Weeper's Bend — Sefa knew it by the trickle that ran eternally down the wall there, cold as a reproach — when the hounds' voices changed.

Anyone who has lived near hunting dogs knows their grammar. There is the eager bay of the chase, and the triumphant clamor of the cornered quarry, and then — rarer, stranger — the confused, questioning whine of a dog that has lost the thread of what it's doing.

That third voice came echoing down the tunnel, multiplied by dozens, and Sefa stopped so suddenly that Tomas walked into her back.

Behind them, back toward the market, the baying fractured. Some hounds still sang the hunt. But others had gone silent, or worse, were making sounds no handler wants to hear — low, troubled, almost pleading noises, the sounds of animals being asked to do something their hearts misgave them.

"What in the deep dark," Brannic muttered.

"It's Noor," Sefa said, and turned.

Noor had stopped in the middle of the flooded passage. Water to their knees, satchel dripping, head cocked toward the distant hounds the way one listens to music from another room. Their lips were moving. Not words Sefa could catch — a whisper, barely breath — but she saw the shape of it, saw Noor speaking into the dark toward those distant voices, and she remembered, with a lurch, what Noor was.

A beast-speaker. The last honest one, perhaps, in a city that had spent fifteen years pretending such things weren't real.

"Noor," Brannic growled. "We haven't time for—"

"I'm not commanding them," Noor said, and their voice cracked in the middle. "I'm just — talking. Asking them to slow down. To lose the scent. Telling them—" They swallowed. "Telling them they're being made to hurt their own kind. That's all. That's all I'm doing."

The hounds' voices shifted again. More silence now than song. Somewhere far back, a warden shouted in frustration, and a whip cracked, and the whip-crack was followed by a sound Sefa would carry the rest of her days: a single hound, howling refusal.

Brannic stared down the tunnel toward the noise. His jaw worked. Fifteen years ago, Sefa realized, he had stood in a palace mews full of birds whose king had gone strange, and he had said nothing, and some part of him had been standing in that mews ever since.

"Good," he said finally, hoarsely. "Good lad. Now *run*."

They ran.

* * *

They should have made the deep junction. Everyone agreed afterward that they should have made it; the route was sound, the timing was close but workable, and Marrow's scouts had confirmed the eastern galleries clear barely an hour before. Fate, Sefa thought, does not read your plans. Fate reads your moths, and hers had been screaming all morning.

The ambush waited at the second fork past Weeper's Bend — a single lantern, a single warden, and six hounds fanned out across the passage like a drawn curtain.

He was young, that warden. Barely older than Pip. His lantern shook. His voice, when he used it, shook worse. "Stop! In the name of—"

Everyone stopped, because six hounds are very persuasive, and for one long moment the only sounds were water dripping and dogs panting and Sefa's own heart hammering her ribs like a debtor at the door.

Then the hounds scented Noor.

Sefa watched it happen. Watched the lead hound — a big brindle bitch with gray at her muzzle, a veteran — go rigid, nose working, and then slowly, slowly lower her head and *step aside*. Just slightly. An inch of paw, a shift of weight. But unmistakable. Making room. And the next hound did the same, and the next, until the curtain of dogs had parted around Noor like water around a stone, and the brindle bitch was looking up at Noor with an expression Sefa had seen on mothers and on mourners and once, memorably, on a moth.

"Noor," Brannic whispered. "What did you say to them?"

"Nothing," Noor whispered back. "I haven't said anything yet."

But the warden heard the whispering, and fear did what fear does. "Stand away from that one!" he shrilled, and lunged forward, grabbing — not at Noor, not at Brannic, not at any adult who might have knocked him flat with a clear conscience — but at the smallest figure in the company.

Pip had hung back the whole flight, Sefa realized too late. Hung back and drifted, drifting the way rust-boys drift, touching things — the wall brackets, the pipe collars, the old iron ring set in the stone — listening to the metal's long slow gossip. He hadn't heard the warden coming. He was fourteen and he was listening to a pipe.

The warden's fist closed in his collar.

"No!" Noor's voice rang off the wet stone, huge, nothing whisper-like about it now. "Let him go! It's me you want — the proclamation says beast-speakers, it's me, I'll come quiet, I'll—"

"Shut it!" The warden was backing away now, dragging Pip, and his hounds were doing a thing Sefa found herself cataloguing even through her horror, because that was what forty years of reading signs did to a person: the hounds were not advancing. They were planted. All six, feet set, heads low, making no move to help their handler, and one of them — the brindle — had begun, very softly, to growl. At *him*.

"You're frightening them," Noor said, stepping forward, hands open and empty, voice dropping into something low and level and terribly gentle. "Not us. Them. Look at them. Look what you're asking."

"I said shut—" The warden's boot slipped on the wet stone, just a little, just enough, and Pip twisted like the eel he'd once wrestled on a dare, and for half a heartbeat he was free —

And then the whistle came. From down the tunnel, sharp and double-noted, a warden's recall-and-seize signal, and from the darkness beyond the young warden's lantern poured reinforcements, and whatever mercy the hounds had been considering was drowned under numbers and orders and the crack of a sergeant's whip.

Sefa saw Pip go. Everyone saw Pip go. She saw him fight, all elbows and fury, and kiss two different pieces of iron in passing — later she would learn he was telling the locks and hinges of the Spire to remember him, to wait for him, though at the time it looked like nothing, like a boy pawing at walls — and she saw the brindle hound lunge after the dragging party and be beaten back, twice, three times, whimpering even as it obeyed.

Then the lanterns receded. Then the splash of boots faded westward. Then there was only the water, and the dripping, and six hounds sitting in a row looking at Noor like penitents, and Noor standing among them with their hands pressed flat against their own sternum as if holding something in.

"Pip," Sefa said. It came out as a croak. She tried again. "Pip."

* * *

They gathered in the deep gallery an hour later, the scattered congregation trickling back one by one to the place Marrow's rats designated — because of course the rats had known, had counted wardens and mapped lanterns, and of course the old matriarch sat waiting on a dry stone shelf with her tail wrapped round her feet and her whiskers drooping in a way Sefa had never seen on her, not once in twenty years.

Grief, in the Tallow Market, did not look like weeping. It looked like the chestnut man banking coals he wouldn't light again tonight. It looked like the glass-mending sisters holding each other's wrists. It looked like Brannic sitting very still on a crate, staring at his remaining hand as though it had failed him, and saying, over and over, so quietly Sefa almost missed it:

"He kissed my knife. Before we ran. Kissed the hinge of my knife-roll. Told it—" His voice broke off. Started again. "Told it to keep its edge true. Fourteen years old."

Noor knelt in the shallows at the gallery's edge, and the six hounds — they had followed, deserters now, hunted things themselves — lay in the water around them like a dark, breathing tide. Noor's forehead rested against the brindle bitch's skull.

"They're sorry," Noor said, hollow. "All of them. They can't — they're bound by their oaths the way we're bound by the law, it goes deeper in them, they *have* to—" A shudder went through them. "One of them bit her own handler rather than hold Pip. They're punishing themselves for it. They keep asking me if the boy is dead. I keep telling them I don't know. I hate not knowing. I hate—"

"Hush now." Sefa lowered herself onto the stone beside Marrow, joints protesting, and laid her omen cloth across her knees. The candle-moth had died sometime during the flight; she hadn't the heart to note what that meant. "Hush, child. Grief is fuel, my grandmother used to say. It burns filthy, but it burns hot."

"And what do we cook with it?" Noor lifted their head. Their eyes in the lamplight — someone's salvaged lamp, passed hand to hand — were red-rimmed and absolutely ferocious. "Tell me what we do. Because I've been hiding for fifteen years, and running for five days, and I am *done* being something that runs."

Silence, then. The good kind and the terrible kind mixed together.

It was Marrow who broke it. The old rat rose, stretched, and descended from her shelf with unhurried ceremony, crossed the wet stone, and climbed into Noor's lap as though it were a throne long reserved. Then she looked up at the gathered company and chittered, a short complex phrase, and every rat in the galleries repeated it, and the sound rolled through the tunnels like a bell through fog.

Sefa translated, because she was the only one who could, and her voice shook only a little.

"She says: the Spire takes our children slowly, and keeps them alive, and we have always known it and always pretended we didn't." She met Brannic's eyes. "She says: no more pretending."

Brannic stood. He picked up his knife-roll, and kissed its hinge himself, once, the way Pip had.

"Then we stop pretending," he said. "Tomorrow we plan. Tonight—" his gaze swept them, chestnut man and glass-sisters and water-finder and moth-reader and charm-seller and a tide of loyal hounds, "tonight we grieve, and we eat, and we stay together. That's how we begin."

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, Vellamark's bells rang the midnight hour, and somewhere inside the Spire, Sefa did not doubt, a boy was telling iron to wait for them.

She meant to make sure it did.
