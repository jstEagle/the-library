## 9. Crown and Cage

Pip had always thought the worst thing about being arrested would be the handcuffs, or maybe the questions. He'd imagined it wrong on both counts.

There were no cuffs. There were no questions. The wardens had simply wrapped him in a blanket that smelled of wet stone and carried him down so many stairs that his legs went numb with counting, and then they'd put him in a cage.

Not a cell. A cage — actual bars, actual hinges, sized for a person the way a birdcage was sized for a wren. Pip knew birdcages. His mum had kept finches before the Proclamation, and he'd spent whole afternoons as a boy listening to them argue about seed. He knew exactly how a creature looked in a cage too small for it: folded up, quiet, pretending to be smaller than it was.

He resolved not to do that. It seemed important, suddenly, to take up space.

"Oi," he said to the dark. "Anyone home?"

The dark said nothing back, which was rude. Then something rustled two cages over, and a voice — old, dry, female — said, "New one's talking. That's either brave or stupid."

"Both, usually," Pip said. "I'm Pip."

"Pip what?"

"Just Pip. I talk to rust."

A pause. Somewhere in the blackness, metal groaned — a long, low complaint, like an old man shifting in a chair that didn't fit him anymore. Pip felt it in his teeth, the way you feel a train before you hear it. The bars around him were iron, mostly, but there was copper threaded through them, thin wires braided among the thicker stock, and the copper was *singing*. Not out loud. In that other way, the way metal sang when it had been worked by someone angry, or hurried, or frightened. Pip had heard it once in a looted church bell and never forgotten it.

"You're feeling it already," the old woman said. "I can tell. Your face is doing something."

"What is it?" Pip asked. "What are these wires?"

"We call them roots," she said. "Because that's what they do."

* * *

They came for him at what he guessed was morning, though morning was a rumor down here. Two wardens with lanterns and faces like closed doors. One unlocked the cage; the other held a pair of padded tongs, the kind farriers used, and clamped them gently — *gently*, that was the horrible part — around Pip's wrist.

"This way," said the first warden. "Don't struggle. It goes easier if you don't struggle."

"Where does it go easier?" Pip asked. "Easier than what? You've given me no baseline here."

Neither of them smiled. Wardens never did, in Pip's experience, though he kept testing. His mum had always said his mouth would get him hanged, and she'd said it with such fondness that he'd never quite believed her until now.

They walked him down a corridor lined with more cages, dozens of them, hundreds, stacked in tiers like a pigeon loft built by someone who hated pigeons. Faces looked out at him from every one. Old faces, young ones. A girl about his own age with frost creeping across her bars from the inside — she pressed her palm flat against the metal as he passed, and where she touched, the copper wire glowed faintly, then dimmed. A man whose shadow moved a half-second behind him, lagging like a bad memory. A child, six maybe seven, asleep, with moths resting on the outside of her cage as though the bars weren't there at all.

"That one's been here longest," the warden holding the tongs said quietly. He wasn't talking to Pip, exactly. He was talking to himself, the way people did when they needed to say a thing out loud to keep from thinking it. "Four years. She doesn't age right, see. None of them do, past a certain point. It keeps them."

"*It*," Pip repeated. "Who's *it*? Is this the crown's work? Whose order—"

"Walk," said the first warden.

But Pip had caught it — the second warden's voice, the crack in it, the way a man says *she* instead of *the prisoner*. And he filed it away, because filing things away was what he did. Rust remembered everything. So did Pip.

At the end of the corridor was a door, and through the door was a room that made him stop breathing for a moment.

It was round, and vast, and its walls were copper. Not panelled in copper — *grown* in it, great branching veins of the stuff spreading from floor to ceiling like the inside of a tree that had dreamed itself into metal. The veins converged overhead and funneled upward into a single thick trunk that disappeared into the rock above, climbing toward the Spire proper, toward the throne room, toward—

"Oh," Pip said softly, because he understood, all at once, the way you understand a joke a beat too late. The roots ran both directions. The cages fed the veins. The veins fed the trunk. The trunk fed whatever sat at the top of the Spire wearing a king's face.

The tongs tightened. The nearest vein brightened.

And Pip felt it leave him.

He'd have said, before this moment, that his gift lived somewhere specific — his hands, maybe, or behind his eyes. But when it drained, it drained from *everywhere*, a slow tide going out of him, and what remained was the shape of himself with the sea gone. He could still hear the metal — that was the cruel trick of it, the roots took the strength of the speaking and left the hearing — but when he reached for the copper and asked it, begged it, *please, loosen, bend, let me out*, it only hummed back at him, fat and drowsy and full, a tick gorged on his own voice.

"There," said the warden, almost tenderly. "First time's the worst. You'll sleep."

"I'll scream," Pip corrected.

"Many do." The warden steered him back toward the corridor, and just before the door shut between them, he added, very fast, very low, words pitched to fall under the other warden's hearing: "Eat what they bring you. All of it. Strength slows the drain. Remember that."

Then the door closed, and Pip stood alone in the ringing dark, holding one sentence like a coal in cupped hands.

* * *

The food arrived twice a day through a slot: bread, hard cheese, a cup of watered wine. Pip ate all of it, every crumb, and discovered the warden was right. Fed, the pull of the roots was a dull ache. Hungry, it was a hand in his chest.

On his third day — he counted meals, four meals, six — the old woman spoke again.

"Dellan," she said. "That's my name. I read thread. Used to. Now I mostly read the roots, which is a miserable occupation, like reading your own eulogy."

"How does it work?" Pip asked. "Really work. Nobody upstairs says anything true."

Dellan snorted. "Nobody upstairs has ever said anything true in my considerable experience. Very well. You know how binding works — the Edict, fifteen years back, everyone's magic tied off and handed over to be 'kept safe.' Kept safe meant kept. Stored. The Spire's been full of it ever since, all that bound power sitting in the vaults like grain in a granary. Only grain rots. This doesn't. It waits. And something up top got hungry."

"The king," Pip whispered.

"The thing that looks like the king," Dellan corrected. "I don't know what it is. Nobody does. But I know its table manners. It eats slowly. That's the part nobody believes until they've seen it. It could drain us dry in a night, I think — empty the whole vault in one gulp and be done. It doesn't. It sips. It keeps us alive because we're not meat, love, we're *orchards*. You don't burn the orchard. You tend it. Prune it. Wait for fruit."

Pip sat with that. Around him, faintly, the copper roots pulsed — slow, patient, rhythmic as breathing. As digestion.

"And the wardens?" he asked. "Do they know?"

"Some know and hate it. Some know and don't care. Some don't know and wouldn't believe it." Dellan's voice dropped. "One of ours was a warden, once. Before me. She says half the guard hasn't slept properly in years. Says they dream of standing in a field while something tall walks the rows. She says the tall thing knows their names."

* * *

It was the moth-girl who changed everything.

Pip learned her name was Wren — no relation to Noor's wren, she said, though she liked the coincidence — and her gift was small and lovely and useless-looking, which was why the hunt had taken her anyway. Moths came to her. Any moths. They landed on her fingers and told her things, or rather she told them things and they carried the news out through cracks and keyholes to whoever else could listen.

Her cage was across the aisle from his, three tiers up. On his eighth day she leaned against her bars and said, conversationally, "You're the rust-boy."

"I'm the rust-boy," Pip agreed.

"Noor Halloran is looking for you."

The name went through him like a struck bell. He gripped the bars. "Noor — the charm-seller? They got out? They're alive?"

"Alive and making trouble below, from what the moths hear. They've fallen in with falconers and rats and a fortune-teller." Wren smiled, and it transformed her face, which hunger and dim light had made gray. "The moths like them. Say they smell honest."

"Can you—" Pip stopped, started again. "Can you send word? Can you tell them where I am?"

Wren's smile faded. She turned her palm up, and a single gray moth settled on it, wings opening and closing like a book deciding whether to speak.

"I've tried," she said. "Every night since they brought you in. The roots eat the message. Anything with magic in it, anything living that carries a spark — the trunk pulls it apart before it clears the vaults." Her jaw set. "But moths aren't much. Barely a spark. And they're stubborn, and stupid, and they fly into flames because they love light more than they fear death." She lifted the moth to her lips and breathed on it, whispering, and Pip watched it climb through the bars — *through* the bars, thin as breath — and spiral up toward the copper trunk, wobbling, drunk, too small and too foolish to be worth eating.

"So we cheat," Wren said.

* * *

The organizing started small, the way rust starts.

It began with Dellan teaching Pip the rhythm of the roots — the feed-times, when the drain deepened and when it eased, the long shallow hours after each meal when the trunk seemed to doze. It continued when Pip discovered, on the fourth feed-day, that he could hear the *other* prisoners' gifts through the copper. Not steal them — touch them. The girl with the frost shivered her bars and the cold traveled the wire to him; the man with the lagging shadow sent a pulse of darkness down the line like a wink. The roots connected everyone. The thing on the throne had built itself a nervous system, and nobody had told it that nervous systems run both ways.

"It's a road," Pip said to Dellan, delighted despite everything, despite the ache in his chest where his gift used to pool. "It's a road and it goes everywhere and it thinks it's theirs."

"Careful," Dellan warned, but her eyes were bright, and Pip noticed that brightness was spreading. Down the tiers, faces turned toward him when he passed. Word traveled cage to cage in whispers and taps and, from Wren, in moth-script traced on palms. The frost-girl — Maren — began freezing her door-hinges brittle at feed-time so the wardens' keys slipped. Someone taught the sleeping child to dream louder, so that moths gathered at her cage in silver drifts and blotted the wardens' lantern-light.

And the warden — the kind one, the one who'd said *eat everything* — began leaving things in the food slot. A bent nail. A scrap of chalk. Once, wrapped in cloth, a file no longer than a finger.

Pip hid the file in the straw and lay awake listening to the roots drink, and for the first time since the blanket and the stairs, he wasn't afraid. He was *furious*, which was better. Furious was fuel.

"They think we're orchards," he murmured to the copper in the dark, and felt it hum, fat and complacent, and smiled at it the way you smile at an enemy who hasn't noticed the knife yet. "Orchards burn, friend. Ask any farmer."

Above him, miles up through stone and vein and trunk, the thing that wore the king went on sipping, patient as winter, certain of its harvest.

It did not hear the nail scraping quietly, cage by cage by cage, along the underside of every bar.

It did not hear Wren's moths, one each night, too small to matter, carrying names upward past its lips like seeds blown over a wall.

And it did not know — how could it know? — that somewhere below Vellamark, in the Tallow Market's smoke and lamplight, a charm-seller with a beast-speaker's throat was already asking the rats the way in.
