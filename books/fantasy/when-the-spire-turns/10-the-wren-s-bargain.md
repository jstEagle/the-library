## 10. The Wren's Bargain

The wren came back on the ninth morning, and she was furious.

Noor knew it before the little bird had cleared the rusted grate at the mouth of Sefa's alcove — knew it in the way her wings snapped shut like a slammed door, in the way she landed on the rim of the chipped teacup that served as her usual perch and immediately began scolding in a torrent of chirps so rapid and so venomous that even Noor, fluent as they were, could only catch every third word.

"Slow down," Noor murmured. Around them, the Tallow Market went about its morning: Brannic hammering a hinge back onto a cupboard with his one hand and his knee, Pip's ghost — no, not a ghost, *absence*, an absence shaped exactly like a boy — nowhere to be seen, and Old Marrow's rats flowing along the gutter-ledge in their endless gray river. "Slowly. From the beginning."

The wren glared at them with one black eye, ruffled herself into a ball of pure indignation, and started over.

Her name was Sorrel, though she hadn't told Noor that for three days after her release from the market cage, because wild birds did not hand out their names to just anyone, thank you very much. She was small and brown and unremarkable, which was precisely why Noor had bought her freedom two years ago from a charm-seller's stall — not for magic, not for luck, but because they could not stand the sound of a songbird singing to fill a cage when the cage was all she had.

And now Sorrel had flown up through the cracks of Vellamark, through the gutters and the bell-towers and the eaves of the Warden's Hall itself, and she carried back what the birds knew.

*The ravens of the palace,* she said, once she'd slowed enough for sense, *know a way into the Spire.*

Noor's hands went still around the luck-knot they'd been braiding. The cord slipped. They let it.

*They've always known,* Sorrel continued, hopping once along the cup's rim. *Ravens know everything. They knew before your king died, if he died, which the pigeons doubt and the starlings argue about endlessly, because starlings will argue about anything.* She paused, and something in her small body shifted — the fury banking down into something more careful. *But knowing is not telling. Ravens do not tell. Ravens trade.*

"Trade what?" Noor asked, though some part of them already dreaded the answer. Birds kept strange currencies. Shiny things, mostly. A button, a ring, a dead beetle of unusual quality. Surely the ravens of the crown's own rookery would want —

*Not shine,* said Sorrel. *The old raven — the one they call Whitebeak, who has lived in the Spire since before the binding, who remembers when the mews smelled of falcons instead of fear — he says:* and here she drew herself up, and her voice took on, unmistakably, the cadence of something repeated verbatim, memorized word for word across four days and forty rooftops, *tell the stall-keeper this: we will show you the way the smoke goes out, but first you must give us a truth. Not a secret — secrets are cheap, everyone has those. A truth. One you have never spoken aloud, not to any ear, not to any god, not even inside your own skull where lies grow comfortable. Speak it in the open air, where the wind can carry it to whoever it pleases, and then we will talk.*

*And,* Sorrel added, in her own voice again, with relish, *he laughed while he said it. A horrible sound. Like a door that needs oiling. I didn't like him.*

Noor sat very still.

Around them the market clattered and murmured. Brannic had stopped hammering — Noor could feel the man's attention land on them like a hand on the shoulder, heavy and patient. He didn't ask. That was the shape of him: he waited until you brought things to him, the way a stone waits for water to go around or over.

"I need to walk," Noor said.

Brannic nodded once and went back to the hinge.

* * *

They walked, and Sorrel rode their shoulder, and the undercity breathed around them.

It was strange, after nine days, how quickly a place became ordinary. The Drowned Stair still made Noor's stomach drop every time — forty steps down through black water that had never been blacker than knee-deep, thank whatever mercies governed drowned stairs — but the Tallow Market itself, with its candle-fat lamps and its stalls of bottled thunder and its fortune-teller whose moths arranged themselves into futures nobody wanted, had become *home* with a speed that frightened Noor more than any warden's hound ever had.

That was the thing about family, Noor was learning. It arrived without asking whether you could afford it.

They climbed the maintenance ladder past the tannery runoff, up through a hatch Sefa had jimmied years ago, and came out behind the cooperage on Tanner's Row, where the smell covered all other smells and the daylight came down thin and gray as dishwater. Vellamark above. The city of the hunt. Noor stood in the cooperage shadow and looked up at the Spire, because everyone in Vellamark looked up at the Spire eventually; it was simply what eyes did here, the way rivers ran downhill.

It rose white and slender out of the palace hill, and it turned. Slowly, imperceptibly, the way the sun moves — you never saw it move, only knew it had. Fifteen years it had been turning, ever since the Binding Edicts, ever since the night the gifted were named criminals and the magics of the city were gathered up like harvest wheat and bound to the crown.

*Bound,* thought Noor, remembering what the rats remembered, remembering the pale procession and the sleeping king carried down and down. *Bound to something that eats.*

"You're going to have to say it eventually," Sorrel said. She had hopped down to the top of a rain barrel and was preening with aggressive casualness. "Whatever it is."

"No one asked you."

"He asked. I'm asking louder." She cocked her head. "You have the face. I've known you two years; I know your faces. That's your *I would rather be flayed* face."

"It's my thinking face."

"Your thinking face has hope in it. This one doesn't."

Noor almost smiled. Almost. "What if the truth is worse than useless? What if I speak it and it costs everything and buys nothing, and the ravens laugh their horrible oiled-door laugh and keep their way in?"

"Then you'll have said a true thing in the open air," Sorrel said, "and the world will be one true thing heavier, and you'll still have to find another way into the Spire, and Pip will still be in a cage, and you'll still be you." She smoothed a wing feather with great precision. "Birds don't trade in might-have-beens. Too heavy for flying."

From a wren, it landed like philosophy from a mountain. Noor looked at her for a long moment.

"When did you get wise?"

"I was always wise. You only listened when I got useful." But she hopped closer, and her voice gentled into something that was almost tender, in the prickly way of small fierce creatures. "Whatever it is, stall-keeper — you've been carrying it nine days now, and I watched you carry it, and it sits wrong on you. Truths are like that. They're meant to be light once they're out. It's only inside that they turn to stone."

Noor looked up at the Spire again. Thought of Pip, small and sharp-mouthed, who spoke to rust and could make a lock weep open, sitting somewhere in the dark beneath that turning white stone with copper roots growing toward him. Thought of the confession waiting in their chest like a swallowed hook.

"All right," Noor said quietly. "Where?"

"The rookery ledge, sunset. He'll come to hear. He's bringing witnesses — ravens always bring witnesses, it makes the trade feel official." Sorrel lifted off, circled once, and settled again. "And stall-keeper? Whatever it is — the wind carries it where it pleases. He said that too. So say it like you mean to let it go."

* * *

Sefa found them an hour later, sitting on the edge of the flood-tunnel mouth with their feet dangling over the black water, skipping stones badly.

She sat down beside them without asking, the way she did everything. Sefa was a compact woman built like a knot in wood, with moth-wing dust permanently silvering her knuckles, and she had the gift of reading futures in the flight of insects, which meant she spent most of her life being right about things people wished she weren't.

"Sorrel told Marrow," she said. "Marrow told me. Rats have no discretion whatsoever; it's their worst quality."

"So you know."

"I know there's a price." She picked up a stone, weighed it, skipped it — four clean hops, showing off. "I don't know what it is. Nobody does. Not even you yet, maybe."

"That's the trouble," Noor said. "I do know. I've always known. That's rather the whole point."

Sefa waited. She was good at waiting. Moth-reading taught patience; a future took its time arranging itself.

"I used to think," Noor said finally, "that the hardest part of hiding was the fear. The wardens, the edicts, the silver-for-names. But it wasn't. The hardest part was that I got *good* at it. Hiding. I got so good that the hiding grew over the rest of me, like bark over a wound, and now I don't always know where the hiding stops." They threw a stone. It sank straight down, no skips at all. "And now a bird is asking me to peel it off in public."

"Moths do that," Sefa said. "When they emerge from the cocoon. There's a moment where the shell splits and they have to decide — well. They don't decide. But there's a moment where everything that kept them alive in the dark becomes the thing that kills them if they keep holding on." She glanced sideways. "I'm saying you're allowed to find it terrible. Terrible and necessary can live in the same hour."

"Do you want to know what it is?"

"No." And when Noor looked at her, startled: "Not unless you want me to. That's the whole difference, Noor. A secret is taken. A truth is given. If you give it to me first, in the dark, then the ravens get nothing new — and worse, *you* get nothing new, because you'll have practiced it, and practiced truths are half-lies." She stood, brushed stone dust from her trousers, and offered a hand up. Her moth-silvered fingers were warm. "Say it to the air first. We'll all catch it after. That's what family is for — being downwind."

* * *

Sunset came to Vellamark the color of old copper, and the rookery ledge on the cooperage roof filled with shadows that were not shadows.

There were seven of them. Seven ravens, arriving out of the west one by one, folding themselves onto the ridge tiles like pieces of the evening coming home. Six kept their distance, silent and watchful, but the seventh — huge, scarred, with a beak gone gray-white at the tip like a blade worn from honing — stalked forward along the ledge with the gait of an old soldier reviewing troops.

Whitebeak.

*Stall-keeper,* he said, and his voice was exactly as Sorrel had promised: a door that needed oiling, dragged open on a century of disuse. *You brought the little loud one. Good. She negotiates terribly but she listens well.*

"She says you're a horror," Noor said.

*I contain multitudes.* He settled, tucked his feet, and fixed Noor with an eye like wet obsidian. Behind him, the six watchers did not move, and the sun went on dying, and the whole city held its breath below. *You want the way the smoke goes out. The old flue from the crown-room runs up through the Spire's spine and opens above the mews — wide enough for a slim person, unwatched, because no one watches a chimney. We ravens have flown it for fifteen years. We have seen what sits at the bottom of it. We will draw you the map in the way ravens draw — by flying it beside you, branch by branch, stone by stone, until your memory is our memory.*

"And your price—"

*A truth.* His head tilted, that horrible mechanical tilt. *Spoken aloud. In the open air. Never before spoken. Do not insult us by rehearsing it — we will know. Ravens taste lies the way you taste salt.* He leaned closer, and the last light caught the pale of his beak. *Understand what you buy, stall-keeper. The way in is real. But truths are not payment the way coin is payment. Coin leaves your pocket. Truth leaves your skin. What you speak tonight, the wind owns forever. Are you prepared to be owned a little?*

Noor's heart was going like a trapped bird of its own. Below them the Tallow Market's lamps were lighting one by one, and somewhere down there Brannic was pretending to fix a cupboard, and Sefa was watching the sky for omens, and Old Marrow's river of rats had paused in its running, hundreds of small ears turned upward. Family, downwind, catching.

They thought of the hawk.

Fifteen years ago — eleven days before the Binding Edicts fell, eleven days before the world changed — Noor had been seventeen and invisible, minding their stall at the autumn market, when the royal procession passed through Vellamark. The old queen's hunting hawk had been on the queen's own gauntlet, and it was dying. Noor had seen it — seen the tremor in the great bird's talons, the film coming over the golden eye — and the hawk had seen Noor seeing, because beasts always knew, and it had spoken. Quietly. Only to them.

*Tell her,* the hawk had said. *Tell the queen I am ready, and tell her the king is not the king. Tell her the thing beside her wears his face the way a glove wears a hand, and she must look at the left eye, the left eye is wrong, the left eye does not blink when it should — tell her, child, tell her before the moon turns.*

And seventeen-year-old Noor Halloran, standing at a market stall with their heart hammering, had looked at the most powerful woman in the realm, at the guards, at the crowd, at the world that outlawed what they were — and had said nothing. Had bowed their head like everyone else. The procession moved on. Eleven days later the Binding Edicts fell, the queen vanished from history between one proclamation and the next, and Noor told themselves for fifteen years that they'd been afraid, that anyone would have been afraid, that silence was survival—

But that wasn't the truth. The truth was smaller and colder. The truth was that for one long moment, the hawk's dying golden eye had held theirs, and Noor had understood exactly what was being asked, and some quiet cowardly voice inside had whispered: *not me. Someone else will say it. Let someone else carry it.*

No one else could. That was the whole horror of gifts. No one else could.

The queen died — or disappeared — and the thing wearing the king's face bound the city's magic and began to eat, and every year of the hunt, every cage, every stolen child, had grown from the seed of one silent moment at a market stall.

"I killed the queen," Noor said aloud.

Their voice cracked across the rooftop, across the open air, and the wind took it — south, toward the river, toward wherever wind pleases. Somewhere below, Noor heard Sefa's sharp intake of breath. Heard Brannic's hammer stop.

"Not with a knife. With silence. The old queen's hawk came to me dying and told me the king was false — told me how to see it, the left eye, the left eye doesn't blink — and begged me to warn her, and I was seventeen and terrified and I thought, let someone else say it, someone braver, and I bowed my head and said nothing, and eleven days later the edicts fell and she was gone and the eating began." Their throat was raw. They kept going, because the truth, once split open, poured. "Every cage in the Spire grew out of that silence. Every child taken. Pip. All of it. I have known for fifteen years that I could have — that maybe — and I have never said it, not to any god, not inside my own skull, because inside my own skull I called it fear and forgave myself, and it wasn't fear. Fear would have been honest. It was cowardice wearing fear's coat. That is my truth. Take it. It's yours. It was always too heavy for me."

Silence on the rooftop. The last sliver of sun went down. The seven ravens sat unmoving against the deepening violet, and Noor stood in the wreckage of their own words, lighter and more naked than they had ever felt, waiting to learn whether they had paid for a chimney with their soul.

Whitebeak unfolded himself, slowly, joint by joint, and walked the length of the ledge until he stood close enough that Noor could feel the cold radiating off his feathers.

*Fifteen years ago,* he said, *I was in the mews when the hawk did not return from that progress. I heard the falconers wonder at it. I have wondered for fifteen years what happened to her, and to whom she spoke.* The obsidian eye held Noor's. *She chose well, in the end. Late — but the dying are permitted lateness, and so are the cowardly, if they spend what's left of their courage well.* He spread his wings, enormous against the first stars. *The trade is made. At the new moon, we fly you the smoke-way. Bring your falconer. Bring your courage. And stall-keeper—*

He paused, head cocked, and for one moment the oiled-door voice softened into something almost kind.

*The left eye still does not blink. We checked.*

Then seven ravens went up off the roof together, a single black exhalation against the dusk, and were gone toward the Spire — and Noor stood alone on the ledge, hollowed and strangely whole, hearing footsteps on the ladder behind them and knowing, before a single word was spoken, that everything had changed.

Because the voice that reached them first, rough and low and shaking slightly at the edges, belonged to Brannic — and Brannic said:

"The left eye. *Gods above.* Fifteen years I served that man, and the left eye—" He pulled himself up over the parapet, one-armed, face gray as ash, and behind him Sefa, and behind Sefa the whole downwind family with the truth caught in their hands like something fragile and burning. "Lad. Lass. *Noor.* Sit down. Because if the hawk spoke true, then I have something to tell you about the night my king stopped being my king — and you are going to wish, as I have wished for fifteen years, that someone had said it sooner."
