## 11. Fifteen Years of Silence

The confession did not begin that night. It began, as most terrible things do, with a kettle.

Sefa had hung it over the brazier in the corner of Brannic's room — the largest space in the Tallow Market, which meant it was large enough for a cot, a workbench, and four people to be uncomfortable together, which apparently was the definition of family Noor had been living inside for a fortnight without noticing. Pip sat cross-legged on the floor, feeding scraps of copper wire to a little construct he'd built from a broken music box, and the construct clicked and shivered at him like an adoring dog. Old Marrow had arranged herself on the windowsill, which was to say she'd claimed the one warm spot where the brick held the day's heat, her grey bulk rising and falling like something geological.

"So," Sefa said, pouring. "Tomorrow we go up."

"Tomorrow we go up," Brannic agreed. He said it the way he said everything now — flat, steady, a man setting stones into a wall. His empty sleeve was pinned neatly at the shoulder. He'd taken to wearing his good coat for these planning evenings, as if the dead king deserved his falconer at his best.

Noor wrapped both hands around the tin cup and didn't drink. The tea was terrible. It was always terrible; Sefa bought her leaves from a woman who read futures in moth wings and possibly also cut them with dock weed. But holding the cup gave Noor's hands something honest to do, and tomorrow they would climb through the mews of the Spire itself, into the place where Vellamark's magic went to die, and there was a thing in Noor's chest that had been sitting there since the ravens made their bargain, heavy as swallowed river-stone.

*Tell them,* said a small voice that sounded annoyingly like the wren's.

*Not yet.*

*You told me you never lie to birds.*

*That's different. Birds forgive easily. They have short memories and long grudges and no time for shame.*

The wren, roosting in the rafters where it had lived ever since flying back to Noor with raven words in its tiny furious body, made a sound that was unmistakably a scoff.

"Noor." Sefa's voice, gentle. She had that way of saying a name so it landed soft and still couldn't be dodged. "Your cup's rattling."

Noor looked down. The tea was trembling in rings. So were their hands.

"I—" The word came out and stuck. Noor cleared their throat. "I need to say something before tomorrow. In case. In case tomorrow goes badly."

"It won't," said Pip, with the magnificent confidence of a fourteen-year-old.

"It might," said Marrow, from the sill, "and I'd rather hear whatever this is while my teeth are still in my head. Speak, cub."

So Noor spoke. Or tried to. What came out first was the smallest true thing they could find.

"You all think I ran because of the hunt. The proclamation. Silver for names, wardens at the market, all of it." They turned the cup in their hands. "That's true. That's part of it. But I've been running longer than fifteen days. I've been running fifteen years."

The construct clicked. Pip stilled it with one finger without looking away from Noor.

"Fifteen years ago I was eleven," Noor said. "My mother kept a stall two rows over from where mine is now. Charms and knots and blessings, same trade, except hers actually worked, because she was gifted too, and she taught me before she taught me anything else: *never let them hear you.* We were careful. We were so careful. And then she died — fever, nothing dramatic, just fever, half the market took it that winter — and I kept the stall because it was hers and because keeping it meant I wasn't alone. And I kept being careful."

They drank, finally. Dock weed and all.

"The old queen — the real queen, King Aldous's mother — she kept hawks. This was before everything changed. Before the binding laws, even. She had a mews on the Spire's west face, and once a year the falconers brought birds down to the market fair, on the leash, for show. I was eleven. There was a gyrfalcon. White as paper, old, half-blind in one eye. She was dying — I could feel it from three stalls away, that thin cold thread a dying animal puts out, like a hand reaching in the dark. And she reached for *me*. Because I was the only one in that whole square who could catch it."

Noor stopped. The room waited. Even the construct had gone still, a small copper heart ticking.

"She spoke to me. Not words, not like you or Pip talk. More like — weather. Like pressure. She showed me the mews, and the queen's hand on the jesses, and she showed me—" Noor's voice cracked, and they hated it, and went on anyway. "She showed me the king. Her mate's son. Aldous. She showed him *sick*. Not fever-sick. Wrong-sick. Something standing behind his eyes that hadn't been there before. She was screaming it at me, the only way she could, and I understood every feather of it, and I was eleven years old and terrified and my mother was six months dead, and do you know what I did?"

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

"I nodded," Noor said. "I nodded at a dying hawk, and I went back to my stall, and I sold luck-knots for the rest of the afternoon. And then the binding laws came, and the criers came, and the years came, one after another, and I told myself the hawk had been confused, animals get confused, maybe it was illness distorting things, maybe the king really was just sick and got better. Fifteen years I told myself that story. And then the rats tell us about a pale procession carrying a sleeping king down into the Spire, and Brannic tells us what he saw in the crown room, and every single day since, that gyrfalcon has been in my head asking me the same question."

"What question?" Pip asked quietly.

"*Why didn't you say?*" Noor looked around the room, at all of them, and found it strangely easier than looking at the tea. "If I'd spoken. If I'd told anyone — a priest, a guard, a fishwife, anyone — maybe none of it happens. Maybe the binding never passes. Maybe Pip isn't in a cage right now. Maybe the city isn't — isn't being *eaten*, slowly, politely, by something wearing a dead man's face. I had the one warning anyone ever got, delivered straight into my hands by a dying queen's own bird, and I swallowed it. I'm not brave. I never was. I'm a coward who got lucky enough to meet people who aren't."

Silence. The bad tea steamed. Somewhere above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, Vellamark went about its evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the bell of the Chantry of Saint Vered ringing the hour, all of it unknowing, all of it riding on what happened tomorrow.

It was Marrow who moved first. She poured herself down off the sill with the creaking dignity of an old ship coming about, crossed the floor, and climbed — with visible effort and zero permission — into Noor's lap, where she settled into a warm, immovable loaf.

"Cub," she said. "Do you know how old I am?"

"Older than sin and twice as smug?"

"Older than the Spire's foundations. I remember when they dug the drowned stair, and I remember when they sealed it, and I remember the men who did the sealing, and what I ate afterward." A ripple passed through her whiskers that was, in Marrow, laughter. "I have watched humans for three hundred years, and I will tell you the single most common thing they are. Not cruel. Clever is second. *Afraid* is first. Afraid, and carrying some small dead weight they whisper to in the dark. You are not special, Halloran. You are merely honest tonight, which among your kind is nearly miraculous."

"That's the least comforting comfort I've ever heard," Noor said, scratching her ears despite themselves.

"Comfort is for kits. Truth is for soldiers, and tomorrow we march." She butted her head against Noor's chin. "Besides. The hawk chose *you*. Out of every beast-speaker in that square — and there were others, I knew their names, I ate their crumbs — she chose the child who would understand her terror best. Fear speaks fear's language. She wasn't asking for courage. She was asking for witness. And you have witnessed, cub. For fifteen years, you have carried it. That is not nothing. That is the whole weight, carried alone."

Noor's eyes burned. They looked up and found Brannic watching them with an expression Noor had never seen on him — not the flat falconer calm, but something rawer underneath it, like a man looking at his own reflection in a window.

"Say it," Noor said. "Whatever you're thinking."

Brannic set down his cup. "The night the king changed, I stood in the mews and felt every bird on the Spire go silent at once. Every one. Like a held breath. And I thought — *that's wrong, that's wrong, that's wrong* — and then the crown's men came to me the next morning with new orders and new wages, and I thought about my sister's children, and I took the orders." His jaw worked. "I lost the arm later, for lesser crimes. I've hated myself fifteen years for taking them. So when you say coward, Halloran, understand you're talking to the guildmaster. Whole guild, one member, meetings nightly."

A wet, surprised laugh escaped Noor before they could stop it.

"And here's the thing I learned," Brannic went on, leaning forward, firelight catching the grey in his beard, "the thing it took me fifteen years and one arm to learn. Guilt is a coin the past keeps minting, and you can pay with it forever and buy nothing. Tomorrow night we don't pay. Tomorrow we spend. We take that guilt and that grief and that stupid, useless fifteen-year silence, and we *spend* it, all at once, on the door of that vault. Understood?"

"Understood," Noor said.

"Not quite yet." Sefa had been quiet through all of it, turning her cup, and now she set it down and reached over and took Noor's free hand. Her palms were always faintly dusty, moth-scale and chalk. "You've confessed. Now hear the verdict. As the resident reader of omens, I rule as follows: the gyrfalcon told an eleven-year-old child an unbearable truth, and the child survived it the only way children survive unbearable truths, which is to bury it deep and plant flowers over the grave. That is not cowardice. That is childhood. The crime belongs to the thing in the crown, and tomorrow it collects its debts. Case closed."

"Seconded," said Pip, raising his teacup like a tiny lord. "Motion carries. Also if you're a coward then I'm a rust-licker, and I am definitely a rust-licker, but that's beside the point."

"Thirded," said Marrow, from Noor's lap, already half asleep. "Now stop shaking the tea. You're spilling my warmth."

And that was the whole trial, Noor thought dazedly. That was the entire weight of fifteen years, and they'd braced for judgment and gotten a cat in their lap and a toast from a boy who talked to rust. The river-stone in their chest hadn't vanished — some things don't, some things shouldn't — but it had changed shape somehow. It was no longer a stone. It was ballast. It was the thing that would keep them steady when the mews went dark and the ravens started whispering and the whole rotten tower of Vellamark leaned over them, waiting to see what they'd do.

Grief, spent, becomes fuel. Sefa would have said it more poetically. Marrow would have said it with more whiskers. But Noor felt it settle into them like a coal into a brazier, and they thought: *gyrfalcon, old white sister, wherever you are — tomorrow I carry it up for both of us.*

The wren dropped from the rafters and landed on Noor's shoulder, and pressed its small hot forehead against their cheek, which in wren is either affection or a demand for suet, and Noor chose to believe it was both.

They stayed up late after that, the four of them and the rat-matriarch, not planning anymore, just talking — Pip demonstrating how the construct could now sort buttons, Sefa telling the story of the time she misread a death omen that turned out to be a very fat moth, Marrow narrating the fall of a minor dynasty purely for the drama. Warmth, banked like a fire meant to last till morning. Noor fell asleep in the chair with the wren in their collar and Marrow on their knees and woke once in the dark to find Brannic awake across the room, oiling the lockpicks, and neither of them said anything, because some vigils are shared better in silence.

Dawn came grey through the high vent slit.

Noor woke, stiff-necked, and found the room already stirring — Sefa folding her moth-cloths, Pip coaxing the construct apart into travel-size pieces, Marrow washing a paw with grim ceremony. On the table, someone had laid out Noor's satchel, packed, and atop it, weighted under a smooth white stone, a folded scrap of paper in Sefa's looping hand:

*For the gyrfalcon. Debts collected at dawn. — the family*

Noor picked up the stone. It was old, river-worn, and warm, though nothing had warmed it.

At the mouth of the drowned stair, the ravens were already waiting, black shapes against the grey, and the eldest of them croaked out the first word of the way in.

* * *

Above them, in the crown room of the Spire, something ancient opened one borrowed eye, and smiled at the taste of approaching names.
