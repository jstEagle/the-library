## 12. Ink and Copper

Brannic had not held a quill in fifteen years. His hand remembered the weight of it anyway — the same hand that had once inked the king's own hunting ledgers in a script so fine the clerks teased him about it — and it shook now not from age but from what he was about to write down.

Treason, in his own neat capitals, on the back of a tallow-crate lid.

"You're pressing too hard," Sefa said from her corner, where she sat with a saucer of honeyed milk and a patience that had outlasted three floods and one minor collapse of the ceiling. "You'll snap the nib."

"It's not the nib I'm worried about snapping." He blew on the ink — lamp-black and goose-fat, the undercity's answer to proper iron-gall — and turned the lid so the lamplight caught the lines he'd drawn. "There. The Spire, as I knew it. As the king's falconer knew it, which is to say: better than the architects knew it, because architects build for kings and falconers have to live inside what they've built."

Pip leaned over his shoulder, rust-brown hair falling into his eyes, and made a noise of profound skepticism. "That's a lot of stairs."

"That's a lot of stairs," Brannic agreed. "Two hundred and six from the mews to the crown-room, if you take the falconer's stair, which you will, because every other stair in that building has a warden posted at its foot and another at its head, and wardens are famously bad at being bribed since they started paying them in something other than coin."

Old Marrow uncurled from the warm stone near the brazier, whiskers twitching, and regarded the map with the flat appraisal of a creature who had personally inspected every sewer in Vellamark and found most of them wanting. "Your drawing is crooked," she said.

"My drawing is *accurate*. The Spire itself is crooked. It settled into the marsh two hundred years ago and nobody had the courage to tell the masons."

"The rats noticed."

"The rats notice everything," Brannic said, and meant it as a compliment, and Marrow accepted it as one, which was more diplomacy than he'd managed with any courtier in his former life.

He set the quill down and rubbed his remaining hand across his face. Fifteen years. He had spent fifteen years keeping this knowledge folded up small and flat, like a letter he was afraid to open, and now here he was spreading it out under lamplight for a beast-speaker, a moth-reader, a boy who talked to rust, and a rat matriarch old enough to remember the plague years. If someone had told him, back when he'd worn the royal livery, that his oath would end like this — sworn anew to a conspiracy of small creatures and kind exiles — he would have laughed himself hoarse.

He would also, he suspected, have been relieved.

* * *

The ravens' map arrived an hour past midnight, which was exactly the sort of theatrical timing Brannic had come to expect from corvids.

It came by way of the wren — Noor's wren, the little brown scrap of feathers who had once lived in a market cage and now conducted herself with the self-importance of a general — who flew down through the flood-grate with a strip of bark clamped in her beak and dropped it onto the crate-lid map with a *clack* of finality.

"From the rookery," the wren announced, hopping twice. "The pale-eye says: count the eyes. The pale-eye says: the copper sleeps at moonset. The pale-eye says—" she paused, ruffled, clearly savoring it— "the pale-eye says the fat man still owes him a shinbone."

"I do not owe him a shinbone," Brannic said. "I owe him a *grudge*, which is entirely different, and entirely mutual, and will be settled after we've saved the city."

"Ravens don't distinguish," Marrow observed dryly.

Noor picked up the bark and turned it toward the light. Bark-scoring was raven shorthand — lines and dots and slashes that looked like weather to human eyes. Noor couldn't read it either, but the wren could translate, and between the wren's recitation and Brannic's memory, the picture assembled itself piece by piece.

"Here." Noor's finger traced the crate-lid. "The mews. North face, third tier. He's marked it with three dots — three sentries?"

"Two, when I served." Brannic frowned. "Three means they've reinforced. Or it means the ravens are padding their intelligence to seem impressive. With ravens it's genuinely difficult to say."

"He wouldn't pad it," Noor said quietly. "Not for this."

Brannic glanced at them. There was something in Noor's face when they spoke of the birds — a trust so complete it looked almost like grief. He understood it, or he was beginning to. When your whole life you'd only been able to talk to beasts in whispers, and then suddenly the whole city's creatures were leaning in to listen, well. That would reorganize a person.

"Then three sentries," he said, and drew a third dot, and hated how final it looked.

* * *

Sefa read the moths at the hour before dawn, because that was when the veil was thinnest and the moths were drunkest on lamplight and most willing to gossip.

Her method, as far as Brannic could tell, involved a shallow dish of salt water, a candle, and an attitude of ferocious concentration while gray-brown flutterers landed on the rim and fanned their wings. She read the dust patterns. She read the wing-angles. She read, once, memorably, a moth that had flown directly into Pip's ear, which she declared to be an omen of "unexpected messages" and Pip declared to be "an assault."

Tonight she was quiet for a long time, and that quiet gathered everyone around her like moths themselves.

"Well?" Brannic asked finally.

"There are two futures," Sefa said. Her voice had the careful flatness she used when she didn't want to frighten anyone, which always frightened everyone. "The moths are very clear about that. They're just not clear about which one lands."

"Mothers," Marrow muttered, "are famously indecisive."

"The first omen." Sefa touched the left side of the dish, where the wing-dust had settled in long clean streaks. "Ascension. A climbing shape. Someone goes up through the dark and comes out above the clouds, and the dawn finds them standing, and the city wakes free. Strong wings. A good flight."

A small, involuntary sound went around the circle — hope, raw and unguarded. Brannic felt it pull at his own chest like a hook.

"And the second?" Noor asked.

Sefa touched the right side of the dish. Here the dust had fallen in a spiral, tight and descending, like water going down a drain. "Descent. Something falls. Wings folded too early. The dark keeps what it catches." She looked up, and her eyes were wet, which she would deny later with great vigor. "The moths won't tell me which future belongs to whom. They never do. They're cowards, bless them. They only ever show the shape of the day, never the name inside it."

Silence. The candle guttered. Somewhere in the tunnels, water dripped its patient count.

"Both can be true," Noor said at last.

Everyone looked at them.

"Someone climbs and stands in the dawn," Noor said slowly, working it through the way they worked through everything, hands moving as if shaping the thought out of clay. "And something falls. Maybe both things happen to the same person. Maybe—" their voice caught, steadied— "maybe the falling is how the climbing happens. You go up through the dark. Things fall away on the way. Fear. Silence. Old names."

"That's very poetic," Pip said, "and also completely useless for tactical planning."

"Pip," Brannic said mildly.

"No, he's right," Noor said, and there was a ghost of a smile on their face, tired but real. "Poetry doesn't get us past three sentries."

* * *

They planned until the candle burned to a puddle and Sefa lit a second one without comment.

Brannic walked them through it the way he'd once drilled new falconers: slow, repetitive, merciless. The mews first — the rookery above it, where the ravens roosted in their hundreds and the wardens had learned, generations ago, not to climb into a structure full of birds that could remember faces and hold grudges. From the mews, the falconer's stair: narrow, unlit, built for men carrying jesses and hoods and injured birds, which meant built wide enough for a person and steep enough to discourage casual use. Two hundred and six steps. Every twelfth step, a landing with a murder-hole overhead, because the old kings had trusted no one, least of all their servants.

"At each landing you stop and listen," Brannic said. "If you hear breathing above you, you press into the alcove and you wait. Wardens rotate on the quarter-hour. I know because I used to be the one they rotated, and I resented it, and I counted."

"You resent a lot of things," Pip observed.

"Yes. It's kept me alive."

Noor traced the route again, lips moving, committing it. Then: "The dogs."

Ah. There it was. Brannic had been waiting for it.

"The vault levels are patrolled with hounds," he said. "Bred in the kennels below the barracks, raised on the hunt-command, taught that gifted-flesh smells like prey." He met Noor's eyes. "They're kin to you. And the last time they scented you, they hesitated. Sefa told me. All of them, hesitating at once, like a rope going slack."

"They were confused," Noor said. "They'd been given an order that tasted wrong."

"So what happens tomorrow night, when the order tastes wrong and you're standing close enough to speak?"

Noor was quiet a moment. "I don't command them," they said finally. "I don't think I could, even if I wanted to. But I can promise. Dogs understand promises. *Stay your teeth and no harm comes to you. Stand aside and your handler never knows.*" A pause. "Whether they believe me is their choice. That's what makes it worth asking."

Brannic looked at this person — this stall-keeper, this whisperer, this exile of three weeks' standing — and felt something in his chest turn over like soil in spring. In the old days, in the king's service, power had meant command. *Do this. Go there. Kill that.* He had spent his whole career passing orders down a chain, and the chain had ended, he now knew, in a thing wearing a dead man's crown, drinking the city through copper roots.

And here was Noor Halloran, planning an infiltration armed with nothing but promises, and offering even their enemies the dignity of consent.

"That'll work," he said gruffly, because his throat was doing something inconvenient. "Or it'll fail beautifully. Either way it's better tactics than anything the wardens have."

* * *

Near the end, when the plan had been said aloud three times and Marrow had assigned her rats to every drain-mouth and Pip had promised — with unsettling relish — to make the Spire's copper gates "very sorry," Sefa caught Brannic's sleeve as the others drifted toward their bedrolls.

"A word, falconer."

They stepped into the alcove by the flood-grate, where the air moved and the lamplight didn't reach. Sefa was holding something cupped in both hands. She opened them carefully: a single moth, pale as bone, sitting on her palm with its wings folded, unnaturally calm.

"This one came at moonset," she said. "Didn't land on the dish. Landed on *me*. Sat on my knuckle while I read the others, and it never fanned its wings once. Do you know what that means?"

"Tell me."

"It means it wasn't there for an omen. It was there for a message." She lifted her hand toward the faint gray light of the grate. "Moths don't carry messages, Brannic. Except when something bigger wants to send one and doesn't want to be seen sending it. This one's been fed on Spire-light. I can taste the copper in its dust."

Cold walked up Brannic's spine, the old cold, the cold of the night he'd lost his arm and his king in the same hour. "What's the message?"

Sefa's mouth was a thin line. "It's watching us. It's been watching us. Whatever sits on that throne knows we're coming — or it *wants* us to come, which is worse." She tipped her hand toward the grate, and the pale moth lifted off, silent, and flew up into the dark like a fleck of ash rising against gravity.

For a long moment neither of them spoke.

"We could turn back," Sefa said quietly. "Scatter. Live. The Tallow Market survived fifteen years underground; it can survive fifteen more."

Brannic thought of the king — the real one, hollowed and dreaming in his cage of copper, the man whose falcons Brannic had flown, whose laughter he could still hear if he let himself, which he mostly didn't. He thought of Pip behind bars somewhere in those vaults. He thought of Noor, asleep now by the brazier with the wren tucked against their collarbone like a second heartbeat.

"No," he said. "We go. But we go knowing it knows. That changes the arithmetic."

"How?"

"It means," Brannic said, turning back toward the warm circle of lamplight where his strange, impossible family lay sleeping, "that tomorrow night, when we climb those two hundred and six steps, we aren't sneaking in."

He picked up the quill one last time and added a single line beneath the map, in his old clerk-perfect hand:

*It expects us. Good. Let it learn what it invited.*

Above them, far up through the drowned dark, the Spire stood against the last stars — and Brannic would have sworn, though he'd have denied it in daylight, that he saw it move. Just slightly. Just once.

Like something turning over in its sleep.
