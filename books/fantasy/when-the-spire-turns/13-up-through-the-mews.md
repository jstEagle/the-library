## 13. Up Through the Mews

**Noor**

The ravens came for them an hour before dawn, which Noor thought was very rude of them, since dawn was arguably the one part of the day that hadn't yet done anything wrong.

They arrived at Sefa's moth-lantern in ones and twos, dropping out of the dark like small black judges, and settled along the beam above Brannic's cot in a row so solemn it looked rehearsed. Which, Noor supposed, it had been.

"They've been arguing about who leads," Pip had told them earlier, before he was taken, back when the worst thing in Noor's life was a bruised rib. "Corvus the Third insists on seniority. The young one — the one missing a tail feather — says seniority means nothing if you can't remember the route."

Noor looked up at the row of birds now. The one without a tail feather sat slightly apart from the others, at the end of the beam, radiating the particular smugness of a raven who has won a vote.

"That's her," Noor said quietly.

Brannic grunted from where he stood by the wall, testing the give of his old falconer's glove, which he'd pulled on over his single hand like armor going to war. "Her name's Quill. She was mine, before." His voice did the thing it always did when he talked about *before* — went flat and careful, like a man walking over ice. "She'll remember me or she won't."

Quill cocked her head at him with one glittering eye, then deliberately turned her back.

"Ah," said Brannic.

"She remembers you," Noor translated. "She's furious about it."

"Fifteen years," Brannic muttered. "You leave a bird fifteen years and she holds a grudge."

"You left a bird fifteen years," Noor agreed, "and she holds a grudge. I'm beginning to think you two deserve each other."

It was the kind of joke that would have gotten Noor a cuff round the ear in another life, but here it got them something better: the ghost of a smile on Brannic's scarred face, there and gone like a fish surfacing. Sefa pressed a bundle into Noor's arms — bread, a flask, a strip of dried apple that Pip had salted as a prank and which no one had the heart to throw away — and Old Marrow, who had insisted on coming as far as the flood-tunnel mouth despite her age, touched Noor's ankle with one grey paw.

*Up is easier than down,* she signed against their boot leather, her whiskers brushing skin. *Gravity helps. Fear doesn't. Feed the fear small lies until it sleeps.*

"And what do I feed it if it wakes up?" Noor asked.

*Apples,* said Marrow. *Everything likes apples.*

Then there was nothing left to do but climb.

* * *

**Brannic**

Brannic had climbed the mews stair ten thousand times in the king's service, and every one of those times he had been climbing toward something good: a morning's flying, a hawk's weight on his wrist, a king who laughed too loud and loved his birds more than his courtiers. Climbing it now, in the dark, toward a vault full of cages, felt like walking backward through his own life.

The rookery occupied the Spire's north face, floors six through nine, built in the old style — open galleries, louvered shutters, perches worn smooth by generations of royal birds. The wardens had repurposed it after the binding. They used it now as a barracks for the night watch, which meant the ravens' home was full of sleeping men, which meant every rung of the ladder was a held breath.

Quill flew ahead, silent as spilled ink. Twice she paused on a bracket and looked back, and twice Brannic felt the old language move between them without words: *clear, come; wait, still.* Fifteen years, and her grammar hadn't changed. His hands remembered it too. Hands were loyal that way. Hands kept faith long after hearts had learned better sense.

At the sixth-floor gallery he froze, because below them, through the slats, came the sound of snoring — three men in the loft where the mews-keeper's stores used to be. One of them shifted in his sleep and murmured something that might have been a name, or a prayer, or both.

Brannic looked down at Noor, a pale smudge in the dark beneath him, and saw them doing the thing they always did when frightened: listening. Head tipped, eyes half-closed, ears open to a world most people walked through deaf.

*Three sleepers,* Noor breathed, barely sound at all. *One dog on the landing above. It knows we're here already. It's deciding.*

"Deciding," Brannic whispered back. "Dogs don't decide."

"Yours didn't," Noor said. "Mine do."

He had no answer to that. He was beginning to suspect no one in Vellamark did.

* * *

**Noor**

The dog on the eighth-floor landing was called Biscuit.

This information arrived unbidden, the way all the best information did, riding in on the animal's own scent-memory as Noor drew near: a kitchen, years ago, a cook with flour on her arms saying *Biscuit, off the table*, and the puppy who had been named for the crime rather than punished for it. Biscuit was five now. Biscuit had been a warden's hound since she was a year old and had never once been told she was good at anything except being frightening.

She stood at the top of the ladder in the pitch dark, and Noor felt her the way you feel a hand hovering near your shoulder — not touching, but present. Her heart was a drum. Her teeth were an argument waiting to happen.

*Stop,* Brannic signed from below, and began to reach for something at his belt — a knife, a weighted cord, some falconer's trick for quiet endings.

"No," Noor breathed.

"It's a warden's hound."

"It's Biscuit."

And because there wasn't time to explain, and because explaining had never once made an animal behave differently, Noor did the only thing they knew how to do. They opened the door in themselves that they had spent fifteen years keeping locked, and spoke.

Not a command. Commands were what the bound magics did, the crown-magic, the copper-root magic: *sit, stay, hunt, die*. Noor had never owned a word like that and hoped to die without learning one. What they gave Biscuit instead was a promise, shaped in the wordless grammar that lived between a speaker and a beast:

*I am not your enemy tonight. If you let us pass, I swear you will eat better than you have ever eaten, sleep somewhere warm, and be told you are good — and mean it — before the moon turns again.*

The silence stretched so long that Noor heard their own heartbeat start competing with hers. Above, Biscuit's hackles rose and fell, rose and fell, a tide arguing with itself. Then she did the last thing either of them expected: she lay down across the landing, put her chin on her paws directly in front of the door they needed to pass through, and closed her eyes.

Not blocking. Guarding. From her side of things, this was obvious: no other warden could walk past a sleeping hound without waking her, and she would be *asleep*, officially, indisputably, if anyone asked.

"Oh," said Noor softly, climbing past her, close enough to smell woodsmoke and kennel straw. "Oh, you clever girl."

Biscuit's tail thumped once against the floorboards, the smallest sound in the world, and the loudest.

* * *

**Brannic**

From the ninth gallery, the rookery opened upward into the Spire's stone throat, and Brannic stopped breathing for a moment, because it smelled the same.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, coming home to a place that had been stolen. Not the sight of it changed, not the purpose of it fouled — those you could brace for. It was the smell. Old cedar shavings and feather-dust and the faint mineral cold of northern stone, exactly as it had been the morning he'd carried the king's gyrfalcon up these stairs on his arm, whistling, whole, believing the world was a place where kings stayed kind.

"Brannic." Noor's whisper, close at his shoulder. "You've gone somewhere."

"Away," he admitted.

"Do we follow?"

"Not yet." He rolled his shoulders, shook the past off like rain. "Quill. The map."

The tailless raven dropped onto his wrist — his empty wrist, the stump-side, and he understood the choice and hated how much it moved him — and began, in the old handlers' code, to tap out their route. Three taps: the shaft ahead was watched. Two taps and a wing-spread: the counterweight channel was clear. A long pause, then a single tap, repeated, insistent, aimed downward through the stone.

Below them, far below, something answered. Not a raven. Something slower, heavier, patient — a rhythm like knuckles on a coffin lid.

Pip.

"He's alive," Noor said, and their voice cracked straight down the middle. "Brannic, he's answering, he's—"

"I hear him." Brannic's throat was tight enough to strangle a songbird. He tapped back, quick and clumsy, the old signal for *hold fast, help comes*, and felt it travel down through nine floors of stone like a heartbeat finding its twin.

Above them, in the loft, one of the sleeping wardens rolled over. The snoring hitched, stuttered—

—and resumed, because on the landing below, a warden's hound sighed loudly and resettled, and the man murmured "Biscuit, off," half-dreaming, and slept on trusting his own dog to guard him.

Brannic looked at Noor. Noor looked at Brannic. Neither of them laughed, because laughing would have woken everyone within three floors, but something passed between them anyway, warm as brandy: *we are going to survive this, and it will be because of a dog named after bread.*

"The counterweight channel," Brannic whispered. "It runs inside the walls, up past the vaults, opens near the crown-room. Quill will take us as far as the ninth gallery. After that—" He hesitated.

"After that we're in the dark with whatever wears the crown," Noor finished.

"After that," Brannic said, "we knock."

* * *

**Noor**

The counterweight channel was exactly wide enough for regret and narrow enough for everything else to hurt. Noor went first, because Quill had decided it, because Brannic's remaining arm was better spent catching people than squeezing through stone, and because — though they'd never have said it aloud — Noor wanted to be the one who reached Pip first.

The stone was cold and spoke constantly. Not words; Vellamark's stones had been silenced along with everything else fifteen years ago. But pressure, memory, the groan of old grief in old joints. And threaded through it, faint as a vein of ore, the hum Noor had been hearing since the Tallow Market: the sound of magic moving upward. Being taken. Being *drunk*.

Halfway up, the channel widened into a niche, and in the niche, someone had scratched marks into the stone. Small marks. Child-height marks.

Counting marks. Days.

Beside them, scratched deeper, in a hand that shook: *I TOLD THEM ABOUT THE RUST. THEY DIDN'T BELIEVE ME EITHER.*

Noor put their palm flat against the scratches and let themselves feel it, just for three breaths, all of it — the fear, the guilt, the terrible tenderness — because Marrow had taught them that swallowed feelings curdle, and they would need everything uncurdled for what waited above.

Then they climbed.

* * *

**Brannic**

At the top of the channel, the stone opened like a mouth, and Brannic stepped out onto a gallery he had last seen in firelight and screaming, and found it lit by a single lamp and utterly silent.

The upper mews. The honor roost, once — gyrfalcons and the king's white ravens, birds fit for coronations. Now the perches stood empty, swept clean, and beyond the louvered shutters he could see the vault stairs descending into blue copper light, and beside those stairs, two wardens standing watch with the perfect stillness of men who had been standing watch too long to believe anyone was coming.

Quill landed on his shoulder and pressed her beak, gently, against his ear. In the old code, the oldest code, the one that predated handlers and crowns and bindings both, she said:

*Now.*

Brannic looked down at Noor, climbing out of the channel behind him, dust-grey and steady-eyed, and behind Noor, far below through nine floors of stone, a boy who spoke to rust was holding fast.

"Two guards," Brannic breathed. "Sleeping city, armed Spire, and two guards between us and the thing that eats kingdoms. Any ideas?"

Noor tilted their head, listening — to the wardens, to the building, to something Brannic couldn't hear at all — and slowly smiled the smile of someone who has just been told a secret by the entire world at once.

"As a matter of fact," they whispered, "the horses want to meet us."

Far below, faint but unmistakable, the Spire's stables erupted — a whinny, a crash, a shout of alarm, hooves on cobbles like applause.

The two guards by the vault stairs exchanged a look, drew their blades, and ran toward the noise.

Leaving the way down — the way *in* — standing open.

"Well," said Brannic. "Lead on, beast-speaker."

And Noor Halloran, charm-seller, outlaw, orphan, friend, stepped past the empty posts of the king's own guard and started down the stairs into the blue light, where something ancient and hungry was waiting, and had been waiting a very long time, and had no idea — not yet, not quite yet — what was coming up to meet it.
