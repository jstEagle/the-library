## 14. The Sleeping King

The ravens' map had been drawn in the only ink they had: droppings and spite. It took Noor three wrong turnings in the dark beneath the Spire to learn that a smear meant *stair* and a peck-mark meant *run*, and that everything else meant *the raven who drew this was laughing at you*.

"Left," Brannic said behind them, for the fourth time.

"You said left last time."

"I say left every time because you keep turning right."

"I'm following a bird's opinion of architecture," Noor hissed. "Forgive me if my instincts are rattled."

They had come down through the mews while the wardens slept — that part had gone almost sweetly, the dogs lifting their heads as Noor passed and settling again at a whispered promise of *morning, I swear it, morning and meat*. But now the passages narrowed, and the air changed, and Noor felt the change before they understood it.

It felt like standing near a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing.

The walls were sweating copper. Not pipes — veins. Thin wires of it ran along the stone like roots under bark, branching, joining, thickening as they went deeper, until the passage floor glimmered faintly red-gold wherever the light from Sefa's lantern-moth jar touched it. The moth itself had gone strange, beating its wings in slow, dreamy circles, drunk on whatever hummed through the metal.

"It's all one thing," Pip would have said, if Pip were here. Noor's throat tightened around the thought of him, caged somewhere ahead of them in this same dark. *One thing.* All the bound magic of Vellamark, fifteen years of confiscated charms and collared gifts, running down into the Spire like rain into a well.

And something drinking at the bottom of it.

Brannic stopped so suddenly Noor walked into his back. His one arm came up — not for balance. For warning.

Ahead, the corridor opened into a round chamber, and the chamber was full of light.

Not torchlight. Not mothlight. A pale, steady glow rose from the center of the room, where copper roots thicker than a man's waist climbed out of the floor and wove themselves together overhead into a dome, a cage, a cradle — Noor couldn't decide which word fit and hated all three. Inside the weave, suspended just off the ground as though the metal refused to let him touch even the stone, hung an old man.

He wore a nightshirt gone grey with dust. His hair was white and long and drifted slightly, as if underwater. His eyes were closed. His chest moved — barely, slowly, once every ten heartbeats by Noor's count — but he breathed.

"That's not possible," Noor said.

"No," Brannic agreed quietly. "It isn't."

Noor looked at him. In the copper light the old falconer's face had gone the color of ash, and there was something in it Noor had never seen there before, not in the Tallow Market, not during the raid, not when Pip was taken: he looked *lost*. A man standing in a doorway of a house he'd been told burned down years ago.

"You knew him," Noor said. Not a question. They'd known since the ravens first spoke his name back to them — *your master asks after you* — and watched Brannic go still as a stuffed hawk.

"I served him eleven years." Brannic stepped forward. Each step seemed to cost him. "Flew his gyrfalcon off the west tower. Mended his jesses. He used to come to the mews himself when the weight of the crown got bad — sit on a straw bale with his boots off and let the birds scream at him until he could think again." He stopped at the edge of the lattice. "I never saw anyone carry a kingdom so lightly."

Inside the copper cradle, the old man's lips moved.

No sound came out at first. Then, thin as wind through a keyhole:

"...Brannic?"

* * *

Later — much later, sitting against the wall with their knees pulled up while the enormity settled over them like snowfall — Noor would try to describe what happened next and fail. For now there was only the moment: Brannic crossing the last distance and pressing his palm flat against the copper, and the king's eyes opening.

They were blue. Terribly, ordinarily blue. Eyes like a fisherman's, like a grandfather's, like any old man waking from an afternoon nap in a garden chair.

"There you are," the king said. His voice was rust and paper. "You've been gone a long time, falconer."

"Fifteen years, majesty."

"Mm." The king considered this. His brow creased with genuine, gentle puzzlement. "That doesn't seem right. We were only just — the hawks were only just —" He trailed off. Looked at his own hands, drifting in the weave. "Why am I up here? I was dreaming about the river. There were herons."

"What do you remember?" Noor asked softly.

The blue eyes found them. And here was the terrible thing, the thing Noor felt land in their chest like a stone dropped down a well: the eyes were clear. Awake. Present. Whatever hollowed him had left the man himself intact, a candle burning in an empty house with all the furniture carried out.

"I remember being tired," the king said. "So tired. And then someone kind said they would carry it for me. Just for a little while." He smiled — a sweet, apologetic smile. "Was that wrong?"

Brannic made a sound low in his throat, like a dog trying not to growl at its own reflection.

Noor knelt beside the lattice. Up close they could see how the copper entered him — fine wires at his wrists, his temples, the hollow of his throat, sunk in without wound or scar, as natural as veins. Through those wires, faintly, pulsed a rhythm that was not his heartbeat. Slower. Vaster. Patient as geology.

Something was using him the way a lamp uses oil.

"Majesty," Noor said carefully, "do you know what's been happening in your city?"

"The hunt?" He sounded mildly ashamed. "I hear it. Through the metal. Everything comes through the metal — the charms, the collars, the poor children in the vaults below." His gaze drifted, then sharpened with sudden, heartbreaking clarity. "There's a boy down there. Rust-touched. He sings to the locks when he thinks no one's listening. Is he yours?"

Noor's breath caught. "Pip. Yes. He's ours."

"Good," said the king. "He should have people. Everyone should have people." And then his face did something complicated — grief moving across it like weather, arriving from a country far away. "I had people. Didn't I? There was a queen. There was—" His hand twitched toward Brannic. "There were herons."

* * *

Sefa had read the moths before they descended. She'd hated the reading; she'd shown them anyway. Four omens, contradicting each other like quarrelsome sisters: *a door opening. A door closing. A man asleep. A man dead.* Noor understood now which omen was true, and understood too late that all four might be.

Because the king was alive. That was the miracle. And the king was a lamp with no oil left of his own, burning on what the crown fed him drop by drop, and that was the horror — because Noor had spent three days planning a rescue, and rescue required waking him, and waking him meant pulling the wires out.

Sefa's voice in memory: *A body runs on what it's given, love. You don't take a man off the vine mid-dream and expect him to remember how to stand.*

Noor laid two fingers near the king's wrist, careful of the wire, and listened — really listened, the way they listened to horses and rats and the lame cart-horse whose real name, they'd learned years ago, was Duchess. They opened the quiet place inside where beast-speech lived and reached for the old man the way they'd reach for any frightened creature caught in a snare.

What they felt nearly made them pull back.

He wasn't dreaming. He was *held*. Fifteen years of dreams, layered like silt, each one a year the crown had poured into him to keep the wick wet — the river with the herons, over and over, ten thousand times, because it was the last year he'd owned himself. If you woke a man from that, all at once, he'd have to carry every lost day in a single breath.

And underneath the dreams: emptiness. His own magic — a king's magic, whatever it had been, some slow deep gift of steadiness, Noor guessed, the gift that makes a kingdom hold — drained to fumes. His body had forgotten how to run on itself. The copper wasn't a prison. It was a feeding tube.

"He'll die," Noor heard themself say. Their voice sounded very small in the round room. "If we wake him now. Maybe not today. Maybe not tomorrow. But soon. His own fire's out. This—" they touched the lattice, and flinched at the vast slow pulse within it, "—this is all that's keeping him lit."

"Then we leave him lit," Brannic said flatly. "We cut him free of the crown's grip another way. Slowly. Properly."

"With what? There's no 'properly' down here, Brannic. Tomorrow the thing upstairs decides the city's ripe and drinks the whole cup — him included. Every hour we spend finding a gentle way is an hour it gets stronger." Noor pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "This is the trap, isn't it. This is exactly what it wants. Either we leave him and it keeps him forever, or we take him and we kill him with our own hands, and either way—"

"Either way," the king said mildly, "you're arguing over my head again. You've been doing it for five minutes. It's very rude. My falconer never argued over my head."

"We're not—" Brannic started.

"Weren't you," the king said. Not unkindly. "Boy. Girl. Whoever-you-are. Come closer."

Noor came closer.

The old man studied them with those ordinary blue eyes, and Noor felt seen in a way that had nothing to do with magic — felt seen the way Marrow saw them, the way Sefa saw them, the way family sees.

"You're a speaker," he said. "A real one. I can tell because the copper hates you. Feel it?" He nodded at the lattice. "It pulls away from your fingers. Clever old thing upstairs, keeping speakers caged. We were friends once, your kind and mine. Before the law." Something flickered across his face — the ghost of an expression, guilt maybe, arriving from very far away. "Before me. Was it me? Did I sign that?"

Noor couldn't lie to him. It would have been like lying to a wren. "Yes, majesty."

"Ah." He absorbed this. Drifted. Came back. "Well. Kings sign things. That's most of the job. The other half is knowing which ones to unsign." His hand turned in the weave, palm up, an offering. "Wake me."

"Majesty—" Brannic's voice cracked clean down the middle. "It may kill you."

"It may," the king agreed pleasantly. "But look at me, falconer. Truly look. What is it you'd be killing? I eat dreams. I breathe wire. I have been a lamp for fifteen years and the thing that lights me has been signing things in my name the whole time." His voice gathered, for one moment, something of the man who had sat on straw bales with his boots off. "Better one honest death than ten thousand borrowed mornings. Wake me. Let me walk out of here on my own feet or not at all."

"And if your death breaks something worse open?" Noor asked. Because they had felt it, reaching into him — felt the shape of the seal, the reason a living king was kept alive rather than replaced. The crown didn't just feed on him. It hid *behind* him. A dead king could be buried, mourned, succeeded. A living king, displayed at windows, was a lock. "If the city sees him fall?"

"Then the city will finally ask why its king fell," the king said, "and questions, young speaker, are the one thing crowns cannot digest."

Silence filled the round room, thick as tallow. Somewhere far above, through miles of copper vein, the vast slow pulse beat on. Noor thought of Pip singing to locks in the dark. Of Marrow's whiskers. Of Duchess the cart-horse, lame and patient, waiting all those years for someone to simply *ask*.

The choice sat between them like a live coal.

Brannic knelt. Took the king's drifting hand in his one remaining one, careful of the wires, and held it the way you hold a hawk you love: firmly enough to keep, gently enough not to break.

"Your gyrfalcon," Brannic said. "Her name was Sorrel. She died in the winter after — after. I buried her under the west tower myself. I never told anyone, because telling felt like treason, and I have hated myself for that silence for fifteen years." His voice shook. "I'm telling you now, majesty, so you know what silence costs. So you know what I'll pay this time. Say the word and I'll pull every wire from your skin with my own hand."

The king smiled. And in his smile, for the first time, Noor saw the whole shape of the tragedy — a good man, worn down to a wick, choosing his own snuffing so the thing wearing his face could be dragged into the light.

"Sorrel," he murmured. "She bit the ambassador. Good bird." Then his eyes cleared, and fixed on Noor, and the mildness fell away entirely. "Speaker. Before you touch anything — know this. When the wires come out, the thing upstairs will feel it. Instantly. Like a tooth pulled from its own jaw. You will have minutes. Perhaps fewer."

Noor nodded slowly. "Then we'd best send word up first."

"How?" Brannic asked.

Noor reached into their coat, where a small brown shape had been riding since the mews — a wren, feathers puffed, eyes bright, sent down as escort and gossip both by the parliament of birds. It hopped onto Noor's finger and cocked its head at the dying king as if taking his measure.

"Ravens above," Noor said. "And below us—" they pressed their palm to the floor, feeling the faint scurry-scurry of a thousand small lives in the walls, the undercity's oldest citizens, Old Marrow's cousins and grandchildren threading through the Spire's foundations, "—we have more family in these stones than the crown knows."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, the great pulse of the copper quickened.

Once. Twice.

As if something ancient had rolled over in its sleep — and begun, at last, to notice them.

The king's hand tightened on Brannic's. "Hurry," he said.
