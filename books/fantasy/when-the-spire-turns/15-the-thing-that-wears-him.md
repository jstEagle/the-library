## 15. The Thing That Wears Him

It felt the intrusion the way a man feels a moth land on the back of his neck: small, weightless, and intolerable.

The thing that wore the king did not wake. Waking was for creatures with bodies to warm and blood to hurry. It simply *attended*, the way a spider attends its web — not looking, not moving, but suddenly aware of every trembling thread at once. Somewhere far above its copper roots, past the vaults full of slow-draining gifts, past the mews and the sleeping wardens and the seventeen hundred steps of the Spire's spine, something had entered that did not belong.

A rat. Two humans. A smell of pigeon feathers and old leather and — it paused over this one, tasting it like a connoisseur at a doubtful vintage — *speech*. Beast-speech, unbound and unregistered and fifteen years of it, hiding in a market stall like a mouse behind the wainscoting.

"Ah," said the thing that wore the king, aloud, in the king's voice, to the empty crown-room. The empty crown-room did not answer. It never did. This was, the thing reflected, one of the great disappointments of wearing rulers: they talked so much while alive and then, once hollowed, became such terrible conversationalists.

It rose from the throne. The body it borrowed rose with it, joints cracking, lungs dragging air out of habit rather than need. Fifteen years of practice had taught it how to make the flesh walk convincingly. There had been stumbles, early on. A queen in some southern kingdom, centuries back, whose hands it could never quite master — she'd played the harp, and the thing had played the harp, and the courtiers had wept at how her music had grown "deeper, somehow, more sorrowful." Sorrowful. Ha. It had been counting the strings.

But Vellamark's king had been easier. A plain man. A kind man, even, which made the wearing almost restful, like slipping into shoes already broken in by someone with similar feet. And he had come willingly, in the end. They always came willingly, once you explained the arithmetic to them. One man's dreams against a city's hunger. What ruler could refuse that trade? Most rulers had never done an honest sum in their lives.

The thing drifted to the window of the crown-room and looked down at its city.

Vellamark at dusk was a bowl of embers and lamplight, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, the harbor glittering like spilled coins. From up here it looked peaceful. From up here it looked *ripe*.

And that was the question of the evening, wasn't it. Ripe, or merely ready? The distinction mattered. It had rushed a harvest exactly once, in the old days, in a river-kingdom whose name no map remembered anymore, and it had learned patience the hard way: a city drained too fast soured. The gifted died raving instead of sweetly, the magic came up the copper roots bitter and clotted, and the thing had spent two hungry decades afterward wearing a fisherman's god in a swamp, which was beneath even its standards.

No. Patience. Patience had built all of this.

It turned its attention inward, down through the Spire's roots, and began — as it did every night, a ritual it found genuinely soothing, the way other beings found tea soothing — the counting.

The vaults first. Four hundred and eleven cages, each cradling a bound gift, each gift feeding a thin bright thread upward through copper into the crown-room's lattice. Four hundred and eleven heartbeats, kept deliberately alive. This was the part the wardens never understood, bless their obedient little hearts. They thought the hunt was about control. Order. Safety. They wrote their reports in tidy script about dangerous magics and threats to civic peace, and the thing let them believe it, because believing themselves righteous made the wardens gentle with the cages, and gentle handling kept the magic sweet.

Control. As if the thing needed control. It had worn emperors who commanded millions. Control was a toy for things that were only themselves.

No — the hunt was a *pantry*, and the wardens were pantry-mice who thought themselves wolves.

Four hundred and eleven. It noted, with mild displeasure, that cage ninety-two had gone quiet three days ago — the rust-speaker boy, the young one, the one whose gift tasted of iron filings and stubbornness. Dead? No. Worse. *Moved.* Someone had taken him deeper, toward the old root-chambers, which meant either a clerk's error or — the thing held the possibility up to the light and examined it — organization among the prisoners.

Organization. In the vaults. It almost laughed. Almost; the king's throat wasn't built for it, and besides, laughter was wasteful. But there was something darkly funny about it. Every century or so, the caged ones organized. They scratched messages in codes, they passed whispers along the copper (which carried sound as well as magic, a design flaw the thing had never bothered correcting because listening to their little rebellions was honestly the best entertainment the Spire offered), they planned escapes with the doomed optimism of mayflies planning retirements. And then the plans collapsed, and the whispering stopped for a generation, and peace returned.

Except this time there was beast-speech in the mews above, and a rat-smell in the stairwells, and a falconer's leather on the wind — and falconer's leather was a very specific memory, one the thing kept filed under *threats*, because Brannic of the Royal Mews had stood outside these doors the night the king went down, and had lost an arm to his own loyalty, and had sworn silence with tears in his eyes, and the thing had let him live because a living witness who tells no one is worth ten dead ones. Silence spreads doubt. Doubt spreads caution. Caution keeps cities asleep.

Had Brannic finally spoken?

The thing considered this. It considered, too, the wren that had flown out of a market stall fifteen years ago carrying nothing but freedom and now, apparently, carried messages. It considered the hounds — its hounds, trained, collared, leashed by law — who had hesitated last night at the flood-tunnels. Hesitated. Hounds did not hesitate. Hounds were the simplest creatures it employed, four legs and an appetite, and yet three of them had stood at a tunnel mouth trembling like maidens while something spoke to them in the old tongue, the pre-tongue, the language before law.

Mercy, they had been given. Mercy, of all things. The thing turned this over and found it fascinating in the way a scholar finds plague fascinating.

*They are speaking to my instruments,* it thought. *My hounds, my rats, my birds. The city's small ears are being turned.*

Which brought it, reluctantly, to the arithmetic it had been avoiding all evening.

The thing that wore the king sat back down upon the throne — the real one, the copper lattice one, hidden beneath the ceremonial oak where petitioners knelt and saw only a tired old man being patient with them — and let itself feel the truth of its own hunger.

Fifteen years ago, Vellamark had been a feast laid out in courses. The binding edict had been its carving knife: outlaw the magic, drive it underground, and frightened people hide their gifts, and hidden gifts ferment, and fermented gifts are *rich*. Oh, the first years had been magnificent. Every smuggled charm, every whispered blessing, every child scolded into silence — all of it ripening in cellars and attics like wine in the dark. The copper roots drank steadily. The thing grew glossy. It had worn kings before who aged visibly under it, but this body stayed plump and pink and boringly healthy, and the court called the king "well-preserved," and the thing agreed.

But a pantry does not refill itself.

Four hundred and eleven cages was a good number, a proud number, and yet the city above had nearly emptied itself of unbound magic. The gifted were caught, culled, caged, or fled. The rats reported fewer sparks in the undercity each season. Even the Tallow Market — that charming little den of moths and rust and fortune-tellers — had thinned to a few dozen souls trading in dregs and memories. You cannot harvest a field forever without planting. It knew this. It had always known this. Every host-city eventually ran dry, and every time, it had faced the same choice: move on, or consume.

Moving on meant starting over. New kingdom, new face, new century of careful sipping. It had done it nine times. Nine cities, nine crowns, nine long patient dinners. It was very good at it. It was, if it was honest with itself — and honesty was cheap when no one could hear — beginning to find it *dull*. Nine times the same meal with different spices.

Consumption, on the other hand. Full consumption. Not draining the gifted but unbinding everything — every hedge-charm, every hearth-blessing, every drop of wild magic sleeping in the city's stones and river and stubborn green things pushing up between cobbles — and drinking it all at once. The city would not survive it. The city would become a beautiful hollow shell, like the river-kingdom, like the fisherman's god's swamp, a place of quiet streets and empty windows where nothing grew and nothing spoke.

Nine times it had declined. Nine times, patience.

It looked out again at the embers of Vellamark, and felt something almost tender.

Because here was the thing: Vellamark was different. Not in its magic — magic was magic, everywhere the same flavor of stolen sunlight — but in its *people*. Fifteen years of the hunt had done something the binding alone never managed. The lawful citizens had learned to look away. The neighbors took silver for names. The wardens' children played with confiscated charms like toys. And underneath, the exiles had learned the opposite lesson just as thoroughly: that no one would save them, so they saved each other; that family was assembled, not born; that mercy was a tactic and trust was a currency spent only among the condemned.

A city split clean in half, lawful and lawless, each side convinced of its own virtue. Do you know what grows beautifully in that crack? The thing had watched it grow for fifteen years, watering it with proclamations and silver coins. Resentment. And resentment, properly ripened, is the richest soil there is. Richer than fear. Fear spoils quickly. Resentment ages like the wine those hidden gifts had become.

And tonight, in the crack, a beast-speaker was climbing its Spire with a one-armed falconer and a handful of rat-whispers and raven-bargains, believing — the thing savored the word — *believing* they were sneaking in.

Let them climb. Let them reach the vaults. Let them free a few cages, even; freed magic scattered is wasted, but freed magic *gathered* — ah, there was the trick no one ever saw coming. When the caged ones broke open all at once, four hundred and eleven gifts would flare like a struck match, and the lattice would catch the flare and funnel it, and the roots would drink deep in a single glorious gulp, and the thing would be strong enough — finally, *finally* — to do what it had wanted to do since the first year of the binding.

Unbind everything else. All at once. The whole city, cracked open like an egg.

The intruders weren't a threat. They were the corkscrew.

This realization settled over the thing with the warmth of a bath drawn by servants. It almost pitied them. Almost — pity was another of those flavors it enjoyed in theory. The beast-speaker, especially. It had watched Noor Halloran for years, actually, through rat-eyes and sparrow-eyes and once, memorably, through the lame cart-horse's big wet brown ones. The horse adored them. The horse would have followed them off a cliff. The thing had noted the stall, the luck-knots, the ache in the creature's posture — a gift strangled down to a whisper for fifteen years, and still *kind* with it, still bargaining gently with horses, still freeing wrens.

Kindness was such useful material. Kind hosts ruled best. A cruel wearer had to spend so much effort making the body smile.

If the beast-speaker reached the crown-room — and it would, the ravens would see to the route, the ravens owed it older debts than they remembered — then the offer would be made. The same offer made nine times across nine centuries, and accepted... well. Never accepted, technically. Twice *nearly* accepted, which had been educational. But the offer itself was sincere, insofar as anything it felt was sincere: wear me. Rule kindly. Your beasts would be heard in every hall of law. Your outcasts fed, your market stalls legal, your wren given a statue in the square if you liked. All true. All achievable. The only price was the city, and the city was the price everyone else paid, and no one who stood at the throne had ever once asked what happened to everyone else. That was the secret of ruling, the thing had learned it in its first body, a bronze-age priest-king who wore a crown of actual thorns: *they always want the crown more than they want the world.*

It would offer. And if the beast-speaker refused — they usually refused, right up until the moment they didn't — then the flare-and-gulp would proceed regardless, and Vellamark would join the list of names no map remembered, and the thing would go looking for a younger city with fresher fears.

Either harvest worked. That was the beauty of patience: every road arrived.

Down in the vaults, it felt the prisoners stirring — the boy Pip whispering through copper to cage after cage, the tall woman with the moth-gift reading omens in the dark, the old rat-matriarch's descendants running messages along the root-pipes like blood through veins. Let them stir. Let them organize. Their hope was the yeast in the dough.

Above, in the mews, the ravens shifted on their roosts and said nothing, which was its own kind of speech, and the thing pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of promise.

And somewhere on the seventeenth stair, small as a moth on a spider's web, the beast-speaker climbed, carrying a confession it hadn't made yet and a name-list it hadn't learned yet and a courage that was going to be so very, very useful.

"Come up," said the thing that wore the king, softly, to no one, in a dead man's voice. "Dinner's nearly ready."

Far below, in the dark of the root-chambers, the copper hummed — and for the first time in fifteen years, it hummed in answer to voices that were not its own.
