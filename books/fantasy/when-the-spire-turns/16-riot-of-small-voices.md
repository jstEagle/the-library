## 16. Riot of Small Voices

### Noor

Noor had spent fifteen years whispering.

Whispering to the cart-horse with the bad hip, to the wren in its cage, to the market cats who traded gossip for scraps of fish. Fifteen years of keeping their voice small, as if the gift itself were a crime — which, of course, it was, written into law in ink Noor had never been allowed to read. Speaking softly had become so much a part of them that when they opened their mouth now, at the mouth of the copper-rooted dark beneath the Spire, and spoke at full volume for the first time since childhood, it came out strange. Loud. Enormous. Their own voice sounded like a stranger's, a giant's, a bell.

*Rats of Vellamark,* they said, in the speech that needed no air. *Sisters of Marrow. You told me what walks these halls wearing a dead man's face. I am asking you: wake everyone.*

The silence afterward lasted exactly one heartbeat.

Then the walls moved.

Noor had imagined, insofar as they had let themselves imagine anything, a kind of orderly response. A delegation. Old Marrow arriving with her grey retinue, whiskers twitching, negotiating terms as she always did. What happened instead was that every rat in the Spire — and there were thousands, tens of thousands, a city beneath the city living fat on the crumbs of an institution — began screaming at once, and running, and biting through things.

"They're *coming*," Brannic said beside them, awed despite himself, his remaining hand gripping his falconer's hook. Above, somewhere up through eleven floors of vaults and stairways and guard posts, came a sound like rain beginning. Then like applause. Then like nothing that had ever been heard in the Spire before, which was the sound of a building's smallest residents deciding, all together, that they were done being furniture.

"Old Marrow sends word," said a young rat at their feet, breathless, one ear notched from some old fight. "She says — she says *finally.* She says she has waited nine years and eaten four kings' worth of dropped pastry for this day." The rat paused. "Those are her exact words. She asked me to be precise."

"Tell her," Noor said, and felt something wild and bright kindle in their chest, "to eat through everything."

### Pip

Pip heard the rats before he understood them.

He was pressed against the bars of his cage — cell 40, third tier of the deep vaults, where the copper roots came down out of the ceiling like the fingers of some vast buried hand — and the wall behind him simply erupted. Rats poured through a gnawed hole the size of a dinner plate, dozens of them, and the largest, a scarred matriarch with fur gone silver at the muzzle, sat back on her haunches and looked at him with eyes like wet black coins.

"You're the rust-boy," she said. "The one who talks to metal."

"I talk to *rust,*" Pip said faintly. It seemed important to be accurate. "Rust's different. Rust is metal that's given up."

"Good," said Old Marrow. "Because we need you to have a conversation with these cages."

The cages were iron, old iron, and iron was not rust — but iron dreamed of rust, the way all things dreamed of ending, and Pip had spent fourteen years in Vellamark's foundling yards learning how to talk to things about their endings. He put both hands flat against the bars. They were cold, and humming, because the copper roots ran through every cage in the vault, siphoning, drinking, feeding whatever wore the crown eleven floors up. The magic of the prisoners leaked upward through that hum like blood through a bandage.

"Hey," Pip said to the cage. Gently. The way you'd speak to anything old and tired. "You've held a lot of people. You've done your job. Nobody's blaming you. But the people inside didn't do anything wrong, and neither did you. You were just made to do this." He swallowed. His voice cracked, which happened when he talked too long to anything, because talking to things was lonely work and he'd never gotten used to anyone listening. "You can stop now. You can just — let go."

The hum changed pitch.

It was not dramatic, at first. A vibration loosening. Then the lock, which had drunk enchantment for years and never once been asked how it felt about its work, sighed — an actual sigh, a small metallic exhalation — and clicked open, and the door swung, and Sefa's cousin Amma stood on the other side of it with her moth-burned hands over her mouth, weeping.

"Go," Marrow said, and her voice carried down the tiers, and forty thousand rats repeated it in relay until the whole vault rang with it. *Go. Go. Go.* "And you, rust-boy — the next cages. There are children two rows down. Move fast. When the horses start, none of us will be able to hear ourselves think."

"What horses?" Pip asked.

Marrow's whiskers quivered in what Pip would later learn was her version of a grin.

"The warden's horses," she said. "Noor just asked them to remember whose side they're on."

### Sefa

Sefa had read moths for eleven years, and in all that time no moth had ever shown her anything like what she saw from the flood-tunnel archway, looking back into the Spire's lower yard.

The wardens' stable stood at the yard's edge, and the wardens' horses were coming out of it without any wardens at all. Not fleeing — emerging. Walking in formation, if six horses could be said to walk in formation, heads high, hooves striking the cobbles in a rhythm that could only be described as *deliberate*. The biggest of them, a black gelding the wardens called Grudge because he bit everyone, stopped in the center of the yard, turned his enormous head toward the gatehouse where three guards were fumbling with a crossbow, and looked at them for a long, eloquent moment.

Then he screamed — a full war-stallion scream — and charged the gatehouse, and five other horses followed, and the guards discovered simultaneously that crossbows are very poor weapons against former colleagues' mounts and that the mounting block makes an adequate barricade.

"Oh," Sefa said. "Oh, that's *lovely.*"

Beside her, Brannic grunted. He did not look lovely-struck; he looked like a man watching his whole life reorganize itself around a new idea. "Fifteen years I served the crown," he said quietly. "Trained those birds above the mews, walked past those stables every morning. Never once thought to ask the horses anything. Didn't think they were — that it counted. Talking to beasts." He flexed the stump of his arm, an old habit. "Thought it was only hawks that mattered. Only useful things."

"Useful things," Sefa repeated, and gestured at the yard, where a horse had now picked up a fallen truncheon in its teeth and appeared to be guarding it, or possibly hoarding it, or possibly just enjoying the novelty. "Brannic. Look at what the useless things are doing."

Above them, the rookery woke. The ravens went up first — a black spiral unwinding against the moon — and then the pigeons, and then the sparrows in such numbers that the sky over the Spire seemed briefly to be boiling. Somewhere in that storm of wings, Noor's voice was still speaking. Sefa couldn't hear it; almost no human ear could. But you could see it land, wing by wing, the way wind moves through a field you can't see the wind itself, only the shape it presses into the grass.

A single raven dropped out of the boil and landed on Brannic's shoulder, and Sefa saw her old friend go absolutely rigid, because the raven said, in a voice like gravel poured through a sieve:

"*Falconer.* You owe the mews thirty years of gossip and one apology. Pay later. Climb now. The crown-room stairs are open — the rats ate the hinges — but the thing upstairs has noticed us, and it is *angry.*"

### The Warden

Warden Corla Hesse had hunted the gifted for six years and believed, right up until this hour, that she was good at it.

She believed it less firmly as she ran down the Spire's third-floor corridor with her lantern swinging and her hounds straining at their leads — because her hounds were not straining forward. Her hounds, veteran hunters, dogs who had pulled down fugitives in flood tunnels and hay wagons and once, memorably, a chimney, had planted all eight feet on the flagstones and were refusing to move.

"Mush," Corla snarled, using the old word, the command word. The lead hound, Bristle, turned and looked up at her, and Corla had trained enough dogs to know what a dog looked like when it was listening to something a human could not hear.

"Bristle. *Heel.*"

Bristle did not heel. Bristle sat.

Down the corridor, the screaming was getting worse — not the screaming of the hunted, which Corla knew in all its varieties, but the screaming of doors, of hinges, of locks, of the building itself. Rats came around the corner in a river, flowed past her boots without so much as glancing at the hounds, which should have been impossible, because Bristle had killed forty rats in his career and here they were, ignoring him, contemptuous, *busy.*

And under it all, threading through the stone like water through cloth, a voice. Corla couldn't make out the words. She only knew that her dogs could, and that whatever the voice was saying, it was saying something true, because Bristle looked up at her again — her own dog, her dog since he was a puppy with oversized paws — and his expression was the one dogs wear when they are deeply, patiently disappointed.

"They pay you in silver to name children," the dark seemed to say, though Corla knew she was imagining it, knew the voice wasn't for her. "They pay me in nothing at all, and I'm here anyway. Come on, brother. Which of us is the fool?"

Corla Hesse stood in the corridor of the Spire of Vellamark with her lantern shaking in her fist, and made, in that moment, the second decision of her life that mattered. The first had been to take the crown's silver. She unclipped the leads.

"Go on, then," she said, and her voice broke in the middle of it. "If you know something I don't."

Her hounds knew something she didn't. They went like arrows.

### Pip

By the time Pip reached the lowest tier, the vaults had stopped being a prison and started being a stampede, and the difference between freeing people and losing them in a crowd had collapsed entirely. Children streamed past him toward the flood tunnels, shepherded by rats standing up on their hind legs at intervals like little grey signposts. An old woman with frost-white hair — a weather-speaker, Pip guessed, from the way the air bent politely around her — paused to grip his shoulder with a hand cold as January.

"Who do I thank, boy?"

"Nobody yet!" Pip shouted after her. "Thank nobody till it's done!"

But the last cage on the tier was not empty, and the last cage was not opening.

The boy inside couldn't have been more than seven, curled small against the far wall, and his gift — Pip could feel it, a low wrong thrum — was the loudest thing in the room. Sound-eater, maybe, or shaker. The copper root above his cage ran thicker than the others, drank deeper, and the boy lay beneath it like something already half-emptied. The cage bars around him were blackened, fused, as if he'd fought them once with everything he had and lost.

Pip put his hands on the iron. "Hey. Hey, it's fine, I've got you, I'm getting you out—"

"They won't listen to me anymore," the boy whispered. "I asked them. I asked and asked. Nothing listens to me. Nothing ever listens."

Something in Pip's chest folded neatly in half.

"Yeah," he said. "I know how that goes." He kept his palms flat on the bar, feeling the old iron's exhaustion, its long dull ache of complicity. "Look — these bars don't hate you. They're just old and tired and somebody made them do a bad job. That's not the same as being against you. Half the scary things in the world are just tired things doing jobs nobody should've asked of them." He glanced up at the copper root, thick as his thigh, pulsing faintly with stolen light. "Even that, probably. Even *him,* probably. Doesn't matter. What matters is—" he took a breath, and talked to the iron the way he'd never dared talk to anything, with his whole heart, no holding back, "—*you can rest now. All of you. Rest.*"

Every lock in the tier opened at once. The sound was like a hundred small bells.

The boy crept out. Pip took his hand, and the boy's grip was astonishingly strong, and together they ran toward the light of the tunnel mouths while above them the whole Spire groaned and shifted on its foundations — because the copper roots, suddenly unfed, hundreds of them, were going dry all at once, and whatever wore the crown eleven floors up had just felt every vein in its body close.

### Noor

Noor felt it happen. Everyone in the Spire felt it happen.

The drain reversed.

For fifteen years the binding had pulled — pulling at the gifted in their cages, pulling at the hedgerow-witches and hedge-wives and charm-sellers of the city above, a gentle constant tug Noor had mistaken all their life for simple tiredness. Now the tug stopped, and then, horribly and wonderfully, it *pushed*, and all the magic the thing had drunk and stored and hoarded in the copper lattice came flooding back down the roots looking for its owners, and the Spire — built to channel power upward, built as a throat — choked on its own meal.

Stone cracked overhead. Dust rained gold through lantern-light. The great spiral staircase, worn by three centuries of royal feet, shivered like a struck harp string.

"It's turning on them," Brannic gasped, hauling Noor back from a fissure splitting the wall. "The whole structure — it was never a tower, girl, it was a *machine*, and you've just pulled its—"

"No," Noor said. They were laughing, or crying, or both; there was genuinely no telling anymore. Around them the vault emptied upward, freed prisoners and rats and two extremely smug hounds flowing toward the tunnels, and Noor stood in the middle of the exodus with dust in their hair and their heart hammering like a bird in a cupped hand. "Not pulled anything out. Just gave everything *back.* It doesn't know how to be empty. None of them ever do."

Old Marrow appeared on a broken balustrade, tail lashing, and even a rat can look grim when the occasion requires it.

"Speaker," she said. "The vaults are empty. The small folk are out. My children counted twice." She flattened her ears. "But the thing upstairs is no longer sitting in its chair. It is coming down. And it is not hurrying, which frightens me considerably more than if it were."

Through the ringing stone, faint and patient and wrong, came footsteps. Unhurried. Regal. The sound of something that had worn kings for centuries and had never once, in all that time, been made to run.

Noor looked at Brannic. Brannic looked at Noor. Above them, through the cracked ceiling, the ravens were circling, and the Spire — groaning, shedding its dust, its stolen magic singing back down into the hands of everyone it had been taken from — began, very slowly, for the first time in three hundred years, to turn.

"Well," said Noor Halloran, thief of whispers, seller of luck-knots, voice of every small thing in Vellamark. They picked up a fallen truncheon, considered it, and set it back down. They wouldn't need it. They had never needed it. "Let's go meet it."
