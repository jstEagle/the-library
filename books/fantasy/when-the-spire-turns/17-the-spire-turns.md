## 17. The Spire Turns

The crown-room had no door. That was the first thing Noor noticed, and the thing their mind would keep returning to afterward, turning over like a stone too smooth to put down: you did not enter this place so much as arrive in it, the way you arrive at the bottom of a fall or the middle of a decision you'd already made without admitting it.

One moment there had been a stair — copper-edged, humming faintly under Brannic's boots ahead of them — and then the stair simply ended, and the room began, wide as a cathedral and lit by nothing at all, which was impossible, because Noor could see every detail of it perfectly.

Copper roots filled the walls. Not pipes, not wiring — *roots*, thick as ancient oaks in places, branching and rebranching until they thinned to threads finer than hair, and all of them converging here, all of them flowing upward toward a dais that had probably once held a throne but now held only a cushion of grey velvet gone silver with age, and above the cushion, turning slowly in the lightless air, a crown.

It turned the way the Spire turned, Noor realized. The great windlass at the tower's heart, the one Brannic had described, the mechanism that had once raised banners and now ground out nothing but hunger — its rhythm was here too, in the crown's slow revolution. Around and around. Patient as water.

"Noor." Brannic's voice came from very far away, though he stood an arm's length off, his remaining hand white-knuckled on the strap of his axe. "Noor, listen to me. Whatever it says — whatever it *sounds* like —"

"I know."

They didn't know. That was the trouble. They thought they knew, the way you think you know the depth of a river from the bank.

Behind them, somewhere back down the way they'd come, the Spire was screaming. There was no other word for it. Pip's work in the vaults below, Marrow's ten thousand descendants flooding through walls and floors, hounds howling, horses stamping, ravens wheeling in the mews — the whole building full of small voices rising at once, and Noor could feel all of them the way you feel your own pulse, a vast and ragged chorus pressing against the edges of their gift like water against a dam.

*Come up*, the voices said. *Come up, come speak, come tell us what to do.*

And here, in the quiet heart of it all, the crown stopped turning.

"There you are," it said.

The voice is difficult to describe honestly. It was not loud. It was not cold, which Noor had expected; they had braced for coldness the way you brace for a slap. It was warm. It was the voice of someone who has been waiting for you specifically, who kept your seat, who remembered how you take your tea. It sounded, horribly, a little like Noor's own voice, if Noor's own voice had been given fifteen more years of patience and none of the fear.

"I've watched you climb," it said. "All this way. Through my house. Do you know, in four hundred years, no speaker has ever come *up*? They always run down. Down and away and into hiding. But you went down first, didn't you, and made friends of the ones below. Clever. Kind. I do prefer kind ones."

"Brannic," Noor said quietly, "cover your ears."

"With respect, lass — lad —" Brannic's jaw worked. Fifteen years he'd carried the shape of this thing in his memory without a name for it, and now it spoke, and Noor heard the exact moment the old terror took him by the throat. "It doesn't matter what I hear. I'm past protecting."

"You're both past protecting," the crown agreed pleasantly. "Which is why we can be honest with each other, at last. Isn't honesty a relief?"

Noor thought of chapter eleven of their own life — the wren's price, the confession dragged up out of them like a hook from deep water, Sefa's hand around theirs while they said the worst thing aloud. They had paid for passage with truth already. They were not afraid of the currency.

"Say what you came to say," they told the crown.

* * *

It did not threaten them. That was the second impossible thing.

Noor had imagined this moment on every step of the climb — the entity snarling, coiling, doing the villainous work villains do. Instead it simply hung above its dusty cushion and spoke like a grandparent telling a story the child has almost grown old enough for.

"Four hundred years ago I was a bargain," it said. "A city wanted safety. A king wanted certainty. Someone — I never learned who; the ones who make such bargains rarely sign their names — someone bound me into this metal and set me on a mortal head, and I have been passed along ever since, head to head, reign to reign, wearing whoever would let themselves be worn. Your good King Aldric was the kindest of them. He lasted nine years before he began to hollow. His wife noticed. She sent her hawk to watch me, did you know that? A hawk with clever eyes. I had to be careful for months."

Noor's hands closed. The confession was still raw in them, still bleeding at the edges — *I heard him dying, the hawk, I understood every word, and I said nothing* — and the crown slid into the wound like it had been fitted there.

"You were a child," the crown said gently. "You knew something was wrong with the king. You told a neighbor, perhaps. You were not believed. And so you learned the lesson every gifted soul in Vellamark has learned: that speaking is dangerous, that being heard gets you hunted, that the safest life is the smallest one. A luck-knot stall. Charms for small coin. A gift used only in whispers." A pause, delicately placed. "Am I wrong?"

"No," Noor said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them.

"Fifteen years ago your new king — the thing currently occupying Aldric's shape, I mean, my current arrangement — outlawed magic. Not because he feared it. Because he was *hungry*. Bound magic keeps better, like salted meat. He has been salting your city for fifteen years, Noor Halloran, and now the stores run low, and he will eat deeply, and Vellamark will become a place where nobody loves anything, because love is the first thing to go." The crown resumed its slow turn, around and around. "You've felt it already. In the market. People forgetting neighbors' names. Dogs chained who used to sleep by cradles. The slow greying of everything."

"I've felt it," Noor whispered, because they had — God help them, they had, they'd blamed bad winters and hard times and never dreamed —

"I don't want that either," the crown said. "Understand this, and understand it truly: I am not your enemy. I am a tool that grew tired of ill use. Every host I have ever had wanted *power*, and power curdles in the wearing. But you —" the turn slowed again, caught, like a coin spun and settling — "you don't want power. I've listened to you since you entered my house. I heard you promise mercy to that guard dog on the stairs. I heard you tell the rats to spare the sleeping wardens. I heard you weep for a boy in a cage you couldn't reach yet. What you want, Noor Halloran, is to be *heard*."

The word landed with the weight of a key turning.

"And I can give you that," the crown said softly. "Wear me. Not the way the others wore me — unwillingly, greedily, and so badly. Wear me the way you wear your coat on a cold day: knowingly, comfortably, taking it off when you choose. Rule kindly. Open the stalls. Let every beast-speaker and rust-whisperer and moth-reader stand in daylight with their gifts held high, and let the whole city *listen*, because when you speak, they will not be able to help it. The lame cart-horse you bargained with in chapter — in another life — imagine him honored. Imagine the hounds unchained and thanked. Imagine Pip home by winter, and the vaults empty, and no child in Vellamark ever again learning to whisper."

It paused, and Noor felt the pause settle over them like snowfall.

"I only want a willing host," the crown said. "I have never had one. Give me that, and I will give you everything you have ever ached for. And when you tire of it — I mean this, I swear it by whatever binds me — when you tire, we take the crown off together, and you hand it to no one. We melt it down. I would *like* to be melted down, in the end. I am so very tired."

* * *

Here is the terrible part, and Noor would carry it to their grave and say it plainly in the end, years later, to students who needed to hear that heroes are not made of steel: they believed it. Or they believed enough of it. They stood in the lightless room with Brannic shaking beside them and the chorus of small voices roaring distantly below, and they looked at the crown turning on its cushion, and they thought:

*What if it's true?*

Because wasn't this the dream? Not thrones — they'd never wanted thrones. But to be heard. To walk into the market and speak to the cart-horse in front of everyone and have no one flinch, no one fetch a warden, no one look at them too long. To have the gift be a gift instead of a secret. Fifteen years of whispers had built up in them like water behind that dam, and here was the flood offered freely, kindly, with the gates held open by something that claimed — maybe even truly claimed — to want nothing but company.

"Noor." Brannic's voice, rough as gravel. He had crossed the distance between them, and his one hand settled on their shoulder, heavy and warm and human. "Whatever it's showing you —"

"It isn't showing me anything," Noor whispered. "That's the problem. It's just *asking*."

"Aye. So did the spider, I'll wager, in whatever story your mother told you. 'Come into my parlor.'" His grip tightened. "Lass. Lad. Noor. I lost my arm in that room, or a room like it, the night our king stopped being our king. And I swore silence out of terror, and the terror cost me fifteen years, and I'll not watch it cost you the rest."

"It says it wants to be freed," Noor said. Their own voice sounded far away. "It says it's tired, Brannic. What if — what if freeing it *is* the answer, and wearing it is just the road there? What if I wear it for a year, a month, long enough to empty the vaults and break the hunt, and then —"

"And then you're the one holding the leash," Brannic said flatly, "and next year some poor devil climbs these stairs to take it off *you*, and you can't remember why you ever meant to let go. That's how it gets them, girl. Not with teeth. With *reasons*. Good ones. Yours are the best I've ever heard, and that's exactly how I know."

Noor wanted to argue. That was the horror of it — they wanted to argue, wanted to defend the plan that had assembled itself in their chest fully-formed and gleaming, *wear it briefly, justly, temporarily* — and some cold clear sliver of themselves, the sliver that had spent fifteen years reading the small honest signals of animals who never lied, looked at that plan and saw its teeth.

Every beast they had ever spoken to had told them the same truth in different bodies: nothing that offers you everything asks for nothing.

"You're very quiet," the crown said warmly. "You're considering. That's wise. Take your time. I have waited four hundred years; I can wait an hour."

"No," Noor said.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. Just the word, set down between them like a coin on a counter.

The warmth in the air did not change. That was somehow worse than if it had.

"No?" the crown said, with the mild interest of a merchant hearing an opening bid.

"You said I want to be heard," Noor said, and now their voice found itself, found the market-day steadiness of it, the voice that had talked a lame horse into one more winter and a terrified boy into breathing. "You're right. I do. Desperately. My whole life. But you want me to be heard *because you'd be speaking through me*, and those aren't the same thing, and I think — I think you know they aren't. I think that's why you need a willing host. Because the unwilling ones fight you, and the willing ones *become* you, and then there's no one left inside to hear anything at all."

Around and around, the crown turned. Slower now.

"Also," Noor added, and here, despite everything, despite the abyss standing open at their feet, something in them quirked — the gentle, stubborn humor that had kept them alive through fifteen years of small coins and smaller charms — "you said we'd melt you down together at the end. Which means you already know how this ends for you. You're asking me to be your last host, not your best one. That's not inheritance. That's burial."

Silence. Real silence, for the first time — the chorus below seemed to hold its breath, ten thousand small voices waiting.

Then the crown laughed.

It was not a nice laugh, but neither was it cruel, and that was the strangest thing yet: there was relief in it, genuine and exhausted, the laugh of someone who has been carrying a lie so long that having it seen through feels like setting down luggage.

"Oh," it said, "oh, the *others* never saw that. Four hundred years and none of them ever asked what happens to me at the end. They only asked what they'd get." The turn quickened, just slightly. "Very well, beast-speaker. You will not wear me willingly. Then we do it the old way, or we finish it the new way. Choose quickly. My other self is climbing, and he is *hungry*, and every minute you spend being noble down here is a minute he spends eating the boy you came to save."

The lightless room brightened — no, darkened, no — the air itself tightened like a fist closing, and every copper root in every wall began, softly, to sing.

Noor reached out — not to the crown. Past it. Downward, through stone and root and rust, to the dam they had built behind their heart their whole life long.

They broke it.

* * *

The rush was enormous.

Every voice in the Spire poured through them at once — Marrow's dry and ancient patience, the ravens' sharp arithmetic, the hounds' confused grief, Pip's small terrified hope two hundred feet below, the horses, the mice in the walls, the moths beating against Sefa's cupped hands somewhere in the flood tunnels, all of it, all of it, a sound Noor had spent a lifetime hearing in whispers and now heard as weather.

And beneath it, deeper than all of it, something else. Something vast and starved and old, waking in the upper rooms, beginning to descend.

The crown rose from its cushion. Of its own accord, hanging in the dark, it began to move toward the door that had never existed — toward its other body, its hungry king — and Noor understood, with the clarity of the last fall before the ground, that the choice was no longer whether to refuse it.

The choice was what to do with everything they'd just set free.

"Marrow," they said aloud, and their voice rang through the whole Spire, through every ear that had ever trusted them. "Everyone. Listen to me."

The Spire turned. Below their feet, the great windlass groaned awake after fifteen years of idleness, and Noor Halloran — orphaned, hunted, offered the world and having declined it — opened their mouth and began to call every name.
