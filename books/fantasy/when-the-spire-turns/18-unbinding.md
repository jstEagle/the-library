## 18. Unbinding

The crown floated in the air between them, turning slowly, patient as a spider.

*Wear me,* it said — not in sound but in the marrow of Noor's bones, in the language they had spoken to horses and wrens and lame old cart-mares since childhood. *Rule kindly. I only want a willing host.*

And oh, it was a beautiful lie. Noor stood in the crown-room at the top of the Spire with the whole city spread below them like a spilled purse of lights, and for one long breath they saw it: a Vellamark where every stable-boy could ask the oxen what hurt; where the hounds were never set on their own kin; where no child was dragged into copper cages because their gift frightened men who had none. A world where beasts and outcasts were heard.

They saw themselves on a throne made kind by magic, wearing kindness like a coat that fit perfectly because it had been tailored from someone else's skin.

"No," said Noor Halloran.

The word came out small in all that vast stone silence. The crown did not move. Below them, faintly, through the floor, they could feel the crowd — Brannic and Sefa holding the stair, the freed prisoners stumbling upward, Pip somewhere in the dark vaults pulling cages open with his rust-singing hands, Old Marrow's thousand children running riot through the walls. The Spire shook with small voices.

"You misunderstand," the crown said, and now there was something new under its honeyed patience, something thin as ice over deep water. "I am not offering you power. I am offering you mercy. Refuse me, and I will drink this city dry before dawn. Accept me, and I will be gentle. I have been gentle for three hundred years."

"Three hundred years," Noor repeated. Their voice steadied as it went, the way it always did around animals, around frightened things. And the thing wearing the king's shape across the room *was* frightened — Noor could hear it now, hear the fear humming under the crown's voice like a fly in amber. Frightened things wanted to be soothed. Frightened things could be spoken to. "Three hundred years of eating slowly. Of keeping them alive so you could keep drinking. You call that gentle?"

"I call it survival."

"No." Noor took a step forward, and the crown turned to follow them like a sunflower following a very stubborn sun. "You call it *keeping*. Everything you are is keeping. Kept magic. Kept names. Kept kings. That's why you're hungry all the time. Power kept rots. My friend Pip says that about bread, and he's eleven, and he knows more than you do."

The entity laughed then — a terrible sound, the king's throat making a sound no king ever made, like a bell full of moths. "Then take it and give it away, if you must. It changes nothing. The binding at my heart is older than your grandmother's grandmother. You cannot unmake what kings built."

Noor looked at it. At the lattice of copper roots rising out of the floor behind the throne, thick as tree-trunks, running down through the whole spine of the Spire, down to the vaults where the gifted hung in their cages with their magic draining upward drop by drop. Fifteen years of bound magics. Three hundred years of stolen names.

And Noor thought of the market stall. Of luck-knots sold for small coin. Of a lame cart-horse named Buttercup who had told them exactly where her harness rubbed, and how much better she'd walked after Noor had whispered to the carter about it. Of the wren, freed, gone, returned. Every good thing Noor had ever done had been done by giving something away. A name spoken. A pain heard. Nothing kept.

"You're right," Noor said. "I can't unmake it."

The crown stilled. Waiting. It had waited three hundred years; it was very good at waiting.

"But I don't have to unmake it," Noor said. "I just have to say everyone's names."

* * *

Here is the truth about beast-speakers that no law ever understood, because no lawmaker had ever bothered to listen: the gift was never command. Anyone who thought you could *order* an animal had never actually met one. The gift was address. It was saying, *I see you, you are called this, you are here.* A horse walked easier when someone knew which shoulder ached. A hound hesitated when someone spoke its name instead of its orders. The whole art, the whole fifteen years of whispered stall-side bargains, had been nothing more than the radical act of calling things what they were.

The binding at the Spire's heart was made of names taken without asking. Noor could feel it now, standing close — could feel the stolen names packed into the copper roots like seeds in a granary, thousands upon thousands of them, each one a person whose magic had been siphoned up through the lattice and pooled here, in this room, in this crown, going bad the way kept things go bad.

"You'll scream yourself hollow," the entity said, and the honey had curdled entirely now. "There are ten thousand names in me. More. You will spend your voice before you spend a hundredth of them, and I will still be here."

"Probably," Noor agreed pleasantly. "But see, I've got help."

They put both hands flat against the nearest copper root. It was cold and it hummed and somewhere far below it, Noor knew, Sefa was reading moths by candlelight even now, and Pip was singing to locks until they blushed open, and Brannic — one-armed, oath-burdened, gentle-eyed Brannic — was holding the door with his falconer's patience, letting people pass him toward freedom one by one.

"Marrow," Noor said aloud, and felt the words go down through the root like water down a drain. "Old Marrow. Matriarch of the drowned stair. Mother of nine hundred children with pink tails. Can you hear me?"

From the walls, from the dark, from everywhere at once: a rustle. Then a voice, thin and dry and utterly unimpressed, arriving through the copper itself as though the metal were simply another burrow. *"We hear you, market-child. We have been listening since you fell down our stairs. Rats are excellent listeners. It is most of what we do."*

"There are names in these roots," Noor said. "Stolen ones. I'm going to start speaking them. When you hear one, carry it. Pass it down. Give it back to whoever it belongs to — or if they're gone, give it to whoever needs a name tonight. Can your children do that?"

A pause. Then, with the particular relish of an old woman who has waited a long time for permission: *"Child, we have been stealing crumbs off this crown's table for three hundred years. It will be an honor to steal the table."*

Noor closed their eyes. Opened their mouth. And began.

* * *

The first name was a woman's — *Hesba Tull, knot-worker, binder of nets, whose hands remembered every rope they'd ever touched* — and the moment Noor spoke it, something in the copper root shivered loose, a bright thread sliding free and racing downward through the Spire, and far below, in a flood-tunnel crowded with refugees, Hesba Tull gasped as her own magic poured back into her like a tide returning.

The second name was a boy's. The third was a goat's, because the bindings had never cared whether the gifted walked on two legs or four.

*"Stop,"* said the crown.

Noor didn't stop. They planted their feet the way they'd taught themselves to stand in gales at market, and they spoke faster, name after name after name, and each one cost them — Oh, how it cost them, each name dragging warmth up their throat like swallowed coals, their voice fraying at the edges — but Marrow's children carried the load too, catching names in the dark and passing them hand to paw to wing, nine hundred rats and a freed wren and, God bless them, a pair of palace ravens who had decided that whatever the ravens' price had bought, they intended to get value for it.

*"STOP,"* the entity screamed, and stopped pretending to be a king.

It came apart as it lunged. The tall shape of the false monarch unraveled like wet rope, and underneath it there was no body at all, only the crown, only the crown grown vast and hungry and suddenly *afraid*, tendrils of shadow reaching for Noor across the crown-room floor. One wrapped around Noor's ankle, cold as a debt.

Noor looked down at it. Still speaking. *Odo Fenwick, lamplighter, who talked to flames and the flames talked back.* The tendril flinched.

"You cannot," the crown hissed, gathering itself, towering, filling half the room with darkness shot through with copper light. "I am older than your city. I wore the first king of Vellamark when his bones were new. I have eaten dynasties. I am patient, I am eternal, I am—"

"You're lonely," said Noor Halloran.

The darkness stopped.

"That's all any of this has ever been, hasn't it?" Noor's voice was wrecked, barely a rasp, but they leaned harder on the root and kept going, because the names kept coming, and Marrow's children kept carrying them, and somewhere below a crowd of freed voices was beginning — Noor could feel it through the floor, through their boots, through their broken heart — to *sing the names back*. "Three hundred years of wearing other people because you couldn't stand being nobody. Well. Here's the thing nobody ever told you, because you never let anybody close enough to tell you anything."

Noor looked the ancient thing dead in what might have been its face.

"Being somebody isn't something you take. It's something people give you. And nobody's giving you anything, because you never once asked."

* * *

The last hour Noor never fully remembered afterward. Sefa would describe pieces of it: how the whole Spire lit up from the inside like a lantern, every copper vein flushing gold as the stolen magic ran home; how the crown-entity screamed with a thousand throats, every voice it had ever worn, kings and queens and lamplighters and one bewildered goat; how the rats swarmed the walls in rivers, carrying names in their teeth like grain.

What Noor remembered was simpler. Cold floor. Hot throat. Names, names, names — *Maren Ostler, tanner; Julek, who sang to pigeons; the queen's grey hawk, whose name had been Sorrel, whom Noor had held as it died fifteen years ago and never told anyone, forgive me, forgive me, fly home* —

—and the moment that last one left their mouth, fifteen years of guilt went with it, torn out clean as a splinter, and Noor sobbed once in the middle of the greatest working in Vellamark's history, and kept going.

The crown fought. Of course it did. It threw shadows like spears and lies like nets — *you'll die, they'll forget you, wear me, WEAR ME* — and Noor's knees buckled twice, and both times something caught them: once Brannic's remaining arm, hooked under theirs, holding steady as a mews-beam; once a small rust-stained hand, Pip arrived at last from the vaults, soot-faced and furious and singing through his tears.

"It's got my friends' names!" Pip shouted at it, defiant, ridiculous, magnificent. "Give! Them! BACK!"

And finally — finally — there were no names left to speak.

The crown hung alone in the center of the room, stripped bare, no bigger than it had ever truly been. Just metal. Just hunger. Just an old, old loneliness with nowhere left to hide.

It turned toward Noor one last time. And it did not rage. That was the worst part. It sounded, in the end, almost small.

*"Who will I be?"*

Noor, kneeling on the cold stones, voice destroyed, thought of Buttercup the cart-horse walking easy. Thought of a wren's wings beating away toward morning.

"Nobody," they rasped. "Same as the rest of us, before somebody loved us. Go find out who that is."

The crown came apart like mist in sunlight. It did not scream, in the end. It sighed — three hundred years of held breath leaving all at once — and the gold dust of it drifted down through the crown-room floor, down through the roots, down into the city, and everywhere it landed, small strange things bloomed: flowers opening out of season, locks unlatching themselves, every hound in Vellamark lifting its head at midnight and howling one long, glad note.

The Spire groaned. Shifted. Began, very slowly, to turn.

* * *

Afterward there was quiet, and blood in Noor's mouth, and Brannic's arm around them, and Pip chattering a mile a minute about everything Noor had missed ("and THEN the cage just OPENED, it heard me, Noor, it HEARD me"), and Sefa sitting down hard on the steps and laughing the shaky laugh of a woman whose contradictory omens had finally, mercifully, resolved into one answer.

And then, from below — from the vaults, from the deepest cage, from the lattice of copper now gone dark and dead and merely metal — a sound that stopped them all.

A cough. Human. Ancient. Alive.

Footsteps on the stair, uneven, disbelieving. And Brannic went white as tallow, gripping the rail, staring past Noor toward the door as if he'd seen a ghost — and perhaps he had, or worse than a ghost, because through the doorway, blinking in torchlight, hollow-cheeked and dreaming and awake for the first time in fifteen years, came the true king of Vellamark.

He looked at Brannic. His ruined face creased slowly into recognition.

"Falconer," the king said, in a voice like a door long unopened. "Brannic. How long—" He swayed, caught himself on the dead copper root, and looked around at the shattered crown-room, the freed prisoners, the rats lining the walls in silent ranks, the four ragged strangers who had unmade three centuries of hunger. Understanding began, horribly, to arrive.

"How long," the king asked again, quieter, "has it been wearing me? And what —" his eyes went to Brannic, and broke, "*what did it do in my name?*"
