## 19. What Waking Costs

Brannic had held a king's wrist before. He had done it a hundred times in another life — checking the pulse of a man who flew his falcons too long and slept too little, a king who laughed like a boy and swore like a dockhand when a gyrfalcon slipped the jess. He had never once thought he would be holding that same wrist fifteen years later, in a copper-choked vault beneath the Spire, waiting to see if the man would wake at all.

The lattice was dead now. Whatever Noor had done in the crown-room above — whatever names they had spoken, whatever terrible arithmetic they had performed with their own voice — the copper roots that webbed the vault had gone grey as old ash. They crumbled at a touch. Pip's cage stood open, its door hanging crooked like a broken wing.

And on the bare stone where the lattice had held him, the King of Vellamark lay sleeping like any beggar off the Tallow Market floor.

He was thinner than Brannic remembered. That was the first cruelty of it. The second was that he looked *young* — younger than Brannic himself now, though they had been near enough the same age once, two men climbing the same hill from different sides. Fifteen years had been taken from Aldric Vellamore and given to something else, and the theft showed. His hair was brown still, not greyed by worry or war councils or the slow rot of ruling. His hands were uncalloused. He looked like a man who had been set down mid-sentence and forgotten.

Sefa knelt across from Brannic, moth-grey shawl pooled around her knees, her palms hovering an inch above the king's chest the way she hovered them over a dying moth she meant to save.

"His thread is thin," she said quietly. "But it holds. It's *his*, too — I'd know if it weren't. There's nothing wearing it anymore." She looked up at Brannic, and her eyes were kind in the way that frightened him most. "You should be the one here when he opens them."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the one who's been carrying him for fifteen years without knowing it. Best set the weight down while someone can see you do it."

Brannic grunted. It was all he had. Around them, the vault was filling with the small chaos of liberation — rats running messages along the walls, freed prisoners being walked gently up toward daylight by hounds who no longer obeyed anyone but themselves, Pip sitting cross-legged beside the ruined cages telling every bird and beast within earshot exactly what he thought of copper roots. The boy's voice was hoarse. He hadn't stopped talking since they'd cut him free, and Brannic suspected he wouldn't for a week.

Old Marrow was not among them. Nobody had said so yet, but everyone moved around that fact like water around a stone. Brannic had seen the rat-matriarch go down under a warden's boot in the riot, seen a dozen of her children carry her body away into the dark with a solemnity that had made his throat close. There would be time for grieving. There always was; grief kept better than bread.

The king breathed. Thin, slow, but even.

Brannic waited.

* * *

It took until the grey light of false-dawn filtered down through the shattered vents — dawn reaching even here, which felt like a promise — before Aldric Vellamore opened his eyes.

They were blue eyes. Ordinary blue eyes, bloodshot and confused, blinking at the vault's dark ceiling as though it were the canopy of a bed he didn't recognize.

"Brannic?" His voice came out rusted. "Why are you crying, man? And where in the nine hells is my arm—"

"It's *your* arm missing, Majesty, not mine," Brannic managed, and then the laugh broke out of him sideways, half sob, the way laughter does after fifteen years underground. "You've been asleep."

"I haven't been—" The king tried to sit up. Failed. Tried again, and Brannic put his one hand behind the royal back and helped, feeling the spine through the ruined silk of a court shirt that should have been replaced a decade and a half ago. "I was in the mews. There was a storm coming, and the gyrfalcon — she hates storms, she'd been screaming all afternoon, and I went to settle her and — and there was a door in the wall that wasn't a door—"

His voice stopped. His ordinary blue eyes went around the vault: the ash-copper roots, the open cages, the rats watching from the walls with bright black eyes, Sefa with her shawl and her patient face, Pip staring openly with his mouth hanging open because he had, apparently, only just worked out who this man was.

"No," the king said. "No, that was — that was yesterday. Wasn't it?"

"Fifteen years ago," Brannic said.

The silence that followed was the longest of Brannic's life, and he had spent a fair number of long silences down here, holding truths in his mouth like hot stones.

Then the king said, very quietly, "Then who has been wearing my crown?"

And Brannic told him.

* * *

He told it plainly, because there was no other way to tell it. The thing in the Spire. The harvest. The hunt for the gifted, the children in the cages, the copper roots drinking magic upward like a tree drinking rain. He told him about Noor Halloran, who had broken it. About a rat-matriarch who had died buying the minutes that made the breaking possible. About a boy who spoke to rust and had been caged in this very room.

He did not soften it. He owed the king truth the way he owed the city truth, and he had learned, at last, what hoarding either one cost.

Aldric listened without moving. When Brannic reached the part about the proclamation — silver for names, neighbors selling neighbors — the king closed his eyes. When Brannic reached the part about the cages, his hands began to shake, and did not stop.

"The wardens knew," he whispered. "My wardens. Men I appointed. They knew and they hunted children."

"They were hunted too, some of them," Sefa said gently. "Fear makes poor instruments, Majesty. But yes. They knew."

"And in your name," Brannic said. "That's the stone of it, sire. Every warrant bore your seal. Every cage was yours on paper."

"I know what my seal looks like," the king snapped — and then, immediately, wearily: "Forgive me. You've not deserved snapping. You've deserved—" He looked at Brannic's empty sleeve, and something moved behind his face like weather. "Where is my arm, Brannic?"

"You lost it the night you disappeared. In the mews." Brannic swallowed. This was the part he had rehearsed for fifteen years in the dark of his own skull, and none of the rehearsals survived contact. "There was a thing wearing a servant's face. It took your arm getting you out of my reach. And then it looked at me, and it spoke with your voice, and it told me that if I ever said a word of what I saw, it would come back for the rest of me. And I believed it." His jaw worked. "I ran, sire. I let the whole city believe its king had gone mad and vanished, because I was afraid. Everything that grew from that night — everything — grew from my silence."

The king was quiet for a long moment.

"Do you know what I remember last?" he said finally. "Before the door? I remember thinking the falconer would handle the storm. That Brannic would see to it, because Brannic always sees to it. I went through a door in the wall rather than face a thunderstorm, because I trusted you with the things I feared." He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, cracked down the middle. "So don't apologize for being afraid. I've been afraid for fifteen years inside my own skull, in dreams I couldn't wake from. Fear's just weather, man. What matters is what you built in it afterward. And you—" he gestured, shaking hands and all, at the open cages, the rats, the boy, the grey dawn coming down through the vents— "you appear to have built quite a lot."

Pip made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a hiccup. From the wall, a rat chittered something that sounded suspiciously like agreement.

"There's more you need to hear," Brannic said. "And it's worse."

"Of course it is," said the king. "Tell me anyway."

* * *

They brought him up through the Mews at first light, because Brannic refused to let him wake to freedom in a hole. Let him see sky first. Let him see the rookery — his rookery, the mews he had loved, still standing, the ravens regarding him from their perches with the frank insolence of birds who have outlived their keeper's usefulness.

Let him see the city.

From the Mews' high gallery, Vellamark spread out below in the early gold: the markets reopening, smoke rising honest and straight from ten thousand chimneys, people in the streets — and not hiding. Gifted folk walking in daylight with their charms showing, their familiars at heel, their heads up. A cart-horse with a braided mane standing outside a rebuilt stall while a slight figure with ink-stained fingers talked to it in plain view of everyone, laughing at something the horse had said.

"That's Noor," Brannic said. "Halloran. Beast-speaker. The one who broke it."

"They broke the crown-thing," the king said slowly, "and they're — selling luck-knots?"

"They're selling luck-knots," Brannic confirmed. "Best ones in the city, if you believe the horse."

For a moment — just a moment — the old warmth flickered behind the king's eyes, the boy who had laughed like a dockhand. "What I wouldn't give to hear what that horse is saying." Then it faded, and he turned to look at the Spire itself, rising over the city with its great turning vanes already groaning against the morning wind — someone had rigged them as a mill, Brannic explained, grinding grain, of all the humble miracles — and the warmth went out entirely.

"Show me the ledgers," the king said. "The warrants. Everything signed in my name. I want to see all of it."

"Majesty—" Sefa began.

"All of it," he repeated, and his voice was steady, and Brannic recognized the steadiness for what it was: the kind you build on purpose, plank by plank, over a pit.

* * *

He read for three days.

They gave him a room in the Tallow Market — a good one, warm, with actual windows onto the flood-tunnels where the exiles had strung lamps like festival lights. Sefa brought him tea. Pip brought him, inexplicably, a collection of interesting rust samples, and the king examined each one with grave attention as though receiving foreign ambassadors, which won Pip forever. Rats came and went through the wall-gap bringing news, and the king learned to nod at them politely, which was more grace than most crowned heads manage with living subjects.

And he read. Warrants. Ledgers. Lists of names — the gifted taken, the ones sold for silver by their neighbors, the ones who had starved in the undercity while their king slept. He read until his hands stopped shaking, and then he read more, and Brannic sat watch in the corner and understood that he was watching a man walk deliberately through his own burning house, touching every beam.

On the third evening, the king set down the final ledger and said, "Bring them. Everyone. The Halloran person, the moth-reader, the rust-boy, whoever leads these people now. I'll speak to them all at once, in the market square, in daylight."

"You don't have to abdicate," Brannic said. It burst out of him before he could stop it, and he hated how much hope was tangled in it. "Sire — the council will take you back. The people will *want* to take you back. A king returned from death, delivered from a monster — they'll call it a miracle. You could rebuild. You could make it right from the throne. Undo the laws, pardon the wardens worth pardoning, hang the ones who aren't—"

"Hang them," the king repeated softly. He looked at his hands. "Fifteen years ago I would have said exactly that, and meant it, and slept well after." He rose, and crossed to the window, and looked out at the lamplights in the tunnels. "Do you know what I learned in the dark, Brannic? All those years dreaming inside that thing's hunger? I learned what it thinks power is. It thinks power is a mouth. Something you feed. Something that eats and eats and calls the eating *order*." He turned. "And I look at the machinery of my own crown — the hunts, the seals, the warrants, the whole beautiful machine that turns fear into law — and I see the same mouth, only slower. Gentler teeth. If I sit that throne again, even kindly, even *well* — I'm asking this city to trust the mouth not to bite. Why should it?"

"Because it's *you* now. Not the thing. You."

"Because it's me now," the king agreed. "Which is why I'm the one who gets to choose how the story ends. If I rebuild the crown, then someday — maybe not me, maybe some well-meaning heir, maybe some frightened council — the mouth wakes again. Machinery doesn't die, Brannic. It waits. So." He straightened, and for a moment Brannic saw the king entire, the one from the mews, storm-fear and all. "I'll stand in the square tomorrow. I'll tell them everything — what was done in my name, what it cost, whose deaths bought their morning. And then I will lay the crown on the steps of the Spire-mill and leave it there. Let it turn grain. Let it feed people. That's the only work a crown was ever fit for."

"And then?"

"And then I'm going to learn to keep bees," said Aldric Vellamore, former King of Vellamark, with complete sincerity. "Or possibly goats. I understand goats hold grudges, which seems fair. I owe the world some humility and I intend to pay it in livestock."

Brannic laughed — really laughed, the first time in fifteen years, and it hurt like setting down something heavy that had grown into the shoulder.

* * *

The square the next morning was fuller than any coronation. Noor Halloran stood at the front with the cart-horse's nose resting on their shoulder like a claim. Sefa held a moth cupped in her hands like a small lantern. Pip sat on Brannic's shoulders, which was undignified and accurate.

The king came out plain-clothed, no guard, no herald, and told them everything. His voice carried. Some wept. Some shouted — a woman whose brother's name was on the third ledger page shouted longest, and the king took it, standing in it, letting it land, answering nothing except "yes, and I am sorry, and it was real."

Then he walked to the base of the turning Spire, and set the crown of Vellamark down on the worn stone step, and left it there in the wind and the grain-dust while the great vanes turned overhead.

And that was when Brannic saw the warden — one of the old hunt's captains, disarmed, unpardoned, standing at the square's edge with a face like a shut door — turn away into the crowd. And saw, moving to follow her, three others in warden's grey, walking not toward the light of the square but away from it, together, unhurried, like men with somewhere to be.

Brannic touched Noor's elbow and nodded after them.

Noor watched the grey backs vanish into the crowd, and their hand tightened on the horse's mane.

"Some of them aren't finished," Brannic murmured.

"No," Noor said quietly. "Neither are we."
