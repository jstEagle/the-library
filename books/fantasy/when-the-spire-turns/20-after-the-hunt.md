## 20. After the Hunt

The morning after the Spire turned, Sefa climbed the Drowned Stair for the first time in eleven years.

Her legs remembered the way even if her heart didn't — up through the dripping dark, past the moss-slick landings where she'd once counted her steps like rosary beads on the night she'd fled below, and out, at last, into a square that smelled of rain and bread and something else entirely: open air without fear in it.

She stood blinking at the light like a fool. A moth had followed her up from the tunnels — a little grey one, riding her shoulder like a passenger who hadn't bought a ticket — and it too seemed stunned by the sun, wings opening and closing slowly, as if applauding.

"Well," Sefa said to it. "Here we are."

Here they were. Vellamark's great square, the one the criers had used to read the hunt's proclamation, was full of people who did not know what to do with their hands. Wardens stood in a ragged line near the fountain, their silver badges already pried off and lying in a small shameful pile at their feet. Some of them wept. One of them — a broad woman with a broken nose — kept opening her mouth as though to shout an order and then remembering there was no one left to obey her, or perhaps no orders left worth shouting.

And everywhere, *everywhere*, the gifted were coming out of doorways and cellars and gaps in the world that Safa hadn't known existed until this week. An old man whose shadow moved a half-second behind him. Twin girls who could find any lost thing, standing shyly by the fountain while people lined up to hand them missing keys, missing rings, missing husbands' letters. A fishwife whose hands made the harbor water calm, wringing them on her apron and looking, for the first time in fifteen years, like a woman with nothing to hide.

Sefa sat down on the fountain's rim, because her knees had opinions about all this standing, and let the grey moth walk out onto her palm.

"You first," she told it. "What do you see?"

It tilted its antennae toward the east — toward the Spire — and then, after a long moment, folded its wings flat against its body. Resting. Not reading omens. Just resting.

Even the moths were off duty today.

* * *

The reckoning took three days, and it was messier than anyone wanted it to be.

Brannic had warned them of that. "Crowns don't fall clean," he said, the morning of the second day, standing in what had been the warden-captain's office and was now, apparently, a meeting room, judging by the number of people crammed into it. "They shatter, and everyone gets cut picking up the pieces."

He said it with his single arm crossed over his chest and his falconer's stillness, which he wore like armor even now. Beside him, Noor sat perched on the edge of the desk — actually *on* the desk, boots swinging, which would have gotten them flogged a month ago and now only got them a fond look from Old Marrow.

Except Marrow wasn't beside them anymore.

Sefa felt the absence every time she glanced sideways, like a tooth gone from a familiar row. She kept looking anyway. Grief does that. It makes you check the wound to make sure it's real.

"Say it plainly," said the broken-nosed warden woman, whose name was Captain Ives and who had, three days ago, unlocked the vault doors herself rather than fire on the crowd. "Some of us want amnesty. Some of us think every warden should stand trial. You're the ones the city's listening to, so tell us."

Noor looked around the room — at Brannic, at Sefa, at Pip sitting very quiet in the corner with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he never drank — and then back at Ives.

"Neither," Noor said. "Trials, yes. For the ones who enjoyed it. We'll name names; you'll name names; a council of citizens will hear it. But the boy wardens who hesitated? The hounds' handlers who slipped them extra meat? The ones who looked away?" They shrugged. "Forgiveness isn't mine to give wholesale. It belongs to the people who were hurt. Ask *them*."

Ives stared. "That'll take years."

"Good," said Brannic. "Years are honest. Amnesty is just a faster way of forgetting, and I've seen what this city does when it forgets." His voice went flat as slate. "Fifteen years of it."

The vote, such as it was, went Noor's way. Of course it did. That was the thing nobody had quite explained to Noor yet — that they had stopped being a charm-seller somewhere between the riot and the unbinding, and had become instead the person everyone watched when hard questions needed answering. Sefa saw it weigh on them. She saw them carry it anyway.

* * *

On the third day, they buried Old Marrow.

Not buried, precisely — Marrow had left instructions, delivered over the years to approximately four hundred rats and one very patient pigeon, and the pigeons proved reliable. Her body was carried at dawn to the garden behind the old queen's chapel, where the roses grew wild, and laid beneath the largest bush while a congregation of rats sat in perfect silence in the hedgerows like small grey mourners in their Sunday best.

Sefa had never attended a rat funeral before. She found she couldn't stop crying, which embarrassed her, and then she noticed half the humans present were crying too, and the embarrassment dissolved into something warmer and sadder.

Pip spoke. He stood at the head of the grave in his too-big coat, thirteen years old and suddenly older than that, his rust-speaker's hands scarred where the copper roots had drunk from him in the vaults.

"She found me when I was six," Pip said. His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "Living under the tannery. Eating candle wax. She said—" and here he did an impression, a scolding tilt of the chin that was so exactly Marrow that several rats chittered at once, "—she said, 'Boy, you speak to rust, which means you'll never starve, but if you keep eating wax you'll die stupid.' And then she fed me. For seven years." He knelt and put something on the grave — Sefa squinted and saw it was a key, green with age, one of the first pieces of metal he'd ever spoken to. "She wasn't soft. She was better than soft. She was *steady*."

The rats came forward, one by one, each leaving a small treasure on the mound — a button, a bead, a coin, a thimble. By the end the grave glittered like a dragon's hoard in miniature, and Sefa thought: *this is what wealth is, actually. This exact thing.*

Afterward, walking back through the waking streets, Brannic fell into step beside her. He didn't say anything for two streets. He was good at silence, Brannic. He treated it like a craft.

"The moths," he finally said. "Did they show you anything? Before."

Sefa considered lying, decided against it — she'd done enough hiding below ground to last two lifetimes. "They showed me everything contradicting itself. Victory and loss in the same wingbeat. I told you that, the night before."

"And you went anyway."

"We all went anyway."

"Marrow knew," Brannic said quietly. "She told me the evening before, in rat-sign. She said, 'Tell the big one I'm not coming back, and tell him not to make a monument of it.'" He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "So naturally I'm building a monument in my head. Granite. Very tasteful."

"Granite's wrong for her," Sefa said. "She'd want something useful. A bench. Somewhere people could sit and argue."

Brannic was quiet another moment. Then: "A bench," he agreed, and Sefa understood that somewhere in the city's future, there would be a bench, and that it would be argued upon constantly, and that this would be exactly right.

* * *

The gifted kept emerging.

They came to the Tallow Market — which was no longer only below; someone had strung lanterns up the Drowned Stair and propped the door at the top open like a mouth that had finally decided to smile — and they came shyly, in ones and twos, holding their gifts out like wounded birds.

A woman who could warm any room with her presence, who hadn't let herself feel it in years for fear of the wardens noticing the houses she passed glowing faintly on cold nights. A stonemason who heard what buildings wanted to become. A child, maybe five, who made flowers bloom out of season and whose mother wept openly in front of strangers because for five years she had taught the girl to crush every bud before it opened, quick, so no one would see.

Sefa set up her stall in the market's new above-ground row — between Noor's charms (now displayed openly, luck-knots beside little carved tokens labeled honestly: FOR SPEAKING WITH ANIMALS, NO REFUNDS) and a potter's wheel that hummed faintly, the clay shaping itself under the potter's humming breath.

Her own trade had changed too. People brought moths to her now the way they'd once brought coins to moneylenders. She read for love and for grief and for "will the harvest hold," and she gave the same answer she always gave, which was that omens were weather, not verdicts, and that knowing the storm was coming had never once stopped anyone from choosing to bring an umbrella or dance in it.

But some evenings a moth would land, and she would look at it, and it would look at her, and both of them would decide there was nothing needing reading tonight. Those evenings were the best ones. She was learning that a gift unused is also a kind of use. Rest. The grey moth on her windowsill, wings folded, doing absolutely nothing, was teaching her more about freedom than any omen ever had.

* * *

Pip did not heal quickly, and Sefa learned not to pretend otherwise.

The copper roots had fed on him for nine days. What they took wasn't blood or bone — it was stranger than that. He came back from the vaults with his gift intact but changed, the way a river changes course after a flood. Where he'd once coaxed rust gently, chatting with hinges and locks like old neighbors, now metal spoke to *him* first, urgently, all of it at once — every nail in a wall clamoring, every coin in a purse shouting its history. He wore wool gloves indoors and out. He flinched at the sound of the market's pots and pans.

And he woke screaming, some nights. From the cages. From the sound of the draining, which he described once, haltingly, as "a note that never ends, played on everyone at once."

Noor took to sleeping in the same room, not touching, just present, the way you'd stable a frightened horse beside a steady one. Some nights Noor spoke softly to the house mice, asking them to keep the walls quiet. Some nights it worked.

One gray afternoon Sefa found Pip at the market's edge, standing before the blacksmith's abandoned forge, gloved hands in his pockets, staring.

"They won't shut up," he said, without turning. "All this iron. It remembers the fires. It wants to work again and there's nobody telling it no, and it's *loud*, Sefa, it's so loud—"

"So talk back," Sefa said.

He looked at her, and there was something raw in his face. "You don't understand. Before, I asked. Now it *begs*. Like I'm—" His jaw worked. "Like I'm the crown. Like I'm the thing on the throne, and everything metal is down in my vaults."

Ah. There it was. Sefa sat down on an overturned bucket, because these conversations went better at eye level, and patted the bucket beside her.

"Pip. Come sit. Look at my hands." He sat, wary. She held them out, palms up, the way she did when a moth landed. "When I read, I'm not the crown either. The moth gives me what it gives. I can't force it. And here's the secret nobody tells you about gifts—" she leaned in, conspiratorial, "—they're not thrones. They're conversations. The difference between a tyrant and a speaker is that a speaker knows the other party can leave."

Pip frowned. "Metal can't leave."

"It can go silent on you. Believe me, rust has ignored me plenty." She nudged him. "Try it. Not commanding. *Asking.* 'What do you want to be?'"

He pulled off one glove with his teeth, slow as a man disarming a trap, and laid his bare hand on the cold forge. Sefa felt the air change — a hum, low, like a struck bell remembering its note. Pip's eyes went wide.

"It says... it says it doesn't want to be a forge anymore." His voice went wondering. "It wants to be a bell."

"Well," said Sefa, after a moment. "There's your answer. And there's the city's next argument, sorted."

They laughed — both of them, surprised by it, the laughter coming up out of them like water finding a crack. It wasn't fixed, whatever was broken in Pip. But it was, for one afternoon, *accompanied*.

* * *

Captain Ives came to the Tallow Market herself, on the fifth day, alone and out of uniform, and stood at the bottom of the stair looking like a woman reporting to her own execution.

"I want to give testimony," she said. "About the ones who enjoyed it. Warden-Captain Reeve. Three others. I kept lists." She held up a notebook, swollen with water damage and years. "I told myself I was waiting for a safe moment. There was never a safe moment. That's not courage, what I did. It's bookkeeping. But it's what I have."

Noor took the notebook with both hands. "Bookkeeping saved lives, Captain. Someone will want to know who to forgive and who to try. You've written the map." A pause. "The hounds. Are they—?"

"With me. All six." Ives's broken nose wrinkled, and for the first time Sefa saw her nearly smile. "They refused new handlers. Made rather a scene about it. Bit the quartermaster. I'm told that's your doing."

"It's their doing," Noor said. "I only asked."

From the stairwell above came the sound of claws on stone, and six hounds descended with the dignity of visiting nobility, arranged themselves around Ives like an honor guard, and dared anyone present to comment.

Old Marrow would have had opinions. Sefa felt the ache and the warmth arrive together, braided, the way they'd been braided all week: mourning and dawn in the same strand, impossible to pull apart, maybe not meant to be.

* * *

On the seventh evening, the whole strange family gathered on the rooftop above the Tallow Market's new door — Noor and Brannic and Sefa and Pip, plus the potter, plus the twin girls who'd wandered down from the square out of curiosity and been adopted within the hour, plus a delegation of rats in the chimney who considered themselves invited.

Below them, the market glowed. Above them, the Spire stood against the sunset — and it was moving, slow and groaning and magnificent, its great vanes turning for the first time in living memory, grinding the grain barges were already hauling in from upriver. Flour by week's end, the millwrights promised. Bread by the next. The city had taken its tallest symbol of fear and made it feed them. Sefa thought Marrow would have found that *deeply* funny.

"Read for us," Pip said suddenly. "One moth. Something true about tomorrow."

Sefa looked around at their faces — hopeful, wary, grieving, lit gold by the last sun — and reached into the jar where a small brown moth had been resting, patient as bread dough. She lifted it to the fading light.

Its wings opened.

Sefa read, and what she read made her go very still.

"What?" Noor demanded. "Bad?"

"No," Sefa said slowly. "Strange. It says tomorrow brings a visitor. Plain-dressed. Alone. Carrying something he doesn't know how to put down." She looked up, and met Noor's eyes. "It says the visitor is going to ask *you* something, Noor Halloran. And it says — and I swear this is what the wings say, word for word — '*the answer matters more than the question*.'"

Above them the Spire turned. Below them the market murmured. And Noor, who had spent fifteen years hiding, who had unbound a crown and inherited a responsibility, looked west toward the palace road and said, very quietly:

"Then I suppose we'd best open the stall early."
