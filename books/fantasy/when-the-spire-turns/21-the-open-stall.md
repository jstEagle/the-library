## 21. The Open Stall

The first stall Noor ever built, at fourteen years old, had been a masterpiece of small dishonesties.

A plank across two barrels. A scrap of awning stolen—no, *borrowed*—from a fishmonger who never missed it. Charms laid out in careful rows: luck-knots braided from ordinary hemp, river-stones painted with symbols that meant nothing at all, little clay birds with holes in their bellies for burning sweetgrass. Harmless things. *Innocent* things. And beneath every one of them, like water under ice, the truth that no charm Noor sold had ever held a drop of magic. The magic was in Noor's hands, and it stayed there, folded small and silent, all day, every day, for fifteen years.

The new stall took three days to build and required no dishonesty whatsoever.

It stood where the old one had, at the crook of Cooper's Row where the morning sun came slantwise off the canal and warmed the cobbles until they smelled like bread. But it was taller now, wide enough for two people to work behind, with proper posts sunk in proper holes, and an awning Sefa had dyed herself—a deep moth-wing grey shot through with threads of copper that caught the light and threw little flickering coins of it onto the ground below, so that customers stepping up to the stall did so through a scatter of dancing light.

Pip had hammered every nail. He'd insisted on it, standing on a crate with the hammer gripped in both hands, tongue between his teeth, and when he drove the last one home he'd looked up at the awning and said, very quietly, "It doesn't rust."

Noor had understood immediately. Nothing Pip built would ever be allowed to rust again; he spoke to metal now openly, asked it politely to hold its temper, and the metal, flattered by the attention, obliged. The nails in this stall would outlive them all.

And on the front board, where once Noor had carved *CHARMS — SMALL COIN* in careful, guarded letters, Brannic had burned a new sign with his good hand and a poker heated in Marrow's memory-honor brazier:

**HALLORAN & FAMILY — CHARMWORK, BEAST-SPEAKING, HONEST PRICES**

Beneath it, smaller: *No job too strange. Yes, we really talk to animals.*

"It's too much," Noor had said, the first time they saw it. "People will throw things."

"People were throwing things anyway," Brannic said, shrugging his single shoulder. "Better they should throw things at a sign worth reading."

That had been a month ago.

Nobody had thrown anything yet.

* * *

The morning crowd arrived the way morning crowds do, all at once, like water finding a drain in reverse.

There was Widow Cassel, who came every day now for a luck-knot she did not need and paid for with gossip instead of coin, because she had decided that what her kitchen lacked was a cat who could *tell her things*, and Noor's negotiations with the gray tom behind the tannery had secured her exactly that, at the price of three sardines weekly, which the widow considered a bargain and the cat considered tribute rightly owed. There was the cooper's boy, who brought his father's lame dog to be spoken with and went away having learned that the dog was not lame at all but *sulking*, over an incident involving a ham bone and an accusation of theft that had actually been committed by the dog's own brother. There was a steady trickle of people who came less to buy than simply to stand near the stall and listen, because word had gone round Vellamark that if you came at midmorning you might hear Noor Halloran talking to somebody's horse about its feelings, and there was something in the watching of it that people seemed to need right now—the way you might need, after a long illness, just to sit in a sunny room.

And there was Bramble.

Bramble had been the lame cart-horse, back when being understood was a thing Noor had to steal in whispers between transactions. She was lame no longer—or rather, she was still stiff in the near hind leg when the weather turned cold, but she had stopped pretending otherwise, which is its own kind of cure. She was enormous and bay-colored and opinionated, and she had appointed herself, without consultation, the stall's second employee.

Her duties were as follows: hauling lumber (done), hauling the stall itself into position each dawn (done, while complaining), standing at the end of Cooper's Row during market hours looking magnificent (ongoing), and correcting customers who attempted to haggle dishonestly.

"No," she said now, in the deep bell-tone voice that only Noor and a handful of others could hear, as a fish merchant tried to talk Sefa down on a storm-charm. "He sold that same charm to the harbormaster Tuesday for twice your offer and lied about the weather coming."

"He did *what*?" said the fish merchant, who could not understand a word and was therefore deeply unsettled to find both Noor and Sefa turning to look at him with identical expressions.

"Tuesday," Noor said mildly. "Twice the price. And you told him the storms would pass by week's end, which Bramble says is also a lie, because she can smell the barometer better than any alchemist in this city."

The fish merchant went pink, paid full price, and left quickly. Bramble made a sound like a horse laughing, which is to say she snorted and then looked insufferably pleased with herself.

"You enjoy that far too much," Noor told her.

"I was a dray horse for eleven years," Bramble said. "I pulled carts for men who beat me and I was *grateful* for the beating because it meant they noticed me. You will forgive me if I savor a career as a professional gossip."

Sefa laughed so hard she knocked over her moth-jar.

* * *

Midday brought the strangest customer.

He came alone, which was the first unusual thing. Plain brown wool coat, scuffed boots, no guard, no banner, no crown—which was the second unusual thing, because everyone in Vellamark knew the king's face even when the king wore nothing but plain brown wool, and everyone in Vellamark had spent fifteen years learning to pretend not to know it.

But Aldric, who had been king for four days before the Spire ate him and was king again now only in the way that a house is still called a house after the fire, had abdicated publicly, loudly, and with what witnesses described as *visible relief*. He was not the king. He was, per his own insistence, "a man named Aldric who is thinking very hard about becoming a gardener." The city council—cobbled together from guildmasters, undercity representatives, and, after some negotiation conducted largely in squeaking, a rat—was running things in the meantime, badly enough that everyone agreed it was honest government at last.

So it was not shocking, exactly, when Aldric walked up to the stall like any other citizen. But it was strange. It was strange the way a familiar street looks strange in fog.

"A luck-knot, please," he said. His voice was quiet and slightly rough, as if it had spent years shouting in a large empty room and was still recovering. "If you have one."

"We have forty," said Pip, who was minding the coin box and had absolutely no sense of occasion. "This one's got river-reed in it, this one's got canal-grass, this one's got—" he squinted at the tag Sefa had written, "—'good for beginnings.' That's my favorite."

"I'll take the one good for beginnings," Aldric said.

Noor came around the stall slowly. Up close, the former king looked thinner than his coins suggested, and tired in a way that sleep didn't touch, and older, and somehow *smaller*—not diminished, exactly. More like a man who had finally been allowed to put down something enormously heavy and hadn't yet figured out what to do with his arms.

"Fifteen years," Noor said. Not accusingly. Just stating it, the way you'd state the weather.

"Yes." Aldric met their eyes. He did not flinch from the beast-speaker's gaze, which Noor noted, and respected, and did not entirely forgive. "I don't remember most of them. Brannic tells me that's a mercy. Some days I believe him."

"The hunt," Noor said. "The wardens. The children in cages. That happened in your name."

"It did." He said it plainly. No deflection, no royal smoothing. "And the men who did it will answer for it—not to me. I'm done being the thing things are answered to. But I wanted—" he paused, and for a moment looked genuinely lost, a man standing in the ruins of his own biography, "—I wanted to buy something from someone I hurt. With money. Like a person. I'm told it's a start."

Noor looked at him for a long moment.

Then they reached past Pip, took a luck-knot woven with canal-grass—the cheap kind, the kind sold to dockworkers and grandmothers, the kind Noor had once made by the hundred to hide behind—and pressed it into the former king's hand along with his change.

"For free," Noor said. "Beginnings shouldn't cost the person starting over. It should cost the rest of us, a little, to let them. That's how you know it's real."

Aldric closed his hand around the knot. For a moment Noor thought he might cry, and had the grace to see that he was fighting it, and had the further grace to look away and give him privacy about it.

"Thank you," he managed.

"Don't thank me. Come back Thursday. Bramble will want to inspect your gardening plans, and she has opinions."

"I heard that," said Bramble, from the end of the row, to nobody who could hear her.

Aldric left. Pip watched him go, then looked up at Noor.

"That was weird," Pip said.

"That was weird," Noor agreed. "Get used to weird. We're going to be doing it professionally."

* * *

Evening came down soft over Cooper's Row, gold going to violet, the canal turning to hammered pewter. They closed the stall together—it took all four of them, plus Bramble's shoulder, plus a rat delegation under Old Marrow's daughter who supervised from atop a barrel and offered unsolicited commentary on the structural integrity of the awning frame ("adequate," she reported, which from a Marrow-rat was practically a parade).

Brannic banked the brazier. Sefa counted the day's take and announced, with the gravity of a state oracle, that they had made a profit of eleven marks and one live eel, the provenance of which nobody could explain and which was released into the canal to general applause. Pip fell asleep sitting up against Bramble's foreleg halfway through dinner, and nobody moved him, because moving him was unthinkable and also Bramble would have objected.

Noor sat on the stall's counter afterward, alone with the lamplight and the water-sounds, and did the accounting that mattered.

Fifteen years of whispering. Fifteen years of folding the gift small, of selling hollow charms and hiding the true one, of hearing horses and rats and wrens speak and answering only in silence, because silence was survival and survival was everything.

And here was the thing Noor had expected to feel, on the far side of all that: triumph, maybe. Or grief. Or the vertigo of a life rebuilt.

What they felt instead was *responsibility*, warm and heavy and entirely unglamorous, sitting on their chest like a contented cat. Because the gift had never been theirs, not really. It had been lent to them, the way the Spire's power had been lent to the thing that wore the crown, and the difference—the whole difference, the entire moral of the story Noor was still learning to read—was that Noor had given it back, every day since, one conversation at a time. To the widow's cat. To the sulking dog. To the fish merchants of Vellamark, who were liars, and the cart-horses, who were saints, and the city itself, which was both.

Power kept is power that rots. Power shared—

"Well," said Bramble, from the darkness at the end of the row. "Are you going to sit there being philosophical all night, or are you going to tell me what the wren wants?"

Noor blinked. "The wren?"

"She's been on the awning post for an hour," Bramble said. "Being very polite and very worried. Apparently she brings news from the ravens, and apparently it can't wait until morning, and apparently—" the horse's tone went dry, "—you're going to wish it could."

Noor looked up. There, silhouetted against the last violet light, sat the small brown bird Noor had freed from a cage five years and one lifetime ago, feathers ruffled, eyes bright, waiting.

"Go on, then," Noor said softly, and reached out a hand, and the wren hopped down onto it, and began to speak.
