## 22. When the Spire Turns Again

The grain came down from the high country in autumn, as it always did now, in barges painted blue and yellow because Pip had declared that the old crown colors were "boring and haunted" and no one had the heart to argue with a man who talked to rust for a living. Noor watched the first barge of the season nose up to the canal gate below the Spire hill and felt the same small, unreasonable joy they felt every year — the joy of watching food arrive in a city that remembered hunger.

Above them all, the Spire turned.

It was strange, still, after six years, to look up at the thing that had once been a prison turned into a windmill. The copper roots that had fed on the city's bound magic had been pulled out one by one — Brannic had supervised that work with the grim tenderness of a man pulling splinters from a wound he'd helped make — and in their place the great sails had been mounted, wide as bridges, catching the hill wind and turning the whole tower slowly, patiently, like a hand turning a millstone. On quiet nights you could hear it from the Tallow Market: a low, rhythmic groan, the sound of something enormous doing honest work.

"It's grinding," said a small voice at Noor's elbow. "Can you hear it? It's *grinding*, Master Halloran."

"I can hear it, Wren."

Wren — named for a bird, obviously; half the children in the undercity schools were named for birds or rats or once, memorably, a particularly beloved horse — bounced on her heels. She was eight, gap-toothed, and had come to Noor's class three months ago when her mother finally admitted what everyone in the flood tunnels already knew: the girl could talk to moths so fluently they'd started leaving her presents. Dead leaves, mostly. Moths were not generous creatures. But once, a fragment of silk ribbon, and Sefa had wept over it for an hour.

"Class is below," Noor said. "You're supposed to be below."

"Marrow says the lesson started without you."

Noor's chest did the thing it always did at Marrow's name — a catch, then a warmth behind it, like a scar that had decided to be a good memory instead of a bad one. Old Marrow, matriarch of every rat in Vellamark, had not survived the riot. But she had left behind daughters and granddaughters beyond counting, and one of them — a gray-whiskered creature of enormous dignity whom everyone simply called Marrow, because some names are not people but positions — had taken up the family business of bossing humans around.

"Then I suppose I'm late," Noor said. "Walk with me?"

* * *

The school lived in the vaults. This had seemed mad to everyone except Noor, who had argued, the night the council first suggested building somewhere else — anywhere else — that the vaults were exactly where the school belonged. "This is where our people were caged," they'd said, standing in the long chamber where copper cages had drained children dry. "If we build the school in the sunny part of town, we'll forget. And if we forget, someone will find a way to do it again."

So the cages were gone, melted down into the mill's new gearing, which Pip considered extremely funny and Brannic considered fitting, and the chamber was swept daily by students who took turns, and the walls were covered in chalk drawings and moth-silk banners and, in one corner, a small brass plaque that read: *For Pip, who was taken, and came back swinging.* Pip himself had insisted on the word *swinging*. He'd been offered *enduring* and *brave* and had rejected both on the grounds that they made him sound dead.

The classroom smelled of chalk dust and mouse (the mice attended voluntarily; Noor had never once asked them to) and the warm bread smell that drifted down through the Spire's stone bones whenever the mill was running, which was always.

Six students sat in a half-circle on benches. A boy who spoke to hounds — his father was one of the forgiven wardens, a fact the boy announced on his first day with his chin up, daring anyone to object, and no one ever had. Twin girls who heard the river. A solemn child who conversed with bees and therefore could not sit still, because bees did not believe in sitting. And Wren, sliding in late, moths already settling on her shoulders like a shawl.

"Late," said the bee-child.

"So are you," said Wren.

"No, I'm *present*. There's a difference. Ask any bee."

Noor let them settle, the way they'd learned to let things settle — markets taught patience, and so did teaching, and so, most of all, did surviving. Then they picked up the chalk.

"Today," Noor said, "we're going to talk about the hardest lesson there is. Harder than hiding your gift. Harder than using it. Do you know what it is?"

"Listening?" said the river twin on the left.

"Saying sorry to a horse," said the other twin, with the air of someone speaking from recent experience.

"Also correct, actually," Noor said, and the class laughed, and the twin went pink and pleased. "But no. The hardest lesson is this: a gift is not yours."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when children lean in.

"You didn't earn it. You can't lose it by being unworthy. It isn't a coin in your pocket that makes you better than the person next to you." Noor set the chalk down. "Fifteen years ago, the crown decided that gifts like yours were dangerous, so it locked them away and fed on them. That was evil, yes. But do you know what the second-worst version looks like? It looks like us deciding that because we have gifts, we should be the ones in charge. Because we hear what the dogs say. Because we know what the rats remember. Because we're special."

A hand went up. Wren's, of course. "Isn't being special good?"

"It's good," Noor said. "It's also heavy. And here's the trick of it — the thing I want you to carry home today, and tomorrow, and when you're old and gray and arguing with your own students." They looked around the circle, at each small face, and thought — not for the first time, but freshly, always freshly — about how close they had come, standing in the crown-room with the entity whispering *wear me, rule kindly, I only want a willing host,* about how sweet the trap had smelled.

"A gift kept is a gift that rots," Noor said. "A gift shared is the only kind that grows. That's the whole lesson. Everything else is practice."

* * *

After class, Brannic was waiting at the vault door, which he did most days, ostensibly to inspect the door hinges and actually because he liked hearing the lessons and would die before admitting it.

"How was it today?" he asked, falling into step. He walked with the rolling gait of a man who had spent six years learning to balance one-armed and now did it better than most two-armed men walked. His empty sleeve was pinned up neat and square, the way Sefa sewed it.

"We covered the difference between power and stewardship."

"Did you use the bread analogy again?"

"The bread analogy is a good analogy."

"The bread analogy is you saying 'if I bake bread and hoard it, it goes moldy, but if I share it, everyone eats,' and then the bee-child asking if magic is bread, and you saying 'magic is like bread in this one way only,' and then twenty minutes of chaos."

"That," Noor said, "is called pedagogy."

Brannic laughed — a real laugh, deep and unguarded, the kind he'd only learned again in the last few years, the kind that still surprised Noor a little every time, because for so long his guilt had sat on his chest like a stone and laughter had been the last thing to climb off. He'd told his truth in a dark tunnel to a stranger with a satchel of luck-knots, and the telling hadn't fixed him, nothing fixed you, but it had opened a door, and he'd walked through it one day at a time until here he was: falconer to the ravens of the free rookery, teacher of the Spire's climbing classes, uncle to every child in the undercity whether they liked it or not.

They climbed the spiral stair together, up past the grinding floors where the millstones thundered their patient thunder, up toward the open gallery near the top where the wind came through clean.

Sefa was there, of course. She was usually there these days; her joints had opinions about stairs, so she'd claimed the gallery as her reading-room and let the world come to her. She sat wrapped in moth-silk with her charts spread around her, and a young apprentice — one of the rescued, grown tall and steady — sat beside her learning to read wings.

"Tell me it's good news," Brannic said.

"The moths say the harvest will be heavy and the winter mild." Sefa squinted at a wing. "They also say it'll snow sideways in spring and someone will spill soup on the council floor. Moths remain unreliable narrators. It's why I love them."

"And the omens? Still contradicting each other?"

"Always. Two moths landed facing opposite directions this morning. One says change is coming. The other says change is coming." She smiled up at Noor. "Even they've learned that both can be true."

Below, through the gallery's open arches, Vellamark spread out in the late afternoon light. Noor never got tired of looking. The market stalls along the canal — including, at the corner nearest the bridge, a stall with a faded awning and a sign that read HALLORAN'S CHARMS & COUNSEL, OPEN, BEASTS WELCOME — where a lame cart-horse named Duchess stood in her customary spot, ancient now, gray-muzzled, officially employed as the shop's Head of Negotiations. Children queued to tell her their troubles. She charged them in apples.

Beyond the market, the wardens' old barracks had become a granary and a hospital. The proclamation posts where criers had once offered silver for the names of the gifted now posted grain prices and festival dates and, twice a year, the list of the reckoned — the wardens judged by the city's own tribunal, some forgiven, some not, their names spoken plainly in the square because the city had agreed that justice done quietly was justice half-done.

And at the very center of it all, the Spire turned, and turned, and turned.

"Do you ever miss it?" Sefa asked suddenly, softly. "The stall before all this. The small life. Selling luck-knots and pretending."

Noor considered it honestly, the way they tried to consider everything honestly now — honesty had cost them a confession and a wren's bargain once, and it had bought back everything, and they'd never stopped paying its price gladly since.

"I miss the quiet sometimes," they said. "I don't miss the hiding. There's a difference too." They leaned on the rail. "Besides, look who's coming up the road."

* * *

He came on foot, as he always did, in plain brown wool, with a walking stick and no escort, because the king — former king, though the title clung to him like burrs and he'd given up correcting people — had abdicated everything except the habit of walking everywhere. He climbed the Spire hill slowly, nodding to bargemen, stopping to let Duchess inspect his pockets, arriving at the gallery slightly out of breath and entirely unbothered by it.

"Bread day," he said by way of greeting. "I smelled it from the bridge."

"You always smell it from the bridge."

"It's my favorite distance from which to smell bread. Close enough to hope, far enough to savor." He settled onto the bench beside Sefa with the sigh of a man whose knees had served in too many campaigns. "How are the students?"

"Wren spoke to a moth today in full sentences," Noor said. "The moth answered rudely. She was delighted."

"Good. Rudeness from animals is the surest sign of respect." He looked out at the turning sails, at the city below, and his face did what it always did — that complicated movement, grief and gratitude and guilt braided together, the face of a man who had slept through the worst years of his own reign and woken to find out what his name had been used for. He had signed the abdication with a shaking hand and then, when the council panicked and asked what he wanted to *be* instead, had said, "A neighbor," and meant it.

He was a good neighbor. He showed up. He carried things. He listened more than he talked, which Noor suspected was penance, and had told him once — gently — that penance was just another kind of keeping score, and wasn't it better to just keep showing up? He'd thought about that for a long time. He was still thinking about it, probably.

"I brought something," he said, and produced from his pocket a small paper twist. Inside: honey sweets, the cheap kind, the kind sold three-for-a-copper at the market. "For the children. If that's all right."

"It's all right," Noor said. "They'll fight over the lemon ones."

"They always fight over the lemon ones." He smiled, and for a moment the hollowed look lifted and you could see the young king he must have been, before the pale procession carried him down into the dark. Then he looked at Noor, and his voice went quieter. "The council asked me again yesterday. About succession. Whether the city ought to choose a new monarch eventually, for stability's sake. I told them what I always tell them." He nodded upward, at the great sails groaning their slow circles against the sky. "I told them we already have a crown. It turns. It grinds grain. Anyone may bring their wheat to it. And no one has to kneel."

Sefa snorted. "You've been rehearsing that."

"For six years," the old king admitted cheerfully. "It gets better every time."

* * *

Evening came down soft. The students' parents collected them one by one — the hound-speaker's father waving from the gate, the bee-child departing in a personal cloud — until only Wren remained, waiting for her mother, sitting on the steps with moths on her shoulders and a lemon sweet in her cheek.

"Master Halloran?" she said, around the sweet. "Can I ask a question? A real one."

"Real ones are the only kind worth asking."

"What happened to the thing that wore the king? Everyone says it unraveled screaming. But where did it *go*?"

Noor sat down beside her. Below them the lamps of the market were lighting one by one, and the mill turned above, and somewhere in the walls a thousand small hearts were settling into sleep — rats in the rafters, mice in the granary, a rookery of ravens who still delivered messages for payment in shiny objects and gossip.

"When we unbound it," Noor said, "we gave back every name it had stolen. Every drop of magic it had eaten, all those years. It went back to the people. Back to the beasts. Back to the ground and the river and the wind." They paused. "What was left of it — the hungry part, the part that only knew how to take — there wasn't enough left to hold a shape. So it came apart. Not screaming, in the end. More like... sighing. Like something setting down a weight it had carried so long it forgot it was allowed to stop."

"Could it come back?"

Noor thought of the moths' contradictory omens. Change is coming; change is coming.

"Not that one," they said. "Hunger like that doesn't come back once it's been fed out of existence. But there's always another kind of hunger wearing a different coat. That's not a reason to be afraid, little wren. It's a reason to stay awake. And you lot—" they tapped her shoulder, scattering moths into the lamplight, "—are the wakest generation this city has ever had."

Her mother's voice floated up from the bottom of the stair. Wren scrambled up, then paused, eight years old and gap-toothed and serious as a judge.

"My moth says you're going to tell us the story of the riot soon. The real one. With Marrow."

"Next week," Noor promised. "The true version. Rats' honor."

She grinned and clattered down the stairs, and Noor stayed a while longer in the doorway, listening to the Spire turn.

Power kept is power that rots, they thought, watching the sails carve slow circles against the first stars. Power shared is the only crown worth wearing.

Somewhere below, Duchess whickered goodnight to the last customer. Somewhere above, a raven called the hour. And the Spire turned, and turned, and turned — not over anyone, not against anyone, just with the wind, grinding grain for a city that had finally learned to feed itself.
