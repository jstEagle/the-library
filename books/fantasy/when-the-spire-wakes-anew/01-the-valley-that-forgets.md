## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats always knew before the weather did.

Ivo Amberdale had learned this by their fourteenth winter, though they had never told a living soul. The herd would turn as one, faces lifted toward the western pass, hours before the first cloud crested the ridge like a slow grey tide. Then the rain would come, and the elders would nod sagely and speak of aching joints and old wounds, and Ivo would say nothing, because saying nothing was the whole of Hollowmere's art.

Hollowmere forgot things. That was its purpose, though no one said so aloud. The valley sat folded between two shoulders of the Greymantle Mountains like a coin in a closed fist, and everything that entered it grew quiet. Grief went soft at the edges here. Old hatreds wore smooth as river stones. Even joy was a muted thing, a hearth-glow rather than a blaze — pleasant, endurable, safe. Travelers who stumbled down the switchback road into the valley often paused halfway, blinking, unable to say what they had been about to do or why they'd come. Most of them turned around. The ones who stayed were the ones who wanted to forget something.

Ivo's family had been in Hollowmere longer than the church records, longer than the names of the fields. Ambedales kept goats on the high pasture and wool-sheep below, and every generation someone in the family could find water with a hazel twig, which everyone agreed was merely good luck and a steady hand. That was how Hollowmere handled such things. It took the uncanny and gave it a sensible name, the way a good housekeeper folds a strange garment into a chest and sits on the lid.

Ivo could speak to animals.

Not in the way of shepherds who claim their flocks know them, not in the way of the horse-whisperers at the spring fair who charged a copper to calm a kicking colt. Ivo spoke, and the animals answered — actually answered, in words that arrived inside the skull rather than the ear, words with weight and texture and sometimes, unbearably, opinions.

It had started, as far as Ivo could remember, with the old nanny goat Bramble. Bramble had opinions the way other goats had lice: constantly, and in great numbers. She disapproved of the salt lick. She disapproved of the new fence posts. She disapproved, most profoundly, of Ivo's cousin Tam, who pulled tails. *Tell him,* she would say, chewing her cud with the deliberate air of a magistrate delivering sentence, *that I have teeth, and memory, and all winter to plan.* Ivo had laughed the first time — a real laugh, startled out of them — and then clapped both hands over their mouth and looked wildly around the empty pasture, because the laugh had come out wrong somehow, too loud for the quiet of the valley, and because Bramble had looked back at them with flat yellow eyes full of ancient patience and said, *Oh. You're one of those.*

One of those. Bramble had never explained what she meant. Ivo had never asked. Some knowledge you carry the way you carry a hot coal — carefully, close to the body, and without letting anyone see your hands shake.

Because everyone knew what happened to people who were one of those. Not in Hollowmere itself — Hollowmere was kind, in its dim, forgetting way. But twice a year the peddler came over the pass from Corvane with his mule and his bundles and his news, and the news was always the same shape. A woman in Marrowdale burned last spring, the peddler would say, settling onto the bench by the well with the relish of a man distributing sweets. Talked to crows, they said. Fed them and asked after things. Her own neighbors did it, mind, after the gray-cloaks came through asking questions. And everyone in the square would murmur and click their tongues and agree that it was terrible, shocking, no telling what folk will get up to, and Ivo would stand among them with their heart going like a trapped bird and their face arranged into the proper expression of horrified innocence, and afterward they would walk out past the last fence post and be sick into the nettles.

So Ivo hid it. Twenty-two years of hiding it, and they had gotten very good. They spoke to the animals only in the high pasture, alone, where the wind covered any movement of the lips. They never spoke to wild creatures near the village — crows especially, because crows remembered faces and held grudges and gossiped across miles in ways that made Ivo's skin crawl to think about. They had trained themselves to keep their eyes moving when an animal spoke, to look at nothing, to let their mouth hang slack and stupid as if their mind were elsewhere. Their mother thought them a dreamy child, half-simple, good with beasts the way some people are good with beasts and no more to be made of it. Their grandmother — Grandmother Maren, who wove and said little and watched everything — had once caught Ivo standing motionless in the dooryard while a crow perched on the fence post and Ivo stared at it, and for one long moment the two of them had looked at each other across the yard, grandmother and grandchild, and Ivo's blood had turned to ice water.

But Grandmother Maren had only said, "Mind the gate latch," and gone inside, and Ivo had spent three sleepless nights waiting for the gray-cloaks to come over the pass, and they never did.

* * *

Autumn came late that year, and strange.

The signs began small. The goats faced west for three days running and would not graze the far meadow, though the grass there was sweetest. When Ivo pressed them — gently, alone behind the sheepfold, where no one could see — Bramble only said, *Something is walking under the world,* and would elaborate no further, and Ivo lay awake that night listening to the ground as if hearing might help, which of course it didn't, and felt foolish, and felt afraid anyway.

Then the bees. Old Hew's hives by the millstream swarmed in October, which had never happened in anyone's memory, and hung in the bare alder branches like a fist of brown lightning, and would not be coaxed into any box. Hew stood beneath them wringing his hat, and Ivo, passing with a load of hay, heard the swarm speaking — not to him, just to each other, a low continuous sound like wind through a keyhole — and caught only one phrase before he made himself walk on, hay hook gripped tight, knuckles white:

*—the door is thin, the door is thin, the door is—*

He told himself it meant nothing. Bees babbled. He had heard them babble about weather, about bears, about the quality of various clovers. A swarm was a swarm. October was warm this year; perhaps the warmth confused them.

But that night he dreamed of a door.

He dreamed of it for six nights running. It stood in a place that was neither the valley nor anywhere else — a grey country of smooth stone underfoot, lightless but lit, the way dreams are. The door was very tall and very old, black wood banded in metal gone green, and it filled the whole horizon of the dream, and behind it something breathed. Slowly. Patiently. The way mountains breathe, if mountains breathe. Each exhalation stirred the hair on Ivo's arms, and each inhalation drew at them, tugging them forward across the stone toward the gap beneath the door, where a thread of light showed — not firelight, not daylight, but a color Ivo had no name for, a color like the moment before a word is spoken.

On the sixth night, the breathing stopped.

And in the silence, Ivo heard — faint, far, beneath the world — a sound like a bell struck once in an empty cathedral, and then a voice, or the memory of a voice, saying words he did not understand and would spend the rest of his life understanding:

*Wake.*

He woke in his own narrow bed with the frost ferns climbing the window glass and his heart slamming and Bramble's words walking round and round his skull. Something is walking under the world.

* * *

The crow came on the morning of the first hard frost.

Ivo was in the high pasture alone, as usual, mending a break in the wall where a ewe had pushed through. The cold had come in the night with real teeth, and the grass stood stiff and silvered, and every breath smoked, and the sky was that particular hollow blue of late autumn that seems to go on forever in all directions. The goats huddled in the lee of the wall, chewing, steaming faintly. Everything was ordinary. Everything was exactly, perfectly, mercifully ordinary.

The crow arrived out of the east.

It did not glide down from the ridge the way the valley crows did, lazy and entitled, angling for whatever the goats knocked loose. It came in level and fast and low over the grass, wings beating hard, like a bird carrying news it could not afford to drop, and it landed on the wall ten feet from Ivo with a crack of claws on stone that sounded louder than it should have been.

Ivo knew at once it was not a valley crow. Valley crows were big and glossy and rude. This one was smaller, ragged at the wing-edges, feathers gone grey-brown at the throat like a bird that had flown through weather no bird should survive. One eye was bright and black and mad with distance. The other had gone pale as milk.

It looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at it. And Ivo did the thing they had trained themselves for twenty-two years never, ever to do: they opened their mouth and answered before thinking.

"You're a long way from home."

The crow cocked its head. Its good eye glittered. And then it spoke, and its voice was not the dry rattle of crow-talk but something older, layered, a voice like pages turning in a dark room, and it said:

*The spire wakes.*

Ivo's knees nearly went. They caught themselves against the wall, fingers finding the cold stone, and for a moment the whole pasture tilted — the silvered grass, the steaming goats, the hollow blue sky — everything sliding sideways as if the valley itself had been picked up and shaken.

"What?" Their voice came out cracked. "What did you say?"

*The spire wakes,* the crow said again, patiently, the way one repeats a thing to a child or a fool. *Under the mountain. Under all mountains. The oldest thing. The first door.* It hopped along the wall, closer, dragging the bad wing slightly. *You heard it. Don't lie to me, beast-speaker. I flew nine days on the smell of your hearing.*

"I don't—" Ivo's mouth was dry as August. They glanced wildly at the village below, at the smoke rising straight from the chimneys, at old Hew's distant figure bent over the millstream. No one was watching. No one was ever watching; that was the mercy of Hollowmere. "I don't know what you're talking about."

*Liar,* the crow said, almost fondly. *That's all right. Lying is a valley trade. You grow it here like barley.* It settled its ragged feathers and fixed Ivo with the milk-white eye, and when it spoke again its voice dropped, and the dropping of it was worse than anything, because suddenly it sounded tired — tired the way Ivo had never heard an animal sound, tired down to the marrow, tired like a soldier at the end of a long retreat. *Listen to me, goat-friend. I have carried this word through three kingdoms and over the Greymantle spine in winter. I have said it to a drowned god in a well and to a fox who used to be a man and to a knight who drinks himself stupid in border towns because he signed a paper once. I am nearly done. So hear it.*

Ivo could not have moved if the herd had stampeded. The cold of the wall seeped through their coat into their chest. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and fell silent, and the sound seemed to belong to another world.

*The ward that keeps this valley quiet is failing,* the crow said. *You've seen it. Don't shake your head — you've seen it. The light in the stones at the standing ring, flickering like a guttering lamp. Your elders call it weather. Your elders call everything weather; it's why they're still alive.* The good eye blinked, slow. *Wards are doors, beast-speaker. Doors are promises. And the promise underneath this valley — the price that was paid to make it forget — is coming due.*

"Price," Ivo whispered. The word came out of them without permission. "What price? Paid by whom?"

The crow regarded them for a long moment. Steam rose off its back in the cold air. Far away, beyond the pass, beyond the mountains, something Ivo could not see and did not want to imagine was marching, gathering, waking — he knew this the way the goats knew rain, the way the bees knew the thinning of the door, the way he had known Bramble's opinion of cousin Tam: absolutely, and without any way to prove it.

*That,* the crow said, spreading its ragged wings, *is the question you were born to ask. Ask it loudly enough, and everything changes. Ask it quietly, and you'll die in this valley like your grandmother intends, never knowing what she wove into her loom besides wool.*

Ivo lunged forward. "Wait — my grandmother? What do you know about my—"

But the crow was already airborne, beating hard for the eastern ridge, small and ragged against the enormous blue, and the last thing it called back drifted down thin and cold as the frost itself:

*Nine days, beast-speaker. The spire wakes. Decide what you are before it finishes.*

Then it was gone over the ridge, and the pasture was silent, and the goats went on chewing, and the smoke rose straight from the chimneys of a village that had forgotten how to be afraid.

Ivo stood a long time with their hands flat on the cold wall, feeling their own heartbeat, feeling beneath it — faint, far, patient as geology — the slow breathing of something under the world that had stopped breathing in their dreams and now, apparently, had begun again.

Bramble nudged their elbow. *Well,* she said, with the weary satisfaction of a prophet vindicated. *Told you.*

Ivo looked down at her, at the flat yellow eyes, at the cud turning and turning like the years themselves. And for the first time in twenty-two years of careful, silent, hidden life, Ivo Amberdale was afraid not of being known —

but of how much they already were.
