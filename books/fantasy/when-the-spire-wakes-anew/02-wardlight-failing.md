## 2. Wardlight Failing

The first sign was the goats.

Not their words — Bramble had said nothing new in three days, only chewing her cud and muttering about the quality of the autumn grass as though it were a personal insult — but their *behavior*. They would not graze the eastern meadow. All eleven of them stood at its edge in a ragged line, ears forward, staring at nothing Ivo could see, and when Ivo tried to shoo them out they turned as one body and looked at them with an expression Ivo had no word for and did not like having to invent.

*It hums,* said the youngest doe, whose name was Sorrel and who thought of herself as a philosopher. *The air over the east field. It hums wrong.*

*Everything hums,* Ivo said. *That's the ward. That's always been the ward.*

*Yes,* said Sorrel placidly. *But it stutters.*

Ivo stood in the cold of that October morning with their breath steaming and felt the truth of it before they could argue. They had lived inside the valley's hum for twenty-two years the way a fish lives inside water — not hearing it, only knowing its absence would mean death. Now, if they listened past the ordinary drone of it, there was something else: a hitch. A skip. The sound a loom makes when a thread snaps and the weaver has not yet noticed.

They did not tell anyone. Telling anyone meant explaining how they knew, and so instead they did what Amberdales did, which was carry things quietly until the carrying bent them.

* * *

The elders blamed weather, because elders always blame weather; weather asks nothing of you but complaint.

It began at dusk on the third day after the goats refused the eastern meadow. Ivo was coming up from the pens with a pail of wash-water when the light over Hollowmere changed. Not dimmed — *changed*, the way a face changes when someone behind it speaks. The valley sat cupped in its ring of grey hills, and above it, invisible for as long as anyone could remember, the ward had hung like heat-shimmer made permanent, a faint bending of the air that you saw only if you looked at the hills through it sideways. Every child learned the trick of seeing it and every adult pretended they'd forgotten.

Now it flickered.

There was no other honest word. The shimmer guttered — once, twice — like a lamp in a draft, and in the instants between, the hills showed through *sharper*, harder-edged, closer than they should have been, as though the whole valley had been painted slightly out of focus for two hundred years and someone had wiped a hand across the canvas.

The pail handle bit into Ivo's fingers. Around them, the lane filled with neighbors come out of doorways, faces tilted up, murmuring. Old Marta Corren crossed herself with a spoon she'd been holding. The Hedley twins, six years old and therefore the last generation born under an unbroken sky, pointed and shrieked with delight until their mother hauled them inside.

"It's the aurora," called Aldous Fen from his doorstep, in the voice of a man rehearsing. "Northern lights. We get them this far south sometimes."

"We have never gotten them," said his wife, not unkindly.

"Weather," agreed Elder Prue, arriving at speed with her cane striking cobbles like a metronome set to panic. She was eighty-one and had run the valley council since Ivo's father was in swaddling clothes, and she surveyed the flickering sky with the expression of a housekeeper finding a stain. "High pressure. Ice crystals. It refracts." She said *refracts* the way she said all words she'd learned late, carefully, daring them to misbehave. "Everyone inside. Nothing is happening."

Nothing continued to happen for another hour, in full view of everyone, while the wardlight stuttered and steadied and stuttered again, and the valley's hum — which Ivo alone could hear, or believed they alone could hear — developed a second hitch, and then a third, like a heart learning arrhythmia.

Ivo carried the water inside. Their grandmother was at the loom.

* * *

Maren Amberdale was ninety years old and had been weaving since before the ward was old enough to have a name among the young. Her hands were knotted like tree roots but they never missed a thread; watching them work was watching something older than skill, some animal patience that predated language. She wove wool mostly, and flax in summer, and once — Ivo had been very small — a cloth so fine that a traveling merchant had offered her a price she refused without looking up from the shuttle.

She looked up now.

"You saw it," she said. Not a question. Her eyes were pale as winter water and they went through Ivo the way her shuttle went through warp.

"The lights," Ivo said carefully. "Aldous says—"

"Aldous says what he's told to say." She set down the shuttle. It was such an unusual act that Ivo's stomach tightened around nothing. Maren Amberdale did not set down shuttles. "Come here. Sit where I can see your face."

Ivo sat on the weaving bench, close enough to smell the lanolin and the faint mineral smell that clung to her always, the smell of the loom-house itself, of stone and oil and long work.

"Your mother had your eyes," she said, which was a strange thing to say, since Ivo's mother had died when Ivo was four and Ivo had spent a lifetime being told they had their father's everything. "And your father's temper, gods rest him, and my stubbornness, which is the worst inheritance of the three." Her hands lay flat on the woven cloth in front of her, on a pattern Ivo didn't recognize — not any of her usual borders, but something angular, repeated, almost like writing. "You feel it too. Don't lie to me, child. Lying runs in the family but it never once worked on me."

Ivo's mouth was dry. There were twenty-two years of careful silence stacked between them and the simplest answer, and Ivo heard themselves choose, for reasons they would examine for the rest of their life, to put one more stone on the pile.

"I don't know what you mean."

Grandmother looked at them for a long moment. Something moved behind her face — disappointment, or relief, or grief wearing both as coats — and then she turned back to the loom as though the conversation had concluded on her terms, which perhaps it had.

"The lights will keep," she said. "Wards are older than weather. Go see to the goats."

* * *

That night Ivo dreamed of the spire again.

It had begun a week ago, the first night after the crow spoke — the dream of standing in a black plain beneath a tower so tall its top was lost in a sky the color of a bruise, and of feeling, rather than hearing, a slow vast breathing that had stopped mid-breath for centuries and now, horribly, resumed. In the dream Ivo always woke at the moment the breathing started, sitting upright in the dark with their own lungs heaving as though they'd been holding the breath for it.

Bramble, sleeping in the corner of the pen below the loft window, opened one eye.

*Loud,* she complained. *Your sleeping is loud.*

*I'm sorry.*

*Don't be sorry. Be quiet.* A pause, cud turning. *Or be interesting. One of those.*

Ivo lay back down and watched the ceiling beams and did not sleep again, and somewhere in the small hours heard, distantly, from the direction of the elder houses, a bell begin to ring — not the alarm pattern, not the meeting pattern, but the third thing, the one nobody under forty had ever heard rung for real: the calling-in of every soul in Hollowmere to the moot-stone, immediately, by night.

* * *

By dawn half the valley stood in the market square in the freezing fog, breath rising in a common cloud, and Elder Prue stood on the moot-stone steps with the other elders ranked behind her like fence posts, and told them about ice crystals.

"There is no danger," she said. "The ward is a wall. Walls do not flicker. What flickers is light upon the wall, and light is weather, and weather passes." She said it well. She had clearly practiced. And Ivo, standing near the front between the Hedleys' fence and the well, watched the crowd watch her, and watched the crowd not believe her — not because she lied badly, but because everyone in Hollowmere had been raised on the same unspoken arithmetic: *the ward does not flicker; therefore whatever flickers is not the ward; therefore we are safe.* It was the oldest trick the valley knew, and it had worked for two hundred years, and you could see in every face the dawning, unwelcome suspicion that arithmetic might finally be coming due.

Old Tam Weller raised his hand. "My grandfather dug the boundary stones reset, back in the famine year. Said the ward drank deep that season. Is it — is it hungry?"

Prue's cane struck stone. "The ward is not a beast."

No one answered her. Everyone thought the same thing, and Ivo felt it move through the crowd like wind through wheat: *everything is a beast. Everything eats. The question is what, and when, and whether it has begun.*

The meeting dissolved into clusters, clusters into arguments, arguments into the grim business of a valley deciding to go on. Ivo lingered. So did Grandmother, who had come without being summoned and stood at the square's edge wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, and when the crowd thinned she beckoned Ivo to her with two fingers.

"Walk with me," she said. "Slowly. My hips are liars."

They walked up the lane toward the loom-house through fog gone thin and gold at the edges. For a hundred paces neither spoke. Then:

"When I am dead," Grandmother said, "you will want to burn the loom-house. Grief makes people stupid about fire. Don't. The house stays. Whatever you find in it, the house stays."

Ivo stopped walking. "Grandmother—"

"And when the lights come back — and they will come back, child, ice crystals are patient liars but they're still liars — you'll be tempted to say something. To someone. About what you hear." She kept walking, so Ivo had to follow. "Don't do that either. Not yet. Promise me the way you promise goats: completely, and without understanding why."

The words landed like a dropped stitch pulled tight. *The way you promise goats.* Ivo's blood went cold and stayed cold the rest of the walk, and Grandmother said nothing more, only hummed — an old tune, a lullaby Ivo half-remembered from being very small, except that humming it now, in the gold thinning fog, with the wardlight stuttering faintly overhead like a candle in a dying draft, it sounded less like a lullaby than like a counting. Like someone keeping time to something.

Like someone measuring how much was left.

* * *

She took to her bed four days later.

It was not dramatic. She finished the cloth on the loom — the strange angular-patterned one — cut it free, folded it, and placed it in the chest at the foot of her bed with the finality of a woman closing an account. Then she said, "I'll lie down now," and lay down, and did not get up.

The healer came and used words like *age* and *quietly* and *a matter of days*. The neighbors came and left bread and tallow and lowered voices. Ivo sat by the bed and did the work of the living — water, broth, the fire fed — and listened to their grandmother breathe with the same hitching rhythm as the ward, and hated themselves for noticing, and noticed anyway.

On the second night she grew restless. Her hands moved on the blanket, working threads that weren't there, and she spoke — fragments, names, half-sentences in a cadence Ivo didn't know, addressed to people who weren't in the room. Once she said, clear as a bell, *"You can't weave a wall from one thread, girl, whoever taught you that?"* and laughed at someone unseen, and the laugh frightened Ivo more than anything the fever had produced yet.

Near midnight she surfaced. Her eyes found Ivo's with sudden terrible clarity, the clarity of a diver breaking water.

"Ivo." Her hand closed on theirs with shocking strength. "Listen. There isn't time for the long version, and you wouldn't thank me for it anyway. You'd have found it. You were always going to find it — you find everything, that's the curse of you, that's the gift—" The grip tightened. "The name. Say you'll remember the name."

"What name?"

But she was sinking again, and the fragments came faster, and Ivo leaned close into the smell of lanolin and woodsmoke and the end of things, and caught them one by one like falling stitches:

"—shouldn't have let them count her in, she was *young*, younger than you—"

"—told myself it was arithmetic, told myself every year, arithmetic, arithmetic—"

"—and the ward held, it held, it *held*, look how it held—"

Her eyes opened one last time. They were fixed on the ceiling, or on something beyond it, and they were full of a sorrow so old it had worn smooth, like a stone in a streambed.

"Sera," she whispered. "Sera. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

Three times, the old number, the number for oaths and debts. Then her hand went slack in Ivo's, and her breathing slowed, and slowed, and somewhere in the middle of one breath the hitch came and the next breath didn't, and the room was very quiet, and outside the window the wardlight over Hollowmere flickered — once, twice — and steadied, and went on.

* * *

Ivo sat with her until the candles burned low. Then, because grief makes people stupid about fire and Ivo had promised not to be stupid, they did not burn anything. They washed her face with warm water the way she'd taught them to wash fleece, gently, against the grain of everything. They closed her hands over the shuttle she'd set down a week ago and never picked up again.

At the window, on the sill, a crow had landed.

It was not the crow from the edge of the world, the one that had spoken the words no bird should know. Or it was shaped like it, black and patient, head cocked. It said nothing. It only watched Ivo through the frost-furred glass with one round eye, and Ivo, hollowed out and strangely calm, watched it back.

*Sera,* the silence said, in their grandmother's voice, in their own voice, in a voice that belonged to a person Ivo had never heard of until tonight and somehow, in the marrow-deep way of dreams and blood, already mourned.

Below in the pen, Bramble lifted her great blunt head toward the loft window, toward the crow, toward Ivo, and said, in the tone of a creature who has watched a hundred winters and intends to watch this one closely:

*Well. Now it starts.*

Down in the loom-house, in the chest at the foot of a dead woman's bed, something waited — folded cloth, angular patterns, and whatever else Maren Amberdale had spent ninety years and one daughter hiding in the warp.
