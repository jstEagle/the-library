## 3. What Grandmother Sold

The house did not know how to be quiet.

For twenty-two years it had hummed with my grandmother's presence — the creak of her chair, the clack-clack-clack of the loom that ran like a second heartbeat through every room, her voice calling my name in that particular way she had, half command and half endearment, *Ivo, mind the goats. Ivo, fetch the wool. Ivo, come sit where I can see you.* Now the house held its breath around the absence of her, and every sound it made sounded like her, and none of them were.

I had not slept. I had sat by her bed for the last three days of her life and I had not slept since, and the grief in me had a strange texture, like wet wool — heavy, and wrong, and hiding something. Because her last words had not been my name. They had been an apology, whispered to the ceiling, to the dark, to someone who was not in the room.

*I'm sorry, Sera. I'm sorry. I kept the light burning as long as I could.*

I did not know anyone named Sera.

The morning of the seventh day after her death, the neighbors came with bread and salt and the careful, aching gentleness that Hollowmere people wear like a uniform. Maren Ostell brought a lamb shank and would not meet my eyes. Old Tam brought firewood and stacked it wrong on purpose so he'd have a reason to come back. They said the usual things — she was a pillar, she was a wonder, the ward held strong through her whole life, didn't it, and wasn't that something — and each mention of the ward landed on me like a stone dropped into still water, sending ripples toward a shore I couldn't see yet.

Because here is what I had not told them, what I had barely admitted to myself: the night my grandmother died, I had gone out to the goat pen in the blue-black hour before dawn, unable to bear the house, and the eldest nanny — Bramble, gray-muzzled, my oldest friend in the way that only a creature you've spoken to in secret can be a friend — had pressed her broad forehead against my chest and said, in her slow, grass-flavored voice:

*The light went out of the edge of the world last night. Did you feel it?*

I had felt it. I had felt it the way you feel a tooth missing — the tongue keeps going to the gap. And I had said, *What light, Bramble?* and she had said, *The old one. The one the valley sleeps under,* and then she had licked my hand, because grief is grief to a goat too, and she had known mine needed licking.

The ward. Failing. The night she died.

I did not believe in coincidence. My grandmother had taught me that, ironically enough, over twenty years of telling me that everything in Hollowmere was coincidence — the crows that watched me too closely were just crows, the dreams I had of running on four feet were just dreams, the way the wardlight shimmered green over the western ridge some evenings was just weather. *Everything is weather, Ivo,* she used to say, and she'd smile, and her smile had always had a locked room in it.

Now she was dead, and all her locked rooms belonged to me.

* * *

I found the loom three days later, which is to say I finally let myself look at it.

I had been avoiding it the way you avoid a grave. The great warp-weighted loom stood in the back room where the light came slantwise through horn panes, her loom, older than I was, older than my mother, oak gone silver with age and polished dark where her hands had worked it for sixty years. The last weaving was still on it — a half-finished length of cloth, undyed wool, plain as bread. She had never left work unfinished. Not once in my life. If she had stopped mid-weave, it was because something had stopped her.

I sat in her chair. It was still warm, or I imagined it was, and I put my hands on the loom's frame the way you'd put your hands on the shoulders of a grieving friend, and I said, out loud, to the empty room, "What were you making?"

The house said nothing. But my fingers, resting on the frame, felt something that my ears had missed — a hollowness. A resonance. The left upright beam sounded different from the right when I rapped it with a knuckle. I rapped again. Again. Moved my hand down, down, to the place where the beam met the foot, and there — so faint that only someone who had spent a lifetime listening for things that weren't supposed to exist would have caught it — there was a seam.

A seam in solid oak.

I want to be honest about what I felt in that moment, because everyone who tells this story later gets it wrong. They want it to be triumph, discovery, the thrill of a door opening. It was not. It was dread. It was the specific dread of a child who has found something in a parent's drawer that they were never meant to see and who understands, in their gut, before they understand it in their head, that seeing it will change them and that the change will not be a kind one.

I got a knife from the kitchen. I worked it into the seam. The wood resisted, then gave with a sound like a held breath released, and a panel the length of my forearm swung inward on hidden pegs, and the smell that came out was old paper and old tallow and something else — something cold and mineral, like the air off a cave, like the air, I would learn much later, of the deep places under mountains.

Inside was a bundle.

Cloth, oilskin, tied with a cord gone brittle. And beneath it, flat and heavy, a slab of pale stone no bigger than a bread loaf, its surface covered in writing that was not writing — lines that moved when I looked at them directly and went still when I looked away, like insects under bark.

I took the bundle to the kitchen table. My hands were shaking. The oilskin crackled as I unwrapped it.

The first thing was a letter, folded small, addressed in my grandmother's hand: *For Ivo. When the light fails.*

Not *if*. *When.*

I sat with that for a long moment. The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere outside a crow said something to another crow, and I flinched, old habit, the reflex of a lifetime of hiding — but the house was empty, the valley was gray and quiet, and no one was listening except the dead woman's letter, which had been listening for me my whole life.

I opened it.

*Ivo. If you are reading this, then I am dead, and the ward is failing, and you have found the loom, which means you are the one the valley chose after all, though I prayed nightly that it wouldn't be you. I have lied to you your whole life. I am going to stop now, because I am out of time to do it gently.*

*You had a sister.*

The words swam. I read them four times. *You had a sister.* The kettle screamed and I didn't move to take it off the stove. Steam curled up to the ceiling beams and hung there like a ghost deciding whether to stay.

*Her name was Sera. She was born four years before you, in the hard winter, and she was — different. You know what different means in this valley. You know it in your own body, in the thing you do that you think I don't know about, the thing you do with the goats and the crows when you believe yourself alone. I have known since you were three years old. I never said. Forgive me. It was the same reason for everything I never said.*

*Sera's difference was larger than yours. The ward saw it. The ward was built to see it — that is what wards are, in the end: eyes that never blink, watching for the thing the valley cannot survive being known for. When Sera was six, the ward found her, and the elders came to this house, and what was decided in this kitchen I have spent fifty years trying to un-decide and cannot.*

I had to put the letter down. My hands would not hold it steady. Through the window I could see the western ridge, and above it, faint as breath on glass, the wardlight — the shimmer the elders called weather, the shimmer that had flickered and guttered the night my grandmother died, the shimmer that Bramble said had gone out at the edge of the world.

It was not weather. It had never been weather. It was a lamp. And lamps burn something.

*There is a court beneath the mountain,* the letter went on. *You will not have heard of it. No one in Hollowmere has, except the elders of each generation, and we are sworn to forget the words as soon as they leave our mouths — there is a craft for that, an old unremembering, and I have unremembered most of it myself. But this much I wrote down before the forgetting could take it, because someone in this family had to carry the truth, and I could not carry it and stay sane.*

*The court beneath trades. It has always traded. Before the valley had a name, before Hollowmere was hollow, the first elders went under the mountain and made a bargain: protection for our people, in a world that burns people like your sister and like you. The price was set then, and it has been paid ever since, and the ward is not weather, Ivo. The ward is a wound that we keep open on purpose, because the alternative was a pyre.*

*The first price was paid by me. They wanted a soul — a living soul, given freely, by blood of the founder's line. They wanted the strongest gift in the family, and Sera's was the strongest gift this valley has produced in two hundred years. I signed the contract. I carried her to the mountain gate myself. I told the village she had died of winter fever, and I wove her burial cloth on this loom, and I hid the contract in the loom because I could not burn it and I could not look at it, and because — gods forgive me — because the loom was the only thing in this house that knew what I had done, every day, under my hands.*

*The ward runs on her. It has run on her for sixteen years. Each year the court sends up the bill, in a manner of speaking, and each year the elders pay it, and none of us remember what we are paying, only that we must. The light you have seen your whole life over the western ridge is her, Ivo. It is her suffering, made into a wall around us, and I bought our safety with my daughter, and I would do it again, and I will burn for it, and I am sorry, Sera. I am sorry. I kept the light burning as long as I could.*

*Now the light is failing, which means the bargain is ending, which means they are coming to collect what remains. There will be a fox on the road east of the ford. Trust it or don't — it serves no one, which in this world is nearly the same as serving everyone. Go east. Do not tell the elders. Do not tell anyone. And Ivo — if you find her, if there is anything left of her to find —*

The letter ended there. No signature. Just a blot where the ink had been touched by something wet, long ago, and left to dry.

* * *

I do not know how long I sat at the kitchen table. The kettle boiled dry and the stove ticked as it cooled and the gray day turned to gray dusk outside the window, and I sat, and the letter lay in front of me like a wound that had finally been uncovered after years under bandages.

A sister. A sister named Sera, four years older than me, with a gift larger than mine, taken at six years old — taken by my grandmother's own hands, carried up the mountain road, given to a court beneath the stone so that a valley of strangers could go on pretending the world was safe. And the wardlight I had loved my whole life, the green shimmer I had watched from the ridge on summer evenings and thought, *this is what home looks like* — that was her. That was a six-year-old girl's terror, stretched thin over sixteen years, spun into a wall.

I thought of the loom. Sixty years of weaving. I thought of my grandmother's hands on the frame, day after day, decade after decade, and I understood at last why she had woven so much, why the loom had never been silent, why she had made cloth the way other people pray. She wasn't making cloth. She was doing penance at a machine built for hiding things, and she had hidden the evidence of her sin inside its body, where her hands touched it every day, where she could never once forget.

And I thought — and this is the part I am ashamed of, the part I have never said aloud — I thought: *she chose Sera because Sera was stronger.* Not because Sera was older. Not because Sera was hers and I was — what, less hers? No. I thought it anyway, in the raw first hour of grief, the ugly thought rising like a bubble through dark water: *she gave the stronger one, and kept me.* And then I hated myself for it, and then I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was exactly the arithmetic the court beneath wanted everyone doing — measuring souls, weighing gifts, deciding which children were currency.

That was what the valley had bought, all these years, with my sister's suffering. Not just safety. The right to do that arithmetic and call it love.

I stood. My legs were stiff and my eyes were dry — I had cried everything I had at her deathbed, and there was nothing left, only a cold bright anger that sat in my chest like a coal that would not go out.

The stone slab still lay on the table where I had left it, its moving lines restless under the lamplight. The contract. *A soul given freely, by blood of the founder's line.* I turned it over, and on the back, in that same crawling script, was a name I had missed: not my grandmother's, not the court's.

*Sera Amberdale.* Written the way you'd write a title deed. The way you'd brand a beast.

And beneath the name, smaller, in a different hand — a hand that shook, a child's hand or a hand that had been made childlike by what it held — three words that I read a dozen times and would read a dozen thousand times in the years after, words that followed me up out of the valley and under the mountain and through everything that came after:

*I can hear you.*

She had heard us. All those years under the stone, inside the wardlight, she had heard the valley humming above her — the goats and the loom and the crows and, once, perhaps, a baby's cry from a house on the hill. My cry. She had heard her mother's apology whispered to the ceiling, night after night, year after year.

*I can hear you.*

Outside, full dark had come. And over the western ridge, for the first time in my life, I saw the wardlight do something it had never done: it flickered, guttered, and went out — all of it, the whole green shimmer, gone in a single breath, like a lamp pinched between someone's fingers.

The valley lay open to the night sky for the first time in two hundred years.

And from the direction of the ford, far off, thin on the wind, I heard a sound I had no name for yet — though I would learn it, soon, on the road east: the voice of a fox, laughing.
