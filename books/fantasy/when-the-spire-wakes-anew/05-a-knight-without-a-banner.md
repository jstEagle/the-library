## 5. A Knight Without a Banner

A fox knows the smell of ruin the way a priest knows incense.

Nettle had been tracking it for three days — not Ivo Amberdale, who moved through the world like someone apologizing for their own footsteps, but the other thing, the older thing, the sour reek of a man who had once worn honor like armor and now wore nothing at all. It led down out of the hills to a border town called Gravenhollow, which was less a town than an argument between two kingdoms that both refused to claim it, and it led finally to a tavern called The Drowned Bell, where the ale was watered with river-water and the company was watered with regret.

The fox sat beneath the eaves across the lane and watched the light spill yellow from the windows, and he considered his approach the way a surgeon considers a knife.

There were, as always, two roads into a man like this. Flattery was the wide road, smooth and well-trodden, and it worked on most knights because most knights had been starving for praise since the day they were weaned. Blackmail was the narrow road, muddy and unpleasant, but it held fast in all weathers. Nettle had walked both many times. He had found that the best journeys used one road going out and the other coming home.

He shook the mud off his paws — a habit left over from hands, though he'd have denied it under torture — and trotted across the lane, and pushed open the door of The Drowned Bell with the confidence of a creature who had never once been thrown out of anywhere he intended to be inside.

* * *

The tavern smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who drank at midday on a weekday. Nettle threaded between the benches, low to the ground, reading the room the way other creatures read tracks. Farmers. A carter asleep in his cups. Two wool-merchants arguing about tariffs. And in the darkest corner, back to the wall, a big man sitting alone before three empty mugs arranged with the neatness of a man who needed order somewhere in his life because he could find it nowhere else.

Ser Aldric of the Vale. Once banner-knight to House Merrow, sworn shield, holder of the King's Peace along the Thornmark. Now a man with a sword too fine for his purse and a scar running from ear to collarbone like a seam where someone had taken him apart and sewn him back wrong.

Nettle hopped onto the bench opposite him.

Aldric did not startle. That was the first mark in his favor. He simply looked down at the fox with the flat, tired eyes of a man deciding how much trouble this was worth, and said, "Kitchen's closed."

"I'm not here for scraps," Nettle said.

The mug stopped halfway to the knight's mouth.

To his credit — and Ntle was keeping score, because keeping score was most of what he had left — Aldric did not shout, did not reach for the sword leaning against the wall, did not do any of the dozen things a sensible man would have done upon hearing a fox speak. He set the mug down slowly, the way you set down something that might go off, and looked around the tavern to see if anyone else had noticed. No one had. The farmers were arguing about drainage. The carter snored.

"You're a demon," Aldric said at last.

"I'm a fox who talks," Nettle said. "Demons are much better dressed." He sat back on his haunches, tail curled neatly over his forepaws, and let the silence stretch. Silence was a tool too; most men rushed to fill it, and whatever they filled it with was usually useful.

Aldric lasted longer than most. But he was three mugs deep and hollowed out besides, and eventually the question came out of him like blood from a reopened wound: "What do you want?"

"Your help."

"No."

"You haven't heard the price yet."

"The price is always worse than the help." But the knight's eyes had sharpened, and Nettle saw the soldier wake behind the drunk the way a hearth wakes when someone finally feeds it. "Out with it, beast. What hold do you think you have on me?"

"None at all," Nettle said pleasantly. "That's what makes this so pleasant. I'm here to offer you a gift, Ser Aldric. Though if gifts don't move you, I can arrange a hold by nightfall. There's a woman in the room above the stable whose husband is a tax-collector for the usurper Veyra, and there's a letter in your pack written to a magistrate in Corvane begging for reinstatement, and there are four people in this tavern who saw you sell your horse's shoes off its feet last winter to pay a gambling debt in Thornmark. Any of those threads, pulled gently, unravels you entirely."

The stillness that came over Aldric then was remarkable. Not rage — Nettle had expected rage, had braced for the flash of steel and the undignified scramble of a fox exiting through a window. Instead the knight went quiet the way deep water is quiet, and when he spoke his voice was low and even.

"That's quite a list for a stranger."

"I'm not a stranger. I've been watching you for six days."

"Why?"

"Because I need a knight," Nettle said, "and you're the nearest one, and the only other ones within fifty miles are either dead, bought, or Veyra's. Which leaves you." He tilted his head, one ear cocking. "Also you're cheap."

Something happened at the corner of Aldric's mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile, or the beginning of a punch. "Flattery," he said, "and blackmail, in the same breath."

"I find they travel well together. Like salt and honey."

"And if I say no? If I throw you out?"

"Then you'll drink yourself back to sleep tonight, and tomorrow you'll hate yourself a little more than usual, and the day after that a little more again, until there isn't enough left of you to bury." Nettle let his voice soften — not much, just enough to slip under a man's guard, the way a paw slips under a door. "I know why you drink, Ser Aldric. I know what you did at Millbrook. I know you signed the order."

The mug cracked. Aldric's hand had closed around it without his seeming to notice, and hairline fracture ran through the clay, and ale seeped out between his fingers onto the table like something wounded.

"You don't know anything," he said.

"I know everything," said the fox, more gently than he meant to. "It's my affliction."

* * *

They ended up outside, because Aldric wouldn't talk in there — too many ears, even deaf ones — and they walked down to the ford where the border-river ran shallow and loud over its stones, and the evening came down purple around them. Nettle trotted at the knight's heel like a dog, which annoyed him profoundly, and he did it anyway, because the resemblance made men underestimate him and underestimation was the finest armor ever forged.

"Millbrook," Aldric said finally. They stood at the water's edge. He hadn't looked at the fox since leaving the tavern. "You want to hear it? Is that the price? My confession, for your — what? Your errand?"

"The confession is free. You've been trying to give it away for years; I'm just the first one who'd take it."

The knight laughed then, one short ugly note. "Gods. You *are* a demon."

"I told you. A fox." Nettle sat down on a flat stone, wrapped his tail around himself, and waited. He was very good at waiting. It was, after all, mostly what foxes did between meals.

And Aldric talked. Standing at the black water with his back straight as if reporting to a superior officer, in the clipped voice of a man reciting charges already read aloud to him every night for eleven years. How he had been captain of the garrison at Millbrook, a village in the hill country where the old shrines still stood and the old gods still answered, small gods only — a god of wells, a god of hedgerows, a god of the lambing season no bigger than a sparrow. How Veyra's writ had come down, sealed and lawful: the villages that fed the old gods were feeding resistance, and resistance was treason, and treason required the standard response. How he had signed the evacuation order that emptied forty houses, knowing — because everyone knew, it wasn't even a secret, it was arithmetic — that emptied villages fed nothing, and unfed gods starved, and starved gods took their blessing out of the ground with them when they went, and the ground remembered. How the wheat at Millbrook had come up gray the following spring. How a child he'd carried on his shoulders during the evacuation had died in the resettlement camp of a fever that good food might have fought off.

"I didn't burn anything," Aldric said. "I never swung a sword at anyone who wasn't armed. I signed my name. That's all. Ink." His hand flexed at his side. "Eleven years I've been telling myself ink isn't blood."

"And is it?"

"It's worse," he said. "Blood washes out."

The river went on talking over the stones. Somewhere upstream an owl asked its single question and got no answer. Nettle found, to his mild irritation, that his throat felt tight — a sensation left over from another body, another life, another set of sins that weighed roughly the same. He had come here intending to use this man. He still intended to use this man. But there was no rule in the world that said you couldn't pity a thing you were using. He'd checked.

"Here's the errand," Nettle said. "Veyra has begun harvesting names. Whole regions, stripped bare. The gods she doesn't devour she drives mad, and the mad ones break their own altars, and the people who lived by them come flooding onto the roads like water from a breached dam. In the east they're burning villages where prayer still works. And in a valley called Hollowmere, a ward that has stood for generations is failing, and a child of that valley is walking out of it carrying something Veyra would burn the world to own."

"What thing?"

"A voice," Nettle said. "The kind that hasn't existed since the first spire went dark. The kind that speaks to beasts, and beasts answer." He watched Aldric's face carefully. "The kind that could command every living thing between here and the mountain, if its owner ever learned what it was for."

Silence, except the river.

Then Aldric said, quietly, without turning, "The traveler. On the north road yesterday. Young, hooded, traveling alone. Stopped at the shrine by the mile-marker — the little ruined one, with the dry well." His jaw worked. "There was a crow on the shrine. Ragged thing. And when the traveler passed, the crow bowed. Bowed, like a courtier. And the traveler said—" He stopped.

"Said?" Nettle prompted softly, though he knew. Oh, he knew. He'd been watching from the hedge.

"Said 'thank you' to it. And the crow answered. Not cawing. *Words*." Aldric turned at last, and his eyes in the dusk were bleak and awake and terribly sober for a man who'd had three mugs. "I've served Veyra. I've seen her nametakers work. I know what happens to people like that when she finds them. So I kept walking. I said nothing. Told myself it was none of my affair."

"Yes," Nettle said. "You recognized them, and you said nothing, and now you feel like a coward. Good. Guilt is the one honest thing left in you; we'll build on it." He rose, stretched, and shook out his fur. "Here's the shape of it, knight-without-a-banner. I'm taking that traveler to the mountain court, whether they wish to go or not, because what they carry is the only coin in the world that buys what needs buying. You're coming with us, because a fox cannot swing a sword and the roads ahead are full of men who will want to. In exchange, you get what you actually want, which isn't ale and isn't absolution — it's a chance to stand between Veyra and one innocent person and *hold*. One time. That's all penance ever is. One time, held."

Aldric stared down at him for a long moment. The owl asked its question again, closer now.

"You're blackmailing me," the knight said slowly, "with my own conscience."

"I prefer to think of it as returning lost property."

And there it was at last, unmistakable: the ghost of the smile, surfacing and staying. Aldric looked out at the dark water, and the dark hills beyond, and the whole broken world spread out under the first stars, and Nettle watched him weigh it — not whether to say yes, that was already decided, Nettle had known it was decided from the moment the word *Millbrook* landed — but how much dignity he could salvage while saying it.

"One condition," Aldric said.

"Name it."

"You tell the traveler what I did. Before they trust me. All of it. I won't have them owing their life to a man they don't know."

Nettle blinked. Of all the terms he had collected in a long career of collecting terms, that was among the rarest. "Done," he said. "Though I warn you, honesty makes a poor bodyguard. It gets distracted."

"So does drink," said Aldric, and turned back toward the town with his spine straight and his stride long, a man remembering the shape of himself. "We leave at dawn."

"Dawn," Nettle agreed, trotting at his heel. "Oh — and Ser Aldric?"

"What."

"Do stop buying ale with the coins in your left boot. Everyone checks the left boot."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and the frost lay on Gravenhollow like a second skin, and at the north gate of the town, where the road climbed away toward the hills and the valley hidden beyond them, three figures met that had no business meeting: a disgraced knight in a rusted hauberk with a sword too fine for his purse; a red fox sitting neatly on a milestone as though milestones were thrones; and a young traveler in a hooded cloak, standing very still, looking from one to the other with the wary eyes of someone who had spent a lifetime learning that being noticed was dangerous.

Nettle had told them everything the night before, as promised — the order signed, the village emptied, the gray wheat. He had watched Ivo Amberdale take it in: the flinch, the long pause, the slow nod of someone who had grown up hiding things and therefore recognized a fellow hider at any distance.

Now the traveler looked at the knight and said, in that soft valley voice, "Did you mean to hurt anyone?"

"No," said Aldric.

"But you did."

"Yes."

Another pause. Frost smoked on the travelers' breath. Then Ivo Amberdale pulled the hood back — revealing a face young and tired and stubborn as a hedgerow oak — and said, "Neither did our elders, I think. Come on, then. We've a long way to walk, and the road's full of people running."

They started north together, three and a fox, as the sun broke the hills behind them and threw their shadows long down the road ahead — shadows that stretched and stretched toward the distant line of mountains, where something beneath the stone was counting souls like coins.

Far behind them, at the edge of sight, dust was rising on the south road. Too regular for weather. Too steady for carts.

An army moves like that, Nettle thought, and said nothing, and lengthened his stride.
