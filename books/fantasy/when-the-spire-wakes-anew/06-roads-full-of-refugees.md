## 6. Roads Full of Refugees

The road taught Ivo what hunger sounded like when it had a thousand mouths.

They had been walking east for nine days, out of the hill country and down onto the long gray flats where the free cities began their sprawl of walls and watchtowers, and in all that time the traffic on the road had only thickened. Carts drawn by oxen too thin to complain. Families with everything they owned bundled into blankets on their backs. Old men leading children by the hand because there was no one else left to lead them. The dust hung over the road like a curtain that never quite finished falling, and everyone breathed it, and everyone coughed it, and no one spoke above a murmur, as if loudness might summon something out of the horizon.

Ivo walked among them and felt, for the first time in their life, ordinary.

That should have been a comfort. In Hollowmere, they had spent twenty-two years folding themselves smaller and smaller — the girl who talked too much to goats, the boy who laughed at crows' jokes, whichever face fit the season — until the secret had grown a shell around them like a pearl around grit. Here, on this road, nobody knew them. Nobody watched. They could have spoken to every animal they met, and the worst that would happen was a stranger thinking them mad.

But the shell did not crack. It only grew heavier.

"You're doing it again," Nettle said.

Ivo looked down. The fox trotted at their heel wearing the particular expression of a creature who has been patient far past the point of virtue. To everyone else on the road, he looked like an ordinary red fox, lean and autumn-bright, following a traveler for scraps. Only Ivo heard the dry amusement underneath.

"Doing what?"

"Carrying your shoulders up near your ears like you're afraid the sky will bite them. You've done it since the ford." Nettle's whiskers twitched. "You're not in the valley anymore, little warden. Nobody here knows what you are. Half of them don't know what *they* are anymore, if Veyra's nametakers got to them."

"I know," Ivo said. "It doesn't help."

"No," Nettle agreed cheerfully. "Fear rarely listens to reason. That's what makes it fear."

Behind them, Ser Aldric made a sound that might once have been a laugh. The knight walked three paces back, as he always did — close enough to guard, far enough to pretend he wasn't part of them. He had shaved since the border town, and without the beard his face looked younger and more tired at once, like a house with fresh paint over old cracks. His sword rode his hip openly now. On this road, an armed man drew less notice than a nervous one.

"Fox is right," Aldric said. "Though it pains me to say so twice in one day."

"It pains you?" Nettle's tail curled. "Imagine being right all the time. The burden is mine."

"The burden," Aldric said, "is the crowd ahead. Look."

Ivo looked. The road crested a low rise half a mile on, and beyond it the refugee stream pooled and backed up like water against a dam. There was a checkpoint there — they'd been hearing about it for two days, passed from mouth to mouth down the line of travelers like a fever: *nametakers at Millbrook crossing, they take your name, they write it in a black book, you come out the other side not quite yourself.* Some people were going around through the marsh to the north, though the marsh took its own toll in fever and lost children. Most people waited. What else could anyone do?

And beyond the checkpoint, far off across the flats, a smudge of smoke stood against the afternoon sky. Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke rose thin and domestic. This smoke stood in a column, fat and black and unhurried, the way smoke stands when there is nothing left behind it worth saving.

"That'll be Wetherall," Aldric said quietly. "Or what was. Third one this month where the gods still answered."

Ivo's stomach turned over. "Answered how?"

"How do you think?" The knight's voice was flat, but Ivo had learned in nine days to hear the things he flattened it over. "A village well that heals. A grove where the dead can be asked questions. A shrine that tells you when the rains will fail. Little gods. Small mercies. The kind of divinity you can hold in two hands." He spat dust. "Veyra burns those first. A god that answers prayers makes people brave, and brave people ask questions, and questions are the one harvest she won't allow."

"And the people?"

"People who lived next to answering gods?" Aldric looked at the smoke a long moment. "Some run. Some burn. She gives them the choice, which she considers generosity."

Nettle said nothing at all, which from Nettle was louder than speech.

They walked on. The column of smoke kept pace with them along the horizon, patient as grief.

* * *

They camped that night in a fallow field with forty other families, because the road had become a village that moved. Ivo sat against a stone wall whose mortar had crumbled a generation ago and watched the fires bloom one by one across the dark like slow orange flowers. Someone was singing, very softly, a song about a river that ran uphill toward heaven. Someone else was crying, equally softly, in the rhythm of someone who had been crying for days and had learned to do it quietly so as not to spend the family's remaining strength.

Aldric sat sharpening his sword by firelight. Nettle lay across Ivo's feet, warm as a loaf, pretending to sleep and listening to everything.

It was the horses Ivo heard first.

Animals carried grief differently than people. People wept or sang or went silent; animals wore it in the body, in the way a dog stopped eating, the way a goat stood facing a corner. And horses — Ivo had grown up around goats and crows, but horses came to the valley only at shearing fairs, big and anxious and honest — horses grieved loudly, in the language of breath.

There was a picket line at the field's edge where the travelers who still had livestock kept them, and among the sounder of breathing, one horse was breathing wrong. Shallow. Quick. With a catch in it, like a door hinge that needed oiling, except the hinge was a lung.

Ivo told themself they were just going to look.

The horse was a bay mare, maybe ten years old, lying down when horses should not lie down, her head propped on a hay bale a child had dragged over for her. A family clustered nearby — a mother, two boys, an old grandfather with his hat in his hands — and they had the look of people who had already begun saying goodbye and hated themselves for starting early.

"She colicked three days ago," the older boy said when he noticed Ivo standing there. He couldn't have been more than twelve, but he said it in the flat voice of someone reciting a diagnosis learned from adults. "We gave her everything the herb-wife said. She got better and then she got worse. Now she won't stand."

"We can't pull the cart without her," the younger one added, and his mother shushed him, but everyone was thinking it. Everyone always thought it. The animal was dying and the first grief was real and the second grief, arriving right behind it, was arithmetic.

Ivo crouched by the mare's head. Up close she smelled of sweat and illness and the sweet-rot undertone of a body beginning to fail. Her eye rolled to find them, huge and dark and lit by the distant fires, and in it Ivo saw the thing that had pulled them across the field before thought could stop it: the mare was frightened, and beneath the fright she was ashamed, because horses are herd creatures and she believed, in whatever way horses believe things, that lying here unable to rise was a failure of duty. She was sorry. She was *sorry*, and she was trying to apologize to the child who had given her the hay bale, and she could not make herself understood.

Twenty-two years of silence rose in Ivo's throat like water.

*I hear you,* Ivo said, in the way that is not words but is understood as words, the way their grandmother had forbidden, the way the valley had buried, the way that would get them burned in any city that remembered why it feared it. *I hear you. You haven't failed. You're sick, and sick isn't failure.*

The mare's ear flicked toward them. Her breath caught — and then, astonishingly, eased, one degree, the way a clenched hand eases when someone holds it.

Behind Ivo, the family had gone very still.

"What's she doing?" the younger boy whispered.

"Hush," said the mother. But she didn't pull the children away. Later Ivo would understand why: because the mare's breathing had changed audibly, and hope makes theologians of us all.

*I can help,* Ivo told the mare. *Maybe. I need you to trust me, and I need you to breathe when I ask.* They put a hand on the great warm neck, feeling the flutter of the pulse beneath skin, and did the thing they had done a hundred times in the dark of the goat shed and never once in the light of other human eyes: they reached.

Beast-speech was not magic, exactly. Or it was, but not the kind with sparks and sigils. It was closer to listening so completely that the boundary between listener and listened-to went soft as wet paper. Ivo sank into the mare's body the way you sink into a warm bath: the ache of the gut, knotted where the colic had twisted it; the fever burning in her blood like a banked coal; the exhaustion sitting on her heart like a hand. And around it all, the simple vast animal truth of her — grass, wind, the road, the child, the duty, the fear.

*Easy,* Ivo breathed, and meant it into her. *Easy. Let the knot loosen. Breathe down. Slow. Again.*

The knot did not obey because Ivo commanded it. It obeyed because the mare believed them — believed that help had finally come in a form she could understand — and a body that believes can do astonishing things. Ivo felt the cramp ease by degrees, felt the fever break like a cloud passing off the sun, felt the heartbeat find a slower floor. Sweat broke out along their own spine; this always cost something, a coin paid from some inner purse they had never been able to count. Their vision went silver at the edges. They held on.

*Breathe down. Good. Good girl. Now — try. Just try.*

The bay mare gathered herself and stood.

It was not graceful. She swayed; the old man lunged forward and steadied her head with both hands, laughing and crying at once. She planted her feet, blew out a long shuddering breath through wide nostrils, and turned her head and lipped gently at the younger boy's hair.

The silence lasted three full seconds.

Then the whole field seemed to exhale at once. The mother seized Ivo's hands and pressed them between her own and called it a blessing, called them blessed, called it a saint's touch, and the word *saint* went through Ivo like ice water, because saints were one step from miracles and miracles were one step from the pyre. The grandfather removed his hat entirely. The boys stared with the uncomplicated worship of children. And along the picket line, and then further, word moving faster than any horse: *the Amberdale traveler healed the Marsh family's mare. Touched her and she stood. She'd been dying three days.*

By full dark, thirty people had come to look at Ivo as though looking might reveal the mechanism. By midnight, five of them had asked — hesitantly, hopefully, shamefully — whether the gift worked on people. A baby with a fever, in the next field. An old woman failing. A man coughing blood.

Ivo stood by the fire with Nettle's warm weight suddenly gone tense against their leg and Aldric's hand resting, very casually, very deliberately, on his sword hilt, and understood with perfect clarity what they had done.

They had traded twenty-two years of silence for one act of mercy, and the price was only now coming due.

* * *

"They'll talk," Aldric said, low, after the family had finally gone to sleep and the field had settled into its murmuring dark. "You know that. By tomorrow night there will be a story on this road with no name attached to it yet, and stories without names grow names. *A healer. A saint. A witch.* Pick your ending."

"Would you rather I'd let it die?"

Aldric didn't answer immediately. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, the bay mare nickered softly to her boys, and the sound was whole and easy, and Ivo held onto it like a rope over a drop.

"No," the knight said at last. "That's the hell of it. No, I wouldn't. I've watched enough things die that could have been saved because saving them was inconvenient." His jaw worked. "I signed papers once about inconvenience. Whole villages' worth." He looked into the fire. "So no. I'm not angry at you. I'm angry at the world that makes mercy a confession. It's a distinction without much comfort, but it's the one I have."

"He's proud of you, by the way," Nettle said. "He'd eat his own boots before saying it. I can smell pride. Also fear. The road smells of fear generally, but his has your name in it now, which is new."

"Fear of Veyra?" Ivo asked.

"Fear *for* you. Worse. Much worse." The fox stretched, claws flexing in the dirt. "Here's what matters, little warden, and listen closely because I'll only say it once and I intend to be insufferably modest about having seen it coming: you cannot carry this gift across Veyra's country in a box anymore. It's out. It's loose. It's a story traveling faster than we are. So the question isn't whether to hide it — that door's shut — the question is what the story becomes before it reaches her ears. A rumor of a wandering saint? Useful. A report of unlicensed magic? That gets nametakers. That gets worse than nametakers."

"So we go faster," Ivo said.

"We go smarter. Faster helps." Nettle's eyes caught the firelight, green-gold and ancient. "And we use what you've done tonight, instead of apologizing for it. These people have nothing. Nothing but each other and their stories, and stories, properly tended, are armor. Tomorrow, when they ask again — the baby with the fever — say yes. Say yes carefully, publicly, and let them see it costs you. A gift freely witnessed binds the witness. Make them your witnesses, Ivo. Make them remember you kindly. Because the day Veyra's riders come asking *have you seen a beast-speaker*, every soul in this field is going to lie for you — or they aren't. Tonight decided which."

Ivo looked out over the sleeping field. Forty fires banked to embers, forty families folded together against the cold, and somewhere among them the baby with the fever, and somewhere the story already growing its wings.

"I never wanted to be anyone's saint," they said quietly.

"No one ever does. That's how you can tell the real ones."

* * *

They left before dawn, while the field still slept, but not before the mother of the mare found Ivo pressing a bundle into their arms: bread, hard cheese, a jar of honey, and a small carved wooden horse, rough-made by a child's knife.

"For luck," she said. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Whatever you're running from. Whatever you're running toward." She glanced, briefly, at the fox trotting at Ivo's heel — a glance that lingered half a heartbeat too long, and said nothing, and gave nothing away. "Go safely, healer."

Ivo carried the wooden horse in their pocket all that day, thumb finding its rough mane whenever the fear rose.

They made good miles with the dawn behind them and the smoke column fading astern, and by mid-afternoon the road bent away from the flats toward a line of low hills, and the hills had a different smell — old stone, deep water, something green and cold. Nettle's ears came up.

"There's a shrine over that ridge," the fox said abruptly. All the play had gone out of his voice. "Ruined. Older than the road. We should water there before the last stretch to the crossing."

"A shrine?" Aldric frowned. "Out here?"

"Ruined," Nettle repeated, with weight. "Emphasis on ruined."

The path left the road through a gap in a drystone wall and climbed between hawthorns still holding their last few leaves. At the top, in a bowl of grass gone wild, stood what remained of a well-house: four broken pillars, a roof fallen in on itself like a cupped hand closing, and a well in the center, ringed with stones carved so long ago the faces on them had weathered into suggestion.

And the well was singing.

Not with water. With something thinner. Ivo stopped dead at the treeline, the hairs rising on their arms, because the sound was almost — almost — the sound of beast-speech, but wrong, strained, a voice speaking through layers of stone and distance and drowning. It was not addressed to them. It was addressed to no one. It was the sound of something calling for help in a place where help had stopped coming years ago.

"You hear it too," Nettle said softly. Not a question.

"Yes." Ivo's mouth was dry. "What is it?"

The fox sat back on his haunches and looked at the ruined well for a long moment, and when he answered, he used the careful tone he saved for prices.

"A god, I think. A small one. And I think, little warden, that it's drowning."
