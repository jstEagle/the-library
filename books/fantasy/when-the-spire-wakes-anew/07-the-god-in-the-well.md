## 7. The God in the Well

They found the shrine by accident, which was perhaps the only way anyone found anything holy anymore.

The road had bent away from the river into a fold of hills, and Nettle — who claimed to know every road in the world and lied about half of them — insisted the shortcut would save them a day. Aldric grumbled. The horse, whose name Ivo had learned in the first hour of their acquaintance and never repeated aloud, tossed her head and said *dirt* and *thirsty* and *why are we going up*, and Ivo translated none of it, only loosened the girth and let her pick her footing.

The hills here were old in a way Hollowmere's hills were not. In the valley, everything felt held, cushioned, wrapped in wool; these slopes were bare-boned, wind-scoured, honest about their age. Gorse hissed in the wind like something whispering behind its hand. And set into the shoulder of the third hill, half-swallowed by bramble and slate, stood the shrine.

It had been small once, and beloved. Ivo could tell that from what remained: the threshold stone worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of feet; the niches where offerings had sat, still holding rainwater and one desiccated apple; the carved lintel, so weathered that its inscription had softened into something like birdsong. A roof of split shingles had mostly fallen in. Moss had taken the walls. But someone had loved this place once, had swept it and sung in it and brought their sorrows here in their two hands, and the stones remembered.

Ivo felt the remembering before they understood it.

It came as a pressure behind the eyes, faint and sweet, like the first note of a song heard through a wall. Twenty-two years inside the ward had taught Ivo to distrust that sensation, to swallow it, to look at goats and crows and see only animals. But there was no ward here. There was nothing between Ivo and the world's quiet voices at all.

*Ivo,* said Nettle, and the fox's voice had gone strange, careful. "You've gone white as milk. What do you hear?"

"Nothing," Ivo said, which was true, and then, because lying to Nettle was like lying to a knife: "Something. Like a room where someone has just stopped crying."

Aldric's hand had already gone to his sword. He did that now whenever Ivo went still and listening, and Ivo hadn't decided whether it was protectiveness or fear or both wearing each other's clothes.

The well stood behind the shrine, sunken into the hillside like an eye that had grown tired of seeing. It was stone-rimmed, older than the building, and dry — or it should have been. As they drew closer Ivo heard water. Not rushing. Not dripping. Something worse: a slow, laborious sloshing, like a man treading water who has forgotten how, like breathing done wrong on purpose.

Ivo looked over the rim.

Far down — farther than any well dug by human hands ought to go — there was light. Not reflected daylight. A gold so deep it was nearly brown, the color of candle-flame seen through closed eyelids, of honey held up to a dying sun. It moved. It struggled. And rising up out of it, thin as smoke off a snuffed wick, came a voice.

"*Please.*"

The word was not loud. It did not echo. It simply arrived, the way grief arrives, all at once and everywhere. The horse shied. Aldric swore. Nettle sat down on the mossy rim with the deliberate calm of a creature deciding not to run.

"It's drowning," Ivo said. Their own voice sounded far away. "There's a god down there. And it's drowning."

* * *

They argued for a while, in the way travelers argue — Aldric insisting it could be a trap, that things which called for help from holes in the ground had ended more knights than he cared to count; Nettle observing with terrible mildness that if Veyra's people wanted to catch beast-speakers they hardly needed to bait wells with gods. Ivo barely heard them. Ivo was already uncoiling the rope from the horse's pack, already testing the rusted iron ring set beside the wellhead, already doing the thing their whole life had trained them never to do: answering.

"I'm going down," Ivo said.

"You're going to break your neck," said Aldric.

"Probably," said Nettle, "but not today. The rope will hold. The ring won't." He flicked his tail toward the wellhouse wall. "Wrap it round that pillar instead. Stone's sounder than the iron."

So it was arranged, the way everything with Nettle got arranged — three steps ahead, reasons given after. Aldric anchored the rope around the pillar and lowered Ivo down with the grim competence of a man who had hauled comrades out of worse places and buried some of them too. The dark took Ivo gently at first, then completely.

The walls wept. Not water exactly — condensation, cold mineral breath, the sweat of old stone. Deeper, the shaft widened into a chamber, and the chamber held water to Ivo's knees, warm as blood, glowing faintly from below. And standing in it — no, *floating*, no, *kneeling*, it changed each time Ivo blinked — was the god.

Small as a coin. That was Ivo's first coherent thought, and it was accurate and absurd at once. The god was small as a coin, if a coin could burn. A disc of dim gold, wavering above the water, its edges fraying like cloth rubbed too long between fingers. It had no face. It had no face, and yet Ivo knew, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, that it was looking at them. That it had been looking at them since they crossed the threshold of the shrine. That it had been looking, perhaps, for years.

"You came," it said. Its voice was the voice of a very old person reading a letter from someone long dead. "No one comes anymore. They stopped coming when the bells changed. Do you remember when the bells changed? No. Of course you don't. You're young. You're *alive*. Oh, you're so alive it hurts to look at you."

"What are you?" Ivo asked, though they knew. Everyone in Hollowmere grew up knowing the shapes of the old stories: gods small as coins, gods drowned in wells, gods eaten by queens. Knowing a story and kneeling in its cold water were different countries.

"A keeper of thresholds," the god said. "A hearth-lighter. A finder of lost lambs and lost keys and lost reasons to keep going. Nothing grand. Nothing worth a war. Which is why I am still here, and my brothers are wind." A ripple moved across the water, though nothing had touched it. "My name was spoken every morning in this valley for nine hundred years. Now it is not spoken anywhere, and a god who is not named is a lamp with no oil. I have been burning myself to stay lit."

"Drowning," Ivo whispered. "You said—"

"The well was mine. Pilgrims dropped their prayers into me like coins, and I kept them, every one, and answered what I could. But prayers need believers the way fire needs air, and when the last believer died—" the coin-gold guttered, brightened, guttered again— "the well began filling with everything I had swallowed and never returned. My own hoard. I am drowning in answered prayers, little speaker. Is that not a fine way for a god to end?"

Ivo stood knee-deep in warm light and found, to their horror, that their eyes were stinging. This was pity of a kind they had no practice with — not the clean pity of seeing a lame lamb, but a vertigo, a falling inward. Here was a being older than kingdoms, reduced to a spark in a hole, asking nothing but to be witnessed.

"What do you want from me?"

"Only passage." The god drifted closer, and its light lay on Ivo's hands like water. "One errand. One prayer carried to the mountain court, spoken aloud, in a voice that can still be heard there. Mine cannot. I have tried for sixty years. My words come out of this well as mist." A pause, and when it spoke again the ancient weariness cracked, just slightly, and underneath was something rawer. "It is a prayer for my sister. She is held beneath the mountain. She is held the way lamps hold flame. And I am too small now, too dim, too *drowned* — I cannot even reach her to say goodbye."

The word *sister* went through Ivo like a hook.

Somewhere overhead, muffled through fathoms of stone, Aldric's voice called down, asking if they were alive. Ivo did not answer. The water rose — or the god sank — and its edges were thinner than before, translucent as horn.

"If your sister is beneath the mountain," Ivo said slowly, "then she's in the court's keeping. Everything the court keeps, it keeps for a price. Why should I spend my breath on a prayer they'll laugh at? Why should they listen at all?"

"They will listen," the god said, "because you will bring them something they want. Not the prayer. That is only the key that opens your mouth." The light steadied, gathered itself, dimmed deliberately, the way a person dims a lamp to make a shadow show better. "I will give you a map. Not ink. Not paper. Those can be taken from you at the nametakers' checkpoints, and you will pass checkpoints, little speaker, before you ever see the mountain's root. No. I will write it where no thief can reach — in your sleep. Every night, one dream further along the road. You will dream your way to the heart of the first spire, to the place where wards are rooted and souls are weighed. And when you stand before whatever wears the crown down there, you will speak my prayer, and the prayer itself will open doors that names and gold cannot."

"And what does the prayer cost me?"

"Nothing," said the god, and then, gentler, more honestly than anything it had said yet: "Nothing but the carrying. Prayer is heavy, little speaker. It is made of everyone who ever hoped it. You will feel its weight in your chest from here to the mountain, and you may hate me by the end, and I will understand, and I will thank you anyway."

Ivo thought of Sera. The name was still new, still raw, a wound less than a season old — a sister sold in a loom-house by lamplight, a soul burning in a lantern under the world while a valley slept safe. Ivo thought: *someone prayed for her too, once. Someone knelt somewhere and begged the dark for her life, and the dark said yes, at a price, and no one ever carried the apology back.*

"All right," Ivo said.

The silence that followed was so total that Ivo heard their own heartbeat, and beneath it, faintly, the god's — a sound like a spoon struck against a bell, slowing.

"You'll regret it," the god said, with something that might have been tenderness. "Everyone who carries a prayer regrets it. That is how you know it's real."

It rose — or seemed to rise; distance meant nothing down here — until its coin-small light hung level with Ivo's eyes. Up close, Ivo could see that the gold was full of movement, tiny figures turning over and over like motes in a sunbeam: prayers, thousands upon thousands of them, still circling inside the god's dying body like fish in a shrinking pool.

"Hold still," the god said. "This is the part that stings. Most gifts do."

It touched Ivo's brow with an edge of light, and Ivo dreamed — awake, standing, drowning-adjacent — of a mountain with a door in its throat. Of corridors lit by hanging lanterns, each lantern a face, each face singing. Of a spire like an inverted root, plunging down instead of up, and at its base a hall where something pale and patient waited behind a table of black glass. Then the dream folded itself small, small as a coin, and slid behind Ivo's eyes, and settled there with a weight like a swallowed stone.

"There," the god said, fading now, its voice coming from farther and farther away, as though it were sinking through the world itself. "Every night, a little more of the way. Guard your sleep. Sleep is a door, and doors can be watched." A last guttering brightness. "And little speaker — when you find my sister, tell her the morning bells still remember her name. Even if no one else does. Tell her the bells remember."

The light went out.

Not gradually. All at once, the way a life goes out, the water around Ivo going dark and cold in the same breath, the warmth draining down through the stone as if the hill itself had exhaled. Ivo groped for the rope with hands gone clumsy from cold and something larger than cold, and hauled upward through the dark, and came out into evening air that tasted unbearably sweet, unbearably ordinary.

* * *

Aldric pulled them over the rim by the collar, swearing steadily and without much heat, and wrapped them in a blanket, and put a tin cup of wine in their hands with the rough tenderness of men who did not know other kinds. Nettle sat close, watching Ivo with those amber eyes that saw too much.

"Well?" the fox said.

"It gave me a map," Ivo said. Their teeth chattered around the words. "In dreams. To the heart of the mountain. And a prayer to carry." They touched their own sternum, where something now sat that had not been there at dawn — dense, humming, patient. "For its sister. Who's held beneath the mountain. Like—" The sentence broke. They tried again. "Like Sera."

Nettle said nothing for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than Ivo had ever heard it. "Gods don't ask favors twice, and they don't forget debts. You know what you've become tonight? A courier for the divine. There are emperors who'd trade their thrones for that burden and kings who died fleeing it."

"I know what I've become," Ivo said. Above them the first stars were coming out, indifferent and beautiful. Somewhere down the valley, a wolf began to sing, and the horse shifted, and Ivo understood her restlessness perfectly, and said nothing. "Someone who answers."

They slept that night in the ruined shrine itself, because it seemed suddenly wrong to leave it empty. Aldric built a fire in the old hearth, the first it had held in decades, and the flames threw shadows into the niches where offerings used to sit. Ivo lay on their bedroll beneath the broken lintel, listening to the wind comb the gorse, feeling the prayer sit in their chest like a second heart.

And they dreamed. The mountain again, closer now, its throat-door yawning. But at the edge of the dream, at the border where the god's map met Ivo's own sleeping mind, something else appeared: a lantern, swinging gently in the dark of a corridor, and inside it a face turned toward them — a young woman's face, worn smooth by decades of singing, and though Ivo had never seen her in waking life, they knew her the way they knew their own hands.

Her lips moved. No sound crossed the dream. But Ivo read the shape of it easily, the way they read the speech of goats and crows, the way they read everything the world said and pretended not to.

The shape of it was: *Who is there?*

Ivo woke before dawn with wet cheeks and a chest full of borrowed weight, and outside the shrine, on the broken lintel, a crow was waiting — silent, watching, its head cocked — and when Ivo met its eye, it opened its beak and said, in a voice like tearing silk:

"The spire remembers you now. Walk carefully. So do I."
