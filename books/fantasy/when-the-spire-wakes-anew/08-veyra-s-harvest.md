## 8. Veyra's Harvest

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time Aldric began to speak, and he chose the moment deliberately. There is a kind of honesty that only comes out at that hour — when the flames have stopped performing and the dark has settled in close around the camp like a congregation waiting for a confession.

Ivo sat across from him with their knees drawn up, the fox curled against their boot as if he were merely an animal and nothing in him listened. Aldric knew better. He suspected Ivo did too, or was learning to. The kid had been on the road nine days now, long enough to lose the valley softness in their hands but not long enough to lose it in their eyes. They still looked at the world like something that could be reasoned with.

"You asked me once," Aldric said, "why a knight drinks in a town like Marrow's Ford instead of dying properly somewhere."

"I didn't ask."

"No. But you wondered. Everyone wonders." He turned his cup in his fingers. Tin, dented, older than his knighthood. "So. You want to know what's coming down the road behind us. You want to know what Veyra is." He looked up. "Then you'll have it whole. Not the ballad version. The one where I'm in it."

* * *

He had been nineteen when he first saw her, and she had not yet been Veyra. She had been a nameless acolyte in the temple-city of Aureth, one of hundreds who swept the marble floors of the god-houses and kept the incense stocked and memorized the liturgies of gods too vast to notice them. He had been a squire then, raw-boned and ambitious, in Aureth to serve his knight through the season of the Concord — the great assembly where the old gods sent their voices and the free cities renewed their bargains.

"She wasn't beautiful," Aldric said. "That's what no one understands afterward. They make her beautiful in the retellings because beauty is easier than the truth. She was *plain*. Quiet. A gray woman in gray robes, and if you'd passed her in the colonnade you'd have forgotten her before your next footstep fell.

"But she listened. Gods, how she listened. Every other soul in that city performed — the knights postured, the priests intoned, the gods themselves spoke in voices like avalanches because they wanted to be heard. And this one small woman moved through all of it like water through a wreck, listening to everything, keeping everything.

"I found her once in the reliquary cellar, where they kept the names of dead gods carved on slate. Little gods. River-mothers and hearth-wardens, the ones whose last worshipper had died generations back, so the temples filed them away like unpaid debts. She was standing among those slates with her hand flat against one of them, and her lips were moving, and I thought she was praying.

"She wasn't praying. She was *learning*."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called, and Aldric felt the old reflexive flinch — the instinct to catalog every animal sound within earshot, to know which sounds might be listening back. Nine days on the road with Ivo had taught him that animals could carry words. It had also taught him that some words should never be said twice.

"A name," he went on, "is not just what you call a thing. That's what the common folk think, and it's wrong, and Veyra built an empire on the wrongness. A true name is the *handle* of a thing. The place where it can be gripped. Mortals guard theirs loosely because most of us are small enough that no one wants the grip. But a god—" His mouth twisted. "A god's name is a lever long enough to move heaven.

"She learned them. One by one. The forgotten ones first, because no one missed them. And here is the part the histories get wrong, Ivo, the part that matters: the first ones, she didn't steal. She was *given* them. Little gods on the edge of silence, drowning in their own neglect, handed her their names the way a drowning man hands over his gold — *take it, take it, do something, anything, don't let me go out like this.* And she took them. And she used them. And every name she took made her bigger, until 'bigger' stopped being a word that fit.

"The first god she unmade outright was the Warden of the Weir. You won't know it. Nobody does anymore — that's rather my point. It kept the fish-souls moving up the river of the dead. A small duty, a faithful one, ten thousand years at its post. She came to it wearing the names of forty lesser powers like a coat of scales, and she said its name back to it, and it *came apart*. Not died. Came apart, the way a word comes apart when you say it too many times, until it's only a noise in your mouth. And she swallowed what was left, because that's what you do with power. You swallow it, or it swallows you, and she had already decided which."

Ivo's face in the firelight was very still. "And the other gods let this happen?"

"The other gods were *busy*." Aldric laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the shape of it worn smooth by years of practice. "The great ones, the storm-fathers and the harvest-mothers — they looked down at this gray woman gathering up the scraps of divinity and they thought, *vermin. Beneath notice.* The same way empires always look at the thing that will kill them. By the time the Concord understood what stood in its hall asking politely to be heard, she had enough devoured names in her to speak with the voice of a hundred dead powers at once, and the Concord's own oaths turned in their mouths and choked them.

"I was twenty-three. Newly knighted. Ser Aldric of the Marches, banner bright, sword consecrated, head full of the stories where right wins because right is heavier than wrong. And she looked across that shattered hall at me — me, a boy with a wet sword and everyone else dead or fled or kneeling — and she said my name.

"My *true* name. The one my mother whispered over my cradle. The one I'd told no living soul.

"And do you know what she did with it? Nothing. That was the horror of it. She held it up between two fingers like a jewel and said, 'You'll serve me, Ser Aldric, and I'll give this back to you when you've earned it.' And she smiled — the first smile anyone ever recorded on that face — and she said, 'Besides. Someone has to remember what we break. Otherwise how will we know when we're finished?'"

He drank. The tin cup was empty; he drank anyway.

"So yes. I served her. Twelve years. Write that on whatever stone you carve these things on. Twelve years, and I told myself every night that I was keeping the memory alive, that I was the witness, that someone inside the machine could someday throw the wrench. And every morning I put on her colors and rode out under her banner and did exactly what I was told."

* * *

Nettle had not moved from his curl against Ivo's boot, but Aldric had the distinct sensation of being watched by more eyes than the fox possessed. He ignored it. This part had to come out or none of the rest mattered.

"There was a village," he said.

Ivo went still. Not dramatically. Just the particular stillness of a person who has heard the first note of a song they know.

"Veyra didn't march on cities at first. Cities are expensive. She started with the countryside — the shrines, the wells, the crossroads gods. Anywhere the old powers still answered prayers, she burned the answer out. Her doctrine, if you can call vomit a doctrine, was that worship was a tax mortals paid to parasites, and she had come to collect the arrears. Her nametakers would ride ahead of the army. They'd find the local god — usually something small, a well-wife, a field-father — and take its name, and without a name the god couldn't hold its shape, and the village would wake to find its guardian gone and its prayers falling like stones into a dry well.

"But sometimes the gods hid. Sometimes a village would shelter its little power the way you'd hide a fugitive — pass it hand to hand, feed it, sing to it, keep its name off any tongue the nametakers could reach. And for those villages, Veyra had another instrument. Not the army. The *orders*. Written orders, sealed with her sigil, carried by officers she trusted. Orders that read, in language clean as a scalpel:

*'Where the divinity cannot be located, remove the conditions of its concealment.'*

"That was the phrase. Remove the conditions of its concealment. Do you understand what it means?" His voice had gone flat, the flatness of a man walking on ice over deep water. "It means burn the houses where they sang to it. Empty the granaries that fed it. And take the people — the singers, the feeders, the children who'd been taught its lullaby — take them and scatter them along the roads with nothing, because a god cannot live on love alone, and neither, as it turns out, can a village.

"I signed such orders. Eleven of them. I have the count because I made myself keep the count, because a man who stops counting has decided to stop being guilty, and I wasn't permitted myself that mercy." He set the cup down. His hand was steady, which surprised him. "The eleventh was a village called Fenmoss. In the hills east of a valley called Hollowmere. Sister-settlement, the old records called it — same founding stock, same dialect, same stubborn habit of hiding small holy things. Our intelligencers reported a well-god there, half-starved, being passed between hearths like a family secret. The order came down. I reviewed it. I signed it.

"Fenmoss had ninety-one souls. Forty of them children. The nametakers never even found the god — it had already starved, weeks before, and gone to dust in some child's cupped hands. So we emptied the village anyway, because the order said the conditions of concealment must be removed, and the conditions were people. Ninety-one people put on the road in autumn with what they could carry. I stood at the crossroads and watched them go. A woman with a loom strapped to her back looked at me — looked at *me*, the seal still wet on the paper in my fist — and she didn't curse me. That's the detail I can't drown, no matter how much I drink. She looked at me with pity. Like I was the one being emptied."

Silence. The fire had nearly given up. Nettle's tail-tip twitched once, precisely, like a metronome marking time for a dirge.

"Your valley survived," Aldric said quietly, "because someone in it bought a ward strong enough to blind our scrying. I learned that later, after I ran — learned it from a broker's ledger I stole, a page listing protected territories and the prices paid for them. Hollowmere. Paid in full. Paid in something better than coin." He looked up at Ivo, and did not look away, because looking away would have been the last cowardice left to shed. "I don't know what your grandmother gave. But I know that whatever peace that valley had, it was purchased, and I know that the woman buying it had a daughter she never spoke of, because I read the household records of Fenmoss and there was an Amberdale girl born the year before the emptiness who appears on no census after. I've carried that arithmetic for six years, Ivo. I'm giving it to you now because you deserve to hold it before the road hands it to you crueler than I can."

Ivo said nothing. Their face did something complicated — grief arriving in a body that hadn't been given permission to feel it yet — and then, slowly, they nodded. Not forgiveness. Acknowledgment. It was more than Aldric deserved, and he knew that too.

"Why tell me this?" Ivo finally asked. "Why now?"

"Because tomorrow we reach the toll-road at Greyford, and past Greyford there are checkpoints, and at checkpoints there are nametakers." Aldric fed the last of the wood to the coals and watched the flames climb. "And when they ask you your name, you'll need to lie — quickly, smoothly, without flinching — because a false name given cleanly tears nothing loose, but hesitation bleeds. And you'll want to hate them. You should. But you need to know that the first nametaker who ever made me sick was a boy of sixteen doing exactly what I did at twenty-three: following orders written by someone who had traded every scrap of her own name away and meant to collect everyone else's to fill the hole.

"Veyra isn't building an empire. Empires want taxes and borders. She's building a *silence*. She means to stand alone in a world with nothing left in it that has a name she doesn't hold — no gods, no true-named heroes, nothing that could ever again grip *her*. She's harvesting the world to make herself the only word in it.

"And I served her because she once held my mother's voice in her fingers, and I was afraid." He met Ivo's eyes across the dying fire. "Fear is the whole of my treason, and guilt is the whole of my penance, and neither one has ever once been sufficient. So tomorrow, when we reach the checkpoint, you watch me. Whatever happens — however ugly it gets — you watch what I do. Because I am done signing orders."

Nettle rose, stretched elaborately, and trotted to the edge of the firelight, where he paused and looked back with eyes like struck coins.

"Charming sermon," the fox said. "Though you left out the part where the Pale Regent's brokers trade in Veyra's stolen names — where she *buys*, Aldric. Even a devourer needs a pantry." His ears angled toward the dark road south. "And you left out the part where the checkpoint at Greyford keeps a ledger of everyone who passes, and ledgers travel to the mountain faster than horses. So sleep fast, both of you. Tomorrow we learn whether your face is on it — and whether mine is."

Beyond the fire's last light, far to the south, something on the road began to ring: a bell, patient and cold, counting travelers through the dark.
