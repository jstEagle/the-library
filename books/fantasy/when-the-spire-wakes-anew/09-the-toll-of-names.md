## 9. The Toll of Names

The road to Corvane narrowed as it climbed through the ash-hills, and the refugees on it narrowed with it — a river forced into a gorge, everyone pressed shoulder to shoulder, breath fogging in the cold morning. Ivo had never seen so many people in one place. Hollowmere's entire population could have fit into the first fifty yards of this column and been swallowed without a ripple.

They came from every direction, it seemed: farmers with their livestock left behind or slung across their backs, weavers carrying looms too heavy for the journey because they could not imagine leaving them, a woman cradling a cage of doves like an infant. Children walked because there was no room to be carried. Nobody spoke much. The road itself made a sound — the shuffling of ten thousand feet, the creak of cartwheels, the low continuous moan of exhaustion — and it was a sound Ivo would carry for the rest of their life.

"Checkpoint ahead," Aldric said.

Ivo saw it a moment later: a wall of pale stone thrown across the road where the hills pinched tight, manned by soldiers in grey-and-scarlet. Beyond the wall, travelers passed through in ones and twos, and beyond *that*, they emerged and kept walking — but something about them looked wrong. Diminished. Like laundry brought in from the rain before it had finished drying.

Nettle trotted at Ivo's heel, disguised by a glamour that made him look like nothing more than a lean red dog, though his tail betrayed him when he forgot himself. "Nametakers," he said quietly. "Veyra's own. They're not checking papers, friend. They're checking souls."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Aldric said, and his voice had gone flat and hard, the voice he used when he wanted to stop himself from saying something else, "that she learned early that a name is a door. Take a person's true name and you can walk into them whenever you like. She doesn't need to garrison the cities she takes. The names are enough."

"How do they take a name?"

"You give it up." Aldric's jaw worked. "That's the cleverness of it. There's a compulsion woven into the gate, but most people don't even feel it. They just arrive, they're asked, and they answer honestly, because why wouldn't you? It's your name. You've had it all your life."

"And if someone lies?"

Aldric looked down at Ivo, and for a moment the drunk from the border town was nowhere in him; there was only the knight who had once served Veyra herself, who knew her machinery from the inside. "Then the tear goes deep," he said. "Or you get caught. Both are bad. One is survivable."

* * *

They joined the queue two hours before reaching the wall. Ivo used the time badly, turning the problem over and over like a stone with something living under it.

Because here was the thing — here was the thing that had been sitting in Ivo's chest since Aldric first explained it, cold as a coin swallowed whole:

*Ivo Amberdale was not their true name.*

Oh, it was theirs. Grandmother had given it, the valley had answered to it, twenty-two years of bread and grief and goat-song belonged to it. But Ivo had been born in the same week as a litter of goats, and in Hollowmere that meant something, and the oldest thing in Ivo's memory was not their mother's face but a voice — warm, animal, amused — calling them by a name no human tongue had ever spoken. A name that lived in bleat and crow-caw and the low grammar of the hills. Their mother had named them Ivo for the neighbors. Something else had named them truly, and only the beasts of Hollowmere had ever said it aloud.

And if the nametakers reached for a name and found *that* one coiled underneath—

"They'll know what I am," Ivo murmured.

Nettle's ears flicked beneath the glamour. "Mm."

"If I give them 'Ivo'—"

"'Ivo' will hold, if you believe it holds." Nettle sat, scratched an ear with a hind paw, entirely too casual for the conversation. "Names are not stones, friend. They're stories. If the story you tell is load-bearing, the gate will bear it. Your trouble is that you've spent your whole life telling yourself two stories at once."

"That's not fair."

"No," Nettle agreed pleasantly. "Most true things aren't."

Ahead, a man reached the table set before the gate — a long trestle draped in grey cloth, behind which sat three figures. Not soldiers. They wore no armor, only robes the color of unwritten paper, and their faces were smooth in a way that took Ivo a moment to understand: they were *unlined*. Not young. Blank, like pages waiting, and the eyes above them were old enough to be tired of reading.

Each traveler stopped. Each spoke. Ivo watched lips move, watched the nametakers nod, watched each person walk through the arch in the pale wall — an arch hung with small brass bells that never rang, though people brushed against them constantly.

And Ivo watched what happened after. Watched closely now, knowing what to look for. Every soul who passed through came out the other side moving slightly differently. Shoulders a little lower. Eyes fixed a little more forward, less likely to wander to the sky or the hills or the faces of strangers. As if a window had been painted shut inside each of them.

A child of perhaps six went through, lisping her name, and came out the other side already forgetting how to skip.

Ivo's hands shook. Beside them, a dove in the woman's cage began to coo — distressed, rhythmic, a warning-song Ivo knew from the dovecotes of home. *Crow above, hawk below, hide, hide.* And without thinking, without deciding, Ivo whispered back under their breath, in the tongue that lived below words: *No hawk here yet. Be still, small feathered thing.*

The dove went silent. The woman holding the cage turned and stared.

Ivo looked away first.

* * *

An hour from the table, Nettle pressed close against Ivo's leg — which drew no attention, dogs being dogs — and spoke so softly the words were mostly breath. "Listen carefully. The gate asks twice. Once aloud, once underneath. The aloud question is your name. The underneath question is *who are you, really?* Most people are the same person both times, so they never notice there were two questions. You are not most people."

"What do I do?"

"Answer both questions the same way. With the same story, all the way down. 'I am Ivo Amberdale, a goatherd of Hollowmere, fleeing the wars.' Say it until it's not a lie. Say it until it's the only story left in you." Nettle's dark eyes, above the dog-shape, held something almost gentle. "It will cost you. I won't pretend otherwise. You'll leave something on that table. But better a piece than the whole."

"And if I can't?"

"Then Veyra will own the name the beasts gave you," Nettle said, "and everything that comes with it. Think, friend. What could she do with a voice that every animal alive must obey?"

Ivo hadn't let themselves think it. Now they did, and the thought went through them like winter water. An army that could not be ambushed — every bird a sentry, every wolf a scout, every horse in every stable a willing traitor. The free cities would fall in a season.

"I'll say it right," Ivo whispered.

"I know," said Nettle. "But knowing a price and paying it are different countries, and you haven't crossed yet."

* * *

The bells did not ring as Ivo stepped beneath the arch. The silence of them was worse than ringing would have been.

Up close, the nametakers were worse still. Their blankness was not emptiness; it was appetite. The middle one turned its unlined face toward Ivo, and Ivo felt its attention settle over them like a net dropped into still water — felt it sink, and sink, seeking the bottom.

"Name," it said. Its voice was the sound of a quill scratching dry parchment.

*I am Ivo Amberdale.* Ivo made the words steady. "Ivo Amberdale. Of Hollowmere."

The net sank deeper. And underneath, in a place that had no sound, the second question opened like an eye: *Who are you, really?*

And everything in Ivo rose at once to answer — the goat-song, the crow-words, the name that was not a word but a warmth, the truth that had been hidden for twenty-two years and screamed to finally be spoken —

*I am Ivo Amberdale,* Ivo said, inside and outside, both doors at once, *a goatherd of Hollowmere, fleeing the wars.*

The net paused.

It *paused*, like a hand hovering over a purse, weighing.

Somewhere below the surface of Ivo, the other name stirred — the warm one, the true one — and Ivo did the hardest thing they had ever done. Ivo did not push it down. Pushing was a struggle, and struggle was a texture the net could feel. Instead Ivo turned away from it, deliberately, the way you turn your back on a crying child because if you look you will break. *Not now. Not here. Later, I promise, later —* and the promise was a knife, and the name quieted, hurt, trusting.

*Ivo Amberdale,* the nametaker repeated. Its quill-scratch voice wrote the name somewhere — Ivo felt it being written, felt it lifted out and copied, felt a thin thread attach between the syllables and something far away, a spool in a dark hand. Then the thread pulled, gently, testing.

And something tore.

Not the true name. That survived, curled small and silent. What tore was smaller and stranger: it was the part of Ivo that had never questioned being called anything at all. Some innocent layer, some skin of self that had believed names were gifts rather than hooks. Ivo felt it part like wet paper, felt the cool air of the world touch something underneath that had never been touched, and the pain of it was not sharp but *vast* — an ache with no edges, a grief with no object.

"Pass," said the nametaker.

Ivo walked through the arch. The dead bells hung overhead. On the far side, the light looked the same and was not.

* * *

They found a ditch half a mile on, and Ivo was sick into it, and then sat shaking while Aldric stood watch with his hand near his sword and Nettle groomed his false dog-face with terrible patience.

"Did I lose it?" Ivo managed at last. "Did they take—"

"The false name?" Nettle said. "Yes. Or rather, they took a copy and left the original wired to their spool. You're marked, friend. Tracked, faintly. If Veyra ever wants to find Ivo Amberdale, she need only tug the thread."

"Then I should never have given it—"

"You gave exactly what you intended to give," Nettle said. "You gave the name your grandmother wrote on your cradle. You kept the one that matters." He paused. "What you lost was smaller. Don't mourn it too loudly; mourning makes noise, and noise travels along threads."

But Ivo was mourning it. Because lying in the ditch-grass, breathing the dust of ten thousand refugees' footsteps, Ivo understood what the tear had been. It was the part of them that had still, secretly, believed they might someday be *only* Ivo Amberdale — a goatherd, a neighbor, a person whose deepest secret was a fondness for talking to crows. That person had died at the table under the dead bells. Whoever stood up out of the ditch was something else, something with a second name buried in it like a seed, something Veyra's empire now had a thread tied to.

Aldric offered a hand up. His grip was careful, the way you handle something cracked.

"For what it's worth," he said gruffly, "you lied better than most knights I've known. And I've known liars who got statues."

"It didn't feel like lying."

"No," Aldric said. "The worst ones never do."

* * *

They walked. The refugee river widened again past the checkpoint, spilling toward Corvane's distant smoke, and gradually the hollow-eyed survivors of the gate mingled with those who had come through other roads, other gates, other tolls. Everyone carried something invisible now. Ivo began to recognize it in strangers — the slight forward-fixity of the eyes, the shoulders that no longer reached for the sky. A whole countryside walking around with threads attached to their names, not one in ten aware of the weight.

Near dusk, on a ridge overlooking the plain, a crow landed on a fence post beside the road. Just a crow — ragged, ordinary, nothing like the messenger that had started all of this. It cocked an eye at Ivo.

Old habit rose like a tide. Twenty-two years of greeting the crows of Hollowmere, of sharing scraps and gossip, of being *known* by the black-winged folk of the valley. Ivo's throat ached with the shape of a word that was not a word.

And then Ivo remembered the thread trailing back from their name to a dark spool beneath a mountain. Remembered the nametakers' blank faces. Remembered Nettle's warning: *noise travels.*

If Ivo spoke beast-speech here, on this open ridge, within reach of whatever listened along those threads — would the words carry? Would some quill-voiced creature write them down somewhere? *Subject speaks with birds. Subject is not what the gate was told.*

The crow waited. The road waited. Somewhere south, Veyra's harvest-fires burned villages where gods still answered prayers, and somewhere deeper than south, a sister named Sera sang to herself in a lantern.

Ivo looked at the crow and said nothing.

The crow tilted its head, puzzled — a bird accustomed, somehow, to being greeted — then shrugged its ragged shoulders and flapped away westward into the red evening.

Ivo watched it go and felt the ache where the tear had been widen just a little. This, then, was the new shape of survival: a person who could no longer afford even kindness, because kindness had a language and the language could be overheard.

"There's the city," Aldric said, nodding ahead.

Below them, spread across the plain where two rivers met, Corvane glowed with ten thousand lamps — and above its highest towers, impossibly far off, impossibly visible, hung the mountain. White-capped, serene, wearing the sunset like a crown. Somewhere inside it: a court, a Regent, and a lantern that held a sister.

Nettle sat down on the ridge-stone, let his glamour slip for just a moment — just long enough for his true ears to show, sharp and black-tipped against the dying light — and smiled the way only foxes smile.

"Corvane," he said. "City of bells that lie. Try to look harmless. It shouldn't be hard — you've just spent the day practicing being nobody."

The road bent downhill, and the three of them descended toward the lamps, while behind them, faint as a hair drawn tight, the thread on Ivo's stolen name hummed all the way to the mountain.
