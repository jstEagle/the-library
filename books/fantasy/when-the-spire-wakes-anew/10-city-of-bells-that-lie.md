## 10. City of Bells That Lie

Nettle had been to Corvane before — twice as a fox, once as something else, in a life he kept folded up small at the bottom of himself like a letter he never meant to read. The city hadn't improved. It never did. Cities only grew more of what they were, the way rot grows more rot and honey grows more honey, and Corvane was both at once.

He trotted ahead of the humans through the Southgate traffic, weaving between cart wheels and ankles with the easy contempt of the small and quick. Ivo walked behind him with their hood up and their hands buried in their sleeves, flinching at everything: at the beggar who sang a hymn off-key, at the lamplighter whose taper spat blue sparks of raw magic, at the sheer weight of the city's noise. Aldric brought up the rear, one hand resting on his sword-hilt out of habit rather than threat, wearing his disgrace like an old cloak that still fit.

The bells were already going mad.

Corvane had ninety-one towers, and every tower had bells, and none of them agreed on the hour. Nettle had once counted eleven different noon-tollings within a single afternoon, each claiming to be the true time, each backed by a guild or a temple or a man rich enough to buy his own dawn. The locals swore you learned to hear through it — that after a year you could pick your own bell out of the chorus the way a mother picks her child's cry from a hundred. Nettle suspected this was a lie the city told about itself, like all its other lies: that the bells told time, that the Watch kept order, that the prayer-markets sold comfort and nothing worse.

*Listen,* he said to Ivo, low, under the din. *Every bell lies here. But they lie in patterns. Learn the patterns and the city will tell you everything.*

Ivo looked down at him. Twenty-two years behind a ward made them wide-eyed as a foal, but there was iron under the wonder now; the road had hammered some in. *You talk like someone who was lied to by a whole city once.*

*I was lied to by something much older than a city,* Nettle said, and did not elaborate, because elaborating was how foxes got skinned.

The gate guards barely glanced at them. One of them did glance at Nettle — a long, professional look, the look of a man deciding whether a talking fox was worth the paperwork — and then decided against it, which was sensible. In Corvane, anything might be someone's familiar, someone's spy, or someone's investment. Best not to touch what you couldn't afford.

Inside the walls, the smell hit Ivo like a wall of its own: tar and fish and frying onions, horse dung and incense and the copper-sweet reek of the tanneries, and underneath it all, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, the smell of bottled prayers — myrrh and lightning, Nettle called it, because no one else had ever gotten close enough to describe it properly.

Ivo stopped in the middle of the street. Their nostrils flared. *What is that?*

*Money,* said Nettle. *Come on.*

* * *

They lodged above a chandler's shop in Tallow Lane, where the beds were cheap and the landlady was deaf in one ear and incurious in the other, which between them amounted to safety. Aldric counted out coin with the grim precision of a man who had once commanded garrisons and now budgeted for three. Ivo stood at the shuttered window, listening to the city with that tilted head they got around animals — and around people too, lately, though they didn't seem to know they did it.

*It's loud,* they said. *Not just the bells. Underneath. Like a hive.*

*It is a hive,* Nettle said, curling on the bed's one thin blanket. He liked being high up when he talked; it made the room feel less like a cage. *Half a million people, and every one of them afraid of something. Fear is the loudest thing there is, except hunger. And Corvane has plenty of both.*

*And prayers?*

*Ah.* He groomed one paw, making them wait. It was cruelty, but a small kind, and he'd earned the right to a little cruelty. *You've seen the stalls along the Processional? Little bottles, colored glass, wax seals?*

Ivo nodded. They'd stared at those stalls for a quarter hour that morning while Aldric bought rope. Bottled prayers: joy, patience, courage, safe passage, a good death. Sold openly, taxed honestly, guaranteed by guild seal. The free cities' proudest industry.

*Those aren't metaphors, cub. Someone caught the prayer. Caught it mid-flight, between the mouth that made it and whatever god was meant to catch it. There are men in this city — nice men, respectable men, members of guilds with banners and feast days — whose entire trade is standing between the faithful and heaven, with a net.* His tail flicked. *The gods have grown thin, see. Thin gods answer slowly. A prayer can hang unanswered for years, drifting, ripe. Easy pickings.*

*And if no one catches them?*

*Then they sour.* He let that sit a moment. *An unanswered prayer doesn't vanish, Ivo Amberdale. It ferments. You've smelled the sour ones already and didn't know it — that edge under the incense, the one that made you wrinkle your nose near the Processional. Despair has a vintage.*

Aldric looked up from his coins. *You're enjoying this lecture far too much, fox.*

*I enjoy very little. Let me have this.*

*Where do the bottles go,* Ivo said. Not a question — a door opening. Nettle heard the click of it. *After the market. After someone buys courage, or patience, or — what else do they sell?*

*Anything. Everything. Grief is popular with poets. Rage sells to duellists.* He sat up, wrapped his tail over his paws. *But the best stock never reaches a stall. The strongest prayers — the desperate ones, the ones wrung out of people at the end of everything — those go wholesale. Downhill.*

*Downhill,* Aldric repeated flatly.

*Everything goes downhill eventually,* Nettle said. *In Corvane, downhill means under the mountain. The court buys prayers by the barrel. But prayers are only the small change, knight. You know that. You've seen their ledgers — or ones like them.*

Aldric's jaw tightened. He'd been seeing things like their ledgers since Veyra's army, since the villages. He didn't say so. He never did. That was going to be a problem eventually, Nettle thought. Guilt that won't speak ferments too.

*Souls,* Ivo whispered.

*Souls,* Nettle confirmed. *Traded openly, if you know which doors to knock on and bring enough gold or enough shame. The mountain court is the largest house in the trade. Has been for centuries. Your valley's peace wasn't charity, cub. It was a purchase. And purchases leave receipts.*

* * *

Finding the receipt took three days, and Ntle hated every hour of it, mostly because it required the one thing he was worst at: being charming in a sustained way.

Foxes charm in flashes. A moment of wit, a stolen glance, a trick performed and gone before the audience thinks to ask questions. Sustained charm — sitting in smoky back rooms, drinking watered wine, laughing at the jokes of soul-brokers' clerks — was work, and work undignified, and it reminded him uncomfortably of a life he had paid dearly to escape.

The first two days yielded gossip. On the third, in a gambling den beneath a dyer's shop where the ceiling sweated indigo, a clerk named Master Fenwick drank enough plum brandy to become interesting.

Fenwick was a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and the haunted neatness of someone who balanced terrible books for a living. He'd noticed Nettle on the second night — everyone noticed Nettle eventually, it was part of the equipment — and had spent a day working up the nerve to say, "My aunt had a fox familiar, years ago. Cleverest thing. Knew her letters."

"Most of us do," Nettle said, "though we keep it quiet. Ruins the mystique."

That had opened him like an oyster. By midnight Fenwick was holding forth, quietly, into his cup, on the glories of the trade: the grading system (prayers by clarity, souls by — Nettle did not ask, and hated himself), the seasonal markets, the great houses. And the crown jewel.

"You want to hear something?" Fenwick said, leaning close, breath like fermented sugar. "Something no one says above a whisper, and most don't even whisper?"

"I live for such things," said Nettle.

"The Regent's collection. The lantern-hall. Everyone knows the court keeps souls — it's not even scandalous anymore, it's *infrastructure*. But there's one light in that hall older than the rest. Older than most of the Regent's court. Been burning sixty years and more, they say, and never dimmed a candle's worth." Fenwick swayed. "Amberdale, they call it in the manifests. An old name. Valley name. Sixty years of perfect burn — do you know what a soul does, to burn like that?"

Nettle's fur lifted along his spine, slow as frost creeping up a window.

"No," he said carefully. "Tell me."

"It sings," Fenwick said, and giggled, awful and delighted. "The strong ones sing. That's how you grade them, in the deep vaults — you listen. And the Amberdale light — the clerks fight over vault duty when she's on rotation, because she sings *beautifully*. She's been singing sixty years and she hasn't gone silent yet. They say the Regent keeps her lit personally. Says she's the finest thing ever sold to the court." He drained his cup. "Finest thing anyone ever sold. Can you imagine selling something like that? What kind of person—"

Nettle was already moving toward the stairs.

* * *

He found Ivo on the chandler's roof, because of course they were on the roof. Three days in the city and the cub had already mapped every climbable surface within two streets, drawn to high places the way they were drawn to animals — hungry for air that didn't smell of other people's fear.

The moon was up. The bells were lying to the night in nine-part harmony. Below, Corvane glittered, ten thousand windows, and somewhere under all of it, under the mountain itself, a woman who had been singing in the dark for sixty years.

Nettle jumped to the ridgepole and settled beside them. For a while he said nothing. This was another thing he was bad at, silence, and he offered it rarely and considered it expensive.

*Ivo.*

*How drunk was the clerk,* Ivo said. Their voice was very steady, which was how Nettle knew it wasn't.

*Drunk enough to talk. Sober enough to be telling the truth. Those are the same thing in this city.* He looked at their profile against the stars. *I'm sorry. I went looking for a ledger and found a lullaby instead.*

*She sings.* Ivo's hands were fists in their lap. *Sixty years. Grandmother was young when — she must have been — * The fists shook. *She knew. She knew the whole time, and she wove her cloth and baked her bread and kissed me goodnight, and every day Sera was singing in the dark under a mountain.*

*Yes,* Nettle said, because lying to them now would have been the cruelest thing he could do, and he was cruel in small ways, never large ones. *Yes, she knew. That's what wards cost, cub. Not gold. Knowing.*

Ivo bowed their head. Below them the city roared its gentle, dishonest roar. Somewhere a bell tolled an hour that was probably wrong.

*I used to think the ward was weather,* they said at last. *When it flickered. Elders blamed storms. I stood there in the field watching our whole lives flicker like a guttering lamp and I believed them, because believing was easier than — than asking what fuel a lamp uses.*

*Everyone in Hollowmere believed,* Nettle said. *Believing together is what a village is.*

*Don't comfort me. I'll hate you.*

*You'd hate me anyway. Cheaper to be hated for kindness than for honesty.*

That startled something loose — not quite a laugh, but its cousin. Ivo wiped their face with their sleeve and looked out at the city, and Nettle watched the grief move through them and come out the far side changed, the way ore comes out of a fire changed. He'd seen it happen to soldiers, to priests, to kings. It was happening faster to this cub than he'd expected. Hope, sharpening.

*She's aware,* Ivo said. *All this time. Aware and singing. Why would she sing?*

*To stay herself,* Nettle said quietly. *Ask any prisoner. Ask any fox in a cage. You hold onto your name however you can — a song, a story, a prayer repeated till it wears a groove. She's been fighting sixty years, Ivo. Alone. With the only weapon they left her.* He met their eyes. *And winning. Don't mistake what that clerk described. A soul that burns sixty years without dimming isn't a victim. It's a siege that hasn't fallen.*

Ivo breathed out, long and slow, and when they spoke again their voice had dropped into a register Nuttle hadn't heard from them before — low, level, the voice of someone reading terms.

*Then we stop looking for a way to steal her. Stealing gets you caught, and caught means she stays lit and we hang.* They turned to face the mountain's black shape on the northern horizon, visible even from here, shouldering into the stars. *We find out what the court wants for her. We learn the price of the finest thing ever sold. And then—*

*And then?* Nettle prompted, when they paused.

*Then we decide whether to pay it,* Ivo said, *or make them wish they'd never set one.*

Below them, all across Corvane, the bells began to toll the midnight hour — eleven towers, eleven different truths, ringing together in magnificent agreement about nothing at all.

Nettle curled his tail around his paws and thought: *there it is.* Not the frightened valley-child, not the healer of horses, not the cub who flinched at lamplighters. Something new, minted tonight on a rooftop, still warm.

Hope, sharpened into purpose. He'd seen it before, in better and worse shapes. It usually ended either gloriously or terribly, and rarely could anyone tell beforehand which.

*Get some sleep,* he said. *Tomorrow we go shopping. Even crowns have price tags — and in this city, cub, everything is for sale to whoever asks correctly.*

Far below, faint through the lying bells, he thought he heard — and told himself he imagined it — a thread of song rising from under the earth, patient and unbroken, keeping time.
