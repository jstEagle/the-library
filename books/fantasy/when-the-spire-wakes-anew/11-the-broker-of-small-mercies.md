## 11. The Broker of Small Mercies

Corvane at night was a city of bells that had forgotten what they were for.

They rang at odd hours — midnight, and again at the third hour past it, and once at dawn in a pattern no one could explain — and the sound drifted down through the crooked streets like weather from another country. Ivo lay awake beneath the eaves of a rooming house in the Salt Quarter and listened to them, counting the intervals the way they had once counted heartbeats in a dying ewe. The bells lied, Nettle had told them. They rang to tell Veyra's informants which districts were sleeping and which were awake. A city that sang to its jailers.

But tonight the bells had gone quiet, all except one: a small cracked thing somewhere near the harbor that rang anyway, off-rhythm, stubborn, as if it had not yet been taught what it was supposed to forget.

Ivo listened to it until sleep came, and dreamed of a loom, and hands they did not recognize working the shuttle, and a voice humming under the clack of wood on wood — *Sera's* voice, though Ivo had never heard it, only read it in ink faded to the color of old tea — singing something wordless and patient, the way one sings to pass a very long time.

* * *

The broker's shop found them before they found the shop. That was how Nettle described it afterward, with the air of a fox describing weather he disapproved of.

It stood between a chandler's and a shuttered pawnhouse on Tallow Row, and it had not been there yesterday. Ivo was certain of this. They had walked the row twice already in three days of searching, following rumors like will-o'-wisps — *ask at the sign of the drowned bell*, *look for the door with no knocker* — and the space between the two buildings had been an alley then, a dark slot of a place where gulls argued over fish heads. Now there was a door. Narrow, black, unpainted, with a small brass plate polished bright:

**SMALL MERCIES, BOUGHT & SOLD**

Above the plate, where a knocker should have been, hung a little silver hand, palm up, fingers curled loosely as if waiting to receive something.

"Charming," said Aldric flatly. He stood with his arms crossed, morning fog beading in his grey stubble, looking at the door the way a soldier looks at a gate he has been ordered to hold and does not believe can be held. "A shop that grows out of alleys overnight. Wonderful. Let's go inside and give it our souls."

"It sells mercies," Nettle said, tail flicking. "Not souls. Souls are the mountain's trade. This is — " he paused, choosing the word with visible care, " — adjacent."

"Adjacent," Aldric repeated.

"There is a great deal of commerce adjacent to the mountain court. Somebody has to move the goods." Nettle sat, wrapped his tail over his forepaws, and looked up at Ivo with eyes the color of wet amber. "You don't have to do this. I want you to understand that first. Whatever happens through that door, you can walk away, and we'll find another way, even if it takes months."

"We don't have months," Ivo said. It came out quieter than they meant it to. Three days in Corvane had taught them the shape of the news: Veyra's columns moving west along the river roads, villages going up like torches, refugees clogging every gate of every free city with their gods' names sewn into their collars so the nametakers couldn't strip those too. Somewhere beneath all of it, under the mountain, Sera Amberdale burned as a lantern in a hall of lanterns, and had burned for fifty years, singing herself sane.

"No," Nettle agreed softly. "We don't."

Ivo reached out and touched the little silver hand.

The palm opened. The fingers closed around nothing — around air, around the shape of a wish — and the door swung inward without a sound.

* * *

Inside, the shop smelled of beeswax and cold iron and, faintly, of rain on hot stone — the smell of Hollowmere in summer, and Ivo's chest clenched so hard at it that they nearly turned around. Someone had done that deliberately. Someone knew what travelers would carry through that door, and had bottled the smell of home to loosen their purses.

The shop was narrow and deep and lit by lamps that burned without flame, each one holding a small curling wisp of light that moved when you looked away. Shelves rose on either side into darkness, crowded with jars and boxes and stoppered vials, each labeled in a fine slanted hand. *Mercy of a mother who forgave too late. Mercy of a king who abdicated. Mercy, unspent, belonging to a soldier who died before he needed it.* Some jars held light. Some held shadow. One, near the door, held what looked like a single snowflake that never melted, and beside it, in smaller script: *Mercy withheld. Price negotiable.*

At the back of the shop, behind a counter of black wood worn glossy by centuries of elbows, sat a person.

Ivo could not afterward have said whether the broker was man or woman, young or old. Their face was pleasant the way a well-made chair is pleasant — correctly proportioned, entirely unmemorable. Their clothes were good but not too good. Their eyes were the problem. They were brown, ordinary, kind-looking, and they did not blink, and looking into them was like looking down a well and seeing, far below, your own reflection looking up at someone else's face.

"Amberdale," the broker said. Not a question. The name landed on Ivo like a hand on the shoulder. "And companions. Sit, please. There is tea. You will want to refuse it, and then you will want to drink it, and you should trust the second wanting."

Three cups stood on the counter already, steaming. Ivo had the sudden vertiginous sense of having walked into a conversation that had begun long before they arrived and would continue long after they left, with or without them.

They sat. Aldric did not sit; he stayed standing by the door, one hand resting on the hilt of a sword he was not permitted to carry openly in Corvane and carried anyway. Nettle flowed up onto the counter and settled himself directly in front of the broker, tail curled, staring with the frank insolence of his kind.

"You know why we've come," Ivo said.

"I know many reasons people come here. Most of them are lies they tell themselves on the way in." The broker poured the tea. It was pale gold and smelled of honey and something sharper underneath, like a memory of winter. "Yours, I think, is passage beneath the mountain. To the court. For a soul named Sera."

"Yes."

"Mm." The broker folded their hands. "Then let us speak plainly, because plainly is cheaper than prettily, and you cannot afford pretty. Passage beneath the mountain is possible. It is even, in the current chaos — Veyra gnawing at the roots, the Regent's household distracted, half the gates sealed — *easier* than it has been in a century. But easier is not open. The ways down are guarded by wards keyed to names. Your name will not pass. No living name passes freely; the court gave none out, and takes none back. What passes are debts."

"Debts," Aldric said from the doorway. His voice was flat as slate.

"The court keeps accounts," the broker said, inclining their head toward him with terrible courtesy. "Everything beneath the mountain is a ledger, Ser Aldric. Every soul a line item. Every favor a debt against future payment. And the gates open, always, for payment rendered." They turned back to Ivo, and the well-eyes deepened. "There is a toll that opens the way for a living traveler. It is not blood. Blood is common. It is not a soul — yours is not yet collateral, praise whatever you still pray to. It is this."

They reached beneath the counter and set down a small glass vessel, no bigger than a thumb, empty, its stopper carved in the shape of a closed eye.

"One year," the broker said. "One year of your memories. Freely given. Chosen by me, drawn whole, leaving no scar — the year simply becomes a year you lived otherwise. You will remember a different twelvemonth in its place, seamless, gentle, mine to compose. And the original goes into the ledger, and the gate knows the taste of it, and opens."

The lamp-wisps curled. Somewhere above, the cracked harbor bell rang, off-rhythm, stubborn.

"Which year?" Ivo asked. Their own voice sounded far away.

"That is my price to choose, within limits. Not the year of your grandmother's death — grief is heavy freight, and heavy freight fouls the toll. Not childhood. I will take—" and here the broker paused, and for the first time something moved behind the pleasant face, something almost like appetite, carefully leashed, "—the last year. The year since the spire woke. The crow, the ford, the knight, the fox, the god in the well, the horse you healed before forty witnesses, the road, the city, this conversation. You will walk out of this shop remembering a quiet year in the valley. Goats. Rain. A dull ache of purpose you cannot name. And beneath the mountain you will find a woman called Sera and know only that you must bring her home, because some things survive the cutting — love is deeper than memory, thank all the small gods — but you will not know *why*. You will not know what she was to you. You will not know what it cost anyone, anything, ever, to keep her burning."

Silence. The snowflake jar caught the lamplight and threw a tiny cold gleam across the counter like a coin.

"And the year itself?" Ivo asked. "What happens to it?"

"It becomes mine. I am a collector." The broker smiled, and the smile fit the face the way a key fits a lock: precisely, and with an ugly suggestion of turning. "A year of beast-speech, freely given, from the last speaker alive in the western valleys? Do you know what that is worth? Princes would bid on it. I might sell it a hundred times — a memory, sold, does not diminish. A hundred buyers would live your year, feel the goat's breath on their palms, hear the crow say *the spire wakes*. Your year would become the most beautiful thing in my inventory." The brown well-eyes glittered. "That is not a threat, Amberdale. That is a compliment. I do not buy ugly years."

* * *

They walked out. They had to walk out — the shop ejected them gently but firmly when the negotiation reached its natural pause, the door opening onto Tallow Row's fog with the courtesy of a host showing guests to the carriage — and the broker's last words followed them into the street like smoke:

*"The offer stands until moonset tomorrow. Debts sour. So do markets."*

They ended up at a cookshop near the harbor that served fish stew to stevedores at any hour, because Aldric insisted on being surrounded by witnesses when he said what he had clearly been building toward since the moment the little glass vessel touched the counter.

"No," he said, before Ivo had even lifted the spoon.

"Aldric—"

"No. Listen to me." He leaned forward, forearms on the table, and the lamplight showed every line of his face, the old scar through one eyebrow, the new lines the road had carved since Hollowmere. "I have stood in rooms like that one. In Veyra's war camps, before — before everything. They are always warm. Always polite. The tea is always exactly right. And the price is never the price. You pay a year of memories, and next month it's two years, because the ledger finds you again, because *you have been weighed and entered*, and once you're in the book the book doesn't close. I watched good soldiers sell a finger's worth of themselves to her quartermasters and end up hollow men marching on villages they loved. It is never one payment. It is the first payment."

"This isn't Veyra," Ivo said.

"It isn't *not* Veyra. It's the same arithmetic wearing better clothes." He dragged a hand down his face. "Ivo. Listen. You give them the year, you forget the road, the map, the god in the well — you forget the *prayer*, the drowned god's prayer, the one thing that might matter more than all of this — and you go down into that court alone in your own head, knowing nothing, trusting whoever stands nearest, and Nettle and I are just two strangers who smell faintly of your missing year. You'd be handing them the knife and turning around."

"They said love survives the cutting."

"They said what gets you to sign." Aldric's voice dropped, roughened. "I signed things once. Orders. Clean words on clean paper, each one reasonable, each one the first payment of the next. You know what I carry now. Don't let them put their ledger in your chest. There are other ways down. We haven't tried them all."

"Name one."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Nettle, who was washing his paws with elaborate unconcern on the bench beside Ivo.

"You see," Aldric said quietly.

* * *

Ivo did not sleep. They climbed to the roof of the rooming house instead, where the fog thinned and the stars showed in ragged patches, and sat with their back against the chimney among the pigeons — real pigeons, city pigeons, whose minds were small gray rooms of bread and fear and territory, saying nothing worth hearing, and oh, how Ivo had taken for granted even that, the murmurous company of any mind at all.

*A hundred buyers would live your year.*

Here was the truth they could not say at the cookshop, could barely say to themselves, here on the cold rooftop with the cracked bell ringing its stubborn wrong rhythm below: part of them wanted to pay.

Not for Sera — or not only for Sera. Something in Ivo had been listening to the broker describe that year — the goat's breath, the crow's words, the raw singing magic of the world outside the ward — and had felt *pride*, fierce and shameful, pride that their life was worth a prince's ransom, that their secret, the thing that had made them a liar for twenty-two years, was *beautiful inventory*. And underneath the pride, darker, quieter: a temptation to be rid of it. To hand over the year and become someone simple. Someone who had never hidden in a barn talking to goats while their family ate dinner ten feet away. Someone whose worst secrets belonged to a stranger in a shop.

*You would not know what she was to you,* the broker had said. And Ivo had thought, in the moment before shame arrived: *but I would still save her. Wouldn't I? Love deeper than memory. Then what is the memory for?*

And then, behind that thought, another one, colder: *if love survives the cutting, what else does? What else in me is deeper than the year they'd take? And what if the answer is nothing? What if I walk into that court an emptied thing wearing Ivo Amberdale's face, and the only true thing left about me is the debt?*

The fog shifted. Below in the street, a lantern passed — the night watch, or something wearing the watch's coat — and Ivo pressed flatter against the chimney and breathed pigeon-smell and coal smoke and waited, heart hammering, for the feeling of being *known* to pass. It didn't, entirely. It never fully passed anymore. Since the ward broke, since the crow, since the horse with the shattered leg had looked up at them with gratitude that forty strangers witnessed — since all of it, Ivo had lived with the permanent vertigo of a person whose hiding places are all on fire.

Grandmother had hidden too. Grandmother had hidden so well that Ivo hadn't known there was anything to hide — a daughter, a contract, a god's bargain folded into the beams of a loom. And look what the hiding bought. Fifty years of peace, paid for in a girl's singing.

*Choose truth over safety.* The drowned god's dream-map had whispered that to them, weeks ago, in a ruined shrine, in a voice like water finding a crack: *the spire wakes for the one who chooses truth over safety.* Ivo had thought it meant telling the neighbors. Telling the valley. It was starting to mean something much larger and much worse. It was starting to mean: *do not buy the mercy. Do not let them cut the year out of you and call it seamless. Go down carrying all of it — the grief, the guilt, the knowledge of what your family did — because a rescue paid for with forgetting is just another ward. Just another Hollowmere, built inside your own skull.*

Ivo pressed their forehead against their knees.

Below, soft as fur, a voice said: "You're doing the thing where you decide to martyr yourself without consulting your staff."

Nettle sat on the ridge of the neighboring roof, amber eyes catching the starlight. He had moved without a sound. He always moved without a sound, and lately Ivo wondered how much of the fox's silence was courtesy and how much was a courtier's habit of hearing everything.

"I'm deciding," Ivo said.

"You're deciding to decide badly." Nettle picked his way closer, delicate as frost. "Aldric's right, for what it's worth, and he's also wrong, which will annoy him. The toll is real. The gate does open for it. I've seen the ledger's work." Something crossed his face then — something old, and fast, and quickly buried, like a fish flashing under ice. "But hear me, Ivo. Before you sign anything, before you give that smug upholstered creature so much as a hair of your year — there may be another way to cover a debt. An older way. A stupider way. Give me until moonset."

"What do you mean, cover a—"

"Until moonset," Nettle repeated, and was gone over the ridge of the roof, a ripple of red-gold, leaving Ivo alone with the pigeons and the cracked bell and the growing, dreadful certainty that the fox intended to spend something of his own.

Far off across the black harbor, the true moon was already leaning toward the sea.

Ivo counted the hours left, and found there were fewer than they needed, and went down to find Aldric, and to lie awake listening to the bells that lied, wondering which of them would ring for them next — and what, when the ringing stopped, would still be left to answer to their name.
