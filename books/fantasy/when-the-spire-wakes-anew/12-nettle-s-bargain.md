## 12. Nettle's Bargain

There is a particular smell to a soul-broker's parlor, and I have known it in two shapes: with a nose that was human, and with the one I wear now. The human nose caught only the incense—myrrh and something colder beneath, like the air off a frozen lake. The fox nose knows the truth of it. Souls have a scent. Ozone and milk. New bread and lightning. And old souls, souls that have been traded and re-traded, handled by many hands, smell faintly of coin.

The Broker of Small Mercies kept her shop at the bottom of a stair that went down when it should have gone along. That is the way of such places. You think you are walking through an alley behind the tanneries of Corvane, and then the walls go soft, and then you are somewhere the city cannot follow, somewhere the bells that lie cannot reach. Her parlor was low-ceilinged and warm as a mouth, lit by lamps that burned without oil—I knew what those were. I had carried them once, in another life, in other hands. Hands. The word sat strangely on me even now, after all these years. *Hands.*

Ivo sat on the offered chair like someone who had never been taught that furniture could be refused. Aldric stood by the door the way he always stood by doors, one hand resting easy near a sword hilt that had not borne his name in eleven years. And I curled on the third chair—the broker had provided it without being asked, which told me she knew exactly what I was, or half of it—and I listened to the transaction unfold the way one listens to water rising in a cellar.

"You want passage beneath," the broker said. She was small and neat and gray, gray everywhere, a woman made of ash and patience. "The mountain does not open for gold. Gold is only metal. It opens for weight."

"Weight of what?" Ivo asked, though I could hear in their voice that they already knew. They had known since the loom. Since the contract. Since the name Sera Amberdale written in a hand that shook.

"Weight of self." The broker unfolded her hands. Beneath them, on the table, lay a pair of brass scales so old their pans had gone black. "A year of memory. A true year, freely given, honestly taken. That is the toll. One year for one descent. It is not cruelty, little warden's-kin. It is arithmetic. Nothing goes beneath the mountain lighter than it came."

I watched Ivo's face do the terrible arithmetic of the desperate. A year. Which year? The year of grandmother's death? The year of learning to keep silent, which was most of them? A year was a room in the house of a life; take it out and the house still stood, but there was a draft where the door used to be, forever.

"No," Aldric said from the door. He said it the way he said most things—flatly, as if announcing weather. "You don't pay that. Memories aren't yours alone. Take away a year and you take away everyone who lived it with you."

"The knight speaks sense," the broker said, and smiled with all the warmth of a ledger. "Which is rare in knights. Usually they speak armor."

I laughed. I confess it. I laughed, a small barking fox-laugh, because eleven years of drinking had not yet scoured the knight out of Aldric and probably nothing ever would, and there was something in that which hurt and healed in equal measure. The broker's eyes slid to me. Gray eyes. Weighing eyes.

"And the fox," she said. "The fox says nothing useful. The fox only laughs."

"The fox," I said, "is thinking."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about names, and I am perhaps the only creature left who can explain it properly, having been on both sides of the transaction.

A name is not a label. A label you can peel off and paste elsewhere—a word like *knight*, like *traitor*, like *fox*. A name is a thread stitched through everything you are, and every year of your life knots it once. Pull the name out and the whole garment comes apart into loose years, unattached to anything, blowing like leaves. That is what Veyra's nametakers harvest on the roads: not the whole thread, but lengths of it, cut and spooled, and the people they leave behind are garments coming undone at every seam.

But a whole name, freely surrendered—that is different. That is wealth beyond the dreaming of it. A whole name has never been spent in the mountain court's memory, because no one sane gives up the whole of themselves. The court runs on fragments. Shorn years. Half-heard prayers. Sera Amberdale's soul, burning in its lantern for sixty years of wardlight.

And mine. Or rather—not mine. Mine had been taken from me long ago, which is a different thing entirely, and this is the part of the story I have lied about, dodged, buried under cleverness and cynicism and the general charm of a talking fox:

I was not always a fox.

* * *

My name—I will say it here, once, because after tonight it will be true again or gone forever—was Nettleworth. Master of the Lower Lanterns, in the court beneath the mountain. A fine title. It meant I tended the soul-lights. I trimmed their flames. I polished their glass. I walked the long galleries where they hung by the hundred, each one a person reduced to a warm and obedient glow, and I told myself, as courtiers must, that the work was neutral. Custodial. Someone had to keep the lanterns clean.

Do you know how long I told myself that?

Forty years. Forty years of walking those galleries, and then one night a new lantern came in, and it flickered wrong—it flickered *words*—and I stood before it with my polishing cloth in my hand and read, in the stutter of its light, the name it had been. A girl from a valley village. Sold by her own mother's mother to buy twenty more years of quiet for the people who forgot she had ever been born.

Sera Amberdale did not flicker words. But her lantern hung in my gallery for a season while it was prepared—new souls are moved about, catalogued, before settling—and I heard her singing. She sang herself sane, that girl. Sang in the dark of her own glass. And Nettleworth, master of the lower lanterns, keeper of forty years of comfortable lies, did the treacherous thing.

He opened the case.

Not to free her—he couldn't, no one can simply *open* a soul-lantern, the glass is older than glass—but to let a thread of her light out into the dark, one small strand, enough that she might see her own hands again, might remember she had hands. A mercy. A small mercy, the kind the brokers later named their trade after, because the mountain finds mockery in everything.

For that mercy I was tried before the Pale Regent, and my sentence was not death, because death would have been a kindness and the Regent does not deal in kindness. My name was drawn out of me entire—all forty years of knots—and I was cast up the dead god's throat into the world with a red coat and a long nose and a mind that remembered everything and belonged to nothing. Every year of my life, loose leaves. Every love, unattached. I could recall my mother's face but not why it mattered.

That was ninety years ago. Foxes do not live ninety years. Whatever I am now, I am it by the court's leftover spite, and I have made a career of knowing things, serving no one, and laughing at the wrong moments.

Until a goat-speaking warden's child from Hollowmere walked into a border town with a dying god's plea in their pocket, and I looked at them, and the leaf-years stirred. All of them. Like wind through a gallery of lanterns.

* * *

In the broker's parlor, Ivo was arguing.

"A year," they were saying. "If I give a year—which year? Do you choose, or do I?"

"The toll chooses," the broker said. "It always takes the year that weighs most. That is what makes it honest payment."

I saw Ivo flinch. I saw them think of grandmother's last winter—the apology whispered to Sera, the loom falling silent, the wardlight guttering over the valley like a candle in a doorway. I saw them understand that the toll would take that year, the very year they had come all this way to make mean something, and that they would descend beneath the mountain to save a sister they could not remember grieving for.

"No," Aldric said again, and now his hand was off the sword and flat against the door, as if he could hold the room shut by main strength. "Ivo. Look at me. There are other brokers—"

"There are no other brokers. There is only her kind, and her kind has one price, and you know it, and I know it." Ivo's voice cracked and steadied, which is the bravest sound a voice can make. "One year. I've had twenty-two. I can lose one and still—"

"You can't still *anything*," Aldric said, with sudden violence. "I've lost years. Not the broker's way—Veyra's way, the fast way, the knife-way—and let me tell you what remains. You remain, and you're a stranger to yourself. You stand in your own house and wonder who hung the pictures."

Silence, then. The oilless lamps burned their cold steady burn. And I thought: *they will pay it. They have already decided. They are only waiting for the courage to stop arguing,* and I understood, with a fox's clarity, that I had maybe ten heartbeats to decide what kind of creature I was going to be for the rest of my unnatural life.

Ninety years I had served no one. Ninety years of freedom, if you could call it that—freedom is what you call it when belonging hurts too much to try.

I stepped down from the chair.

* * *

"Madam Broker," I said, and my voice came out differently than I meant it to—older, rounder, with court vowels in it that ninety years had not worn away. Both Ivo and Aldric turned. They had never heard the court in me before. I saw Aldric's eyes narrow; he had served Veyra, he knew what courtiers sounded like. "Let us discuss a better collateral. Something heavier than a year."

The broker's gray gaze settled on me and did not move. "Foxes carry nothing. Foxes own nothing."

"This fox owns a debt owed *to* him by your masters. A grievance, rather. An injustice, unamended these ninety years." I sat back on my haunches, tail wrapped, the posture of a courtier giving testimony. "Ask your ledgers who was Master of the Lower Lanterns. Ask them why he wears this coat."

Something shifted in the woman's stillness. Brokers feel the pull of large sums the way sailors feel weather. "Names," she said softly, "are not restored by grievance."

"No. But they can be *redeemed*. Draw out my ledger-page. Read the entry. Nettleworth, attainted, transformed, name confiscated to the treasury of the deep court. Confiscated names revert to the crown—unless discharged by payment. And I am here to discharge it." I looked at Ivo then. I could not help it. They were staring at me with their mouth slightly open, and there was a question forming in their face that I did not want answered yet, so I went on quickly. "One whole name. Ninety years' interest accruing in your masters' vault. Enough weight to buy a descent, and a guide's license, and the forgiveness of the toll besides. Check your scales, madam. I promise you they will tilt."

"And what," the broker said, "becomes of the fox, when the name is paid out?"

"That is the beauty of it," I said. "Nothing becomes of the fox. The fox was never anything. The name goes home to the man, and the man—"

I stopped. Because I had not actually thought past that point. Ninety years, I had wanted my name back the way the starving want bread—abstractly, ferociously, without a plan. And now it was on the table, priced, and the price was this: the name would return to Nettleworth. A courtier. Master of lanterns. A man with hands. And the fox—whatever the fox was, whatever the fox had become in ninety years of roads and refuges and a goat-speaking child who fed him cheese rinds and never once asked why a fox should trouble himself—

The fox would be what remained when the thread was pulled out. Loose leaves. A red coat with nobody in it.

"Ivo," I said. My voice had lost the court vowels. "Don't let me do this if you'd grieve the fox."

They came off the chair and knelt down on the broker's floor, in the dust and the old wax, and took my face in both hands, which no one had done since I was human and fewer still had done *then*. Their palms smelled of goats and road dust and honesty.

"You listen to me, Nettle," they said. "Whatever name comes out the far side of those scales, whoever walks out wearing it—you found me in a ford when I didn't know which way the world turned. You chose this road. So if you're buying yourself back, buy all of yourself back, and come with us under the mountain anyway. Both of you. The man *and* the fox. I'll not lose either one to a ledger."

"It doesn't work like that," I said. "It has never once worked like that."

"Then make it work like that," they said. "You're the one who's always telling me the court respects audacity."

And I laughed again—the same barking laugh, the fox-laugh—because they had been listening. All this time, they had been listening.

"Done," I said to the broker.

* * *

She brought out the ledger then, a book bound in something I did not let myself identify, and she turned pages until she found mine, and there it was in ink the color of dried blood: NETTLEWORTH — ATTAINED — NAME HELD IN TRUST, and beneath it, in the ledger's patient arithmetic, a figure that made even Aldric swear under his breath.

The scales were set. The broker spoke the discharge, and the parlor filled with the smell of ozone and milk, and something rose out of the book like heat-shimmer off summer stone—thin as wire, bright as morning, ninety years of knots in one silver thread. It hung above the pan and turned slowly, and in its turning I saw my mother's garden, and the gallery of lanterns, and the strand of light I had freed, and a ford at dusk where a stranger's voice first said my chosen name aloud.

The pan dropped. The needle swung. The broker read the weight, and for the first time in our acquaintance her ash-gray face showed something like respect.

"Heavy enough," she said. "Passage for three beneath the mountain. The toll forgiven." She paused. "The redemption completes at the threshold of the deep court. Not before. Your name returns to you when you stand again in the place it was taken."

"Of course it does," I murmured. "They were never going to let me be paid at a distance. They want to watch."

"Who?" Aldric asked sharply. He had gone very still, hand back on the sword.

"The court," I said. "My former colleagues. The Regent's people." I looked at the empty scale-pan where the thread had rested, already feeling the shape of the man I might be again and mourning, quietly, absurdly, the animal I was choosing to spend. "And one of them, I imagine, will be waiting by the lanterns. Because if Sera Amberdale's soul is the crown jewel of the collection, then someone has been keeping her glass polished for sixty years—and there was only ever one office charged with the lower galleries."

Ivo rose slowly to their feet. "Nettle. Who tends the lanterns now?"

I trotted toward the stair that would lead us down and down, into the dark under Corvane, under the world, into the house of everything I had betrayed and everything that had unmade me. At the top step I looked back at them both, knight and warden's-kin, the only family ninety loose years had left me.

"No one you've met," I said. "But I knew her well, once. She apprenticed under me. She reported my treachery herself—with tears in her eyes, which somehow made it worse. And if she recognizes what I've become, she will not hesitate twice."

I started down. The stair swallowed our lamplight a step at a time.

"Her name," I called back over my shoulder, "was Liss. And she always did keep the best lanterns for last."
