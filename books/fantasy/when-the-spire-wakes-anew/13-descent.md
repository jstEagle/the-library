## 13. Descent

The dead god's throat opened in the mountainside like a wound that had never learned to close.

Ivo had expected a cave. What they found instead was flesh grown to stone — ribs of black rock arching overhead, hung with pale curtains of mineral lace that dripped, slowly, over centuries, like the residue of some vast grief. The air that breathed out of it was warm and sweet and wrong, the way fruit is sweet just before it rots.

"His name was Ossuar," Nettle said, padding ahead with his tail low and his ears flat. "God of thresholds. Of doorways and the moments between one thing and the next. Veyra ate his name forty years ago, and everything he governed began to die. Doors stopped being doors. Moments stopped passing." He glanced back, eyes catching the grey light like coins at the bottom of a well. "This is what's left of him. A mouth that remembers how to open."

Aldric stood at the threshold with his hand on his sword hilt, not drawing it, just resting there, the way a man touches an old scar. "And we're walking down its throat because?"

"Because every other door is watched," Ivo said.

Their voice came out steadier than they felt. Three days in Corvane's underbelly had taught them how to sound steady; the city was a school for it. They still carried the broker's token against their sternum — a sliver of bone, cold as riverbed stone — and the memory of the price Nettle had paid for it sat in their chest like a swallowed coal. *He gave up his name.* Ivo kept turning it over and over, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth. A name, freely surrendered, to cover a stranger's debt. And Nettle had laughed about it afterward, had made jokes about all the things he could no longer be called, as if a soul were a coat you could shrug off and not miss until winter.

The fox caught them looking and bared his teeth in something that was almost a smile. "Say it," he said. "You've been chewing on it since the broker's house."

"I don't know what to say."

"Liar. You want to say thank you and you want to say it was foolish and you can't decide which is true, so you say nothing, which is the worst of the three." He turned back to the dark. "For the record: it was foolish, and I'd do it again, and you're welcome. All three can be true at once. I was a courtier; I know how to hold contradictions."

Then he stepped through the dead god's lips, and the light took him.

* * *

Inside, the world changed its mind about what it was.

The passage sloped downward at first gently, then steeply, and the walls lost their stony character by degrees. The mineral lace became membrane. The ribs became — Ivo refused to name it, and then named it anyway — became the fluted arches of a gullet vast enough to swallow a chapel whole. Underfoot, the ground gave slightly, springy as moss over peat, and each footfall produced not an echo but a response: a slow tidal shudder, deep below, like the breathing of something asleep.

Ivo could feel it through their boots. Could feel it in their teeth.

They had grown up warded. Twenty-two years inside Hollowmere's quiet, where magic was a rumor and the gods were stories grandmothers told to frighten children into behaving, and Ivo had thought, leaving the valley, that they understood what the world outside was like. They had walked through raw magic at the ford and thought they understood. They had stood in a ruined shrine while a god the size of a coin drowned in its own well, begging, and thought they understood.

They had not understood anything. The mountain's interior pressed against their skin like a second atmosphere, thick with the residue of divinity — old worship gone stale, prayers said so long they had worn grooves in the rock the way water wears grooves in limestone. Ivo could taste them. Copper and honey and smoke. Someone had prayed here for centuries, thousands of someones, and their devotion had soaked into the walls and now seeped out again passively, flavorlessly, like tea leaves brewed too many times.

"You're doing the thing," Aldric said quietly, beside them.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your eyes go distant and your breath goes short and you start listening to something none of the rest of us can hear."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. Just tell me if it's bad."

Ivo considered. Around them the throat pulsed, faintly luminous — the walls themselves gave off a light like moonlight through milk, sourceless, shadowless. Somewhere far above, wind moved across the dead god's mouth, and the sound came down the passage transformed: a long low note, mournful, almost musical.

"It's not bad," Ivo said. "It's like standing inside a bell after it's been rung. Everything is still ringing. It's just — very loud. In a way ears aren't for."

Aldric nodded slowly. "Veyra's court is louder still," he said. "When we get there. If we get there." His jaw worked. "I served her for six years, Amberdale. You should know that whatever's coming, I've seen the inside of it before, and I came out the other side worse. Whatever you feel in these walls, multiply it."

Nettle, ahead, snorted. "Cheerful company you keep, Ivo."

"He's honest," Ivo said. "That's rarer than cheerful."

"Honesty is cheap," Nettle said. "Everyone's honest when the alternative is lying badly." But his voice had lost its edge, and Ivo noticed that the fox kept to the center of the passage, never letting his fur brush the walls, and that he did not look up at the great fluted arches overhead, as if he recognized them from somewhere, or someone.

They walked down into the dark, and the dark walked up to meet them.

* * *

Hours passed, or something shaped like hours. There was no sun to measure by, and Ivo's sense of time went soft and unreliable, stretched like taffy. They ate dried apples and hard bread from Corvane, and the food tasted of copper and honey, because everything here tasted of copper and honey, because everything here was steeped in prayer.

It was Nettle who smelled the change first.

"Stop," he said, and his whole body went rigid, tail straight out behind him. "Do you smell it?"

Ivo breathed in. Under the omnipresent sweetness there was something else now: woodsmoke, tallow, the particular smell of many bodies living close together. And underneath even that — faint but unmistakable, threading through everything —

"Singing," Ivo whispered.

Aldric's hand went to his hilt. "Singing? People?"

"Not people."

They rounded a bend in the gullet — the last bend, though they didn't know it yet — and the floor of the world fell away.

The throat ended. Below them opened the court beneath the mountain, and Ivo's mind, which had spent twenty-two years learning the shape of the possible, simply declined the assignment.

It was a cavern that could have held Hollowmere three times over, hollowed from the roots of the mountain, and it was lit — oh, it was lit — by hundreds upon hundreds of small lights hanging in the air at every height, drifting slow as pollen, rising and falling on currents Ivo could not see. Each light was the size of a fist, or a heart, and each burned a different color: gold and green and bruised violet, one that was the exact blue of dusk in autumn, one that flickered red-gold like a hearth seen through a window on a night you are locked out of your own house.

They were beautiful. That was the first thing, and for one long breath it was the only thing. Ivo stood at the lip of the descent with tears starting in their eyes, thinking: *this is what worship looks like from the inside. This is what all those centuries of kneeling built.*

"They're souls," Aldric said. His voice was flat, which was how Ivo had learned to hear the horror in him. Flat meant he could not afford texture. "Lanterns. That's what they call them. That's what they *are*. The court burns them for light."

The beauty curdled.

Ivo looked again, properly this time, and once they knew what they were looking at they could not unknow it. The lights weren't hanging. They were *held* — tethered by threads too fine to see, drifting at the length of their leashes like dogs chained just past reach of food. And they moved wrong. Lanterns sway; these *reached*. When a figure passed along one of the carved galleries far below — a tall figure in robes the color of wet slate — the nearest lights strained toward it, all together, all in silence, the way flowers turn toward a sun that will not warm them.

"There," Nettle said softly. "See the galleries? The court keeps its public face on those levels. Audiences, bargains, brokers. The Regent's hall is the big one — see it, center, the black dome?" His voice had gone strange, thin, and Ivo remembered: he had been human here. He had been a courtier of this place, before whatever treachery had bought him his fur and four legs. "Below the galleries, the warrens. Below the warrens—" He stopped.

"Below the warrens what?"

"Nothing. Nothing you need to think about yet."

But Ivo was already listening past him, because the singing had grown clearer as they descended, and it was coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the lights themselves, and beast-speech — the gift they had hidden their whole life, the gift they were going to trade away, though only two people in the world besides Ivo knew it — rose in them unbidden, answering, translating:

*It isn't singing,* the gift murmured, in the wordless way it had. *It's counting. They're counting. Each one is counting itself, over and over, so it doesn't forget its own number.*

Oh, Ivo thought. Oh, that's worse.

And then, beneath the hundreds of voices counting themselves, beneath the drone and drift of the lantern-souls, another voice. Faint. Worn smooth as a river stone by decades of repetition. But threaded through it, unmistakable, a melody Ivo had known since before memory — because Grandmother used to hum it over her loom, and had never once said where she'd learned it, and had gone silent as a grave whenever Ivo asked.

A lullaby. Their family's lullaby. Sung in a voice that sounded like Grandmother might have sounded, sixty years ago, young and unbroken.

Ivo gripped the edge of the passage. The stone — flesh, it was flesh — gave under their hands like a cheek under a palm.

"That song," they said. Their voice cracked down the middle. "Nettle. *That song.*"

The fox turned. Read their face. Something moved behind his eyes — pity, maybe, or fear for them, or both wearing the same mask.

"The sister," he said quietly. "Sera."

"She's *here.* She's not a story in a contract. She's not a line in Grandmother's guilt. She's *here*, she's *singing*, she's been down here singing herself sane for fifty years while Grandmother wove and the valley slept and nobody—" Ivo's voice failed. They pressed their forehead against the warm wall of the dead god's throat, and the gift translated without being asked, helplessly, faithfully, every syllable of the far-off lullaby rendered into the language of living things:

*I am Sera. I am Sera. I am Sera. I am still Sera. Count me, count me, count me: one sister, one daughter, one soul, still mine, still mine, still—*

Below, among the drifting lights, one small flame — amber, ordinary, the color of lamplight in a farmhouse window — strained hardest of all toward the surface, toward the dark throat where Ivo knelt, reaching the full length of its invisible leash, and held there, trembling.

As if it had heard its own name spoken after fifty years.

Aldric put a hand on Ivo's shoulder. Heavy, gauntleted, gentle. "Not tonight," he said. "Tonight we go down as brokers' guests, heads down, mouths shut. Tonight we look and learn. Tomorrow—" He didn't finish. There wasn't a tomorrow he could honestly promise.

Nettle stepped to the lip of the descent, and for once the cynicism fell off him entirely, and what was underneath looked old and sad and very tired. "Once you walk down there," he said, "you will see things that cannot be unseen and hear things that cannot be unheard, and some part of you will stay in this mountain forever, whatever happens. I know. I left part of myself here once." He glanced back at Ivo, and his eyes were coins at the bottom of a well again, sunk deep. "So decide now. With your whole heart. Are you coming?"

Ivo looked down into the burning dark, at the small amber light straining upward on the end of its thread, singing *still mine, still mine* into a mountain that did not care — and stood, and wiped their face, and answered with the only truth they had.

"She waited fifty years for someone to come down. I am not making her wait one more hour."

They descended together into the court beneath the mountain — and far below, in galleries lit by stolen souls, a door opened, and footsteps began to climb toward them.
