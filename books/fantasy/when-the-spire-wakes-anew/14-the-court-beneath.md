## 14. The Court Beneath

The dead god's throat let them out into a hall so vast that Ivo's breath seemed to steal away and keep going without them, a small gray ghost wandering off between pillars of black stone.

The mountain's interior was not carved in the way men carve. Nothing here had the honest chisel-marks of human hands. The walls had grown, or been grown — ribbed like the inside of a shell, veined with channels where light moved instead of blood. And the light itself was wrong, or rather it was *made*, which amounted to the same thing: pale gold, sourceless, and it flickered. It flickered the way a candle gutters when someone walks past it, though nothing walked past that Ivo could see.

Then Ivo looked up, and understood.

Lanterns. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They hung from the vaulted dark on chains of silver wire no thicker than spider silk, and each one held a soft coiled brightness that pulsed — slowly, patiently, at its own rhythm, none of them in time with any other. They were not lamps. Ivo had known since Corvane what the mountain court traded in; knowing had been an idea, a thing to carry in a pocket. This was the idea turned inside out and hung in the sky.

Each lantern was a soul.

"Keep your eyes forward," Aldric murmured. His voice had gone flat and hard, the voice he used when he wanted his body to believe something his heart did not. "Looking up is a mistake down here. Looking up is how they know you're soft."

"They already know we're soft," Nettle said. The fox trotted at Ivo's left heel, tail low, ears swiveling constantly, cataloguing every echo. "We came here to buy something with our insides. That is the softest thing a person can do."

Ivo did not answer. They were afraid that if they opened their mouth, everything in them would come pouring out — and down here, in a place that ran on souls the way mills ran on river-water, spilling yourself seemed unwise. So they walked, one foot and then the other, along a causeway of polished obsidian that crossed the great hall toward a dais at its far end, and they kept their eyes on the middle distance where there was nothing but stone, and they tried not to listen.

Listening was the worst part. Because among all those small burning voices overhead — voices like wind through wheat, like distant bells underwater — Ivo's gift kept reaching, the way it always reached, toward anything alive enough to have something to say. The souls did not speak in words. They spoke in *weather*. Grief like long rain. Terror like a storm front. And underneath it all, threaded thin and constant as a stream under snow, singing.

Someone down here was singing. Had been singing for a very long time.

Ivo's chest hurt. They realized their hands had curled into fists and made themselves uncurl them, finger by finger, the way Grandmother had taught them to unclench when the goats were frightened — *slow hands, little wren, slow hands* —

Don't think of her. Not yet.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain received them in state, and the state was silence.

There were courtiers — if that was the word. They lined the causeway on both sides, tall figures robed in gray so fine it moved like smoke, and where their faces should have been there was only smoothness interrupted by a suggestion of features, the way fog suggests trees. As Ivo passed, each figure inclined its head slightly. The courtesy of it was more frightening than hostility would have been. These things were not guards. They were servants, and servants only behave this way toward guests who matter to the master.

"They think you're a buyer," Nettle whispered. His whiskers trembled. "Good. Be a buyer. Buyers leave."

At the foot of the dais stood the broker who had taken Nettle's name — she was waiting there still, hands folded, wearing her borrowed face like a well-fitted glove — and beside her, at the top of seven shallow steps, sat the Pale Regent.

Ivo had expected a monster. What sat on the throne of bone-colored stone was worse than a monster, because monsters are simple. The Regent was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and old the way mountains are old, and it wore a shape that was almost human — tall, spare, draped in white that might have been linen or might have been frost — as if humanity were a coat it had put on for the occasion, knowing it would be polite.

Its eyes were lanterns. Small ones, twin flames behind the smooth white of its face, and in them Ivo could see the same slow pulsing light as the thousands above.

"Amberdale," the Regent said. Its voice was quiet, and it carried to every corner of the hall anyway, the way cold does. "Child of the ward. You have come a long way to stand in my house. Sit, if you like. Guests who have walked through a god's throat deserve a chair."

There was no chair. A servant produced one anyway, unfolding from the wall like paper, and Ivo did not sit, and the Regent smiled at that — a small movement, precise, practiced.

"Defiant," it said. "Yes. Your grandmother was defiant too, at first."

The words went through Ivo like a hook through soft flesh.

"You knew her." Their voice came out lower than they intended. "You met her. Twenty-two years ago. She came here and she—"

"She signed a contract." The Regent rose. Up close, moving, it was somehow less human and more terrible, its whiteness catching the soul-light and holding it. "Would you like to see it? We keep excellent records. Eternity is a long ledger, and we are careful bookkeepers. It is the one virtue we have never been accused of lacking."

It lifted one hand. From the darkness behind the throne, a servant glided forward bearing a tray, and on the tray lay a sheet of vellum that Ivo recognized with a lurch — the same heavy cream paper, the same iron-gall ink gone brown with years, the same seal pressed in wax the color of dried blood. A twin to the contract hidden in Grandmother's loom. Or perhaps the original; perhaps the copy in the loom had been permitted, allowed to stay there like bait in a trap, like a receipt.

Ivo stepped forward before they decided to. Read the lines they had already read once by candlelight in a dead woman's house, and then read the lines below them that the valley-copy had never shown.

*In consideration of the ward's renewal, delivered season upon season without lapse, the undersigned conveys unto the court beneath the person of SERA AMBERDALE, daughter of the undersigned, aged nine years, whole of soul and sound of body, to be held in perpetuity.*

Not *the soul of*. Not a bargain of essence, some abstract coin of spirit such as brokers traded in bottles in the alleys of Corvane.

*The person of.* A girl. Nine years old.

The hall tilted. Or Ivo did. Aldric's hand arrived at their elbow, steadying, saying nothing, and Ivo heard their own breathing as if it belonged to someone standing near them.

"There was no sister," Ivo said. "She never — there was no grave, no story, no *name*. Nobody ever said—"

"Of course nobody said." The Regent descended one step, unhurried. "Your valley forgets, Amberdale. That is its nature and its purpose. Hollowmere does not merely hide from the world; it hides from *itself*. The ward was built of forgetting long before your grandmother ever touched it, and she understood that better than anyone. To make her bargain hold, the valley would need to release Sera — every memory of her, every mention, every small grief — and let the forgetting be mistaken for peace. Your grandmother agreed to that too. It was part of the price. She sold her daughter twice over: once in flesh, once in memory."

"And the ward—" Ivo's mouth was dry. The words tasted of ash and candle-smoke. "What does the ward *run* on? The elders said it was old. Older than the town. They said—"

"The elders say many things. Weather, they blame. Leylines, when they are feeling learned." The Regent stopped three steps up from Ivo, close enough now that Ivo could feel the cold coming off it like the cold off a lake at dusk. "Let me tell you what your elders do not know, since you have come so far and paid so much to hear it. Wards do not run on stones and prayers, child. Stones are only the frame. Every ward must have a heart, and a heart must beat, and a beating heart must suffer to know it beats. Yours has beaten for sixty years on the suffering of one girl. It renews itself each generation — the contract renews, the suffering continues, and Hollowmere sleeps safe inside its circle of quiet while my lanterns burn."

Sixty years. The arithmetic moved through Ivo like cold water finding its level. Sixty years — and Sera would be thirty-one now, if she were alive, if a soul hung in a lantern could be said to be alive—

But Grandmother had died only last spring, old and bent and full of years. Which meant she hadn't made the bargain twenty-two years ago, at Ivo's birth, as Ivo had somehow assumed without ever letting the thought form fully. She had made it *thirty-one* years ago. When Sera was nine. When Ivo was not yet even a hope in anyone's eye.

Grandmother had lived thirty more years in the valley she'd bought with her daughter. Thirty years of bread and lambs and loom-work and slow evenings, thirty years of teaching Ivo herbs and weather-sense and *slow hands, little wren* — and all of it paid for, season upon season, by a nine-year-old screaming somewhere no one could hear.

Except that someone could hear. Someone whose gift reached into the weather of every living thing.

"I can hear her." Ivo's voice cracked on it. Above the dais, among the thousands of lights, one small flame sang on, and on, and on. "All the way down here, I can hear her. She's been *aware*. All this time. She's been aware and singing and everyone in Hollowmere just — slept. Safely. In her suffering."

"Yes," said the Pale Regent, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was the most obscene thing yet. "That is generally how peace is purchased. Someone pays, and someone else sleeps."

Ivo struck at it.

They were not a violent person. They had never in their life struck anything harder than a fence post. But grief and fury took the decision out of their hands, and their fist flew at that smooth white face — and Aldric caught their arm mid-swing, and the servants did not move at all, and the Regent regarded Ivo with its two small lantern-eyes and did not even lean back.

"Strike me," it said softly. "Truly. I invite it. Do you know what happens to a soul that dies in this hall, Amberdale? It does not pass on. It hangs. It becomes another pretty light in my ceiling, and your sister will have family near her at last, after all these years. Your grandmother nearly took me up on the same offer, in her last winter. Guilt is a long road and some travelers prefer to end it quickly." Its head tilted. "But you did not come here to die, or to strike me. You came here to buy. So let us speak of prices, buyer, and spare us both the theater."

Aldric eased his grip, slowly, ready to catch again. Nettle had gone very still against Ivo's ankle — still in the way of a fox who has scented something between himself and the far side of a field.

"What's your price," Ivo said. Flat. Hoarse. Somewhere beyond tears, in the country past them.

"For the girl?" The Regent returned to its throne and sat, folding its long white hands, and for a moment it simply looked at Ivo, and looking at it was like being weighed. "You misunderstand your situation, and I regret that, because misunderstandings lead to unpleasant scenes. You speak as though you have something I want. Consider, instead, the possibility that everything you have, you have because I have allowed it."

It raised one finger, lazily, and pointed upward — not at Sera's lantern, but at Ivo's own chest.

"That gift of yours. The beast-speech. The wild-ear. Whatever your valley calls the thing you've spent your life smothering under silence." The lantern-eyes brightened. "Did you truly believe it survived the ward by accident? Hollowmere burns magic, Amberdale. It has burned it out of children gentler than you. But yours was *permitted*. Cultivated, even, like a tree grown in a walled garden. Do you know why?"

The ground of Ivo's life — the solid, familiar, twenty-two-year ground of *I am Ivo Amberdale of Hollowmere, quiet, careful, cursed with a secret* — gave way beneath them all at once, the way ice gives way, suddenly and with a sound like a sigh.

"No," they whispered.

"Because a gift like yours is rare, and rarer still unspoiled, and my court has uses for those who can hear the weather of living things. Your grandmother knew it when she signed. It was written into the margins of her contract, in terms she was too desperate to read closely. *The second child of the line shall come of age under the ward, and in the fullness of time the court may call upon what grows in him.*" The Regent spread its hands, gracious as a host presenting a meal. "Twenty-two years I have waited, watching you grow like fruit on a branch, and at last you have done the most convenient thing imaginable. You walked into my house of your own free will, carrying the harvest."

The hall was very quiet. Above them, ten thousand small fires pulsed at their own patient rhythms, and among them one sang.

Nettle's voice came out barely louder than breath. "That wasn't the bargain. Her bargain was Sera. The debt was paid—"

"The debt," the Regent said, without turning its head, "was structured. Everything worth having is. Ask your broker friend; she structures debts for me beautifully." Now it did turn, taking in all three of them — the fox, the knight, the heir — with the mild pleasure of a man surveying a table set exactly as he likes it. "So. Here is where we find ourselves, Amberdale. Your sister hangs above you, and she is mine, bought fairly and kept fairly, and I am within my rights to keep her until the last star goes out. You, however, are also mine — called for in ink older than you, delivered to my door by your own feet. Two debts, one debtor's blood. I am a reasonable sovereign. I dislike waste. So I will make you the only offer I have made a mortal in four hundred years."

It leaned forward, and the twin lantern-flames in its face flared, and every soul-fire in the vast hall guttered at once, as if the whole mountain inhaled.

"*Choose,*" the Pale Regent said. "Leave here whole, with your gift intact and your sister hanging, and live out your small life in a valley that will forget you too, someday, the way it forgot her. Or stay — give me what was promised in the margins, the thing you have guarded all your life — and I will consider what your sister's freedom might be worth against it. One choice keeps you hollow and safe. The other costs you everything you believe you are." The smile again, small and precise. "Take whatever time you need. We have eternity, and you have perhaps a hundred heartbeats before I decide that indecision is also an answer."

Ivo stood on the black stone with the weight of the mountain overhead and the weight of thirty-one years of song, and their heart counted, loudly, whether they wished it to or not.

And then, faint and clear through the vast hush — through stone and silence and the slow pulse of ten thousand captive lights — the singing changed. Stopped being weather. Formed, for the first time, into words, into a voice worn thin but unmistakably shaped like Ivo's own, aimed downward through the dark like a hand reaching:

*"Wren? Little wren — is that you? Come and find me."*

Sera knew they were there.

Ivo turned their back on the throne and looked up, up into the burning dark, and began to search the lanterns for one small light among thousands — and behind them, softly, the Pale Regent began to laugh.
