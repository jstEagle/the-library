## 15. Sister, Lantern

The lanterns hung in their thousands from the vaulted dark of the court's great gallery, and each one burned a different color, and each one was singing.

Ivo had not understood that at first. From the throat of the dead god, from the long stair of bone-pale stone, the lights below had looked like a city seen from a mountain pass at night — beautiful, orderly, warm. It was only when they came down among them that Ivo heard what the warmth was made of. Voices. Thin as threads drawn through a needle's eye, high as winter starlight, low as roots growing in the dark. Every lantern sang. None of them sang the same song.

Aldric walked with his hand on his sword hilt and his eyes fixed on the middle distance, the way a man walks past a beggar he cannot afford to see. Nettle trotted close to Ivo's heel, his fur bristling, his ears flat.

"Don't listen," the fox said quietly. "That's the first rule down here. Don't listen too closely, don't look for faces in the light, and whatever you do, don't call out a name."

"But I have to call out a name," Ivo said. "That's why we came."

Nettle's tail went still. "I know," he said. "I'm telling you what it will cost before you do it anyway. It's the only honest thing left to offer you."

The gallery stretched farther than any hall had a right to stretch, as though the mountain had swallowed the idea of distance along with everything else. The floor was smooth black stone that gave back the lantern-light like still water. Pillars rose into darkness overhead, and from them hung chains, and from the chains hung glass — no, not glass. Ivo stopped trying to name it. Glass was cold and empty. These vessels were warm to the eye, alive at their centers, each one holding a slow-turning brightness that might have been a soul or might have been a small captive sun. They swayed without wind. They breathed.

And they sang.

Some sang lullabies in languages that had been dead for a thousand years. Some sang counting songs, over and over, one and two and three and four, as if arithmetic were the last rope holding them above an abyss. One near the path sang a wedding hymn, joyously, endlessly, forty years past anyone's wedding. One sang nothing at all — it only breathed, in and out, a lantern keeping time with lungs it did not have.

Ivo walked slower and slower. Aldric noticed and did not stop. Nettle noticed and did not leave.

"You're looking for her," the fox said. Not a question.

"I'll know her." Grandmother's loom had held more than a contract. In the drawer beneath the treadles, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, there had been a lock of hair tied with red thread — hair the color of river-copper, of autumn beech leaves, of nothing else in Hollowmere. Ivo had carried it against their heart through every mile since the ford. *If she looks like anything,* Ivo thought now, *she will look like this.*

But how did a soul look? How did a sister look, when the sister had been sold before Ivo was born, when the only portrait was a lock of hair and a name whispered by a dying woman? Sera Amberdale. Sera. The syllable had lived in Ivo's mouth for weeks now like a seed under snow. *Sera.* Grandmother had said it last, at the end, with her hand pressed flat over her heart as if holding a door shut. *Tell her — tell her I never stopped —* and then the sentence had died the way old people's sentences die, unfinished not because they forgot but because finishing was more than the body could bear.

Ivo walked the rows. The singing thickened. It got into the teeth, into the breastbone, into the soft places behind the eyes. Once, passing a lantern the color of deep water, Ivo heard a voice singing a song about goats — about white goats on a grey hillside, about a girl who fed them turnips from her apron — and Ivo's whole body turned toward it before the mind caught up. Not her. A farmwife, someone's mother, someone's grandmother, harvested from some village Veyra had emptied or the court had bought. She sang her goats back into being every day, and every day they dissolved when the song ended, and she began again. Ivo stood before her lantern until Nettle pressed his flank against their ankle, warm and solid, an anchor of fur and stubbornness.

"She isn't here either," the fox said gently. "Keep walking."

"How can you tell?"

"Because you're still standing in your own body. When you find her, you won't be able to stand anywhere at all."

* * *

They found her in the deepest row, where the pillars grew close together and the light pooled like honey in the hollows of the floor. Ivo knew it before knowing it — knew it the way the goats had known storms coming, the way the crows had known the spire waking. Something in the blood leaned forward like a compass needle finding north at last.

The lantern hung low, nearly within reach, its chain run down almost to the ground as though whoever hung it had wanted it close to the earth, close to living things. Inside the vessel — which was neither glass nor crystal nor anything with a name — a brightness turned slowly, and the brightness was copper-colored. River-copper. Autumn beech. The exact color of the lock of hair over Ivo's heart.

And she was singing.

Not a lullaby. Not a counting song. Not a hymn worn smooth by repetition. What she sang had verses — new ones, Ivo realized with a jolt that went through them like cold water, *new ones*, rhymed and metered and shaped, and she was making them up as she went. She sang about a valley between two grey hills. She sang about a loom and the sound the treadles made, *clack-clack-hum*, and how the warp smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke. She sang about a kitchen garden and a goat named Brindle who ate the washing off the line. She sang about a grandmother whose hands were always floury and whose apologies came out sideways, baked into bread instead of spoken. Verse after verse after verse, decades of them, a life rebuilt in rhyme and sung into the dark so it could not be taken from her twice.

She had been aware the whole time. All those years while Hollowmere slept safe behind its wardlight, while Ivo learned to hide beast-speech from their own family, while grandmother wove and apologized to no one — Sera had been down here in the dark, awake, singing herself sane one verse at a time.

Ivo did not fall to their knees. Ivo simply stopped existing as a thing with knees. The world narrowed to a point of copper light, and everything else — the gallery, the mountain, the mission, the war marching somewhere far above — fell away like a dropped cloak.

"Sera," Ivo said.

Nettle had been right. Ivo could not stand. The word left them and took the floor with it, and they were on the black stone with their palms flat and their forehead nearly touching it, and the sound they made was not a word at all but something older, something the ward had spent twenty-two years teaching them to swallow.

The singing stopped.

Silence in that place was louder than all the thousand voices together. Every other lantern seemed to lean in. And inside the copper light, the slow turning quickened — brightened — flared — and a voice came through the vessel's skin, thin and clear and astonished, the voice of a young woman who had been young for a very long time.

"Someone said my name." A pause. "No one has said my name. They speak *at* me, the pale ones, they speak *about* me, but no one has said my name in — I don't — how long —"

"Forty years," Ivo said, and hated themselves for knowing it precisely. "Give or take. Grandmother kept count. She marked it on the loom frame, little notches, where no one would see. I found them. I didn't know what they were until I found the contract."

Another silence, but a different kind — the silence of someone gripping a rope with both hands.

"The contract," Sera said. "You've read it."

"I've read it."

"Then you know what I am. You know what I'm *for*." The light dimmed, just slightly, the way a face goes careful. "You shouldn't have come. Whoever you are — whatever you are — you should not have come."

"I'm Ivo." The name came out cracked down the middle. "Ivo Amberdale. You're my — you were going to be my aunt. Or my — grandmother never explained it right, whether you were her daughter or her sister, the contract calls you daughter and her dying mouth called you sister, and I think maybe she couldn't decide which lie hurt less—"

"Daughter," Sera said softly. "I was her daughter. But she raised me as her sister because a woman selling her daughter is a monster, and a woman selling her sister is a tragedy, and she could live as a tragedy." A sound came through the lantern that might have been laughter and might have been something else entirely. "So I suppose you're my — oh. Oh, you'd be my grand-niece. My grand-nephew. Whatever you are. Little Ivo." The light flared again, helpless and bright. "I used to sing about you. Before you existed. I sang a cradle that hadn't been built yet. Did she ever — did anyone ever sing to you a song about a fox and a well?"

Ivo's breath stopped. "That's — Mama sang that. That's a Hollowmere lullaby. Everyone knows it."

"It's mine," Sera said. "I wrote it down here in my first year, and it leaked up through the ward, the way small things leak through seals. Your mother hummed it to you without knowing she was humming me." The voice trembled. "That's the only part of me that ever got out. A lullaby. Forty years, and I am a lullaby."

Ivo knelt in the light of their aunt — their aunt, their kin, their blood — and pressed their forehead to the cold floor, and wept the way they had not wept at the graveside, the way they had not wept on any road since the ford. Nettle curled against their back, a warm weight, saying nothing, giving them the animal mercy of simple presence.

* * *

It took a long time for the crying to burn down to embers. When Ivo could speak again, Sera was singing very softly — not a new verse, but an old one, one of the first, the one about the smell of the warp. She stopped when Ivo stirred.

"I need to tell you why I came," Ivo said. "All of it. The crow, the ward cracking, the god in the well, Veyra, the mountain. You deserve the whole shape of it."

So Ivo told her. The valley forgetting. The wardlight failing. The contract in the loom. The fox at the ford, who flicked one ear at his own mention and pretended to groom his shoulder. The knight without a banner. The refugees. The god small as a coin drowning in its well. The nametakers. Corvane and its lying bells. The broker of small mercies. The Pale Regent and the truth of the ward: that it ran on her, on Sera, on her suffering renewed each generation like a crop that never failed. That grandmother had not sold a soul in the abstract — she had carried her daughter down a mountain road and handed her to the dark and walked home and lived fifty years beside the loom that hid the proof.

When it was done, Sera was quiet for so long that Ivo feared the light had guttered.

"A crop," she said at last. "Yes. That's what I am. They come down — the pale ones come down, once a generation, and they open the vessel a little way, and they take. Not enough to end me. Never enough to end me. Enough to keep the ward fat and the valley asleep and the peace unbroken." Her voice was steady in the way of things that have passed beyond breaking. "Do you know what I decided, down here, around year ten? I decided that if suffering could buy a whole valley's safety, then I would suffer *well*. I would make it mean something. Every verse I sang was a brick in that decision. So don't you dare," and the light blazed, sudden and fierce, a hearth-fire in a glass throat, "don't you *dare* pity me, little Ivo. Pity is just another harvest."

"I didn't come to pity you," Ivo said. "I came to take you home."

"No."

The word was small and absolute as a nail driven flush.

"You can't," Sera said. "Listen to me. Listen the way you listen to goats and crows — I know what you are, I felt it the moment you spoke my name, the beast-blood sings in you like a struck bell — listen. If they release me, the ward dies. Not fades. *Dies*, all at once, and everything it has been holding back comes through. The things the ward keeps out aren't weather, Ivo. I can feel them pressing on the far side of it, every hour of every year, patient as roots. And worse than that —" the light steadied, deliberate now, a teacher's voice — "worse: the moment I go free, the court loses its anchor in the upper world, and the Regent will not mourn. She will sell what remains of me to Veyra, or burn the ledger, or simply let the valley's name be eaten like all the others. Hollowmere survives because I stay. That is the arithmetic. I have done it ten thousand times in the dark. It does not change."

"Hollowmere also survives," Ivo said, "the way a prisoner survives. Behind walls, asleep, forgetting. Mama doesn't even remember you existed. The elders blamed the wardlight on *weather*. We have been a valley of sleepwalkers standing guard over your prison and calling it peace."

"Yes," Sera said. "And they are still alive. And the children are still children, and the goats still give milk, and my lullaby still gets sung. I chose that. Given the same choice again, I would choose it again. Do you understand? This is not despair talking. This is the sanest thing in me. I sang myself sane *for this* — so that when someone finally came, I would be able to say no with a clear mind."

Ivo knelt before the lantern and felt the impossible choice settle onto their shoulders with the weight of a yoke. Love demanding. Not love permitting, not love blessing — love *demanding*: demanding that Ivo honor the one wish Sera had left, which was to remain in a glass throat forever so that a valley of strangers could keep sleeping.

"There's another way," Ivo said. "There has to be. The god in the well — the drowned one — gave me a map written in dreams. It leads deeper than this gallery. Deeper than the Regent's throne. Down to the first spire, the root of all wards. The old gods are gathered there, diminished, waiting. Waiting for someone willing to pay." Ivo's hands closed on the black stone. "I don't know yet what the price is. But I know it's real, and I know it's mine to pay, and I know that I am not leaving this mountain without you."

"Ivo—"

"You sang a cradle before it existed," Ivo said. "You put yourself into a song and sent it up through forty years of stone so a child you'd never meet would grow up humming you. Don't tell me what love costs. I've been paying it my whole life without knowing the creditor's name."

The lantern was silent. Then, very slowly, the copper light began to move — not turning anymore, but reaching, straining against the vessel's inner curve like a hand pressed flat against a window.

"Then listen to me once more," Sera said, "because if you go down to the spire, you will meet things that trade in truth the way brokers trade in souls, and they will offer you bargains wrapped in your own best feelings. Whatever they ask — however fair it sounds, however *right* it sounds — you come back up here first. You stand where you're standing now, and you say my name, and I will tell you yes or no. Promise me. Promise me that even at the bottom of the world, my voice gets the final word on my own life."

"I promise."

"Say it like a vow. Say it the way you'd swear it to a god, because down here, little Ivo, everything listens, and words have weight, and you may need the weight later."

Ivo lifted their head. Around them the thousand lanterns had gone quiet, every soul in the great gallery listening, and the dark above was listening, and the deep below was listening.

"I promise," Ivo said, and the words rang off the pillars and sank into the black floor and did not fade. "By the goat-path and the grey hills, by the loom and the lullaby, by every verse you sang alone in the dark — whatever waits below, whatever price is asked, I return to this place and I say your name, and your yes or your no is the last word. On my voice. On my life."

The light in the vessel shuddered — and settled — and Sera began, very softly, to sing the fox-and-well song, the lullaby, the one piece of herself that had escaped, and Ivo knelt in the glow of their aunt's voice until Nettle rose at last and touched his nose to their ear.

"That vow will hold," the fox murmured. "That's the trouble with vows made down here. They become *things*. Things with edges." His ears pricked toward the gallery's far entrance, where the distant air had begun, very faintly, to taste of smoke and iron. "And I think, little Amberdale, that we are about to learn exactly how much weight yours can carry. Because something is coming down the mountain — something with an army behind it — and it is singing *her* name."

Far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the first bell of the mountain court began to ring — not its slow ceremonial toll, but fast, frantic, over and over, the way bells ring when the doors must be sealed.

Veyra had come.
