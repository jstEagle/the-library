## 16. The Usurper Arrives

Aldric had heard armies before. He had stood in shield-walls at Redford Ford and listened to the Veyran drums roll across the water like thunder deciding where to strike. He had marched in columns that made the earth hum. He knew what ten thousand boots sounded like on a road.

He had never heard an army arrive *inside* a mountain.

The bells were still ringing when the sound began — a deep, grinding groan from the stone itself, as though the mountain's bones were being pried apart. Dust sifted down through the lantern-light of the great hall in slow golden curtains. Around them, the court of the Pale Regent did not panic. That was the worst of it. The courtiers — those elegant, hollow-eyed things with their faces like polished bone — simply turned their heads toward the distant doors in unison, the way wheat turns toward wind.

"They're not afraid," Ivo whispered. Their voice was hoarse; they had been weeping near the soul-lanterns for what felt like hours. "Why aren't they afraid?"

"Because they don't understand yet," Aldric said. "Or because they think they're too proud to die." He put his hand on his sword hilt out of habit, knowing it would do nothing. A man carries a knife into a storm because his hand needs somewhere to be.

Nettle sat on Aldric's shoulder, tail lashing, ears flat. The fox's fur had gone pale along the spine — Aldric had learned to read that as fear. "She's not here to negotiate," Nettle said. "I told you what she does. She eats names. Gods first. Then whatever's carrying them."

"And this court is full of—"

"Lanterns." Nettle's voice was very quiet. "Thousands of them. A harvest already gathered, hanging in the dark, waiting for someone with the appetite to take them. Veyra hasn't marched all this way for territory. Territory rots. This—" he gestured at the vaulted dark above, where the captive souls burned in their glass and iron cages, "—this is a pantry."

The grinding stopped.

Then came the knocking.

It was soft, almost polite. Three impacts against the mountain's outer gate, spaced like a courteous caller at a manor door — except each one rang through the stone like a struck bell, and with each one, every soul-lantern in the hall flickered. Dimmed. As if something vast had drawn breath and pulled the light a little deeper into its lungs.

Aldric had seen Veyra do this once before, from the wrong side of a wall. He had been twenty-four, newly knighted, standing on the ramparts of Callow's Rest while she walked up to the gates alone, unarmed, in a grey dress, and asked — politely, courteously, three times — to be let in. On the third knock she had not broken the gate. She had simply begun to speak, and the gate had opened itself, because the gate's maker had died two hundred years ago and his name was one of the names she had eaten, and dead men's work answers to whoever holds their voice.

He had deserted that night. It was the only order he'd ever followed quickly.

"She'll ask three times," he said now, to no one and everyone. "Then she won't ask again."

* * *

They ran.

Not out — the ways out were already closing, the court's servants sealing doors with rituals older than any kingdom, pouring molten ward-silver into hinges, chanting names of stone and salt. Out was where Veyra wanted everyone to go: a sealed fortress full of souls is just a jar, and a jar needs only one lid.

"Deeper," Nettle said. "Down. There are old ways under the court — the throat we came by, and things below even that. Dead gods make poor walls but they make *many* doors."

"The drowned god," Ivo said. "Its well. Its map." They touched the dream-shapes behind their eyes like a person touching a bruise. "If there's a heart of this place — the first spire —"

"Then it's down," Aldric finished. "And so is she, eventually. Everything goes down eventually." But he said it without conviction, because the alternative — climbing back up through halls filling with lightless panic — was worse, and because some part of him, the part he hated most, wanted to see the heart of the thing that had taken everything from him twice.

The court was coming apart around them as they moved. Not screaming — the Pale Regent's courtiers did not scream — but the lesser folk, the small gray servants, the soul-brokers with their ledgers and their bottled mercies, those screamed beautifully and briefly. Aldric saw a broker he recognized — the smooth-voiced creature who had offered Ivo a year of memories for passage — standing in the middle of a corridor with his ledger open, tearing pages out and swallowing them, one after another, faster and faster, as if he could keep himself by keeping what he knew. Then the corridor lights went out ahead of him and behind him, both directions at once, and when they flared again he was gone, ledger and all, and there was nothing left where he'd stood but a smell like cold candle smoke.

"What is she doing?" Ivo gasped as they ran. "The gates haven't fallen. We'd hear it."

"She doesn't need the gates open yet." Nettle leapt from Aldric's shoulder to a ledge to Aldric's shoulder again, conserving effort with a soldier's economy. "She's doing what she always does. She's eating the *edges*. Every name tied to this place that isn't inside it — the names of the mountain itself, spoken by pilgrims; the names of the roads; the names of the dead who built it. She's making the outside hers first, so that when the inside opens, there will be nowhere for anything to run *to*. You can't flee a place if the world around it belongs to her."

"That's how she took the free cities' countryside," Aldric said. His mouth tasted of ash and old shame. "Village by village, but never straight at them. First the roads. First the fields. First every god whose shrine stood within a day's walk. By the time her soldiers arrived, the villages weren't defending themselves. They couldn't remember why they should."

Ivo looked at him sideways as they ran, and Aldric saw the question in their face — the question about the signed orders, the sister-village, the confession wrung out of him on the road weeks ago. They didn't ask it again. They had learned, this soft valley-child who could talk to goats, that some questions only need asking once.

* * *

The second knock came while they crossed the Hall of Ledgers, a cathedral-sized vault where every bargain ever struck beneath the mountain was recorded in script that crawled slowly across vellum like insects. Aldric felt it through his boots, through his teeth, through the fillings of old wounds.

Every contract in the room changed.

He watched it happen. The crawling script — centuries of bargains, trades, souls sold and services owed — shuddered, stilled, and then began to rewrite itself, line by line, the old ink fading and new words surfacing beneath like corpses rising in a pond. He caught one line as it passed: *the ward of Hollowmere shall endure so long as the named soul endures* — and watched the word *endure* dissolve and re-form as *belong*.

"No," Ivo breathed. They had stopped running. "No, no — Sera's bargain — it's changing —"

"It's being *transferred*." Nettle's hackles had risen fully now; the fox looked twice his size and half his courage. "That's what the second knock buys her. Ownership. Every debt this court holds, she now holds. Every soul pledged to the Regent is pledged to *her*. She doesn't have to break in, little crow-bait. She only has to walk in and collect."

Aldric grabbed Ivo's arm. "Then your sister isn't safe here and she isn't safe anywhere. If Veyra takes this court, Sera's lantern comes off the shelf and into Veyra's throat, and no god, no spire, no bargain gets her back. Ever. Do you understand me?"

Ivo understood. Aldric watched them understand — watched the color leave their face in stages, like a tide going out. For a moment the boy — the person, he corrected himself, Ivo was grown, whatever the valleys thought — swayed as if the floor had tilted. Then something settled in them. Aldric had seen it before, in green soldiers and dying horses and once, memorably, in a cornered bear: the moment terror finishes its work and hands the reins to something harder.

"We go down," Ivo said. "To the heart. Before she does."

"There may be nothing there but the gods' bones and worse questions," Nettle warned.

"There's nothing left up here but a shelf," Ivo said, "and we know whose soul is on it."

* * *

The third knock came as they descended the Stair of Teeth, a spiral cut through raw rock where the steps had never been dressed or smoothed, where the mountain's own granite jutted in fangs from the walls. Aldric counted without meaning to. Old habits. At Redford Ford he had counted the drumbeats before the crossing; the count had saved forty lives and cost him the use of his left arm for a winter.

Three knocks. Silence afterward, which was somehow louder than the bells.

Then the singing started.

It rose through the stone from far above — thin, sweet, many-voiced. For one horrible heartbeat Aldric thought it was the soul-lanterns, freed or screaming. Then he understood, and wished he didn't: it was Veyra's host. Her soldiers sang as they worked. He remembered that. He had marched among them once, before he understood what the songs were *for* — harvest hymns, old ones, older than Veyra herself, songs meant to lull whatever was being harvested into calm. Sheep-songs. Songs sung over fields.

"They're opening the gates," Nettle said flatly. "From the outside, the guards will open the gates themselves. Half of them will believe she's come to save them."

"Half of everyone believes that," Aldric said. "That's how she's never lost a battle. Someone always opens the door."

Behind and above them, the mountain court began to die. There was no other word for it, though Aldric suspected poets would find prettier ones later, if there were later. The light changed first — the warm gold of the soul-lanterns dimming to a hungry blue-white, the color of ice over deep water. The temperature dropped so fast their breath plumed. And through the halls they had left, they heard the Regent's court finally, at the very end, begin to make noise: not screams, but arguments. Bargaining. The sound of creatures who had spent eternity buying and selling discovering, all at once, that they were stock.

Aldric kept his hand on the wall and kept walking down. Behind him, Ivo stumbled, righted, kept walking. Somewhere above them a bell fell silent mid-toll, and the silence of it was like a tooth pulled.

Then, faintly, threading down through fathoms of stone — impossible, and yet he heard it, and saw by the way Ivo froze that they heard it too — a voice. A woman's voice, singing alone amid the host-hymns, a different song, patient and low and terribly familiar to someone. Ivo turned toward the sound the way flowers turn toward sun.

"Sera," they whispered.

"You can't know that," Aldric said. "You've never heard her. You were born after—"

"I know my own blood." Ivo's eyes had gone strange in the blue light, wide and lit from within, and for a moment Aldric saw exactly what the valley had spent twenty-two years burying: not a farmhand, not a goat-talker. Something the old world used to build shrines for. "She's calling down. Through the stone. Through the lantern-glass. She knows Veyra's come, and she's telling me — telling me to run, to get out, that she'll hold, that she's held this long—"

"Can you answer her?" Nettle asked softly.

"I don't know how." Ivo pressed both hands flat against the cold rock, closed their eyes. Their lips moved. Nothing Aldric could hear. But under his palm, through his boots, in the marrow of him, Aldric felt it: a tremor pass through the mountain, gentle as a footstep, going *up*.

And then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the stone, from the dead air, from the singing — an answer came back down. Not words. A single sustained note, high and clear and stubborn, the note of someone who has sung herself sane for sixty years in a glass cage and refuses, now of all moments, to stop.

The hymns faltered. Just for a moment. All of them, all together, as if the whole army above had drawn one startled breath.

Nettle laughed — a short, wild, disbelieving bark of a laugh. "Oh, *she's* going to hate that. Sixty years in a bottle and she still breaks the choir." The fox spun to face them both, eyes bright as coals. "Listen to me, both of you. Whatever's about to happen up there, it's happening fast, and none of our plans survive contact with it. So here's the last plan: we reach the heart of this mountain before Veyra does, we find whatever's left of the old gods, and we ask them what they want. Because I have a terrible feeling they've been waiting a long time for exactly this."

"For what?" Aldric asked.

"For someone desperate enough," Nettle said, "and foolish enough, and *kind* enough, to pay their price." He looked at Ivo when he said it. He tried not to. He failed.

Above them, the third knock's echo finally faded. Below them, in the dark where the drowned god's well waited, something ancient shifted its weight, and the whole staircase trembled like a held breath about to break.

Aldric drew his sword anyway — because his hand needed somewhere to be — and went first into the dark.
