## 17. Prayer Answered Wrong

The mountain closed behind them like a mouth.

Ivo had no other word for it. One moment there was the passage — the throat of a dead god, Nettle called it, and he would know — and the next there was only stone, seamless as a healed scar, where the way in had been. The rumble of Veyra's army above them came down through the rock like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Dust sifted from seams in the ceiling, glittering in the lantern-light of the captive souls strung along the corridor like festival lamps.

"Charming," Nettle said. His fur was gray with powdered stone, his ears flat to his skull. "We've been swallowed. This is what being swallowed feels like. I want it noted that I objected to this plan."

"You didn't object," Aldric said. The knight moved ahead of them with his sword drawn, though Ivo had seen nothing down here worth a blade. "You complained about the *manner* of it."

"I object retroactively. It's one of my finest qualities."

But his voice was thin, and Ivo understood why. The souls burned differently down here than they had in the upper halls. Above, they had flickered like candles, each with its own color, its own trembling warmth. Here, deeper, they burned steady and white and terrible, and when Ivo looked at them too long, Ivo heard singing.

Not Sera's song. Not yet. But songs — dozens of them, hundreds, braided together into something like a hymn and something like screaming. The sound lived in the stone. In their teeth. In the soft hollows behind Ivo's eyes.

They walked because stopping felt like drowning.

The corridor bent twice, descended once, and then opened without warning into a gallery so vast that Ivo's soul-lantern light couldn't find its far wall. Pillars rose out of darkness like the legs of drowned giants. And between the pillars — movement. Shapes that might have been courtiers once, before whatever they were now finished happening to them: tall figures with faces smooth as river stones, gliding on errands Ivo did not want named, carrying vessels that wept softly as they passed.

"Don't look at their hands," Nettle murmured. "And don't answer if anything asks your name."

* * *

They hid in an alcove behind a pillar while three of the smooth-faced things drifted past, close enough that Ivo could hear what was inside their vessels. Prayers. Bottled prayers, sloshing gently, each one whispering its small desperate wish — *let her live, let him come home, let me be forgiven* — over and over, patient as water wearing at stone.

It was Aldric who found the crack.

He didn't announce it. He simply stopped, crouched, and pressed his palm flat against a section of wall where the soul-light fell wrong — where the glow pooled instead of spreading. When he drew his hand back, there was a seam there, hairline-thin, breathing cold air against Ivo's cheek.

"A service tunnel," Aldric said quietly. "Every palace has them. Every *court*. The people who carry the filth out never walk the same halls as the people who carry the gold in." A pause. Something old and bitter crossed his face. "I know how these places are built. I helped build one once."

Nettle looked at him for a long moment but said nothing, which from Nettle was a kind of mercy.

The crack widened when Aldric leaned his weight into it — groaned, ground, gave. Beyond it was a passage barely wide enough for a man's shoulders, unlit by any soul-lantern, descending.

"The heart of the court will be warded," Aldric said. "Guarded. There'll be no honest road to it."

"No," Ivo agreed. Their voice sounded strange in their own ears — hoarse from smoke and grief and the singing in the walls. "But I'm not looking for an honest road."

* * *

They went down.

For hours, by the feel of it — time ran strange beneath the mountain, stretching and pooling like wax — they crawled through the dark service veins of the court. The singing faded behind them, which should have been a mercy but felt like losing a hand. Twice the passage split, and both times Ivo felt the pull of the drowned god's dream-map rise in them like a tide: a certainty without words, a knowing that arrived the way dreams arrive, whole and unreasonable.

*Left,* the knowing said. *Down. Through the place where the stone remembers being alive.*

The map had come to Ivo in the ruined shrine, weeks ago and a lifetime ago, when the little god small as a coin had drowned in its own well and pressed its last prayer into their keeping along with its last geography. Ivo had thought then that the map led to the court's throne room, its seat of power, the Pale Regent's hall of lanterns.

They were wrong. They understood that now, deep in the dark, with the dream-map pulling them past every grand approach like a current pulling past a dock. The map didn't lead to power. It led *under* power. To the root.

The passage narrowed. Then narrowed again. Aldric had to sheathe his sword to fit; Nettle flowed through gaps that shouldn't have admitted a fox at all, and more than once Ivo caught him looking back with an expression that was not entirely fox.

Then the stone changed.

It happened between one step and the next. The dead grey basalt of the court's bones gave way to something older — veined and faintly warm, shot through with threads that glowed when Ivo breathed near them, dimming when they held their breath. Living stone. Or stone that remembered living. Underfoot it pulsed, very slowly, like something sleeping.

"We shouldn't be here," Nettle said, and for once there was no irony anywhere in him. "Ivo. We shouldn't be *here*. This is pre-court. This is before the Regent, before the bargain, before—" He stopped himself, ears flat. "This is older than names."

"That's why we're here," Ivo said.

The dream-map sang in their blood. *Almost.*

* * *

The passage ended at a threshold of black stone, and beyond it the world opened into the impossible.

The first spire.

Ivo had imagined — without ever admitting it — something like the spire of stories: tall, proud, crowned with light. What lay before them was inverted. The spire grew *downward*, a vast pale column hanging from the unseen roof of the cavern like a stalactite the size of a cathedral, its tip lost in darkness below, its body ringed with roots. Roots of stone. Roots of light. Roots that spread outward through the cavern walls and, Ivo understood with a lurch, outward through the whole world — the taproot from which every ward ever raised had grown, Hollowmere's included.

Wardlight moved in those roots. Dim, exhausted, guttering wardlight — but moving still, climbing the spire's pale length drop by drop like sap.

And around the base of it, gathered in the dark like moths too tired to fly, sat the gods.

There were perhaps twenty of them. Maybe fewer. Ivo counted and recounted and could not be sure, because they did not hold their shapes. One moment a figure like a woman made of river-reeds; the next, a heap of autumn leaves breathing. A shape like a bull carved from glacier ice became a hearth-fire became an old man mending a net. They were diminished — that was the only word. Gods worn down to embers of themselves, huddled at the root of all wards the way villagers huddle around the last fire of winter.

None of them turned when Ivo stepped into the cavern. But the smallest of them spoke, and its voice was everywhere at once, in the stone and the air and the marrow of Ivo's bones.

"You brought the prayer."

Ivo's hand went to their chest, to the place where they carried it — not in any pocket, but folded somewhere behind their ribs, where the little coin-sized god had placed it in the well-water dark. "I did. I promised."

"We know what you promised." The speaker resolved briefly into a shape like a moth the size of a child, wings patterned with fading constellations. "We watched you cross the valley. We watched you take the road. We watched you stand in the Regent's hall and hear the truth of your sister." A ripple went through the gathered shapes — grief, or its echo. "You are the one who speaks with beasts. The last of that line. Did you know that? Your gift is the oldest thread left in the old weave. Older than wards. Older than courts."

"Then you know why I've come."

"We know." The moth-god's wings dimmed. "Veyra comes. She is eating her way down through our siblings even now — every name she takes makes her larger, and every name she takes makes us smaller. When she reaches this place, she will drink the first spire dry, and every ward in the world will become hers. Every boundary. Every protection. Every hidden thing." Its voice frayed. "Including your valley, Amberdale. Including everything your grandmother died trying to protect."

"I know what she comes for." Ivo stepped closer to the huddled gods, and the dream-map burned behind their ribs. "So tell me how to stop her."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

Then the reed-woman spoke, and her voice was the sound of water finding its way downhill. "There is one way. There has only ever been one way. The spire is a root, and a root draws up what is given to it freely." Her leaf-shape shivered. "Blood can be taken. Names can be stolen. Souls can be bargained away — your grandmother proved that, may she rest, may she be forgiven. But a *gift*, freely given, willingly paid — that is the only currency the spire has ever accepted. It is how the first ward was raised. Someone gave something they could not take back."

"What did they give?" Aldric asked quietly, from the shadows by the threshold.

"They gave their voice," said the moth-god. "The first warden of the world spoke the first ward into being, and the speaking took their speech entire. That is the price of waking the spire. Not blood. Not memory. Not years." Its constellation-wings folded shut. "A voice. Freely given. Whole and forever."

Ivo stood very still.

Behind their ribs, the drowned god's last prayer stirred — and Ivo finally understood what it had been carrying all this way. Not a plea for rescue. A *seed*. A prayer meant to be planted here, at the root, by someone willing to pay what the planting cost.

"And if I give it," Ivo said. Their voice cracked on the words, and they let it. "If I speak the waking — my beast-speech, my gift, the only true thing I am — what happens to Sera?"

"She goes free," the reed-woman said. "Her lantern breaks. Her soul returns to flesh, wherever flesh will have her." A pause, gentle and merciless. "And the wards fall. All of them. Hollowmere stands naked under the sky for the first time in two hundred years. Everything your family hid. Everything your family *was*. It ends — and begins — with your voice."

Nettle made a sound low in his throat, almost human. "Ivo. Don't. There has to be another—"

"There isn't." Ivo was surprised at how calm they sounded. How certain. Somewhere above them, faint through miles of stone, came the tremor of Veyra's army, closer now, hungry. "There was never another. Grandmother knew it. She just couldn't pay it herself, so she paid with Sera instead, and bought us two hundred years of forgetting." They looked down at their own hands — hands that had spoken with goats in the dark of the barn, with crows on the fence posts, with horses dying on the road. Hands that had never once been empty of words.

Above them, the army shook the mountain again. Dust rained from the spire's pale length. The gods flinched in their huddle like children.

"One condition," Ivo said.

The gods waited.

"When I wake it — when I speak — I get to choose the words." Ivo lifted their chin, and for the first time since entering the mountain, they smiled. "And I have a very large family. Most of them aren't human."

The moth-god's wings opened slowly, constellation by constellation, until it blazed — the brightest thing in the dark.

"Then kneel at the root, Amberdale," it said, "and say what must be said."

Ivo knelt.

And high above, the mountain began to scream.
