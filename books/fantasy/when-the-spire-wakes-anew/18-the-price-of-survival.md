## 18. The Price of Survival

The throat of the dead god went down and down, and the deeper we walked, the more I understood that we were not walking through stone at all. We were walking through memory. The walls were ribbed like gullet-flesh in places, veined with a faint amber light that pulsed when I breathed, as though the mountain remembered having lungs. My boots found no dust here. The dark drank it.

Nettle trotted ahead of me, his tail low, his usual sardonic chatter abandoned somewhere on the stairs above. Aldric came behind with his sword drawn and his shield strapped, though we had all agreed there was nothing left behind us worth fighting — only ahead, only the thing at the bottom that the dream-map had shown me night after night: a door of root and bone, a hall where something small waited to be asked.

The god in the well had been no bigger than a coin. It had drowned slowly in its own water for three hundred years, offering prayers up to a sky that had stopped listening, and when I had lifted it out — cupped in two hands, light as an ash flake — it had pressed its map into my sleeping mind in gratitude or desperation or both. Dreams of descending. Dreams of a heart-room. Dreams of *they are waiting, they have always been waiting, bring them someone who is willing.*

I had not told Aldric what willing might cost. Nettle, I suspected, had guessed.

* * *

The last passage opened without warning. One moment the gullet narrowed to a crawl; the next, the air broke open around us like a held breath released, and we stood in a cavern so vast that the ceiling was a rumor, a darkness hung with slow-turning lights that might have been stars or might have been souls too old to burn.

And there they were.

The old gods. All of them. Diminished past imagining.

They sat in a ring on the cavern floor like beggars around a dead fire — shapes that had once been mountains and rivers and the turning of seasons, now worn down to almost nothing. A woman made of grey river-water, thin as spilled milk, her edges dissolving and reforming. An old man whose body was a stand of leafless birches, each trunk no thicker than my wrist. Something that had perhaps been a stag once, or a storm, crouched with its antlers broken off at the roots. They were small. That was the horror of it. Not monstrous, not terrible. *Small.* Gods the size of children, the size of birds, the size of the coin-god who had begged me from its well.

At the center of their ring stood a spire.

Not the spire of songs and stories — not white, not crowned, not tall. This was the first one, the root of all wards ever woven, and it was a stump. A broken pillar of black glass rising no higher than a man's chest, its top sheared away as if snapped by a careless hand, its surface crawling with hairline veins of light. The wardlight of Hollowmere, I realized, and felt my stomach turn over. Every charm over every hidden valley in the world had been cut from this tree. And this was the stump left behind.

"They're dying," Nettle said quietly. He had gone very still, ears flat, and his voice had none of his usual armor on it. "Ivo. They're not waiting to bargain. They're waiting to be finished."

Aldric's hand tightened on his sword hilt. "Then this is a trap."

"No." The river-woman spoke, and her voice was the sound water makes under ice — patient, cold, and very far away. Her eyes, if eyes they were, found me across the whole breadth of the cavern as though I stood a hand's breadth from her. "No trap. Traps are for those who have something worth taking. We are past taking, knight. We are down to asking."

She rose. It took her a long time. Water does not hurry.

"You are the beast-speaker," she said. "The one born after the doors closed. We felt you before you crossed the first threshold — a voice moving through the world's quiet, waking things that had decided to sleep forever. You healed a horse on the refugee road and told no one. You carried a drowned god's prayer in your mouth like a live coal. You heard your sister singing in her lantern and did not look away."

"How do you know these things?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted it to.

"We are gods of small things now," she said. "Small things are all we hear."

The birch-man stirred, his twigs clicking together like knucklebones. "Ask your question, child of the valley," he rasped. "You climbed all this way carrying it. Set it down."

So I set it down. I told them about Sera — the lantern-voice, the song she sang to keep herself whole, her refusal, her love that would rather burn than let Hollowmere fall. I told them about Veyra marching on the mountain with her harvest-carts and her nametakers, meaning to drink this court dry and every soul in it. I told them about the ward, about twenty-two years of quiet bought with a girl's suffering, renewed generation after generation because no one in Hollowmere had ever thought to ask what the silence cost.

"And you want her free," the river-woman said. "And the usurper unmade. And the ward broken, and the valley opened, and every lantern in the court beneath lit and loosed. You want the world put right."

"Yes."

"Then hear the price." She drifted closer, and where she passed, the air smelled of thaw and river-mud, of springs beginning. "Veyra takes names by force and calls herself their owner. The court beneath buys souls and calls itself their keeper. Both are theft, and theft is why everything you see in this room is small. Power taken never lasts. Power *given*—" her cold voice cracked, just slightly, like ice underfoot, "—power given freely is the only coin that has ever been real."

The birch-man spread his twig-hands. "To shatter Veyra's claim, to break the root-ward, to open every lantern in the deep — the spire must wake fully for the first time since it was broken. And the spire wakes only for a voice."

"A voice?" Aldric stepped forward, putting himself between me and the gods, which was absurd and which I loved him for. "Whose? Take mine. I've got a name worth nothing and a voice full of confessions—"

"Not yours." The river-woman's gaze did not leave me. "It must be freely given, and it must be the gift itself. Not words, knight. The *gift*. The thing that lets her speak and be answered. The beast-speech. The oldest voice there is — older than names, older than courts. The voice the world listened to before it learned to lie."

The cavern went very quiet. Even the slow-turning lights above seemed to stop.

"Give it up," she said gently, "and you will never speak to anything that is not human again. The goats will be animals. The crows will be birds. The horse you saved will remember your hands and nothing else. You will walk through a world suddenly, permanently silent, for the rest of your life. And in exchange, every creature in this mountain — every soul-lantern, every chained god, every stone that remembers being alive — will hear your last command and rise."

I looked at the spire. Its black glass caught my face and gave it back warped and small.

Twenty-two years, I thought. Twenty-two years of whispering to goats behind the barn with my collar up, of pressing my palm against a crow's feathers to feel words travel through bone instead of daring to say them aloud. Twenty-two years of being half a person in a valley built to make halfness feel like safety. I had hated the hiding. But I had never hated the gift. The gift was the only true thing about me. The gift was *mine.*

That was, I understood dimly, exactly why it was worth anything.

* * *

"I need a moment," I said.

Nettle made a sound low in his throat — not quite a word, not quite a growl. Aldric sheathed his sword, which was his way of saying he'd run out of things to fight. We walked apart from the ring of gods, out to the edge of the cavern where the ribbed dark began again, and the three of us stood in a rough triangle like people around a grave.

"Don't," Nettle said.

"You don't get to—" I stopped. Breathed. "Nettle. You gave your name to a soul-broker for me. You don't get to tell me what's too much."

"That was a *name*. I hadn't used it in three hundred years. It was gathering dust." His green eyes were fierce and wet in the spire-light. "This is different. This is the only thing you have that's actually yours. Everything else — the valley, the family, even your sister — the world gave you those and the world can take them back. But the speech. Ivo, I've heard you talk to foxes. Do you know what that's worth? There are kings who would trade their kingdoms—"

"And that," I said, "is why it has to be me. Don't you see? Veyra takes. The court buys. Everyone powerful in this story got that way by taking something that wasn't theirs." I looked back at the small gods in their ring, at the stump of the first spire. "Maybe the only way anything gets fixed is if someone gives. Freely. On purpose. Even when it costs exactly what it looks like it costs."

Aldric was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the flat steadiness he saved for the worst truths. "On the retreat from Callow Ford, I signed the order that emptied your sister-village. Two hundred people moved at sword-point so Veyra could strip their shrine. I told myself it was mercy — better moved than burned." He looked at me, and there was no plea in his face, only the arithmetic. "I have been looking for a payment big enough for eleven years. There isn't one. There never will be. So I'll tell you what I know about giving: it doesn't buy back what's lost. It doesn't balance anything. It's just the only direction left to walk."

"That's the worst comfort anyone's ever offered me," I said.

"It's the only kind I stock."

Nettle still hadn't moved. Then, quietly, in the voice he used perhaps once a year: "If you do this — when you do this — I want you to know I'd have followed you anyway. Voiceless. Stupid. Human as bread. It was never the talking, Ivo. In case you thought it was."

Something in my chest came loose and would not go back.

"I know," I said. "Same."

* * *

We returned to the ring.

The river-woman watched me come with the patience of water finding its level, and I saw in her worn face something I had seen in the drowned god's coin-small features in the ruined shrine: hope, held so long and so carefully that it had scarred over into something like shame. Gods should not have to be ashamed of hoping. The sight of it settled the last argument in me, if any argument had been left.

"I'll give it," I said. "Freely. No conditions."

"There is one thing," the birch-man said, twigs whispering. "A freely given gift must be given in words. The last command must be spoken with the voice that is being given — spoken to everything that will hear it. Choose your words well, child. They will be the last ones the world answers."

My mouth went dry. Every creature in the mountain. Souls in lanterns who had forgotten language. Stones that remembered being alive. Gods small as coins. Whatever I said, I would say once, to all of them, forever.

I looked at Sera's lantern-light far above us, somewhere in the court's high halls — I couldn't see it, but I could feel it, the way you feel a fire in the next room. Sing yourself sane, sister. Almost done.

I knelt at the stump of the spire. The black glass was warm, warmer than it should have been, warm like a living thing pretending hard to be a stone. I laid both palms flat against it. It beat against my hands like a second heart, slow and enormous, keeping time with nothing.

"What do I do?" I whispered.

"Speak," said the river-woman. "And mean every word down to the root of you."

I closed my eyes. I reached for the speech — really reached, the way I had never dared in Hollowmere, not behind the barn, not anywhere, holding it leashed my whole life — and let it rise up through me whole and unhidden for the first time. It came like floodwater. Like dawn. I felt it fill my chest and throat and skull, felt it touch the spire beneath my hands, felt the whole mountain lean in to listen.

And I spoke — not loudly; it didn't need loudness. It needed only truth, and I had finally stopped lying about what I was:

"Hear me. I am Ivo Amberdale, of Hollowmere, and I give my voice freely — all of it, the whole gift, to the last word — for the sister in the lantern, and the strangers in the dark, and every small god who forgot it was ever worshipped. No one bought this. No one stole it. It is given."

The spire flared. Light ran up the broken stump in veins of gold, and the whole cavern groaned like a ship coming about, and the small gods rose to their feet — all of them, the water and the birches and the broken-antlered storm, standing as tall as their ruin allowed.

"Now rise," I said, and my voice was already fraying at the edges, unraveling into the spire thread by golden thread, and I poured everything remaining into the last of it: "*Rise.*"

The word went out through the black glass, down the root of all wards, into every lantern and chain and sleeping stone in the mountain. I felt it land. I felt ten thousand minds open at once like eyes. Far above us, in the court beneath, the first lantern-shackle burst with a sound like a bell struck by lightning — then another, then dozens, then a storm of them, and a sound began to climb out of the deep that I will hear until I die: the sound of everything chained in the dark remembering, all at once, that it had legs.

My voice went with it. I felt the last of it leave — a warmth behind my breastbone guttering out like a candle pinched between wet fingers — and I opened my mouth to call Sera's name and heard only my own breath, ordinary and human and terribly alone.

Nettle's paw closed around my wrist. Aldric's arm came under my shoulder, hauling me up as the cavern shook and the small gods streamed upward past us, growing as they climbed, drinking the waking mountain like rain.

And from the heights above — distant, vast, and closing fast — came a new sound, a marching sound, iron on stone, and a cold voice amplified by stolen names announcing that the harvest had arrived.

Veyra had reached the mountain.
