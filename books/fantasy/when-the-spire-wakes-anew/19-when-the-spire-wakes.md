## 19. When the Spire Wakes

### IVO

Ivo gave their voice to the mountain, and the mountain answered.

The word they spoke — *rise* — was not a word in any tongue Ivo knew. It came up through the soles of their feet, through the black glass of the spire's root, and it went out of them like a river leaving a broken dam. They felt it pass their teeth, their tongue, the soft hollow of the throat where the beast-speech had lived for twenty-two years, and it took everything with it as it went: every conversation with goats at dusk, every argument with crows, every whispered goodnight to the fox at the ford.

It did not hurt. That was the strange mercy of it. It simply emptied, the way a bell empties of sound after the last ring has faded, and what remained was a silence so complete that Ivo could hear their own heart stumble in it.

Then the world began to wake.

Light came first — not from above, where Veyra's fires were chewing through the upper halls, but from below, from the roots, from everywhere at once. The captive souls hanging in their lantern-cages flared like ten thousand dawn-suns caught in glass. The dark stone of the deep halls turned translucent, then transparent, then gone altogether, and Ivo saw the shape of the mountain from within: vast and honeycombed, every chamber threaded with light, every corridor a vein of gold.

And then the voices came.

Not words. Not yet. First there was only sound — a rising chorus that made the bones of the mountain hum, made Ivo's ribs resonate like struck wood. It was every soul in every lantern speaking at once, and beneath that, deeper, older, the diminished gods uncurling from their long sleep, stretching powers grown thin and strange with disuse. Ivo could not understand any of it anymore. Whatever language had let them hear the drowned god's plea, whatever gift had let a dying horse feel its own legs again — it was gone. They stood in the middle of the greatest singing the world had ever made, and to them it was only music.

They would have wept, if Sera's hand had not closed around theirs.

"Come on," she said. Her voice was thin as paper after decades of song, but it carried. "Up. While everyone is looking at *her*."

Above them, far above, something vast turned over in its sleep and opened its eyes.

### ALDRIC

Aldric had held gates before. He'd held the gate at Marrowford for three days against men he'd trained himself, and he'd held the gate at the sister-village — though there had been nothing on the other side but Veyra's order and his own signature, and the gate had never needed holding at all. He had learned that a gate is not a wall. A gate is a promise you make to people behind you: *not yet. Not through me.*

The gate of the court beneath the mountain was a thing of pale bone and older iron, tall as a cathedral door, and beyond it Veyra's host poured down the throat of the world like water finding a crack.

She had come fast. Faster than any army should move — her soldiers did not march so much as *pour*, rank after rank, faces blank with the names they'd surrendered at the checkpoints, eyes lit from inside with borrowed divinity. Aldric had seen her harvest a village once. He had told himself, all these years, that he had watched because he could do nothing else. He had been lying to himself for a very long time, and lying, he'd found, keeps a man warm right up until it doesn't.

He planted his feet on the stair above the gate, put his back to the climbing dark where two figures were hauling themselves upward through falling light, and drew his sword.

It was a poor sword. He'd sold the good one years ago, drunk, to a man whose name he couldn't remember, and this one had a nick in the edge and a grip wrapped in donkey-hide. Nettle had laughed at it. "You're going to hold a gate against a god-eater with *that*?"

"I've held worse doors with less," Aldric said.

The first soldiers reached him at a run, and the fight swallowed him whole.

It was not skill that kept him alive, though he still had some. It was the mountain itself. Every time the line pressed him back, the walls shivered and shed their light, and the light did things. A lantern-soul burst somewhere overhead and a soldier stopped mid-swing, staring at the falling sparks, weeping without knowing why — Aldric understood later that the man's stolen name had come home to him in that moment, and that a man meeting his own name after years of absence cannot fight anyone. Another soldier raised his blade and the blade remembered being ore in a mountain's side, and simply declined. Veyra's power was built of taken things, and taken things, tonight, were going home.

But she herself was coming down, and nothing in the halls could stop her. Aldric could hear her now through the din — not footsteps but an absence of them, a silence moving through the noise like a hole cut in cloth, and around that silence her voice, calm and terrible, giving orders to men who no longer had names to obey with:

"The spire is waking. Bring me its heart. Bring me the thief who woke it."

Aldric spat blood onto the stairs and set his feet wider.

"Not yet," he promised the dark behind him. "Not through me."

### NETTLE

Nettle had been many things in a long and mostly shameful life — courtier, traitor, fox — but he had never once, in all of it, been a hero, and he intended to lodge a formal complaint about how it felt.

The climb out of the spire's root was not a climb so much as a pilgrimage through a cathedral mid-collapse. Light poured down the shaft in sheets; the walls sang; chunks of ceiling the size of cottages came loose and fell past them into depths that glowed instead of ending. Sera went first, finding handholds by instinct — sixty years of singing in the dark had taught her the mountain's grain better than any mason — and Ivo followed, and Nettle ran along the ledges beside them, four-legged, low, cursing under his breath in three dead languages.

Then Ivo's legs failed.

Nettle heard it happen before he saw it: the rhythm of their steps breaking, the small wet sound of knees striking stone. He turned. Ivo was down on the ledge, hands flat against the rock, chest heaving, and when they lifted their head their face was gray as ash.

"I can't—" Ivo tried, and the voice that came out was wrong. Thin. Human, entirely human, stripped of every echo that had ever lived in it. They looked at their own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "It took everything. I didn't know it would take *everything*."

"It always does," Nettle said. "That's what makes it worth anything."

He looked up the shaft — Sera already twenty feet above, silhouetted against cascading light — and then down, where the singing was swelling toward a pitch that made his teeth ache, and then at Ivo, crumpled on the ledge like a coat someone had dropped.

He sighed the sigh of the profoundly put-upon.

"Well," he said. "Get on, then."

Ivo stared. "What?"

"My back. Your weight. Basic arithmetic, Amberdale. I'm stronger than I look, which admittedly isn't hard, since I look like a scavenging opportunity." He crouched, tail lashing. "Unless you'd rather wait here for the god-eater. She's taking visitors."

Ivo got onto his back.

Nettle had carried things before — hares, stolen hens, one memorable and deeply regrettable lamb — but he had never carried a human, and he braced for the weight to be crushing. It wasn't. It wasn't only the mountain's waking light either, though that helped, flooding the stone with strength the way spring floods a dry streambed. Some of it, he suspected, was the bargain he'd made with the broker: *my name, freely given.* Names had weight. He had paid his away, and perhaps what remained of him weighed lighter for it, or perhaps — and this thought he shoved away before it could become hope — the mountain remembered who he used to be, and lent him the difference.

He ran. Ledge to ledge, wall to wall, paws finding purchase on stone that seemed, tonight, almost eager to be climbed. Below them the singing crested and broke, and something enormous rose through the mountain's core — he felt it pass them like a ship passing a swimmer, displacing air, displacing *time* — and Ivo clung tighter and said, close to his ear, in that new thin human voice:

"Nettle. What is that? What's coming up?"

"That," said Nettle, leaping a gap wide enough to swallow a house, "would be the old gods. All of them. At once." He grinned, and it was not a nice grin. "Someone finally asked them nicely."

### IVO

They came out of the mountain's shoulder into night air and found the sky full of war.

Veyra's host had ringed the peak — ten thousand torches, siege engines, ranks of the nameless standing shoulder to shoulder in the snow — and the mountain was rising against them. It was not armies that poured from the waking spire. It was everything else. Wolves with lantern-light behind their eyes. Eagles whose wingbeats scattered sparks. Bears, stags, foxes in their dozens, and stranger things besides: river-things and root-things, shapes of mist with antlers, small gods riding their oldest servants into battle. The beasts did not roar. They *spoke* — and though Ivo could no longer understand a single word, they could see the effect, could see Veyra's front lines falter as every horse, every mule, every war-dog in her train turned its head to listen and then, gently, deliberately, lay down.

An army that cannot ride cannot charge. An army whose animals will not carry its banners cannot even retreat well.

"She can't hold them," Sera breathed beside Ivo. She stood at the mountain's mouth with the wind tearing at her hair, and her face was wet. "All those names she ate. Everything she took is turning around to look at her."

Below, at the great gate, a lone figure held the stairs.

Even from here Ivo knew the shape of him: broad shoulders, ruined stance, a sword with a nick in it moving in the old disciplined patterns. Ser Aldric fought alone against a tide, and the tide broke around him and broke around him and could not pass, because the mountain kept unmaking the men who pressed him — returning their names, loosening their weapons, filling their eyes with the light of the souls they'd marched past — and because Aldric, it appeared, refused absolutely to die on a night when dying would be convenient.

"He's buying us the sky," Nettle said softly.

And above it all, descending through the smoke like a queen descending into her own wedding, came Veyra.

She did not fly. She walked down the air, each step finding invisible stone, and where she passed the freed gods flinched away from her the way dogs flinch from a master's raised hand — old habit, older fear. She was beautiful in the way of things made from other things: her hair was storm-light harvested from a hundred temples, her cloak was woven from prayers nobody had answered, and when she spoke, her voice reached every ear on the mountainside at once, gentle as a mother's.

"Little thief," she said, and Ivo knew she meant them, knew she was looking straight at them across half a mile of burning air. "You woke my larder. Do you know what I built here? Order. Quiet. A world where nothing suffers except what I choose." Her gaze slid to Sera, and something like hunger moved across her face. "And you. The crown jewel. Sixty years of suffering, aged like wine. Come here, child. I'll wear your pain so much better than you ever have."

Sera stepped forward — frail, paper-voiced, sixty years a lantern — and Ivo grabbed her arm, and it was Ivo who shouted back, in the only voice they had left, the plain human voice that could command nothing and compel no one:

"You want the spire's heart?" Their voice cracked across the snowfield, small and furious and utterly mortal. "Come and take it. But you'll have to go through everyone you ever stole from."

For a moment — just a moment — Veyra hesitated.

And the mountain, which had been waiting, I think, for exactly that hesitation, brought up its oldest resident.

The drowned god rose out of the peak like a coin surfacing from a well — small, so small, smaller than the smallest of Veyra's stolen names, barely more than a flicker of light with a voice attached. Ivo could not understand its words. No one could; it spoke in the first tongue, the tongue before names, the tongue Veyra had spent her whole ascension devouring. But its meaning needed no translation. It rose up before her, tiny and drowning still, and it said, in effect:

*You forgot me. You forgot all of us. And forgotten things are the only ones you cannot eat.*

Then it opened what served it for hands, and gave back to the night every dream-map it had ever carried, and the maps became roads, and the roads became doors, and through every door came everything Veyra's empire had buried.

The mountain went to war in earnest.

### ALDRIC

Later — much later, when his hands had stopped shaking and someone had pressed a cup of something hot into them — Aldric would try to describe the fall of Veyra and find he couldn't, not honestly, not in a way that fit inside words.

He remembered fragments. The moment the tide against him simply stopped coming, every soldier turning at once to watch the sky. Veyra's face as the small god rose before her — not fear, worse than fear, *recognition*, the look of a woman seeing a debt she'd hoped was dead. The souls pouring from the spire in their thousands, streaming upward like reversed rain, and each one pausing, as it passed, to touch her — not to strike her, just to *touch*, and at each touch she grew slightly less, slightly thinner, slightly more wind-shaped, until there was not enough of her left to walk upon the air.

He remembered that she screamed, at the end, in a great many voices that were not hers.

Mostly, though, he remembered the gate. Because in the last hour, when the rout began and Veyra's nameless soldiers fled down the mountain with their names newly returned and their minds full of horror at what they'd done, some of them had come back up. Not to fight. To surrender. To kneel at the foot of his bloody stairs, dozens of them, hundreds, and say in cracked and rusty voices: *We have names again. Tell us what to do with them.*

And Aldric — disgraced, drunk, signatory of the order that had emptied Hollowmere's sister-village, holder of gates — had looked down at all those upturned faces and found, to his astonishment, that he knew the answer.

"Bury the dead," he told them. "Free the prisoners. Then go home, if you have one, and if you don't, build one." He wiped his sword on his cloak. "You don't need anyone to tell you more than that. That's the whole secret. Nobody ever had more than that."

They went to do it. And Ser Aldric, knight of nowhere, bannerless and purposeless and alive, sat down on the top step of the gate he had held, and laughed until he cried, and then cried until he laughed, while the freed souls of the mountain rose around him like snow falling upward.

* * *

### IVO

Dawn found them on the mountainside, among the ruins and the singing.

The spire stood open now, its heart bare to the sky, and the freed gods drifted about it like moths about a lamp — small, dim, dazed, free. The lanterns hung empty. Somewhere below, the Pale Regent's court was dissolving, its masters fled or unmade, its halls filling with daylight for the first time in an age. Sera slept against Ivo's shoulder, truly slept, her breathing slow and even, her hands — real hands, warm flesh, sixty years late — folded in her lap. In the morning light she looked young. Younger than Ivo, almost. Grandmother's daughter. Ivo's aunt. A girl who had been a lamp.

Ivo tried to speak to her, out of habit, in the old way — the way that had once crossed a valley to a crow, that had gentled goats and steadied dying horses. They opened their mouth and reached for it and found, where the gift had lived, only smooth ordinary silence, the same silence every other human carried.

It should have hurt more than it did.

Instead Ivo found they could only sit with it, the way you sit with a wound that has finally stopped bleeding, and listen to the world make its sounds without translation. The wind in the rocks. The murmur of the gods. A fox's slow breathing nearby, and — faintly, from the slopes below, where the freed beasts were wandering home through the snow — the wordless morning talk of ten thousand creatures, none of whom Ivo would ever understand again.

They were strangers now. All of them. The goats in Hollowmere would look at them across the fence and see only a person, and the crows would say only *caw*, and the whole bright hidden country Ivo had lived in for twenty-two years was closed to them forever.

Ivo found, to their surprise, that they were smiling.

Footsteps crunched through the snow. Nettle trotted up and sat down heavily beside them, singed, exhausted, wearing the expression of a creature who has done one heroic act too many and intends to be unbearable about it.

"So," the fox said. "Still human, then."

"Still human," Ivo agreed.

"Mm." Nettle gazed out over the smoking, singing, liberated mountain, and his tail curled around his paws. "Terrible loss, I suppose. All that beast-speech. Whole flocks of crows in mourning, I shouldn't wonder." A pause. "Although — and I mention this purely as gossip — the small gods are saying the spire remembers every voice that ever woke it. Says a debt like yours doesn't just vanish. Says it goes... dormant. Sleeping. Waiting."

Ivo turned to look at him.

"Waiting for what?"

Nettle smiled the smile of a fox who knows exactly how much he knows and has decided you may have a quarter of it.

"For a reason, Amberdale. Debts like that wait for a reason." He nodded downhill, toward the road, toward the valleys, toward home. "Now come on. You have a sister to feed and a valley to unhide, and I have a reputation as a cynical mercenary to rebuild from scratch. We've a long way to walk, and the mountain, I'm told, is letting us leave by the front door."

Far below, the great gate of the court beneath the mountain swung slowly open, and the morning poured in.
