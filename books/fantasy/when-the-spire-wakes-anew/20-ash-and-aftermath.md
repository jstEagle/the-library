## 20. Ash and Aftermath

The wind that had been Veyra did not scream when it died.

Aldric had expected screaming. He had spent eleven years learning to expect it — from the villages, from the harvest-camps, from the men and women who knelt in the ash of their own burned gods and begged. He had built a whole architecture of dread around the moment of her ending, and so when it came quietly, he did not trust it. He stood in the shattered mouth of the mountain's great gate, sword still in his hand, and watched the last of her come apart.

She had been mid-harvest. That was the thing he could not stop seeing. She had stood in the court beneath with her arms raised and her mouth open, drinking the light of the soul-lanterns, and the light had been pouring into her like water into a cracked cup — and then the spire woke, and the cup broke, and the water remembered it was not hers.

She came apart the way a name comes apart when you say it too many times. First the edges went. Her hands blurred, then her shoulders, then the terrible crown of her, which was not gold at all but a ring of stolen syllables that had learned to shine. She looked at Aldric. He was sure of it, even through the unraveling — those grey eyes finding his across the chaos, the way they had found him the first day he knelt to her, young and hungry and certain that serving a woman who ate gods was a kind of ambition.

She did not speak. Perhaps she had nothing left to speak with. Perhaps, in the last of it, she was only wind again, the way she had been before the first name found her — a thing that moved and wanted and could not hold its shape. The wind went through the great gate, past Aldric, out into the grey morning, and it smelled for one moment of every burned village he had ever stood in, and then it smelled of nothing at all, and then it was gone.

The silence afterward was the largest thing Aldric had ever stood inside.

* * *

He did not remember sitting down. He was sitting, though — on a slab of fallen masonry, sword across his knees, in a hall where lanterns had hung from the ceiling like fruit and now hung like embers going out. The captive souls were leaving. He could see them, or he could see the light that was them, drifting up through the cracks in the world the waking spire had made. Some of them paused. One of them — a small bright thing, no bigger than a candleflame — drifted close to his face and stayed there a long moment, close enough that he felt warmth on his cheek like a hand.

"I don't know who you were," he told it. His voice came out ruined, sanded down by smoke. "I'm sorry. I don't know who any of you were. That was the whole crime, wasn't it. Nobody was supposed to know."

The light did not answer. It touched his cheek once more, the way a bird might land and think better of it, and then it rose and was gone, and Aldric sat alone in the cooling dark with the ash settling on his shoulders like a second cloak.

He had held the gate. That much he could count. When the mountain had shaken and the court's guards — those grey, nameless soldiers Veyra had made of men who sold themselves too thoroughly to keep their shapes — had come up the deep stairs to stop the boy and the fox and the sister, Aldric had stood at the gate with a sword he had not properly sharpened in six years, and he had held it. He did not know how long. His arms told him it had been long. The bodies told him too, grey and slack around him like shed coats. Somewhere in the middle of it he had stopped fighting like a knight and started fighting like a man with nothing left to lose, which was a thing he had thought he understood at thirty and understood now, at forty-odd, in a way that sat in the bones.

The boy had made it up. He had seen him go — Ivo, the beast-speaker, the quiet one from the valley that forgot, walking into the spire's heart with the drowned god's dream-map glowing faint as frostbite on their skin. And the sister. And the fox.

Aldric tried to stand, and his legs informed him, at length, that this was a negotiation, not a request. He made it up eventually, one hand on the wall, and began the long walk out through halls that were dying around him.

The court beneath had been lit by captive souls for longer than anyone living remembered. Now the souls were free, and the light was leaving, and without the light the halls were only stone — old stone, patient stone, stone that had been hollowed out of the mountain's living root and furnished with the trophies of a long, quiet theft. He passed the throne of the Pale Regent and found it empty. He did not look closely at what was on the floor beside it. Some mercies you grant yourself.

He passed the room where they had kept the ledgers. The ledgers were burning. He did not question this; the mountain seemed to be tidying up after itself, the way a body burns a fever out. Names he had helped put into those pages were curling off them now as smoke, rising, going wherever freed names go. He stood in the doorway and let the smoke get into his eyes so that he would have an excuse, and then he walked on.

* * *

Outside, the world had gone strange and clean.

It was morning — a real morning, the first he had seen in a week that was not filtered through battle-smoke. The mountain's slopes were littered with the debris of Veyra's host: not bodies, mostly, because most of her host had not been bodies to begin with, but the shed armor of men who had woken from her service to find themselves standing on a mountainside with no memory of why. They were wandering down the slopes in ones and twos, blinking, holding their own hands as if the hands belonged to strangers. Some of them were crying. Most of them were just walking, in whatever direction their feet chose, going home to homes that had learned to live without them.

Aldric understood them completely. He watched one — a big man with a farm-broad back and a soldier's ruined hands — stop at the edge of the road, look back up at the smoking mountain, and mouth something to it. A thank-you or a curse; from this distance there was no telling, and Aldric suspected the man himself did not know.

The beasts were everywhere. That was the other thing the spire's waking had done. Every creature in the mountain — and the mountain had been *full*, Aldric realized now, full in a way no natural place is full — had risen when Ivo's last word went through the stone like a bell through water. Wolves and wrens and things with too many joints and things with no joints at all, the small gods' old companions, the beasts that had served the first spire before the first theft. They were dispersing now into the wide world like a spilled handful of seeds. A stag with antlers like white coral picked its way past Aldric close enough to touch and gave him a look of such mild, ancient indifference that he felt briefly, absurdly, blessed.

He found a stream below the treeline and drank until his stomach complained, then washed the ash out of his hair and the blood off his hands — some of it his, most of it not — and looked at his face in the water.

It was the same face. That was the strange part. Eleven years of Veyra's service, and the gate, and the ash, and the face in the stream was the same face that had knelt to her in the rain outside Corvane, young and hungry and certain. He had thought unburdening would show. He had thought a man who put down a weight that heavy would look *lighter*. But the water only gave him back a tired soldier with grey coming in early at the temples, and after a while he stopped looking, because there is only so long a man can wait for his own reflection to tell him who he is.

That was the thing, he decided, wringing out his cloak. That was the thing he would have to carry out of the mountain instead of the weight. Veyra had given him his purpose the day she took his oath: *hold the line, keep the harvest, be the sword at the throat of the world's small mercies.* Cruel purpose, but purpose. A man can march a long way on cruelty if it has a direction. Now the direction was ash on the wind and the cruelty was nameless, and Aldric of the broken banner stood up from the stream with nothing in his hands but a sword and no idea in his head of where a sword was wanted.

He was, he realized, free. And freedom, it turned out, was just the word for a road with no reason on it.

* * *

He found the fox at midday, sitting on a flat stone in the middle of the ruined road that wound down toward the valley lands, grooming one paw with an air of tremendous patience, as though the fate of the world were an appointment he had been kept waiting for.

"You look terrible," Nettle said.

"You look like a fox," Aldric said.

"Yes." The fox stopped grooming. Something moved behind his amber eyes — the thing that was not a fox at all, the courtier who had been changed for treachery, the name that had been sold and bought back in one long night. "Yes, I rather do."

Aldric sat down on the road across from the stone. His legs were grateful. "I saw the broker's price get paid. I saw what you gave."

"You saw me give a *name*," Nettle said, "which is not the same as seeing what it cost, and I'd thank you not to bring it up at dinner parties. If we ever attend dinner parties. Which we won't, because I am a fox, and foxes are not invited to dinner parties, they are *served* at them." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but his paw had gone still.

"The court's gone," Aldric said. "The Regent's gone. The old gods are loose in the spire's root. Your name is yours again. You could be human. You could walk into Corvane tomorrow and take back whatever you had — house, title, whatever a courtier of the deep court has. You could be *known* again."

The silence went on long enough that Aldric heard a wood pigeon somewhere up the slope, and the wind in the pines, and his own heartbeat, slow and stubborn.

"Do you know what I remember most about being human?" Nettle said at last, and his voice had lost its polish; underneath it was something older and wearier, a voice that had sat in the dark of the mountain for a very long time wearing a shape it hated. "I remember doors. Doors that opened when I knocked. Rooms I was welcome in. And I remember that every one of those doors opened because of the name, not the man. I was charming, I was clever, I was *useful* — but it was the name that opened the doors. Take the name away and what was left?" He looked down at his own paws, at the red fur and the black claws, at the body that had been his punishment and had become, somewhere in the long years, his own. "This is what was left. And do you know, knight, in two hundred years of being this, not one creature has ever opened a door for me because of what I was *called*. They open it because I sit by their fire and I make them laugh and I tell them the truth when it matters. That's not the name. That never was the name."

"So you stay a fox."

"I stay a fox." He said it the way a man says *I stay married*, or *I stay home* — a decision worn smooth with handling. "The name is mine. I'll keep it somewhere safe. But a name is a coat, Aldric, and I have spent two centuries learning that the body underneath was the honest one all along." He flicked an ear. "Besides. Someone has to keep an eye on the boy. Have you *met* the boy? They'd apologize their way into a dragon's mouth inside a season."

"They're not a boy," Aldric said. "They're older than they look. They paid more than any of us."

"Yes," Nettle said, and for once did not make a joke of it. The wind moved through the pines. "Yes, they did."

* * *

They found Ivo and Sera an hour before dusk, at a shrine.

It was a small one — a roadside thing, the kind that had dotted the free lands once, before Veyra's harvesters burned them for kindling. This one had been burned too; Aldric could see the old scorch on the stones. But someone had set it right again, recently, clumsily, with river mud and wildflowers jammed into the cracks, and on the flat altar-stone someone had left a bowl of water and a crust of bread, the old small offerings, the kind you give to gods too little to demand.

Ivo sat on the altar step with their hands open in their lap, empty. Sera stood behind them with a hand on their shoulder — a real hand, real flesh, the soul that had sung itself sane in a lantern for forty years now wearing a body the waking gods had made her out of morning and mercy and, Aldric suspected, a certain amount of spite. She looked like her grandmother. She looked like Ivo would look in thirty years. She was humming something under her breath, and the bread on the altar had a bite taken out of it, and a wren was sitting on the rim of the water bowl drinking, unafraid.

Ivo looked up as they came down the road. Aldric saw the moment they recognized him, and braced for it — for the hatred or the fear or the careful blankness that people wore around a man who had signed the order that emptied a village. He had earned all of it. He had a speech half-made in his throat, the one he had been building since the gate, the one that began *I was the one who* and deserved to end with a sword.

"You're hurt," Ivo said.

It was not the speech he had expected. "It's nothing."

"You held the gate." Ivo's voice was their own voice again — the beast-speech was gone from it, traded away in the spire's heart, and what remained was only a human voice, tired and plain and kind, and somehow that was worse to hear than any accusation. "Nettle told me. The whole length of it, alone."

"Nettle exaggerates."

"I never exaggerate," said Nettle, arriving. "I curate."

Aldric stood there in the road with his ruined speech and nowhere to put it, and Sera looked at him — looked *through* him, the way the freed soul-light had, in the hall — and said, quietly, "You were at the sister-village. The one like ours."

"I signed the order," Aldric said. The words came out of him like teeth. "I didn't swing the torch. I signed the paper. I've told myself for eleven years that's a difference."

"It isn't," Sera said.

"No," Aldric said. "It isn't."

She nodded, and did not forgive him, and did not condemn him either; she simply let the truth sit on the road between them like a stone, and walked past it to refill the water bowl, and that, Aldric thought, was more mercy than he had any right to, and harder to bear than a sentence would have been.

Ivo got up from the altar step. They looked at the little shrine, at the wren, at the bread and the water and the flowers in the cracks, and Aldric saw them reach for something — saw the old habit of it, the listening, the turning of the ear toward the small voices — and find nothing there. The wren said nothing, because to Ivo it could say nothing now. It was only a bird. Ivo's face did something very quiet and very controlled around that nothing, the way a hand closes around a wound.

"They'll need someone," Ivo said, after a while. "The small gods. The ones that are left. There's nowhere for them. There never was — that was the whole disease, wasn't it? Everything too big ate everything too small, and the small ones learned to hide, and hiding is how you drown in your own well." They looked down the road, west, toward the valley — toward Hollowmere, unwarded now, the valley that could no longer hide anyone. "I'm going home. Sera's coming. There's a loom-house with an empty room in it, and a hillside that used to be a shrine before my grandmother's grandmother forgot it."

"And you want an old knight with a broken banner," Aldric said slowly, "to come along."

"I want someone who knows how to hold a gate," Ivo said. "Because I'm going to spend the rest of my life building doors that open, and the world being what it is, sooner or later something is going to come up the road wanting them closed."

The wren flew up out of the bowl and sat on Ivo's knee and said nothing, and Ivo looked at it with love and loss in the same breath, and Aldric found, to his considerable surprise, that the answer was already out of his mouth.

"Yes," he said. "All right. Yes."

Nettle made a sound that in a human would have been a laugh and in a fox was a sort of delighted sneeze. "Well," he said, trotting to the head of the road and sitting down facing west, tail curled around his feet like a signature on a contract. "Four of us, then. A beast-speaker who can't speak to beasts, a soul with a secondhand body, a knight with no banner, and a fox who is exactly what he appears to be, for the first time in two hundred years." He looked back over his shoulder, amber eyes catching the last of the light. "The free cities are going to *hate* us."

Behind them, far up the darkening slopes, the mountain breathed out a long plume of ash — and in it, for half a heartbeat, Aldric could have sworn he saw the ash turn against the wind, curling westward, following the road. Following *them*.

He decided he was too tired to be haunted tonight.

He picked up his sword, and they walked west together, into the dusk, toward the valley that had forgotten how to hide.
