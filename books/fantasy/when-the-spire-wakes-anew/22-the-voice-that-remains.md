## 22. The Voice That Remains

The first stone they set was the one Ivo had carried down from the mountain.

It was not a large stone — a slab of grey granite no bigger than a loaf of bread, its face worn smooth by centuries of wind that had blown through a dead god's throat. But it had been part of the spire once, before the waking, before the pouring-forth, and when Ivo pressed it into the wet mortar at the corner of the rebuilt shrine's foundation, they felt nothing at all. No hum. No whisper. No old and patient attention turning toward them like an eye.

Just stone. Cold under their palms. Heavy in the ordinary way that stones are heavy.

Ivo knelt there a long time with their hands flat on it anyway, the way you might press your ear to a door long after the house behind it has gone dark, listening for footsteps out of habit.

Nothing came. And that, they were learning, could be a kind of grace.

* * *

Winter passed the way winters do in Hollowmere now — loudly, at first, and then more quietly than anyone remembered winters being.

There was no ward to muffle things. That was the difference everyone felt and nobody quite knew how to name. Under the old wardlight, snowfall had been a hush, a held breath; the valley had seemed to listen to itself. Now the wind came over the ridge and down the slopes without softening, rattled the shutters, worried the eaves, and sang in the chimneys all night like something with a voice and opinions. The river spoke openly. The crows argued on the fences at dawn about matters of crow importance, at full volume, unashamed.

Some people hated it. The Harrow family packed their cart in the third week of thaw and took the south road toward Corvane, and Tam Harrow, who had been Ivo's friend since they were both small enough to fit under the same fence, stood in his yard a while before he left and said only, "It's too awake here now, Ivo." Ivo had nodded. There wasn't anything else to say. The valley that had spent generations forgetting how to be alive was alive again, and aliveness is not comfortable for everyone.

But others stayed. Old Marta Bracken, who had once reported her neighbor's hen for "sounding unnatural," now sat on her step every evening talking to it, and swore the hen gave better counsel than her late husband. Children who had grown up believing magic was a story told to frighten them into obedience went barefoot through the new grass after the rain, holding their hands out to feel whatever moved in it. One of them — the miller's youngest, a girl named Pip with a gap in her teeth and mud to her knees — announced to anyone who would listen that she was going to learn beast-speech when she grew up.

"You can't just learn it," Sera told her gently, weeding the garden rows she'd cut into the loom-house foundations.

"Why not?"

"Because—" Sera glanced at Ivo, standing by the fence, and something passed between them that was not grief exactly anymore, or not only grief. "Because it isn't learned. It's given. Or it isn't."

Pip considered this with the ferocious pragmatism of an eight-year-old. "Then how do you get it given?"

"You'd have to ask something very old," Ivo said, "and be willing to pay what it asks back."

Pip looked at them the way children look at adults who have said something true and inconvenient, then ran off to chase a frog, which Ivo thought was probably the wisest response available.

* * *

They worked on the shrine through all of spring.

It sat on the low hill above the ford, where the drowned god had waited so long in its dry well — for it had never been a well of water, Ivo understood now; it had been a well of prayer, and the god had drowned slowly in the silence of a warded valley where no one's prayers could reach anything. The original structure had been humble even before Veyra's soldiers cracked it: a circle of stones, a roofless ring, a floor of packed earth. Ivo rebuilt it the same way, but better. Aldric came up from the border town twice with a mule cart and stayed a week each time, hauling stone with the methodical endurance of a man who had finally found labor that asked nothing of him except his shoulders. His hair was going grey at the temples now. He still wore no banner. He had begun, tentatively, to smile.

"It's a strange thing you're building," he said one evening, sitting on the wall with bread and hard cheese, watching the light go gold across the water. "A church for gods nobody remembers."

"That's rather the point," Nettle said. He was lying along the top of the wall like a scarf someone had draped there, orange fur gone bright in the sunset, tail flicking in slow contentment. "The loud ones have their mountains and their spires. Somebody ought to keep a room where the quiet ones can sleep."

"And will they come? The quiet ones?"

"They're already coming." Nettle nodded toward the shrine, where — if you knew how to look, and Ivo no longer could, but Sera could, and had described it — faint shapes gathered sometimes in the dusk: small gods, minor presences, the spirits of springs and hedgerows and forgotten crossroads, drifting in from the wounded countryside like birds returning to a roost that had finally been repaired. "You built an inn, Aldric. Inns fill. It's what they do."

The first resident arrived in early summer: a god of beekeeping, or possibly of the specific patience required to stand near bees, no larger than a thimble and smelling faintly of smoke and honey. It settled into the niche above the doorway Ivo had carved for exactly this purpose and did nothing, and asked nothing, and was, as far as anyone could tell, deeply at rest.

Sera cried a little when she saw it. She did that more often these days — wept easily, at small kindnesses, at the sound of the river, at nothing — and Ivo understood why. She had spent sixty years as a lantern in a dark hall beneath the mountain, aware, singing herself sane, watching the world only through the cracks in her own captivity. Every ordinary thing now arrived in her hands like a gift she had stopped expecting to receive.

She had come back into flesh the way frost comes back onto glass — slowly, in patterns, beautiful and slightly strange. Her body was young, younger than Ivo's, because souls reborn take the age they were taken. She was learning to eat, to tire, to ache in the morning, and she did all of it with an appetite that made Ivo laugh. She ate bread like it was a love letter. She fell asleep mid-sentence and woke apologizing. She planted her garden over the loom-house foundations in rows straight enough to draw blood from a ruler, and every seed she put in the ground she patted down with both hands, as though tucking in a child.

"Grandmother's loom stood right there," she said once, kneeling in the turned earth where the hearth had been. She didn't say it with bitterness. That was the strangest thing — how little bitterness she carried, after everything. "I used to watch her weave, when I was very small. Before the men came from the mountain with their paper and their ink. I remember thinking her hands were so clever. I didn't know what they were clever at."

"Do you forgive her?" Ivo asked. They had never dared ask before.

Sera was quiet a while, working compost into the row with her fingers, black soil under her nails.

"She traded me for a town," she said at last. "And the town lived. Everyone I would have loved grew up in it, married in it, buried their dead in it. You grew up in it." She looked up, and the sun was behind her, and Ivo had to squint. "I don't know if that's forgiveness, Ivo. But I know I'm tired of carrying it heavier than she did. She carried it sixty years alone in that house. At least I had company in my dark. She was alone in hers." She went back to the soil. "Plant beans with me. Beans forgive everything."

So Ivo knelt in the ruins of the house where their grandmother had woven a daughter's soul into a contract, and planted beans.

* * *

The goats never came back to them.

That was the truth of it, and Ivo had stopped dressing it up. After the mountain, after the last command — *rise*, spoken in a voice that was heard by every living thing within the stone, and obeyed, and never spoken again — the gift was gone. Not muted. Not sleeping. Gone, the way a tooth is gone from the jaw: you kept reaching for it with your tongue, finding only absence shaped like presence.

The goats watched Ivo from the paddock with flat, polite animal eyes, the eyes they gave any stranger. When Ivo called them, they came, because goats come for food regardless of who holds the bucket, but there was no conversation in it anymore. There had been conversation for twenty-two years — complaints and gossip, warnings about weather, the endless philosophical grievances of a creature whose entire life consists of wanting things it cannot reach. Ivo had not realized until it was gone how much of their inner life had been conducted in goat.

The crows were harder. Crows remembered. The ones on the fence lines had known them, had traded words with them since childhood, and for the first weeks after the waking they would land near Ivo on the wall and tilt their heads and make sounds — ordinary crow sounds, caws and rattles — but with a quality of waiting, of *your turn*. As though Ivo had simply fallen silent in a conversation and they were being patient about it.

Ivo could not explain to them what had happened. That was the whole wound of it: the one audience that would have understood was the audience they could no longer address.

They learned, eventually, to speak to the animals the way everyone else did — aloud, in human words, knowing it fell on ears that caught only tone. "Good morning," to the goats. "Mind the fox," to the hens. It was absurd. It was also, in some stubborn way, an act of faith: speaking to creatures who could not answer, trusting that being heard was not the same as being understood, and that love did not require translation.

Nettle helped more than he would ever admit to. On bad days — and there were bad days, evenings when the silence in Ivo's head got so loud they had to walk out into the fields just to be somewhere the quiet couldn't corner them — Nettle appeared, trotting at their heel through the dusk, and talked. Endlessly, shamelessly, about everything: court gossip two hundred years stale, the scandalous private lives of hedgehogs, his ongoing feud with a heron at the ford whom he described as "a liar with wings." He filled the silence with noise so Ivo wouldn't have to live inside it alone.

"You gave up your name once," Ivo said to him once, in the deep of summer, sitting on the hill above the shrine. "Your true name, to cover my debt. And then the waking gave it back, and you chose to stay a fox anyway. Why?"

Nettle was quiet for a moment, which for Nettle was nearly unprecedented, like the sun pausing at noon.

"When I was a courtier," he said, "I had a name that opened doors. Every door in the mountain, and most doors outside it. Do you know what I did with it? I schemed. I climbed. I collected secrets the way other men collect coins, and none of it ever once made me happy, and when they transformed me — when they took the name away — do you know the first thing I felt?"

"What?"

"Relief." He licked a paw, fastidiously. "A fox wants what a fox wants. Warm sun, a full belly, one or two people worth following. It turns out that's the whole secret, and I needed to lose a name to find it." He looked at Ivo sidelong, green-gold eyes catching the last light. "You lost a gift and gained the same lesson. We're a matched pair, Amberdale. Two creatures who paid everything and ended up owning exactly enough."

* * *

Autumn came, and the garden came in heavy.

Beans beyond counting, squash fat as skulls, late cabbages wrapped tight as secrets. Sera walked the rows every morning with dirt permanently under her nails and an expression of profound satisfaction, and the neighbors came — hesitantly at first, then freely — to trade and talk and stare, a little, at the woman who had been a lantern, and to leave offerings of flour and honey at the shrine on the hill, because word travels in a valley, however long it takes, and the valley had decided that the Amberdales' strange hilltop church was worth feeding.

On the evening the story ends, Ivo was up at the shrine alone, setting the last capstone on the eastern wall.

The work had gone on longer than building strictly required, and Ivo knew why and let it be. Building was a thing hands could do instead of voices. Every stone laid was a sentence in a language Ivo still spoke fluently: the language of making something safe. The shrine was finished now, truly finished — circular, roofed with slate, warm inside even in October, with niches for nine small gods and eight of them occupied, and a central hearth where a fire burned that never quite needed tending, because the god of hearths had moved in uninvited and considered the arrangement mutual hospitality.

Ivo set the capstone, wiped the mortar from their hands, and stood in the doorway looking out at the valley.

Hollowmere in autumn: smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys, the river running loud and silver, the hills wearing rust and gold. Somewhere below, Sera was bringing in the last of the squash. Somewhere beyond the ridge, the world went on being wide and dangerous and awake — Veyra's ashes scattered, the free cities rebuilding, refugees walking home along roads that no longer had nametakers on them. The drowned god's dream-map had burned away months ago, having delivered them exactly as far as it promised, and Ivo found they didn't miss it. Maps are for people who don't yet know where they live.

They sat down on the doorstep of the shrine, in the last of the light.

And a crow landed on the wall.

It was one of the old ones — Ivo recognized the notch in its left wing, an old injury from a hawk, tended years ago by a child who could speak crow and had splinted the wing with a twig and thread. The crow regarded Ivo for a long moment, head tilting, black eye catching the sunset.

Then it said nothing.

No words no bird should know. No warning of waking spires. Just crow-sounds, ordinary and untranslatable, and a hop closer along the wall, and then — this was the thing Ivo would carry for the rest of their life — it settled its wings, fluffed against the cooling air, and stayed. Not waiting for an answer. Not keeping a place open in a conversation. Just staying, the way a companion stays, in the simple animal present tense where all of this had begun.

Ivo reached out slowly, and the crow permitted, once, the touch of a human finger to the feathers of its head.

"I don't know what you're saying," Ivo told it softly. "But I think I understand you anyway."

The crow rattled something noncommittal and began preening, and Ivo sat on the doorstep of the house they had built for homeless gods, in the valley that could hide no one and no longer needed to, and listened to the wind move through a world that answered to no contract — and found, at last, at the bottom of everything, that being only human was not a loss.

It was a room. And they had furnished it themselves.
