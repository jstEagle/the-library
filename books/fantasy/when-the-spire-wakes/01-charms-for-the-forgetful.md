## 1. Charms for the Forgetful

The market city of Vell woke the way it always did: in smoke and argument, with gulls screaming over the fish stalls and the harbor bells tolling the hour badly. Ada Nakamura was awake before all of it, as she was every morning, because the dead did not sleep, and neither, truly, did she.

She sat cross-legged on the floor of her workshop above the chandler's shop on Tanner's Row, her tray of bone charms spread before her like a field of small pale moons. Each one had been carved the night before — rib-bone slivers from the knacker's yard, boiled clean, polished smooth, then worked with her finest knife into shapes that meant nothing to anyone but her: interlocking circles, a ladder of tiny rungs, a bird mid-flight with its wings folded wrong. To her customers they were trinkets. Luck-pieces. Tokens against bad dreams and drowned nets.

They were not trinkets. Ada had known that since she was seven years old, when she'd picked up her grandmother's half-finished carving and heard, clear as a voice through a door, *don't forget the north wall, girl.* She had never learned whose voice it was, or what north wall, or why. She only knew that bone held sound the way a well holds water, and that if she pressed a charm to her ear at night, in the dark, when the city's noise drained away, she could hear them — the whisperers. The dead of Vell and everywhere else, murmuring their small unfinished business through whatever piece of the past she happened to be holding.

She told no one. In Vell, hearing voices was the fastest road to the Ministry's gray coaches, and the Ministry had been sniffing around the low country for months now, asking after "resonant objects" in their clipped, paper-smelling way. So Ada sold her charms with a smile and a lie — *just bone, madam, just luck* — and kept the true ones, the loud ones, locked in a box beneath her floorboards where their whispering kept her company like a second heartbeat.

By midmorning her stall in Copperlane Market was up, its striped awning sagging against a wind that smelled of tar and coming rain. The market swallowed her whole, as markets do: haggling fishwives, a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking, children darting between cart wheels with stolen plums. Ada liked the noise. It made it easier not to listen.

"Charms against forgetting," she called, because that was what she always called, a joke she'd invented years ago and never examined too closely. "Charms so you don't lose what matters."

An old woman stopped at her stall.

Ada noticed her the way you notice weather changing — a pressure behind the eyes, a sudden hush in one ear as though someone had cupped a hand over it. The woman was small and bent as a question mark, wrapped in a gray shawl gone soft with age, and she stood looking at the tray of charms with an expression Ada had never seen on a customer's face before. Not greed. Not idle curiosity. Grief. A grief so old it had worn smooth, like a river stone.

"You're the carver," the woman said. Her voice was dry leaves and thread. "The one who works in bone."

"I am." Ada slid the tray forward. "Something for luck? For remembering? I've a charm here shaped like a knot — good against losing things—"

"The loud one," the woman said quietly. "Give me the loud one."

Ada's hands went still above the tray.

There were eleven charms laid out. Ten were quiet — new-carved, empty, waiting for nothing. The eleventh, tucked at the back where she'd half-hidden it without quite deciding to, hummed. She could feel it humming through the wood of the stall, through the bones of her wrist. A charm she'd carved three nights ago from a strange piece of bone she'd found washed up on the mudflats east of the city — dense, yellowed, older than any knacker's beast, older maybe than the city itself. All night while she carved it, the whispers had poured out of it in a language she didn't know but somehow understood anyway, the way you understand weeping.

"I don't know what you mean," Ada said.

The old woman looked up. Her eyes were milk-pale, clouded with cataracts or age or something else, and yet they fixed on Ada with terrible precision, as though they saw straight through the awning, the market, the sky itself, to something far away.

"You hear them," she said. "Don't insult us both, girl. You hear them, and you carve what they tell you, and you don't know why. I know. I've walked a long way to find the last one who does."

The market roared on around them — a man selling eels, two porters cursing over a dropped crate — and none of it seemed to reach the little space in front of Ada's stall, which had gone quiet and close as a chapel.

"Who are you?" Ada whispered.

"A customer." The woman fumbled in her shawl and produced coins — real silver, worn nearly blank by other hands, other decades. She set them down with the care of someone laying flowers on a grave. "And a dying woman, which makes me honest. Give me the loud one, and I'll tell you a name, and you'll wish to God I hadn't."

Ada should have refused. Every instinct honed by years of hiding told her to refuse — to laugh, to call it luck-piece nonsense, to sell the woman a knot-charm and send her on. But her hand moved without her, drew out the humming charm, and laid it in the old woman's palm.

The whispers surged. Ada felt them even from arm's length, a rising tide of voices all saying one word, over and over, in that language-that-wasn't-a-language: *remember, remember, remember.*

The old woman closed her fingers around the charm and bowed her head over it, and for a moment she looked less like a beggar and more like a queen holding a crown she'd renounced. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — steadier, sadder, carrying distance in it like the smell of rain.

"There was a country called Kestrelmark," she said. "North and east of here, past the Salt Fells, where the rivers run backward toward the mountains. It had a Spire at its heart, taller than anything your kings built, and wards around its borders sung by wardens, and stars over it brighter than any you've ever slept under. Five years ago it went away. Not destroyed. *Erased.* And every soul who escaped it forgot it by morning, except those of us who paid not to." She lifted her clouded eyes. "I bought my forgetting with everything I had, and it didn't take. That's what dying does — it pries the doors open again. Now I remember all of it, and there is no one left alive I can tell."

"I've never heard of any Kestrelmark," Ada said. Her mouth was dry. "There's nothing past the Salt Fells. Just the wastes. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone *knows* that." The woman smiled, and it was the worst thing Ada had ever seen. "That's how thorough they were." She pressed the charm back across the stall, into Ada's hand, folding her fingers over it. The bone was warm. It had never been warm before. "Keep it. Carve more like it. Because the wards are failing, girl — five years late, but failing — and when they go, the world is going to need someone who can hear the dead of Kestrelmark. Someone with warden's blood, though she doesn't know it yet."

"I'm not—" Ada began.

"*Remember Kestrelmark,*" the old woman said, and now her voice cracked down the middle, and Ada understood she wasn't speaking to her anymore, or not only to her; she was speaking to the air, to the past, to everyone and no one. "*Remember Kestrelmark.* Promise me. Promise me, whoever you are. Someone has to carry the name until it can come home."

And then the woman turned and walked into the crowd, gray shawl fading between bright stalls like a candle guttering, and Ada stood with a warm bone charm in her fist and a name in her ears that fit nowhere in the world she knew.

Kestrelmark.

She looked it up that evening, as much as one could look anything up in Vell. The map-seller on Cordwainer Street had charts going back two hundred years, and she turned through all of them, and there was nothing north and east of the Salt Fells but blank vellum and, on the oldest one, a faded ink stain roughly the shape of a country, scraped at by some long-dead hand that had tried to remove it and given up.

*Just an ink blot*, she told herself, walking home through streets gone blue with dusk. *Old women ramble. Bone charms are bone charms. You sell them for three coppers apiece and the whispers are just echoes, just—*

The first star went out while she was climbing her stairs.

She felt it rather than saw it, a faint cold snap somewhere behind her ribs, like a string parting in her chest. Then another. Then, through the garret window over Tanner's Row, she watched the evening sky begin to die — star by star by star, not falling, not dimming, simply *unhappening*, the way a word fades when you say it over and over until it's nothing but noise in your mouth.

Ada dropped the bucket she was carrying. Water spread across the floorboards and she barely saw it. She was at the window, both hands pressed flat against the frame, watching the constellations she'd known since childhood go dark in ragged rows, and the whispers in the charm on her table rose from murmur to chorus to a single vast cry, hundreds of voices braided into one:

*Kestrelmark. Kestrelmark. Remember Kestrelmark.*

And then the horizon to the north and east lit up.

Not with fire — with light, cold and white and structured, great sheets of it standing off the distant hills like curtains of frozen lightning. Ada watched, heart slamming, as the light climbed and spread and took on shape, and she realized with a lurch that she was seeing walls. Wards. Enormous, ancient, woven out of light, ringing a stretch of empty country she had never once thought about in her life — and they were shattering.

They broke the way glass breaks when it has been slowly cooling for years and finally gives: first a fine spiderwebbing crack that glowed along the whole northern sky, then a silence so total that even the harbor bells seemed to hold their breath, and then the entire invisible country came apart at once, sheet after sheet of light collapsing inward like snow falling up, and where each pane fell, the darkness behind it was not empty.

It was full of stars. Thousands upon thousands of them, blazing back to life in a black field where no stars should be, a whole sky's worth of sky pouring back into the world through the cracks in the broken wards — and beneath that returning sky, faint and far and unmistakable, a silhouette on the horizon that had not been there at sunset. Tall. Slender. Impossibly high.

A spire.

Ada's legs gave out and she sat down hard on her kitchen floor among the spilled water, clutching the warm charm to her chest, laughing and crying in the same breath because it was beautiful, it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was the most terrifying, because she could hear the dead singing now without touching the bone at all, filling the room, filling her skull, and every one of their voices was saying the same thing, urgent and joyful and afraid:

*The door is opening. The door is opening. Warden — the door is opening —*

Below her window, in the street, dogs began to howl. Somewhere across the city a church bell started ringing wildly, out of turn, and was joined by another, and another. And from the direction of the Ministry offices on the hill, where the lamps burned late every night, came the thin, rising whine of a signal horn being blown for the first time in living memory.

Ada Nakamura stayed on her floor a long time, holding a dead woman's charm, listening to a vanished country call her by a title she did not understand.

Then she got up, found her carving knife, and began, with shaking hands, to cut new bone.
