## 2. The Country That Wasn't

Ada woke to a city that had forgotten how to be itself.

She knew it first by the silence. Vell was never silent — not at dawn, not in the dead hours before it. The market city breathed all night: fishwives arguing with gulls, cart wheels groaning over cobbles worn smooth by three centuries of trade, the bells of the Salt Cathedral tolling the hour even when no one listened. But this morning there was only wind moving through streets that seemed, somehow, unsure of their own shape.

Ada stood in her doorway above the workshop and looked out at Copperlane Row, and her stomach dropped slowly, the way it does in dreams when the stairs keep going.

The row was still there. The buildings were still there — the dyer's house with its streaked blue walls, the cooper's yard stacked with hoops, the crooked sign of the Gilded Eel swinging on its bracket. But between the cooper's yard and the Eel, where the alley should have run down to the Tanner's Stair, there was nothing. Not a wall. Not rubble. Nothing — a seam in the world, smooth as the surface of a closed eye. Her gaze slid off it every time she tried to hold it, like water off oiled cloth.

And the people. Old Sefa the dyer stood in her doorway in her nightdress, holding a child's shoe, turning it over and over in her hands with an expression of polite bafflement.

"Whose is this?" Sefa asked the air, and then, catching sight of Ada, asked her instead. "Whose is this? It was by my bed. Why would I have a child's shoe?"

"Sefa," Ada said carefully, "that's Pip's. Your grandson. He sleeps in the room under yours."

Something moved behind Sefa's eyes — a fish under ice, a shadow passing beneath a frozen pond. "I don't—" She looked at the shoe. Looked at her hands. "I live alone," she said, with the gentle certainty of someone reciting something true. "I've always lived alone."

She went back inside and closed the door, and through the window Ada watched her set the little shoe neatly on the mantelpiece, beside a row of other small objects that she clearly could not explain but had not been able to make herself throw away.

The dead began to whisper.

They were always there — that was Ada's secret, the thing she'd carried since she was nine years old, selling charms made of chicken bones and river-clay to customers who wanted luck or love or an easy death. The dead murmured through bone the way heat moves through iron; she'd learned to let it wash past her, half-heard, like rain on a roof. But this morning the whispers came up through the floorboards, through the mortar of the walls, through the very cobblestones, and they were not murmuring.

They were shouting.

*Warden.* The word arrived in twenty voices at once, layered like a choir singing slightly out of unison. *Warden. Warden of Kestrelmark. You are seen. You are known. Wake.*

"Stop." Ada pressed her palms over her ears, which did nothing at all, because the voices weren't coming through her ears. They were coming up through the charm cord at her wrist, through the half-finished carvings on her bench, through the drawer of blanks waiting for her knife. Every piece of bone in the workshop had become a mouth. *Wake, warden.*

"I'm not a warden," she said aloud, to an empty room. "I'm a charm-seller. My mother sold charms. Her mother sold charms. There's no such place as Kestrelmark."

*There is,* said the dead, gently now, the way you'd correct a child who has insisted the moon is made of salt. *There was. There is again, if you choose it. Five years ago it was folded away. Five years ago the wards were sung shut and the stars above it were put out, and the world agreed — because we made the world agree — to forget.*

"But that's mad." Ada heard her own voice crack on the word. "People don't forget countries. Maps don't—"

She stopped. Because she was thinking about maps, and about how her mother had kept one rolled in a cedar tube above the hearth, an old survey map with a border drawn along the Ashen River and a name inked in faded brown where the highlands rose northward — a name that had been scraped away so thoroughly the parchment was thin as onion skin there, translucent, holding nothing.

Her mother had never once said what that name was. And Ada had never once thought to ask. Five years ago. Her mother had died five years ago, and Ada had never wondered why the map had a hole in it, why her mother had touched that hole sometimes at night, why she'd taught Ada the warding-cuts with names that sounded like no language Ada had ever heard anywhere else.

*You were born warden,* the dead said. *You were marked at your naming. We hid you inside an ordinary life because the Ministry was hunting us, and ordinary lives are the best camouflage there is. Forgive us. We meant to tell you when you were ready.*

"When I was—" Ada laughed, a short, ugly sound. "When exactly did you plan to mention it?"

But the dead had gone quiet again, or quieter, settling into her bones like silt into riverbed, and outside the window the strangeness was spreading.

She saw it happen to the cooper. Tammas Broad, who had shouted good morning to her for eleven years, stopped mid-stride in his own yard and stared at his stacks of staves as though he had never seen wood before, as though he had to work out from first principles what a barrel might be for. His wife found him there and took his arm, and neither of them could remember which house was theirs until they walked to it and recognized it the way you recognize a stranger wearing a friend's coat.

Down by the Tanner's Stair, a woman was screaming — a raw, animal sound that brought people running and then left them standing in a helpless ring, because none of them, including the woman, could say what was wrong. Her son was gone, she kept saying. Her boy, he'd been right here, he'd been holding her hand — and then the crowd looked at each other, and someone said kindly, there's no boy, love, there's never been a boy, and the woman's face did something terrible: it believed them. Ada watched the belief take her like frost takes a window. The screaming stopped. The woman thanked them. She walked away lighter, smiling, and Ada had to grip a doorpost not to be sick, because she understood what she had just seen.

The country wasn't being destroyed. It was being *unremembered*, person by person, street by street, and forgetting felt like peace.

That was the horror of it. That was the trap. If she let go — if she stopped gripping the names, Pip, Tammas, the Tanner's Stair, the Salt Cathedral, Vell — wouldn't it feel better? Wasn't some part of her already leaning toward that soft white silence?

"No," Ada said through her teeth, to herself, to the dead, to whatever was eating the world one memory at a time. "No. I remember. I'll damn well remember for everyone."

*Warden,* whispered the bones, and this time it sounded almost proud.

* * *

They came at noon, and they came quietly, which told Ada everything before she ever saw their faces.

Three of them, walking up Copperlane Row against the flow of a dazed, drifting crowd. They wore grey — not uniform grey, but the grey of men who had chosen clothes specifically to be forgotten the moment you looked away, and it worked; Ada found that her eyes kept sliding off them, that she had to deliberately re-count them each time, *one two three*, like keeping time. The tall one carried a case of black lacquered wood. The woman in front held a small brass instrument like a tuning fork crossed with a compass, and its needle swung as she walked, lazy and aimless, until she reached the foot of Ada's stairs.

Then the needle snapped upright and quivered, pointing at the workshop door like a hound's nose.

"Resonance," the woman said. Not loudly. The tall one nodded and opened his case, and inside, packed in grey wool, lay objects that made every dead voice in Ada's body fall suddenly, utterly silent — and the silence was worse than the shouting. Bone whistles. Iron bells. A mask of birch bark with the eyeholes stitched shut. Things that had once belonged to wardens, perhaps, or to the people who loved them. Confiscated. Collected. Muted.

The dead didn't fear many things. Ada felt them fear this.

She blew out her lamp, swept her finished charms into the hem of her apron, and slipped down the back stair into the cellar as the knock came above her — three measured raps, courteous as a tax collector.

"Madam Nakamura." The woman's voice filtered through the floorboards, pleasant and toneless. "Ministry of Records Reconciliation. We're conducting a survey of heirloom objects in the district. May we come in?"

There was no Ministry of Records Reconciliation. There never had been. Ada crouched among her stores of river-clay and bundled bones and realized she was breathing through her mouth, slow and shallow, listening to her own footsteps above her head as the agents moved through her workshop. A drawer opened. Another. The woman's voice drifted down, mild as milk: "These carvings. Interesting technique. Who taught you these cuts?"

"My mother," Ada heard herself answer from upstairs — and realized with a jolt that she hadn't answered; the woman was speaking to empty air, rehearsing, or perhaps asking questions of a workshop the way you'd question a witness. Learning her already. Building a file around a person who had, until this morning, successfully been nobody.

The brass needle hummed. Ada felt it humming, felt it pluck at the charms hidden against her stomach like fingers plucking harp strings, and she understood with cold clarity that she had perhaps minutes.

*Back stair,* whispered the dead. *Tanner's Stair. The river gate. Go east, warden. East toward the Spire.*

"I don't know where the Spire is," Ada breathed, gathering her knife and cord and the bundle of charms, clutching them like contraband, which, apparently, they were.

*Follow what remembers,* said the dead. *The country will show you its bones if you ask. And warden — you are followed already. One comes behind you. A thief. Do not trust him.*

"Do not trust—" Ada was halfway up the far stair, out into the blinding noon, into a city dissolving gently around her like sugar in warm water. "Which part? All of it? Give me something to work with!"

The dead, with the maddening serenity of those beyond consequence, offered only: *He is sorry. It may matter. It may not.*

Behind her, the black-lacquered case clicked shut. Below her, the Tanner's Stair ran down toward a river that still remembered flowing. Ahead of her, east, somewhere past the ash-fields and the erased villages, something vast and sleeping waited beneath a sky with holes where stars used to be.

Ada pulled her shawl over the hidden charms, fixed her eyes on the seam in the world where Kestrelmark's border should have shone, and ran.
