## 3. A Thief with Good Timing

Ada did not sleep that night. She sat at her workbench with every lamp in the workshop burning, the dead murmuring through the bone at her throat like wind through a keyhole, and she watched the sky through her one cracked window where no stars had been.

The city below had gone quiet in a way cities never went quiet. No dogs. No drunk singing from the Tanner's Row. Just the sound of Vell breathing shallowly, like a man trying not to wake something.

*You felt it,* the dead said. *You felt the door close.*

"I don't even know where the door is," Ada whispered.

But she did, in the way she knew the shape of her own hands. Somewhere east, past the hills everyone swore were only hills, something vast was holding its breath.

She heard the window latch give at the hour when even the dead dozed.

Not a smash — a whisper of glass cut with a diamond, a sliver of frame lifted just so. Professional. Ada closed her hand around the carving knife on her bench and did not turn around.

"There's nothing worth taking," she said to the dark.

"There's everything worth taking." The voice was low, roughened, amused in the way of a man who had learned that charm was cheaper than violence. "Forty-one bone charms, give or take. Warden-grade resonance, if my buyer's honest, which he isn't, but his coin is."

Ada turned. A figure crouched on her sill, silhouetted against the starless sky — lean, quick, dressed in the muted greys of someone who made his living being overlooked. He had a satchel already half-full of her stock from the display case by the door, and he moved toward her workbench like a man who knew exactly what he wanted.

She put the knife point-down in the bench between them. The sound of it was very loud.

"That's mine," she said.

"And it's about to be mine, so we're both right." He reached for the tray of charms she'd carved that evening — and stopped. His hand hung in the air over them as if over a stove. Slowly, he pulled back his sleeve and looked at his forearm, where a faint red line had risen, like a burn in the shape of a warning.

"You warded them," he said, and there was something almost wounded in it. "A charm-seller. You warded your charms."

"They're picky about who touches them."

"Clearly." He flexed his fingers, shaking off the sting, and looked up at her for the first time properly. He was younger than his voice suggested — thirty, maybe, with a smuggler's weathering already settled into the corners of his eyes, and a scar through one eyebrow that had been stitched badly by someone in a hurry. "Well. This is embarrassing. Usually I'm gone before anyone's holding a knife."

"The wards woke me."

"No," he said slowly, glancing at the window, at the empty sky beyond it. "Nothing wakes anybody anymore. Everyone in this city is lying awake staring at the ceiling since midnight. Whatever fell out of the sky tonight, it didn't leave room for sleep." He straightened, weighing his options, and Ada watched him decide — visibly, like watching a scale tip — that robbing her wasn't worth the knife. "Renn," he said, and gave a little bow that managed to be mocking and sincere at once. "Smuggler. Salvager. Occasional thief with good timing, though tonight the timing appears to be terrible."

"Ada." She didn't move the knife. "Give back the bag."

He looked down at the satchel of charms. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, unhappy sound — and set it on the bench between them like a man laying down cards he couldn't play.

"You know what's funny? I came here because of what happened tonight. My buyer sent word an hour ago: double price for resonant bone, all of it, tonight, before the Ministry seals the gates. And I thought, easy money, I know exactly where forty-one pieces of warden-grade bone are sleeping." He rubbed the red mark on his arm. "I didn't know they'd object."

"The Ministry?" Ada's mouth had gone dry. Since midnight the dead had been saying the word over and over, soft and terrible, like a tide. *They are coming. They are coming. They remember us.* "Why would the Ministry care about trinkets?"

Renn went still. It was a particular kind of stillness, the kind she'd seen in animals and in guilty men — the whole body agreeing at once to be smaller.

"They're not trinkets," he said.

*Tell him what you are,* the dead breathed against her collarbone. *No. Not yet. First make him tell you what he did.*

"Sit down," Ada said.

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down, Renn the smuggler, or I'll find out what else these wards can do."

He sat. He perched on her stool across the workbench like a man settling onto a gallows, and for a long moment he studied the charms laid out in their velvet grooves — small carved bones, each one etched with patterns Ada had copied all her life without understanding, patterns the dead had finally begun explaining to her only two days ago.

"My family sold salvage," Renn said at last, quietly, all the performance gone out of him. "Down from the eastern hills. Five years ago there was a rush — ruins nobody remembered existing, suddenly full of old things. Bone, stone, metal worked in ways no smith could name. Everyone made money. Nobody asked why the ruins hadn't been there before." He turned one of the charms over with a fingertip, careful now, the way you touch a relic. "I was nineteen. I found a cache in a collapsed vault under a hill that wasn't on any map. Carved pillars. Sheets of pale bone big as doors, covered in writing like this." He tapped the charm. "I sold it all to a broker in Vell. Good price. Best season of my life."

The dead had gone utterly silent. Even they, it seemed, wanted to hear this.

"Three years later," Renn went on, "the Ministry came through buying back everything with that pattern on it. Everything. They paid triple and burned the ledgers and asked no questions we were allowed to answer. That's when I learned the word for what I'd been digging up." He looked up at her, and his eyes in the lamplight were bleak. "Warden-work. Relics of Kestrelmark."

"Kestrelmark," Ada repeated. The name filled her mouth like cold water.

"You know it?"

"It's been whispered at me since yesterday by people who are dead." She kept her voice level. "Keep going."

"The pillars I sold — six of them, white as milk, humming if you put your ear against them. I always thought that was my imagination. Last month I met a man who'd seen the Ministry's inventory manifests, and he told me what got stamped on the crates from my cache." Renn's jaw worked. "*Spire components.* I didn't sell dishes and doorframes, Ada. I sold pieces of the thing itself. The heart of a whole country, and I stripped it out for drinking money, and five years ago that country stopped existing and nobody noticed, and I—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Nobody notices, because there's nothing left to notice with."

Ada stood very still. Through the charm at her throat the dead were weeping — not loudly, never loudly, but she could feel it now, the way she could feel weather coming. All those voices insisting she was a warden. All that grief with nowhere to pour itself.

And this man had carried the cargo of its destruction on his own back, young and grinning, and had only now learned what his shoulders had borne.

"Why tell me this?" she asked. "You broke into my shop to rob me."

"Because your charms bit me." He gave a hollow ghost of his earlier grin. "Wardens' work doesn't bite thieves unless the wardens want something. And because tonight the sky went black, and I know why, and I've known for years, and I have never once said it out loud until sitting in your chair." He pushed the satchel toward her. "Keep your charms. Keep your knife. Here's what I actually came to offer, now that the robbery's fallen through: I have a boat. River-berthed, fast, shallow draft, crewed by two idiots who owe me money. The Ministry is going to seal Vell by dawn and start house-to-house searches for anything that hums. Come morning, everyone holding resonant bone gets taken. So. Passage out. Tonight. South, west, anywhere that isn't here."

"And then what?"

"And then you're alive somewhere the Ministry isn't, which is the entire ambition of my offer."

"And Kestrelmark?" Ada asked softly. "What happens to Kestrelmark while we're being alive?"

Renn flinched as if she'd named a dead wife. "There is no Kestrelmark. That's the whole horror of it. There's nothing to save. You can't restore a country that every map and memory has agreed never existed."

"Something remembers," Ada said. She lifted the cord over her head and laid the dying woman's charm on the bench between them — the one bought with trembling hands by a grandmother whose last words had been *remember Kestrelmark*. The bone caught the lamplight and held it strangely, the way ice holds fire. "The dead remember. The wards remember. The Spire remembers — that's what you said your pillars were humming with, isn't it? Memory. It's all still in there, Renn. Erased isn't destroyed. Erased is just locked."

He stared at the charm. His hand rose halfway toward it and stopped, remembering the bite.

"And how would you know?" he asked hoarsely.

It would have been easy to lie. She had been lying about the whispers her whole life — smiling at customers, telling worried mothers the voices were drafts in the walls. But Renn had just handed her the worst thing he'd ever done, unprompted, unpurchased, because the weight of carrying it alone had finally exceeded the weight of shame. Mutual currency. She paid in kind.

"Because I'm a warden," Ada said. "The last one. The dead have been telling me for two days, and tonight, when the stars went out, I finally believed them."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere outside, boots — many boots, moving in formation — rang off cobbles three streets over. The Ministry, early. The dead stirred against her skin like birds feeling a storm.

Renn looked at her the way men look at lighthouses: hope and dread in the same expression.

"A boat won't reach the Spire," he said. Which meant: I'll do it anyway.

"No. But a smuggler knows the river roads the maps forgot. And a man who sold Spire pillars once might know where the rest of them went." She began gathering her charms into the satchel, swift and certain, hands steadier than she felt. "You want absolution, Renn? Fleeing south buys you nothing but distance. Take me east. Take me to what's left of the Spire, and we'll see together whether erased means forever."

He stood. Outside, the boots grew louder, and somewhere a whistle blew three sharp notes, and the city that had forgotten how to forget began, at last, to scream.

Renn shouldered his pack, looked at the starless window, and said, "East dock. Don't be seen. And Ada—" he paused at the sill he'd broken through, thief once more, "—if the dead ever mention a nineteen-year-old with a wheelbarrow full of white pillars, I'd rather not hear what they say."

"They say," Ada answered, slinging the satchel across her back and following him into the dark, "that you're going to carry them home."

* * *

The river fog swallowed the city behind them as the little boat slid north-east against the current, running dark, lamps hooded. Renn's two crewmen asked nothing; they were paid not to.

Ada sat in the bow and opened her hand. On her palm, the dead woman's charm had begun to glow — faintly, steadily, pointing east like a compass needle made of bone and memory.

Behind them, Vell's gates boomed shut. Ahead, beneath hills that were only hills, something vast waited to be remembered.

"Kestrelmark," Ada whispered to the fog, practicing the name.

The dead, for the first time in two days, whispered it back.
