## 4. The Ward-Singer's Silence

The river town of Brackwater had exactly one use for Maren Solenne, and that was none at all, which suited her fine.

She lived above the Drowned Eel, in a room that smelled of mildew and spilled gin, and she spent her evenings at the corner table where the lamplight couldn't quite reach her face. Three years in Brackwater and nobody knew her name — not the barkeep, not the ferry-wives, not the children who threw stones at her window when the mood took them. She had been careful to be nobody. Nobody was the safest thing a person could be.

She was drinking her second cup of the evening — she no longer called it morning, because mornings implied a day worth waking for — when the door opened and the cold came in wearing a young woman's coat.

Maren noticed her the way she noticed everything now: dully, from behind a gauze of drink. Dark-haired, travel-stained, with the wary set of someone who'd been walking too long through country that didn't want to be walked. The girl scanned the room the way travelers did, looking for exits, and then — this was unusual — she looked at Maren. Not past her. At her.

Then she crossed the floor and set something down on Maren's table with a click like a knuckle rapping wood.

A bone charm. Carved fine, the spiral-work crisp even in bad light, strung on a cord gone grey with handling. And threaded through the spiral, faint as breath on glass, a thread of song.

Maren's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Three years since the wards fell, and three years since she had heard anything at all — not the river-song under Brackwater's pilings, not the low hum of the earth's own turning, not one note of the great slow music that ward-singers were born hearing and died still hearing, if they were lucky. When the wards shattered she had felt it go out of her like a lamp snuffed between fingers, and what remained was silence, total and absolute, a silence so complete it made ordinary sound feel like shouting in an empty church. She drank to blur the edges of it. It never worked.

But the charm on her table sang. Thin and cracked and half-swallowed by distance, but singing — a woman's voice, old and frightened, caught in the bone like a fly in amber.

"Where," Maren said, and her voice came out ruined, "did you get this."

"From its owner." The girl sat down across from her uninvited. "She was dying. She bought it from me and then she gave it back, more or less, and told me to remember Kestrelmark."

The name went through Maren like a fishhook. She gripped the table's edge. Around them the Eel went on clinking and muttering; nobody else flinched, because nobody else remembered there was anything to flinch at. That was the shape of the world now. A hole in it, shaped exactly like a country, and only the dead and the drunk seemed to notice the draft coming through.

"You're the charm-seller," Maren said. "From Vell. They're saying there's a girl in Vell whose charms carry voices."

"People say things."

"They say the Ministry burned your workshop."

"My workshop is standing. My neighbors are the ones who aren't." The girl — Ada, she said her name was Ada Nakamura, though Maren hadn't asked — turned the charm over with one finger. "You heard it. Just now. I saw your face."

"I heard nothing." Maren reached for her cup. Her hand wasn't steady enough to lie well. "Whatever you think I am, I'm not. I'm nobody. Ask anyone here."

"That's exactly why I asked you." Ada leaned forward. Up close her eyes were the worst kind of eyes, the kind that had already decided something about you and were only waiting for you to catch up. "Because everyone in this town looks *through* you, and you've arranged your whole life around being looked through. I know the posture. I've been wearing it myself lately. Only ward-singers sit like that."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You sat facing the door, but your chair is angled toward the river. You've been listening to the water all night out of habit, and there's been nothing to hear for three years, and you can't stop checking anyway." Ada tapped the charm. "Sing to it."

"No."

"It has a dead woman inside it. Her name was Odile Vass. She spent five years forgetting her own daughter a little more every day, and right up until the end she kept asking me to remember Kestrelmark for her. Sing to it, and maybe she'll tell us both why."

"I said *no*." It came out louder than Maren intended. Two heads turned near the bar. She lowered her voice to a rasp. "Girl. Whatever you think I did or was — I can't sing anymore. There's nothing in me. The wards broke and took the music with them, and what's left is just—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the wet ring on the table, at the whole gray ruin of her evening— "*this*. So take your dead woman's trinket and find another listener, because this one's broken."

Ada didn't move. "I'm not asking you to sing wards. I'm asking you to come with me."

"To where?"

"Toward the Spire."

The word dropped into Maren's chest like a stone into a well, and she listened — out of three years' habit — for the splash. There was no splash. There was never any splash. Just the same bottomless quiet.

"There is no Spire," she said. "There's no Kestrelmark. There's a border that leads nowhere and maps with a smudge on them and a whole generation of children who've never heard the names their grandparents died keeping. I know, because I'm the one who sang the border shut. Me and eleven others, on the Archon's direct order, in the dark, before dawn, and we weren't told what we were singing and we weren't allowed to ask." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "So yes. I'm the disgraced ward-singer. Congratulations on finding me. Do you want the autograph, or would you rather skip straight to the part where you tell me what I deserve?"

For a long moment Ada said nothing. Then, quietly: "What do you think you deserve?"

"Everything they'd give me, if anyone could remember enough to give it." Maren stared into her cup. "I sang eleven hundred thousand people out of the world. I don't know why. I don't know if the Archon was mad or right or bought. I just — sang. Because that's what I was *for*, and I'd never once in my life asked what the song was for, and by the time I thought to ask, my own mother had forgotten my face." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that most of all. "I went home after. Stood in the doorway. She offered me a bed like I was a stranger off the road. Kind woman. Would've fed anyone. She just — didn't have a daughter anymore. I made sure of it personally."

The tavern noise swelled and receded around them like weather. Ada picked up the charm and held it out flat on her palm between them.

"Odile Vass's voice is in here," she said. "And last night, before the stars went out, she held my hand and made me promise. I don't intend to break that promise. But I can't hear what the dead are trying to say clearly — not alone, not yet — and every person alive who remembers how to listen is either dead, forgotten, or sitting in front of me drinking herself blind in a town called Brackwater." She pushed the charm an inch closer. "So. Come or don't. But first — hold it. Just hold it once. If you feel nothing, I'll leave you to your gin and your nobody, and you'll never see me again."

Maren should have refused. Every instinct honed over three years of hiding said *refuse, refuse, refuse* — but her hands had already moved, traitor hands, hands that had once coaxed whole harmonies out of standing stones, and they closed around the little carved bone.

The voice rose up through her palms like water through a wick.

*Maren.* Not Odile Vass — or not only Odile; the voices braided together, dozens, hundreds, the dead of a drowned country all crowding toward one narrow opening. *Maren, we remember you. We remember the singing. We don't blame you — we were there, we heard the order come down, we heard what they threatened — Maren, we need the song again. The fire is dying. The Spire sleeps and the fire is dying and if it gutters out nothing wakes, ever, nothing anywhere—*

Maren ripped her hands away as if the charm were iron fresh from the forge. The chair went over behind her. She stood in the middle of the Drowned Eel with every eye in the room on her, shaking, tears cutting hot tracks down a face that hadn't wept in three years because crying required feeling something and feeling something required the music—

"You felt it," Ada said. It wasn't a question.

"It hates me." Maren's whisper barely carried. "They hate me. They should."

"They came to you because they trust you." Ada stood too, unhurried, and righted Maren's fallen chair like it was the most natural thing in the world. "Do you understand? Five years erased, and the first thing they do when someone finally listens is look for the woman who sang their border — not to curse her. To ask for help. That's grace, Maren. I don't know what you think you deserve, but apparently the dead disagree."

Maren looked at the girl, at the charm, at the door beyond which lay the river and the road and the long dark smear on every map where a country used to be. The silence in her head yawned open, vast and familiar, and she understood suddenly — with a clarity the gin had never once given her — that she had not been hiding from guilt these three years.

She had been hiding from the quiet. Guilt was at least *hers*. The silence belonged to whatever had done this to the world, and it would swallow her whole unless somebody sang against it.

"If I come," she said hoarsely, "it's not for redemption. Redemption's a story people tell about you afterward. I'm coming because I cannot stand one more day of that silence. Understand? You're not saving me. You're giving me somewhere to put my hands."

"Fine," Ada said. "Put your hands on a horse. We ride tonight — Renn knows the fire roads north, and the Ministry's already hunting us, and every hour we stand still the fire burns lower." She paused at the edge of the table, and something flickered across her face that might have been fear, quickly mastered. "There's a fourth thing you should know. The dead keep saying a word when I sleep. Over and over. *Warden.* They say I'm the last warden of Kestrelmark, and that the Spire won't wake without one, and that the price of waking it is—"

She stopped. Outside, far off across the black fields, a light that was not a lantern swept along the ridge road — white, deliberate, sweeping side to side. Searching.

"—that the price is a name," Ada finished quietly. "Mine."

Maren picked up her cloak. Three years of nobody, folded over one arm, lighter than she expected.

"Then we'd better move fast," she said, "before the world finds out who you are. It has a habit of taking things."

* * *

They were two streets from the stables when the bells of Brackwater began to ring — not the harbor bell, not the chapel bell, but the old ward-bell on the moot-hill that hadn't sounded since before Maren was born, ringing wild and cracked and desperate, and beneath its clangor Maren heard, for the first time in three years, faint and far off and rising like floodwater:

singing.
