## 5. What the Dead Keep

The villages came apart the way old sweaters do — one pulled thread at a time.

Ada had expected ruins. What she found instead was worse: houses still standing, doors still on their hinges, gardens gone to seed in neat rows that no one had ever planted, because no one remembered planting them. Wells with buckets still tied off at the lip. A schoolhouse whose chalkboard bore half a lesson in a hand that had simply stopped mid-word, as if the teacher had stepped out to fetch water and never come back — and in every way that mattered, she hadn't. The country of Kestrelmark hadn't been burned or broken. It had been *unwritten*, and unwritten things did not rot. They just waited.

Renn walked ahead with his hood up and his hands jammed in his pockets, which was how he walked everywhere, but Ada had learned to read him anyway. When his shoulders climbed toward his ears, he was remembering something he'd sold. In the third village — a place called Ossery, though only the dead could say it now — his shoulders nearly touched his jaw.

"I passed through here," he said quietly. "Five years back. Bought wool. There was a woman who argued me down two coppers on a bolt of grey." He stared at an empty doorway as though the woman might still fill it. "I can't even remember her face. I remember the coppers."

"The dead remember her," Ada said.

Maren, walking behind them with her collar up against the wind, made a sound that was almost a laugh. "The dead remember everything. That's the trouble with being dead. No Ministry comes for *you*."

They had been three days out of Vell, following a road that kept trying to forget itself. Twice it had simply ended in a field, and twice Tam's ember-lantern had shown them where it used to run — the dragon-fire light fell strangely on erased ground, catching on edges nothing else could see, like lamplight through fogged glass. The girl carried the lantern against her chest the way other children carried dolls, and she talked to it, low and constant, and Ada pretended not to hear the words *mama* woven into the murmuring.

It was Tam who spotted the chapel.

"It's warm," she said, stopping so suddenly that Renn nearly walked into her. "The hill's warm. Underneath. Like a sleeping animal."

The chapel stood on a knoll above a village that had no name left, built of pale stone shot through with seams of something darker. As they climbed toward it, Ada saw what the dark seams were, and her breath went thin.

Bones. Not scattered, not heaped — *set*. Femurs laid in courses like bricks, ribs arched into window frames, skulls crowned along the roofline with their faces turned outward, watching the road. Thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands. The mortar between them glittered faintly, and when Ada lifted her charm-hand to shield her eyes from the low sun, every bone in the wall hummed against her palm's calluses, a chord too low to hear and too large to ignore.

"They built it from themselves," Maren whispered. She had gone very still, the way she went still when a ward-song wanted out of her and she wouldn't let it. "Wardens' bones. This is a reliquary. I've sung in chapels like— " She stopped. Frowned. "No. I haven't. I've never seen anything like this."

"You have," said the dead, through the charm at Ada's throat, in a voice like dry leaves. "You sang its sister. You've forgotten. We haven't."

Inside, the chapel was one high room, rib-vaulted, lit by the last red light of evening falling through windows of fused vertebrae. There were no pews. Instead the floor sloped gently down toward a shallow basin at the center, and the basin was full of bones worn smooth as river stones — handled so often, held so long, that grief itself had polished them.

Ada knew, without knowing how she knew, that you were meant to lie down among them.

"Sleep here tonight," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "All of us. Something wants to show me something, and I don't think it can do it while I'm standing."

Renn looked at the basin of bones, then at Ada, then at the door, in that order, and Ada watched him weigh running the way other men weighed coin. "I've slept in worse," he said finally, which from Renn was practically a vow of devotion.

* * *

She dreamed the way you fall — all at once, and a long way.

The chapel dissolved and Kestrelmark assembled around her in its final hour, rendered in the silver-grey of memory: the capital with its nine bridges, the terraced vineyards, the mountain pass where the Spire stood like a needle through the world's fabric, stitched into the sky itself. Stars hung thick above it — more stars than Ada had ever seen, constellations she had no names for, and she understood with a lurch that these were *Kestrelmark's* stars, that the sky over Vell had always been missing pieces and she'd never known.

Then the dream showed her the wardens.

There were hundreds of them gathered at the Spire's base, and Ada recognized their work before she recognized their faces — the bone charms at their throats were hers, or hers-before-her, carved in the same hand across four generations. At their center stood a woman with iron-grey hair and Ada's own stubborn chin, and when she turned, the dead woman looked at Ada across five years of oblivion and *saw* her.

*"You came,"* the woman said. Her voice was the voice from the walls, from the road, from every whisper Ada had ever pretended not to hear. *"Late. But you came."*

"Who are you?" Ada asked, and hated how small her voice sounded.

*"I am Warden-Superior Ede Nakamura. I am your grandmother's grandmother's mother, and I am the one who closed the door."* Around them, the dream tightened; the sky began to flicker at its edges like a candle in wind. *"Listen. We have one night, and it is already spent. The Ministry found us. Not our borders — our fire. They traced the dragon-vent under the Spire, and they came to take it, because whoever holds the last waking fire holds the making of the world. We could not fight them. So we hid."*

"Hid it how?"

Ede's face did something terrible then — grief and pride wearing each other's clothes. *"We sealed the Spire. All of it. And a seal strong enough to hide the heart of waking magic from the Ministry must hide everything the fire touches. The wards. The roads. The names. The stars we had named. Ourselves."* She spread her hands, and Ada saw that the dream-Kestrelmark was already thinning around her, bridges going translucent, vineyards fading like breath off glass. *"We unmade our own country to keep one flame alive. Five hundred thousand people, folded out of the world's memory, so that the fire would sleep unfindable until a warden came who could wake it again."*

"And no one came," Ada said. "For five years."

*"Everyone who could have come forgot there was anywhere to come to. Including us, at the end. Including me."* For the first time, Ede's voice cracked. *"That is the price they do not put in songs, child. We chose it with open eyes, and the choosing ate the chooser. By dawn none of us remembered why we stood in the cold. We only knew we had to finish. So we finished, and we died, and we stayed — because the dead are the one thing the seal cannot reach. Grief runs deeper than forgetting. We held the shape of this country in our bones the way a mold holds the shape of a blade."* She gestured, and Ada saw it: the chapel rising, generation after generation giving their bodies to the walls, so that somewhere, someday, a charm-carver with warden blood might walk in and be *told*. *"Every bone in that wall is a page. You are reading us now."*

The dream shuddered. Below, at the Spire's roots, Ada saw what she had been brought to see: a vast slow pulse of gold light, deep as a buried sun — and around its edges, dark. Cinders drifting where flame should be. The pulse came slower each time, like a heart deciding whether the next beat was worth it.

"The dragon-fire," Ada breathed. "It's dying."

*"Guttering,"* Ede corrected softly. *"A guttering flame can be fed. A dead one cannot. The vent under the Spire is the last of nine. The Ministry snuffed eight before we sealed ourselves away, and they have spent five years hunting embers — your friend knows what they do with them."* The dream was going grey at the edges now, Ede with it. *"When the last fire dies, waking magic dies with it. Charms become trinkets. Songs become noise. The dead go quiet forever, and the world closes like a book no one is reading. You have perhaps a season, Ada Nakamura. Less, if the Ministry finds the Spire first."*

"How do I wake it?"

*"Fire needs feeding. A keeper's ember will rekindle the vent — there is a child carrying one, yes? We have watched her. Good. But fire only opens the door partway. The Spire itself must be *woken*, and a Spire wakes to a warden's true name, carved into the heartbone by the warden's own hand."* Ede smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ada had ever been given. *"Ask me what that costs. Ask me, so you cannot say afterward that you were not told."*

But the dream was tearing. The last thing Ada saw clearly was Ede pressing her hand flat over her own sternum, over the heartbone beneath, mouthing words the forgetting had already eaten — and then the whole shining country of Kestrelmark folded shut like a hand closing over a coal, keeping it safe, keeping it secret, keeping it *burning*, and Ada woke with tears frozen on her cheeks and every bone in the chapel singing.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the vertebral windows. Tam was already awake, feeding splinters of chapel-kindling — dead wood only, she'd insisted, the bones were not for burning — to her lantern, and when Ada sat up the girl looked at her with those too-old eyes and said, "You cried in your sleep. The lantern flickered when you did. It doesn't usually care about people."

Ada got to her feet. Her body ached as though she'd done a week's labor, and inside her chest something had been rearranged — grief still, but grief with a spine in it now, grief standing up straight.

"Kestrelmark sealed itself," she said, without preamble, because Renn and Maren were both awake and pretending not to be, and lying to them had stopped being possible somewhere on the road. "Five years ago. To hide the last dragon-fire from the Ministry. The whole country — the wards, the names, the stars — folded away on purpose. Everyone in these villages chose to be forgotten." She looked at the walls, at the ten thousand patient faces turned outward toward the road, waiting. "And the fire under the Spire is dying. When it goes, all magic goes. Everything. Forever."

Silence. Then Renn laughed — one short, ugly note with no humor in it. "I sold pieces of the Spire to the people killing it. Of course I did. Of *course*." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Tell me there's a way to fix it. Tell me you didn't come all this way to give me a eulogy."

"There's a way," Ada said. "An ember to feed the fire. A name to wake the Spire." She did not say the rest. She would carry that alone a while longer — the image of Ede's hand pressed flat over her heart, the shape of a price she'd been warned about and hadn't yet been allowed to read.

Maren rose slowly. She hadn't spoken since the dream-telling, and when she did, her voice came out rusted but certain. "The fireline," she said. "The vents ran up through the mountains east of here. If any ember survives outside the Spire, it'll be at a keeper's station on the fireline." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes — dread, or hope, or both wearing the same coat. "There was a station at Cairn Hollow. Keeper's post, hereditary line. If anyone's still tending a flame after five years of forgetting—"

"Then they're a child," Tam said.

They all turned. The girl stood with her lantern held against her chest, and the ember inside it pulsed once, bright, like a heartbeat answering its own name.

"Keepers start young," Tam said. "My mama told me. Somebody has to stay up all night with the fire, and children don't sleep anyway."

Nobody answered her. Nobody had to. Outside, the wind came down off the eastern mountains carrying the faintest smell of woodsmoke — impossible, in a country where no one remained alive to burn anything — and Ada picked up her pack, and the dead in the walls hummed one low sustained note that she understood at last was not mourning.

It was a compass. It pointed east.
