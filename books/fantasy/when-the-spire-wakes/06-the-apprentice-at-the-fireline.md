## 6. The Apprentice at the Fireline

Tam counted the flame's breaths the way her mother had taught her, back when back meant something.

*In through the mountain's nose,* Mama would say, crouched beside the vent with Tam between her knees, both of them lit orange to the eyebrows. *Out through the crack in the world. You don't feed a fire like this, Tam-tam. You listen to it. Feeding is for cook-stoves. This one talks.*

Six years since Mama walked up the fireline trail toward the high vents and did not walk back down. Tam was twelve now, and she still listened, because listening was the only prayer she knew.

The vent sat in a bowl of black stone halfway up Mount Serrin, or what the maps said was Mount Serrin, though the maps had gone strange in the last days — rivers running uphill on paper, whole valleys printed twice, one valley inked over so hard the page had torn. The fire itself was no bigger than a wash-basin: a tongue of white-gold flame rising out of a seam in the rock, burning without wood, without smoke, without any food Tam could offer it except attention. It had been taller once. She knew that the way she knew her own hands. Every year it came up to her knee, then her waist, then her shoulder. Now it barely cleared her hip, and some nights — like last night — it lay down flat against the stone and shivered, and Tam had lain down beside it and put her arms around nothing at all and talked to it until dawn about anything, everything, the weather, the lichen, the way her boots had finally split at the toe.

"You're not allowed to go out," she told it now, morning-voiced and croaky. "That's the rule. Keeper's rule. Somebody stays up all night with the fire, and I'm somebody."

The flame leaned toward her, faintly, like a cat deciding whether to trust.

The trouble with tending a miracle alone was that nobody had ever told Tam what it was *for*. Mama had called it dragon-fire, said it was the root-stock of every warm magic in the old country, said the ward-singers used to draw their songs from vents like this one the way you'd draw water from a well. But that was story-talk, and stories were all Mama had left behind — that, and a fire-keeper's iron lantern with glass on four sides, hanging empty on its hook by the vent, waiting for whatever keepers carried when they carried fire.

Tam had never been allowed to carry fire out. *You carry an ember only when there's somewhere it needs to be worse than here.* Six years, and nowhere had ever needed it more than here.

Until three nights ago, when the stars went out.

She had seen it happen. She'd been asleep under the overhang with her back to the stone, and the dark had changed — gone from night-dark to cellar-dark, a darkness with weight — and Tam had scrambled out into the open to find the sky bald. No stars. Not clouds, not haze. Gone, like they'd never been hung. And the flame in the vent had screamed. That was the only word for it. It hadn't made sound, exactly, but the whole bowl of rock had hummed a note so low and wrong that her teeth ached and the snow up-slope let go in slabs, and Tam had pressed her palms flat to the warm seam and hummed back, stupid little market songs, anything, until the trembling stopped.

The flame had come up off the stone after that like a drowning thing breaking water. Then it settled again. Lower than before.

Something was drinking it, she understood, though not how or why. Somewhere down at the root of the world, the fire was being pulled thin as wire, and Tam could do nothing but sit by the last vent and count breaths.

So when she saw four figures coming up the goat trail from the south, she did what any sensible keeper would do.

She hid the fire.

Not really — you couldn't hide a wonder — but she banked it into the seam, mounded ash over it, dragged her bedroll and cook-pot and Mama's lantern into a scatter of junk that said *orphan girl lives here, nothing else*, and stood in front of the vent with her arms crossed, trying to look twelve years old and harmless, which required no acting at all.

Except they weren't gray-coats. That was the first thing. Gray-coats wore gray. These four looked like a sack of misfortune that had learned to walk: a tall woman moving like her feet hurt, a wiry man with a smuggler's rolling gait and eyes that counted exits, a broad quiet person whose coat was lined with what Tam hoped was fur — and at the front, a young woman with dark hair going silver at the temples, carrying a bone charm on a cord, and the charm was *glowing.*

Tam's heart did something complicated. Fire answered charms? Charms answered fire?

"You," the dark-haired woman called up the trail, and her voice cracked on the second word like a dry branch. "Keeper's girl. How long has your flame been failing?"

Nobody had ever asked Tam a question like that. Nobody had ever climbed six thousand feet to ask Tam anything.

"Since the stars went out," she said. "Worse since. Who's asking?"

"My name's Ada." The woman stopped an arm's length away, respectful of the vent's warmth, and held up the charm. Bone, carved fine as lace, and inside its whorls a light moved like minnows. "I need to see the fire. All of us do. And then we need to ask you something impossible, and I'd rather ask you fed and warm."

"We brought bread," said the smuggler, holding up a loaf like evidence. "Real bread. Well. Mostly real."

Behind them, the tall woman — the one who moved like her feet hurt, like everything hurt — was staring past Tam at the ash-mounded seam, and her face was doing something terrible. Recognition. Grief. Tam would learn, later, that Maren the ward-singer had grown up singing to a vent just like this one, before her order burned its own songs; but that morning all Tam saw was a stranger crying at a pile of ash.

"It's still alive," Maren whispered. "Oh, thank the — it's still *alive*." She dropped to her knees at the seam and began brushing ash away with bare fingers, gentle as uncovering a sleeping face, and the flame rose to meet her hand like a dog hearing its name, and Tam forgot to be suspicious entirely.

* * *

They ate bread around the fire, which Tam decided was acceptable, because the fire clearly liked them, and the fire's opinion outranked hers.

Their story came out in pieces while they ate. A country called Kestrelmark that no one remembered. Wards that failed. A Spire. A Ministry that unmade things and then unmade the memory of the unmaking. Tam chewed slowly and felt the shape of her whole life tilt. Mama's stories had names in them — Kestrelmark, the Spire, the ward-singers' college at Dell Hollow — and Tam had always assumed they were fairy-tale places, the way *the moon was Mama's first customer* was a fairy tale. But this woman Ada said Kestrelmark was real and erased, and the dead talked to her through bone, and the bone glowed brighter the higher they climbed, and Tam looked at the vent, at the flame leaning toward the strangers like a plant toward sun, and thought: *of course it does. It's lonely. It's been dying alone for six years.*

"I know where you're going," Tam said, when Ada got to the part about the Spire. "Under this mountain. Under everything. The fire runs downhill to it — you can feel the pull if you lie on the seam. My mama said the Spire's heartbone sits in the deep root, and all the vents are its fingers." She swallowed. "That's story-talk. Probably."

"It's not," Ada said quietly. "It matches what the dead show me."

"So take me with you."

Four faces turned. The broad one — Pell, they called her, though Tam never did learn the full name — made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"Absolutely not," said Renn the smuggler. "Ministry hunters on our trail, wards collapsing, a Spire that eats names — no. You're twelve."

"Keepers start young," Tam said. "My mama told me. Somebody has to stay up all night with the fire, and children don't sleep anyway."

"That's not—" Renn began.

And that was when the dogs cried out on the southern trail.

* * *

Later, Tam would replay those minutes a hundred times and never get them straight in order. There were gray coats among the rocks — six, seven of them, with leashed things on scent-lines that were not dogs, not quite — and there was Renn swearing fluently and reaching for a knife he'd told her he didn't carry anymore, and there was Ada stepping in front of the vent with her charms out like a hand of cards, and the dead in the bones screaming so loud that Tam heard it too, a sound like a city clearing its throat.

But mostly, afterward, there was Maren.

Maren rose from beside the seam the way Tam imagined her mother must have risen for the last walk up the fireline — knowing, somehow, what it cost, and paying anyway. The ward-singer's mouth opened, and what came out was not a song. It was the wreck of a song, three notes and a broken fourth, a ward sung with half its ribs snapped, and Tam felt the air above the bowl of stone *harden* — felt it go tense and bright as hammered tin — just as the lead hunter raised something that drank the light.

The broken ward caught whatever he fired. It caught it, bent it, tore it apart into snowfall. And Maren folded up like laundry, both hands at her throat, and when Tam reached her — when all of them reached her — she was trying to speak and producing nothing at all, not even breath-noise, her mouth shaping words into perfect silent O's while the hunters below scattered from whatever their own weapon had become in reflection.

"Maren—" Ada was holding her shoulders. "*Maren.*"

Maren smiled. It was the worst smile Tam had ever seen, and the bravest. Her lips moved once, deliberately, and Tam, sitting close, read it the way you read a fire: *worth it.*

Pell lifted the ward-singer like she weighed nothing, and Renn covered the retreat down the western trail, and Tam stood in the falling brightness of the ruined ward with her heart hammering and understood two things at once, cleanly, the way children understand things adults spend decades unlearning:

These people would bleed for each other. And she wanted — had always wanted — exactly that.

* * *

They camped that night in the shelter of the vent, because the flame calmed when Maren was near it, leaning toward her damaged silence like a friend sitting vigil. Tam packed by lantern-light: Mama's blanket, the good knife, the dried fish, the iron lantern with glass on four sides.

Then she knelt at the seam and did the thing she'd never been allowed to do.

An ember came off the mother-flame willingly, almost eagerly, a bead of white-gold no bigger than a currant, and Tam bore it on an iron poker with steady hands — *steady hands, Tam-tam, or don't bother* — and lowered it into the lantern, and closed the little door, and the lantern filled with light like a heart filling with blood.

"You carry an ember only when there's somewhere it needs to be worse than here," she said aloud, to Mama, to the flame, to whichever of them was listening.

Ada crouched beside her. Up close her eyes were the color of the ember, and just as tired. "We can't promise you'll come back."

"I know." Tam stood, and hooked her thumb through the lantern's handle, and felt the small fierce pulse of it against her palm, in time with her own pulse, two heartbeats arguing about who kept whom. "But if the fire dies down there and we win anyway, someone still has to carry what's left. That's the job. Somebody stays up all night with the fire."

She looked east, where the trail dropped into dark country and the dead, they said, were humming a compass-note under everything.

"And I'm somebody," she said.
