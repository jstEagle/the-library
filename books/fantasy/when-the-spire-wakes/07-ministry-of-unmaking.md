## 7. Ministry of Unmaking

They took him in the dark between Vell and the mountains, and Renn had time to think exactly one thought before the hood went over his head: *of course they did.*

He'd been walking rearguard, the way he always did, because that was where a smuggler belonged — close enough to the road behind to hear trouble coming, far enough from Ada's firelight that he could pretend he wasn't watching her. He'd heard the horses a half-mile out. He'd had time to wake Maren. He'd had time to shove Tam into the gully with the lantern wrapped in his own coat.

Instead he'd stood in the middle of the road like a man waiting for a coach he'd paid for, and let them take him quietly, because the alternative was letting them find the others.

That was the thing about being a coward, Renn thought, as the hood came off and lamplight needled his eyes. It didn't mean you ran. Sometimes it meant you stayed perfectly, exquisitely still while they put the irons on, because you'd already done the arithmetic and this was the cheapest way to lose.

The room they gave him was not a cell. That was the first courtesy, and courtesies frightened him more than chains ever had. A table, two chairs, a lamp with a green glass shade that made everything look drowned. A pitcher of water, beaded with cold. Someone had even left a plate of bread and hard cheese, arranged neatly, like an offering at a shrine.

He sat across from it for a long time before he touched anything.

*They know what I am,* he told himself. *Smuggler. Fence of relics. The man who sold Kestrelmark's bones to the Ministry one crate at a time.* If they'd wanted him dead they'd have shot him on the road and been done with it. No — they wanted him comfortable. They wanted him *talkative*.

The door opened without a sound, which meant it was well-made, which meant money, which meant he wasn't in some garrison lockup but somewhere the Ministry kept its real work. The man who entered was gray in the unremarkable way of clerks and undertakers, with ink-stained fingers and a face you would forget while looking at it. He sat, poured two cups of water, and slid one across.

"Renn Vasq," he said. "Of the *Kestrel's Wake*. Formerly of the *Saltwife*. Formerly of—" he consulted a card he hadn't needed to consult— "several other vessels, several other names."

"You've got me confused with someone interesting," Renn said. "I haul cloth. Dye. Occasionally cheese."

"Occasionally bone." The clerk set down a small wooden box, opened it, and turned it so Renn could see inside: a strip of carved vertebrae, pale as candle-wax, threaded on wire. "Recovered from your warehouse on Tanner's Row. We've been meaning to visit for five years, Mr. Vasq. You were very careful. But careful men keep ledgers, and ledgers are just confessions written patiently."

Renn looked at the vertebrae and felt the old shame come up in him like floodwater. He knew those bones. He'd carried them. Somewhere in a Ministry vault there was a shelf with his name scratched into it in his own hand, an inventory of a dead country sold by weight.

"All right," he said, because lying now was like lying about the weather in a storm. "What do you want?"

"To talk. That's all anyone here wants, Mr. Vasq. To talk." The clerk folded his ink-stained hands. "You're traveling with the warden's heir. The charm-carver from Vell. And a ward-singer who should be voiceless by rights, and a child carrying fire in a lantern. Four people walking toward a Spire that does not exist. You can see how that might interest us."

"I see four travelers and a road," Renn said. "Roads go places."

"The Spire goes nowhere." For the first time the clerk's voice changed — not louder, just deeper, as though something under the gray had shifted its weight. "It has gone nowhere for five years. It will go nowhere forever. That is not a threat, Mr. Vasq. It is a kindness. You cannot imagine what we are protecting the world from."

"Protecting it from a country?"

"From a *choice*." The clerk leaned forward, and the lamp caught his eyes, and for one moment Renn saw that the forgettable face was a mask worn over something very old and very tired. "You sold our relics, so let me pay you in kind. Let me sell you the truth, since no one else will."

And then the clerk told him.

*

There had been a catastrophe. Of course there had; there was always a catastrophe, Renn thought, listening with his heart going slow and cold, because that was how every butcher explained the knife. Five years ago — the year the stars over Kestrelmark had begun to dim, the year the wards had started singing wrong — something had come through the border. Something the old wardens had invited, or failed to stop, or both. It had eaten a city. Not burned it. *Eaten* it — streets, bells, children mid-laugh, whole and entire, leaving a hole in the world shaped like ten thousand lives.

The wardens had gathered at the Spire. They had argued for three days. And in the end, one of their number had stood up — a woman named Sereth Vane, first warden, keeper of the eastern door, the finest ward-singer of her generation — and said the only word that could save what remained: *enough*. She would seal the Spire. She would fold the country closed like a letter, hide it from the world and the world from it, starless and doorless and dreaming. And because the seal required a warden's whole self — name, memory, love, the thread of her that the world held onto — she would pay it herself. She would make the world forget her, and in forgetting her, forget the wound she was closing.

"She did it," the clerk said softly. "She walked into the heartbone and carved her name out. And the country closed, and the bleeding stopped, and the world went on. Except—" his mouth twisted— "except forgetting is not a clean surgery, Mr. Vasq. The wound came with her. The hunger that ate that city — it sleeps under the Spire still, coiled around the dragon-fire, and every year the fire gutters lower, and every year the seal thins. And if anyone ever wakes the Spire — if anyone opens that door again —"

"They wake the thing that ate the city too," Renn said.

"They wake *everything*. Which is why the Director—" the clerk paused, and smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing Renn had seen in a life full of sad smiles— "the Director takes such a personal interest in your little caravan. She remembers, Mr. Vasq. She is the only person alive who remembers, because you cannot forget what you paid everything to become. She walked out of the Spire with no name and no past and a purpose instead, and she built us, and we have spent five years making certain the choice stays made. Collecting resonant objects. Burning relics. Silencing singers. Because somewhere out there, the dead keep whispering to a charm-carver in Vell, telling her she's a warden, telling her to open the door."

The lamp hummed. Renn found that his hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as though the room might tilt otherwise.

"And when she opens it," he said carefully, "and the fire comes back up, and the stars come back — what happens to the hungry thing?"

"It wakes with them," the clerk said simply. "The Director watched it eat a city once. She will not watch twice. Whatever your charm-carver believes she's restoring — she is opening a cage, and she is standing inside it."

*

They gave him until morning.

They gave him a cell after all — a proper one, narrow and clean, with a straw tick and a slot of window showing a sky with no stars in it, which was answer enough about where he was. He lay on the tick and did the arithmetic he was famous for, and hated himself with a thoroughness that would have impressed a saint.

Because here was the shape of it. Either Ada Nakamura was right — the dead were honest, the fire could be raised, the stars relit, and the hungry thing either dead or bound or beatable — or the Director was right, and the woman he'd been following toward the mountains was going to open a cage with a god-shaped monster in it, with a child carrying a lantern walking beside her.

And he couldn't know. That was the rot of it. He'd never known anything for certain except the weight of a crate and the honesty of a scale, and now everyone wanted him to bet a country on a feeling.

But there was a third thing, and it was the third thing that kept him staring at the ceiling. The Director remembered. She remembered everything — the city, the eating, the choice. Five years of being the sole keeper of a nation's grave, and what had she built? A ministry of erasure. Burned relics. Hunted singers. A child's fire hunted through the mountains. She'd had five years to tell the truth, to teach the world to guard against the hunger, to prepare — and instead she'd spent them making sure no one could ever *try*.

People who were saving the world, Renn thought, generally let the world help.

People who were hiding from what they'd done built ministries.

He got up in the gray hour and knocked on the door until someone came, and asked to see the clerk again, and when the clerk arrived — same gray face, same drowned lamplight — Renn sat down and put both hands on the table where they could be seen, like a man laying down cards.

"Here's my offer," he said. "I'll give you the caravan. Route, camp-signals, the lot. The girl's ember, the singer, all of it. And I'll walk the warden's heir right up to the Spire's threshold myself, and hand her to you at the door — because you're right about one thing. She doesn't know what's sleeping under it. She's walking blind, and somebody blind shouldn't be holding the key."

The clerk studied him for a long moment. "And why would you sell her? Sentiment rarely survives interrogation."

"Because I'm a smuggler," Renn said, and found, horribly, that the words came easy — smooth, worn, fitting his mouth like an old coat. "I sold her country's bones already. This is just finishing the job. Besides—" he shrugged— "she'll never make it. Better I bring her in gentle than she dies in a ravine with the lantern."

The lie came out of him whole and warm and fluent, and he understood, sitting there in the green light, that this was the true punishment the Ministry dealt: not the cell, not the irons, but discovering how easily you could do this. How naturally betrayal fit a man who'd practiced. The clerk wrote it all down, route and signals, and Renn dictated, and every word was true and every word was a lie, because the route was real and the signals were real and the only false thing was the reason — and maybe that would be enough, and maybe it wouldn't, and either way he'd learn by morning whether he was a traitor or the only kind of friend Ada Nakamura could afford.

"One condition," Renn said, as the clerk rose. "When you take her at the threshold — the child walks away. The ember goes free. That's the price. Break it and I'll burn every signal I've given you and laugh while they hang me."

The clerk considered, then inclined his head. "The Ministry honors its bargains. It is rather the point of us."

They took him back to the cell, and he slept two hours, dreamless, and woke hating the ease of it.

In the morning they gave him back his boots and his knives — *both* knives, which told him exactly how much they trusted him — and a gray-cloaked escort, and pointed him up the mountain road with a paper in his pocket signed in the Director's own hand: *Let the bearer pass. He brings us the warden.*

He walked. The sky stayed starless. Behind him the Ministry's gates closed, and ahead of him, three days up the trail, a small fire would be burning with a charm-carver beside it, and a singer, and a child with a lantern, all of them trusting the rearguard to come home.

By noon he could see the pass. By dusk he could see smoke — thin, domestic, hopeful — rising from a hollow below the ridgeline.

He stood a long time at the edge of the trees, a traitor's paper in his pocket and a liar's speech rehearsed smooth in his mouth, watching the smoke of the only family he'd ever managed to gather.

Then he stepped out of the treeline and walked toward the fire, wearing his easiest smile, and began — even at forty paces, even before anyone could see his face — to rehearse the words that would crack their trust down to the bone:

"I found a way through," he called. "Ada. I found a way — but we need to talk."
