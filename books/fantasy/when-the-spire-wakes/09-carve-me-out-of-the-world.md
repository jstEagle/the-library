## 9. Carve Me Out of the World

The Spire breathed.

That was the only way Ada could describe it as they climbed the last of the spiral stair — a slow, tidal rhythm in the walls themselves, bone and stone expanding and contracting like the ribs of some vast sleeping animal. The air tasted of iron and old snow and, beneath it all, something sweeter: dragon-fire, banked down to its final coals, waiting five years for someone to remember it existed.

Her lantern cast their shadows long against the stairwell wall — hers, Renn's, Maren's silent silhouette, Tam's small shape pressed close with the ember-lantern held against her chest like a second heartbeat. The ember had been burning steady since the Fireline, since Tam had poured everything she had into keeping it alive through the ambush, through Maren's collapse, through the three-day climb into the mountains where the stars should have been and were not.

"Almost there," Ada said. Her voice came out strange in the Spire's throat, doubled slightly, as if something inside was learning to speak by mimicking her.

*You are almost home,* whispered the dead, from every charm at her belt at once. *And you will not be here when you arrive.*

She pushed that thought away the way she'd been pushing it away for days — badly, with both hands.

The stair opened onto the heartchamber without warning. One moment ceiling; the next, space so wide the lantern couldn't find its edges. Ada stopped on the threshold because her body refused to carry her further.

At the center of the chamber rose the heartbone.

It was the size of a chapel, pale as moonlight, curved like a rib torn from the world itself. Across its surface ran channels and grooves — and Ada understood, with a lurch, that they were carvings. Names. Thousands upon thousands of names, worn shallow by centuries, each one a warden who had given something to this place. And threaded through the grooves, dim as dying embers, moved the last light of the dragon-fire — a pulse, a pulse, then nothing, then a pulse, like a heart deciding whether to bother beating again.

Above them, where a ceiling should have been, there was only blackness. No stars. The hole Kestrelmark had left in the sky, seen from inside.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "It's beautiful."

"It's dying," Ada said.

"She's dying," Maren corrected — and though no sound came out, Ada heard it anyway, felt it land against her skin the way Maren's songs always had, written now in ash-gestures and expression instead of voice. *She,* Maren insisted. Not an object. A singer whose song had been cut short mid-note.

Ada stepped forward. The floor hummed under her boots. In her pocket, her fingers found the old woman's charm — the one bought with a dying breath and the plea *remember Kestrelmark* — and she held onto it like a handrail over a cliff.

"You shouldn't have come this far."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, and Ada knew it before she turned. She had heard it once before, filtered through Renn's interrogation, described in his shaken whisper afterward: *calm, kind, tired in a way that made you want to apologize for existing.* But hearing it in person was different. Hearing it in the heartchamber of a murdered country was different.

The Director stood between them and the heartbone. She looked like no one in particular — a woman of middle years, grey-streaked hair, plain traveling clothes, the kind of face you'd forget while looking at it. That, Ada realized, was the point. This was what remained of a warden who had carved herself out so thoroughly that even she could barely remember being one.

"The wards failed," the Director said. "I know. I felt them go. Do you know how long I waited to feel anything again?" She smiled, and it was almost gentle. "Five years, girl. Five years of quiet."

"You did this," Ada said. "Kestrelmark. You unmade it."

"I *saved* it." For the first time the calm cracked, just a hairline. "You've seen the memories. The bones in the chapel. Then you know what happened the night the fire went mad. What the wardens did to stop it. What it cost." Her eyes — grey, forgettable, terrible — fixed on Ada. "I was there. I sang the closing with your predecessors' hands on my shoulders. And when it was done, when the country was folded out of the world to keep the Ministry from prying the Spire open and letting the fire loose again — do you know what I learned?"

No one answered. Tam had gone white. Renn's hand had drifted, unconsciously, toward a knife he would never use.

"I learned that forgetting is mercy," the Director said. "I learned that memory is a wound that never closes. So I made myself forget too. And it worked. It worked beautifully, until a charm-seller in Vell started hearing voices."

Behind Ada, Maren moved — a sharp gesture, ash-pale fury. The Director's gaze flicked to her, and something like grief crossed the forgettable face.

"And you. The ward-singer who let me use her. You never asked why, and I loved you for it." She turned back to Ada. "But you ask why constantly. It leaks out of you. That's what resonance *is*, girl — the refusal to stop asking why. So here is my offer, and I will make it only once."

She raised her hand, and the darkness above them shifted — and Ada gasped, because for one impossible moment there were stars up there, whole constellations of them, reflected or remembered or simply offered.

"Walk away," the Director said. "Take your smuggler, your broken singer, your little fire-keeper. Go back to Vell. I will put the wards back around the Spire myself, gently this time, and I will give you a life. A remembered life. Your name in mouths, your face known in the market, friends who grew up beside you, a mother—" she paused, delicately, "—or the shape of one, if you prefer. Every memory anyone could want, built fresh and seamless. You will never hear the dead again unless you wish to. You will be ordinary, and loved, and *real* in the way the world agrees real things are real."

The silence rang.

"That's not a life," Ada said quietly. "That's a charm. A pretty bone trinket with a lie inside it."

"It's peace."

"It's *theft*." Her voice cracked and she let it. "You'd steal me from myself and call it kindness. You already stole a whole country and called it salvation. How many people forgot their own children, Director? How many mothers woke up with empty arms and no word for the ache?" She pulled the old woman's charm from her pocket and held it up, and the dead spoke through it, all of them at once, a tide of remembered names — *Kestrelmark, Kestrelmark, we were here, we were here, we were here* — and the Director flinched as if struck. "They didn't get to choose. At least I'm choosing."

"You're choosing erasure," the Director said. "Carve your name into that bone and no one will ever know you. Not them." She nodded past Ada's shoulder. "Not the child who carried fire for you across half a mountain. They will stand in this room and feel you slip out of their minds mid-heartbeat, and they will spend the rest of their lives with a hole shaped exactly like you and no idea what filled it. I know. I *am* the hole."

"I know," Ada said. And she did — that was the worst of it, that she knew precisely, having spent five nights lying awake doing the arithmetic of it. "But here's what you never learned, because you did your forgetting alone." She looked back at them. Renn, jaw tight, eyes wet and furious about being caught being wet. Maren, already fumbling for the stub of charcoal she kept in her sleeve. Tam, chin lifted, twelve years old and immovable.

"They won't be alone either. And neither will I. Because they'll tell stories. That's all remembering is, Director — someone telling the story, and someone else telling it wrong, and someone else fixing it." She smiled, and her eyes stung, and she didn't wipe them. "You erased a country and it survived in bones and charms and a drunk ward-singer's nightmares. You think you can erase *me* worse than that?"

The Director looked at her for a long moment. And Ada saw it — saw the warden under the grey, the woman who had once believed exactly this and been ground down by the weight of what she'd lost. Something in the forgettable face came briefly, terribly, into focus.

"I hope you're right," the Director said. "For their sakes. Because I am too tired to stop you, and too honest to pretend I don't want to watch."

She stepped aside.

* * *

The heartbone was warm.

That surprised her — she had expected cold, marble, monument. Instead it gave under her palm like a sleeping animal's flank, and the dim fire in the grooves brightened as she approached, gathering toward her hand like moths. The carvings of the old wardens rose around her, and among them, faint but unmistakable, she found names she knew from the chapel of bones: *Sera Ilvane. Odom Bright. The nine who closed the door.*

*There is still time to stop,* said the dead — but gently now, the way family speaks.

"No," Ada murmured. "There really isn't."

She took out her carving knife — her oldest tool, the one she'd used on ten thousand market charms, whalebone-handled, worn to fit her grip like it had grown there. She pressed the tip to the heartbone below Sera Ilvane's name.

"Ashgrove," Renn said behind her. "Wait."

She turned. He crossed the distance in four strides, took her by the shoulders, and kissed her forehead — clumsy, desperate, entirely Renn. "For later," he said. "Since there isn't going to be a later where I remember wanting to."

Maren came next. She couldn't speak, so she did the thing she'd been practicing since the Fireline: she wrote on Ada's arm with the charcoal stub, quick and shaking. *I sang you a ward once. It didn't hold. This one will.* Then she hugged her hard enough to crack a rib, and Ada let her.

Tam last. The girl set the ember-lantern at Ada's feet, right against the base of the heartbone, and the fire inside stood up straight like a soldier reporting for duty. "My mother kept flame," Tam said. "So do I. Go on, Ada. I'll keep yours lit too. Whoever you come back as."

Ada picked up the knife.

She thought of the market in Vell, the smell of tallow and rain. Of the dead woman counting out coins with shaking hands. Of streets leading nowhere, neighbors hollowed out, the stars going dark like lamps snuffed room by room. Of the word the dead had been saying since the beginning, the word that had never been a warning, only an instruction:

*Remember.*

She carved.

The first stroke hurt like pulling a tooth. The second like losing a finger. By the fifth she couldn't remember what she'd been afraid of losing, which was the point, which was the price, which was fine, which was —

Her name sank into the heartbone and blazed.

Light detonated upward through the grooves — through *Sera Ilvane*, through *Odom Bright*, through nine thousand wardens and five years of dark — and the heartbone *sang*. Not Maren's broken music, not the Ministry's sealed silences, but the first note of the world, the one under all other notes, and the dragon-fire roared up through the Spire like dawn through a keyhole.

Above them, in the black hole where a country used to be, the stars came back.

Not all at once — one, then ten, then a flood of them, pouring into the sky like water finding its old riverbed, constellations snapping into place, and with each one Ada felt a memory return to the world somewhere: a baker remembering his street, a mother her child, a border town its own name. Kestrelmark flooding home. Somewhere far below, she somehow knew, maps were redrawing themselves and archives were groaning open and people were waking up weeping without knowing why.

The Director watched the starlight pour down, and it touched her forgettable face and made it unforgettable for exactly one moment — a young warden, laughing, alive, forgiven — and then she came apart into it, willingly, a handful of light joining the sky she had denied for five years.

Ada turned to her family.

Renn reached her first. Maren. Tam, small arms around her waist. She held on. She felt herself thinning even as she held on — her name unraveling out of their minds thread by thread, and she watched it happen in their faces, the focus going soft, the love staying while the *her* drained out of it —

— and then there was a stranger in the heartchamber of the waking Spire, and three people standing very close to her, holding each other, all of them crying and none of them knowing quite what for.

The dead, in the silence where her name used to be, said the only thing left to say.

*Welcome.*
