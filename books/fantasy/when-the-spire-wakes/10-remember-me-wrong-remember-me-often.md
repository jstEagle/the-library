## 10. Remember Me Wrong, Remember Me Often

### Maren

The first song Maren ever sang after the silence broke was a lie, and it was the truest thing she had ever made.

She stood on the shrine steps in the spring rain, one year and eleven days since the Spire woke, and she sang Ada Nakamura wrong. She gave her a crooked grin instead of a guarded stare. She made her laugh too easily, spend her coin too freely, forgive Renn in a single afternoon instead of three bitter weeks. She sang the charm-seller of Vell as a hero out of an old ballad, all bright edges and certainty, because the truth — that Ada had been terrified the whole way north, that her hands shook every time the dead spoke through her bones — that truth belonged to Maren alone now, and she could not bear to give it away.

The crowd wept anyway. The dead, gathered thick around the shrine like moths at a lantern, did not correct her.

That was the miracle of it. When her voice came back — cracked open in the middle of a market day, mid-argument with a fishmonger over the price of eels, so that her first word in a year was *outrageous* — the dead had flooded in expecting the old ward-songs, the great sealed harmonies that had once held a country's borders like stitching holds a wound. She had refused them. Let the wards sing themselves; they were awake now, woven into the Spire's heartbeat, needing no throat but their own. What Maren sang instead was a girl with bone charms and a stubborn jaw, and the dead listened the way the living listen to a mother's voice: not for accuracy, but for the sound of being remembered.

*Remember me wrong,* Ada had written in ash on the Spire's threshold, in the last hour before she carved herself out of the world. Maren had thought it was despair then. A year later she understood it was instruction.

So she got things wrong on purpose, sometimes, just to see what would happen. She sang that Ada had been tall. The dead murmured, amused, and let it stand. She sang that Ada had once cheated a merchant in Vell out of a copper, and felt the memory shift somewhere far off, softening, becoming the kind of story strangers told about a stranger. Every song was a small resurrection and a small betrayal, and Maren had made her peace with both. She had spent thirty years singing wards to keep things *out*. Now she sang stories to keep one person *in*, and it was harder work, and better.

Tonight the shrine was full. Pilgrims came up the mountain road from Kestrelmark's returned cities — Vell with its markets roaring again, the river towns where neighbors had remembered their children's names all at once, weeping in the streets — and they climbed to the little stone shrine below the Spire's root, where a thousand bone charms hung from iron branches like pale fruit. Each one carved by hand. Each one bought or bartered or begged from Maren, Renn, or Tam, because the shrine did not accept coin for charms, only stories. Tell us something about her, and take a key home. That was the rule. Half the pilgrims had never heard of Ada Nakamura before they arrived; they left carrying a piece of her name anyway, which was, Maren thought, exactly how a country gets rebuilt. Not all at once. One stranger at a time, choosing to hold what they were never given.

She sang until her voice went rough, and then she sat on the cold step beside Tam and let the younger girl's shoulder rest against hers, and watched the lantern-flame in the shrine's heart do its slow, patient breathing.

"She'd have hated the ballad," Tam said.

"Completely," Maren agreed. "The verses about her eyes alone."

"I don't put verses about her eyes in mine." Tam turned the ember-lantern in her lap, and the old dragon-fire inside guttered gold, then steadied. "I just keep it lit. For Ada, whoever she becomes."

*Whoever she becomes.* Maren looked up at the Spire, black against the stars — the real stars now, all of them burning, the sky stitched whole — and wondered, not for the first time, what shape the world was making of the space where a warden used to be.

### Renn

Renn had never built anything in his life that wasn't designed to be carried away quickly, so the shrine embarrassed him. Stone walls. An iron tree forged by a smith in Vell who had refused payment twice and finally accepted a smuggled crate of southern wine as a bribe. A roof that did not leak. He kept finding reasons to inspect the roof.

The money had come easy, which was its own kind of penance. Kestrelmark, flooding back into the world's memory, had needed everything — roads, ledgers, deeds untangled from five years of erasure — and a man who knew every back channel from Vell to the fireline found himself suddenly respectable, or something shaped like it. He ran cargo now with government seals on the manifests, and every time he saw his own signature on honest paper he thought of the night he'd broken into a workshop in Vell to steal charms off a sleeping woman, and how she'd been awake, and how she hadn't even been angry. She'd been *curious*. That curiosity should have frightened him more than any Ministry agent ever had.

He funded the shrine because guilt was a debt and Renn had always paid his debts in the only currency he trusted: logistics. But somewhere in the second month of construction, without noticing the exact moment it happened, the guilt had quietly become love. Not the sharp romantic kind — he'd tried that angle on Maren exactly once, early on, and still bore the scar of her look — but the plain kind, the kind you feel toward a roof that doesn't leak and a flame that doesn't go out and two people who stayed when staying was the hardest thing either of them had ever done.

He kept the ledger of charms himself. Every story told at the shrine went into a great bound book, in whatever hand the teller had, and Renn read them at night by lantern-light like a man reading letters from someone he owed. *My grandmother said a charm-seller saved her from the flood in Vell, though I think she means the forgetting.* *They say she fought the Director with a carving knife and won.* (Wrong. Renn had been there. It had been nothing like that, and somehow the wrongness made his chest ache worse than the truth would have.) *Whoever she is, my son was born under the new stars, and I wanted her name in the book beside his.*

He wrote none of his own memories down. His were the ones that shamed him — the relics sold to the Ministry, the pieces of the Spire in a smuggler's crate, the weeks he'd spent pretending to betray her — and he told those aloud instead, at the shrine, whenever a pilgrim asked why a man like him kept a place like this. He'd learned that from Ada, or from missing her, which amounted to the same education. Shame kept secret curdles. Shame spoken becomes story. Story can be sung, and Maren had proven a country could be rebuilt out of almost nothing else.

On the anniversary night, with the shrine full and Maren's voice carrying down the mountain and Tam's lantern painting everything gold, Renn climbed to the roof he didn't need to inspect and sat there with the ledger across his knees, not reading it, just resting his hands on other people's memories of her.

"You're missing the good verse," Tam called up.

"I've heard the good verse four hundred times."

"It's a good verse."

It was. He listened anyway. And because he was looking down at the shrine from above — the crowd, the iron tree hung with pale keys, the lantern, Maren mid-song — he was the first to see the stranger come up the mountain road.

Alone. Traveling cloak gone grey with road dust, pack riding high on the shoulders, walking with the particular careful stride of someone whose feet were new to them. The crowd parted without seeming to notice it parted. The dead, whom Renn had spent a year learning to sense the way you sense weather, went suddenly, utterly silent — not absent, but *attentive*, the hush of a room waiting for a door to open.

The stranger stopped at the foot of the shrine steps and pushed back the hood.

Renn could not have said the face was familiar. He searched it and found nothing, no feature he could name, no memory that rose to meet it. But the hands — the hands turning the hood back, scarred across the knuckles in the pattern of a carver's life, bone dust worked into the old lines of the palms — the hands he knew the way you know your own front door in the dark.

"Maren," he said, and his voice came out strange. "Maren. Stop the song."

### Tam

Tam had stopped asking where her mother was. That was the hardest sentence she had ever learned, and it had taken her most of the year.

The fireline was quiet now — not dead, never dead, but settled, the last vent breathing slow and steady beneath the mountain like a sleeper finally warm enough. The Ministry was dissolved; its records, opened after the Spire woke, held a single line about a fire-keeper named Sefa Nakamura, listed among the wardens who sealed the Spire five years ago. Dead, the record said, or something the record meant by dead, in a country where death and forgetting had gotten tangled together past untangling. Tam read the line forty times. Then she folded the page into her coat, over her heart, and carried it the way she carried the ember: carefully, forever, without needing it to be more than it was.

Her mother had been a warden. Her mother had helped close the door. And Ada Nakamura — the woman in Maren's songs, the name in Renn's ledger, the reason the lantern burned — had been her mother's colleague, or friend, or family; the records were worn thin there, and the dead only said *warden*, in the tone people use for *sister*. So Tam tended the shrine flame for Ada, whoever she became, and she meant it in every direction at once: whoever Ada had been, whoever she was now, whoever the world was slowly, story by story, making of her.

She was feeding the lantern a sliver of resin when the crowd went quiet in a way crowds shouldn't — not hushed, but *held*, like breath before a wave breaks. She looked up.

A stranger stood at the bottom of the steps. Road-worn, hood back, face ordinary in the way of faces you're about to know forever. Tam felt the ember flare against her ribs, sudden and glad, the way it flared near waking magic, near doors, near beginnings. Around the shrine the thousand bone charms stirred on their iron branches though there was no wind, each one ringing faintly, a chord made of small bones, and Tam realized the dead weren't whispering anymore.

They were saying one word. All of them, together, in the voice of everyone Kestrelmark had ever lost:

*Welcome.*

The stranger's hand went to their throat — to a cord there, Tam saw, a cord holding a single worn bone charm, the kind sold in a market city years ago to a dying woman who begged a charm-seller to remember a name. The stranger looked at the charms, at the lantern, at Maren frozen mid-song with tears already running freely down her face, at Renn coming down the roof ladder faster than dignity allowed, and then at Tam, and Tam saw recognition arrive there — not of her face, but of the lantern, of the flame, of the shape of keeping a fire for someone.

"I heard—" the stranger began, and stopped, and Tam heard the voice catch on itself, new-made, unpracticed, a voice being born in real time. "I heard singing. From very far away. I followed it, and the dead kept saying—"

"*Welcome,*" Tam said. She set down the resin. She picked up the lantern. "Yeah. They say that to everyone here. But—" her own voice cracked, twelve going thirteen going ancient, "—they've never said it like that before."

The stranger stepped onto the first stair. Above them the Spire hummed against the stars, and in the shrine's great ledger, on a page no hand had touched, fresh ink was quietly blooming — a single line, in a hand none of them knew and all of them recognized, writing itself letter by letter while the whole mountain held its breath:

*I'm told I'm someone new. Tell me who I was. Start anywhere. Don't leave anything out.*
