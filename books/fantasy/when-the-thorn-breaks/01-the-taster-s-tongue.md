## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There is an art to dying gracefully, and I have practiced it for ten years.

Not my own death, you understand. Other people's. The poison meant for a duke, the rot slipped into a borderlord's wine, the slow arsenic some ambitious cousin feeds his elderly aunt by the spoonful in her evening posset. My job is to die first, beautifully, so that no one else has to die at all. I am very good at it, which is another way of saying that I am very good at not being dead, which is another way of saying that somewhere along the road of my service, I lost the trick of being alive.

My name is Dominic Delacroix, and I am the Queen's Taster of Veyra.

The title comes with silver at the collar, a room in the palace with south-facing windows I am never awake to see, and a tongue that has been ruined for everything except its purpose. Honey tastes like wet stone. Wine tastes like pennies and regret. The last thing I remember enjoying—the truly *enjoying*, mouth and memory both—was a plum, eaten on a wall in the summer heat, juice running down my wrist, someone laughing at me for eating it that way. I don't remember who was laughing. That's the cruelty of my profession: I kept the plum and lost the laugh.

That night, the palace held a state banquet for the Ambassadors of the Salt Coast, which meant eleven courses, four hundred candles, and enough political venom in the air to kill a man before he ever reached the wine.

I stood behind the queen's left shoulder in my grey livery and watched the dishes come up from the kitchens on their little silver elevators of ceremony. Cold sorrel soup. Quail in ash-berry glaze. A fish that had been alive that morning in the palace ponds and had died, I imagine, with more dignity than most of the guests would manage. At each course I took my spoonful, my sip, my measured bite, and waited out the ninety seconds that stood between four hundred people and catastrophe. Ninety seconds is a long time when your body is the instrument everyone's life depends on. You learn to fill it. I count candle flames. I name the spices. I recite, silently, the smuggler roads of the Thornfell passes, every switchback and hidden ford, as though someday I might need to run them.

Old habit. Older than I can account for.

It was the fourth course—the quail—when my tongue spoke to me.

People think tasting for poison is about flavor. It isn't. After ten years, poison doesn't taste like anything. It feels like weather. There's a pressure behind the palate, a wrongness in the way the spit turns over, a small cold hand closing around the back of the throat. This one came in quiet, under the glaze, beneath the fat, wrapped in something bitter and herbal that a clever cook might have used on purpose. Wolfsbane, cut with essence of thorn-ash to mask the numbing. Refined work. Expensive work. The kind of work you buy when you want a queen dead before the dessert course and don't care what it costs you in coin or conscience.

My body knew before my mind caught up. That's the other thing about immunity—you don't get to be immune all at once. You get immune in increments, each dose teaching your blood a new betrayal to refuse. Enough wolfsbane will still stop me. Probably. I spat into the silver spittoon beside the dais—a gesture that silenced the string musicians mid-phrase, if you're wondering how seriously the court takes my spitting—and said, in a voice I'd rehearsed being calm in for a decade:

"Fourth course. Don't serve it."

What followed was exactly what always follows: guards, shouting, the ambassador from the Salt Coast going white and then going loud about insult and suspicion, the queen rising with her face composed into that particular royal blankness that means she is frightened down to her shoes. They carried the quail away under cloth like a corpse. In a sense, they did.

And I stood there in the ringing silence of four hundred aristocrats pretending not to stare at me, and felt—nothing. That was the part that frightened me, later, when I let myself think about it. Not the poison. Not the near-miss. But the nothing. Somewhere in that hall was a person who had wanted the queen dead, and I had just made an enemy with deep pockets and better pharmacists than mine, and my heart hadn't even sped up. I have been poisoned so many times, in training and out of it, that assassination has become a kind of weather to me too. Rain expected. Bring a coat.

Afterward, the queen sent for me. She does this after every incident—an audience of three minutes in which she thanks me with genuine warmth and gives me a token, because protocol demands the Queen's Taster be visibly valued. That night it was a pin of Veyran silver shaped like a mountain thorn. "You saved my life again, Master Delacroix," she said. "Is there anything you want?"

Every year she asks. Every year I say no.

That year, for reasons I still couldn't name, the word *no* snagged on something coming up my throat. What came out instead, quietly, was: "Do you know, I can't remember my mother's voice?"

The queen blinked. Courtiers froze. I heard someone set down a glass too hard.

"I only ask," I went on, because apparently I had decided to ruin my own career, "because I've tasted two hundred poisons and I can name each one by its weight on the tongue, but I cannot tell you what my mother sounded like. Or why I came to the capital. Or who taught me the Thornfell roads well enough to walk them blindfolded—which I can, madam, and no one in this palace ever taught me. I sometimes think I gave those things away a spoonful at a time, and nobody warned me the toll was memory."

The silence went on long enough that I began composing my resignation letter mentally. But the queen only looked at me for a while with those pale eyes, and then she said, softly, "Then take a holiday, Delacroix. Go find your mother's voice." And she dismissed me before either of us had to be embarrassed further.

* * *

I did not go find my mother's voice. I went walking, which is what I do instead of sleeping, which is what I do instead of remembering.

There are doors in the palace walls that the palace pretends not to have, and through one of them lies the smuggler road north—the old trail that runs from the city slaughterhouses up through the terraced vineyards and into the foothills below Thornfell. Officially, the pass has been closed for twenty years, warded shut by the Crown against whatever it is wards shut out. Unofficially, the road breathes. Goods move along it. People move along it. And three, four nights a week, so do I.

I've asked myself why. The honest answer is that the road is the only place my body isn't bored. Out there, past the last lantern, my deadened tongue doesn't matter; the cold tastes like cold, the pine resin smells sharp even to me, and my feet know every stone as if the knowledge were poured into them before my memories were. Some nights I walk as far as the old ward-stones at the tree line—tall black monoliths carved over with script in no alphabet any Veyran scholar will admit to recognizing—and I put my hand flat against them, and they are warm. Not sun-warm. Warm like a banked fire. Warm like something alive and patient, keeping its promise.

I told no one about the walks. Telling people things is how you lose them, or how they lose you, and I have done enough losing for one lifetime.

But tonight, after the banquet, after the thorn-pin sitting in my pocket like a small accusation, I walked farther than usual. Past the ward-stones. Up the switchback called the Shepherd's Stair, where in summer the wild thyme grows so thick the whole hillside hums with bees. And there, sitting on the milestone at the top with her boots off and her stockinged feet in a stream of snowmelt, laughing—

Laughing at me. At the way I was eating a plum, juice running down my wrist.

She was gone before I drew a full breath. Not fled—*gone*, the way a candle goes out, leaving the shape of the light printed on the inside of my eyelids. Brown hair coming loose from its braid. A ward-keeper's grey-and-blue sash, the kind the royal corps wears, though I have never seen her in any corridor of the palace. A scar on her left forearm, white and old, shaped like a bird in flight.

And her voice saying, with a smile in it: *You'll ruin your shirt, Dominic.*

No one had called me Dominic on that hillside in ten years. No one calls me anything at all.

I stood on the milestone until the moon moved a hand's width across the sky, telling myself it was exhaustion, that the banquet had shaken something loose, that tasters are famously prone to strange dreams with their eyes open. But my hand remembered hers—not her face, her *hand*—the specific weight of fingers laced through mine, and grief arrived in my chest with the suddenness of a door blowing open, grief for someone whose name I could not produce, whose face was already fogging, and I understood with terrible clarity that I was mourning a person I must once have loved enough for the loss to still be armed.

Sera.

The name surfaced the way a drowned thing surfaces—slowly, unwillingly, trailing weed. Sera. I said it aloud to the empty mountainside and the mountainside did what mountainsides do, which is nothing, and kept its counsel, and kept its warmth in the ward-stones below.

I walked home in the dark along roads my feet knew better than I did, and it was only when I passed the black monoliths and laid my palm against one, as I always do, that the night turned strange for the second time.

The stone burned.

Not warm. Burning—hot as a kettle left on the fire, hot enough that I snatched my hand back and stood sucking my fingers in the frost like a child. And beneath my feet, faint but unmistakable, running up through the soles of my boots from deep in the bones of the mountain, I felt a tremor. A vibration. A sound too low for ears, like the first crack across the face of a frozen lake, or glass under slow, enormous pressure.

The ward-stones had been warm for ten years. Tonight they were dying, or waking—I couldn't tell which—and far above me, past the black shoulder of Thornfell, the sky over the pass seemed darker than the sky had any right to be, as though the stars themselves had drawn back from the ridge.

I stood in the cold with a stolen name in my mouth and a burn across my palm, and somewhere behind the peaks, in a country I had never seen but my blood apparently remembered, something ancient shifted its weight.

The quail, I thought absurdly, had only been the appetizer.
