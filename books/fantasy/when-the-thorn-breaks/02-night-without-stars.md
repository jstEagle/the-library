## 2. Night Without Stars

The night the stars went out, I was drunk on nothing, which is to say I was sober, which is to say I was Dominic Delacroix, Royal Taster, standing on the cold balcony of my quarters counting constellations like a miser counts coins he'll never spend.

Ten years of swallowing poison will do strange things to a man's hobbies. Most people collect stamps or debts. I collected stars. The Thornfell range cuts a jagged black line across our northern sky, and above it the old shepherds swore the heavens were different—denser, stranger, as if whatever burned behind those peaks had always leaked a little light into ours. I'd mapped them all. The Wagon, the Weeping Sisters, that crooked red one the smugglers called the Coal because it never quite set.

That night I counted forty-one stars over Thornfell.

Then thirty-eight.

Then none.

I want to tell you I understood immediately. I want to tell you some covenant-blood instinct roared awake in me and I knew, knew in my bones, what had happened. What actually happened is that I blinked, rubbed my eyes, blamed the wine I hadn't drunk, and stood there for a full minute being quietly, professionally wrong about the weather.

*Clouds,* I told myself. *Winter haze.*

Except clouds move. Except winter haze doesn't have edges sharp enough to cut. Except every star south of the pass still burned exactly where it should, and the darkness over Thornfell sat there like a hole punched in the world with a knife, and the more I stared at it the more my chest did something it hadn't done in ten years.

It hurt.

Not the dull ache of an old wound. Heat. A low, banked heat under the skin of my scars—the thin white ones along my forearms from the early years, when the poisoners were still calibrating their craft against me, back before everyone agreed that Dominic Delacroix could drink anything and only blink. Those scars had been dead things for a decade. Dead skin, dead nerves, dead history. Now they burned like someone had laid embers beneath them and was blowing gently on the coals.

I pressed my palm flat against the balustrade, frost biting my fingers, and watched the dark where the sky used to be, and felt my own body warm as if it recognized something I didn't.

*Hello,* said my blood, in a language I'd never been taught. *You're late.*

* * *

The sound came three hours past midnight.

I was still awake—I was always still awake; sleep and I had negotiated a distant professional relationship years ago—when the air over the palace shivered. Not wind. Shiver. The way glass shivers just before it decides to be a thousand pieces.

Then the whole horizon north of us rang.

There's no honest way to describe it except that it sounded like breaking glass, if the glass were fifty miles long and the breaking took four seconds instead of one. It came down the valley in a rolling peal, off the mountainsides, rattling in windowpanes, in teeth, in the marrow of everything alive. Dogs howled across half the city. Somewhere below, a horse screamed. And high overhead, faint as breath on a mirror, I saw it: a lattice of pale light over Thornfell, vast and geometric, flickering once, twice—

—and going out like a lamp pinched between wet fingers.

The wards. Everyone in Veyra grows up knowing the wards are there without ever seeing them, the way you know your own heartbeat. They'd held for six hundred years. Grandmother stories said a queen had paid for them with something precious. Soldier stories said they'd hold until the sun died.

They held eleven days into midwinter, and then they didn't.

My scars blazed. For one white-hot moment I swear the heat in them flared *in time* with the shattering, like two instruments tuned to the same string, and I gripped the railing and breathed through my teeth and thought, with a clarity that frightened me more than the dark did:

*I know that sound. I've known it before.*

Which was impossible. Which was nonsense. Which was also, apparently, the truest sentence I owned, sitting in my chest alongside a memory that wasn't there—a shaped absence, the way you know a tooth is missing by running your tongue over the gap.

I stood on that balcony until dawn, which never quite arrived. The sky stayed bruised and dim, the sun rising like a man climbing out of debt. Over Thornfell, where the wards had been, the darkness remained—not night-dark but *absence*-dark, a hole in the morning.

And down in the city, the bells began.

* * *

They started coming on the third day.

Refugees, I mean. First a trickle—shepherds, trappers, the ragged edge-people who live where Veyra pretends its map ends—then families, then a river of them pouring down the old trade road into the valley with carts and children and the thousand-yard stare of people who have seen the floor drop out of the world.

I was sent to the relief yards, because the crown had learned that I'm useful for more than tasting. Poison shows up in grain shipments during famines; desperate people sell anything, including arsenic. So there I stood in the gray slush outside the eastern granaries, checking sacks, checking water, checking the hollow-eyed men ladling soup, while the line of the displaced stretched away into the fog.

They brought stories the way rivers bring silt.

"The sky behind the peaks went black," a wool merchant told anyone who'd listen, his hands still shaking around a cup he wasn't drinking. "Not night. Black. Like the stars up there weren't covered, they were *eaten*."

"Fire," insisted a shepherd woman with frostbitten cheeks. "Whole valleys burning with no smoke. Light coming up out of the ground itself, gold-red, like the mountain's bleeding."

"My brother worked the high passes." This from an old man near the front of the soup line, quiet enough that I only caught it because I was close. "Sent word the wards were failing weeks ago. Said the stones were going cold. Said something was *drinking* them."

Drinking. My scars prickled under my sleeves.

A child—six, maybe seven, wrapped in a blanket that had once been somebody's good tablecloth—grabbed my wrist as I passed. Her fingers were ice and her eyes were older than her face had any right to allow.

"You smell like the fire," she said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Behind the mountain. The dying fire. You smell like it." She frowned at me with enormous seriousness, then released my wrist and let herself be pulled away by her mother, who apologized the way exhausted people do—automatically, already leaving.

I stood in the slush for a long moment, heart hammering, scars singing.

Smelled like fire. Me. The man whose entire gift was that nothing affected him—no taste, no toxin, no fear worth the name. Ten years of being the crown's blank page, and now a refugee child was reading writing on me I couldn't see.

* * *

That night I did the thing I probably shouldn't have done, which is to say I did what I always do: I went walking.

There's a network of old smuggler trails threading the hills east of the city, and I've walked them for years, alone, in the dark hours, though I've never managed to explain to myself why. Duty, I told myself—scouting, vigilance, a taster keeping his tongue sharp. But honestly? A man can only be numb for so long before he starts looking for the place the feeling went. On those trails, with no one watching, I sometimes pretended I remembered something worth remembering. A woman's voice. Green light through leaves. A name I kept almost saying.

Sera. Always almost Sera.

I was three miles out, on the ridge where the trail crests above the tree line, when I stopped walking without deciding to stop.

Below me, the Thornfell passes lay open in the starless dark. And they were glowing.

Faintly. Flickering. Along the broken line of the shattered wards, in the rock itself, seams of ember-light pulsed like veins under skin—dying pulses, irregular, guttering, but unmistakably *alive*, or recently alive, or trying desperately not to die. The whole mountain range looked like a great beast lying in the dark with its fires going out, one coal at a time.

And my scars answered.

Heat rolled up my forearms, my chest, the old faded burn along my ribs, each scar lighting with warmth as if the mountain and I shared a pulse. I pulled back my sleeve and in the dark I would have sworn—would swear now, under oath, before any court—that the thin white lines of my oldest wounds glowed faint gold.

"Covenant," whispered a memory I didn't have, in a voice I couldn't place. *Covenant-son.*

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough for the cold to find me anyway, numbing everything except the places that burned. Long enough for the ember-light below to fade another shade closer to ash.

When I finally turned back toward the city, the palace towers rose black against the bruised sky, and I thought about the queen I'd saved at the banquet, and the crown that fed me poisons and called it honor, and the mother who walked into Thornfell when I was nine and never came back, and how everyone had told me she abandoned us, and how I had believed them because believing was easier than the alternative.

She hadn't gone into those mountains despite the danger.

Somehow, with absolute certainty, the way you know your own heartbeat—I knew she had gone home.

Behind me, far down in the valley, a watch horn sounded from the eastern gate. Then another. Then a dozen, relayed along the walls, urgent and repeating: riders approaching under a ward-keeper's banner, come to investigate the fallen border. Come, I would learn soon enough, bearing orders and oaths and a face my mind had forgotten but my blood, apparently, had not.

I started down the trail toward the lights of the city, my scars burning like a second heartbeat, like a summons, like a hand reaching across ten years of silence to take mine.

*Hold on,* the heat seemed to say. *We're coming.*
