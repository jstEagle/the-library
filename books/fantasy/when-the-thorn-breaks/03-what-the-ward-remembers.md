## 3. What the Ward Remembers

The orders came at dawn, sealed in grey wax, and they were strange in three ways.

First, they named me by my full title—Sera Vane, Third Keeper of the Royal Wards, Sworn of the Inner Circle—which was excessive even for the chancellery, who loved titles the way misers love coins: hoarded, counted, never spent.

Second, they sent me alone. A fallen border ward is not a one-woman job. It's a dozen keepers, a survey team, and a chaplain for whatever's left when you're done. Sending one woman into the Thornfell passes in midwinter was either an insult or a death sentence, and I couldn't decide which.

Third—and this is the one I still turn over at night, like a stone with something underneath it—the orders contained a clause. *Should any person of interest be encountered within the affected region,* it read, *the Keeper shall proceed without reference to prior acquaintance.*

Prior acquaintance. As if I had any. I'd never been north of the river garrisons. My whole career had been spent inside city walls, mending the small domestic magics that keep a capital running—warding the treasury against thieves, the granaries against rot, the palace against everything. I knew Thornfell the way I knew the moon. From below. From far away.

So why did the chancellery think I might meet someone I knew?

I packed anyway. You don't argue with grey wax.

* * *

The pass was wrong before I ever reached it.

Wards have a texture, if you've worked them long enough. A healthy ward feels like taut silk under your fingers—a hum, a resistance, a sense of something holding. Even a broken ward has texture. It frays. It unravels in patterns, and the pattern tells you what broke it: force leaves ragged ends, neglect leaves thin ones, sabotage leaves clean cuts.

What I found at the Thornfell line had no ends at all.

The wards hadn't broken. They'd been *drunk*. That's the only word I have for it. The magic was simply gone—not dispersed, not shattered, but consumed, the way a candle isn't destroyed when it burns, only changed into light and heat somewhere else. I knelt in the snow at the border stone and pressed my palm to where the ward-anchor should have been singing, and I felt nothing. Not silence. Silence has weight. This was absence, clean as a scraped plate.

And beneath the absence, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something warm.

I did what any keeper would do. I took out my kit, unspooled a thread of testing-silk, and set a probe-spell to map the residue.

The spell came back wrong.

Not failed—wrong. It returned a shape I recognized, the way you recognize a face across a crowded market before you know whose face it is. A signature. Resonance. Whatever had fed on these wards had left a fingerprint, and my magic knew that fingerprint, leaned toward it like a plant toward sun, and then—

Then I got a headache like a nail driven behind my eyes, and the resonance was gone, and I was kneeling in the snow with blood on my lip and no idea what I'd just touched.

Here is the thing about being a royal ward-keeper: we are trained to notice our own minds the way sailors notice weather. Memory-work is part of our craft. We know what a forgetting looks like from the inside, because we've performed enough of them. And what I felt, in that moment on my knees in the snow, was the smooth edge of something removed. Not a hole—holes are rough, and grief gets into them, and you feel the shape of what's missing. This was seamless. Polished. A wall painted to look exactly like the sky above it.

Someone had edited me.

I sat down hard on the border stone and laughed, which frightened the crows off a dead pine, and thought: *well, Sera, either you're going mad, or the crown has been in your head, and honestly the odds aren't great on which is worse.*

* * *

I followed the warmth.

That wasn't in the orders either, but the orders had stopped making sense around the time my own mind stopped making sense, so I figured I was operating outside their jurisdiction now. The warmth led up the old trade road—or what had been the trade road, before the wars closed it and the smugglers adopted it. Snow had buried the ruts, but the wards along the waystation markers still flickered, dying things, guttering like lamps out of oil. Each one leaned the same direction. Toward the mountains. Toward Emberhold, if half the refugee stories were true, and refugees' stories are usually half true, which is more than you can say for chancellery dispatches.

I found him at the second waystation.

He was standing at the ruined hearth with his back to me, a tall man in a plain traveller's coat, dark-haired, snow gathering on his shoulders because he'd been there long enough to stop caring. He was looking at the dead hearth-fire the way people look at graves.

And every ward-thread in my body stood up and sang.

I don't have better words than that. Keepers carry a little ambient magic the way smiths carry burn scars—it's soaked into us, it's part of the skin. And all of mine turned toward this stranger like compass needles finding north, humming a chord I didn't know I knew, resonating with that same fingerprint I'd touched at the border stone. The one that had made my nose bleed.

My magic knew him.

My magic knew him the way you know a song you can't name—you hear two notes and your throat tightens and you're humming the rest before you've decided to. He turned around, and he had a face like a closed door, handsome in a worn sort of way, and he looked at me and said:

"You're a long way from the palace, keeper."

And I said, "You're a long way from anywhere," which was clever of me, considering I couldn't breathe.

Because here is what happened in the space of that sentence, in the time it took the snow to fall between us: I looked at his face and I grieved. Not recognized—*grieved*. A wave of it, sudden and enormous and completely unaccountable, grief with a specific shape, the shape of a voice saying my name in the dark, of someone's shoulder against mine on a wall at night, of laughing until it hurt over something I could not for the life of me remember. Ten years of mourning arrived all at once, addressed to a total stranger, and I stood there in the snow receiving it like a widow at a door.

"Are you all right?" he asked, and his voice did something to me too. Familiarity like a knife going into an old wound that had healed over so smoothly I'd never known it was there.

"I don't know," I said, which was the most honest thing I'd said to anyone in years. "Who are you?"

Something crossed his face—quick, guarded, gone. "Dominic," he said. "Dominic Delacroix."

Delacroix. The name meant nothing to me. The name meant nothing, and my hands were shaking.

"You knew me," I said. "Before. Didn't you."

"No." Too fast. Then, quieter, watching me with those careful eyes: "Why do you ask?"

"Because my magic remembers you," I said, "and I very much doubt it's the only part of me that does."

* * *

We talked by the dead hearth while the light died, and I'll record here what I learned, though I believed perhaps half of it.

He was the royal taster. Ten years swallowing poison for the queen, which explained the stillness of him, the way he held himself like a man who has learned that flinching costs extra. He'd been sent to scout the pass after the stars went out. He claimed—casually, the way you mention a scar—that he'd walked these roads at night for years, alone, chasing memories of a mother who'd gone through the mountains and never come back.

"And you?" he asked. "Why does the crown send one keeper to a dead border?"

I told him about the orders. The clause. *Without reference to prior acquaintance.* His face went very carefully blank, which is its own kind of answer.

"They erased you," he said finally. "Or ordered it done. Someone decided you were a liability and cleaned the ledger."

"People don't get erased," I said. "It's not—it's forbidden. Consent statutes. The Keeper's Guild would—"

"The Keeper's Guild answers to the same chancellery," he said, gently, the way you tell someone their house is burning. "You're a sworn officer of the Inner Circle, aren't you? They don't need your consent. They need plausible deniability, and a clause in grey wax."

I wanted to argue. I was raised by the crown—orphaned into it, trained by it, shaped by it the way a tree is shaped by a wall it grows against. Arguing with the crown was like arguing with weather. But I kept hearing my own voice say *my magic remembers you*, and I kept feeling that polished seam behind my eyes, and I thought about all the smooth walls in my mind I'd never thought to knock on, because why would you? Who knocks on the sky?

"What was I to you?" I asked.

He looked into the dead hearth for a long time. Long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer.

"I'm not sure I should tell you," he said. "If they find out the erasure didn't take—if they think you're compromised—they'll send someone to finish the job. And the second cutting is never as clean as the first."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's not."

But when he said it, he looked at me—really looked, the way you look at something you've been rationing yourself—and for one unguarded moment the closed door of his face swung open, and what was behind it was so lonely and so familiar that I had to turn away first.

* * *

I should report back. That's the discipline of it; that's the whole architecture of my life. Findings go to the chancellery, findings go into ledgers, ledgers become decisions made by people who have never been cold. And my findings, properly written, would read: *Resonance signature at fallen border matches subject D.D., royal taster. Subject displays anomalous connection to ward consumption. Recommend detention and full examination.*

I have the words. I've written harder sentences than that.

Instead I'm sitting in the waystation loft with my kit open and my oath-scroll unrolled beside me—the grey wax, the clauses, the beautiful seamless machinery of my loyalty—and I'm looking at the clause again. *Without reference to prior acquaintance.* Written weeks ago, before the stars went out, before the wards fell. Which means someone knew. Someone knew what I'd find in these mountains, and knew who I'd find, and decided in advance what I was permitted to feel about it.

They didn't erase a memory. They armed a forgetting and aimed it at me.

Below me, in the ruins of the common room, Dominic Delacroix is sleeping by a fire he coaxed from wet wood, because of course he can coax fire from wet wood, because nothing about this man adds up except the way my magic leans toward him like a tide. And I find that my hand keeps drifting to the seam behind my eyes, knocking, knocking, and tonight—for the first time—I'm not asking whether the wall will break.

I'm asking what the crown is so afraid I'll remember.

Tomorrow we walk deeper into the pass, he says. Tomorrow we find out what fed on the wards. And I am going with him—not because the orders permit it, but because my magic remembers what my mind cannot, and for the first time in my careful, walled, well-kept life,

I trust it more than I trust the grey wax.
