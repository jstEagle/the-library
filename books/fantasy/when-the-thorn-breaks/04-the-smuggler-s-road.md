## 4. The Smuggler's Road

The crown gave me the pass the way it gave me everything else—wrapped in duty, tied with a ribbon of suspicion, and priced well above what I could afford to pay.

"You'll scout Thornfell," Chancellor Verre said, not looking up from his ledgers. "Determine whether the ward collapse is natural, sabotage, or invasion. Report within a fortnight."

"And if it's invasion?"

"Then you'll report that too. Preferably before you're invaded yourself." He finally glanced at me, and I saw the arithmetic behind his eyes: *the taster is expendable, and conveniently expendable near the border.* "You'll take an escort."

"I work alone."

"Not anymore. Not after you pulled poison from the queen's cup in front of half the court. You've made yourself visible, Dominic. Visible things need protection." He slid a sealed writ across the desk. "Ward-Keeper Sera Ilvane. She knows the old border magic better than anyone we have left."

I took the writ without reading it. My hands were steady. They always were—that was the first thing they'd trained into me, steadiness, back when I was nine years old and swallowing my first dose of nightshade extract under a master taster's eye.

What they never trained out of me was the way my chest went tight when I heard her name.

---

She was waiting at the north gate before dawn, wrapped in a grey traveling cloak, her ward-keeper's satchel slung crosswise like a soldier's bandolier. Frost silvered the cobbles. She looked at me the way people look at a word they've forgotten but almost remember.

"Taster Delacroix," she said.

"Ward-Keeper Ilvane."

We stood there for a moment in the cold, two strangers performing introductions they both somehow already knew by heart. Then she frowned—small, quick, like a crack in porcelain—and shook it off.

"You know this road?" she asked as we passed through the gate.

"Blindfolded. Sometimes with one."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be."

* * *

The smuggler trails began where the king's road ended, which is to say they began where honesty ended. For three days we climbed through pine and shadow along paths no surveyor had ever charted—goat tracks widened by generations of men who moved goods the crown preferred not to see moving. Brandy, silk, fugitives, once, memorably, a shipment of live hawks. The roads remembered all of it. I remembered most of it.

Sera walked behind me, and every few hours I caught her watching my feet rather than the trail ahead.

"You keep stepping where there aren't stones," she said on the second morning, at a switchback above the valley fog. "There's nothing there. No marker, no cairn. But you step exactly where the footing is solid."

"Ten years of walking them."

"No." She said it quietly, not arguing, just... correcting. "That's not practice. Practice doesn't put your foot precisely on a buried root you couldn't possibly see." She knelt, brushed snow from the ground, and revealed nothing—a bare patch of frozen earth. "Something remembers these paths for you."

"Maybe I'm lucky."

"Maybe." She rose. Her eyes lingered on me a moment too long, and again came that frown, that hairline fracture. "Do you ever get the feeling you've forgotten something important? Not mislaid it—*forgotten* it. On purpose. By someone else's order?"

I kept walking. If I answered that question honestly, I'd have to answer the ten others underneath it, so instead I said, "Careful here. Ice over the stream crossing."

"There's no stream."

"There will be by noon. Snowmelt." And I stepped across the dry bed without breaking stride, because I knew it would be there, the same way I knew which stones lied about their footing and which innkeepers lied about their brandy. Some knowledge you earn. Some knowledge, apparently, is left in you like furniture by tenants who've gone.

* * *

On the third day the forest changed.

It wasn't dramatic. Forests rarely are, when they're dying. But the pines went silent—no jays, no squirrels, none of the small commerce of a living wood—and the frost on the branches had a grey cast, like ash sifted over sugar. Sera felt it before I did. She stopped mid-stride and pressed her palm flat against the trunk of a spruce.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, no."

"What?"

"The wards didn't break." She turned to look at me, and her face had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with cold. "Dominic—the wards weren't destroyed. Look at the trees. Look at the *ground*. There's no rubble of magic, no shatter-pattern, no residue of violence. There's—" she searched for the word, and hated the word when she found it—"*feeding*. Something fed on the wards. Drank them down to the roots."

"Can wards be eaten?"

"Everything can be eaten. That's what wards are *for*—they're meals offered to hungry things, bargains struck with fire. Someone stopped paying the bargain and let the hunger run loose." She scrubbed her hand on her cloak as though the tree had stained it. "This isn't sabotage, Delacroix. This is debt collection."

We climbed toward the pass proper through a landscape growing stranger by the hour. Snow fell upward in thin spirals where no wind should carry it. Our shadows lagged half a step behind us, then caught up, then pointed the wrong direction entirely. And everywhere, faintly, under everything, a warmth—not sunlight-warmth, something older and more interior, like standing near a banked oven. My scars began to ache. Not the dull winter ache they'd carried since childhood, but a low heat, a banked coal of feeling, and I pressed my hand against my ribs and thought: *this is what pain is supposed to feel like, isn't it? I'd nearly forgotten.*

"You're glowing," Sera said suddenly.

"I'm what?"

"Faintly. Your hands. When you touch the stone." She caught my wrist before I could protest and turned my palm toward the light. Sure enough: a shimmer beneath the skin, gold-amber, fading even as we watched. "That's covenant-script. Old fire-language. It only shows in bloodlines that—"

She stopped. Her grip tightened on my wrist. The frown returned, deeper this time, and I watched something move behind her eyes like a fish under ice—some memory circling, testing the surface, unable to break through.

"Bloodlines that what?" I asked.

"I don't know." Her voice went strange. "But I know I used to know. Isn't that the cruelest thing? To feel the shape of knowing and find nothing inside it?" She released my hand as if burned—which, given the circumstances, was perhaps literal. "We should reach the ward-stones by dusk. Stay close."

* * *

The great ward-stones of Thornfell stood where the smuggler road crested the pass, twelve monoliths in a broken ring, each taller than two men. In my earliest memories—they came in fragments, always fragments—those stones had glowed faint blue-green at dusk, the color of safe harbors. Now they were black as burnt bread, and warm to the touch, and humming.

Not silent. Humming. A note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the fillings of old wounds. Sera circled the ring with her satchel instruments out, muttering measurements I didn't understand, and I watched the light drain from her face measurement by measurement.

"They've been drained for weeks," she said finally. "Months, maybe. Slowly. Carefully. Whatever did this wanted them *empty*, not dead. There's a difference. A dead ward is a door blown off its hinges. An empty ward is a—" she swallowed— "a larder, waiting to be restocked. Or drawn from again."

"Drawn from where?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She looked up at the dark sky—starless now for weeks, the whole vault of heaven a smooth grey ceiling like the inside of a closed lid. "And here's the worse one: whatever fed on these wards drank *light*. Starlight, ward-light, hearth-light. Everything bright. Which means whatever's on the other side of this pass isn't invading, Dominic. It's starving. And starving things don't stop at borders."

I put my hand on the nearest ward-stone, and the hum climbed into my bones, and my scars blazed, and for one vertiginous moment I saw—

—a woman's hands cupped around a flame too large for them, laughing—

—a mountain lit from within like a lantern—

—my mother, saying words I couldn't hear in a language I somehow understood: *keep the fire, little ember, keep the fire*—

I came back to myself on my knees in the snow, gasping, Sera's hands on my shoulders, her face very close and very frightened.

"Dominic. *Dominic.* What did you see?"

"A kitchen," I lied, because the truth was a country I hadn't entered yet. "My mother's kitchen. It's nothing."

"That was not nothing. Your skin was writing." She pulled back, hugged her arms around herself. "Your skin was *writing*, and I could almost read it, and I hate that I could almost read it, because I think I learned to read it once, from someone—" Her breath caught. She pressed the heel of her hand against her forehead hard, as though trying to shake loose something lodged there. "Why do I keep almost remembering you?"

"Sera—"

"Don't." She held up a warning finger. "Don't say anything kind right now. I'm holding myself together with procedure and bad tea and I cannot afford kindness."

So I said nothing kind. I helped her up instead, and we made camp in the lee of the dead ward-stone, and neither of us slept, and the starless sky pressed down on us like a held breath.

* * *

Near dawn, the sprite found us.

Or perhaps I should say it *recognized* us, because recognition seemed to be the last thing it had left. It came stumbling out of the treeline—a creature the size of a child's doll, roughly humanoid, its body a lattice of cooling embers, dim orange light pulsing through cracks in its charcoal skin. Each pulse was slower than the last. It dragged itself toward our fire on hands that flaked as they moved, leaving little drifts of ash behind it like a snail-trail of dying.

Sera was on her feet instantly, satchel open. "Ember-sprite. They're ward-tenders, hearth-spirits—they shouldn't be outside Emberhold, they *can't* survive outside—" She dropped to her knees as it reached us. "Its core's nearly spent. It's been walking for—weeks, maybe. Looking for something."

The sprite ignored her. Its eyes—two points of guttering orange light in a face of cooling char—fixed on me with terrible precision, and it smiled, and its smile cracked its face like drying mud.

"Covenant-son," it rasped. The voice was small and vast at once, a whisper and a bellows. "Little flame. Keeper's get. You walk the old roads. You step where the earth remembers. We felt you coming for ten years—ten years of swallowed fire, little ember, burning slow in the dark of Veyra, and we wondered who would come, and now—" the light in its chest stuttered— "now you come, and the wards are empty, and the hearth is failing, and the dragon—"

"What dragon?" Sera whispered.

"—the last dragon gutters, and the covenant breaks, and only covenant-blood can reforge it, only the keeper's son, only—" The sprite reached up with a crumbling hand and touched my cheek, and where it touched, warmth bloomed, deep and familiar, the warmth of a kitchen I had never consciously remembered. "Carry it home, covenant-son. Carry the spark home before the last light goes out. She waited for you. She *waited*—"

"Who waited?" I grabbed its shoulders, ash sifting between my fingers. "*Who waited?*"

But the light behind its eyes was going out even as it tried to answer, pulsing slower, slower, a lantern in a closing fist. With its final flicker it leaned close, and its voice was almost tender:

"She never stopped setting a place for you at the table."

Then it crumbled. Not collapsed—*crumbled*, gently, entirely, into a drift of fine grey ash that the pre-dawn wind lifted and scattered across the snow, across the dead ward-stones, across the border of a realm that had just become my problem, my inheritance, my unpayable debt.

For a long moment neither of us moved. The ash settled. Somewhere far below the pass, in the world we'd come from, dawn was arriving over a kingdom that still believed its stars would come back on their own.

"Well," Sera said at last, very quietly. Her hands were shaking. So were mine, but mine were also, impossibly, warm. "I suppose we'd better go meet this dragon."

I looked east, past the ring of dead stones, to where the pass descended into darkness—and saw, deep in the black, a single point of amber light. Faint. Guttering. Waiting.

"Yes," I said, and my voice sounded strange to my own ears, like someone else's voice wearing mine. "I suppose we'd better."

I started down into Emberhold carrying more than I'd packed for—ten years of numbness beginning, at last, to thaw into something that hurt far worse than cold.

Responsibility, it turns out, tastes like everything I'd ever swallowed, all at once, and none of it numbed.
