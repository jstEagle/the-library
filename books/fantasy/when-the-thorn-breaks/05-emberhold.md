## 5. Emberhold

### Dominic

The pass didn't end. That was my first thought, somewhere between the ninth false summit and the moment the sky stopped making sense: that Thornfell had simply decided to go on forever, and I had been a fool to bring anything but a coffin.

Then the mountain opened.

There was no door. There was a seam in the rock where the frost fell away like a curtain drawn back, and beyond it the air changed—warmer, thicker, tasting of woodsmoke and iron and something older underneath, something like the smell of a struck flint in a room that has been dark for centuries. The ember-sprite's ash was still on my gloves. I kept flexing my fingers, as if I could rub the word *covenant-son* off my skin along with it.

"You're doing the thing," Sera said behind me.

"What thing."

"The thing where you make a joke so you don't have to feel whatever enormous feeling is currently trying to climb out of your chest." She came up beside me at the seam, her ward-lantern guttering in the new air. "I've watched you do it for two weeks. You did it when the sprite died. You're about to do it now."

"I'm not about to do it now."

"Go on, then. Say something about how at least the view will be nice if we die."

I looked at her. She looked back, one eyebrow up, entirely too pleased with herself, and I realized with genuine irritation that she'd learned to read me faster than anyone in ten years of Veyran courtiers who were paid to do it. "At least," I said, "the view will be nice if we die."

"There it is." And she stepped through before me, which took more nerve than anything else she'd done on this road, and I followed because following her had apparently become the organizing principle of my life without my having been consulted.

* * *

Emberhold does not look like what the stories say. The stories—what few smugglers' songs bothered to mention it—describe a kingdom of fire, all obsidian and lava-falls, a hell with good manners. What we found instead was a valley under a ceiling of stone so high it might have been sky, lit from within by veins of ember running through the rock like blood through skin. Terraced fields climbed the walls, growing something pale and luminous instead of grain. Water ran in channels, steaming gently. Everything was dim. Everything was warm. It was like walking into the inside of a banked hearth, and after three weeks of starless winter dark it felt less like another realm and more like being swallowed by something patient.

"It's dying," Sera said quietly. Her lantern hand trembled; her ward-sense was reading things mine couldn't. "Dominic—the whole wardwork of this place, the light itself—it's guttering. Like a candle in a draft."

She was right. Once I knew to look, I saw it everywhere: ember-veins that pulsed strong and then stuttered, fields whose glow had gone gray at the edges, houses with windows dark where others still burned. A beautiful room with someone forgetting to breathe in it.

They came out to meet us before we reached the first terrace. Not soldiers—there didn't seem to be enough of them left to spare soldiers—but a dozen figures in ash-gray robes, carrying lanterns that held actual flame, actual fire, and they looked at our little traveling party the way starving people look at bread. An old woman stood at their center, small and straight-backed, with white hair braided with copper wire and eyes the exact color of cooling coals.

She looked at me and stopped walking.

The whole column stopped with her, twelve lanterns swaying, and nobody said anything for long enough that I started composing the joke—I'm told I have a very trustworthy face—and Sera's elbow found my ribs before I could spend it.

"You have her hands," the old woman said. Her voice was rough, like a file on iron. "The way you hold them. Open. Ready to catch something falling."

"I don't—" I began.

"Your mother," she said, "held her hands the same way. When she carried the flame."

The word went through me sideways, the way a blade goes between ribs if it knows where to look. Ten years I'd carried the question of my mother like a stone sewn into my coat—Maren Delacroix, who walked into the Thornfell passes one autumn morning with a pack of dried plums and never walked out, whom the crown called a deserter and my father called dead and I, privately, in the hours when taster's numbness wore thin, called *gone ahead of me.* I had followed her trail here without ever saying so aloud, not even to myself.

"She was called Maerin here," the old woman said. "Fire-keeper of the Third Hearth. She went into Veyra carrying a covenant-child under her heart, and the covenant went with her, and none of us blamed her, because a dying land has no right to ask a mother to stay." Her coal-colored eyes didn't blink. "But we grieved her. And now she has sent you back."

"My name is Dominic Delacroix," I said, because names were the last currency I trusted. "I'm a poison-taster. For the crown of Veyra. That's all I am."

"That is what you were *paid* to be." She smiled, and it was a terrible kind smile, full of pity. "Come and see what you are."

* * *

They led us down through terraces and tunnels to the heart of Emberhold, which turned out to be exactly what the smuggler's song promised and nothing like it at all: a vast cavern where the ceiling opened onto a shaft of darkness, and coiled around a raised hearth of black stone lay a dragon.

Ashvane. I knew the name the way you know a word in a language you don't speak—hearing it all your life without understanding it until suddenly you do. She was smaller than the legends claimed. That was the horror of it. Legends need dragons the size of weather; Ashvane was perhaps the length of a fishing boat, wings folded like a mourner's arms, scales gone dull as old charcoal except along her throat, where a faint red light rose and fell with her breathing. Before her, on the hearth-stone, burned the hearth-flame—a fire no bigger than a cooking fire, gold-white and trembling, throwing shadows that reached every wall of the cavern and beyond it, into the veins of the whole realm. Every ember-light in Emberhold answered it. I watched a vein overhead flicker when the flame flickered, half a heartbeat later, and understood that I was looking at a heart.

And the heart was going out.

"The wards of Thornfell were never a wall," the old woman—Haleth, she was called—said beside me. "They were a vein. Emberhold fed light up through the mountains into your sky, and your sky fed warmth back down to us. Reciprocity. Covenant. Your kings' ancestors sealed it with ours, blood to blood, and for nine hundred years both realms ate at the same table." She paused. "Ten years ago the feeding slowed. Three months ago it stopped. Something on the far side began drinking instead of sharing. The stars went out because the stars were ours, taster. You have been living under borrowed fire your whole life and never once tasted it in your food."

Sera made a small sound. I glanced at her; her face had gone the particular gray of a woman doing arithmetic she hates. Ward-keepers' arithmetic. *How long has the crown known? What has it been taking?* I'd have asked her later. There was a later. I was beginning to fight for the assumption of a later.

"Why am I immune?" I asked. It came out steadier than I felt. "That's what this is about, isn't it. Why you brought me to the dragon instead of a bed."

"Because immunity cannot be trained." Haleth's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "Any Veyran apothecary worth salt knows tolerance can be built, but never against everything, never against the unnameable poisons, never against *venom of the deep wards*—and yet you drank them all and lived. Because you are not only Veyran, child. Your mother fed you covenant-blood at her own breast. You are a hearth that walks. The flame would know you anywhere."

Ashvane's head lifted.

It was not a dramatic motion. That was the thing I'd remember—how quiet it was, the slow turn of a skull the size of an oven, eyes opening like twin banked coals, and settling on me with an attention so total that every other gaze I'd ever endured in banquet halls and throne rooms shrank to nothing. She breathed in. The hearth-flame leaned toward her as if pulled. And when she spoke, her voice was smoke given grammar, arriving not through my ears but through my scars—all those years of swallowing ruin, all those places in me that had learned to burn and keep burning.

***Covenant-son,*** she said. ***You carry her silence. Will you carry her fire?***

And there it was. The belonging. Offered plain and whole: a mother explained, a body explained, ten years of numbness retroactively becoming inheritance instead of damage. Somewhere to stand. Somewhere I was not a tool with a tongue.

It should have felt like coming home. Instead I heard my own voice, wry and thin, saying the true thing for once:

"With respect—you're asking a man who's spent his life tasting poison for strangers to hand over his body one more time. Give me one reason this table feeds me back."

The flame guttered. Ashvane's eyes held mine, ancient and exhausted and honest.

***Because,*** she said, ***we are too weak to use you. Only to need you.***

Behind me, Sera exhaled like someone breaking surface. And above us, far up through the mountain's throat, faint and wrong, came the sound of horns—Veyran horns, three notes, the crown's marching call.

Haleth's coal-eyes narrowed. "So," she said. "Your kings have noticed the light is missing."

### Sera

I had been raised on maps. Lord Cassel taught me that a map is a promise: every line means someone drew it, every border means someone guards it, and everything outside the ink is either empty or enemy. Standing in the heart of Emberhold with a dragon's attention resting on Dominic like sunlight, I understood for the first time that my whole life had been lived inside someone else's map—and that whoever drew the borders I was trained to defend had left this entire realm off the page on purpose.

Because the crown knew. I was almost certain of it already, and the certainty was growing teeth. Wards don't fail overnight. Someone in Veyra had watched the flow reverse for ten years—maybe longer—and done nothing, or worse than nothing. And now the horns.

I looked at Dominic. He stood in the dragon-fire light with his hands open at his sides—her hands, Haleth had said, ready to catch something falling—and he had just asked a dying dragon for honesty and received it, and he hadn't run. He'd joked first, because he always jokes first, but he hadn't run. I thought: *I burned my oath-scroll on a mountain road and I have not once regretted it, and I do not know why I love this man, and I am beginning to suspect that not knowing is the least of what was taken from me.*

The horns sounded again, closer.

"Dominic," I said. "We need to talk about what's coming down that pass. Because I know those signals. And the third one means they're not here to negotiate."

He turned to me, and in the guttering light his face did something I'd never seen it do: it stopped performing.

"Then tell me," he said, "everything you know."

Above us, the hearth-flame shuddered, and the last dragon of Emberhold drew one long breath, preparing—for war, or for worse.
