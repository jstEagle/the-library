## 6. The Crown's Price

I had burned my first oath-scroll at nineteen, as a formality—a practice scroll with my name written in fading ink, consumed in the ward-house brazier while Master Cassel watched and pronounced me *suitably committed*. It was a small fire. A tidy one. I remember thinking that oaths must be very light things if they burned that easily.

I was wrong about that. I have since done the research.

* * *

The horns came up the valley at dawn, three notes repeated—the royal summons, the sound every ward-keeper is trained to answer with her spine straight and her doubts folded away like winter cloaks. I stood on the ridge above the Emberfolk terraces with ash still in my hair from the crossing and watched the column come down the smuggler road I'd walked two days before, when it had been only a road and not an invasion route.

Two hundred soldiers. Siege wagons. And at the head of them, riding a gray courser like he was riding to a garden party, Lord Cassel, First Warden of Veyra, the man who taught me to read wards the way priests teach children to read scripture.

"He brought the *army*," I said.

Beside me, Dominic didn't answer right away. He was watching the column with an expression I was learning to hate—not fear exactly, but the flat arithmetic of a man counting how many people he'd have to watch die.

"Of course he did," he said finally. "You don't send a letter for a treasure. You send a treasury."

"You could go down there," I said. "Talk to him. Explain—"

"Sera." He turned to look at me, and there it was again, that terrible gentleness he wore like armor worn inside-out, all its sharp edges facing himself. "The last time your crown sent someone to explain something, they erased you. What do you think they'll do to a realm full of strangers who are sitting on a dragon?"

He was right. I hated that too. Lately there was a lot of hating going on and very little of it was productive.

* * *

Cassel received us in the great hall of the Emberfolk—which is to say, he sat in a borrowed chair under a ceiling carved with a thousand years of Emberhold's history and behaved as though none of it were real. The Emberfolk elders stood along the walls in silence, watching the Veyran banners the way you watch water rising around your ankles.

Dominic stood beside me. That was already a problem. Cassel's eyes found him within the first minute and stayed there, patient and cold, a man filing information for later use.

"Lady Oyelaran." Cassel rose. He had aged well, my mentor—silver at the temples now, the same voice that had talked me through my first ward-collapse, warm as banked coals and about as safe to sleep on. "You were meant to send a report."

"The passes were closed, my lord. The wards fell. I judged a firsthand account more valuable than parchment."

"And what did you find?" His gaze slid past me to the hearth at the hall's far end—to the light of it, which even guttering was unlike anything Veyra had ever produced. Even dying, Ashvane's flame made our ward-lanterns look like candles drawn by a child. "Ah," Cassel said softly. "Yes. I see what you found."

"My lord, the situation is delicate. The realm behind these mountains is dying. The fire isn't a weapon, it's a life support system. If we take it—"

"*We* are not taking anything." He smiled at the elders, a diplomat's smile, and lowered his voice for me alone. "Sera. Walk with me."

We walked. Out onto the terrace, into the cold, away from listening ears—or so he believed. Dominic had told me about the old smuggler listening-holes cut into Emberhold's stone, and I had made sure we stood near one.

"I'll be plain," Cassel said. "The stars went out over half the kingdom. The border wards failed. Crops are failing in the northern provinces because whatever fed those wards is gone, and the people are calling it the Crown's failure. The Queen needs a victory, or at least a story. A weapon of light—" he gestured back toward the hall, toward the hearth-glow spilling from its windows, "—is both. We take the flame, we study it, we bottle what can be bottled, and Veyra survives the dark. That is the mission. You will facilitate it."

"It belongs to someone," I said. "To an entire realm of someones. My lord, they're *people*, they have children, they—"

"They are a hidden nation of no strategic record, behind a border that officially does not exist." His tone never changed. That was the horror of Cassel; he delivered atrocities in the voice of a man recommending wine. "Sera, you were raised in the ward-houses. You know what the crown spends and what it protects. Sentiment is not a line item."

"No," I said. "It's just the thing the wards were built out of, originally. Before we forgot."

He looked at me then, really looked, and something flickered across his face—recalculation, maybe, or disappointment. "You've been out here eleven days," he said. "Eleven days among strangers and smugglers and—" his eyes drifted, deliberately, toward where Dominic waited at the terrace door, "—and distractions. I think the mountain air has affected you. You'll ride back with the column. There will be a review of your conduct."

"My conduct."

"Your *judgment*," he amended, gently, which was worse. "It seems compromised."

And there it was: the choice, laid out on frozen stone with all the subtlety of a drawn blade. Come home. Be forgiven. Forget the man your own crown made you forget once already—they'd do it again, and this time they wouldn't leave the cracks where the love leaked through.

Or stay. And be nothing. An oath-breaker. A traitor to the only family I'd ever been permitted.

"You ordered my memory cut once," I said quietly. "Ten years ago. I don't know why. I don't know what I was to him." I nodded toward the doorway, where Dominic stood pretending not to listen through a wall that was absolutely telling him everything. "But I've seen the shape of the hole you left in me, my lord. And I've decided I don't want it back."

"Sera—"

"You want the fire. The Emberfolk would sooner let it die than hand it to an army, and the dragon would sooner burn the mountain down. So you'll take it over their ashes, or you'll fail. Those are your options, and I won't help you with either."

For a moment—one long, cold moment—I thought he might order me seized on the spot. His hand actually moved toward the signet ring that commanded the ward-collars. I felt my own magic rise to meet it, unbidden, the old patterns singing in my blood, and realized with strange clarity that I would fight him. My teacher. My almost-father. I would fight him on this terrace and probably lose, and I would do it anyway.

Then Dominic stepped out onto the terrace, and the whole calculus changed, because Cassel looked at him and I watched recognition move across my mentor's face like ice cracking on a pond.

"Delacroix," Cassel said. "So *you're* where she's been hiding."

"She hasn't been hiding," Dominic said. "She's been remembering."

Something passed between them that I couldn't read—a history I only knew the edges of, ten years of erased correspondence. Cassel studied Dominic the way he studied unstable wards: assessing load, estimating failure.

"You should come home too, boy," Cassel said, almost kindly. "Whatever you think you are out here, the crown has uses for you. It always did."

"I know," Dominic said. "That was rather the problem."

* * *

They gave me until nightfall to decide. Cassel was thorough if nothing else—he posted guards at the terrace doors, "for my protection," and had my travel kit carried to the soldiers' camp, a small domestic violence dressed as courtesy.

I sat in the guest chamber the Emberfolk offered me, which was warm in a way no Veyran room had ever been—warm from below, from the deep veins of the mountain, warm like being held—and I took out my oath-scroll.

Every ward-keeper carries it. A scroll of vellum and living ink, renewed at each promotion, signed in blood-ink that answers to the crown's seal. Mine had been rewritten four times. Each renewal, the ink drank a little deeper into the vellum. Each renewal, I'd felt it drink a little deeper into me. I had told myself for ten years that the gaps in my memory were ordinary wear, the natural erosion of a demanding profession. Sitting there with the scroll open across my knees, feeling it hum against my skin like a caged bird, I finally admitted what I'd known since the moment I saw Dominic on the broken pass and my magic *wept* without asking my permission:

They hadn't just given me orders. They had edited me. Cut a man out of my heart the way you cut a tumor out, except the tumor was the healthiest part of me, and I had been walking around ever since with the wound cauterized and called it wholeness.

A knock. Dominic came in without waiting—smuggler manners, though he'd deny it—carrying two cups of the Emberfolk's bitter red tea.

"I heard most of it," he said. "Your walls gossip."

"Our walls."

Something moved through his face when I said that—hope, handled carefully, like a live coal.

"Dominic." I turned the scroll in my hands. "If I burn this, there's no going back. No pardon, no negotiation, no *it was all a misunderstanding*. Cassel doesn't forgive defection. He erases it. People who cross him stop existing, officially, and sometimes otherwise."

"I know." He set the cups down. "I've spent ten years not existing. The food's better than you'd expect."

"This isn't funny."

"No," he agreed. "But I learned a long time ago that if you can't laugh while you're burning your life down, the smoke gets in." He crouched beside my chair, so we were eye to eye, and his voice lost its wry edge entirely. "Sera. I'm not going to tell you what to choose. I watched my mother choose duty over her own son and I swore I'd never do that to anyone. But I will tell you one thing, and then I'll shut up and let you decide."

He waited until I nodded.

"When they wiped you, they took *you* out of *your* head. They couldn't touch what I carry. For ten years I remembered you for both of us—the way you hum when you work a ward, the way you argue with recipes, the way you looked the last morning, before the pass, before everything. And I used to think that was the cruelest part. That I kept it all and you kept nothing." His hand closed gently over the scroll in my lap. "But now I think maybe it means something else. Maybe it means what's between us doesn't live in either of our heads. It survived an erasure. It crossed a dead border. It's the single most stubborn thing I've ever met, and I've met dragons."

I laughed. It came out wet and cracked, but it was a laugh.

"That," I said, "was a terrible speech."

"I'm a taster, not a poet."

I looked down at the scroll. Blood-ink signatures marching down the vellum, mine newest and smallest at the bottom, the crown's seal pulsing faintly at the top like a heartbeat I'd mistaken for my own for twenty years.

*Crown,* I thought. *You raised me. You trained me. You took my memories when they inconvenienced you and called it protection. You are sending two hundred men to strip a dying people of their last light because your kingdom is afraid of the dark.*

*I am afraid of the dark too.*

*I'm just done being afraid of you.*

I rose, walked to the hearth—the true one, the little Emberfolk cooking-fire where a grandmother had left a kettle—and held the scroll over the flames.

The ink screamed. I hadn't known ink could scream; it came off the vellum in threads of black light, writhing, fighting the fire, whispering my name in Cassel's voice, in the Queen's voice, in my own. The vellum curled. The signatures blackened one by one, each one a small death, each one a door closing on a life I'd been living on behalf of other people.

And underneath the screaming, quiet as embers, I felt something else. The sealed places in my mind—the smooth scar-tissue where a man used to be—warmed. Not opening. Not yet. Just warming, the way ground warms in early spring before anything has grown.

The scroll burned to nothing. I dropped the last flakes into the fire and watched them die.

"Well," I said, turning around. Dominic was standing by the door with both teacups forgotten in his hands, looking at me like I'd just done something miraculous instead of merely unforgivable. "I believe I'm unemployed."

"You're not," he said. "Emberhold needs ward-keepers. Terribly. The pay is ash and gratitude."

"Ash and gratitude," I repeated. "Better benefits than the crown ever offered."

* * *

The alarm came an hour later—bells, and the Emberfolk word for *fire on the wind*—and we ran out onto the walls to see Cassel's forward companies moving on the lower terraces by torchlight, siege engines unlimbering beneath the dark and starless sky, and Lord Cassel standing at their center with his hand raised, giving the order to take the hearth-flame by force before dawn.

Dominic's hand found mine in the dark. His fingers were hot. Hotter than they should have been—hotter than blood—and I felt the heat pulse in time with something vast and far away and guttering.

"Ashvane knows," he breathed. "Sera—the flame's reacting. Something's coming."

Above Thornfell, where no stars had shone for weeks, the darkness began, very faintly, to glow.
