## 7. Ash and Interrogation

They say a taster knows poison the way a priest knows prayer. What they don't tell you is that the reverse is also true: poison knows the taster. And when they poured the first cup in front of me—black as spilled night, bitter with something I hadn't tasted since my mother's kitchen—I understood that Lord Cassel hadn't brought me here to break me.

He'd brought me here to *introduce* us.

The tent smelled of wet canvas and lamp oil, of men who had marched three days through snow and resented every mile of it. My wrists were bound to the chair's arms with oiled cord—the good kind, the kind that doesn't fray—and Cassel sat across from me like patience itself, a gray man in a gray coat, turning a silver goblet between his long fingers.

"You understand the position," he said. "The crown has spent ten years feeding you, housing you, teaching you your trade. And now we learn you've been carrying a second loyalty in your blood this whole time." He smiled without warmth. "I'd almost admire it, if it weren't treason."

"It's not loyalty if you're born into it," I said. "Ask any bastard."

One of the guards hit me across the mouth for that, which was fair. My lip split against my teeth, and I tasted copper and, beneath it, that black bitterness again—already in the air, already in the water they'd given me. Clever. Cassel didn't waste anything.

"The Emberfolk claim you are covenant-son," he went on, unbothered. "That your body is a vessel for their fire. That's a very useful rumor, Delacroix. It's also a testable one." He nodded to the guard, who picked up the black cup. "Drink."

"I've drunk better at worse parties," I said, and meant it, which should have frightened me more than it did.

Here's the thing about swallowing poison for a decade: somewhere along the way, the fear stops being about dying. It becomes about the *taste*—the little biography each toxin writes on your tongue. Wolfsbane announces itself like a herald. Nightshade whispers. But this—this was old. Older than Veyra. It tasted of hearth-smoke and hot stone and something underneath like grief, and my body knew it the way an orphan knows a lullaby he can't place.

It knew it because it was *home*.

I drank. All of it, because a taster never sips what he means to survive.

For perhaps six heartbeats, nothing happened. Cassel watched me with the expression of a man reading a ledger. Then the cold started—not in my chest but in my scars, the old ones, the ones that had ached with heat since the stars went out. Every wound I'd ever taken lit up like a row of windows in a dark house, and the fire came up through them, not out of them.

"Lord—" one of the guards began.

The cords on my wrists turned to ash. So did the chair, mostly. I stood up in a ring of ember-light with smoke curling off my sleeves and my heartbeat sounding, for the first time in ten years, *loud*—not numbed, not muted, but a drum.

Cassel did not flinch. I'll grant him that. He leaned back, studied me the way he'd study a weapon he intended to requisition, and said, "There it is."

"There it is," I agreed, and put my hand through the tent wall, because conversation seemed finished.

* * *

The camp outside was chaos, but organized chaos—Cassel's vanguard moved like a machine even when it burned. Torches guttered as I passed, bending toward me like flowers toward sun, and men shouted and scrambled back from the heat rolling off my skin. I wasn't burning. That was the terrible part. I felt *fine*. Better than fine. The numbness of ten years was cracking off me like ice off a spring river, and underneath it everything I'd ever swallowed whole—every fear, every grief, every memory I'd deadened to keep working—was awake and screaming.

I ran anyway. Rage is fuel, but fuel needs direction, and mine pointed uphill, toward Thornfell, toward Sera, toward the pass where an old friend owed me a debt he was going to hate paying.

I found Wick at the tree line, exactly where I'd have stashed him: flat on his belly in a drift, watching the camp through a spyglass he'd stolen from a corpse two wars ago, chewing a stick of dried venison like the fate of kingdoms was a mildly interesting play.

"You look warm," he said.

"Wick."

"That's generally considered my name, aye."

"They'll come up the smuggler's road. The army. Cassel won't wait for orders—he wants the dragon-fire before the crown changes its mind about wanting it."

Wick lowered the glass and looked at me properly. Twenty years of running contraband over the Thornfell passes had carved his face into something like a topographical map, all ridges and sudden drops, and just then every line of it was doing arithmetic. "How long do you need?"

"Till dawn. Maybe past it. There's a gate in the mountain, and it only opens for—"

"—for folk with fire in their blood, apparently." He shrugged. "I heard the sprite story. Tam told it four times in the taverns before anyone believed him." He rolled onto his knees, spat out the venison stick, and started checking the knives in his boots with the calm of a man laying a dinner table. "Go on, then. I'll hold the narrows."

"Wick—"

"Dominic." He looked up, and for once there was nothing wry in him at all. "You remember the winter of the red snow? When you carried my boy down the pass with fever, blindfolded, in the dark, because you know these roads so well you don't need eyes?" He tapped his own chest. "That boy's got daughters now. Two of them. You held the road for us your whole life without knowing why. Let me hold it once knowing exactly."

I could have argued. I want you to know that. I opened my mouth to argue, and the fire in my scars pulsed like a second heart, and I understood that every minute I spent arguing was a minute the flame in Ashvane's hearth guttered lower, a minute Sera stood closer to the edge of whatever was coming down that pass behind me.

So I gripped his forearm, smuggler-fashion, wrist to wrist, and said, "Dawn. If I'm not back by full light, take the girls south."

"If you're not back by full light," Wick said, grinning his gap-toothed grin, "there won't be anywhere south worth taking them."

* * *

The narrows were a place I knew blindfolded, which was fortunate, because by the time I reached the upper trail the world had gone strange—snow glowing faintly gold wherever my shadow fell, the dark pines leaning away from my heat like courtiers from a scandal. Behind and below me, I could hear the vanguard finding Wick's welcome: a shout cut short, a knife-flash of movement among the rocks, the particular silence that follows when a smuggler decides a chokepoint belongs to him.

He'd spent thirty years learning to fight in places where two men couldn't stand abreast. Cassel's soldiers had spent those same years learning to fight in fields. Mathematics, in the end.

I climbed. At the great cleft where the hidden gate breathed its thin warmth, Sera was waiting, soot-streaked, her ward-keeper's cloak scorched at the hem, her eyes going wide at the light coming off me.

"What happened?" she demanded. "Your scouts reported—Dominic, you're *glowing*—"

"Cassel wanted to test a theory." I flexed my hands; embers drifted from my knuckles and died in the snow. "The theory held. We need to move—how is the flame?"

"Guttering." Her voice cracked on it. "Ashvane says hours, not days. And Dominic—" she caught my arm, and where her fingers touched, the fire gentled instead of flaring, which told me things my heart wasn't ready to hear, "—the Emberfolk asked what fuels the reforging. Ashvane answered. It isn't blood. It isn't magic. It's *memory*. True memory, willingly given. The fire eats what made you."

I thought of ten years of swallowed cups. Of a mother walking into the passes. Of a woman whose face I'd carried through every numb midnight like a coal I refused to let go out. I thought of how much of me was already ash, and how much remained.

"Then it's going to eat well," I said.

Below us, far down the black throat of the valley, the fighting sounds stopped—all at once, the way snowfall stops. And then, rising up the pass, thin and clear over the frozen air, came the sound I'd dreaded more than any army: a single voice, Wick's voice, laughing, followed by a roar of flame that had no business existing in a man with no fire in his blood at all—unless love counts, unless stubbornness counts, unless thirty years of holding roads finally gets to burn bright just once.

Sera's hand tightened on my arm. "Someone's holding the narrows."

"The best of us," I said, and turned to the gate, and the gate—smelling covenant-blood and covenant-fire—opened like an eye.

Behind us, the last light of Wick's defiance flared and faded. Ahead, the mountain swallowed us whole, and somewhere in its gutters and vaults, a dragon waited with an empty hearth and a question I already knew the answer to:

What would I feed it?

Everything, I thought. Everything I am. And God help me—I was starting to be glad of it.
