## 8. The Guttering

### Dominic

The mountain had a heartbeat.

I'd been telling myself for two days that it was the wind in the pass, the way wind does when it funnels through stone and gets ideas about itself. But smuggler's roads don't breathe. This did—a slow swell and fall under our boots, faint as a pulse felt through a wrist, coming up from somewhere deep in Emberhold's dark.

Sera heard it too. She kept stopping on the trail, one hand pressed flat to the rock like she was taking its pulse back.

"It's slowing," she said, the third time.

"I know."

"You can feel it?"

I could. That was the part that frightened me most—not the wards falling, not Cassel's soldiers at our backs, not even the poison still humming in my veins from the vanguard's interrogation tent. What frightened me was that my body knew this mountain the way it knew my own heartbeat, and it was grieving.

We came over the last shoulder of ridge as the sun set—or rather, as the sun gave up trying. Below us lay Emberhold, and I understood in one look why the refugees spoke of it in whispers. It should have been a valley of fire. Instead it was a valley of embers: fields of dim orange light guttering across black earth, rivers running slow and red as cooling iron, and at the center, coiled around a hearth the size of a cathedral dome, a dragon.

Ashvane. Even dying, she made the word *dragon* feel insufficient. She was vast and ash-grey, her scales dull as old charcoal, her wings folded like a cathedral's collapsed roof. And beneath her, cradled in her foreclaws, burned the last flame of her realm—the hearth-fire, barely knee-high now, trembling like a candle in a drafty church.

A candle. A realm's whole covenant, down to a candle.

"Ten years," I said quietly. "My mother left when I was nine. Ten years, and this is what she was keeping alive."

"You didn't know."

"No." I watched the flame shudder. "But some part of me did. Every time I swallowed poison and didn't die. Every time the tasters' guild called it discipline and I couldn't explain why the taste never touched anything deeper than my tongue." I looked at Sera. "They didn't train an immunity into me, Sera. They were spending an inheritance and calling it wages."

She didn't answer. Her hand found mine anyway, which was answer enough, and we went down into the ember-fields together.

* * *

The Emberfolk met us at the edge of their dying city—and it was a city, or had been, towers of fused glass and dragon-glass rising from streets where every lamp was out except the ones nearest the hearth. They were gaunt, grey-eyed people, and they moved toward us with the terrible courtesy of the starving who have decided, finally, to be gracious about it.

An old woman walked first, leaning on a staff of white wood. When she saw my face, she stopped so abruptly the others nearly walked into her.

"Covenant-son," she said.

"That's twice now something small and dying has called me that," I said. "I'm starting to take it personally."

Her mouth twitched—the ghost of humor in a face that hadn't had much use for it lately. "You have her eyes. Maren's eyes. The fire-keeper who went over the mountains and married a Veyran lord and thought distance would hide her blood from the crown." She spat, delicately, with real feeling. "Crowns smell covenants like sharks smell blood. Come. Ashvane will want to see you before—" She stopped. Before the flame goes, her silence said. Everyone in that city was finishing sentences the same way.

The chamber beneath the hearth was warm the way a grave is quiet. Close. Ashvane's head lowered as we entered—one vast golden eye opening, focusing on me with the weight of a verdict.

*So,* a voice said, and it wasn't sound so much as heat against my thoughts, *the thorn sends back its broken branch.*

"I've been called worse," I said. "Usually by the crown, usually on paperwork."

The eye narrowed, which I chose to interpret as draconic amusement. *You carry my fire in your blood, little taster. Do you know what you are?*

"A mistake," I said. "A half-blood accident the crown found useful."

*A covenant.* Heat flared along her jaw, and for a moment her scales glowed dull red, remembering what they used to be. *Your mother bound her line to this flame so that neither realm would starve of the other's light. Our warmth for your world's rain. Our magic woven through Thornfell's wards. And in return, her descendants would taste every poison meant for kings and live, because covenant blood burns what would kill it.* The great eye turned to Sera. *And you. The keeper whose order was to forget.*

Sera went very still beside me. "You know about that."

*I know everything the wards knew, before they broke. Including why they broke.* Ashvane's head swung back toward the hearth-flame, and all the memory of heat went out of her voice, leaving only exhaustion. *They broke because I am failing, and the wards were fed from me. Not destroyed. Starved. As this realm is starving. As yours soon will, though your kings haven't noticed yet that their stars are going out.*

"We brought no soldiers," Sera said. "No weapons worth mentioning. We came because Dominic—"

*Came to refill the flame.* Ashvane exhaled, a long grey sigh. *Yes. And here is the truth your crown will hate, and probably kill you for carrying home: the fire cannot be fed with blood.*

I blinked. "Every story ever told—"

*Was written by men who only understood payment in flesh.* The dragon's lip curled. *Blood fuels nothing but rage. The hearth needs true memory. Willing love. The stuff a soul is actually made of. Feed it a life freely given—not the body, mind you, the *living* of it—and it will burn another age. Feed it unwillingly, and it chokes.*

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"How much?" I asked. "How much living?"

*All of it,* said Ashvane, gently, which somehow made it worse. *Or enough. The flame takes what is offered. It has taken keepers whole before. Your mother offered fragments, year by year—that was the covenant's toll, and why she grew distant from you near the end. She was feeding me pieces of herself. Her lullabies first. Then your father's face. Then—*

"Me," I said. My voice came out flat. "That's why she forgot me. Why she could leave."

*Why she wept on the pass without knowing what she wept for.* The eye closed. *Forgive her. Forgive me. We were both starving, and you were the meal either of us could least afford.*

I stood there in the warm dark and did arithmetic I didn't want to do. Ten years of tasting. My mother's face—I'd already lost most of that to time; the flame would just be collecting a debt. My name. My—

Beside me, Sera stepped forward.

* * *

### Sera

He was already opening his mouth. I knew that mouth. I knew, with the strange borrowed knowledge that lived in my hands and my chest and apparently nowhere in my head, exactly what he was about to say—he'd say *me, take mine, I've got ten years of numbness to spare and a name I'd happily lose*—and so I stepped forward before he could spend himself like coin.

"I'll do it."

Ashvane's eye opened. Dominic's head snapped around.

"Sera—"

"She's offering her memories of him," I said to the dragon, ignoring him, which took effort, because his face was doing things to my composure that no oath-scroll ever had. "The ones the order didn't erase. The ones I got back on the road. The night at the ward-station. The garden. All of it." My voice held. I was almost proud of it. "It's willing. It's true. Take it."

"No." His hand caught my wrist. Not hard. Just there, warm, immovable. "Absolutely not."

"Dominic."

"You spent three years having a man erased out of your own head. You think I'll stand here while you hand him over *again*? Voluntarily this time?" His voice cracked on *again*, and I hated how much I loved hearing it crack. "No. Take mine. I'm the covenant-son. It's my debt."

"It's not a debt if it's a gift," I said. "That's rather the point."

*Children,* rumbled Ashvane, with the weariness of someone who had been listening to mortals argue for four thousand years. *You misunderstand the mechanics entirely.*

We both turned.

*The flame does not weigh offerings on a scale,* she said. *It weighs them on a balance. Memory given alone is fuel. Love given alone is smoke. But memory and love, offered *together*—offered as partners, each holding the other's share—* the great head lowered until her breath stirred my hair, warm as a hearth in winter, *that is what the original covenant was. Two realms holding each other's fire. Not one soul burning alone so another can stay warm. Never that. Your crown taught you sacrifice, little keeper. I am asking for something much harder.*

"What's harder than sacrifice?" Dominic asked.

*Partnership.*

The word landed in the chamber like a dropped bell. I felt Dominic's grip shift on my wrist—not restraining now. Holding. The way you hold something you've decided, against all evidence, to believe in.

"If we give together," I said slowly, "what happens?"

*To the flame, rebirth. To the covenant, reforging. To you—* the eye moved between us, ancient and oddly kind, *I cannot promise. Some come through the burning with their lives intact and their pasts rearranged. Some remember everything and recognize nothing. The flame keeps what it loves and spends what it must. But you would go into it *together*. Whatever remains on the other side remains *of* you both.*

I looked at him. He was already looking at me, and there was something in his face I'd only seen once before, three years ago through a ward-station window—the same expression, terrified and certain, of a man deciding that some losses are worth walking into.

"They're coming, aren't they," he said to the dragon. "Cassel's forces. That's why you asked."

*They crossed the lower pass at dusk. The Emberfolk will hold them at the broken wards as long as they can, which will not be long. So decide now, children. The flame gutters. I can hold it perhaps an hour more. Perhaps less.* Ashvane drew herself up, and despite everything—despite the ash-scales and the starving valley and the army marching on the ones I loved—she was, for one moment, magnificent. *Will you feed my fire? Together?*

Dominic's thumb moved once over my pulse. An old habit, I realized. A taster checking a heartbeat. Checking that I was real, and alive, and choosing this.

"Together," I said.

"Together," he said, and then, wry even at the edge of the fire, because he was always wry at the edge of things: "If I forget your laugh, Sera Oyelaran, I want it on record that I'll be very annoyed about it for the rest of eternity."

"Noted," I whispered.

Below us, far away at the broken wards, the first war-horns of Veyra sounded through the dark—and the last flame of Emberhold reached up toward us like a hand.
