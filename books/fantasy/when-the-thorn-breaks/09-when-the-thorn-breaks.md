## 9. When the Thorn Breaks

There is a particular comedy in discovering, at the end of your life, that the one thing you were never allowed to keep is the one thing you'd have traded everything else to save.

I knelt before Ashvane's hearth-flame and tried to remember my mother's face.

Not her story — I had that. Fire-keeper of Emberhold, smuggled through the Thornfell passes heavy with a half-blood child, gone before I could walk. Not her voice — I had fragments, a lullaby in a language that tasted like woodsmoke. Her *face*. The hearth had taken it years ago, quietly, the way it had taken so much. Every poison I swallowed for Veyra bought its numbness with a coin drawn from somewhere, and I'd never asked where the mint was.

Now I knew. Now I knelt at the mint itself.

The flame was the size of a candle stub. That was the joke of it. Ten years of state banquets, ten years of swallowing death so queens could eat quail in peace, and the entire fate of two realms came down to a fire you could cup in your palms and carry out into the snow like a lost bird.

"Ask me again," Sera said behind me, "what happens if you do this."

"You've asked me four times."

"And you've answered vaguely four times. It's a bad sign when the man feeding himself to a dragon's hearth gets vague."

I turned. She stood at the mouth of the hollow, sword drawn, wardlight crawling up her forearms like ivy made of morning. Beyond her, through the broken archway of Emberhold's gate, the sky above Thornfell was a black ceiling. No stars. Just Cassel's army camped on our side of the shattered wards, a thousand fires burning in neat military rows, because Lord Cassel believed in order even at the end of the world.

"They're coming at dawn," she said.

"They'll come before dawn. He'll want the dark." I stood. My knees cracked. Somewhere in the last week I'd become the kind of man whose knees crack. "Sera. If this works—"

"If this works, the covenant reforges, the wards relight, the stars come back, and you won't remember any of it. Yes. You've been vague about that part too."

"Not vague. Precise." I held up my hands, turning them over. Taster's hands. Steady hands. "The flame eats memory. True memory, willingly given — that's the fuel Ashvane named. Mine are the only ones here worth anything, apparently. A lifetime of them. My mother. The tasting. The night roads. My name." I smiled, because if I didn't I'd have to feel something instead, and feeling things was how you ended up kneeling in front of a guttering fire offering yourself as kindling. "What walks away from this hearth will be a man who doesn't know why his hands shake around honey."

"That's not a man," Sera said. "That's an empty coat."

"It's a covenant-son. It's what Emberhold needs."

"What *I* need—" She stopped herself. Jaw tight. Ten years ago someone had ordered her to forget me, and she had obeyed, and her magic had spent a decade quietly grieving a man her mind couldn't name. I wasn't going to make her say it now. There wasn't time, and there was too much time, all at once.

"The embers," I said. "You saved them. From the sprite, from the hearth. Whatever the flame takes, some ash falls back. You told me the Emberfolk regrow their dead from cinders. So." I shrugged. "Regrow me."

"Dominic—"

"That's not my real name anyway, did I mention? Delacroix is a crown name. A working title." I grinned at her. "Feed me back something better."

She crossed the hollow in three strides and kissed me like a woman settling a debt, hard and thorough, and when she pulled back her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "When this is over," she said, "you will taste honey, and you will remember my laugh, or I will personally teach your empty coat to regret it."

"Worth aiming for," I said.

And then the horns sounded from the pass, earlier than even I'd guessed, because Cassel had never once done me the courtesy of being predictable, and there was no more time for anything except the doing.

* * *

I put my hands into the hearth.

It didn't burn. Of course it didn't. It *recognized* me — heat without pain, the old ache in my scars finally explained, every poisoned banquet of the last decade suddenly making sense as training for exactly this: a body taught to take fire in and call it nothing.

"Here," I said, to the flame, to the dragon coiled vast and dim around us, to whatever was listening. "Take the mother first."

And she came back to me all at once, cruelly whole, the way memory does when it knows it's leaving. Her face — dark-eyed, soot-smudged, laughing at something I couldn't hear. Her hands teaching mine the shape of a warding sign. The lullaby complete now, every verse, the words meaning *burn low, burn long, my ember, until morning*. The smell of her hair when she carried me through the pass with soldiers behind us and sang the burning song to keep me quiet.

*I'm sorry,* I thought, which was absurd, because she'd sacrificed everything and I was the one apologizing for forgetting her twice. *Burn long, my ember. I'll be the morning.*

The flame took her. It flared to the height of a man.

"More?" it seemed to ask. Dragons don't speak in words; they speak in appetite. Ashvane's eye, huge and ancient and dying, rolled toward me with something like pity in it.

"More," I said. "Take the tasting."

Ten years arrived together. The first poison at nineteen, bitter as betrayal, and the queen thanking me with a medal while I vomited into a courtyard fountain. The hundredth, which tasted of nothing at all. The thousandth, which tasted of *duty*, which was worse. All those nights walking the smuggler roads alone because sleep meant dreaming and dreaming meant remembering that I had no one. All those mornings waking up numb and calling it discipline. I gave it to the fire gladly — let it have the loneliness, let it have the fear, let it have every swallowed cup — but it took the good years tangled in with the bad, because that's how memory works, it never lets you donate selectively. The taste of rain on the high passes went with the poisons. The sound of Sera's laugh across a ward-circle went with the fear. The one perfect summer went with everything else.

The flame roared up to fill the hollow. Behind me, I heard the first clash of steel at the broken gate, and Sera shouting orders in a voice I'd only ever heard her use in dreams I no longer trusted myself to have had.

"One more," I whispered. My tongue was already going strange in my mouth, words arriving like letters from a country I was emigrating from. "My name."

Because that was the keystone. The crown had built me into Dominic Delacroix, taster, tool, and the flame needed to know what it was eating. A name, freely surrendered, was the oldest fuel there was. Every story agrees on that.

*I am Dominic Delacroix*, I thought, holding it up like a cup. *And I give it away.*

The world went white, and warm, and very quiet.

* * *

The last thing — I want to be honest, since honesty is all that's left of me — the last thing I felt was not loss.

It was relief. Ten years of carrying everything, and suddenly light-handed, empty-pocketed, a man walking out of debt. The flame rose through the roof of the hollow and up, up past the broken archway, and I heard Ashvane move for the first time, scales the size of doors sliding against stone, a sound like mountains deciding something. The dragon breathed in the new-fed fire and breathed out across Thornfell.

I saw none of it. But I heard it, the way you hear weather from inside a house.

I heard the wards relight — a chord, not a crack, the reverse of that shattering-glass scream from midwinter, harmony rebuilding itself note by note across the peaks. I heard stars ignite, which turns out sounds like distant applause. I heard Cassel's army stop being an army. A thousand disciplined men looked up at a sky suddenly full of light and remembered, all at once, that they had homes and mothers and names, and that no crown pays enough for the end of the world. They ran. Even Cassel — especially Cassel; men who worship order cannot stand inside a miracle — his voice cracking orders that no one obeyed, until the returning dawn swallowed him too.

And I heard Sera.

She came down into the hollow with soot on her face and blood on her sleeve and her oath-scroll's ashes still somewhere in her pocket, and she found a man sitting on the floor by a roaring hearth who looked up at her with pleasant, total blankness.

"Hello," I said, because it seemed polite. "Are you hurt?"

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she laughed — wet and cracked and real — and sat down beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched.

"No," she said. "I'm fine. Do you like honey?"

"I don't know," I said honestly. "Should I?"

"Yes," said the woman whose laugh I would spend months earning back one spoonful at a time. "Yes, you really should."
