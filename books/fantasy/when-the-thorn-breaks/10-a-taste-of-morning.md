## 10. A Taste of Morning

They tell me I saved the world.

I find this difficult to credit, mostly because the world in question now smells permanently of yeast, and I spend my mornings arguing with a baker named Hurn about whether cardamom belongs in morning rolls. (It does not. Hurn is wrong with tremendous confidence.) Somewhere between the fall of Lord Cassel and the relighting of the stars, the woman who burned her oath-scroll and stood against an army became a person who measures flour and steals honey from her own pantry stores.

I can't say I mind. Heroism, it turns out, is mostly paperwork and ash. Baking is at least edible.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me start where all good stories about love start: in a kitchen, with a man who doesn't know his own name.

* * *

He works the morning shift at Emberhold's rebuilt kitchens—the long stone hall beneath the mountain, where the hearth-flame Ashvane gifted to us burns in a proper hearth now instead of a dragon's coils. The Emberfolk call him *the Taster*, because that is what he was, though none of them knew him before. He answers to it the way you'd answer to rain: without complaint, without recognition.

He has no name of his own. He fed it to the fire, along with his mother's face, his ten years of poison, every memory that made Dominic Delacroix into someone the crown could use and use up. He walked into that flame knowing he would come out the other side as a stranger to himself, and he did it so that Sera—me—wouldn't have to burn alone.

I have spent six months being furious at him for this. It's very hard to stay furious at someone who hands you bread.

The embers help. That was my one act of theft in all of this, and I'd do it again with both hands. When the covenant reforged itself and the flame took everything he offered, I had already cupped a handful of dying coals from the old hearth—coals that had touched his memories on their way down, that held their shape the way a footprint holds the shape of a foot. Ashvane looked at me holding them and said nothing, which from a dragon is a great deal of speech.

"You're going to try to give them back," she'd said instead, later, watching me wrap those coals in cloth like relics.

"I'm going to *cook* with them," I said. "There's a difference."

There is, too. I don't shove the memories back into his head—that would be its own kind of violence, forcing a man to be someone he didn't choose to be. Instead I bake the embers into the food. Slowly. One dish at a time. When he eats something cooked over those coals, something returns—not all at once, but like frost melting off a window, a shape appearing behind the glass. Sometimes it's small. Sometimes it's a whole season of his life arriving at once, and he has to sit down in the middle of the kitchen with his head between his knees while ten years of swallowed poison reassemble themselves somewhere behind his eyes.

The Emberfolk think I'm performing a resurrection. Hurn thinks I'm showing off. Both are partially correct.

* * *

Here is how it goes, most mornings:

I arrive before dawn. The coals go into the oven first, banked among the ordinary firewood, and I knead dough while the kitchen fills with that smell—warm grain, woodsmoke, and underneath it something faintly metallic and strange, the scent of memory catching light. He arrives when the bells ring, ties on his apron, and nods at me the way you nod at a colleague. Polite. Distant. Once, I would have wept at that distance. Now I just hand him a knife and point him at the onions.

"How many?" he asks.

"All of them," I say. "We're feeding a garrison."

He chops onions like a man who learned during a war, fast and vicious, tears streaming down his face, and every time I watch him cry from onions alone I feel a savage little joy. This man spent a decade unable to taste arsenic. Let him weep over alliums. Let him feel things that are stupid and small and safe.

"Your knife work's good," he said once, squinting at my julienne. "Military?"

"Worse," I said. "Ward-keeper."

"What's that like?"

"It's like being a locksmith who also does surgery," I said. "On reality."

He laughed. And here is the thing I have never told anyone, not even Ashvane, not even my journal: when he laughed, my magic flared. Every ward-sense I possess lit up like a struck bell, because his laugh *fit* something in me, some shape carved years ago that my orders made me forget but could not make empty. My mind lost him. My magic kept the mold.

I am patient. Ward-keepers learn patience or they learn explosions, usually in that order. I can wait months for a lock to turn.

* * *

The memories come back out of order. Nobody warns you about that part.

Last month it was his mother—not her face, not yet, but her hands, folding dumplings in a kitchen that smelled of cedar smoke. He stood at the counter with flour to his elbows and said, suddenly, "Someone taught me this fold. Small hands. She hummed when she worked." Then his brow creased and the shape slid away again, and he went back to folding, quieter.

I gave him space. I also wrote down *cedar smoke, humming, dumplings* in my journal that night with shaking hands, because every fragment is a brick and I am building him a door.

Two weeks ago it was Veyra—the capital, the banquets, the taste of nightshade blooming on his tongue like a lie told by a friend. He came to me after service, wiping his hands, wearing the particular stillness he gets when something large has moved through him.

"There was a queen," he said. "I saved her life once. She never thanked me properly. She just looked at me like I was a tool that had performed to spec." He frowned. "Why do I remember that? Why does that feel like the truest thing I own?"

"Because it is," I said. "You were used, and you knew it, and you stayed anyway, because you thought it was duty. It wasn't. It was just loneliness wearing armor."

He looked at me for a long moment. "How do you know all this?"

"Good guesswork," I said, and handed him a roll, and watched him eat the memory of his own captivity without knowing it, and hated myself a little, and did it anyway.

Ashvane says I must let him choose. That the man who sacrificed himself deserves the dignity of deciding who he becomes—and that if I curate his return too carefully, I'm only building a better throne. She says this in the tone of a creature who has watched empires rise and fall and found most of them tedious, which lends her opinions a certain weight.

She's right. I know she's right. But she is not the one who watches him reach for something just behind his own eyes and come back with empty hands. She is not the one who keeps a ledger of his life in a locked box under her bed, waiting to feed it back to him spoonful by spoonful, praying he'll forgive the withholding when—if—he understands why.

Love, it turns out, is mostly patience with a side of guilt. Nobody writes songs about that part either.

* * *

Today the bells rang late, because it snowed in the passes overnight—the Thornfell passes, open now, honest roads where smugglers' trails used to run. Veyran traders come through sometimes. They bring news, spices, occasionally a nervous official who takes one look at the dragons circling the peaks and remembers urgent business elsewhere.

Him and I had the kitchen to ourselves until the garrison arrived. Snow-light came blue through the high windows. The coals glowed in the oven like patient eyes. I had planned something ambitious—a spiced porridge, Emberfolk recipe, heavy on the honey—because last week he'd dreamed aloud about a garden, "someone laughing in a garden, I couldn't see her face," and I knew that memory. I knew exactly which garden, and whose laugh, and I had been carrying that coal for three weeks like a live grenade made of my own happiness.

He stirred the pot. I hovered, which he noticed, because he notices everything; ten years of tasting for poison will do that.

"You're watching me again," he said. Not accusing. Curious. "You always watch me eat. Like you're waiting for something."

"I'm a taster's colleague," I said. "It's professional habit."

"Mm." He ladled porridge into two bowls, set honey on the table, and then—this is the part I'll keep forever—he paused, honey dipper suspended, and said: "Can I tell you something strange? Every time I taste honey, I almost remember laughing. Not mine. Someone else's. Someone who thought I was funny, which frankly suggests poor judgment."

My heart did something acrobatic and undignified.

"Eat your porridge," I managed.

He ate his porridge.

And the ember I'd baked into it—his memory, my memory, ours—came home. I watched it happen. His shoulders dropped. His breath caught. The spoon stopped halfway to his mouth and hung there while six years of a garden in Veyra unfolded behind his face: a younger Sera, off-duty and reckless, laughing so hard at something he'd said that she snorted, which mortified her, which made it worse, which made him laugh harder, until a passing gardener asked if they needed medical attention.

He set the spoon down very carefully, the way you handle something that might detonate.

"Sera," he said.

Just my name. Just that. Ten years of numbness and a crown's worth of poison and a self-immolation at the heart of a mountain, and the first thing to claw its way back whole was my name in his mouth, slightly wondering, slightly accusing, entirely certain.

"That's me," I said. My voice cracked on the second word. Very dignified. Hurn will never let me live it down.

"You—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a garden. You laughed like the world was ending and you'd decided to enjoy it. And then—" his jaw tightened, the memory reaching for the ugly parts, the forgetting spell, the order that scraped him out of her skull— "then they took it. They took *me*. From you."

"Yes."

"And you've been here the whole time. Feeding me. Waiting." He looked at the bowl, at the coals glowing in the oven, at me, doing arithmetic no one should have to do. "Months. You let me call you 'colleague' for *months*."

"It seemed rude to introduce myself as 'the woman you died for,'"

"Sera." He reached across the table and took my hand—flour-dusted, honey-sticky, trembling—and pressed it between both of his, warm as a hearth, warm as the thing he carried through the dark for all of us. "Thank you. For keeping the embers. For not forcing them. For the porridge." A wet, disbelieving laugh escaped him. "For the terrible cardamom stance. I remember that too, actually. You were right, by the way. It doesn't belong in the rolls."

"Hurn will be devastated."

"Hurn can fight me."

And there it was—the grin, the real one, the one from the garden, the one that had survived fire and forgetting and six months of onion duty. Outside, the snow was turning gold in the sunrise, the way it always does now over Emberhold, because the stars above Thornfell keep their posts and the dragon-fire keeps its promise and the world, improbably, goes on.

He squeezed my hand. "I don't have all of me back yet," he said quietly. "Some days I'm mostly smoke. But I remember your laugh. I'm going to build the rest around that, if that's—"

"It's everything," I said. "That's the whole architecture. Everything else is load-bearing decoration."

He laughed, and my magic sang, and the honey shone on the table like a small preserved sun, and I thought: *let the histories say whatever they want.* Let them write about sacrifice and covenants and the boy who swallowed the crown's poisons. I know how the story really ends.

It ends in a kitchen. It ends with honey. It ends with a name, remembered.

It ends with breakfast.
