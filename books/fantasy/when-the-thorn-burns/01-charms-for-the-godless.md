## 1. Charms for the Godless

The lamps of Vell's night market were lit one by one as the sun went down, and Cyrus watched them from behind a curtain of dried lavender and river-glass, counting them the way other people counted prayers.

Forty-one. Same as last night. Same as the night before that, going back three years now, since they'd paid a ferryman six coppers and a lie to bring them across the Serein into a city where magic was spoken of the way plague was spoken of—in whispers, in past tense, in the voices of people grateful it hadn't come to their door.

Vell did not burn witches anymore. The Guild had made that very clear, in pamphlets nailed to every church door and read aloud at every market opening: *The age of heresy is ended. The age of vigilance begins.* No pyres. Only questions. Only ledgers. Only neighbors asked to remember what their neighbors had said, and when, and to whom. Vell had learned that fire drew crowds, and crowds remembered, and remembering was dangerous. Better to erase quietly. Better a man simply stopped selling charms and started selling rope instead, and no one ever said why, because there had never been anything to say.

Cyrus sold charms anyway. That was the joke they kept folded up inside themselves like a knife wrapped in wool—the kind of joke you could die laughing at.

They arranged the night's wares with hands that trembled only slightly, and not from cold. A luck-charm: knotted red thread around an iron nail pulled from a drowned church. A sleep-charm: poppy seed and moth-wing in a vial of blown glass. A charm against lying tongues: a sliver of mirror backed with wax, so that flattery slid off and truth stuck. None of them worked. That was the point. They worked exactly as well as charms were supposed to work in Vell, which was to say: they worked if you believed, and believing was free, and free things were the only things the Godless city trusted.

Underneath the table, underneath the floor of Cyrus's skin, something else waited. It always waited. It had been waiting for twelve years, patient as water finding a crack.

They pressed their palms flat against the tabletop until the crackle under their fingernails went still.

* * *

The widow came at the hour when the market smell turned from frying oil to tallow smoke, when the fish-sellers began covering their bins and the lamplighter made his slow round of the eastern stalls. Cyrus knew her by her walk before they saw her face—too fast for the crowd, too careful of her purse, the particular hunched urgency of a person carrying grief like a second spine.

She stopped at the edge of the stall, pretending to examine the river-glass, and Cyrus let her pretend. You didn't rush a customer like this. You let the goods do the talking.

"You're the charm-seller," the woman said finally. Her voice had been used hard lately; it frayed on the last word.

"People say so." Cyrus looked up—a calculated thing, timed, practiced. Enough eye contact to seem honest, not enough to seem hungry. Their face was the most carefully maintained thing they owned, more than the stall, more than the rented room above the chandler's shop with its one window facing a wall. A forgettable face. Mid-thirties, maybe, or older if you looked at the eyes, which nobody did. Hair the color of wet sand, cut short and unremarkable. Hands gloved, always gloved, even in summer—"a skin condition," they'd say, and people would look away, because Vell was polite about afflictions and terrified of everything else.

"The luck-charm." She touched it without picking it up, the red thread, the iron nail. "For my son. He's going down to the docks to sign on with a grain ship, and the last three ships out of Vell have lost men overboard, and his father—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "His father drowned two springs ago. In the harbor. Ten feet from the quay."

"And everyone watched," Cyrus said.

Her eyes came up, startled. "Yes."

"Everyone always watches." Cyrus took the charm off its hook and set it gently in her palm. "Iron remembers being part of something holy. That's what makes it hold luck. It wants to go back to being sacred, so it holds on tight to whatever you ask it to keep." It was nonsense. It was beautiful nonsense, the kind that had kept Cyrus fed through three winters, and they delivered it in the low, warm register they'd learned from a fortune-teller in another life, another country, another name.

"It's four coppers," they added.

"Two," she said, automatic, and then flushed, because she hadn't meant to haggle over her dead husband's memory. "I'm sorry. I don't—I'm not good at this anymore."

"No one is." Cyrus closed her fingers over the charm. "Three. And I'll tell you the words to say when you give it to him, and the words are worth more than the thread."

She paid without looking at the coins. People never did, at the moment of purchase; money was real again later, when it was gone. "What are the words?"

"*Hold what is held. Keep what keeps me.* He says it once, touching the knot, and then he doesn't think about it again. Luck doesn't like being watched. Neither does grief."

Something moved across her face—not a smile, but the place where a smile would live if she could afford one. "You talk like someone who's buried people."

"I talk like someone who's sold a lot of charms."

She laughed, short and cracked, and Cyrus felt the laugh land somewhere under their ribs, in that hollowed-out room where other people's warmth was stored and rationed. This was the part of the work that mattered. Not the coppers. This. Two minutes of being seen by a stranger, safely, briefly, and then gone.

"He should also eat before he signs anything," Cyrus said. "Ships' clerks cheat hungry men. Tell him the charm-seller said so."

"I will." She tucked the charm into her bodice, against her heart, where all the important things went, and paused. "Bless you."

"Don't," Cyrus said, too quickly, and softened it with a shrug. "Bad luck, blessings, in my trade. Competing products."

Another almost-smile, and then she was swallowed back into the current of the market, and Cyrus sat alone behind the lavender and the glass, and let their shoulders drop half an inch, and hated how much those two minutes had cost them, and how gladly they'd have paid double.

* * *

The storm under their skin woke at dusk, as it always did. It wasn't weather, exactly—or it was, but weather with intent, weather that knew their name. It started as a hum in the back teeth and spread downward, into the wrists, the knuckles, the scarred lines across both palms that the gloves hid. Old scars. Straight ones. Deliberate ones. Made by a brand that hadn't been iron at all but something colder, something that had drunk rather than burned.

Cyrus breathed through it. Four counts in, seven counts out, the rhythm an old woman had taught them in the mountains north of the Serein, a hedge-witch who'd taken one look at their hands and started charging them double for lessons. *It isn't a beast,* she'd said. *Beasts get tired. It's a sea. You don't fight a sea. You build seawalls.*

So: seawalls. Every evening. Every waking hour, if they were honest, though some hours the walls stood taller than others. When a cart rattled past with a Guild seal painted on its side—white tower on black field, the sigil that made mothers pull children close—Cyrus built the walls higher. When two watchmen stopped three stalls down to laugh about something, the walls went up like siege works. When the sky over Vell clouded over, gray-bellied and heavy with rain it wouldn't release, Cyrus stared at their own knees and thought about nothing, about arithmetic, about the price of lamp oil, about anything except the fact that the clouds were speaking to them, softly, in a language only they could hear, asking permission.

*Come up,* the sky said, the way it always said. *Come up and we'll make something together.*

*No,* Cyrus answered, the way they always answered. *Not here.*

Here was Vell, where the Inquisitor's Guild kept offices on Cathedral Square with windows of clear glass—clear, so that the city could see in and know itself observed. Here was a country where the word *mage* had been replaced, by decree, with the word *heretic*, and the replacement had taken so thoroughly that old women who'd once left bread out for the hearth-spirits now crossed themselves when a candle guttered wrong. Here, twelve years ago, a girl named Sefa had stood in a marble hall and said, in a voice that never shook, *I saw what he did. I testify to it willingly*—and Cyrus had learned that the worst storms were never the ones in the sky.

The lamps burned. The market breathed. Forty-two lamps now; the lamplighter had reached the far row.

Cyrus sold a sleep-charm to a dockhand with shaking hands. Sold a mirror-charm to a young wife who didn't meet their eyes and paid in coins that might have been her household's whole reserve, and Cyrus charged her one copper less than marked and told her it was a discount for buying early. Sold nothing at all to a lean man in a gray coat who drifted past the stall twice, slowly, looking at everything and nothing, and Cyrus catalogued him the way they catalogued everything: height, gait, the cut of his collar, whether he wore a Guild pin (he didn't) and whether he walked like a man following someone or a man followed (unclear), and then put him away in the drawer of their mind labeled *probably harmless, verify tomorrow.*

This was invisibility, done properly. Not hiding—hiding drew the eye, the way a shuttered house drew the eye. Invisibility was being so ordinary, so precisely calibrated to the city's expectations, that looking at you was like looking at a wall. A charm-seller who was slightly too cheap. Slightly too polite. Slightly too tired. A man whose face you couldn't describe an hour after seeing it, because Cyrus practiced that face in the mirror the way soldiers practiced drills.

Except the hands. The hands were the problem. The scars ran palm to wrist in deliberate lines, and once, years ago, in a border town whose name they'd let fall out of their memory like a coin dropped in deep water, someone had grabbed their wrist and gone white and whispered a word in a language Cyrus hadn't heard since—and so: gloves. Always gloves. Winter, summer, funerals, weddings, fever-sweats. *A skin condition.* Vell, polite about afflictions, looked away.

And so Cyrus sat behind their little wall of lavender and glass, in the lamplight, in the smoke, in the tenth year of a five-year plan to become no one, and waited for the market to end, and was lonely in the specific, practiced way of a person who has gotten very good at it.

That was the thing nobody warned you about exile. Not the hunger—you got used to hunger; hunger was honest. Not the fear—fear sharpened you, and Cyrus had been whittled down to something keen and small and efficient. It was the loneliness that rotted you, because loneliness didn't want you dead. It wanted you comfortable. It whispered, every single night, in the voice of reason: *you could talk to someone. You could let one person in. What are the odds, really? After twelve years? After ten thousand strangers? What are the odds that this one—*

High, actually. That was the terrible mathematics of it. Every stranger was a door, and doors opened, and behind every door was a witness, and witnesses were how a boy in a mountain village had become a name on a Guild ledger, and the name on the ledger had become a brand, and the brand had become this: a stall, a glove, a lie worn smooth as river stone.

Cyrus checked the drawer in their mind. The gray-coated man was gone. Good. Probably.

* * *

Near midnight, when the market had thinned to stragglers and the fish-stalls stank honestly of the day's losses, an old man Cyrus had never seen before shuffled up to the stall. He was bundled wrong for the season—too many coats—and his eyes had the milky, wandering quality of someone who navigated by sound. He set both hands on the table's edge, and Cyrus noticed, with a small cold start, that the man's knuckles were scarred too. Old burns. Straight lines. Deliberate.

"Evening," the man said. His voice was gravel and wind. "You're the charm-seller."

"I'm the charm-seller."

"They say your charms are honest ones." He smiled, and there were too few teeth in it. "Honest nonsense. My favorite kind."

"What can I get you, grandfather?"

"Oh, nothing. Nothing for me." He leaned closer, and the milkiness of his eyes seemed suddenly less like blindness and more like a choice about where to look. "I'm just an old man who walks the market every night, and I notice things. I noticed you count the lamps. Forty-one, forty-two. Never miss one. I noticed you breathe in fours and out sevens when the watch comes near. And I noticed—" he tapped the tabletop, twice, right where Cyrus's gloved hand rested, "—that when the thunder rolled last night, over the harbor, you didn't flinch like the others. Everyone else jumped. You listened."

The market noise fell away. Or Cyrus's hearing did; it was hard to tell the difference. Under the table, out of sight, their fingers curled, and the air between them prickled with the smell before a strike—ozone, hot metal, the taste of a coin held between the teeth.

"You're mistaken," Cyrus said, and their voice came out level, which cost them more than any charm they'd ever sold. "I'm deaf in one ear. I miss half of everything, including thunder."

"Mm," the old man said. "Deaf in one ear." He straightened up, unhurried, and reached into his coat, and Cyrus's heart slammed once against its seawall—but what he drew out was only a book. Small, thick, bound in leather gone soft and dark with age, its corners worn round as beach pebbles. Water-stained. Older than any book had a right to be, older than the Guild, older maybe than the cathedral whose bells were ringing the midnight hour just then, each toll landing in Cyrus's chest like a fist.

"Wrong stall, grandfather," Cyrus said. "We sell charms here. Not books."

"I know what you sell." The man set the book down on the table between them, gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping animal. "I found this thirty years ago, in a wreck off a coast that isn't on any map. Been carrying it ever since, waiting for the right buyer. Tonight I dreamed about your hands." He turned away, shuffling back into the dark between the stalls, and called back over his shoulder, without turning his head:

"Gloves and all."

Then he was gone, into the crowd that wasn't there, and the bells finished striking, and the book lay on Cyrus's table under the lamplight, breathing, almost, in the way that old things breathe—slowly, patiently, like something that had been waiting far longer than twelve years and intended to wait longer still.

Cyrus did not touch it.

Cyrus looked at it for a long time, while the lamps guttered and the ozone smell faded and the storm beneath their skin pressed its ear against the seawall, listening.

Then, because the alternative was leaving it for the morning street-sweepers, and because curiosity had always been the first crack in every wall they'd ever built, Cyrus picked it up—and the moment their gloved fingers closed on the cover, every one of the forty-two lamps in the night market flared white at once, all together, like a drawn breath—

—and somewhere across the sleeping city, in an office with clear glass windows, a bell that was not a bell began to ring.
