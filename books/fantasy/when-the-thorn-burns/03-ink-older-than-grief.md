## 3. Ink Older Than Grief

She came for me at dusk, when the market lamps were being lit one by one and the fog rolled in off the canal like something patient coming home.

I had spent the day selling charms I didn't believe in to people who needed to. A fisherman's wife bought a knot-charm for safe passage; a boy with chapped hands bought a luck-knot for his mother's cough. None of them worked, of course—or rather, they worked exactly as much as any lie works, which is to say the buyer did the labor of believing it and I took the coin. It was an honest arrangement, as far as dishonest ones go.

Then Wren stepped through the mist with her hood back and her hair damp, carrying a leather case under one arm like it was both precious and dangerous, and every careful habit I owned stood up inside me at once.

"You came," she said.

"I work here."

"I know. That's why I came here." She smiled, and I noticed she'd dressed plainly—plain wool, plain boots, no guild colors, nothing that announced her. She was good at not announcing herself. I recognized the craft the way you recognize a fellow swimmer by how they tread water. "Walk with me?"

"Where?"

"Somewhere we can't be overheard. Which in Vell means somewhere with a roof that leaks and neighbors who mind their own business out of long practice." She glanced at my stall, my little folding table, my tray of knots and beads and braided thread. "Bring nothing. You won't need your wares where we're going."

That sentence should have sent me home. Instead I folded the table, banked the lamp, and went.

* * *

She led me through streets I knew and then through a courtyard I didn't—a gap between two dyers' houses where the walls were stained blue and green from decades of runoff, and up an exterior stair so narrow we had to go single file. The room at the top belonged, apparently, to no one. It had one window, a stove, two chairs, and shelves. Shelves everywhere—rough planks on iron brackets, sagging under the weight of books wrapped in oilcloth against the damp.

"Do you live here?" I asked.

"No one lives here," she said. "That's the point. It's registered as a storage space for a rope merchant who died eleven years ago. The rent is paid through a dead man's estate, which means the Guild has never once looked at it, because the Guild does not audit the dead." She unwound her scarf. "Vell hates magic, but it adores paperwork. Paperwork is just magic that can't hurt anyone, so they trust it absolutely."

"And you? What do you adore?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face made her stop smiling—not sadly, just honestly.

"My name is Wren Halloran," she said. "Third archivist of the Sable Collection, formerly attached to the University at Corvane, currently attached to nothing, because the University decided my area of research was embarrassing and let me go with a letter of recommendation so bland it could have been written by a wall. I study erased things. Cities, faiths, languages, people. Things that were struck from the record so thoroughly that even the striking was struck."

"Ashkarin," I said.

Her eyebrows rose. "You know the word."

"It's a word mothers use to frighten children. *Be good or Ashkarin will take you.* Nobody knows what it means."

"That's what erasure looks like when it succeeds," she said quietly. "The name survives only as a bogeyman. A whole country reduced to a nursery rhyme." She crossed to the deepest shelf, the one behind the stove where the oilcloth wrapping was oldest, and lifted down a book.

It was big—big as a paving stone—and bound in something dark that wasn't quite leather. The clasps were iron, green with age. Even before she opened it, my skin prickled. Not the prickling of my own power, which I knew like my own heartbeat, low and banked beneath the sternum. This was different. This was like standing near a door in winter and feeling cold come through the seam.

"What is it?" My voice came out lower than I intended.

"This is the *Ledger of Thorns*," she said. "Copied in the last year of Ashkarin, from originals older still. There are four copies in the world that I know of. Two are ash. One sits in a sealed vault beneath the Grand Temple in Vell, where no archivist is permitted and where I am fairly sure no one has read it in eighty years. And this one—" she set it on the table between us, and her hands were steady, but she swallowed first, "—this one I have been carrying for six years, waiting to show it to someone."

"To whom?"

She didn't answer. She unclasped the iron.

The pages inside were vellum so thin it was translucent, dense with columns of script in an alphabet I didn't know—angular, thorned, each letter bristling like a hedgehog's back. Between the text ran illustrations: maps, constellations, diagrams of doors. Doors everywhere. Doors in hillsides, doors in the bellies of ships, doors drawn floating free of any wall at all, hanging in empty space with light spilling around their edges.

And dragons. Small, precise, lovingly rendered dragons coiled through the margins like ivy, their scales inked scale by scale, their eyes left blank white.

"The language is Ashkarin's own," Wren said. "It has no living speakers. It has no dead speakers either—that's the trick of the erasure. It wasn't just banned. It was *unlearned*. Everyone who knew it forgot it at the same moment, all across the country, mid-sentence if the stories are true. Priests stopped mid-prayer. Mothers stopped mid-lullaby. Whole libraries became decoration overnight." She turned a page. "Except."

"Except?"

"Except that certain kinds of knowing sit deeper than memory." She looked up at me. "Storm-calling, for instance. You feel it in your body before you understand it in your head, yes? Nobody taught you the shape of it. It was already there."

Every hair on my arms stood up. "Who told you about storm-calling?"

"You did," she said. "The night you sold me a protection charm. Your hands shook when the wind picked up, and you pressed them flat against the table to hide it, and you told me the weather in Vell always made your joints ache. Cyrus. Weather doesn't make anyone's joints ache like that. Only one kind of person's."

The room felt smaller. I kept my hands flat on the table anyway—an old habit, hiding old scars.

"I don't know what you think I am," I said, "but—"

"Let me show you first," she said. "Please. Then run, if you're going to run. I'd rather you ran knowing than not knowing. That's the whole religion of my profession: better a terrible truth than a comfortable blank."

I should have stood. I should have walked down those narrow stairs and back into the fog and never thought of Wren Halloran again except as a beautiful mistake I'd nearly made.

I nodded once, and she turned the page.

* * *

The next page was a portrait.

Not a flattering one, not a courtly one—the figure was drawn small, kneeling, seen from above, as if the artist had been looking down from a tower while the world burned below. The figure's head was bowed. Their hands were spread on the ground before them, palms up, fingers wide, and the artist had rendered those hands with obsessive care—far more care than the face, which was left unfinished, a smooth oval with no features at all.

Across the hands lay scars.

Long ones. Diagonal, crossing the backs of both hands, the kind a brand leaves when it's dragged rather than pressed—the kind that comes from being marked in a hurry, by someone whose hand slipped, or who wanted the mark to hurt going on as well as staying on.

I looked at my own hands.

I had spent twelve years not looking at them, and now I couldn't stop. The scars ran diagonal across both. Crossing. Dragged. I had told myself for twelve years that everyone's exile-brand healed differently, that mine was crooked because the priest's hand had shaken, because the brazier had guttered, because—

"Cyrus." Wren's voice was very soft. "May I?"

I don't remember agreeing. But her fingers hovered over the page, not touching it, tracing the air above the painted scars, and beside them my real scars sat on the wood grain, and they matched. Line for line. Angle for angle. Even the little starburst where the iron had caught and torn, here on the left hand, between the second and third finger.

"That's not possible," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "This book is a hundred years old. Older. You said—"

"The last year of Ashkarin. Before the fall. Before the erasure." She turned the page again, gently, as if the paper might bruise. "Read the caption. Well—you can't read it. No one can. But I've spent six years on this alphabet, cross-referencing fragments, and I can tell you what it says, and you should hear it sitting down, although you already are, so perhaps just breathe."

"Breathe," I repeated stupidly.

"It says: *Here is the Thorn, who comes twice-born, marked on the hand, walking unseen among the godless. The Thorn will stand at the Door in the last hour of the last fire, and the Thorn will choose what burns.*"

The stove ticked. Somewhere below us a dog barked twice and gave up.

"There's more," Wren said.

"Don't stop now," I said, and hated how steady it came out. Twelve years of practice, steadiness. It held.

"*When the Thorn burns,*" she recited, eyes on the page, "*the fire dies and the world forgets warmth. When the fire dies and the Thorn remains, the Door opens and the erased return. But the Thorn cannot have both hands—one holds the storm, and one must hold the flame, and the price of either is everything the other loves.*" She looked up. "That's the passage. There are commentaries, marginal notes, arguments between three different scribes about what the last line means. They died arguing. The city fell before they finished."

I stared at the kneeling figure. At the featureless face.

"Why is there no face?" I asked.

Wren hesitated. "Because the scribes didn't know who it would be. The prophecy isn't a portrait, Cyrus. It's a description. Marked hands. Twice-born—exiled, reborn somewhere else. Walking unseen among the godless—" she gestured, taking in Vell, the whole grey hating city, "—that's here. That's now. And the hands—" 

"The hands match mine," I finished.

"Yes."

"Which means one of two things. Either this prophecy is a hundred years old and describes a man who wasn't born yet—which is impossible—or someone forged a book six years ago, learned about my scars, and built a trap around me." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too high. "Which is it, Wren? Because I've met traps before. I've lived inside one for twelve years. They usually come with nicer furniture and a dinner invitation."

"Dinner was real," she said, and there was an edge in her voice now, hurt and honesty tangled together. "But I'm not going to insult you by swearing oaths. Look at the ironwork on the clasps. Look at the vellum—I'll put it in your hands, burn it if you want, smell it, hold it to the light. I've had offers for this book, Cyrus. Serious ones. Men who would pay enough to buy this building and every book in it. If I wanted to sell you a forgery I could have sold it cheaper and slept better."

"Then why show me? Why wait six years? Why me?"

She closed the book. Her hands stayed flat on its cover, and for the first time since I'd met her, Wren Halloran looked afraid.

"Because three weeks ago," she said, "someone else started looking for you. Someone with Guild seals and a budget and a list of charm-sellers in the night market, working down it alphabetically. You're a C, Cyrus. There are only four sellers between you and D, and one of them talks too much and owes the Guild money." She exhaled. "I showed you the book tonight instead of next month because next month may belong to whoever's holding that list. Whatever you decide—run, laugh at me, report me to the magistrates—you deserved to see it before they found you. Everyone deserves to see the shape of the cage before it closes. That's the other half of my religion."

The candle guttered. Outside, the fog had thickened until the window showed nothing but grey, as if the whole city had been erased while we talked, as if Vell itself were practicing for what had been done to Ashkarin.

My hands wouldn't stop trembling now, and I let them. There was no one here to hide them from anymore. The secret I'd guarded with my life for twelve years was sitting on a table between us, wearing iron clasps, older than grief.

"Say the caption again," I said. "All of it. Slowly."

"*Here is the Thorn, who comes twice-born—*"

Twice-born. Exile and return. Marked on the hand. Walking unseen among the godless.

"—and the Thorn will choose what burns."

Choose. Not suffer. Not fulfill. Choose. Some small stubborn part of me, some part that had survived a pyre-adjacent childhood and twelve years of small magics and small lies, seized on that word like a drowning man seizing driftwood.

"And the rest?" I asked. "The part about the price. *Everything the other loves.* What does that mean?"

"No one knows," Wren said. "That's the honest answer. The scribes argued about it until the end. Some read it as literal—everything you love dies. Some read it as payment—whatever you love most, you give up. And one marginal note, in a different hand, much later, just says—" she paused, and her voice dropped, "—*ask the dragon.*"

"The dragon," I repeated. "Yssara."

Now it was her turn to go still. "How do you know that name?"

I hadn't known I knew it. It had simply risen, the way a storm rises—out of some deep pressure front in me, unbidden. Yssara. A name like a struck bell, like heat shimmer, like the space above a candle flame. I looked at my scarred hands and they seemed suddenly less like wounds and more like a lock waiting for a key I'd been carrying all along without knowing.

"I don't," I said slowly. "Or I do. Both. Wren—if this book is true, then someone is hunting me, and I'm named in a century-dead prophecy, and there's a dragon involved, and a Door, and a country that got deleted. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means your quiet life is over," she said gently. "I'm sorry. I truly am."

"No." I shook my head. "What it means is that in the morning, I'm going to open my stall like always, sell my worthless charms like always, and be very, very careful. And you're going to tell me everything—who's hunting me, what the Guild knows, why an archivist carries apocalypse around in a satchel—and then, Wren, then we decide what happens next. Together. Because if this cage is closing, I am not standing in the middle of it waiting to find out what it's made of."

Something loosened in her face. Relief, maybe. Or respect. With her it was hard to tell; everything arrived at once, layered, like strata in the books she loved.

"Fair terms," she said.

"One more term." I pointed at the Ledger, at the kneeling, faceless figure with my hands. "Nobody calls me the Thorn. Not yet. Maybe not ever. I've been called things by priests before and none of them stuck, because I decided what I was. That's the one thing the exile never managed to take."

"Agreed," she said softly. Then, almost to herself, looking at the blank oval where the figure's face should be: "You know, that's why the face is empty. I used to think it was damage, or reverence, or fear. But I think the scribes understood something the Inquisition never has. Prophecies don't make people. People make prophecies true. The face was left blank because whoever fills it chooses how."

Outside, far off toward the Grand Square, a bell began to ring—the evening bell, ordinary, municipal, dull as bread. Neither of us moved for a moment.

Then Wren stiffened, head tilting, listening past the bell the way I listened past weather.

"That's wrong," she murmured. "The evening bell rings seven times. Listen. It's still ringing."

It was. Nine strokes. Ten. Eleven—faster now, urgent, the rhythm of alarm rather than ritual. Down in the fog, voices were rising in the streets, and torchlight bloomed orange against the grey like a stain spreading through water.

"They've reached the night market early," Wren breathed, snuffing the candle with two fingers. "Cyrus—they're not searching alphabetically anymore. They're burning stalls."

Through the fogged window, faint and terrible, I watched the glow gather over the district where my stall stood, where my neighbors slept, where a boy with chapped hands had bought a charm for his mother's cough not twelve hours ago.

And underneath my fear, underneath twelve years of discipline, something ancient stirred and stretched, tasting the coming smoke on the air like rain.
