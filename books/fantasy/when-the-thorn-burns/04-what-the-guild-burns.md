## 4. What the Guild Burns

The bells of Vell rang wrong that morning.

Cyrus heard it before they understood it—three strokes where the dawn bell should have been one, a stutter in the bronze like a heart skipping. They had lived in this city long enough to know its sounds the way other people knew their own pulse: the fish-carts rattling over the cobblestones on Tallow Street, the gulls arguing above the harbor roofs, the particular groan of the ferry chains at slack tide. Vell's bells were its heartbeat, and this morning the heartbeat stumbled.

They were packing up their stall when the second bell came, and then a third, and then the criers.

*"By order of the Inquisitor's Guild—"*

The words rolled down the narrow streets like oil down a drain, finding every crack, seeping into every doorway. Cyrus's hands went still around a luck-charm—a knuckle of river-stone wrapped in copper wire, worth three coppers and a lie. Around them, the night market was folding itself away, awnings coming down, vendors suddenly very interested in their own ledgers. Fear moved through the crowd the way weather moves through wheat: you couldn't see it, but you could watch it bend everything.

*By order of the Inquisitor's Guild, all unlicensed sellers of charms, wards, and blessings will present themselves for inspection.*

Cyrus had papers. Of course they had papers—forged so carefully, so expensively, that even Cyrus sometimes forgot they were lies. A license stamped with the seal of a guild clerk who had never existed, dated four years back, ink aged with tea and patience. The papers would hold. The papers always held.

But the bells kept ringing, and the criers kept walking, and Cyrus stood in the gray light with a stone charm in their palm and felt, for the first time in months, the storm under their skin stir—not summoned, not called, just *turning over in its sleep*, the way a dog turns when it hears a stranger at the gate.

"Crane." Old Maretta from the candle stall, her voice low, her eyes flicking toward the harbor square. "You hear? They've got one already. Down by the fish-weighing stones. Caught him last night, they're saying. Selling *blessed* thread to sailors."

"Blessed thread." Cyrus made their voice bored, easy, the voice of a man whose only crime was commerce. "Gods help us all."

Maretta didn't smile. She was seventy if she was a day, and she had survived Vell long enough to know when a joke wasn't wanted. "You should come see," she said. "Everyone's going. Better to be seen going, I think. Better to be part of the crowd than absent from it."

It was good advice. It was the kind of advice that kept old women alive in cities like this one. And so Cyrus closed their stall, tucked the luck-charm into their coat pocket where it pressed against their ribs like a small cold heart, and walked with the thinning market crowd toward the harbor square, wearing invisibility like a second skin.

They told themselves it was strategy. They told themselves it was necessary.

They did not tell themselves the truth until they saw the scaffold.

* * *

Halvard Voss stood at the rail of the inspection platform and watched the crowd gather, and he counted them.

That was the first thing they had taught him, thirty years ago, when he was a raw-boned novice with ink still under his fingernails: *a crowd is not a mob until you count it.* Two hundred souls, give or take, spilling out of the morning lanes into the harbor square, drawn by the oldest hunger in any city—the hunger to watch someone else suffer and be glad, for one hour, that it isn't you. He counted them because somewhere among them, if the reports held true, was a storm-caller wearing a merchant's face.

The reports had begun eleven days ago. A sailor in the Salt Quarter, drunk, swearing he'd seen lightning crawl along a charm-seller's arm in the dark of an alley—lightning with no cloud to mother it, no thunder behind it, just a blue-white shimmer that died as soon as it was seen. The man had recanted twice, wept once, and finally, under the gentle pressure of a guild truth-tincture, given a description: youngish. Dark-haired. Hands scarred across both palms, old burns, the kind left by rope or wire pulled hard through a closing fist.

Voss had read the description four times.

He had read it four times because he had seen those hands before. Twelve years ago, in a courtroom in the provincial capital of Harrowgate, attached to a frightened nineteen-year-old who had called a storm out of a clear sky and drowned half a valley's worth of fields saving a village that then had the temerity to testify against him. Voss remembered the hands because he had described them himself, in his deposition, in language chosen for its precision: *the accused exhibits scarring consistent with ritual binding practices upon both palms.*

He had written that sentence, and that sentence had ended a life—or begun its exile—and built a career on top of it like a house on a foundation. Senior Investigator. Then Chief Interrogator. Then, two years ago, when the old Grand Inquisitor had died choking on his own ambition, Voss had been passed over. Passed over for a younger man with better family connections, and the word *ruined* had begun to follow him through the guild halls like a dog.

But now. Now there was a storm-caller in Vell. Now there were scarred hands selling charms in the night market. Now there was a prophecy-book rumor moving through the archive offices like smoke through a keyhole—something about Ashkarin, something about a Thorn—and Voss, who had spent thirty years learning to read the shape of what powerful people feared, could smell which way the wind blew in the Council chambers.

If he found the Thorn, he would not be passed over again.

"Master Voss." His junior, Pell, a soft-bodied young man with a nervous talent for cruelty, nodded toward the prisoner. "Shall we begin?"

Below the platform, chained to the weighing-stones at the center of the square, knelt a charm-seller named Odrik Fenn. Voss had reviewed his file at dawn. Fifty-one years old. Licensed, technically—his license lapsed eight months ago when the renewal fee rose and his wife's cough got worse. Sold blessed thread, saint-metals, small woven knots against nightmares. The kind of magic that wasn't magic at all, really. The kind that worked only because frightened people needed it to.

Which was, of course, precisely why it had to burn. Faith was a door, and doors swung both ways.

"Begin," Voss said.

Pell read the charges in a voice pitched to carry. *Unlicensed practice. Trafficking in consecrated goods without sanction. Impersonation of priestly office.* The crowd murmured, settled, went quiet in the way crowds do when the entertainment starts. Odrik Fenn lifted his head. He had been made to kneel all night, and his face had the gray, washed-out look of a man who had already done all his crying in private and had nothing left for the audience.

"Do you deny the charges?" Voss asked. His voice was not loud. It never needed to be. Thirty years of standing in quiet rooms with terrified people had taught him exactly how much volume fear required, and the answer was almost none.

"I sold thread," Fenn said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Thread and tin medals. My wife is dying, master inquisitor. The physickers want forty crowns a visit. The thread sells for two bits. I don't—I never claimed—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "I never hurt anyone."

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "You never do. That is what makes your kind so dangerous, Master Fenn. The poisoner announces himself. The cutpurse leaves a wound. But the charm-seller—" he let the pause stretch, let the crowd lean in, "—the charm-seller teaches people to stop looking for real help. To put their trust in string and superstition while the true sickness spreads. You are not a criminal, Master Fenn. You are a *symptom*. And symptoms must be treated at the source."

He gestured, and two guildsmen brought forward the brazier.

It was a modest thing, iron-legged, already glowing. In it lay the confiscated stock: the blessed thread coiled like pale hair, the tin medals, the little knotted cords. As the crowd watched, the fire took the thread first—it burned fast, smelling of tallow and cheap dye—and then the medals began to weep, tin running in bright silver threads down into the coals.

Fenn made a sound. Not a word. Just a sound, low in his chest, the sound of a man watching his wife's medicine go up in smoke.

And Cyrus, standing in the third row of the crowd with a stranger's elbow in their ribs and the luck-charm burning cold against their sternum, felt twelve years collapse like a folded map.

* * *

Harrowgate. The provincial court. Nineteen years old, wrists bound in salt-iron, standing in a dock built for murderers while the gallery stared at him the way people stare at a dangerous animal that has, inexplicably, learned to speak.

Cyrus had saved them. That was the thing no one ever said aloud, the thing that sat at the bottom of every memory of that day like a stone at the bottom of a well. The river had come up overnight—spring melt plus the week of rain—and the village of Lowmarsh had gone under in the dark, and Cyrus had stood on the high ground with Sefa's hand slipping out of theirs and called the storm *down*, bent it, wrung it out over the hills where it could do no harm, spent every scrap of everything they had, and woken three days later in a guild cell while the village elders gave testimony about crops blighted by unnatural weather and a boy who *commanded the sky*.

And Halvard Voss—younger then, sharp-faced, precise, kind in the way that a scalpel is kind—had taken their statement gently. Had offered water. Had said, *I believe you meant no harm,* and written down every word anyway, and shaped those words into a noose.

*The accused exhibits scarring consistent with ritual binding practices upon both palms.*

Cyrus looked at their hands now, at the crowd's edge, at the scars that crossed each palm in pale lines. They hadn't been ritual bindings. They'd been fishing line, and rigging rope, and a childhood on the water. But Voss had known what to call them, and what you called a thing, in Vell, was what it became.

On the platform, the interrogation continued. Pell was working through the formal questions—names of suppliers, names of customers, names of anyone else who might have bought or sold or believed—and Fenn was answering, answering everything, giving up names like a man pulling splinters out of his own flesh, and none of it mattered because the verdict had been decided before the bells rang. Everyone in the square knew it. That was the function of the square.

Then Voss raised one hand, and Pell stopped, and Voss leaned over the rail with the patient expression of a teacher addressing a slow student, and said:

"One more matter, Master Fenn. A formality." He produced a sheet of paper. "In recent weeks, my officers have received reports of unusual activity in the night markets. Charms of a more... potent character. Weather-workings. Storm-charms." He paused, delicately. "Have you heard such talk?"

The square went very still.

Cyrus's blood went cold—not the dramatic cold of storybooks but the animal kind, the cold of a body deciding, all on its own, to pour everything into running. Because that question wasn't for Fenn. That question was a net thrown over the whole crowd, and everyone standing in it knew it, and everyone standing in it performed the same small theater of blankness, and Cyrus performed it too, blankness layered over blankness, while under their skin the storm turned restlessly in its sleep.

"I—no, master. I sell thread. Thread and medals. I don't know anything about storms."

"No," Voss said softly. "I imagine you don't." He straightened. He surveyed the crowd—not searching, exactly; that was the artistry of it, the survey was leisurely, almost bored, a man looking at nothing in particular—and Cyrus understood, with the sick clarity of the hunted, that Voss was letting *them* look at *him*. Letting the description circulate. Letting two hundred witnesses carry his face home to their dreams, so that tomorrow, someone would remember a neighbor who fit, a stranger seen once, a stall that closed too quickly.

The hunt had begun. Everything else this morning—the thread, the medals, the broken man on the stones—was scenery.

"Master Fenn," Voss said. "For the traffic in false blessing, the penalty is forfeiture of goods and a fine of fifty crowns. For practicing without license, six months' labor. These are mercies, and you will receive them." He let the crowd exhale. He let hope get one full breath of air. "However. If it should emerge that you have withheld knowledge of proscribed practitioners—if it should emerge that you *know* things you have not told me—then the penalty becomes fire. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Fenn whispered. "Yes, master."

"Wonderful." Voss smiled. It reached his eyes. That was the worst part, Cyrus thought. It reached his eyes. "Then we are finished here. Someone fetch this man a chair. He's had a long night."

* * *

The crowd dispersed the way water finds its level—slowly, without any single decision, everyone drifting toward the exits while pretending they weren't fleeing. Cyrus drifted with them, past the candle stalls shuttering early, past a knot of children reenacting the burning with sticks and a scrap of cloth, out onto Tallow Street where the ordinary noise of the city closed over the morning like water over a dropped coin.

They made it three streets before their hands started shaking.

Cyrus ducked into the lee of a cooper's doorway, pressed their back against the planks, and breathed. In. Out. The old discipline, the one they'd taught themselves in twelve years of exile: *you are a charm-seller. You are tired and slightly dishonest and entirely unremarkable. There is no storm here.* Under their palms, the scars ached the way they always ached when the memory got loose—Harrowgate, the cell, Sefa's face through the bars, saying *I can't* in a voice that meant *I won't*.

*Storm-charms,* Voss had said. *Weather-workings.*

Eleven days since the sailor's drunken tale. Eleven days, and already the machinery was turning: criers, bells, a public breaking, a question cast over a crowd like chum into deep water. Voss was methodical. That was what made him terrifying—not cruelty, though there was plenty, but *patience*. He would work the markets stall by stall. He would buy charm-sellers' silence and then spend it. He would follow the trade routes of rumor backward, link by link, until—

Until he found a stall that sold luck-charms of river-stone and copper wire, run by a dark-haired seller with scarred hands.

Cyrus pushed off from the wall. Their legs felt strange, distant, like borrowed limbs. They needed to move the stall. Today. New quarter, new name on the awning, new face in the crowd. They needed to warn Maretta off—no, not warn, that would endanger her, just... drift away, let the association cool. They needed to burn the forged license and forge a better one. They needed—

They needed, some traitorous corner of their mind observed, to find Wren.

The thought arrived uninvited and unwelcome, wearing the memory of last week like a stolen coat: the archivist with the quick laugh and the ink-stained fingers, the dinner that had stretched to midnight, the way she'd listened—actually *listened*—as if Cyrus were a person rather than a performance. *Come find me at the Great Archive,* she'd said. *Any day. Ask for the maps room.*

Wren, who worked among old books. Wren, who had asked more questions than curiosity strictly required. Wren, who had appeared in Cyrus's life three weeks ago out of nowhere, charming and warm and *exactly* when a certain sailor happened to be drinking in the Salt Quarter.

No. That was fear talking. Fear saw conspiracies in kindness because kindness was more frightening than knives. Wren was just a woman who liked old books and bad wine and—

And the bells rang again, midday now, and Cyrus realized they were standing motionless in the middle of Tallow Street with people flowing around them like a stream around a stone.

They turned toward the harbor district instead of the Archive. Toward the stall. Toward work, packing, flight, the thousand small labors of disappearing. One more day of being nobody. One more day of the small magics, the safe ones, the ones that cost nothing but a lie.

Behind them, unnoticed, a guild clerk in a gray coat noted something in a ledger: *Tallow Street, charm-seller, dark-haired, lingered at dispersal. Description appended.*

The hunt had begun. And it had already written down its first new name.

* * *

That evening, in the guildhouse on Candlemaker's Row, Halvard Voss stood at his window with a glass of thin red wine and read the day's reports by lamplight.

Forty-one charm-sellers inspected. Six arrests pending. Three informants recruited—one of them promising, a widow in the night market who talked too easily when frightened. And one anomaly: a vendor on Tallow Street who had packed his stall within the hour of the square's dispersal and not reopened it.

*Dark-haired. Youngish. Scarred hands, per the widow Maretta's account—old burns, both palms.*

Voss set down the report. He did not smile. Smiling was for public rooms. What he felt was quieter and far more durable: the feeling of a lock beginning to turn after years of rust.

Twelve years ago, he had written a sentence that ended a storm-caller's life in Harrowgate, and the sentence had been *true*—that was the thing his critics had never understood, the thing that kept him awake on the bad nights, turning it over like a stone. Every word of his deposition had been accurate. The boy *had* commanded the sky. The village *had* suffered. Voss had simply chosen which truths to set side by side, and the arrangement had done the killing, and the arrangement had been *art*.

Now the same hands were selling charms in his city. Now there was talk of a book, of Ashkarin, of a *Thorn*—nonsense, mostly, archive-dust and superstition, but nonsense with weight behind it now, nonsense that made Council members lower their voices. And if the Thorn was real, and if the Thorn was the boy from Harrowgate grown into a man with twelve years of hiding behind him—

Then Voss would do what he had always done. He would arrange the truths. He would build the sentence. And this time, the sentence would not end in exile.

He poured the wine out, untasted—he allowed himself the smell, never the drink, not on working nights—and lit a fresh lamp, and began, in his precise, beautiful hand, to write the warrant.

Across the city, in a rented room above a shuttered stall, Cyrus Crane sat awake in the dark with a knife across their knees and the storm humming under their skin, listening to the bells count the hours, and did not sleep.
