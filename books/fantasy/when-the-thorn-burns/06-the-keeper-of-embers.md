## 6. The Keeper of Embers

Bren Halloway had three rules for the Saltcellar: nobody touched the lantern, nobody asked what was in the lantern, and anybody who used the word *magic* within earshot of his table bought the whole room a round.

He'd made it eleven days without breaking rule three himself, which he counted as a personal triumph. Then the woman with the ink-stained fingers walked in with a stranger on her arm, and Bren knew—before they'd crossed half the tavern, before she said a word—that the streak was over.

He knew Wren Calloway by reputation only, which in Vell meant he knew her by rumor, which meant he knew nothing true at all except that archivists were worse than tax collectors and twice as persistent. She'd been asking after him in the low quarters for a week, according to Tam the fishwife, who sold information the way other people sold herring: cheap, fresh, and slightly rotten. A scholar looking for a dragon-keeper. In Vell. Where dragons were a bedtime story mothers told to make children afraid of open flames.

*Come on, then,* Bren thought into his cup. *Let's see what you're selling.*

The man beside her was the problem.

Bren had spent thirty years around creatures whose blood ran hot enough to melt iron. He'd learned to read heat the way sailors read weather—a skill in the wrists and eyes more than any formal training—and the stranger's skin read wrong. Not fever-wrong. *Weather*-wrong. There was a pressure under him, the way the air went flat and metallic before lightning, and when the lantern-light caught his hands, Bren saw the scars: long silver welts across both palms, branching like river deltas seen from a mountain.

Lichtenberg figures. Lightning flowers.

Bren set down his cup very slowly and did not look up again until they were standing right in front of him, which took another forty heartbeats because Wren wanted to be dramatic about it, and dramatic women had gotten him killed before. Well. Gotten other people killed before. Close enough.

"Bren," Wren said. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive." He gestured at the empty stools across from him with two fingers, an invitation shaped like an insult. "Sit. You're making my customers nervous, and my customers are the kind that stab first and apologize to nobody."

"Charming as ever." She sat. The stranger didn't. He stood there like a fence post with anxiety, scanning the room—the exits, the windows, the fat merchant snoring over his wine—and Bren revised his assessment upward. Not just a storm-caller. A *hiding* storm-caller. Which meant hunted, which meant trouble, which meant exactly the kind of company Bren had sworn off along with sober mornings and honest work.

"I'll stand," the stranger said.

"You'll sit," Bren said, "because standing men draw eyes, and I'm about to say words that'll get us all fitted for pyres if the wrong ears catch them. Sit."

The stranger sat. Up close, the wrongness was worse—a faint smell of ozone under the tavern's grease-and-ale stink, and a tension in him like a wire strung too tight, humming at some frequency Bren felt in his back teeth rather than heard. Thirty years since he'd felt that hum. He'd have paid good money never to feel it again.

"Wren tells me you're an archivist," Bren said, keeping his voice at tavern-murmur level. "And your friend here sells charms at the night market. Luck-charms. Love-charms. Perfectly harmless knickknacks for the godless masses." He let that hang. "So why is she bringing him to me?"

"Because you still have it," Wren said.

"Have what? I have a debt to Marla behind the bar, a bad knee, and a bottle of Kessrin red that's older than both of you. That's the inventory."

"The coal, Bren."

The word landed in his chest like a dropped anvil. Bren kept his face still through sheer professional habit—he'd once held a dying hatchling's jaw closed for six hours without letting his grief show, because dragons could smell despair the way wolves smelled fear—but inside, everything went cold and then hot and then cold again.

"There's no coal," he said. "There's no dragon. There's no Ashkarin. There never was, officially, and if you go around saying otherwise in this city, the Guild will introduce you to a bonfire and a confession. So drink your wine or order something, and then take your lightning-flowered friend and get out of my tavern."

The stranger flinched. Small, but Bren caught it. So: the scars were known to him. Good. Meant he wasn't entirely stupid.

"We know about Yssara," Wren said quietly.

The name went through Bren like a spear through wet paper. Thirty years of careful forgetting, of drinking until the name blurred into noise, and this slip of a woman with her ink-stained fingers just said it out loud in a Vell tavern like she was ordering bread.

He stood up. His knee screamed. He ignored it the way he ignored most things these days, which was badly.

"Outside," he said. "Both of you. Now."

* * *

The alley behind the Saltcellar smelled of rotting cabbage and old rain, which Bren supposed was fitting, given the company. He'd brought the lantern. Of course he'd brought the lantern—he hadn't let it out of arm's reach in twelve years, not to piss, not to sleep, not when the Guild swept the quarter and he'd hidden in a drain culvert for two days with rats for company. The lantern came. It always came.

Inside its smoked-glass housing, behind three layers of charm-work that would fool anything short of an Inquisitor's own sight, the ember sat in its bed of white ash.

It was smaller than a fist now. When Bren had taken it—stolen it, rescued it, *saved* it, the verb changed depending on how drunk he was and who was asking—it had been the size of a heart, pulsing slow and vast, warm enough to feel through the glass like a second sun against his palm. Now it was a coal. A dying coal. Its light came in fits and starts, like breathing through water, and some nights Bren woke convinced it had finally gone out and lay there in the dark too afraid to look.

He set the lantern on a crate between them and stood over it with his arms crossed, feeling like a father presenting a disappointing child to strangers.

"That," he said, "is the heart-flame of Yssara, last of the ashwyrms, last of anything with wings bigger than a pigeon. She burned for four thousand years. Her fire kept the passes open, ripened the vineyards, taught the storms where to walk—" he nodded, deliberately, at the scarred-handed stranger, "—and when Ashkarin fell, her fire was all that was left of it. All that anyone thought was left of it." He looked at Wren. "How do you know that name? The records were burned. The minds were scoured. Even I can't remember the shape of the place anymore, and I *lived* there. I remember that I lived there the way you remember a dream—knowing it happened, unable to hold a single room of it in my head. So how does an archivist from Vell know a dead dragon's name?"

"Because someone wrote it down before the erasure," Wren said. "In a book the Guild doesn't know exists. And that book says the fire isn't dead yet. And it says something else, too." She glanced at the stranger. "It says the Thorn will come to the keeper of embers, and the keeper will know him by his hands."

Bren laughed. It came out ugly.

"The Thorn. Right. The prophecy. Let me tell you what I know about prophecies, girl. I watched the priests of Ashkarin read theirs every dawn for a hundred years, and the day the empire came over the mountains, do you know what the prophecy did? Nothing. It sat there being holy while the sky filled with war-galleys and the cities burned. Prophecies are what frightened people write so the future feels like a plan instead of a knife coming down." He jabbed a finger at the stranger. "And you. Whatever she told you, whatever the book showed you—walk away. Find a hole deeper than mine and crawl in it. Because I'll tell you what happens to people who get close to this flame."

His voice cracked, and he hated it, and he kept going anyway because if he stopped he'd never start again.

"I had a crew. Eleven keepers, the finest oath-sworn blood in Ashkarin—or whatever Ashkarin was; I can't even picture the towers anymore, isn't that a joke, I fed that dragon every morning for twenty years and I cannot picture the color of her door. Eleven keepers. We carried the ember out through the tunnels while the erasure ate our home behind us. Do you know how you outrun something that eats *memory*? You don't. You just run faster than the part of you that knows where you're going. Oren went first, and one morning we woke up and none of us could remember his face, only that we'd lost someone, and we cried for a man who was a hole in the shape of a man. Then Marette. Then the twins. One by one, until it was me and Doss and a coal going cold in a lantern. Doss drank himself into the canal four years back. Very tidy. The Guild called it an accident." Bren picked up the lantern, and his hands were shaking, and he let them shake because there was no one left alive whose opinion of him mattered. "This is what comes of the keeper's oath. You carry a fire the world has decided to forget, alone, in a country that burns people like you for existing, watching it die by inches, and when it dies, the last of a four-thousand-year-old species dies with it, and nobody will even know to mourn. That's the job. That's the *glory*. Go home."

Silence. Cabbage-rot and dripping eaves. Somewhere above, a cat knocked over a bottle, and the crash rang out and faded.

It was the stranger who spoke. His voice was quiet, roughened, like a man who'd done his shouting years ago and found it changed nothing.

"How much longer?" he asked. "If nothing changes. How long does she have?"

Bren blinked. Of all the responses he'd braced for—pity, fear, the glazed look of people deciding he was mad—this one caught him sideways. He looked down at the lantern. Through the smoked glass, the ember pulsed. Dim. Dimmer than last month. Dimmer than last week.

"Seasons," he said. "A year, maybe, if I feed it well. There are... rites. Old ones. They slow the guttering but they cost—" He stopped. "They cost things. Everything costs things. Didn't you know that yet, storm-caller? No. Look at your face. You don't. Well." He almost laughed again. "Enjoy that. Enjoy not knowing. It doesn't last."

"My name's Cyrus," the stranger said. "Not storm-caller."

"Cyrus." Bren tasted the name and found nothing in it, no omen, no weight. Just a name. That was almost restful. "Fine. Cyrus. Here's what I know that you don't, since Wren's clearly been rationing the truth the way I ration this—" he tapped the lantern. "That ember isn't just a relic. It's *her*. Not a symbol of Yssara, not a souvenir. Her actual living heart-fire, cut from her chest the night Ashkarin fell, while her body sleeps somewhere no map remembers, waiting for the fire to either return or go out. If it goes out, she dies in her sleep, gentle as an old dog, and the world loses the last creature that ever loved a storm on purpose." His eyes went to Cyrus's hands, to the silver deltas crawling over them, and something old and tired and desperate turned over in his chest—the thing he'd sworn was dead, the thing that had kept him walking for twelve years, and he hated it, hated it, hated that it still moved. "And if the fire returns..."

"Then she wakes," Cyrus said softly.

"Then she wakes," Bren agreed, "and the world finds out what woke her. And every priest, king, and Inquisitor on this rotten continent decides whether that's salvation or apocalypse, mostly without asking her opinion. So no, charm-seller, I don't want your help. Your kind of help has a body count, and I've buried enough of my crew to fill a—"

He stopped. Because the ember had flickered.

Not guttered—*flared*. Once. A single pulse of gold through the smoked glass, bright enough to throw their shadows huge against the brickwork, bright enough that Bren saw Cyrus's face lit from below like a man standing before an altar. And in thirty years of tending dragonfire, Bren had learned its moods the way you learn a lover's breathing: hunger, sleep, contentment, pain.

This was none of those.

This was *recognition*.

"Oh," Bren said. His mouth had gone dry. "Oh, you absolute bastard."

"What?" Wren demanded. "What is it? What happened?"

"She sees him." Bren stared at the lantern, and the lantern seemed to stare back, and somewhere beneath the ash a heart four thousand years old was beating a little faster than it had in twelve years, and Bren Halloway, disgraced keeper, drunk, coward, last of eleven, felt his oath wake in his chest like an animal uncurling. "Twelve years I've carried her, and she hasn't so much as sighed, and this one walks into an alley smelling of lightning and she *flares*." He looked up at Cyrus, and it wasn't kindness in his face and it wasn't hope, exactly. It was the expression of a man reading his own death warrant and finding, to his horror, that he was relieved. "The book was right. Damn the book. Damn prophecy to every hell there is. You're the Thorn."

Cyrus's face went pale as candle wax. "I don't want to be the Thorn. I never asked—"

"Nobody asks!" The laugh tore out of Bren, wild and bitter and half grief. "Nobody *asks*, boy, that's the entire point! I didn't ask to be a keeper. She didn't ask to be the last. Ashkarin didn't ask to be erased. You don't ask, you just wake up one morning and the story has your name in it, and the only choice left is whether you run from the page or stand on it!"

He crouched, knees cracking, and lifted the lantern, holding it out—not giving it away, never that, but showing it, letting its dying light fall on them both.

"So here's how it goes," he said. "I don't trust you. I don't like you. You've got Guild hunters sniffing after you, a prophecy hanging round your neck like a millstone, and hands that scream *storm* loud enough to wake the dead—which, congratulations, you already did." He jerked his chin at the ember, glowing faintly, stubbornly, against the dark. "But she chose to show herself to you, and in thirty years she has never once been wrong about a person. Not once. So." He straightened, and the oath rose in him, the old words, the ones he could no longer fully remember in his own language but which lived in his body like marrow: *What the fire loves, the keeper guards.*

"Sunrise tomorrow, the drowned chapel on Tanner's Row. Come armed with the truth, the whole truth, because I'll be asking questions Wren here won't enjoy." He shot her a look that promised exactly that. "And if you're late—if you decide this is too heavy and slink off to sell luck-charms till the pyres find you—I will drink to your health and never think of you again. That's the deal. That's the only deal I've got left."

Cyrus looked at the lantern a long moment. Something passed over his face—fear, wonder, recognition, Bren couldn't parse it and didn't try. When he spoke, his voice was barely above the drip of the eaves.

"Why sunrise?"

"Because," Bren said, turning toward the tavern door, the lantern swinging warm and dim against his hip, "last night, for the first time in twelve years, she dreamed aloud. Whole words. And do you know what she said?" He paused at the threshold, silhouetted against the light spilling from the Saltcellar's windows, and delivered it flat, the way you deliver news you've been carrying too long:

"*The Door is opening.*"

He walked into the tavern and poured himself the first honest drink he'd had in years.
