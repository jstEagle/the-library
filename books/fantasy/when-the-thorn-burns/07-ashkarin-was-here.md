## 7. Ashkarin Was Here

Bren had a system for mornings, and the system was this: drink until yesterday made sense or until today stopped mattering, whichever came first.

Today neither was working. The charm-seller sat on his one good stool with their hands wrapped around a cup of tea Bren had made from boiled pine needles because it was that or nothing, and Wren stood at his window like she owned it, peering through the gap in the shutters at the street below. The coal sat in its iron cradle on the table between them, doing what it always did — burning without consuming, glowing without warming, being the single most impossible thing in a city full of people who would burn Bren alive if they knew he kept it.

"You said last night you'd explain," Cyrus said. "The Door. Ashkarin. All of it."

Bren took a long pull from the bottle. It was early for drinking, but then, it was late for everything else.

"There's a word," he said, "for what happened to Ashkarin, but nobody remembers it, which is rather the point."

* * *

He'd been twenty-three when they gave him the ember. Younger than that when they taught him the oath. *Keeper of the Last Flame: I will hold what cannot hold itself. I will remember what is made to be forgotten.*

He'd thought it was poetry. Keepers' oaths were always poetry; old men wrote them by candlelight and young men recited them without understanding, the way soldiers learn marching songs about battles they haven't bled in yet. Bren had learned his in a stone hall under the Ashkarin mountains, in a country whose name he could still say only because the oath held it like a hand holds water — loosely, reverently, knowing it would run through his fingers the moment he let go.

"Ashkarin," he said aloud now, testing it.

Cyrus frowned slightly. "Say it again."

"Ashkarin." He watched the charm-seller's face do something complicated — recognition and non-recognition fighting for the same ground. "You felt that. The slip. Like a name on your tongue that isn't yours to have."

"I've never heard of—"

"You have. Everyone has. That's the trick of it." Bren set the bottle down, because some things deserved a steady hand. "Ashkarin didn't fall the way countries fall. It didn't burn, didn't starve, wasn't conquered. It was *unmade*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Wren turned from the window. She'd been listening the way she listened to everything — completely, hungrily, like a woman who had spent her life eating other people's memories secondhand and never got enough. "Unmade how?"

"That's not mine to know. I was a keeper, not a historian. What I know is what I was told on the night it happened, by the last High Keeper, who was old enough to remember when remembering was safe." Bren looked at the coal. It pulsed, slow as a sleeping heart. It always did that when someone spoke its name — its true name, not the polite lie of "ember" or "heirloom" he used in taverns. "They took the name out of the world. Out of maps, out of books, out of graves. Out of *minds*, Wren. Mothers who buried children in Ashkarin soil woke up the next morning not knowing why they were crying. Soldiers guarding the border woke up guarding nothing, and couldn't tell you what they'd been guarding against. The roads are still there if you walk them, but no road leads anywhere anyone remembers going."

"That's not possible," Cyrus said quietly. "Magic that large—"

"You're a storm-caller hiding in a city that hangs people for luck-charms, and you're telling me what magic can't do?" Bren laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Yes, it's possible. It was done. And the reason I know it was done — the reason I'm the only man alive who knows it — is sitting on my table burning without fuel."

* * *

The High Keeper's name had been Odessa Vane, and she had smelled of tallow smoke and iron, and she had gripped seventeen-year-old Bren by the collar on the worst night of his life and told him the truth the way you pour medicine down a child's throat — fast, bitter, all at once.

"The fire is leaving," she'd said. "Not dying — leaving. Yssara is old beyond counting, and when her kind dies, the world forgets them, and when the world forgets them, the warmth goes out of things. We built Ashkarin around her heart-flame so that something would remain. A country is just a shape we press into memory. So we pressed hard."

"And now?"

"Now the ones who unmade us are coming back for the flame itself. They couldn't take it while the country remembered how to protect it. So first they took the country." Her grip had left bruises. "You will carry the ember out. You will hide it somewhere small and stupid and mean, because no one looks for treasure in squalor. And you will wait, Keeper. You will wait for the Thorn."

Bren hadn't known what a Thorn was. He'd asked. She'd died before answering — or stopped existing before answering; there was a difference, and twelve years later he still wasn't sure which it had been. One moment she was talking, the next she was an outline of ash on the floor, and every record of her life in the great archive had gone blank at once, pages wiping themselves clean like a throat closing.

He'd run. That was the part he didn't say out loud anymore. The keeper's oath was to stand and hold, and Bren Ovist had run through collapsing tunnels with the heart of the last dragon wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and he'd kept running for twelve years, and he'd drunk himself stupid at every stop, and the ember had come with him the whole way like a debt that grew interest.

* * *

"So the Door," Cyrus said. They had a way of pulling conversations back on course with the gentle ruthlessness of someone who'd spent years selling things to people who wanted to talk. "Wren said the prophecy mentions it. A passage out of Ashkarin. But Ashkarin's gone."

"It's not gone. That's what I've been trying to make you understand." Bren leaned forward. "Erasure isn't destruction. If they'd destroyed the country, they'd have had to destroy the flame too, and they couldn't — the flame doesn't answer to them. So instead they made the world *forget where it was*. The country still exists. Every street, every mountain, every drowned temple. It exists exactly where it always did, wearing invisibility like a shroud. And underneath it — beneath the deepest foundation of it — there's a Door. The keepers built it in the first age, when we still believed a country should have a way out as well as a way in."

"A door to where?" Wren asked.

"To outside. To everywhere. It's not a door into another world — it's a door out of erasure. Step through it, and the world has to admit you exist again. It's the crack in the spell, the seam in the garment. The unmakers sealed it, of course. Sealed it with the same forgetting that took the country." He tapped the table, twice, the old keeper's rhythm, and the coal flared faintly in answer. "But here's what they didn't count on, and what I didn't understand until I heard the prophecy read aloud in a language I half-remembered from my oath: the seal responds to the storm-caller's gift. Old magic recognizes old magic. The storms that ran over Ashkarin's mountains before it was erased — those storms were called by people, once. Your gift, charm-seller, is older than the thing that ate my country. You're not just hunted by a book. You're the key to a lock nobody else can even see."

The silence after that was long. Rain had started against the shutters — real rain, Vell rain, gray and apologetic — and it filled the room with the sound of a city washing itself.

Cyrus looked down at their scarred hands. Bren had noticed those hands the night before, in the bad light of the taproom: old burns laid over old burns, the mark of someone who'd been holding lightning since childhood. Now, watching them turn their palms up as though checking for weather, he understood why the prophecy had drawn those exact hands. Some marks don't fade. Some marks are signatures.

"If the Door leads out of Ashkarin," Cyrus said slowly, "and Ashkarin is... here? Under here?" They gestured at the floor, at Vell entire, at the weight of a million tons of city pressing down on a country that didn't exist. "Then Vell was built on top of it."

"On purpose or by accident, who knows. Cities grow like mold. Maybe the unmakers put Vell there deliberately, a cork in the bottle. Or maybe Vell just grew over the wound because cities always grow toward whatever's warmest, and even forgotten, even sealed, the flame leaks." Bren shrugged. "Ask me why the crops near the salt flats come in sweeter than anywhere else in three provinces. Ask me why the catacombs under the temple district stay dry in flood season. Ask me why this city of godless magic-haters has never once frozen in winter, not in recorded history, not ever. Something under your feet is breathing, charm-seller. Has been the whole time. Nobody down here wants to hear that, so nobody asks."

* * *

Wren had gone very still. Bren had seen archivists go still before — it was the look of someone standing in front of a shelf and realizing the book they'd hunted for ten years was right there, spine out, waiting. But there was something else under it tonight, something that looked almost like guilt, and he filed that away with the other things he'd filed away about Wren Halloway: the too-good questions, the way she'd found Cyrus, the way she'd found *him*, a drunk keeper in the worst tavern in the Salt Ward, as if she'd been following a thread only she could see.

Later. Everything was later. That was the arrangement Bren had with his own cowardice.

"Show me the prophecy again," Cyrus said.

Wren produced the pages from her coat — she carried copies, Bren had gathered, the originals living somewhere he didn't want to ask about. She spread them on the table beside the coal, and the ember's light fell across the old ink, and Bren felt the familiar vertigo rise in him, the same vertigo he'd felt the first time he'd seen them: words in the high script of Ashkarin, the script carved over the keeper's hall door, describing a person who existed, here, now, pouring pine-needle tea down their throat in a hovel above a fishmonger's.

He translated the way he could — haltingly, the way a man translates a language he learned as a child and betrayed as an adult. "*When the thorn takes root in the garden of the enemy...*" His voice caught. He cleared it. "*The Door shall know its key, and the key shall know the price.*"

"What price?" Cyrus asked.

"The book doesn't say. Books never say the parts that matter." Bren sat back. "Here's what I know, and I'll say it plainly because I'm too old and too drunk to dress it up. Twelve years ago I was given one job: keep the flame alive until the Thorn comes. I've done it badly. I've done it alone. I've done it in a haze of cheap spirits because the alternative was feeling what I feel right now, which is—" He gestured at the coal, at the pages, at the two strangers in his home. "This. Terrified and certain at the same time. Because if the prophecy is true, then the waiting is over, and if the waiting is over, then what happens next happens fast, and what happens next involves walking into an erased country under the feet of a city that executes people like you, to open a door that the most powerful thing that ever happened in history went to great lengths to seal shut."

"And the alternative?" Wren asked softly.

"The alternative is the flame goes out." He said it flatly, because dressing it up wouldn't help. "And when it goes out — not if, when, it's dimmer every year, I can measure it in my teeth — the world starts forgetting how to be warm. Not metaphorically. Crops fail first. Then tides stutter. Then people, in the deep places, start to forget each other. I've seen the edge of it. There are villages on the old Ashkarin border where grandmothers hum lullabies in a language nobody speaks, and when you ask them where they learned it, they look at you like you're the ghost."

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the street a dog started up and quit again.

Cyrus reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward a strange animal — and held their palm above the coal. Not touching. The ember's glow climbed their fingers, and for a heartbeat Bren saw it clearly, the way he hadn't seen it in years: not a lump of burning mineral but a *heart*, a captured pulse, vast beyond its size, beating inside a shell of iron and patience.

It flared toward Cyrus's hand. Brighter than it had burned in a decade.

"Well," Bren said, and his voice came out rougher than intended. "That answers whether it likes you."

* * *

They talked until the rain stopped and the sky over Vell went the color of dishwater. They talked logistics — routes into the catacombs, which districts sat deepest over the old foundations, whose silence could be bought and whose couldn't. Wren knew archives; Bren knew tunnels, or had known them once, under a different sky. Cyrus mostly listened, asking the occasional question with the precision of a merchant weighing coin, and Bren found himself doing the dangerous thing, the thing he'd sworn off after Odessa Vane died: trusting someone.

By the time Wren left — slipping out the door with her pages folded away and a promise to return at dark — the bottle was empty and the coal was pulsing like it hadn't pulsed in years, quick and bright, and Bren sat across from the exiled storm-caller and felt the shape of the rest of his life rearranging itself around a decision he'd already made and hadn't admitted to yet.

"One more thing," Cyrus said, at the door. "The people who erased Ashkarin. The unmakers. Are they still out there? Still looking for the flame?"

Bren looked at the coal. He thought of blank pages, of ash outlines, of a bruise on his collarbone that had faded twelve years ago and never quite stopped aching.

"They never stopped," he said. "Why do you think I drink?"

Cyrus held his eyes a moment longer, then nodded once and stepped out into the waking noise of the Salt Ward.

Bren barred the door behind them and turned back to the table, and that was when he saw it — the ember, flaring and guttering, flaring and guttering, in a rhythm he'd never seen in twelve years of keeping.

Not a heartbeat.

A knock.
