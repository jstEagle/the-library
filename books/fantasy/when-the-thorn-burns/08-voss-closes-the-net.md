## 8. Voss Closes the Net

The stall was still smoking when Halvard Voss arrived.

It had been a good fire—thorough, professional. Whoever set it had known their work. The awning of dyed canvas had collapsed inward, folding over the little table like a shroud, and the charms that had hung from it in their dozens were fused into a single black mass of wire and glass and charred wood. Rain from the night before had soaked everything into a glistening sludge, so the ash came away on his boots in pale streaks as he circled the ruin.

And there, punched through the wreckage at every corner, at every height, deliberate as scripture: guild seals. Seven of them. Lead medallions stamped with the Inquisitor's Guild sigil—the eye within the flame—hammered deep into scorched timber with nails meant for coffin lids.

Voss stood in the mud of Vell's night market in the gray hour after dawn, hands clasped behind his back, and let the sight settle into him like a stone settling into a well.

Someone had done this *for* him.

Or someone had done it to look as though they had.

"Senior Inquisitor." The district warden, a soft man named Pell who sweated even in the cold, hovered at his elbow with a ledger clutched to his chest like a breastplate. "We've canvassed the surrounding vendors. No one saw anything, which—" he swallowed, "—which is itself unusual. Night market crowds see everything. They sell what they see."

"They didn't see," Voss said, "because they were made not to. Or because they're afraid. Fear and blindness look identical from the outside, Warden. Which do you find more likely?"

Pell's throat worked. "I—couldn't say, Senior Inquisitor."

"I could." Voss crouched, ignoring the wet soaking through his coat at the knee, and studied one of the seals where it jutted from a blackened post. The stamping was clean. One blow. A strong arm, an experienced one. He touched the metal and found it still faintly warm, hours after the fire had died.

Not warm from the fire.

Warm from the hammering.

Fresh work. Done this morning, or in the last dark hour before dawn—after the rain, before the market woke. Which meant the fire itself might be old news, days old, left to smolder under tarps until the right moment. Which meant someone had staged this scene the way a playwright stages a scene: for an audience of exactly one.

Him.

He rose slowly. His knee protested—he was fifty-one years old and twelve years past the height of his career, and his body kept its own honest ledger—but he ignored that too. He had grown very skilled, over twelve years, at ignoring things.

"Warden. Who held this stall?"

"A charm-seller." Pell consulted his ledger with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own death warrant. "Licensed under the name Corin Vale. Paid his fees quarterly, no violations on record, no complaints. Quiet sort. Sells luck-charms, protection knots, that class of goods."

"And where is Corin Vale now?"

"Gone, sir. Neighbors say he hasn't been seen since two nights past."

Gone. Of course gone. Voss turned in a slow circle, taking in the market as it woke around them: shutters rattling up, braziers being lit against the chill, fishwives arranging their silver piles with hands blue from cold. Nobody looked at him. Everybody was looking at him. That was how fear worked in Vell—it turned every citizen into an eye that pretended to be a wall.

Twelve years ago, Halvard Voss had been the rising blade of the Inquisitor's Guild. Twelve years ago he had stood in the great hall at Carrowmere and given testimony that ended a storm-caller's career with nine words and a signature—the testimony that put down the heretic Cyrus Crane, the boy who'd called lightning out of a clear sky over Thessaly Vale and drowned half a village doing it. Nine words. Voss could still recite them. *I saw him raise the storm with his own hands.* It had been true. Every word. And it should have crowned him.

Instead it had buried him.

Because the storm-caller had been young, and frightened, and beautiful in the way martyrs are beautiful, and because the village had mostly survived, and because the Council loved nothing so much as a mercy it could perform at someone else's expense. Exile instead of the pyre. A brand instead of ashes. And afterward, when the story softened in the retelling—as stories always did—Voss found himself recast: not the man who stopped a disaster, but the man who destroyed a boy. Cruel. Ambitious. *Eager*, they said, in the tones people used for a disease. The testimony that should have been his coronation became his tombstone, and he had spent twelve years being passed over, demoted, sent to provinces with names like apologies, until the only thing left of Halvard Voss was the testimony itself—and the certainty, bone-deep and unkillable, that he had been right.

Storm-callers did not stop being dangerous. They stopped being caught.

And now the prophecy book had surfaced from whatever grave it slept in, and an archivist had come asking questions in the wrong quarter, and a charm-seller named Corin Vale had vanished two nights after the Guild's seal began appearing in his neighborhood—and Voss stood in the mud looking at seven lead medallions and understood, with the clarity he'd once been famous for, that the net was closing.

His net. Whether he'd cast it or not.

"Burn it," he said.

Pell blinked. "Sir?"

"The remains. All of it. Today, before the market fills. And take those seals into custody—they're evidence, not decoration." He was already walking. "And Warden—find me everyone who sold to Corin Vale. Suppliers, ink-mongers, the man who sharpened his scissors if he had one. A person is a trail of transactions. Follow the transactions."

"Yes, Senior Inquisitor." Pell scurried off, grateful to be given something to do that wasn't thinking.

Voss paused at the edge of the square and allowed himself one long look back at the smoking ruin.

*You want me to come,* he thought at the empty stall, at whoever had arranged it like flowers on an altar. *Good. I'm coming.*

* * *

The archive of the Temple Quarter occupied a former granary, which told Voss everything he needed to know about what Vell thought of recorded knowledge: it was something you stored against famine. Three floors of shelves, dust thick enough to write in, and clerks who moved through it like moths—gray, silent, nibbling.

Wren Ashfall worked the third floor.

He knew this because he had spent four days learning it, quietly, through channels that left no ripples. The archivist who had come to the night market asking about Ashkarin-era texts. The archivist whose request chits, pulled three weeks ago, included a title that made the requisition clerk's hand shake so badly she'd dropped her pen: *The Thorn Cycle, fragment, provenance disputed.*

Provenance disputed meant stolen. Stolen meant erased country. Erased country meant the prophecy.

And the prophecy meant the Thorn.

Voss climbed the stairs with a junior inquisitor trailing him—a boy called Merrick with a face like unbaked dough, chosen precisely because he was forgettable—and found the third floor nearly empty at this hour. One clerk dozed over a ledger. Another shelved folios with the tenderness of a nurse.

And at the far table beneath the high window, bent over a manuscript, sat a woman with ink-stained fingers and hair escaping its braid, so absorbed in her work that she didn't hear them until Voss's shadow fell across her page.

She looked up. She had quick eyes, brown and bright, and they went from his face to his guild pin and back with a speed that told him she'd already catalogued everything important. Clever, then. He'd expected clever. Archivists who survived long enough to handle disputed provenance were clever by natural selection.

"Senior Inquisitor Halvard Voss," he said, and showed the pin though she'd already read it. "A routine inquiry regarding acquisition records."

"Of course." Her voice was level. She closed the manuscript in front of her—not hurriedly, not guiltily, just closing it, the way anyone closes a document when business arrives—and folded her hands atop it. "How can the archive assist the Guild?"

"The archive can assist," Voss said pleasantly, "by telling me who has requested restricted materials in the past month."

Her expression didn't move. But her hands did—a small thing, the thumbs pressing together, one nail whitening against the other. He noted it the way a hunter notes a snapped twig.

"That would require the requisition master," she said. "Second floor. I can fetch him."

"You are the requisition master's deputy. I checked." He let that sit. "Which means requests pass across your desk first. Which means, Mistress Ashfall, that either you know exactly what I'm asking about—or you've been asleep at your post for a month, and I'd have to write that up instead. Your choice which conversation we have."

Something flickered behind her eyes—calculation, fast as a card sharp's. Then she smiled, and it was a good smile, warm and rueful, and Voss felt the old familiar pleasure of watching someone decide to lie to him. People always thought the smile was the disguise. The smile was never the disguise. The smile was the tell.

"Restricted materials," she said lightly, "are requested constantly. Scholars, collectors, the occasional priest with more curiosity than sense. If the Guild is worried about a specific item—"

"I'm worried about a specific *person*." He drew out a chair and sat, uninvited, unhurried, laying his hands flat on her table the way an executioner lays out tools. "A charm-seller from the night market. Corin Vale. You purchased from him—protection charms, if my information holds. Three purchases in six weeks. An unusual rate for someone who doesn't believe in magic."

"I buy charms the way other people buy amulets against the plague," she said. "Cheap insurance."

"Insurance against what?"

"The same things everyone in Vell insures against. Fire. Sickness. Bad luck." Her eyes held his, steady now, and he gave her credit: she was better than most. "Senior Inquisitor, if I've committed some offense by frequenting a licensed vendor, I'll gladly—"

"Corin Vale's stall burned last night."

The pause before she answered lasted perhaps half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat too long.

"How terrible," she said. "Was anyone hurt?"

"No one. Because no one was there. He vanished two days ago." Voss leaned forward. "Now here is what interests me, Mistress Ashfall. A quiet, careful, invisible little man—who has sold charms in that market for eleven years without ever once drawing the Guild's eye—suddenly draws the attention of persons unknown, gets his stall burned, gets guild seals nailed into the wreckage like threats, and flees. Persons unknown wanted him afraid. Wanted him running. And I keep asking myself: what did Corin Vale *have?*"

"He sold trinkets."

"People don't run from a burned trinket stand." He watched her face with the patience of a man who had once broken harder faces than hers in softer rooms than this. "They run from what the stand was hiding. So I'll ask you plainly, since you bought his insurance: did Corin Vale ever show you anything older than a trinket? Anything with Ashkarin script? Anything with a name in it?"

And there—there, finally, a crack. Not in her face; her face was a fortress. In her breathing. One breath, taken slightly higher in the chest than the ones before it.

"No," she said.

"May I see your personal effects?" He gestured at the satchel hooked on her chair. "Routine. You understand. Everyone connected to the case will be cleared in turn."

For a moment—one long, taut moment—he thought she would refuse, and oh, how he hoped she would refuse. Refusal was a confession with better manners.

But she unhooked the satchel and set it on the table between them, and opened the flap herself, and turned it toward him like a woman showing an empty cupboard. Papers. A lunch wrapped in cloth. A pencil stub. A copy of some municipal registry. Nothing else.

Voss went through it anyway, thoroughly, feeling each item with his fingertips while she watched with polite boredom, and found nothing, and knew he'd found nothing, and hated it.

"Thank you for your cooperation," he said, standing.

"Is that all?" She was already turning back to her manuscript.

"One more thing." He paused beside her chair, close enough to smell the ink on her fingers. "Your colleague mentioned—you weren't at your desk yesterday afternoon. Out on errand. Where does an archivist run errands?"

"The binderies. The paper-sellers." A shrug. "Wherever the work sends me."

"Mm." He smiled down at her, and made the smile as gentle as he could, because gentleness frightened people far more than anger. Anger they could fight. "Do be careful, Mistress Ashfall. Vell is full of fires lately. It would be a shame for the archive to lose someone so... thorough."

He walked out without looking back. He didn't need to. He could feel her gaze between his shoulder blades like a knife warming against skin.

Merrick fell into step beside him on the stairs. "She lied, sir. About the breathing—I saw—"

"I know she lied."

"But the satchel was clean."

"The satchel was *staged*, boy. Clean satchels are what guilty people carry." Voss took the stairs two at a time, his blood moving faster than it had moved in years. "She knows what Vale had. She knows what he is. Which means she isn't the buyer—she's the courier. Couriers are better than buyers. Buyers hide things. Couriers *deliver* them."

"Deliver to whom?"

"To whoever paid to have that stall burned." He pushed through the archive doors into the gray morning light. "Find me the paper-sellers and binderies she visited yesterday, Merrick. Every one. Someone on that route saw her meet somebody. Somebody always sees."

* * *

They met in the undercroft of the Hall of Seals, where the Guild kept its oldest instruments and conducted its least deniable business, and Voss laid out what he had on the scarred oak table like a surgeon laying out knives.

The burned stall. The seven seals. The vanished charm-seller. The archivist with the steady eyes and the staged satchel and the request chit for a stolen book. And beneath all of it, weighted down with an iron paper-knife so the page wouldn't curl, the thing that had started everything: the transcription of the prophecy fragment, copied from the requisition records before anyone thought to restrict them.

*When the last fire gutters low, the Thorn shall rise—scarred of hand, storm-born, twice-marked—to save the dragon or end it.*

"Scarred of hand," said Sister Ondine, the Guild's senior examiner, tracing the line with one gloved finger. She was seventy, dry as parchment, and had interrogated heretics back when the pyres were still fashionable. "The Crane boy was branded. Both palms. I remember the report."

"I remember the sentencing," Voss said. "I gave it."

"Then you remember what the Council gave instead." Her eyes lifted to his, and there was no cruelty in them, which was worse. "Exile. Mercy. And your name went into the river with the rest of that day's washing. Twelve years, Halvard. You think catching him now washes it out?"

"I think," Voss said carefully, "that a prophecy naming the Thorn has surfaced in my jurisdiction, that a man matching its description has fled a burned stall, and that if the Thorn rises and I am the one who puts him down—truly puts him down this time—then no one will ever again say the words 'eager' and 'Voss' in the same sentence."

Ondine studied him for a long moment. Around the table, the others—Merrick, two ward-captains, a lean treasury man whose loyalties were purchasable and therefore reliable—waited in the candlelight like furniture.

"You believe the prophecy," she said at last. Not a question.

"I believe someone believes it strongly enough to burn stalls over it." Voss straightened. "And I believe that when powerful people burn things to hide a man, the fastest road to that man runs through whoever helped him hide. We have the archivist. We watch her, we follow her, and we let her lead us down the chain—Vale, and whoever stands behind Vale." He tapped the prophecy page. "And when we reach the bottom of the chain, we will find a storm-caller with branded hands. And I will be holding the chain."

Sister Ondine leaned back. For a moment the candlelight made her face look carved, ancient, almost kind.

"There's an old rule," she said, "from the founding charter. The Guild doesn't hunt prophecy. The Guild hunts *people*. Prophecy is God's handwriting, and men who edit God's handwriting tend to end up as footnotes themselves." She rose, joints cracking like green wood. "But rules bend, these days. Even mine. Do what you must, Halvard. Only—when you catch him, make certain the city sees it done properly. Spectacle. Order. A clean ending the crowd can carry home." Her mouth thinned into something that was not quite a smile. "You were always better at endings than beginnings."

She left. The others dispersed to their tasks. Voss stood alone in the undercroft with the candle guttering and the prophecy page pinned under iron.

*To save the dragon or end it.*

Somewhere beneath his feet, in the dark of the city's bones, the last dragon-fire was dying—he'd felt it these past months the way sailors feel weather, in the stuttering tides, the crops coming in thin, the winters arriving early and leaving late. The world was forgetting how to be warm. And somewhere above that darkness, in a rented room or a cellar or a friend's bed, a man he had exiled twelve years ago was running with a burning brand in his veins and a choice in his hands that could save the world or end it.

Let him run a little longer. Let him gather his allies—the archivist, the drunk keeper, whichever traitors crawled out of the woodwork to help him. Let the net fill.

Voss pinched out the candle.

A net, after all, was useless until it closed.

* * *

The report came at midnight, delivered by a runner who'd been told to bring it to no one else.

Merrick's handwriting, breathless: *Paper-seller on Candle Lane confirms the archivist met an unknown woman yesterday, veiled, paid in temple scrip. Followed her route as far as the Drowned Steps. Lost her in the catacomb quarter.*

And below it, added in different ink, in the paper-seller's own crude hand, the detail that stopped Voss's heart for one full beat:

*The veiled woman asked after a second name besides the charm-seller's. Asked whether he'd been seen lately with a tall keeper, former dragon-yards, drinks at the Salt Cellar. Description matched. The paper-seller remembered because the woman described the keeper's missing fingers.*

Voss set the letter down on his desk with great care, as though it might go off.

The charm-seller. The archivist. And now the keeper.

Bren Halloway. Disgraced dragon-keeper, cashiered from the yards nine years past, keeper—if the rumors held any truth at all—of a certain coal that was not entirely a coal.

The Thorn was gathering his pieces. Just as the prophecy promised. Just as Voss needed him to.

He lit a fresh candle, took out a clean sheet, and began to write the orders that would put watchers on the Salt Cellar, seals on the catacomb entrances, and quiet inquiries into the whereabouts of Bren Halloway—orders that would, by morning, tighten another loop of the net.

Outside his window, over the rooftops of Vell, the sky was utterly, unnaturally clear. No clouds. None at all.

In a city where a storm-caller was moving, Voss found that silence louder than thunder.
