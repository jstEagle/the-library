## 10. Sefa's Shadow

The stranger who had paid for Cyrus's storm did not die.

Cyrus learned this three days later, in the worst possible way: from a rumor overheard through the canvas wall of a pie-seller's stall while they haggled over a bag of dried plums they could not afford.

"—the cooper's daughter, Marta, forty years old on her wedding day and now she's got white in her hair, swears she was struck by something—"

"Not lightning. No burn."

"Old age, then, all at once? In the middle of Copperlane?"

"Gods are punishing us," said a third voice, older, certain in the way that fear makes people certain. "Magic in the city again. That's what my cousin says. He saw it. Saw *him*."

Cyrus paid for the plums with hands that would not stop shaking and walked home the long way, through streets that seemed to lean in around them, every shutter a closed eye.

Marta. The woman with the flour-dusted apron who had been laughing at a fruit-seller's joke when the guild hunters came around the corner with iron hooks and netting woven through with prayer-script. The woman whose life Cyrus had spent—four years of it, maybe five, snatched out of her like coins from a purse—in the three heartbeats it took to tear the sky open and drop the hunters into the canal.

She lived. She lived with gray hair at thirty and a husband who would spend the rest of his marriage wondering what sickness had touched her without touching him.

*I'm sorry.* Cyrus said it to no one, walking, saying it to the cobblestones, to the gray sky, to whatever ledger kept accounts of such debts. *I'm sorry. I didn't know. I didn't know.*

But that was a lie too, wasn't it? Some part of them had always known. You could not pull fire from nothing. Every charm they sold, every luck-knot and fever-warming sigil, every small magic they'd performed for twelve years of careful, invisible living—the price had been going somewhere. They'd just never seen where. Never wanted to look.

Now they had looked.

* * *

Wren found them at dusk, sitting on the floor of the rented room with their back against the bedframe and the plum bag unopened in their lap. She took one look at their face and set down the satchel she carried everywhere, the one with the archivist's wax seals and the folded prophecy pages Bren still refused to touch.

"You used it again." Not a question.

"One time. To get away. There were four of them, Wren, and they had—" Cyrus pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "There was a woman in the street. She aged. Years, in seconds. I felt it go through me like water through a sieve, and I couldn't stop it, couldn't aim it, couldn't—"

"I know." Wren crouched in front of them. Her fingers hovered near theirs—not touching; she had learned in three days of knowing each other exactly how much contact Cyrus could bear and when. "I know. And you're going to do it again, because next time it'll be your life or someone else's, and you'll choose yours, because you're alive and alive things want to stay that way."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No. It's supposed to be true. Comfort comes later, if we're lucky." She sat back on her heels. "Which brings me to the reason I climbed half the city today. We have a problem, and it isn't the Guild."

Cyrus lowered their hands. Wren's face was carefully arranged into calm, which meant underneath the arrangement was something else entirely.

"The woman who aged. Marta. Her story spread. And stories, in this city, don't stay stories—they become testimony. Someone brought it to the Inquisitors' Council. Said the storm-caller was back. Said it matched an old file."

"What old file?"

"The exile file." Wren's voice went quiet, the way voices go quiet in this city when they brush against the erased places, as if Ashkarin might hear. "Twelve years ago. A village called Merrow's Bend. A drowning. A storm called down by a child of seventeen with scars on both hands. And a name on the warrant that the Council has apparently never stopped reading."

The room tilted, very slightly. Or perhaps it was only Cyrus tilting.

"They pulled the file," Wren said. "I have a friend in the records hall—don't ask, the answer will embarrass both of us—and she says the file was requested by name. By rank. By someone with standing to reopen a cold case and put it at the top of the Guild's business, ahead of the smuggling rings and the plague quarantines and everything else they pretend to govern." She swallowed. "It was requested by the Second Chair of the Inquisitor's Council."

"Wren."

"The hunt has a face now, Cyrus. It isn't Voss anymore—he's First Chair in name but he's been losing ground for months, everyone knows it, he needs a victory or he's finished. The one who pulled your file, the one who's leading the search personally, the one who argued before the full Council that the storm-caller must be taken alive—"

"*Wren.*"

"Sefa Amaris." The name landed between them like a dropped knife. "Second Chair. Twelve years ago she testified against you. Now she's the one hunting you."

* * *

Afterward—after Wren had gone to warn Bren, after the lamp had burned down to a bead of blue flame—Cyrus sat in the dark and let the memory come, because there was no longer any use in barring the door against it. It had keys. It had always had keys.

Merrow's Bend, twelve years ago. The river coming up over its banks in the black hours of the morning, and sixteen-year-old Sefa pounding on the Cranes' door with rain already to her knees, screaming that the levee had gone, that the low quarter was flooding, that people were trapped in the granary and the water was still rising.

And Cyrus, seventeen, scarred since birth in patterns no healer could explain, standing in the doorway with the storm already gathering in their chest like a second heartbeat, knowing—knowing even then—that what they were about to do would cost them everything. Knowing and doing it anyway, because Sefa was crying, and because somewhere under the floodwater were children.

They remembered the storm leaving them. It had felt like being turned inside out. They remembered the river bending, actually *bending*, away from the granary, the water rising off the fields and hanging in the air like a curtain while two hundred people waded out beneath it. They remembered Sefa looking up at that impossible ceiling of held-back sea and staring at Cyrus with an expression that was not gratitude.

That expression had been the first warning. Cyrus had been too young, too emptied, too proud of the miracle to read it.

Three weeks later, the Inquisitors came. And Sefa stood in the village square with her hands clasped like a good daughter of the faith and told the assembled town that she had seen the storm rise at the storm-caller's call, that she had witnessed sorcery, that the law was the law. Her father was a Guild clerk. Her family's whole position hung on her doing exactly what she did.

She never looked at Cyrus while she said it. That was the detail that had kept Cyrus alive for twelve years in a way hatred never could: she hadn't been able to look. Whatever she'd felt, it had been real enough to be unbearable. Duty and love had stood in the same square, and duty had won, and love had at least had the decency to flinch.

Cyrus had built a life on that flinch. On the idea that somewhere under the uniform and the testimony, the girl who pounded on doors in floods was still there, still choosing, every silent year, not to say more than the law required.

Now the law required everything. Now she had a chair, a seal, a hunt with her name at the head of it.

*Taken alive,* Wren had reported. *She argued for taking you alive.*

Cyrus turned that phrase over in the dark like a coin of doubtful metal. Alive. Was that mercy, or was it worse? Dead, they were a martyr, a loose end, a file closed. Alive, they were a trial—a public reckoning, the Thorn dragged into the light, and whoever conducted that trial would own the story forever. Voss would want that. But Voss hadn't asked for alive.

Sefa had.

And underneath the analysis, underneath the strategy, was the simpler wound that no amount of thinking could dress: *she knows where to look.* Twelve years of Cyrus's careful invisibility—the false names, the moving stalls, the charms sold small and cheap and forgettable—and it had taken the Council three days once her eye was on it. Because Sefa knew how Cyrus thought. Knew the markets they favored, the alleys they'd find comforting, the exact weight of caution they'd mistake for safety. You could hide from an institution. You could not hide from someone who had once known the way you slept.

Heartbreak was useless. Heartbreak was a luxury with a body count—Marta's gray hair, the next stranger, the one after that. So Cyrus did what exiles learn to do with grief: they hammered it flat and beat it into shape, and what it became was urgency.

Get up. Move. Burn the room behind you, take nothing, tell no one where—

Except that was wrong too. That was the old arithmetic, twelve years of it, and it had led here: alone in the dark with a prophecy book naming them a sacrifice and a hunter who knew their heartbeat. The old arithmetic said run. But running was what the prophecy expected. Running was the shape of the trap. *The Thorn flees, the Thorn burns*—every line of that cursed book assumed a person trying to escape it.

Bren had said something, drunk, three nights ago, that had seemed like rambling at the time: *"You can't outrun a story, boy. Stories are faster. Only thing you can do is change what happens in the middle."*

Fine. Then the middle changed tonight.

* * *

Dawn found Cyrus on the roof of the tannery where Bren kept his rooms, watching the city wake below—the smoke, the bells, the gray light coming up over Vell like a slow bruise—and laying out their situation the way Bren laid out his worthless relics, item by item, honest about the condition of each.

Item: they could not stay in Copperlane. The market stall was already burned; the room was blown; by tomorrow the Guild would have eyes on every charm-seller within a mile.

Item: they needed the Door. Which meant Ilyra—the priestess Bren spoke of only when very drunk, the traitor of an erased faith, the one person who might know what the prophecy actually demanded and whether it could be survived. Wren had been asking after her for days. Tonight they would ask together, loudly enough for the right ears to hear.

Item: Sefa Amaris was hunting them personally, and every hour Cyrus spent guessing at that fact was an hour wasted, because there was exactly one thing a hunted person could do with a hunter who had loved them once, and it was the hardest thing on the list.

You stopped hiding from her. You made yourself legible instead. You sent a message only she would understand, and you watched what she did with it.

Because if some part of that girl from the flood still existed—if the flinch was still in her—then she was not merely the blade falling. She was the crack in the Guild's armor, wide as a door, and doors were suddenly the only thing Cyrus cared about.

And if there was nothing left of her but the uniform—well. Then better to learn it now, with time left to run, than later, in irons, in a square, in front of a crowd.

Below, the bells began the morning hour, and among them—one tower, one sequence, slightly off-pattern, the way it had been every morning for twelve years without anyone in authority ever caring—rang the old flood-warning of Merrow's Bend. Three strokes, a pause, three more. A dead village's signal, kept alive by habit in a city that had forgotten why.

Cyrus stood, and let the wind off the rooftops move through them, feeling the storm stir obediently under their skin like a dog that had bitten someone and didn't understand why that changed anything.

*Not yet,* they told it. *Soon. And when I call you, someone is going to anchor you, or so help me, I will bury this magic in the deepest hole in Ashkarin myself.*

Then they went down the stairs to find Wren, and write a letter to the Second Chair of the Inquisitor's Council that said, in the private shorthand of two people who had once counted the same raindrops through the same window:

*The levee broke. I'm still holding the water. Where will you stand?*

By nightfall, the letter was gone from where Cyrus had left it—slipped into the offering-box of the Guild chapel, addressed to no one, sealed with a scarred thumbprint in beeswax.

By the following midnight, there was an answer waiting in the same box, written in a hand that shook only slightly, in ink the color of deep water:

*Under the old bell. Come alone. If you bring your friends, I cannot protect even the memory of you.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost illegible, as though the writer had pressed hard and then tried to scratch it out and failed:

*You should not have come back, Cyrus.*
