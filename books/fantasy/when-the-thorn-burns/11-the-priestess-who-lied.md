## 11. The Priestess Who Lied

The candles had to be stolen.

That was the first law of Ilyra's shrine, the one she had written herself in the years after Ashkarin was unmade: nothing given, nothing bought, everything taken from the god who forgot her. The tallow stubs that guttered along the walls of the cistern-chapel came from Inquisitorial vigils, palmed away while mourners wept for their dead. The wine in the chipped chalice was decanted from a guildmaster's cellar by a servant whose name she never learned and never asked. Even the water in the font—black, still, smelling of iron and old stone—had been carried up bucket by bucket from the drowned levels below Vell, where the city's memory pooled like blood under a bandage.

She lit the last candle with a splinter from the pyre-ground, as she did every night, and knelt on the cold flagstone before an altar that held no idol.

There was nothing to put on it. Ashkarin's gods had been erased too thoroughly for even their shapes to survive. She had tried, once, early on—carved a face into river clay the way her grandmother had taught her, the long jaw, the storm-bright eyes, the mouth open in song. The clay had cracked in her hands before it dried. Every time. Seven faces, seven failures, and then she understood: you cannot sculpt a thing that has been unwritten. You can only remember toward it, the way a tongue worries the socket of a missing tooth.

"Yssara-of-the-Ember," she said, using the old vocative, the name-that-calls. "Mother of the Door. Keeper of the Burning Year. I am still here. I am still yours, though I lie to everyone else."

The candleflames leaned together, all nine of them, as if whispering. Then straightened. The black water in the font did not stir.

Ilyra stayed kneeling a moment longer, listening to the cistern drip its slow count. Twelve years of this. Twelve years of performing marriages over salt-rings and funerals over closed coffins, of blessing ships whose captains would burn any true charm-seller they found, of smiling at Inquisitors in the street while the erased name of her country sat behind her teeth like a stone. Twelve years since the Synod of Vell had declared the Ashkarin faith heretical, then nonexistent, then—when enough priests had been burned or fled or quietly strangled—*never having existed at all*, which was a cleaner cruelty altogether. You could be martyred for what you believed. There was no word for what happened when the world decided your belief had never been anything but madness.

She had lied to survive. That was the whole of her confession, and she made it nightly, and it changed nothing, and she made it anyway.

But tonight, the candles had told her something. Tonight, when she trimmed the wicks, three flames had bent *southward*, all at once, all together—the sign of the Returning, the gesture her order used to mean *what was lost is walking back*. And beneath the drip of the cistern she had heard, very faint, very far, a sound like wind moving through a place wind should not reach.

Someone was coming down toward the drowned levels. Someone carrying an ember.

Ilyra rose, blew out eight of the nine candles, and went to see whether her god had finally sent her salvation or her ruin.

* * *

They came at moonset, by the flooded stair.

She watched them from the shadow of a buttress, three figures picking their way down slick steps with a hooded lantern, and she knew them before the light reached their faces because she had been told to know them. Word moved through Vell's underworld faster than plague: the charm-seller from the night market whose stall had been burned; the archivist woman who paid for rare pages in coin that smelled of noble cellars; and the drunk, Bren of the keepers, whom Ilyra had last seen eleven years ago being carried out of a gutter on the Salt Side, weeping about a coal.

And around the charm-seller, faint as heat-shimmer, the smell of a storm about to happen.

Ilyra stepped out of the dark with her hands open and empty, palms forward, the way one greets in the old rite—*see, I hold nothing, not even a knife*.

"You're looking for the Door," she said.

All three of them flinched. The tall one—the charm-seller, Cyrus, though the name meant little to her yet—put their body between her and the lantern light, and Ilyra noted the instinct, the economy of it. Someone used to being hunted. Their hands were wrapped in fingerless gloves, but she could see the scars at the wrists, crossing and recrossing like a script.

"Bren," she said, turning to the drunk, whose red-rimmed eyes had gone wide. "You've gotten old."

"Ilyra." His voice was gravel left out in rain. "They said you were dead."

"The Guild says that about everyone from home. It saves them the trouble of proving it." She let her gaze move past him to Cyrus, and let herself look, really look, the way she had looked at the cracked clay faces. *Storm-bright. Oh, you poor creature. You walked in here wearing your own doom.* "And you are the Thorn."

Wren's hand went into her coat. Bren said, sharply, "Now how in the hells—"

"The candles bent south last night. All nine at once. That is the sign of the Returning, and there is only one thing in this world it can mean." Ilyra kept her voice low and level, a priestess's voice, built for crypts. "I have waited twelve years for someone to come down my stairs asking after the Door. I confess I hoped you'd take longer getting here. Hope is heavier than fear; it bruises more."

"We were told you could help," Wren said. Her voice was pleasant, trained, and Ilyra marked the way her eyes catalogued—the exits, the font, the value of the candlesticks. An archivist who worked for someone. Everyone always worked for someone. "We were told you were a priestess of Ashkarin."

"I was consecrated in the Temple of the Burning Year," Ilyra said. "Which stood where the Inquisitors' Hall stands now, if it ever stood anywhere, which the world has decided it did not. Yes. I can help you. Whether I will depends entirely on what you're willing to hear."

Cyrus spoke for the first time, and their voice caught her off guard—quiet, worn, honest in a way that seemed almost accidental. "We want to find the Door. Before the Guild finds us. That's all."

"That's never all," Ilyra said gently. "Nothing that walks through my door is ever only fleeing. Come inside. The water rises with the tide and I'd rather not conduct theology standing in it."

* * *

The cistern-chapel swallowed them, candlelight climbing the wet walls, and Ilyra poured the stolen wine into four cups because hospitality was the last rite she still performed perfectly.

Bren drank his like a man apologizing to it. Wren held hers and didn't sip, watching Ilyra over the rim with those ledger-keeping eyes. Cyrus turned the cup slowly in their scarred hands without drinking at all, and asked, "You said the Thorn. You knew the word before we said it."

"I knew the book," Ilyra said. "Or what remains of it. When the Synod burned the Ashkarin canon, some pages survived in ways fire doesn't reach—in memories, in copies smuggled into other books, in the margins of tax ledgers where a scribe got sentimental. I have spent twelve years buying those fragments back. A verse here. A torn leaf there. I own perhaps a tenth of the Book of the Ember now, scattered across this city in hiding places I won't describe even to you." She smiled, and knew the smile was cold. "Especially not to you."

"Fair," Wren murmured.

"The book names a figure called the Thorn. It gives the hands—" Ilyra nodded at Cyrus's wrapped wrists. "Scarred in a specific pattern, storm-cut, the marks of lightning taken into the body rather than through it. The Thorn comes to the Door in the year the ember gutters. And the Thorn—" Here she stopped. She had rehearsed this part on her knees that morning, and found, as always, that rehearsal did not survive contact.

"The Thorn what?" Cyrus asked.

"The prophecy demands the Thorn burn." Ilyra said it plainly, because plainness was the only mercy left in it. "Not flee. Not hide. Not rule, whatever the nobles whispering in Wren's lady employer's ear may hope. *When the last fire gutters, the Thorn shall stand at the Door, and the Thorn shall burn, and by that burning the way shall be made.* That is the line as I have it. There are gaps—whole verses missing—but that line I have seen with my own eyes, twice, in two separate fragments that agree."

The silence afterward had weight. The candles leaned.

"So we're supposed to believe," Bren said at last, hoarse, "some book wants our friend to climb into an oven."

"Some book wants the world to believe that," Ilyra corrected. "Whether the book wants it is another matter. Prophecy is not a sentence, keeper, whatever the Guild teaches. It is a description. It describes the shape of a road; it does not compel the feet. I have spent twelve years telling myself that, so I suppose I owe you the same comfort I use myself." She turned to Cyrus. "But understand what you carry. The people who wrote those verses watched their entire country be unmade. They were desperate men writing in the dark, and desperate men write cruel arithmetic. They believed one burning could buy back everything. Perhaps they were wrong. Perhaps they were lying. Priests lie. I am one; I should know."

"Why tell us, then?" Cyrus asked. The question came out soft and terribly direct. "If the prophecy might just be a trap—if helping us might get you everything you claim to want, why warn us?"

And there it was. The question Ilyra had been avoiding since the candles bent south.

She rose, walked to the font, and looked down into the black water, where the only reflection the chapel allowed was a smear of candlelight like a wound.

"Because I don't know anymore what I want," she said. "Every night I pray to a god who may not exist, in a language the world has agreed was never spoken, and I ask Yssara for one thing only: let me be done lying. Let me spend myself on something true. And every night the answer is silence, and every night I come back anyway, which is either faith or madness, and I have stopped being able to tell them apart." She turned back to face them. "You want the Door. I can take you to it—or near it. No one living knows exactly where it opens, and I include myself. But I know the roads down, the drowned stairs, the temple that floods twice a day and keeps its secrets between tides. I'll guide you. In exchange—"

"Name it," Wren said, too quickly, and Ilyra saw the flicker of calculation and thought: *there is the buyer's hand in you, girl. We will deal with that later.*

"In exchange, when you reach the Door," Ilyra said, keeping her eyes on Cyrus, "you tell me what you find. Whatever it is. The whole truth, spoken aloud in this chapel, to me. If the Thorn burns and something answers on the other side—I want to hear it. If the Door opens onto nothing, I want to hear that too. I have given my life to a rumor, charm-seller. I am asking to be present at the rumor's trial."

Cyrus looked at her for a long moment, and Ilyra felt examined in turn—felt the storm behind those quiet eyes take her measure, the way weather takes the measure of a coastline.

"You're afraid of us," Cyrus said finally. "Of me."

"Terribly."

"But you're coming anyway."

"Faith," Ilyra said, "or madness. I told you I can't tell them apart anymore."

It was Bren who broke the tension, laughing—a short, ugly, relieved sound—and thumping his cup down on the altar beside the chipped chalice as if re-consecrating it. "Well. A liar, a thief, a drunk, and a doomed storm-caller walk into a flooded temple. Sounds like home."

Almost, Ilyra laughed with him. Almost.

Then the water in the font moved.

Not the tide—that came later, she knew its schedule to the minute. This was a single ripple, ring-shaped, spreading from the center outward as if something far below had exhaled. The candleflames stood perfectly straight. The air in the chapel went warm, abruptly, unbearably warm, the warmth of a hand held near a coal, and Ilyra heard—only with her heart, only in the bone behind her ears—a sound she had not heard in twelve years.

A voice in the old tongue. Two words.

*He comes.*

She gripped the edge of the font. The ripple faded. The cold crept back. Wren was saying something about the tide; Bren was looking at her strangely.

"Priestess?" Cyrus asked. "What is it?"

Ilyra made herself smile, made her voice steady, added one more lie to the account she would someday have to settle.

"Nothing. The settling of old houses. It does that." She took up the lantern. "Come, then. If we're going to find a Door that shouldn't exist, we should start before dawn, while the Guild sleeps and the water is low."

And as they climbed toward her, out of the dark of the stair, she thought: *the ember spoke of he, not she. He comes. Then who, little god, do you think I have just let into my house?*

She did not have an answer. She blew out the ninth candle, and led them down.
