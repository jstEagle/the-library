## 12. Under the City's Skin

The entrance to the drowned catacombs was a drainage grate behind a tannery, which Bren said was fitting, because everything holy in Vell had been flushed downhill eventually.

"Mind the third step," he grunted, hauling himself down into the dark. "It minds you."

Cyrus went last. Above them the night market was packing up its lanterns, the widow's luck-charm money still warm in their pocket, and below—below was a black throat that breathed cold air smelling of wet stone and something older than stone. Wren stood at the lip of the grate with her lamp raised, its flame doubled and tripled in the standing water at the bottom.

"You're sure Ilyra's mark is down there?" Cyrus asked.

"I'm sure she told me it would be," Wren said. "Which in my experience is either certainty or bait, and I've learned to stop asking which." She caught his look and added, more gently: "You wanted the Door. Doors are always under things."

Bren snorted from the darkness. "Everything true is under Vell. The city's built like a liar—layers of it, each one covering the last."

Cyrus took a breath of tannery-stink and followed them down.

* * *

The water came to their knees at first, then their ankles as the passage climbed through what must once have been a cistern. Bren walked with a drunkard's confidence, never hesitating, though he claimed not to remember the way. "The stones remember for me," he said, tapping his boot against a step worn smooth as glass. "Keeper's trick. You spend enough years walking toward one thing, your feet learn it better than your head ever did."

The coal he carried rode against his chest in an iron box lined with ash, and Cyrus could feel it even through the metal—a faint warmth against their senses, like a candle glimpsed through a wall. It had felt stronger on the surface. Here, underground, it pulsed slower, and Cyrus wondered if the dragon's heart-flame knew it was returning somewhere old.

Wren counted turns aloud, sketching their path in a waterproof notebook with a stub of pencil. Left at the carved arch. Right where two tunnels braided together. Down seventeen steps, up eleven. Her handwriting grew cramped and fast.

"How deep does it go?" Cyrus asked.

"Vell sits on Vell sits on Vell," Bren said. "Every city does. But most cities only bury their garbage. This one buried a country."

They passed evidence of it within the hour.

First it was small: a brick stamped with a sigil no guild used, a crescent split by a vertical line. Then a doorway bricked shut so long ago the mortar had become part of the wall, but the doorframe itself was granite, and granite doesn't grow in the Vell basin. Then a stretch of corridor where the masonry changed entirely—larger blocks, fitted without mortar, each one bearing the same crescent-and-line—and Bren stopped talking altogether, and stayed silent for a quarter mile.

"It's real," Wren whispered finally. "Gods, it's actually real. The texts say Ashkarin fell and the histories say *what fall*, and everyone argues about armies or plague or flood, and none of them—" Her lamp hand shook. "None of them mention that the city is *underneath us*. That we live on top of it. That we walk our dogs over its temples."

"People don't remember," Bren said flatly. "That's rather the point. Ask them tomorrow and they'll tell you this tunnel flooded in the Guild's grandfather's day. Ask them about the crescent mark and they'll see a moon. The mind slides off it like rain off oilcloth." He glanced back at Cyrus. "Except yours won't. Hasn't yet. Notice that?"

Cyrus had noticed. They had been noticing since the first sigil, when a word had risen unbidden behind their eyes—not read, exactly. *Recognized.* A word they had no memory of learning: *threshold*. It had arrived the way a storm arrives, pressure first, then meaning, and it had frightened them more than the dark did.

"I don't know why," they said.

"Don't you?" Bren's voice was gentle in the way that drunk men are gentle—bluntly, without padding. "Storm-caller. Storms were Ashkarin's whole liturgy, far as anyone can guess. The erased keep their keys close."

* * *

The water rose again as they descended past the old cistern level, and here the character of the dark changed. Their lamps began to behave strangely—the flames leaning away from the walls, as if flinching. The dripping grew musical, each drop ringing a different note off unseen surfaces. And the cold stopped being the ordinary cold of underground places and became a cold with texture, a cold that seemed to be paying attention.

"We should have brought Ilyra," Wren said. Her voice had gone thin. "If she knows this place—"

"She knows enough to be dangerous," Bren muttered. "So do we all, tonight."

Then Cyrus rounded a bend ahead of the others, held their lamp high, and stopped so abruptly that Wren walked into their back.

The corridor opened into a vaulted hall, dry at last, its floor a mosaic of river-stones arranged in spirals. And every surface—walls, pillars, the ribs of the vault overhead, even the mosaic floor between the spirals—was written on.

Inscribed. Chiseled. Inlaid in copper that had gone green as pondwater. Lines of script ran in columns and around curves and sometimes straight through images of storms, of towers, of a great winged shape coiling above a walled city. Thousands of words. Tens of thousands. A library flayed open and hung on stone.

"Oh," Wren breathed. She lifted her lamp with both hands now. "Oh, this is—do you know how many scholars would weep blood to stand here? Do you know what I would give—" She was already moving along the wall, notebook out, copying blind, copying everything. "I can't read any of it. Not a word. But the *structure*, the grammar of the layout—it's liturgical, it has to be, see how the lines break before the winged figure, like verses bowing to—"

"Wren," Bren said quietly. "Look at him."

She turned. Cyrus hadn't moved. Their lamp hung forgotten at their side, and tears were running down their face without permission, without heat, the way rain falls out of a sky that hasn't decided to storm.

"What is it? What do you see?"

"I can read it," Cyrus said. Their own voice sounded foreign. "All of it."

"That's impossible. Nobody reads Ashkarin. Nobody has in a hundred years. The language wasn't just lost, it was *taken*—"

"I know." Cyrus stepped forward, and their boot heel rang on the mosaic, and the word rose again, pressure first, then meaning. "*Welcome, child of the thunder-road.*" They touched the first line of the nearest column, copper cool under their fingertips. "It says—here, this opening verse. It says: *We who lived beneath the storm's eye write this so that forgetting cannot finish what fire began. If you read this, the sky has kept one of ours.*"

Silence. Even the dripping seemed to hold itself.

"That's not possible," Wren said again, but her voice had changed; it was the voice she'd used over the prophecy book, hungry and afraid at once. "Unless—Cyrus, unless it isn't reading. Unless it's *remembering*. Things you knew before you knew yourself."

"My mother sang songs I never heard twice," Cyrus said slowly. The words kept coming, translating themselves as they looked, effortless as breathing, terrifying as drowning. "Songs in no tongue from anywhere. She said they came from her grandmother, who came from nowhere anyone could name." They laughed, a cracked sound. "I thought they were nonsense. They weren't nonsense. They were *verses*. This is verse nine of a hymn cycle, and my grandmother's grandmother probably sang it while she churned butter."

"The gift isn't just weather," Bren said. He had lowered himself onto a fallen pillar and sat there looking older than Cyrus had ever seen him. "Never was. The storm-calling is the loud half. The quiet half is this. Ashkarin didn't worship storms, boy. It *spoke* them. The lightning was the alphabet. Every caller born after the fall—that's a letter that got loose from a burned book, still trying to find its sentence."

Cyrus looked down at their hands. Ordinary hands, scarred across both palms from a charm-seller's tools and a lifetime of small careful work. The same hands inked into a century-old book. *The Thorn.*

"And the Door," they said. "The inscriptions—do they say where—"

"I'm getting there," Wren whispered, though Cyrus hadn't finished the question. She was three columns down, following their translations as fast as they could speak them, pencil flying. "Keep going. Please. I'll sort it into order later."

So Cyrus read. They read the welcome-verse and the lament-verse that followed it—*they came at dusk with iron and ledgers, and by dawn our name was a debt no one owed*—and the practical verses after that, instructions, warnings, maps rendered in words. They read about the Door. It was called the *Last Honest Gate*, and it did not lead out of Ashkarin so much as it led *around* the forgetting, a seam left deliberately in the erasure, stitched by the city's last storm-callers on its last night.

"*Only the sky may open what the ledger closed,*" Cyrus translated, voice steadying into something almost liturgical. "*And only when the last fire gutters, for the gate is keyed to grief, and grief must be whole before it will turn.*" They frowned. "There's more. A clause. A warning." They bent closer, tracing the greened copper, and the next words came slowly, reluctantly, as if the stone itself were choosing whether to say them:

"*Let the Thorn beware: what opens for grief will also open for greed. We could seal the gate against all but sorrow, but sorrow is honest and greed is patient, and patience picks locks.*"

Bren exhaled through his teeth. "The cabal."

"If nobles want dragon-fire," Wren said, "and the Door opens on the dragon dying—then they don't need to steal the ember. They just need to let Yssara gutter out and walk through the gap." Her pencil had stopped moving. "Or hurry it along."

The cold in the hall seemed to deepen. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of stone and a lying city, Vell went on sleeping, ten thousand people dreaming their safe dreams on top of a murdered nation.

* * *

They might have left then. Cyrus, looking back, wished they had. But Wren found the second chamber.

It lay beyond a curtain of hanging chains, each link cast in the shape of a lightning bolt, and it was smaller, rounder, intimate as a cupped palm. No script on the walls here. Instead, a single image dominated the floor: a mosaic of two figures facing each other, one crowned in stormlight, one robed and kneeling, and between them an object rendered in red glass that had once been vivid and was now the color of dried blood.

"A coronation?" Wren guessed.

"No." Cyrus knelt. The translation came before they wanted it. "A *binding*. See the words around the rim? It says: *The keeper gives the breath. The caller gives the key. Neither may open the fire alone, and neither may close it alone. This is the mercy and the lock.*" They looked up at Bren, who had gone very still in the doorway, the iron box against his chest seeming suddenly heavier.

"The keeper's oath," Bren said quietly. "This is the original of it. My order's founding rite—we had a corrupted version, fragments, a chant nobody understood. *Breath for the flame, breath for the flame, and my years are the wick.*" His laugh had no humor in it at all. "We thought it meant devotion. Give your life to guarding the coal. Twenty generations of keepers thinking they were being pious." He met Cyrus's eyes. "It means the binding runs both directions, doesn't it. Read it again. All of it."

Cyrus read. "*When the caller spends the storm, the toll is taken from those bound nearest the flame—for the flame holds the account, and the account must be paid in years, and the keeper's years stand surety for the world's.*"

The silence afterward had weight, like snowfall.

"So it's not random," Cyrus said. Their voice came out strange, distant, as though someone else were speaking through them—which, perhaps, in this room, someone was. "When I call a storm and a stranger collapses, aged twelve years in a breath—it's not chaos. The flame chooses who pays. Nearest the binding. Nearest the—" They stopped. Started again. "Nearest the people I love."

"Not random," Bren agreed. "Worse than random. *Ordered.*" He rose, joints cracking, and stood over the mosaic with his lamp raised, staring down at the red-glass object between the two figures. "Yssara's been rationing the bill this whole time, hasn't she. Spreading it. Taking it from whoever's closest because the alternative is taking it from *everyone*." He turned, and his eyes in the lamplight were wet and furious. "Twelve years ago you called a storm to save a village. Who died for it, Crane? Who was standing nearest the flame?"

And Cyrus, who had asked themselves that question every night for twelve years, finally had an answer that wasn't guilt. It was worse.

"No one," they whispered. "No one died. My exile-brand. The storm took it as surety—my magic, my years, chained to the fire. That's why the brand burns when I use too much. That's why the prophecy calls me—" They pressed a hand over their sternum, over the scarred seal they'd carried since they were nineteen. "*The Thorn.* The thorn is the brand. The book didn't mark me. It *described* me. It described what I already am: the collateral on the last dragon's loan."

Wren sank down beside them on the cold mosaic, notebook forgotten, and took their scarred hand in both of hers, and turned it palm-up to the light.

"Then the choice in the end," she said softly, "isn't whether you burn. It's what the burning buys."

Above them, faint through stone and soil and the sleeping weight of a city that had forgotten how to remember, the first grey suggestion of dawn began. And in the iron box against Bren's chest, the last dragon's heart-flame pulsed once—twice—like something knocking politely at a door.

From deeper in the dark, from a direction no map of Vell admitted existed, came a sound none of them expected: footsteps. Unhurried. Accompanied by the soft chime of many small bells.

Bren blew out his lamp. "Well," he murmured. "Either the dead are keeping hours down here now, or the priestess found *us*."
