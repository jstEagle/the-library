## 13. Wren's Honest Lie

The lamp in the archive annex burned low, and Wren had been lying to it for three hours.

That was how she thought of it—lying to the light, because lamplight was honest in a way she had stopped being. It showed every smudge of ink on her fingers, every tremor in her hand as she traced the Ashkarin glyphs onto fresh vellum. The original book sat open before her, splayed like something dissected, its century-old pages soft as moth wing under her fingertips.

She was copying the Thorn's prophecy for a buyer who paid in coin she told herself she didn't need.

*Third page,* she reminded herself. *Only three more.* The buyer wanted the whole text reproduced in her own hand—her hand was certified, her transcriptions trusted by half the scholars in Vell—and then the original would go back to whatever vault the Guild didn't know about, and Cyrus Crane would never have to know that the woman he'd started to look at like she was sunlight had sold him before she'd ever kissed him.

Wren pressed her stylus down and copied the line that haunted her most: *And the Thorn shall burn, and by its burning the last fire shall be judged.*

Her hand shook on the word *burning*. A blot of ink spread across the vellum like a wound.

"You're bleeding on my prophecy."

The voice came from the doorway, and Wren's whole body went cold before her mind caught up to it. She knew that voice. She had spent two weeks learning it—the dry humor, the careful Vellish accent laid over something older and wilder underneath, the way it dropped lower when Cyrus talked about the weather because weather was never just weather to a storm-caller.

Cyrus stood in the doorway of the annex with rain still beaded on their shoulders, holding a paper bag of honeyed chestnuts they must have bought from the night market. For her. They'd bought chestnuts *for her.*

"Cyrus." Her own voice came out wrong. Flat. Archivist-flat, the tone she used for cataloguing dead people's letters. "You're early."

"I was early for dinner." They set the bag down on the table with terrible gentleness, as if it might break, as if anything here weren't already breaking. Their eyes moved from her face to the book, to the vellum, to the neat certified transcription hand that every archivist in Vell could identify at twenty paces. "I was early, so I thought I'd surprise you. And then I heard the scratch of a stylus from the hall, and I thought, Wren works late, of course she does, she loves this work."

They stepped closer. Rain dripped off their sleeve onto the flagstones. Under their skin, always, faintly visible when the light was low, something flickered—storm-light, trapped lightning moving through the channels of their arms like fish beneath ice.

"But you don't love this work," Cyrus said quietly. "Not tonight. Tonight you're copying it. Page by page. In your own hand."

"It's not what you think."

"Do you know what's funny?" They laughed—a small, awful sound. "Everyone says that. Every person I've ever watched lie, in twelve years of hiding, says exactly that. *It's not what you think.* And it's always exactly what I think."

* * *

Wren had been lying for so long that she knew all the architecture of it—the load-bearing lies, the decorative ones, the ones you told yourself so you could sleep. She had built her whole life inside that architecture.

She'd been nine years old when she first understood that truth was a currency in Vell, and that her family had none to spend. Her father had been a records-clerk in the Guild's outer offices, a man who copied inventories of confiscated books before they burned. He brought home fragments. Scraps. A page of Ashkarin poetry here, a child's primer of forbidden glyphs there, kept in a box under the floorboards like other families kept savings.

"Remember everything," he used to tell her, tapping her forehead. "Paper burns. Heads are harder to search."

He was wrong. They searched heads too. When Wren was fourteen, the Guild came for her father—not for the fragments, which he'd already surrendered and sworn against, but for a single line in an old inventory ledger he'd failed to redact: *one (1) volume, prophetic, provenance: Ashkarin, location unknown.* The Inquisitors wanted to know why a clerk who'd reported every other item had left one line standing. What was he keeping alive? What was he hoping for?

He denied everything for six hours. Then he gave them Wren's name, because she had been the one who found the ledger line and asked him about it, and a child's curiosity had signed his death warrant.

She watched the pyre from the public square, standing between her weeping mother and a wall of Guild guards, and she made herself a promise that had nothing to do with truth or honor: *never again would anyone be able to take something from her that she couldn't also sell.* Knowledge was currency. Grief was currency. Everything burned anyway; better to set your own price.

She'd gotten good at it. Better than good. By twenty-five, Wren Solenne was the most trusted transcriber in Vell—certified by three archives, unimpeachable, invisible in the way that only genuinely useful people are invisible. And she took commissions. Quiet ones. Copies of things the Guild would burn if it knew they existed, passed to buyers who paid in Guild-stamped coin because hypocrisy, Wren had learned, was the one universal language.

Then, eleven months ago, a buyer had come to her with a commission unlike any other.

He was a fat, soft-spoken man with rings on every finger, and he laid a book on her table—an *original*, bound in grey leather, its pages written in a script that predated the fall of Ashkarin—and said: "Copy everything. And then find me the person in it."

"The person in it," she'd repeated.

"The Thorn." He'd smiled with small white teeth. "The prophecy describes them, Wren. Scarred hands. Storm-gift. An exile hiding in a godless city. There's only one city in Ashkarin's shadow that hates magic enough to hide someone, and only one kind of person desperate enough to hide there. Find them. Bring me proof. The payment will buy your mother a house with a garden and servants until she dies."

Her mother. Who still woke some nights calling Wren's father's name. Who had never once blamed Wren aloud, which was somehow worse than blame.

So Wren had gone looking for a storm-caller in the night markets of Vell, and it had taken her four months, and when she finally found the charm-seller with the careful hands and the careful eyes—the one whose luck-charms actually *worked*, which no honest fraudster's charms ever did—she had prepared her approach like a scholar preparing a translation. Charm first. Warmth second. A protection charm purchased as pretext, an invitation to dinner, patience layered over patience.

She hadn't prepared for the part where Cyrus Crane turned out to be *funny*. Where they memorized how she took her tea by the second evening and never got it wrong again. Where they listened—actually listened, hunger and all—to her opinions about archival cataloguing systems as though she were describing the geography of heaven. Where she'd sat across from them at a tiny corner table in the Lamplight district, watching candlelight move in their storm-grey eyes, and felt something crack open in her chest that she'd bricked over at fourteen years old in a public square that smelled of smoke.

She hadn't prepared for wanting the lie to be true.

* * *

"I can explain," Wren said now, and heard even as she said it how thin the words were, how transparent, like a curtain in wind.

"Can you?" Cyrus didn't sit. They stood with the table between them, between her and the open prophecy and her ink-stained betrayal, and their face had gone very still—the stillness, she realized, of someone who had practiced being unreadable for twelve years and was now doing it as armor. "Explain. Yes. Let's hear it. Tell me about the buyer. Tell me how long."

The terrible thing was that she respected them too much, in that moment, to keep lying well. That was the flaw in her life's whole architecture: she had never accounted for what it would feel like to hurt someone she—

Someone she *what?* She refused to finish the word even inside her own skull.

"A year," Wren said. "Eleven months. I was given a commission to copy the book and find the figure it describes. Scarred hands. Storm-gift. An exile in Vell." She lifted her chin, because if she was going to be destroyed she would do it with her spine straight, the way her father had gone to the pyre. "I found you in the night market in Marrow Row. Four months of searching. Your luck-charms work. Nobody's charms work in Vell unless they have real power behind them."

"My scars." Cyrus looked down at their own hands as if seeing them for the first time—as if they were objects in someone else's inventory. "That first night. You asked to see the burn scars. You said—" Their voice caught and they steadied it. "You said you'd read that scar-tissue held memory better than skin. You said it so beautifully I almost believed you cared."

"I did care." The words came out of her fast and graceless. "I do. That's not—Cyrus, that part isn't a lie, none of it was a lie except the beginning. The dinner. The reason I found you. Everything after that was real."

"How would you know?" Now Cyrus did sit, dropping into the chair opposite her like a marionette with cut strings, and up close she could see what the stillness was costing them—the fine tremor in their jaw, the storm-light under their skin flickering out of rhythm. "You've lied so long and so professionally that you probably don't know anymore. Maybe nobody does. Maybe you sat across from me at dinner feeling exactly what you felt and thinking, all along, *this is what he wants to see.*"

"That's not fair."

"No," Cyrus agreed softly. "It's not. Isn't that the whole point of tonight? Nothing about us is fair. I was a job, Wren. I was a *line item*. Somewhere out there is a person with money who wanted proof of my existence, and you delivered it wrapped in chestnuts and tea and—"

They stopped. Looked at the chestnuts. Looked away.

"Did they send you back for more?" they asked. "Is that what tonight is? Copy the pages, confirm the details, note any changes?"

"Nobody sent me back. This—" Wren put her hand flat on the transcription, the half-finished copy, her own damning handwriting. "This was my idea. My addition. The buyer wants the text; I decided to give him something extra."

"Why?"

"Because knowledge is the only thing that survives." It was her father's creed coming out of her mouth, worn smooth from a lifetime of handling. "Because the Guild burns everything it fears, and the only defense against burning is copies. If the prophecy exists in ten hands instead of one, then killing the original doesn't kill the meaning. I've spent my whole life making sure the things they want erased stay remembered. I wasn't going to stop for—"

"For me." Cyrus's laugh was worse this time. "You were going to protect the prophecy *about me* from being erased, by selling it, while the man it's about gets erased instead. Do you understand what happens when that buyer has my name, Wren? Have you met the Inquisitor's Guild? There is a man named Halvard Voss who has spent twelve years building his career on the ashes of people like me. He burned my stall last week. He's interrogating his way through this city, street by street, looking for exactly the trail you've been laying."

"I know about Voss." Her voice cracked. "I know. I've known since the market burned, and I kept copying anyway, because I told myself the commission was nearly finished, because I told myself the payment would fix my mother's life, because I am—"

She stopped. Breathed. Finished it honestly, since honesty was apparently tonight's currency and she was bankrupt either way.

"Because I'm a coward who tells herself she's a librarian."

The silence after that was long. Rain ticked against the annex's high windows. Somewhere in the archive's depths, a clock chimed the third hour, and neither of them moved.

When Cyrus finally spoke, their voice had changed—not cold anymore, but tired in a way that frightened her more than anger would have. Anger meant she still mattered enough to fight with.

"Do you know what I did today?" they said. "Before I came here. Before the chestnuts." They rubbed at their forearm, where the old exile-brand sat hidden under their sleeve. "I stood outside your door for twenty minutes rehearsing how to tell you about my scars. The real story. Not the charming version. Because I decided—" a breath, unsteady, "—I decided you were worth the risk of being known. Twelve years of being nobody to everyone, and I chose you to be somebody to. And while I was rehearsing honesty in the street like a fool, you were in here selling me."

"Cyrus—"

"The worst part," they went on, relentless, quiet, "is that I can't even hate you properly. Because I understand. Gods, I *understand*. You needed the money, and I needed—" they gestured helplessly at the space between them, "—whatever this was. Two liars at one table. We deserve each other, and I still feel like my ribs have been opened."

Wren reached across the table—slowly, the way you'd reach toward a spooked animal—and stopped short of touching their hand. Her fingers hovered over theirs, trembling, ink-stained, guilty.

"Tell me what happens now," she whispered. "Tell me how to fix this. I'll burn the copy. I'll refuse the payment. I'll take you to the buyer myself and let you decide what he learns and what he doesn't. I'll—"

"The buyer knows my description already." Cyrus pulled their hand back, gently, which hurt worse than snatching it away. "You said it yourself—eleven months. Proof delivered. Whatever he needed from you, he has. The only question left is what he does with it, and whether he needs the *book itself* or just the man." Their eyes lifted to hers, and in them the storm-light reflected, and for the first time since she'd known them, Wren saw fear in Cyrus Crane's face—not of the Guild, not of prophecy, but of her. "Which means the only variable left in my survival, Wren, is whether you tell him where I am."

She flinched as if struck.

"I would never—"

"You sold me once already."

"That was *before!*" It tore out of her louder than she intended, echoing off the archive shelves, and she lowered her voice to a fierce whisper. "Before I knew you. Before the dinners and the tea and the night you told me about Sefa and I lay awake afterward hurting for you. Before I—" Her breath shuddered. "I was going to finish the commission, take the payment, and disappear from your life before you ever learned the truth. Clean extraction. That was the plan. And then the plan stopped being possible because I fell in *love* with the assignment, and I kept telling myself I'd find a way to make it right, and instead I sat here tonight copying the sentence that says you have to burn, because I am a coward, Cyrus, but I am not—"

She pulled the transcription toward her, seized the topmost sheets, and held them to the lamp's flame.

The vellum caught instantly—her own careful handwriting curling into black lace—and she held on until the fire licked her fingertips, because some promises should cost something, and then she dropped the burning pages into the inkwell and killed them with a hiss.

Cyrus stared at the smoke rising from the inkwell. At her scorched fingers. At her.

"Three pages left," she said, shaking, cradling her blistered hand against her chest. "Of the copy. Three pages, and then the original goes back to the buyer, and he'll have the full text either way because I already delivered the first sections weeks ago. But those three pages contain the final verse—the conditions, the Door, everything about how the Thorn's story ends. As long as I hold them back, he doesn't have the ending. And I don't think he'll move against you without the ending, because men like him collect complete things."

"You're bargaining," Cyrus said slowly, "with prophecy pages."

"I'm bargaining with the only leverage I have left that doesn't put your blood on my hands." She swallowed. "But Cyrus—there's something else. Something I found while copying. Something I should have told you days ago."

Their eyes narrowed. "What."

"The prophecy doesn't just describe you. It describes *them*." Wren leaned forward, lowering her voice as though the dead archive might be listening, as though the Guild's ears grew from walls. "There's a second figure in the final verses. The Keeper. A keeper of embers, drunk and disgraced, guarding a coal that cannot die. Cyrus—I've read that description six times tonight, and I know where I've seen it before. I've seen it in a gutter off Salt Row, sleeping off a bottle, muttering to a tin box he won't let anyone touch."

Understanding moved across Cyrus's face like weather.

"Bren," they said.

"Bren," Wren agreed. "The prophecy names him, too. Which means whoever wrote this book, a hundred years ago, wrote it knowing both of you—which means this was never a prediction at all. It's a *record*. Someone watched all of this happen once." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Or someone is watching it happen *now*, Cyrus, and writing it down as we go. And there's one more figure in the final verse. One more person the book knows by deed, if not by name."

"Who?"

Wren looked at the smoke still rising from the drowned pages, at the charred lace of her own handwriting, and told the truth one more time—freely, this time, for whatever it was worth.

"The woman who betrays them."

Outside the annex window, far below in the sleeping streets of Vell, a guild-sealed lantern flared to life at the end of Archive Lane, and began, unhurried, to climb.
