## 14. The Buyer Below

Wren had copied forbidden texts before. She had smuggled pages sewn into her coat lining, memorized incantations she was never meant to read, sold secrets to buyers whose names were spoken only in whispers behind the archive's sealed doors. She had told herself, every time, that it was scholarship—that knowledge belonged to no one, that the Guild had no right to burn books any more than it had the right to burn people.

She had never once believed herself a traitor.

Standing in the cold antechamber beneath the Verrin estate, watching her own breath fog in air that smelled of candlewax and old money, Wren finally ran out of ways to lie to herself.

The invitation had come three days after she'd taken the pages from Cyrus—a note slipped beneath her door at the archive, written on paper heavy as cloth, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. *You have found something rare. Bring it. Bring him, if you can manage it. We will make you rich beyond the arithmetic of archivists.* No signature. Only a small stamped mark: a coiled serpent eating its own tail.

She had known what that mark meant. Everyone who worked the deep shelves knew. The Ouroboros—the circle of collectors who bought what the Inquisitors burned, who kept alive everything the city pretended to forget. For years they had been an abstraction to her, a rumor with deep pockets, the faceless patronage that made her dangerous work profitable. She'd imagined scholars, maybe. Eccentric lords with too many candles and too little purpose.

The reality was waiting for her now, behind a door carved with vines and thorns.

"You're early." The voice came from beside her, not through the door. Wren startled—she hadn't heard anyone approach—and turned to find a woman leaning against the corridor wall, wrapped in grey fur despite the warmth of the lamps. She was perhaps fifty, handsome in the way old portraits were handsome, with eyes the flat silver of coin. "That's good. Eager. Lord Verrin likes eagerness—it's so much easier to spend."

"I was told to come alone," Wren said.

"You were told to bring the Thorn." The woman smiled without warmth. "I'm Countess Amara Vell-Sarres. My family lent this city its name, back when names still mattered. And you, little archivist, are the first person in a hundred years to find a living piece of Ashkarin."

*Ashkarin*. The name landed in Wren's chest like a dropped stone. Outside these walls, almost no one could even pronounce it—the word slid off memory like water off glass. But this woman said it the way one says a lover's name: slowly, tasting it.

"You know what it is," Wren said carefully.

"Of course I do. I've spent forty years and most of my fortune trying to remember it." The countess pushed off from the wall and drifted toward the carved door. "Come. They're waiting, and Lord Verrin hates to be kept from his treasures."

* * *

The room beyond the door was round, windowless, and lit by a ring of tall white candles that burned without flickering—which meant magic, which meant these people, for all their wealth, trafficked in exactly what the Guild hunted. A table of dark wood dominated the center, and around it sat seven figures, though only five had faces Wren could see; two wore masks of plain porcelain, featureless except for eye-holes, and the anonymity of them made her skin crawl worse than any grotesque would have.

Lord Aldous Verrin sat at the head. Wren recognized him from public functions—the archive staff were sometimes paraded before donors like exotic birds. He was a spare man in his sixties with a scholar's stoop and a magistrate's hands, and when he looked up at her, his gaze went straight past her face to the satchel at her hip.

"You have it?" he asked. No greeting. No courtesy. Just hunger, held on a very tight leash.

"The pages." Wren did not move to open the satchel. "As agreed, you'll tell me what this is about first. All of it. I don't sell blind."

A murmur moved around the table—not annoyance, exactly. Appraisal. The countess laughed softly. "Oh, she negotiates. Verrin, you said she wouldn't."

"She's here," Verrin said. "That's all the negotiating that matters." He gestured to an empty chair, and when Wren didn't take it, he shrugged and rose instead, crossing to a cabinet against the wall. From it he lifted something wrapped in black velvet and set it on the table with the reverence of a priest handling relics.

"This," he said, unwrapping it, "is why we brought you below."

It was a shard of stone no larger than a loaf of bread, veined through with threads of dull gold—and those veins were warm. Wren could see them pulsing faintly, slow as a sleeping heartbeat, and the light they gave off was wrong for a lamp or a fire. It was the light of embers. Of a hearth going out.

"Do you know what you've been carrying around in your satchel, Mistress Wren?" Verrin continued. "Copies of a prophecy concerning a figure called the Thorn, written in Ashkarin, in a hand at least a century dead. You think you've found a curiosity. A footnote." His thin lips curved. "What you've found is a map to the last dragon's heart."

The words seemed to fall from a great height. Wren heard them, understood each one separately, and felt the floor tilt beneath the meaning of the whole.

"That's not—" Her voice came out thinner than she wanted. "The dragon-fire is dying. Everyone knows it's dying. That's not a secret, it's just—the world getting colder. Slowly. Over years."

"Yes. Slowly." Verrin traced one golden vein with a fingertip, and the ember-light brightened briefly under his touch, like a coal breathed upon. "And when it finishes dying, the Door out of Ashkarin opens—if it can be found, if someone can walk through it, if someone can carry what remains of the fire back into the world. The prophecy describes exactly such a person. The Thorn. Marked hands. Storm in the blood. Destined to burn." He looked up at her, and his eyes were terribly patient. "You found him. A storm-caller hiding in the night market with scarred hands and a charm-seller's smile. Do you have any idea how long we've been looking?"

The room was very quiet. Wren became aware that she was gripping the strap of her satchel hard enough to hurt her fingers.

"You want to give the fire back to the world," she said slowly. "That's—good. That could save—"

"We want to *take* the fire," Countess Vell-Sarres corrected gently, settling back into her chair. "Giving back is such a small ambition, dear. Let me show you what we intend."

She snapped her fingers, and one of the masked figures rose, crossed to the far wall, and drew back a curtain Wren had taken for decoration. Behind it, filling the entire wall, hung a painting—and Wren's breath left her.

It depicted the world, but transformed. Cities of white stone climbing into skies crossed by rivers of visible flame. Fields green in midwinter. Ships sailing on seas that steamed and glowed. And above it all, vast enough to shadow continents, the silhouette of a dragon—not chained, not caged, but *harnessed*, its fire pouring down into great engines and aqueducts and towers crowned with burning crowns. At the base of the painting, small as ants, human figures raised their arms toward the sky. Some wore robes. Some wore armor. One, rendered in gold leaf, wore a crown.

"Ashkarin had this," the countess said, her voice gone soft and reverent. "Before your Guild erased it. Dragon-fire fed every hearth, healed every wound, lit every road. And a handful of families—the ones who knew how to hold the reins—lived as near-gods. When Ashkarin fell, the fire was scattered, hidden, hoarded. What's left is guttering in a drunkard's lantern somewhere under this city." She turned her coin-flat eyes on Wren. "We are the descendants of those families. Every one of us at this table has spent a lifetime being *almost* powerful—rich enough to matter, too magical to be safe, too ambitious to kneel. The dragon's heart is our inheritance. We mean to claim it."

"And the Thorn?" Wren asked. Her mouth had gone dry. "The prophecy says he burns. It says—"

"Prophecies are instructions, Mistress Wren, written by clever people for obedient ones." This from Lord Verrin, who had returned to his seat. "The Thorn burns. Fine. Fire needs tending. Someone must carry the coal to the altar. Whether he survives the carrying is a question of engineering, not fate." He spread his magistrate's hands. "But we will need to *find* the Door, and find the keeper, and control the storm-caller—and you, my dear archivist, have already done half our work. You found him. You gained his trust. You copied the pages." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Unless you're telling us you've grown attached."

The masked figures had not moved. But Wren felt their attention settle on her like a physical weight, and she understood suddenly, completely, what she had walked into. Not a salon of scholars. Not collectors preserving what the Guild would burn.

A board of directors. And the commodity was a living man.

"He trusts me," she said. The words tasted like ash. "He's—he's been alone for twelve years. He let me in because I was the first person who—" She stopped, because finishing the sentence would have been obscene in this room. *Because I was kind to him. Because I made him believe he could be loved.*

"Then he'll follow you anywhere," the countess said pleasantly. "Which saves us the expense of hunters. Though do understand—we have those too. If sentiment makes you unreliable, the arrangement simply changes hands. There are other archivists. There are other ways into a lonely man's heart." She smiled. "We'd rather it were you. He knows your face. People forgive faces they love things they'd never forgive in strangers."

Wren thought of Cyrus in the lamplight of the market stall, laughing for the first time since she'd known him, hands cupped around a cup of tea like it was something precious. She thought of the way he flinched at guild seals. The way he counted exits in every room. The way he had looked at her—looked *at* her, not through her—for the first time in twelve years, according to Bren.

She thought: *I did this. I am the reason they know.*

"How much?" she heard herself say. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "For the pages. For everything I've gathered."

Verrin named a number that would have bought her wing of the archive outright. Behind her, distantly, she registered that she should feel something—triumph, maybe, or terror—but mostly she felt the specific numbness of a person watching themselves do something unforgivable and being unable to stop.

"The pages," she said, and opened the satchel.

* * *

They kept her below while clerks verified the copies—hours, in a side chamber with wine she didn't drink and bread she didn't touch. Through the walls she could hear the murmur of the cabal conferring, occasionally a single sharp laugh, the scrape of chairs. Once, footsteps passed her door and paused, and she heard the countess's voice through the wood, low and amused: "Leave her. Guilt is useful. It keeps people working."

When they finally released her, it was past midnight. A servant walked her up through the Verrin house—room after room of inherited splendor, portraits of ancestors whose faces grew subtly more reptilian the older they were—and out a garden gate into the ordinary stink and noise of Vell's streets.

The city hit her like cold water. Lantern-sellers dousing their flames. A drunk singing badly two streets over. The normal, indifferent machinery of a place that had no idea seven people beneath its feet were planning to unmake it.

Wren stood in the garden gate's shadow for a long time, clutching the purse of coin she'd accepted like it might burn through her palm.

*You could warn him,* said a voice in her head that sounded like her mother, dead twenty years. *You could go to the market tonight. You could tell him everything and run.*

And then another voice, quieter, wearing her own tone: *Warn him and lose everything. The countess was right—there are other ways into a lonely man's heart. They'll simply hire someone kinder than you. Someone who won't hesitate. At least if you stay close, you'll know where he is. At least if you stay close, you can—*

She stopped the thought there, because she knew where it led. Every collaborator in history had told themselves they stayed to soften the blow.

She began to walk. Not toward the night market. Toward the archive—toward her narrow room and her locked desk and the one page she had not copied for them, the final line of the prophecy that she'd told Verrin was illegible, smudged, useless. It wasn't illegible. She had read it a dozen times by candlelight, and she still couldn't decide what it meant, and she had kept it back from the most dangerous people in Vell for reasons she couldn't yet name—instinct, maybe, or the last functioning piece of her conscience whispering *hold something back, hold anything back*.

*The Thorn burns,* the line read. *But the fire remembers whose hand it leaves.*

She mouthed it again as she walked, faster now, her heels striking the cobblestones like a countdown. Somewhere behind her, high on the Verrin hill, candles burned in a windowless room, and five faces and two blank porcelain masks bent over her copies of a dead country's warning, planning their ascension.

And somewhere ahead of her, in a cellar that smelled of rain and old feathers, a drunk old keeper was guarding a coal that dreamed, and a man with storm in his blood was probably awake, counting exits, trusting her.

Wren realized she was crying only when the tears went cold on her jaw.

She wiped her face, squared her shoulders, and walked on into the dark—carrying her thirty pieces of silver and one stolen truth, trying to decide, step by step, which of them she was going to betray first.

Because behind her, faint but unmistakable, she had heard Lord Verrin give the order as she left the chamber:

"Send for the hunters. Quietly. If the archivist falters, we take the storm-caller ourselves—and we take him *tonight*."
