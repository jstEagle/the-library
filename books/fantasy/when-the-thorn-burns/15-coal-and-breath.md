## 15. Coal and Breath

The ember did not glow that night. It *breathed*.

Cyrus noticed it first as a change in the room's silence—the small rented room above the tannery where Bren had grudgingly agreed to let them hide, the air already thick with the smell of lye and old blood from the vats below. Bren slept in a chair by the window, his boots still on, one hand curled loosely around a bottle he'd never opened. Wren had taken the floor by the door, her satchel of stolen pages beneath her head like a pillow she didn't trust anyone else to guard. And on the table between them, wrapped in three layers of oilcloth and a charm of Bren's making, the coal that was not a coal sat and breathed.

Slow. Slow. Like something sleeping very far down at the bottom of the sea.

Cyrus had meant to take first watch. They had meant to sit up with their back against the wall, counting the creaks of the tannery beams, listening for Guild boots on the stair. Instead they had sat down at the table just to look at it—just to make sure it was still there, still alive, still the impossible thing it was—and sleep had come up behind them like a tide.

They dreamed of rain.

Not Vell's rain, which fell thin and guilty, ashamed of itself. This rain came down in ropes, in curtains, warm as bathwater, and it struck rooftops that sang. Clay tiles, thousands of them, terracotta and slate and copper gone green, and every drop that hit them rang a different note until the whole city was an instrument being played by the sky.

Cyrus stood on a high balcony and looked out over Ashkarin.

*This is what it was,* said a voice that was not a voice, that arrived not through their ears but through their bones, through the scarred hollows of their palms, through the brand-shaped ache under their collarbone that had never stopped hurting since the day they were exiled. *This is what you were told never existed.*

The city went on farther than any city had a right to. It spilled down the hillside in white-walled tiers toward a harbor full of ships with sails dyed every color grief could name. Beyond the harbor, the water. Beyond the water, nothing—but a beautiful nothing, a horizon so clean it looked freshly made. Storm clouds stood over the eastern hills like patient cattle, and Cyrus could feel them the way they had once felt storms as a child: as neighbors. As things that knew their name.

People filled the streets below. Not crowds—*people*. A woman arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the price of silverfin. Two boys racing paper boats down a gutter swollen with rainwater, shrieking when theirs capsized. An old man on a stoop mending a net, singing to himself in a language Cyrus did not know and yet understood perfectly, the way you understand your own heartbeat. Every one of them wore a small charm at the throat or wrist or belt—a shell, a knot of colored thread, a sliver of storm-glass—and Cyrus realized, with a feeling like falling, that none of them were hiding. That here, magic was not contraband. It was jewelry. It was habit. It was the ordinary way people loved each other.

*We called them breathings,* the voice said. *Small magics. A grandmother breathing warmth into a fevered child. A sailor breathing calm into rope before a gale. Nothing that would frighten a Guild man. Nothing worth burning a country for.*

"Then why—" Cyrus started, and their dream-mouth worked, but the voice went on as if it hadn't heard, or as if it heard everything all at once and had no time for questions. There was a vastness to its attention that made Cyrus feel like a candle flame standing in front of a sunrise.

*Come further,* it said. *There is little left. I spend what I have to show you this.*

The city blurred like wet ink and re-formed, closer now, at street level. The rain had stopped. Evening light lay gold along the white walls, and the smell of the place rose up around Cyrus—bread and brine and woodsmoke and some flowering tree they had no name for, sweet enough to hurt. A market square. Stalls with striped awnings. And there, at the center of the square, a fountain around which stood twelve statues, weathered smooth, and Cyrus knew without being told that these were the Twelve Storm-Callers of Ashkarin, the line from which their own gift had descended like water down through generations of hands.

A girl of perhaps eight ran past Cyrus, laughing, chasing a cat with one white ear. As she passed, she stumbled, caught herself against Cyrus's leg—and looked up.

She saw them. In the dream, in the memory, across a hundred years of erasure, she looked straight into Cyrus's face and smiled.

"You're one of the callers," she said. "I can tell. Your hands are loud."

Then she was gone into the crowd, and Cyrus stood in the golden light of a murdered city with their hands trembling.

*Her name was Issa,* the voice said quietly. *I remember forty thousand names. I am running out of fire to hold them.*

* * *

The dream turned, the way dreams do—not gradually but all at once, like a page torn out and replaced with another.

Now the sky over Ashkarin was wrong.

Cyrus felt it before they understood it: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like coins, the particular electric dread that every storm-caller knows, that lives in the marrow before the mind catches up. But there was no storm coming. That was the horror of it. The sky was clear. Clear and hard and blue as kiln-fired ceramic, and it stayed clear, and it *stayed* clear, and the pressure kept building anyway, building like a held breath, like a scream swallowed for days—

*They came at harvest,* the voice said. *When our granaries were full. When the roads were crowded with pilgrims. They chose the day when we would be hardest to save.*

The white walls of the dream-city began to smoke at the edges. Not burn—smoke, as if reality itself were smoldering, as if the world had decided Ashkarin was a mistake and was rubbing it out while it was still warm.

And the people—Cyrus tried to run toward them and could not; their dream-body moved through the streets like smoke through fingers. The woman with the fishmonger was frozen mid-laugh, her face going gray from the edges inward. The old man with the net sat on his stoop and simply became less, the way footprints become less in wind. And the girl—Issa—stood in the middle of the square looking up at the too-clear sky, her hand raised as though to catch rain that would never fall again, and Cyrus screamed at her to run, screamed with a voice that made no sound in any world, living or remembered—

*Do not grieve yet,* the voice said, gentle and terrible. *Grieve after. First witness.*

*It took nine days. Nine days for the Guild's unmaking rites to chew through a nation. We fought. Oh, how we fought. Our callers tore the sky open and threw the storm of the age at their ships, and the storm fell on them like the wrath of a god—and then the Inquisitors spoke their nullifying words, and the storm died in the air like a bird shot mid-flight, and every caller who had poured themselves into it fell empty as dropped gloves.*

*So we hid our greatest treasure. The last of us, the keeper-line, carried me out of my mountain while the city smoked, and I let myself be folded down, down, down—compressed into this, into coal, into a coal's worth of pretending to be dead—because a dead thing can be forgotten, and forgotten things survive.*

*They erased the name of the country from maps and minds both. Your grandparents could not have found Ashkarin on a map if the map lied to them, and it did lie, because maps are written by survivors and survivors are written by victors. But hear me, Thorn: erasure has a seam. Everything unwoven leaves a thread. The Door they sealed behind them when they fled—the Door out of the unmade country—it still stands. Sealed. Sleeping. Waiting for a key that walks on two legs and reads storms like scripture.*

*That is why I show you this. Not for pity. For weight. You asked once—I heard you ask Wren, that night in the archive—why you? Why does the prophecy wear your face?*

*I will answer you now, and you will wish I hadn't.*

The dream tilted. Cyrus was falling upward through smoke and gold light, and the voice came with them, closer now, intimate as a heartbeat pressed to their ear:

*Because the storm-caller's gift is not a power. It is a language. And languages do not belong to the people who speak them—they belong to the thing spoken to. Your gift descends from the Twelve. The Twelve learned it from us. From dragons. Every storm you have ever called, you called in our mother-tongue. You are the last fluent speaker of a dead nation's oldest prayer, and the Door knows the difference between a speaker and a parrot.*

*You are not chosen, Thorn. You are inherited.*

* * *

Cyrus woke on the floor beside the table, cheek pressed to cold boards, and did not know how long they had been asleep or whether they had slept at all. Their hands were numb. Both of them, from fingertips to wrist, as if they had been holding ice, and when they flexed their fingers the scars ached in a rhythm that was almost—but not quite—the rhythm of speech.

On the table, the ember pulsed. Once. Twice. Slower than before. Dimmer.

Bren was awake. He sat in his chair with the bottle finally open and untouched, watching the coal with an expression Cyrus had never seen on his ruined, joking face—an expression stripped of everything, raw as a wound. He looked, suddenly, much older than his years, and also much younger: a boy again, the last apprentice of a dead order, sitting vigil over the only heart he had ever been allowed to touch.

"It talked to you," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"What did it show you?"

"The city," Cyrus said. Their voice came out scraped. "Alive. All of it. The markets, the rain, the people—" They stopped. Swallowed. "There was a girl."

"There's always a girl." Bren's laugh had no humor in it, only rust. "Mine was named Odile. She used to feed the ember-hounds on the lower terraces. I was nineteen when they burned the terraces. I've spent thirty years telling myself I dreamed her." He took a long breath, and Cyrus watched him put his face back on piece by piece, like a man dressing in the dark. "Here's what matters, charm-seller. Did it tell you what it wants?"

Cyrus hesitated. On the table, the ember pulsed again—slower. They thought of the voice saying *I am running out of fire to hold them.* They thought of forty thousand names packed into a coal's worth of dying light.

"It wants to go home," Cyrus said. "Through the Door. And it says the Door won't open for me until—" They stopped.

"Until what?"

"Until the fire gutters out." Cyrus looked at the ember, at its dimming pulse, and felt the shape of the trap close around them with a tenderness that was worse than violence. "It has to nearly die first. The Door opens on near-extinction. That's the price of the lock."

Bren was silent for a long moment. Then he laughed—one short, broken note—and dragged a hand down his face.

"A lock that opens on a funeral," he said. "Of course. Of *course*. The Guild builds a cage even into its own ruins." He stood, joints cracking, and looked down at Cyrus with eyes that had gone hard and bright. "Then we don't wait for it to die. We find Ilyra's Door, and we find it fast, because that thing on the table is spending its last breaths showing ghosts to strangers, and every ghost it shows costs it heat. Do you understand me? Every vision is a withdrawal from an account that's already bleeding out."

"I understand."

"Do you?" Bren leaned close, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Because it showed you the pretty parts tonight. Wait until it starts showing you the murder. Wait until it shows you what the Twelve did in the end—the bargain they cut to buy those nine days. Then see if you still want to carry it home."

He gathered the oilcloth, wrapped the ember with hands that were gentler than his voice, and carried it to the window seat where the first gray light of morning was seeping in over the tannery roofs.

Behind him, Cyrus sat on the cold floor with their numb hands open in their lap, palms up, scars catching the dawn.

*Loud hands,* the dead girl had said, smiling.

And somewhere across the sleeping city, in a tower of black iron, an Inquisitor was waking to reports that a charm-seller's stall had been found burned and riddled with guild seals—and the hunt, which had been circling like a hawk all week, was about to fold its wings and drop.
