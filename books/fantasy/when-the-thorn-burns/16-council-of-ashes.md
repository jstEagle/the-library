## 16. Council of Ashes

The Council chamber of the Inquisitor's Guild had not changed in twelve years, and that was the cruelest thing about it.

Sefa Vane stood at the base of the amphitheater's central dais, her boots planted on the same worn stone where she had once knelt as a candidate taking her oaths, and looked up at the nine seats arranged in a crescent beneath the Guild's iron seal—a wheel with nine spokes, each spoke tipped with a flame rendered in blackened silver. The braziers burned the way they always burned, with that particular sanctified oil that gave off no smoke, so that the fire seemed to hang in the air like a thought no one was willing to speak.

She had rehearsed this argument forty times on the walk from the barracks. She had rehearsed it in the bath, in the archive, in the grey hour before dawn when the bells of Vell rang the waking and she lay staring at the ceiling of a room she had occupied for twelve years without ever once calling it home.

*Capture, not kill.* That was the whole of it. Everything else was scaffolding.

"Speaker Vane." The voice came from the center seat, where Prelate Ondrey sat—old, dry, his skin like parchment left too long near a flame. "You requested audience before this Council. The floor is yours. Be brief. We have other business."

Other business. She knew what the other business was. Everyone in Vell knew what the other business was. The pyres had been burning for six nights now.

Sefa bowed—not deeply; a Speaker of the Second Chair bowed to the Council as an equal bows to equals, and she had earned that chair with eleven years of flawless service—and began.

"Honored members. I come to you regarding the matter of the Thorn."

At the name, something moved through the crescent of seats like wind through wheat. Not alarm. Appetite. She had spent twelve years learning to read these people, and what she read now made her stomach turn: they were *hungry.*

"The prophecy recovered from the Ashkarin fragments names a figure—the Thorn—who will either preserve or destroy the last dragon-fire," she said. "The Guild's position, ratified by emergency decree, is that this figure must be found and eliminated before the prophecy can begin its turning. I am here to argue against elimination. I am here to argue for capture."

"On what grounds?" This from Inquisitor Maren Hale, third seat to Ondrey's left, a woman whose face never did anything at all, which was itself a kind of eloquence. "The decree is signed. The theology is settled. A prophesied agent of chaos is a blade already falling. You do not catch a falling blade, Speaker. You step aside and let it land."

"A blade lands once," Sefa said. "And then it is only metal on stone. But the Thorn is not a blade. The Thorn is a *person.* And the fragments are explicit—read them again, honored members, read them closely. The prophecy does not say the Thorn will burn the world. It says the Thorn will choose what burns. *Choose.* That is a word of agency. A creature of pure destruction has no choices. If we kill the Thorn before the choice is made, we learn nothing—we do not even know whether we have killed a savior or a monster. And if the fragments are right about one thing more—" she paused, let the silence gather, "—they are right that the dragon-fire cannot be preserved by force. It answers to the Thorn. Kill the Thorn, and whatever hope remains of understanding the fire dies with them."

She had them for a moment. She could feel it—the small, dangerous stillness of people actually listening.

Then the doors at the back of the chamber opened, and Halvard Voss came in.

He did not hurry. That was the first thing she noticed, and hated herself for noticing—that he entered the most powerful room in Vell as though he were entering his own kitchen, unhurried, unannounced, his grey coat immaculate, his hands folded behind his back. Twelve years ago he had been the rising star of the Guild, the man whose testimony had broken the storm-caller heretic of Corren's Ford. Then had come the failures—the escaped suspects, the burned evidence, the whisper campaign that had stripped him of rank after rank until he was a senior interrogator with nothing left but his reputation's ghost. Sefa had watched him fall the way you watch a tower lean: with pity, and with the distant expectation of dust.

But the tower had stopped leaning. Lately, watching him across the Guild's halls, she had begun to feel something else. The lean had become deliberate. He was not falling anymore. He was *aiming.*

"Forgive the interruption, honored members," Voss said, bowing with exactly the correct depth. "I would not presume—but I understand the matter under discussion is the Thorn, and I have information bearing directly upon it."

"You were not summoned," Ondrey said mildly.

"I bring news that will not keep." Voss turned his head, and his eyes found Sefa's with the gentle precision of a surgeon finding the wound. "Speaker Vane argues for capture. A compassionate position. A *personal* position, perhaps." He let that sit. He was very good at letting things sit. "I ask only that the Council hear me before it votes. Because I believe the Speaker's argument, however elegant, rests on a fragment she has misread—or has chosen not to read fully."

"I have read every fragment twice," Sefa said, and heard the heat climb into her own voice, and despised it.

"Then you have read the passage of the Two Hands." Voss walked slowly along the curve of the crescent, addressing the seats rather than her, making her a spectator in her own argument. "The fragment recovered from the drowned temple district—authenticated by our archivists three days past. Shall I recite it? '*Two hands bear the mark of the storm. One hand holds, and one hand lets go. The hand that holds shall be named Thorn, and the hand that lets go shall be named Ash, and the Ash shall serve the Thorn's enemy or be consumed by the Thorn's mercy.*'"

Silence. Sefa kept her face still because stillness was the last discipline left to her.

"That fragment is unverified," she said.

"It is authenticated," Voss said. "Seal of the Ashkarin scriptorium, carbon-dated by the Guild's own conservators. I can produce the report." He finally looked at her again, and there it was—not triumph, which she could have fought, but *sympathy*, which she could not. "Speaker. No one in this room doubts your loyalty. Your record is beyond reproach. Eleven years of exemplary service. But you must forgive us for noticing that your interest in the Thorn predates the recovery of the fragments by a considerable margin. Your request, four months ago, to reopen the cold files on the Corren's Ford heresy. Your questions about the charm-sellers' quarter. Your—shall I say—*unusual* diligence regarding a certain market stall."

"I investigate anomalies. That is my function."

"And your function has been performed beautifully." Voss smiled, thin as a knife laid flat. "Because your investigation led us somewhere remarkable. The stall you flagged—the charm-seller with the scarred hands, the one who vanished two nights ago leaving behind guild seals riddled through his awning—the archivists have confirmed the hand-marks on his recovered wares match the Thorn's description. Scarred palms. Storm-scarred." He paused. "You found him for us, Speaker. I want the Council to understand that. Whatever else is said here today, Sefa Vane is the reason we know the Thorn walks in Vell."

It was masterful. She saw it happen the way you see a flood take a valley—from above, helplessly, watching the water find every channel she herself had cut. Every query she had filed, every instinct she had followed since the day she'd seen those scarred hands in a century-old sketch and felt the world tilt sideways—all of it, laid end to end, made her look either like the Guild's finest bloodhound or like something far worse. Voss had simply arranged the facts and stepped back, and let the Council choose which story to tell itself.

And she knew which story frightened them.

"Speaker Vane," said Prelate Ondrey, and his voice had gone from mild to grave, the transition of a man closing a door. "You argue for capture. Tell the Council plainly: if you were ordered to lead the hunt for this Thorn—this heretic who shares a history with you, whose file you personally reopened—could we rely on your hand?"

There it was. The question inside the question. Twelve years ago she had stood in a smaller room, before fewer faces, and been asked a version of the same thing: *Your lover calls storms. Choose.* She had chosen duty then. She had told herself for twelve years that the choosing had cost her nothing, or that whatever it had cost was paid and done, a debt settled, a door closed.

Now the door stood open again, and the whole Council was looking at her through it.

"Yes," Sefa said.

The word came out steady. She would give herself that much. Whatever it cost, it would not cost her composure.

"I served this Guild when it cost me everything I loved," she said, and let them hear, just barely, the old grief—because they needed to hear it, because it was the only currency that would buy her what she needed. "I know better than anyone in this room what a storm-caller's mercy is worth. I watched a village drown while the Guild debated jurisdiction. I watched the heretic of Corren's Ford raise a storm to save it and damn himself doing it. I have no illusions about these people." *These people,* she said, plural, careful, and prayed the plural would cover the singular. "Capture, honored members. Not because the Thorn deserves mercy—but because a living Thorn can lead us to the Door, to the dragon-fire, to the truth of Ashkarin itself. A dead Thorn gives us ash and nothing else. Give me the hunt, and I will bring you the Thorn alive, and the Council will have both the answer and the weapon."

For a long moment, no one spoke. The smokeless flames hung in their braziers. Somewhere below the chamber, deep in the Guild's bones, water dripped in the flooded tunnels—the sound Vell made when it was thinking.

Voss inclined his head, gracious in the manner of a man who has already counted the votes.

"The Speaker makes a moving case," he said. "And I confess a part of me hopes the Council grants her request. Who better than she? She knows the Thorn's history. His habits. His—attachments." The word landed softly, precisely, on the bruise twelve years old. "But I must raise one final concern, and then I will yield the floor entirely. The fragments of the Two Hands. If the Thorn is the hand that holds, then somewhere there is a hand that lets go. An *Ash.* The prophecies are not clear whether the Ash serves the Thorn's enemy willingly or under duress—which means, honored members, that any person close to the Thorn is potentially compromised. Potentially *already* serving." He turned to the crescent and spread his hands, a gesture of terrible modesty. "I do not accuse. I only observe that the person best suited to lead this hunt is also, by the prophecy's own logic, the person most at risk of being turned by it. And should that happen—if the Ash should choose the Thorn over the Guild—then the Council will have handed the heretics both their savior *and* their inside voice. On the same day we burn the sixth pyre of the plague-season. While the city watches. While the city *waits* to see if we are equal to this."

He let the silence do the rest. It was very long, and it was very full, and Sefa understood, standing there with her spine straight and her hands quiet, that she had lost before she opened her mouth. Not because her argument was weak. Because Voss had understood something she had refused to understand for twelve years: that the Council did not want the Thorn captured or killed. They wanted to be *safe*, and safety, in a frightened city, wore whichever face promised it hardest.

"Speaker Vane." Prelate Ondrey's voice was almost gentle now, and gentleness from Ondrey was the sound of a verdict being read. "The Council thanks you for your counsel. Your argument for capture is noted and entered into the record." A pause, delicately cruel. "As is your declaration of fitness. The Council finds that fitness... compromised. By proximity. By history. By the fragment of the Two Hands, which names a role—the Ash—for which no candidate currently stands except yourself. You will appreciate that we cannot in good conscience place you in command of a quarry the texts may bind you to protect."

"Then let me recuse myself entirely," Sefa said. "Remove me from the matter. I will surrender my chair."

"No." Ondrey shook his head slowly. "That would look like flight, Speaker, and the city cannot afford the appearance that its Speakers flee. You will remain a Speaker. And you will serve the hunt—in the capacity the prophecy itself suggests." He lifted a paper from the table before him, and she saw the black wax of the Guild seal already pressed into it, already cooling, prepared before she ever entered the room. "You will accompany the hunt. As advisor. As—assurance. Senior Interrogator Voss will command."

The paper crossed the chamber floor in the hands of a clerk, and Sefa took it because refusing to take it would have been the last piece of herself she had left to lose. The ink was neat. The orders were simple. *Speaker Sefa Vane is seconded to the Thorn inquiry under the command of Senior Interrogator Halvard Voss, with authority to advise and obligation to comply.*

Obligation to comply.

She looked up one last time at the crescent of seats, at the nine black flames burning in their silver, and found Voss watching her—not gloating, worse than gloating, *kindly*, the way a man looks at a horse he has just bought.

"You see, Speaker," he said quietly, for her alone, as the Council moved to its other business and the clerks began gathering papers around them like leaves, "you taught me this, twelve years ago. Duty is the sharpest blade there is. It cuts whoever holds it. All I have done today is make sure it points the right direction." He gathered his coat, and paused beside her, and lowered his voice further still. "One more thing, since you'll be riding with us. My hunters tracked the Thorn's associates to the salt road out of the city—an old keeper, a disgraced archivist, a priestess off one of the burned registers. They move tonight. Toward the flats." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was the worst thing about it. "So you'll want to rest well, Speaker Vane. We ride at dawn. And I suspect—you more than anyone—will want to be present when we take him."

* * *

Her quarters in the Speakers' Wing had one window, high and narrow, looking east toward the salt flats and the pale line of the horizon where Vell ended and the erased country began. Sefa sat on the edge of her narrow bed with the order scroll unrolled across her knees and did not read it again. She knew every word.

On the shelf above her desk, between a treatise on evidentiary doctrine and a book of common prayers she had not opened in twelve years, stood a small clay lamp. Unglazed. Crooked. Worthless. She had bought it in Corren's Ford the week before everything ended, from a stall next to a boy who sold charms and laughed too easily and had hands that crackled when he touched iron railings in winter. Cyrus had teased her about it—*you're going to light a lamp? You, who sleep in full dark like a sensible person?*—and she had never lit it. Not once. She had carried it through the exile proceedings, through the branding, through the wagon ride back to Vell with her oath freshly sworn and her heart freshly buried. She had carried it through eleven years of promotions. It was the only object she owned that predated the person the Guild had built out of what remained of her.

She took it down now. Turned it in her hands. The clay was cool, and faintly rough, and under her thumb she found the place where the glaze had failed, a small flaw like a scar.

*The hand that lets go shall be named Ash.*

Voss thought he had trapped her. Perhaps he had. But he had made one error, the same error the Council made, the same error she herself had made for twelve years: he believed that naming a thing bound it. That calling her the Ash made her the prophecy's instrument, that ordering her compliance made her obedient, that the girl who chose duty over love was a finished thing, sealed and shelved.

He did not know that she had lain awake every night for twelve years composing the argument she had failed to make at Corren's Ford. He did not know that today, before the Council, she had finally spoken it—too late, in the wrong words, to the wrong ears—but spoken it, at last, aloud. He did not know that the Council had heard her argue for mercy and mistaken it for weakness, and that she had let them, because a Speaker who is underestimated can walk places a suspect cannot.

They rode at dawn. They rode toward the salt flats, toward an old drunk keeper and a lying priestess and a disgraced archivist, toward a charm-seller with storm-scarred hands who had every reason to hate her and every reason to run.

And Sefa Vane, seconded, obligated, leashed, sat alone in the dark with an unlit lamp in her hands, and began—to the horror of everything she had built, and the relief of everything she had buried—to plan how she would fail.

Outside, far off toward the flats, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky.

She closed her hand around the crooked little lamp, and started packing.
