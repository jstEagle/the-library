## 17. The Toll Road

The salt flats began where the road gave up pretending.

Bren had walked them once before, forty years ago, when he was young and the Guild still paid him and Yssara's ember sat warm against his chest like a second heart. Back then he'd thought the flats were beautiful — a white so total it looked like the world had been scoured down to bone, horizon to horizon, and the sky bent low over it like a lid on a pot. Now he knew better. The flats were a wound that had never been allowed to close. Everything the country had cut out of itself had drained this way: the erased villages, the unmapped roads, the names nobody was permitted to say. Salt grew over all of it like scar tissue.

"Keep your hood up," he told Wren, who was squinting against the glare. "The light out here remembers faces."

"That doesn't mean anything," she said.

"No," Bren agreed. "But it sounds wise, and I'm old, so people let me say things." He shifted the pack on his shoulders and felt the coal-shift inside it settle — the wrapped iron box with its impossible cargo, the last dragon-heart in the world, sleeping against his spine like a coal that refused to die. He'd carried it eleven years. He could carry it across salt for another three days if his knees agreed, which they were increasingly reluctant to do.

Behind them, somewhere past the haze, Vell burned in their memories only now — they'd left before dawn, three days ahead of the Guild's riders, or so Sefa's letters promised. Cyrus walked at the rear, as always, watching the way behind them the way a man watches a door he's not sure he locked.

Bren had spent enough years alone to recognize loneliness even when it traveled in company of four. The storm-caller had it worst. Wren had her guilt; Sefa had her duty, heavy and familiar as a uniform; but Cyrus had nothing to hold except the knowledge that every kindness he performed cost someone else their life. Bren understood that arithmetic. He'd done his own version of it for a decade — counting coals, counting breaths, telling himself the ember would last if he just rationed his hope carefully enough.

He'd been wrong about that too. He was wrong about most things. It was practically his trade.

* * *

They found the camp at dusk on the second day, where the flats rose into low chalk hills pocked with cave mouths, and smoke stood above one of the hollows thin and straight as a held breath.

Wren saw it first. "Refugees?"

"Borderers," Bren said. He'd smelled the camp before he'd seen it — tallow smoke, boiled grain, the particular sourness of too many bodies in too little shelter. "Look at the way the tents are pitched. Facing east. That's Ashkarin-facing. Old habit. People from the erased lands pitch toward home even after home stops existing."

"You can't face a direction that doesn't exist," Sefa said.

"Tell them that," Bren said. "Go on. I'll hold your coat."

There were perhaps sixty of them, in shelters made of cart canvas and reed mats and, in one case, half a boat carried impossibly far inland, upturned and roofed over with hides. Children ran between the cookfires barefoot on ground that would flay a boot. A woman was mending a net there was no water within fifty miles to use it in, and when Bren passed her she touched the netting to her forehead, quick and automatic, the way you'd touch a relic.

An old man came toward them leaning on a crook of blackthorn, moving with the deliberate economy of someone who had learned that every gesture costs. His eyes went over each of them in turn — cataloguing, pricing — and stopped on Bren's hands.

"Keeper," the old man said.

Bren's stomach dropped through the salt. "You're mistaken, grandfather."

"I've seen the oath-mark." The old man turned his own palm up. There was a scar there, old and white, shaped like a hook — a keeper's brand gone wrong, or done by someone who only half remembered how it was done. "My father kept the fire-shrines on the Verge. Before." *Before.* In the borderlands they said *before* the way other people said a god's name: carefully, and only when necessary.

"We're passing through," Bren said. "We'll take no food we don't pay for."

The old man's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You'll take what's given. That's different." He looked past Bren then, at Cyrus, and his face changed — not with recognition, Bren realized, but with the particular hunger of a dying man looking at weather. "You're the charm-seller. Word came up the flats ahead of you. Word travels faster than feet, out here. They say you mend things."

"He sells luck-knots," Wren said quickly. "Trinkets."

"They say he laid hands on a fevered child at Tollman's Rest and the fever left like a bird out a window." The old man's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. Sixty people in a chalk hollow had gone quiet around six cookfires, and quiet carries. "My granddaughter has the lung-rot. She is nine years old."

"Wren," Cyrus said softly. Just the name. A question and a warning both.

Bren watched Wren look at Cyrus — really look, the way she'd been learning to since the rain, since the anchoring lessons, since the night she'd confessed everything and he'd chosen, impossibly, to teach her instead of hate her. Something passed between them that Bren couldn't read and didn't want to.

"The lung-rot," Cyrus said to the old man. "How long?"

"Since the first frost."

"And the healers in Vell—"

"Would not see us." Flatly. No bitterness left in it; the bitterness had been refined away long ago, like ore worked until only metal remained. "We are not from anywhere. You cannot refuse treatment to people who were never born."

Bren heard the sentence land in Cyrus like a stone in a well. He knew that sound. He'd made it himself, once, standing in front of the Guild tribunal while men in grey read aloud the record of his failures.

"Show me the child," Cyrus said.

* * *

She lay in the upturned boat under hides, small and sharp-elbowed, breathing in a way that made Bren's chest hurt to listen to — a wet whistle on every inhale, a rattle on every exhale, the sound of lungs filling slowly with something that should have stayed outside them. Her grandmother knelt beside her, and her mother beside the grandmother, and the whole camp seemed to have arranged itself at a respectful distance, close enough to see, far enough to pretend they weren't hoping.

Cyrus crouched, rolled his sleeves back. Bren saw the exile-brand on his forearm, the old burn in the shape of a thorned band, and saw the old man see it too and say nothing.

"I should tell you," Cyrus said quietly, to the grandmother. "I'm not trained. What I do isn't medicine. It's — borrowed. And sometimes borrowing goes wrong."

"Borrow from whom?" the grandmother asked.

Cyrus didn't answer. Bren answered for him, because someone had to, and because he was tired — tired in a way that had nothing to do with his knees.

"From the air," Bren said. "From whatever's nearest. Storm-callers don't make anything. They move it. And the bill—" He stopped. Looked at Cyrus. "The bill gets paid by whoever's standing close."

Silence, except for the child's whistling breath.

"Then stand close to me," the grandmother said. She was maybe sixty, weathered to leather, hands like root-balls. "I've had my years. More than my share. Take mine."

"It doesn't work like that," Cyrus said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, and Bren understood suddenly that it had never worked like that, and that Cyrus had learned the shape of the rule only recently and badly, and that every healing charm he'd ever sold in Vell's night market had been a coin pulled from some stranger's purse without their knowing. "I can't choose who pays. If I could—" He shut his eyes. Opened them. "If I could choose, I'd pay myself. But the storm takes what it takes."

"Then do it," the grandmother said, "and let the storm be ashamed of itself."

Bren laughed — one short, ugly bark — before he could stop himself. Every head turned. He waved an apology and didn't mean it. *Let the storm be ashamed of itself.* Forty years he'd served an order whose entire liturgy was about containing fire, hoarding fire, rationing fire down to the last sacred gram because fire was holy and holiness must not be wasted. And here was a woman with salt-cracked hands proposing to shame a thunderstorm.

"Do it," she said again.

Cyrus looked at Wren. Wren nodded, once, and knelt opposite him, and put her palms flat against the boat-hull — against the ground beneath it — and Bren remembered what she'd told him on the road, half-boasting, half-frightened: *he taught me to anchor. When he pulls, I pull back. Like holding a rope in wind.*

Cyrus laid his hands on the child.

Bren had seen magic used exactly twice in his life at full strength — once at Yssara's side, once the night Vell's square filled with storm — and both times it had been enormous, architectural, a thing with buttresses and vaulting. This was none of that. This was small. That was what made it terrible to watch. Cyrus's hands moved over the girl's chest the way a man moves his hands over a trapped bird, gentle and precise, and the air above the child went strange — a shimmer, a pressure change, the feeling before lightning that lives in the teeth. The girl's breathing hitched. Whistled. Hitched again.

And then, deeper.

Bren felt it happen before anyone spoke. A cold thread through the camp, a wrongness like a note played slightly flat. Across the firelight, a young man stacking brushwood swayed, caught himself, blinked, and looked at his own hands as if they'd belonged to someone else a moment ago. An old woman by the second fire sat down abruptly on a mat, frowning, touching her cheek. Somewhere a child stopped mid-run and stood very still, confused by a tiredness that had arrived from nowhere, wearing someone else's footsteps.

The girl coughed — a real cough, deep and clearing — and drew a breath that whistled only a little, and then hardly at all. Her color came up like dawn.

The grandmother took Cyrus's wrists. Not thanks. Inspection. She turned his hands over and looked at the palms, at the fine new tremor there, and then she looked up at him with an expression Bren had last seen on the faces of keepers standing over a shrine they knew they could no longer maintain.

"How many years?" she asked.

"I don't know." Cyrus's voice was very steady, which meant it was costing him. "That's the point. I never know. I find out later, sometimes. A stranger ages in front of me and I count backwards and do the sums and hate myself at the end of them."

"Then you'll come back tomorrow," the grandmother said, "and the day after. There are others. And we will stand close, all of us, every one — and if the storm wants years, it can haggle with us. We know what our years are worth. We've priced them our whole lives." She released his wrists. "That is what it is to be from nowhere, charm-seller. Nobody has ever spent us gently before. Even roughly is an improvement."

* * *

Later, when the fires had burned down to red eyes and the child slept breathing like a person instead of a bellows, Bren found Cyrus sitting on the lip of the hollow, looking east, at nothing.

Bren lowered himself down beside him with the care of a man dismantling his own skeleton, joint by joint. For a while neither of them spoke. Out on the flats the salt glowed faintly under the stars, miles of it, the color of ash that had forgotten it used to be wood.

"My fault, you know," Bren said finally.

Cyrus glanced at him.

"The toll. Your magic spending strangers." Bren turned his hands over, studied the keeper's scars. "When the ember was whole — when the Order still existed — storm-magic drew from the fire-field. From Yssara. That was the whole design of it. The dragons burned, and their burning paid for every working in the world, and keepers like me tended the exchange. Balanced ledger. That was the job. That was the *point* of keepers." His laugh came out gray. "And I lost the ledger. Lost the shrine, lost the Order's records, drank through the years I should have spent rebuilding the exchange lines. So now when you call a storm, it reaches for the nearest fuel, because the proper hearth is gutted. Every year those strangers lose is a year the Order failed to protect. My failure. Compounding interest."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. "You were one drunk man against a whole institution falling."

"I was the last keeper," Bren said. "One was supposed to be enough. That was the oath. Not 'try.' Not 'do your best.' One." He rubbed the scar on his palm. "You want to know the worst part? When the Order fell, everyone blamed the dragons. Too wild, too costly, the fire too dangerous to tend. And the Council believed it, because it was easier than believing that forty sworn keepers had simply — gotten tired. Gotten comfortable. Gotten human." He looked east, at nothing, at a country that wasn't there. "Ashkarin wasn't murdered in a day, boy. It died of neglect. Same as the child in that boat would have. Same as Yssara is dying now, one unattended hour at a time. And I stood watch the whole time and called it keeping."

"You're still keeping," Cyrus said. "You've carried that ember eleven years."

"Carrying isn't keeping." But Bren said it without heat, and he found, to his surprise, that something in his chest had loosened — a knot he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for an organ. Confession, he thought. Cheap medicine, foul taste, works more often than it should. "Eleven years I've told myself I'm preserving her fire until someone worthier comes along. But there is no one worthier. There's just the next pair of hands, and whether they're honest ones." He looked sideways at Cyrus, at the fine tremor in the storm-caller's fingers. "You spend strangers' years and you hate yourself for it. Good. Hate is the beginning of bookkeeping. The day you stop hating it is the day you become the Guild."

Down in the hollow, a woman began to sing — low, wordless, the kind of song that exists only to prove that someone is still awake and listening. Others picked it up in fragments, off-key, unhurried. Bren recognized the tune distantly, from his childhood, from a shrine he'd been carried to in his mother's arms: an Ashkarin lullaby for children afraid of storms, promising that the thunder was only the sky remembering its dead.

"They still sing it," he said. "A hundred years of erasure, and they still sing it facing east."

"Erasure doesn't take," Cyrus said, "if people keep humming."

* * *

It was near midnight when the sentry's dog started barking.

Not the idle barking of dogs at dreams — the flat, continuous alarm of an animal that had decided something needed killing. Bren was on his feet before his knees finished voting, one hand already on the iron box at his back. Down in the camp, fires were being smothered with sand, practiced and fast; these people had done this before.

Sefa materialized out of the dark at the hollow's rim, moving low, and Bren had to admit the woman could move like poured oil when she chose. "Riders," she said. "Three. Coming up the flats from the west. Guild colors, or something pretending to be them."

"Voss's outriders can't have tracked us this fast," Wren whispered.

"Not Voss's," Sefa said, and Bren heard it, the fracture in her voice, the thing she was carrying that she hadn't yet set down in front of them. "These aren't hunting warrants. They're flying parley-white." Her jaw worked. "It's a Guild courier. And he's carrying a summons." She looked at Cyrus, and then, horribly, at Bren. "Addressed to the Keeper of Embers. By name. Signed by the Grand Inquisitor himself."

Bren's hand tightened on the iron box, and beneath his palm, faint as a heartbeat, the coal-shift warmed — the first time in eleven years it had answered to anything but his own body heat.

Yssara, it seemed, had opinions about visitors.
