## 18. Ilyra's Bargain

The temple had no name anymore.

That was the first thing Ilyra loved about it—the way forgetting clung to its stones like moss. Once it had been the House of Ninefold Dawn, high seat of Ashkarin's faith, and once she had knelt on its marble floor as a girl of nine with her palms pressed flat and her heart full of a god who was real to her then. Now the roof was half open to the sky, and rain came through in silver threads, and no one in Vell remembered that this ruin at the edge of the tanner's quarter had ever been anything but rubble. They walked past it daily. They did not see it. Erasure was a mercy for some and an exile for others, and for Ilyra it had been both.

She lit the candles herself. Real wax, real flame—she trusted no other kind of light. Nine of them, set in a circle on the broken altar, and in their glow the old carvings woke: spirals and thorns, a dragon coiled around a door, words in a script that made ordinary readers' eyes slide away like water off oiled leather.

She had spent thirty years keeping this faith alive alone, in cellars and attics and the spaces between other people's thoughts, whispering prayers to a god whose name only she could still pronounce. Thirty years of being the last ember of something vast. She knew what it was to gutter.

So when Cyrus Crane climbed down through the collapsed stairwell behind the altar, dripping and wary, with Wren at their shoulder and Bren grumbling about his knees, Ilyra felt something she had not permitted herself to feel in decades.

Hope. And beneath it, coiled and patient, appetite.

* * *

"You're smaller than the book says," she said, by way of greeting.

Cyrus stopped three steps up, hand raised, and Ilyra watched the air around their fingers shimmer—that faint static tremble, the smell of a sky about to break. Even banked, even buried under twelve years of charm-seller's caution, the storm in them leaked. It was beautiful. It was exactly what the old texts promised.

"The book doesn't say anything about me," Cyrus said. "The book is a hundred years older than me."

"The book says what it says." Ilyra rose from her knees with the slow care of a woman whose joints had learned every kind of weather. She let them see her face fully—the scarred brow, the priestess's brand half-scraped from her cheekbone, the eyes she'd been told were unsettling even before anyone knew why. "Come out of the rain. Both of you. The keeper can stand where he likes; he smells of ash either way."

Bren snorted from the shadows. "I like her already."

"I don't," Wren said quietly, and Ilyra marked it. The archivist was sharp. Sharp things would need handling later. But tonight, tonight was for the Thorn.

Cyrus descended the last steps slowly, the way one approaches a shrine or a trap, and perhaps they knew those were often the same thing. Ilyra gestured them toward the altar's edge, where a bench of stone had been worn smooth by centuries of kneeling congregations. She poured tea from a dented copper kettle—chamomile cut with saltbush, the old Ashkarin blend—and watched Cyrus hesitate over the cup.

"It's not drugged," she said. "If I wanted you harmed, I'd simply tell the Guild where you sleep. Don't look so surprised—I've known which cellar you favor since the night you crossed the Salt Gate. You pay your tolls in copper, you haggle like a woman with six mouths to feed, and when it rains you stand in doorways watching the lightning the way other people watch a lover leave." She smiled. "I know my own faith's children when I see them."

"Exiled," Cyrus said. "Not faithful."

"All exiles are faithful. That's what exile means—it means you still believe in the thing that cast you out."

She let that sit. She had long ago mastered the art of silence, how to make it a vessel the other person fills. Around them the candles burned, and the rain threaded down through the broken roof, and somewhere above Vell the Inquisitor's Guild was nailing new decrees to doors, and Ilyra waited.

It was Wren who broke first, because archivists always do. "You said you could read the final clause. That the standard translation is wrong."

"The standard translation." Ilyra laughed, low. "There is no standard translation. There are Guild translations, burned into every schoolchild's catechism, and there is the truth, which has survived in nine copies and two living memories. Mine. And now—" she inclined her head toward Wren—"hers, if she's honest about what she copied."

Wren's jaw tightened. Cyrus looked between them, and Ilyra saw the wound there, still raw: the betrayal in the archive, the pages copied for a buyer. Good. A person with a fresh wound listens differently. A person with a fresh wound wants someone to tell them the pain has meaning.

"Read it," Cyrus said.

Ilyra rose and went to the altar wall, trailing her fingers over the carved spiral until she found the lines she wanted—deep grooves, filled with candle soot so they showed black against pale stone. Her voice dropped into the cadence of liturgy, the tongue of Ashkarin rolling off her like water she'd never learned to stop carrying.

Then she gave it to them in trade-speech, word by careful word:

*"When the last fire gutters, the Thorn shall stand at the Door, and the Thorn shall burn, and by the burning the world shall be saved—or unmade. What burns is the Thorn's to choose. Choose wrong, and everything ends. Choose right, and the Thorn burns with it."*

Silence. Rain. Candleflame bending in a draft.

"That's not what the Guild version says," Cyrus said finally. Their voice was very controlled. Ilyra recognized control like that; she'd worn it herself for years, lacquered over panic. "The Guild version says the Thorn burns, full stop. Sacrifice, doom, the end."

"The Guild version serves the Guild. A prophecy that demands sacrifice makes obedience holy." Ilyra turned back to face them, and here it was, the moment she had rehearsed for eleven days, since the refugee camps on the salt flats, since she'd watched Cyrus press healing charms into strangers' hands and seen the toll taken and paid without understanding. "But hear the whole of it, Thorn, because the whole of it matters. *What burns is yours to choose.* Do you understand what that means? Not whether. What."

Cyrus set down the cup. "Say it plainly."

"Gladly." Ilyra clasped her hands. "Your magic is bound to the exile-brand. Every storm you call spends years that aren't yours—you've seen that now, haven't you? The man in the market street. The refugees. You've been spending strangers' lives like a drunk spends inheritance." She didn't soften it; softness would have insulted them both. Cyrus flinched anyway, and she noted the flinch, filed it. "And the prophecy says that at the Door, the Thorn must choose what burns. Everyone assumes it means the dragon, or the world, or yourself. But there is another option. One thing bound to you that could burn instead. One thing that, if consumed in the choosing, would satisfy the clause entirely."

The candle flames leaned. Cyrus had gone very still, the stillness of prey and predator at once.

"My magic," they said.

"Your magic," Ilyra agreed. "Burned at the threshold. Freely given. The clause satisfied, the world spared, the dragon spared, the Thorn walking out the other side mortal and free. No more toll. No more years stolen. No more prophecy hanging over you like a blade. You'd be"—she chose the word with exquisite care—"ordinary."

Bren shifted in the dark. Wren said nothing, but her eyes had gone bright and fixed, and Ilyra thought: *there it is, the hunger of the clever, they always want to ask the next question.* Let them. Tonight the question was for Cyrus alone.

"And you're telling me this," Cyrus said slowly, "out of the goodness of a faith everyone forgot?"

"No." Ilyra let the smile fade. Honesty, now. Precision. This was the hinge on which everything turned, and she had learned the hard way that lies fail at hinges. "I'm telling you because the offering comes with a shape. Fire does not simply vanish, Thorn. It never has. When your storm burns at the Door, that power goes *somewhere*. Into whatever vessel stands ready to receive it. The texts are clear: the burning requires a keeper of the flame. Someone of the erased blood. Someone ordained in the rite that the rest of the world no longer remembers exists."

The rain grew louder, or perhaps it only seemed so.

"You," Cyrus said.

"I am the last priestess of Ashkarin," Ilyra said. "The last of nine thousand. I have kept a dead god alive in a dead language for thirty years with nothing but my memory and my stubbornness. I have earned the right to be the vessel. And in exchange—" she stepped closer, close enough that Cyrus could see the candlelight move in her eyes, could see that they were amber, dragon-colored, though Cyrus couldn't know yet what that meant— "in exchange, I will bind the burning clause for you. A shielding rite. Old magic, older than the Guild, older than the maps they burned. Under its protection, the prophecy cannot compel you, cannot mark you, cannot use your body as its instrument until you stand at the Door and choose freely. You would walk free of fate itself. No hunter's vision could find you. No book could hold your face. You would be, for the first time since you were nineteen years old, *yours*."

She saw it land. She saw hope move through Cyrus Crane like weather through wheat—the shoulders dropping a fraction, the breath changing, the terrible exhausted want of a person who had been hunted so long they'd forgotten hunting wasn't the natural state of things. And beneath the hope, she saw the other thing, the thing she needed most: doubt, reaching for a reason to be quiet.

That was how temptation worked. Never by force. By offering rest to the tired.

"What happens to it?" Cyrus asked. "After. The power. In you."

Ilyra spread her hands, and for a moment—only a moment—she let the truth show through, because a lie this large needed a skeleton of truth to stand on. "Ashkarin died because its fire was stolen and scattered. Its people, its storms, its name—all of it fed into the erasure that bought the victors their clean new maps. I have spent my life beside that theft like a daughter beside a murdered parent." Her voice roughened, and she let it. "If the storm-magic comes to me, I will carry it back to the Door myself. I will pour it into the land that held us. Ashkarin will rise again—not its empire, I am not a fool—but its *name*. Its memory. Its dead will have graves again. Its god will have a voice again. Your power, freely burned, becomes resurrection." She lowered her hands. "Or it dies with me, if you refuse, and the clause waits at the Door with its teeth open. Those are the shapes of it, Thorn."

* * *

They argued afterward. Of course they argued.

Wren first, sharp and fast: "You can't know she's telling the truth. Shielding rites, vessels, resurrection—we have one woman's word and a wall full of carvings we can't verify—"

"You can't verify," Ilyra corrected mildly, "because the archives that could were burned. Which is rather the point of burning archives."

"—and conveniently," Wren went on, relentless, "every part of her story ends with the power in *her* hands. Doesn't that strike anyone as tidy?"

"Tidy things are usually true," Bren said from his corner. "Messy things are usually lies. I've been lied to by professionals." But his eyes on Ilyra were flat and measuring, and she marked him too: the keeper distrusted her on instinct, the way dogs distrust certain houses.

And Cyrus—Cyrus stood apart, near the broken stairwell, looking up through the ruined roof at a sky the color of wet slate, and Ilyra watched them watch the gathering storm with an expression she recognized from mirrors. Homesickness. For weather. For a self.

She gave them space. She refilled tea. She knelt at the altar and murmured the dusk-office of the Ninefold Dawn in a language only the dead remembered, letting the sound of it work on them—the way a mother's voice works on a crying child who cannot understand the words but understands the tone. This, too, was technique. Everything was technique. She had made peace with that long ago; sainthood was just strategy with better lighting.

At length Cyrus spoke, without turning. "The toll. If I take your shielding—if I keep my magic until the Door—people will keep paying. Every storm. Strangers' years. Maybe people I—" A pause. Ilyra heard the name they didn't say, and smiled privately at it. *Wren. Already, then. Faster than expected.* "Maybe people I love."

"Yes," Ilyra said. "That is the cost of waiting."

"And if I burn it now—give it now—no one pays. Ever again."

"If you give it now, freely, in rite, the clause is satisfied early. The Door opens for you clean. Yes."

Cyrus turned. In the candlelight their face was terrible and young, twelve years of exile carved into features that should have been softer. "And you'd swear it. On your god. That the power goes to Ashkarin. That you won't—"

"On my god," Ilyra said, and placed her hand over her heart, and spoke the oldest oath-form her faith possessed, the one sworn on the Nine Names, the one that could not be spoken falsely by any true believer. She was a true believer. That was the elegance of it, the thing that would keep her sleepless only some nights and not all: she did not intend to lie. She intended to resurrect Ashkarin. What shape the rising took, what throne might stand at the center of a nation reborn—those were details for a future Ilyra, and future Ilyras had always proven wiser than present ones.

She watched Cyrus weigh it. Watched them glance at Wren—there, unmistakable, the seeking, the checking—and watched Wren look back with an expression Ilyra catalogued carefully: not encouragement. Fear. The archivist feared the offer, or feared losing Cyrus to it, and fear was a lever like any other.

"One condition," Cyrus said.

Ilyra kept her face still. "Name it."

"I think about it. Until the Door. You do the rite whenever I say—now, tomorrow, at the threshold—but it's my hand that chooses the hour. No pressure. No deadlines. If you push, the answer's no."

Everything in Ilyra wanted to seize, to insist, to perform urgency—*the hunters are closing, the ember gutters, there may be no Door-time left*—and she strangled all of it in the cradle. Urgency was the amateur's tool. Patience built cathedrals.

"Agreed," she said warmly. "Think as long as the road allows. The rite needs only an hour's preparation, wherever we are." She extended her hand, and after a pause Cyrus took it, and her grip was dry and strong and priestess-cool, and she held it one heartbeat longer than necessary because contact builds trust and trust builds compliance, and because—she admitted it to herself, in the small honest room at the back of her mind where she kept such things—because the storm under that skin hummed against her palm like a caged bird, and it was magnificent, and she wanted it the way she had never wanted anything in thirty years of ash and whispers.

* * *

They slept in the temple's side chapel, the three of them, wrapped in tarps among fallen saints. Ilyra sat vigil, as she always did, feeding the candles.

She listened to their breathing settle. She listened to Bren mutter in his sleep—a name, *Yssara*, tender as a prayer. She listened to Wren turn toward Cyrus in the dark, and Cyrus toward Wren, two bodies finding each other by gravity alone, and felt nothing cruel about it, only noted it, the way a navigator notes currents.

Then she went to the altar wall, to the carving beneath the ones she'd shown them—the deeper lines, the ones her body had blocked from view all evening, the ninth line of the rite that she had translated for herself alone, twenty years ago, kneeling in this exact spot with blood on her fingers from tracing it.

*What the vessel receives, the vessel becomes.*

She read it again, as she read it every night, and felt the old vertigo, the old exhilaration. The texts said the Thorn's fire would pass into the keeper. They did not say what a keeper became who received a storm-caller's whole soul-wild power, layered over a lifetime of hoarded rite-craft, in the shadow of a dying dragon's ember. They did not say, because no author had imagined it. Prophets wrote warnings for the obedient. Ilyra had never been obedient.

Outside, far across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring—the Guild's alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one. Torchlight moved along the tanner's quarter wall. Voices. Dogs.

They had found something. Or someone. The hunt was tightening, exactly as she had promised the Thorn it would, exactly as she had counted on—for fear drove sheep toward shepherds, and Cyrus Crane would come back to her now not as a customer considering an offer but as a drowning creature grasping at the only rope in sight.

Ilyra pinched out the nine candles one by one, leaving the last for the god, and stood in the dark listening to the bells, and began, softly, in the language of the erased, to practice the opening words of the rite.

Her shadow on the wall moved wrong while she spoke—too many arms, too much wingspan—but there was no one awake to see it, and by dawn it would be only a shadow again.
