## 20. Voss Ascendant

The Council chamber of the Inquisitor's Guild had not smelled of smoke in sixty years. By dawn it reeked of it.

Halvard Voss stood at the head of the long table where seven seats had stood empty for a week—three to plague, one to flight, and three because their occupants had made the mistake of hesitating when he asked them to sign the Emergency Mandate. Their names were already struck from the rolls. Their offices were being repainted. The Guild did not mourn; the Guild *corrected*.

He laid his hands flat on the table and let the silence do its work. Around him, the surviving councilors sat like birds in a cage with a cat. Good. Fear kept better order than loyalty ever had.

"Read it back," he said.

Inquisitor Dane, a gray woman with ink-stained fingers and no imagination whatsoever—the best kind of subordinate—unrolled the scroll. Her voice was dry as old vellum.

"*By authority vested in this Guild by the Concordat of Vell, in the hour of the city's peril, all civil authority is suspended. Curfew is declared from dusk bell to dawn bell. Gatherings of more than five persons are forbidden. The sale, barter, gifting, or possession of charms, wards, blessings, talismans, or any object bearing arcane inscription is hereby classified as heresy of the first order.*"

"And the clause I dictated last night," Voss said.

Dane's throat moved. "*The individual known as the Thorn, formerly Cyrus Crane of Ashkarin, is named author of the plagues afflicting Vell: the gray fever, the blight in the granaries, the failing tides, and the cold that will not lift. Any citizen providing shelter, aid, coin, or silence to this individual shares his guilt and his sentence.*"

"There," Voss said softly. "You see how simple history becomes when you write it early enough."

One of the younger councilors—a soft man named Ellery who had inherited his seat from a braver father—found his voice. "Inquisitor Voss. The fever predates Crane's arrival in Vell by two years. The granary blight was traced to rot, we have the reports—"

"You have reports," Voss agreed. "I have a city." He let that sit until Ellery's face went the color of porridge. "A city does not need truth, Councilor. It needs a shape for its fear. Give a mob nothing to hate and it will hate itself. Give it one face—" He touched the warrant on the table, the charcoal sketch of Cyrus Crane copied from a twelve-year-old exile record, the storm-scarred hands rendered in careful detail. "—and it will march for you through fire."

"The pyres," Dane said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Forty-one charm-sellers identified in the lower markets. Thirty-three arrested overnight." He had watched the arrests from the Guild steps, wrapped in his gray cloak, anonymous as frost. "Burn their stock in the squares. Burn the sellers if they resist. Let the smoke be visible from the harbor."

Ellery stood, actually stood, chair scraping. "This is not the Guild. This is—you've turned us into—"

"Sit down, Councilor."

"I will *not*—" 

Voss did not raise his voice. He simply looked at him, and Dane and two others rose and took Ellery's arms, and the young man was escorted out still protesting, his voice fading down the stairwell into the sound of drums—Voss had ordered drums beaten through every quarter since midnight, so the city would know something enormous was happening even before it knew what.

When the doors closed, Voss addressed the remaining four as if nothing had interrupted him.

"Burn notices go up within the hour. Every wall, every bridge, every temple door. The Thorn's face, the Thorn's name, and the price on both." He paused. "Two hundred crowns. Enough to make a mother weigh her son against her winter. Not enough to make her feel like a traitor for choosing."

* * *

By midmorning, Vell had begun to burn itself.

Voss watched from the balcony of the Guildhall as the first pyre took light in Sable Square—not a person, not yet; only confiscated charms, hundreds of them, luck-knots and fever-wards and love-blessings heaped into a tower and doused with lamp oil. The flames went up green and gold and strange, magic dying loudly, and the crowd made a sound he had heard before, once, twelve years ago, outside another city: a low collective moan that was half hunger and half horror, and entirely useful.

They had come out despite the curfew. They always came out for fires. And they had brought their children, because parents understood instinctively what preachers took years to teach—that a child shown an enemy young would keep that enemy for life.

An old woman near the front was weeping. Voss marked her. One of his cordon-inquisitors moved toward her, unhurried, and Voss lifted two fingers: *not yet*. Let her weep publicly. Grief observed became grief reported; grief reported became grief corrected. The machine worked best when it appeared to be merely weather.

"Inquisitor."

He turned. A runner, guild-gray, sweat-streaked, holding out a slate. Voss read it and felt the morning sharpen around him like a blade coming off a strop.

*Stall in Tanner's Row searched. Empty. Walls riddled with guild seals—old ones, months old. Seller fled. Neighbor reports a woman, dark-haired, archivist's ink on her fingers, seen entering twice.*

Wren Halloway. The archivist from the Hall of Records who had requested—three times, the fool, *three times*—restricted materials relating to Ashkarin. Who had then vanished from her desk the same week the Thorn surfaced in the night market.

Voss had spent twelve years learning patience in a city that rewarded it, climbing from the disgrace of the Crane trial back up through interrogations and quarantines and quiet disappearances, telling himself the climb was duty when he knew it was penance. He had lost everything to Cyrus Crane once—his standing, his certainty, the clean shape of a world with magic safely at its edges—and he had told himself for a decade that what he wanted was redemption.

He had been lying to himself, and he knew it now, and the knowing was a relief. What he wanted was the moment when Crane understood that the world had been rebuilt without him, brick by brick, year by year, and that no prophecy and no dragon-fire would buy him back into it.

"Find Halloway," he said. "Quietly. She's worth more unburned than burned—an archivist knows where other archives sleep." He handed back the slate. "And double the watch on the drowned quarter. If Crane has a friend below the waterline, that's where they'll meet."

* * *

At dusk bell the drums changed rhythm, and Voss went down to address the city.

He had chosen the Guild steps deliberately: high enough to see, low enough to seem reachable. Torches in ranks. Behind him, forty inquisitors in formation, faces shadowed under gray hoods. Before him, a crowd grown fat on fear and free spectacle—fishwives and clerks and dockhands, the desperate and the merely curious, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the cold.

He did not shout. He had learned long ago that a quiet voice on a loud street makes people lean in, and leaning in is the first act of obedience.

"Citizens of Vell." His voice carried the way cold carries. "For twelve years, your children have sickened while the temples sold you ribbons. Your grain has rotted while the merchants blamed the rain. Your river has forgotten how to rise while your priests blamed the moon." He let the murmur build, then cut it with one raised hand. "You were told these were accidents. They are not accidents. They are *taxes*—levied upon you by a creature who spends your years like coins."

He held up the warrant, and men in the front rows passed the sketch backward hand to hand, and Voss watched the face travel through the crowd like a spark through dry grass. Scarred hands. Storm-pale eyes. A face built to be remembered and cursed.

"This is the Thorn. Once named Cyrus Crane. Exiled from this country for heresy twelve years ago, and returned—*returned*, in secret, in hiding, wearing the mask of a common peddler—to finish what he began." He lowered the parchment slowly, letting every eye follow it down. "Every fever that took a child. Every field that failed. Every hearth that would not stay lit. His work. His *harvest*. You are not dying of misfortune, citizens. You are being eaten."

Someone screamed it before he could: *"Burn him!"* And then the crowd said it, dozens of voices, then hundreds, the words rising off the stones like heat off a roof, and Voss stood in the middle of it with his hands folded and his face grave and grieving, and thought: *there it is. There is my army. I didn't recruit a single one of them.*

He raised his hand again and the roar guttered, obedient.

"Magic taught this city to fear its own shadow. Magic made you inform on your neighbors and distrust your own dreams. So hear the law of this hour, and hear it plainly." He stepped to the edge of the steps, close enough that the torchlight found the seams of his face. "From tonight, there are two kinds of people in Vell: those who stand with the Guild against the Thorn, and those who are his kindling. Report a charm-seller, earn bread. Shelter the Thorn, share his fire. There is no third road. There is no neutrality. Neutrality is how he has hidden among us for twelve years."

Behind him, the great seal of the Guild—hammer and eye—was unveiled on the Guildhall facade, freshly gilded, and the crowd gasped at it as though the building itself had spoken.

Voss let them look. Then, quietly, almost tenderly: "Go home. Bolt your doors. And when you hear someone whispering in the dark—when you smell ozone before a storm that isn't coming—remember that you know his face now. Remember that you know *yourselves*. That is the difference between you and him. He was given a choice twelve years ago, and he chose himself." His voice dropped to almost nothing, and ten thousand people leaned in to catch it. "Tonight, you choose too."

* * *

The reports came in through the small hours, and Voss read them alone in his office with the shutters open to the smell of smoke.

Thirty-one pyres lit across six quarters. Two charm-sellers dead resisting arrest; nine in cells awaiting trial that would take exactly as long as he wished it to. Four hundred citizens had come forward voluntarily with denunciations—neighbors, landlords, a wife against her husband, two brothers against each other over an inheritance and a luck-charm found beneath a floorboard. He read each name without flinching. This was not cruelty. Cruelty was waste. This was architecture: every denunciation poured another stone into the wall between the citizen and the past, until going back became impossible and loyalty to the Guild became the only country left to them.

It was nearly the dawn bell when Dane knocked and entered carrying a single sheet, and Voss knew from the set of her shoulders that the night had finally given him something worth the burning.

"A sighting," she said. "Salt-gate watch. A party of four leaving the city before curfew—two days ago, before the lockdown, which is why it surfaced late. A big man, keeper's brands on his forearms, drunk or pretending it. A woman matching Halloway. An older woman, priestess's hands." She paused. "And a fourth description that stopped the gate-sergeant's breath. Scarred hands, Inquisitor. Storm-colored eyes. Traveling under the name *Crane*—he gave it to the toll-keeper without flinching, paid double, and walked out the salt road toward the flats."

Voss sat very still.

He had imagined this moment for twelve years in a dozen shapes—in a courtroom, in a cell, across an executioner's block—and in none of his imaginings had Crane simply *walked out the gate*, calm as a merchant, paying his toll. It should have angered him. Instead he felt something colder and cleaner settle over him, the feeling of a lock finding its hasp.

He's running to the Door. To Ashkarin. To the ember and the dragon and the prophecy, all the threads Voss had spent months pulling loose from the Guild's oldest sealed records while the Council slept. Crane believed he could outrun the story written about him. He still believed stories were things you escaped rather than things you *finished*.

"He'll come back," Voss said. "Or he'll be stopped at the border, and he'll come back in chains. Either way, he returns to Vell, because everything he wants is underneath it. Post riders along the salt road. Triple bounty. And send word to the border garrisons—if he crosses back into Vell territory, I want him taken alive and delivered to me personally. No one else lays hands on him. No one else touches that brand on his wrist." He rose, and the candleflames leaned away from him as if from a draft, though the shutters were shut. "He made me wait twelve years, Dane. I intend to make the waiting worth it—for both of us."

Dane bowed and withdrew. Voss stood at the window and watched the city smolder in a dozen places, orange points scattered across the dark like a constellation someone had dragged down to earth, and beyond the walls, far off toward the salt flats, a faint flicker that might have been lightning where no clouds hung.

He smiled without warmth.

"Run, Thorn," he murmured to the horizon. "Every road bends back to me."

But far out on the flats, in a way station he would learn of only later, four travelers were already moving—and among them, a priestess with priestess's hands was smiling too, and hers, Voss would come to understand, was the more dangerous smile by far.
