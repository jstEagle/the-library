## 22. Sefa at the Threshold

The rain had followed them down from the salt flats, and Sefa had followed the rain.

She told herself it was procedure. The Thorn's trail ran through the drowned quarter like a vein of silver through slate—burned stalls, a dead hunter's report, a witness who swore he'd seen lightning walk on two legs—and the Council had ordered her to run it to ground. That was the truth she wore like her guild badge: *I am doing my duty. I am hunting a fugitive.*

But she had taken the tunnel alone. She had dismissed the two novices assigned to her without explanation. And now she stood in a doorway across from the ferryman's wharf where Cyrus Crane was buying passage with coins that weren't quite the right weight, and her hand was nowhere near her sword.

Twelve years, and he still bought bread before anything else. He still haggled the way he used to haggle with the fruit-sellers in their village—smiling, apologetic, relentless—so that by the end the seller felt he'd won something. She watched him tuck the loaf into his coat like a secret and felt twelve years collapse into a single breath.

*Arrest him,* said the voice of the Guild, the voice of Voss, the voice of everything she'd built out of the wreckage of that day at the sea-cliffs. *You are an Inquisitor of the Third Rank. This is what you are for.*

But the Guild had also taught her to read a scene, and the scene read this way: Cyrus Crane, the most wanted heretic in Vell, standing alone in the rain with a loaf of bread and no sword and shoulders curved inward like a man trying to take up less space in the world.

She crossed the street before she could stop being brave about it.

* * *

He saw her coming. Of course he did; storm-callers felt weather, and she had always been a storm to him—the kind you could see building for miles. She watched his face do three things in the space of a heartbeat: fear, recognition, and then something worse than either. Hope, briefly, before he strangled it.

"Sefa." He didn't run. He set his back against the wharf post and looked at her the way people look at floodwater rising past the third stair. "You're alone."

"I am." Her voice came out steadier than her hands felt. She was glad for the rain; it gave her an excuse for the wetness on her face that wasn't yet tears. "Cyrus. I need you to listen to me, and I need you to not make it difficult."

"That depends entirely on what you say next."

"Run." The word tore loose before she'd planned its shape. "Not tonight. Now. There are hunters on the north road already, and Voss has doubled the watch on every gate, and—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "There is a coal barge leaving on the morning tide. The captain owes me. It goes downriver to Marrowgate, and from Marrowgate you can be anyone you want to be. A fisherman. A dead man. Anything but the Thorn."

Cyrus stared at her. Rain dripped off the brim of his hood between them like a slow metronome counting out seconds neither of them could afford.

"You came here," he said slowly, "to let me go."

"I came here because if I don't arrest you, someone else will kill you, and I have spent twelve years learning exactly how little the difference between those things means to the Guild." Her hand finally went to her sword—not to draw it, but because she needed something solid to hold. "Voss wants your head on a pike above the execution square. He's built a whole theology around it. Every plague since your exile, every bad harvest, every child's fever—he's named you the cause of all of it, and half the city believes him because believing is easier than being afraid."

"And the other half?"

"The other half remembers the storms." She hadn't meant to say that. It slipped out the way old grief slips out, sideways, when you're not looking. "They remember that once, a long time ago, someone called the lightning away from the fields instead of into them."

* * *

The wharf creaked beneath them. Downriver, the coal barge sat low and black against the grey water, its lanterns blurred by rain. Somewhere behind them the city bells rang the hour—seven strokes, muffled by wet air—and Cyrus counted them under his breath the way he used to count thunder, measuring the distance of danger by sound.

"Why?" he asked at last. "Why now? You've had my trail for weeks. Wren told me—I know about the archive, I know you found the pages she copied, I know you've known where we were sleeping since the salt flats. You could have brought thirty hunters down on us any night you chose." His eyes were fierce and wet and unbearably familiar. "So why are you standing here offering me a coal barge?"

Because I lie awake at night replaying a single afternoon twelve years ago, she thought. Because I chose duty over you and called it love of country, and then I watched what my country did with the choice. Because they made me hold the torch at your exile ceremony, and I held it, and the brand on your wrist is the shape of my whole life since.

What she said was: "Do you remember the cliffs?"

His face changed. "Sefa—"

"Do you remember the cliffs," she repeated, "or have you managed to forget them too?"

"No," he said quietly. "I haven't forgotten anything. That's rather the problem."

* * *

She had rehearsed this speech for twelve years without knowing it. It lived in her like a stone swallowed whole, and now, standing in the rain with the man she'd lost, she felt it begin to dissolve and cut her on the way up.

"They put me in the front row," she said. "At your trial. Did you know that? They seated me in the front row so everyone could see that even the girl you saved—the girl whose family you pulled out of the flood—had turned on you. It wasn't testimony they wanted from me. It was a picture."

"I remember," Cyrus said. His voice was very soft.

"And I gave it to them. I stood up and I said—" Her breath caught. Twelve years, and the words still tasted of ash. "I said, *this man is dangerous.* I said it looking right at you. And you just... nodded. Like you understood. Like you were letting me do it. Do you understand what that did to me? That you let me?"

"What would you have had me do?" He spread his hands, and there—the left wrist, the scar tissue over the exile-brand, pale even in the rain. "Deny it? The storm was mine, Sefa. I called it. Forty-one people lived because I did. The Inquisitors asked me to apologize for saving them, and I wouldn't, so they took everything else. If I'd dragged your name into it—if I'd told them you'd begged me to try, that you'd knelt in the mud and begged me—they'd have burned you beside me." He shook his head. "I let you turn on me because it was the only door out of that square that had you on the other side of it."

"And look what I did with the door." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "I walked through it straight into the Guild. I told myself I'd change it from inside. Reform it. Slowly. Carefully. Twelve years, Cyrus. Do you know how many charm-sellers I've arrested in twelve years? How many hedge-witches, how many children with sparks in their fingers who never hurt anyone? I told myself each one was mercy, because I arrested them instead of letting Voss burn them. I measured mercy in what didn't happen." She pressed her fist against her mouth. "And every single time, some small part of me was standing in that square again, watching you nod."

The rain thickened. Out on the river, the barge lanterns swayed.

"You could have come with me," Cyrus said.

"There was no *with*. They exiled you to the wastes beyond the border. There was no coming with."

"There was before that." His voice cracked open. "Before the storm. Before any of it. We had four years, Sefa. Four years of—you used to fall asleep reading with the candle still lit, and I'd blow it out and you'd wake up furious. You were going to teach me your mother's songs. You drew a map of a house you wanted, with a garden, and you put it under your pillow, and I—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "I found it after you left. I kept it. Through the exile, through everything, I kept that stupid map, and I don't know why I'm telling you this except that you asked me why I'm not running, and the answer is that I spent twelve years running from the thing I actually wanted and it looked exactly like you."

The world narrowed to the space between them, which was three feet of rain and twelve years wide.

* * *

She should arrest him.

The thought arrived on schedule, dutiful as a bell. *Shoulder, wrist, irons, done.* She even moved—one step, half a gesture—and Cyrus didn't flinch, which was somehow worse than flinching. He just stood there with his loaf of bread and his ruined face and waited to see what she'd become.

Her hand fell away from the sword.

"I can't," she said. "I can't arrest you and I can't explain why, and both of those things mean I'm finished. Do you understand? When they find out I stood here—when Voss finds out—he won't interrogate me, he'll *use* me. He'll parade my treason next to your heresy and call it proof that the Thorn corrupts everything he touches. He'll call it evidence that I was compromised twelve years ago, that everything I've done since was a traitor's long game. My whole career, my whole life, will become his argument." She was shaking now, and she let herself shake, because there was no one left to perform steadiness for. "I am standing in the rain ruining my life, Cyrus Crane, and I need you to be worth it."

"I'm not," he said immediately. "Honestly. I'm a charm-seller with a bounty on his head and a prophecy problem and friends who keep lying to me. Run the other direction. Arrest me. I'll even come quietly."

A laugh escaped her, sudden and real and half a sob. "You're still doing that. Turning yourself into the punchline so no one has to look at the wound."

"It works better than crying."

"Does it?"

He considered. "No."

* * *

They stood together under the wharf's broken awning while the rain hammered the river flat, and she told him the rest—the parts she'd sworn she'd never tell anyone.

That she'd requested assignment to his case eleven times and been denied ten. That the eleventh time, Voss had granted it personally, smiling, and she'd understood only later that he wanted her precisely because of the history—that a broken heart was, in Voss's arithmetic, a lever. That three months ago she'd found the archived testimony of a charm-seller named Odric Fenn, the man whose lies had sealed the verdict, and discovered Fenn had recanted everything in a private letter to the Guild archives—a letter marked *sealed by order of H. Voss*, dated nine years ago. Voss had known. For nine years the Guild had known the conviction was built on perjury, and they'd buried it, because the Thorn was more useful guilty.

Cyrus received this the way he seemed to receive all catastrophes lately: with a long exhale and a look like a man reading his own obituary and finding it inaccurate but familiar.

"So it was never justice," he said. "It was inventory. I was a problem they stored."

"You were a *story* they stored." She stepped closer, close enough to see the storm-light flickering faintly under his skin when he was frightened—she'd forgotten that, or maybe she'd never been close enough at the end to see it. "And stories can be rewritten. That's why you have to run, Cyrus. Not just from the hunters. From the ending Voss wrote for you. As long as you die on his pyre, his story holds. Every lie holds. But if you live—if you get downriver, if you disappear so completely that even the Guild's story loses the thread—then someday the truth comes up for air, and there's nothing he can burn to stop it."

"And if I don't want to disappear?" Quietly. Dangerously. "If there are things I have to finish here?"

"Then you'll die," she said simply. "And I will have ruined myself for a ghost, and Voss wins twice."

* * *

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him weigh it—she could almost see the scales, the same scales she'd watched him weigh forty lives against his own freedom on a cliffside twelve years ago. He had always been terrible at this weighing. He always tipped it toward everyone else.

Then his gaze went past her shoulder, toward the dark mouth of the street behind the wharf, and his expression shifted into something alert and alarmed.

"Sefa," he said. "How many novices did you dismiss?"

"Two. Why?"

"Because there's a third set of footsteps on that cobbles, and they've been there since before you arrived." His voice dropped. "Guild boots. Heavy heels. They stopped moving about a minute ago—which means whoever it is isn't following you. They're waiting to see what you do."

Cold slid down her spine, colder than the rain. *Voss doesn't trust me. Voss has never trusted me. The eleventh assignment wasn't a gift—it was a test, and I am failing it in front of a witness.*

"Cyrus." She turned back to him and took his face in both her cold hands, the way she had once, a lifetime ago, under a different sky. "Listen to me. The barge. Morning tide. Captain Orla, tell her the tide-priestess sends her regards. Go now, go through the fish-gate, don't go back for anything or anyone—"

"I can't leave them."

"*Cyrus.*"

"I know." His eyes closed briefly under her hands. When they opened, the hope she'd seen strangled earlier had come back to life, stubborn as a weed through pavement. "Sefa. Thank you. For the map—I mean, for—everything. Whatever happens after tonight—"

Footsteps on cobbles, closing fast. Not one set anymore. Three.

She shoved him hard toward the fish-gate and drew her sword at last, turning to face the street, rain streaming down the blade, her heart hammering out the oldest rhythm she knew: *not him, not him, not him.*

Three figures in grey cloaks resolved out of the rain. Not guild livery. Grey masks, grey gloves, and on the breast of the foremost, a seal she recognized from a burned market stall—the crest of men who paid better than the Guild and asked worse questions.

Nobility. Or what passed for it these days.

"You're a long way from your jurisdiction, Inquisitor," said the foremost figure pleasantly.

"And you're a long way from anywhere at all," Sefa answered, setting her feet. Behind her, she heard the fish-gate bang shut, and allowed herself one breath of relief before the night came apart.
