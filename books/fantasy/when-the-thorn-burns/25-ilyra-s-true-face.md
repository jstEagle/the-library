## 25. Ilyra's True Face

Ilyra had worn many faces in her long descent from priestess to traitor, and the face she wore now—humble, weary, devoted—was the finest of them.

She knelt at the edge of the flooded temple's inner pool while Cyrus and Wren argued over the map of Ashkarin, their voices low and urgent, their candlelight shivering on black water. Bren sat apart, cradling the iron casket that held the ember-heart like a man cradling a sleeping child he did not trust himself not to smother. The dragon's coal pulsed faintly through the seams of the box, a heartbeat out of rhythm with any living thing, and Ilyra felt it against her skin the way a drowning woman feels the surface: distant, precious, impossibly far away.

*Soon*, she thought. *Soon you will be close enough to hold.*

"Here," Wren was saying, tapping the map where Vell's catacombs branched beneath the old merchant quarter. "The Door has to be under the Guildhall itself. That's why they built it there. They didn't build it there—they *built on top of it*. You don't put your seat of power anywhere else when you know what's underneath."

"You're guessing," Cyrus said. His voice was rough with exhaustion; the storm-magic sat in him like swallowed lightning these days, and Ilyra could hear the crackle of it whenever he spoke too fast. She had spent three weeks listening for that crackle. Three weeks of prayers she did not mean, of warnings she delivered half-true, of watching the Thorn fumble toward his own salvation with all the grace of a man walking backward into a fire.

"I'm guessing with citations," Wren said, and even now, even here, Cyrus almost smiled.

Ilyra lowered her head so they would not see her expression. It was not contempt. Contempt would have been simpler. What she felt watching them was something closer to grief—the grief of a woman who had once believed love was a sufficient architecture for a life, before she learned that love was a door that closed from the outside.

She had loved Ashkarin the way these two loved each other. And Ashkarin was gone. Not fallen. *Gone.* Unwritten, unremembered, unmade by cowards who could not bear to mourn what they had failed to defend. Every name in her country had been scraped off every tongue. Her own mother had looked at her, in those last terrible months before the forgetting completed itself, with the polite blankness one gives a stranger asking directions.

That was the price of losing. That was what the world did to the faithful: it erased them politely, and then it went on selling luck-charms in night markets as though nothing had ever burned.

Well. Ilyra had found something the erasers had missed. Something that remembered.

She let her hand drift, casually, to the water at her knee—and beneath its surface, where no one watched, she traced the old signs. The ash-script. The language only Cyrus could read, because the storm-gift was also a key—but keys can be copied, and Ilyra had spent thirty years copying.

The water answered. Faintly. A warmth rising through the drowned stone, like a pulse in a wrist.

*I am here*, she said to the thing below. *I have brought you the Thorn.*

* * *

She had made her bargain with the cabal nine days ago, in a counting-house that smelled of ink and fear, before four nobles whose names history would soon forget anyway. Lord Casserin had done most of the talking—a soft-bellied man with rings on every finger and the particular hunger of the mediocre powerful, men who had inherited everything except greatness and could not forgive the world for it.

"The ember," he'd said, "will fuel the Ascension. All of us. Fire enough to remake ourselves." He'd smiled at her then, condescending, the smile of a man who thinks he is using a priestess. "You'll have your temple restored, of course. A shrine. Whatever you like."

Ilyra had bowed her head and said, *Whatever Ashkarin requires of me,* and inside she had been doing arithmetic.

Four nobles. Four greedy mouths, each certain they alone understood the arrangement, each planning to betray the others the moment the fire touched their lips. Men who wanted to *consume* the last dragon's heart and call it ascension—as if divinity were a vintage to be drunk rather than a debt to be paid.

They were fools, but they were useful fools, and fools with soldiers. Let them steal the ember from Bren. Let them carry it down to the Door. Let them open the way with their greed what no amount of courage could have opened. And then, when the moment came—when the ember was in the open air between the Door and their grasping hands—Ilyra would take it, and she would not share.

Because she alone understood what the fire actually was.

She had read the true liturgy, the one the Ashkarin priesthood had guarded for a thousand years and died protecting in the fall. The nobles thought the ember was fuel. Cyrus feared it was a life to save. Even Bren, poor hollow Bren, thought he was keeping a pet flame alive out of guilt. But the fire of Yssara was none of those things alone. It was a *covenant*—a living contract between a people and the sky, renewed each generation in blood freely given, in names freely spoken, in memory freely kept. The dragon-fire did not burn because dragons were strong. It burned because someone, somewhere, *remembered*, and fed the remembering into flame.

Ashkarin had been erased. The covenant had been severed mid-word. And so the fire guttered—not because Yssara was dying, but because she was *starving*, starving for a nation that no longer existed to feed her.

But a covenant can be re-signed by anyone willing to pay the full price.

Ilyra was willing.

She had already begun paying. In the dark hours of the last three weeks, while the others slept, she had been feeding herself to the ritual—drops of memory, drops of years, small pieces of the woman she had been, laid on the altar of preparation like coins counted out in advance. She no longer dreamed of her mother's garden. She had sold that dream two nights ago. She could no longer quite remember the color of the robes she'd worn at her ordination, and she found she did not miss them. This was the mercy of the work: every piece you surrendered made the next surrender lighter.

By the time she took the fire, there would be very little of Ilyra left to object. Only the purpose, purified. Only Ashkarin, resurrected—in her flesh, through her flesh, *as* her flesh. A god-queen born of an erased country's final prayer.

The others called it madness when she described it, later, in the words she chose. She preferred *completion*.

* * *

The opportunity came sooner than she'd planned, and from a direction she had not expected.

They surfaced from the catacombs near dawn, into a ruined tannery above the drowned district, and found the street already crawling. Guild lanterns. Cabal soldiers in plain clothes that fooled no one. And at the mouth of the alley, waiting with his hands folded like a patient schoolmaster, Lord Casserin himself.

"There she is," Casserin said warmly, to his soldiers rather than to her. "Our priestess."

Wren turned to Ilyra slowly. Cyrus's hands came up, and Ilyra heard the sky tense above the clouds—he was gathering, even now, even exhausted, the storm answering him like a hound hearing its name.

"No," Ilyra said quietly. "Don't. Please. Not here."

"You—" Wren started.

"I know." Ilyra stepped forward, away from them, and let her shoulders curve into the shape of surrender. It cost her nothing anymore; she had practiced surrender until it fit her like her old vestments. "I have been theirs since before I met you. Since before the temple. I led you to the map, Cyrus. I led you to the Door. Everything you've found since the salt flats, you found because I placed it in front of you." She paused, and gave them the truth as a parting gift, because even betrayal deserves craft. "The prophecy needed the Thorn at the threshold. So I made sure you walked there."

Bren made a sound low in his chest, half growl, half sob, and clutched the casket tighter.

"Give them the ember, keeper," Casserin said, bored now. "Or we take it, and take your hands as a receipt."

What happened next, Ilyra had not planned, and it taught her something about the limits of her own arithmetic.

Cyrus moved first. Not toward the soldiers—toward *her*. He crossed the space between them in three strides, and the storm came with him, wind screaming down the alley hard enough to flatten the lanterns, rain starting out of a sky that had been clear all night. Lightning stood in his eyes. He grabbed the front of her robe and pulled her close, and the thunder rolled through his voice:

"Why?" he demanded. "We trusted you. Wren *trusted* you. After everything—after the cabal, after the pyres Voss is building—you want to hand the fire to *these men*?"

And there it was again, that terrible grief, because he genuinely did not understand, and he was kind, and kindness deserved an honest answer even from a liar.

"Not to them," Ilyra said. "Through them. There is a difference, Thorn, and it is the whole difference. Look at them." She gestured at the cabal's ranks—at Casserin checking his rings, at the soldiers shifting, at the hunger barely dressed as discipline. "These are the men who erased my country's neighbors and called it peace. These are the heirs of the ones who burned Ashkarin and then burned the *memory* of Ashkarin, because they could not abide that anything had existed which they did not own. Do you think I give them a god?" She laughed, and heard how the laugh had changed in recent weeks—deeper, resonant, with something underneath it that was not entirely her voice. "I give them rope. I let them carry the fire to the Door with their soft ringed hands, and at the threshold I take back what was never theirs, and I pay the price they cannot even see, and Ashkarin rises. Not on maps. In *flesh*. In fire. In me."

"You'll destroy yourself," Wren said. She had tears on her face, furious ones. "Ilyra. Whatever you lost—we can rebuild it. Names can come back. You told us the Door restores what was erased—"

"The Door restores what passes *through* it," Ilyra said gently. "It does not restore what was forgotten. Memory is not a door, child. It is a fire. And fires need keepers who are willing to burn." She reached out—she could not help it—and touched Wren's wet cheek, one brief benediction. "You will make a fine keeper someday. Better than Bren. Kinder than me. Remember that I said so, when you tell this story."

Then she raised her voice, and the soldiers moved.

* * *

She had expected Cyrus to fight. She had prepared for the storm—for the toll it would take, for the stranger in some nearby street who would collapse gray and aged while the Thorn bought his escape with borrowed years. She had even, in a corner of herself she did not examine, prepared to grieve him. The prophecy demanded the Thorn burn; perhaps fate would manage it here, messily, and spare her the final act.

She had not expected him to *refuse*.

He stood in the rain he had called, lightning coiled around his fists, and he looked at the soldiers—and then past them, at the tenement windows where faces had begun to gather, drawn by thunder at dawn. Families. Children. People who had done nothing but live in the wrong city during the wrong century.

And he turned the storm *upward*. Away from the soldiers, away from the street, into the empty sky, discharging it harmlessly into the clouds with a sound like the world exhaling. Buying nothing. Saving no one but the bystanders—from him.

"Take the ember if you must," Cyrus said hoarsely, lowering his arms. "But no one dies for it. Not today. Not for me."

Ilyra stared at him, and felt, for one vertiginous instant, the ground of her certainty shift. *This* was the Thorn? This exhausted, self-lacerating man who would not spend a single stranger's year even to save himself? The prophecies had pictured a pillar of fire. The book had promised ruin or salvation on a scale of nations. And here he was, negotiating for the lives of dockworkers.

For a moment—one breath, no more—she wondered if she had misread everything. If the covenant could be renewed not by conquest but by *this*: a man refusing the arithmetic she had built her soul upon.

Then Casserin's soldiers wrenched the casket from Bren's arms, and Bren went down under three of them with a broken sound, and the moment passed, and the arithmetic held. Sentiment was the luxury of people with countries. She had none.

"The Door," she said to Casserin. "Tonight. Before the Guild learns what you carry."

"And the Thorn?"

Ilyra looked at Cyrus—at the rain drying on his scarred hands, the hands the prophecy had drawn in ink older than grief.

"Bring him," she heard herself say. "The Door will want its key."

* * *

They marched them through the waking city—Cyrus and Wren bound with guild-iron that dampened nothing but dignity, Bren limping between soldiers with his hands still curled around the shape of a casket that was no longer there. Ilyra rode in the cabal's carriage, and through the window she watched Vell burn in small ceremonial ways: pyres in the squares, Voss's seals nailed to doors, the city devouring itself in fear while its rulers called it purification.

*A fitting funeral*, she thought. *For the world that forgot my mother's name.*

In the floor of the carriage, beneath a loose plank, lay the thing she had taken from the temple before the soldiers came: a single page, ash-script, torn from the true liturgy. The Rite of Consummation. On it, in her own careful hand, she had amended the ancient words—struck the clause requiring a congregation, struck the clause requiring consent of the flame, added instead the name of the one who would sign the covenant alone.

Her hand had shaken writing it. Not from fear. From eagerness. That was the detail she would remember afterward, if any of her remained to remember: that her hand had shaken with joy.

Below the city, deeper than the catacombs, deeper than the drowned temple, the Door waited behind stone that only a storm-caller could open. And beyond it—she could feel it already, a warmth rising through the carriage floor like summer through soil—beyond it, Yssara's stolen heart beat in its traveling cage, starving, magnificent, almost hers.

*Hold on*, Ilyra whispered to the fire, to the nation inside the fire, to the mother whose face she had sold. *I am coming. I am bringing everything.*

Behind her, in the wagon with the prisoners, she heard Wren say something low to Cyrus, and Cyrus answer—too quiet for her to catch. Planning, probably. Hoping, certainly. Those two never learned; hope was the wound that never closed in them.

Let them hope. Hope would carry them to the threshold, and the threshold was where she needed them. The prophecy had one line left, and everyone had read it wrong, and Ilyra—alone of all the readers, alone of all the believers—understood that the final word did not belong to the Thorn at all.

It belonged to whoever stood closest to the fire when it guttered.

And no one, she vowed as the carriage descended into the dark, would ever stand closer than she would.
