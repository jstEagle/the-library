## 26. Guttering

The frost came to Vell in the last week of harvest, and it came wrong.

Cyrus knew frost. They had grown up with it in the high valleys of Ashkarin—before Ashkarin was anything but a word that slipped out of the mind like water through cupped hands—frost that laced the windowpanes in feathers, that crunched underfoot, that made breath a visible prayer. That frost had been *seasonal*. It had arrived on schedule and departed with an apology.

This frost arrived in the middle of the afternoon, in the middle of a market square, while children were still selling roasted chestnuts from iron pans. It came down out of a clear sky, silvered every awning and fruit cart in a single breath, and killed the chestnuts' fire as if someone had pinched out a candle between two fingers.

The vendors didn't shout about witchcraft this time. They didn't blame Cyrus or any of the other charm-sellers who still dared to work Vell's edges. They just stood there, staring at their dead coals, at their breath hanging white in air that should have been warm, and something in their faces had gone past fear into something quieter and much worse.

*Confusion*, Cyrus thought. The city had forgotten what cold out of season meant. Had forgotten that weather was supposed to keep its appointments.

They pulled their collar up and walked home through streets where the puddles had frozen in mid-ripple, little sculptures of water caught mid-fleece, and they thought: *She's guttering.*

Yssara's fire was dying. Everyone could feel it now. Nobody could say so.

* * *

The house Wren had found them—a rented room above a dyer's shop that smelled perpetually of mordant and wet wool—had become a war council's headquarters disguised as a squalid love nest. Bren sat by the window with the coal-casket open on his knees, doing what he did every hour of every day now: watching the ember breathe.

It breathed less each time.

"Three seconds," he said without looking up when Cyrus came in. "Last week she held a flare for eleven."

The ember sat in its bed of ash like a dying man's pulse-point, a red coal no bigger than a walnut, pulsing slow and dim. When Cyrus first met Bren, the thing had thrown light enough to read prophecy pages by. Now you had to cup your hands around it like a beggar sheltering a match.

"We need to move faster," Cyrus said.

"We're moving as fast as we can." Wren looked up from her papers—prophecy transcriptions, Ilyra's riddles, maps of drowned temples sketched from memory and half-memory both. There were shadows under her eyes deep enough to lose coins in. "The Door opens when the fire gutters out. Not before. We've been over this."

"Then we find a way to make 'not before' come sooner."

"And if we're wrong?" Bren's voice cracked across the room like a whip. He hadn't touched his bottle in three days—that was how you knew he was terrified. A drinking Bren was resigned; a sober one was desperate. "If the Door needs her *fully* dead? If opening it early kills her outright instead of just—" he gestured at the ember, at everything, "—this?"

"Then we've traded one apocalypse for another."

"Or we've done the cabal's work for them." He closed the casket gently, the way you'd close a coffin. "Ilyra's people are still out there. Voss is still out there. Every soul in this city who wants a piece of that fire knows exactly where the ember sleeps now, because *we told them*, and you want to gamble her last hours on a door we haven't even found yet?"

Cyrus had no answer for that. They crossed the room instead and sat beside Wren, and after a moment she leaned into them, her shoulder fitting into the hollow of their shoulder like a key into a lock they'd only recently discovered they had.

Outside the window, the light was failing. Not evening-light. Something else. The sun hung low and orange over Vell's rooftops at what should have been mid-morning, dim as a lamp behind smoked glass, and it had been doing that for six days.

"The tides stuttered again," Cyrus said quietly. "Ferryman at Grayharbor told me the water went out past the channel markers yesterday and just... stayed there. Two hours. Fishermen wading in mud, picking up fish like apples off an orchard floor."

"That's not possible," Wren said, but she said it the way scholars do, cataloguing rather than denying.

"No," Cyrus agreed. "Neither is frost at noon. Neither is the sun going out like a bad lamp." They turned the thought over, examining its ugly underside. "You know what I think it is? I think warmth isn't a thing the world does anymore. I think it's a thing *she* did, all this time, and nobody ever noticed, the way you don't notice your own heartbeat until it skips."

Wren was quiet a long moment. "A world-sized habit," she said finally. "And the habit is breaking."

"Bren said it once, back at his stall. Dragons don't guard the fire. They *are* the fire. Everything else—the crops, the tides, the blood in your veins keeping you at the right temperature—all of it is downstream." Cyrus watched the gray light crawl across the wall. "We've been living inside her exhale our whole lives. And she's running out of breath."

* * *

They went down to the market anyway, because hunger didn't care about apocalypse.

Grain prices had tripled in a fortnight. The granaries of the noble quarter were sealed behind guild locks, guarded by men with Voss's new armbands, and the rumor running through the breadlines was that the Inquisitor himself had declared the cold a punishment sent by heaven for the Thorn's presence in Vell. Cyrus heard their own name spat in three languages before noon. A woman clutched a scrawled sigil against evil and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes.

But it wasn't the hatred that unsettled Cyrus most. Hatred they understood; they'd been hated for twelve years and had learned its dialects fluently.

What unsettled them was the *forgetting*.

At the flower-seller's corner, where an old woman had sold marigolds for as long as Cyrus had known the market, the stall stood empty—not abandoned, but *emptied*, the boards scrubbed, the awning folded away, as if no one had ever sold flowers there at all. And when Cyrus asked the pie-man next door about it, the man frowned like Cyrus had asked him to describe a color from a dream.

"Flowers? There's never been a flower-stall here, friend. Cold's too hard on blooms. Nobody'd be fool enough."

"There was. She wore a blue shawl. She—"

"Friend." The pie-man's voice dropped, kind and uneasy. "Nobody sells flowers in Vell. Never have."

Cyrus walked away with their skin crawling. By evening they'd counted four other absences: a cobbler's stall, a shrine niche whose god had no name on anyone's tongue anymore, a street child who used to sleep in the doorway of the chandlery and whom no one now remembered seeing, a song the fishwives used to sing whose melody half the market could hum and none could name.

Things weren't just dying. Things were being *unwritten*. The way Ashkarin had been unwritten. The erasure that had swallowed a whole country was no longer content with history—it had begun eating the present, one small warm thing at a time, working outward from the guttering heart of the world like frost spreading through a windowpane.

That night, sitting up with Wren while Bren slept fitfully beside the coal-casket, Cyrus said it aloud.

"It's not waiting for her to die. The forgetting. It's already started. Her death is just going to be the part everyone notices."

Wren turned the candle low. In the stub of light her face looked carved from something older than her years. "Then whatever we're going to do," she said, "we do it soon. Before there's nothing left to save."

"Ilyra said the Door opens when the last dragon-fire gutters out. But Ilyra also wanted us to believe the prophecy demanded my burning, and she was lying then too. What if the Door doesn't need death, Wren? What if it needs *darkness*—a night so complete that a door can be seen? What if we don't have to wait for the fire to die, only to fail?"

Wren stared at them. "Where did that come from?"

"The catacomb walls. The language only I can read." Cyrus flexed their scarred hands, feeling the old storm-burns ache in the cold—which they always did now, more sharply than the rest of them felt it, as if the storm in their blood recognized its kin in Yssara's failing flame. "There's a line I couldn't parse. I think it says *when the light forgets itself, the way opens.* I thought it was poetry. I'm starting to think it was instructions."

* * *

Two nights later, the crops died.

Not failed—died. All at once, in the space of a single dark, the winter wheat in the fields beyond the walls blackened in the ground, every stalk from root to tip, as though a great invisible hand had passed over the countryside and closed it. Refugees began arriving at Vell's gates by the hundreds, then thousands, carrying stories that made the hair stand up on Cyrus's arms: wells that gave down warm water and then nothing; birds falling from the sky mid-flight, frozen stiff; a village where the hearth-fires would no longer take, where people huddled around unlit wood and slowly, politely, began to freeze.

And among the refugees, in the trampled snow outside the gate, Cyrus saw a sight that would follow them into every dream afterward: an old man kneeling in the drifts, holding his hands out to the sky, saying over and over, with the mild puzzled tone of a man who has misplaced his spectacles:

"I can't remember what warm is. I know the word. I know I knew it. I can't remember what it felt like."

The world wasn't just cooling. The concept of warmth itself was being subtracted, memory by memory, sensation by sensation, and the people were too busy surviving to notice that they were losing not just their harvests but their *knowledge* of what had been taken.

Bren stopped speaking entirely that day. He sat with the casket in his lap and fed the ember drops of oil from a dropper, one every hour, like a man spooning broth into a dying lover's mouth. Once, near midnight, Cyrus heard him talking to it—low, broken words in a language that predated Vell, predated maybe everything—and the ember flared weakly in response, a pulse of red that lasted barely a heartbeat.

Three seconds. Then two.

* * *

On the ninth morning of the cold, the sky cracked.

Not thunder—there was no storm left in the heavens to make thunder, or so it seemed; the clouds hung motionless, gray on gray, a ceiling of slate. But just before dawn, a sound rolled across Vell that every person in the city heard and no two described alike: some called it a bell, some a scream, some the groan of a ship's hull breaking. Whatever it was, it came from everywhere and nowhere, and when it faded, the dawn did not arrive.

The sun rose that morning the way an eyelid rises halfway during fever—a dull disc, pale as bone, visible without pain, casting shadows so faint they seemed apologetic. It climbed perhaps a handspan above the rooftops and stopped.

And it stayed there.

Noon came and went. The light neither strengthened nor moved. Birds roosted confused in the eaves. Bells rang the hours into air that ignored them. In the markets, men checked sundials and found them useless, and began, quietly, in ones and twos, to weep—not loudly, not hysterically, but with the deep embarrassed grief of people who have realized something fundamental has broken and cannot be fixed by any guild or god they know.

Time itself had begun to stutter, hitching on the world's failing heat the way a wheel catches on a bent axle.

Cyrus stood in the street below the dyer's shop with Wren and Bren, the casket strapped now to Bren's chest like a breastplate, and watched the stuck sun, and felt the last of their hesitation burn away.

"We go tonight," Cyrus said.

"To the flooded temple," Wren said. "The true map."

"To the Door. Whatever's between us and it, we cut through or we go around. Because look at it—" Cyrus swept a hand at the paralyzed sky, the frozen ripples, the empty flower-stall, the whole vast quiet catastrophe of a world forgetting itself. "Every hour we spend being careful is an hour the cold takes something else. Something small. Something no one will miss until it's gone. And eventually it will take something no one can afford to lose."

Bren spoke for the first time in a day and a half. His voice was rust and gravel. "She held a flare today. One second. Just one." He looked at Cyrus, and his eyes were terrible and steady. "If you're wrong about the darkness—if the Door really does want her dead—then tonight we kill her ourselves by walking through. You understand that. You're betting her life on your reading of a dead language."

"I understand."

"And if you're right?"

"Then tonight we stop the world from forgetting how to be warm."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The stuck sun hung its pale disc over Vell like a coin someone had nailed to the sky. Somewhere in the refugee camps beyond the walls, ten thousand people who could no longer remember summer huddled together for a warmth they could no longer name.

Then Wren shouldered her satchel of stolen archives, Bren cinched the casket's straps tight against his heart, and Cyrus pulled their hood up against a cold that was no longer weather but something closer to intention.

As they turned toward the drowned quarter, toward the temple beneath the waters, a boy ran past them down the street, chasing a hoop, laughing—and mid-laugh he stopped, frowned, and looked around at the gray world with the sudden lost expression of someone waking in an unfamiliar room.

"What was I doing?" he said, to no one. "I was... something. I was happy about something."

He stood there a moment longer, searching his own face for the missing thing. Then he shrugged and walked away, and Cyrus stood in the street and understood, with absolute clarity, that they were out of time.

Whatever waited beneath the flooded temple, they would face it tonight—or the world would finish forgetting, gently and completely, and no one would remain who remembered there had been anything worth saving at all.
