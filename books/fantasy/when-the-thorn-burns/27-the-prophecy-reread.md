## 27. The Prophecy Reread

Wren had counted the words of the final line four hundred and eleven times.

She knew this because she had made a tally mark on the margin of her working copy every time she traced the Old Ashkarin script from beginning to end without the meaning changing—and the meaning never changed, and the meaning was impossible, and so she counted again, because the alternative was to believe that four hundred and eleven repetitions of the same impossible thing had made it true.

*The Thorn burns, and the burning is the door.*

Eleven words. She had learned to read them before she could read her own name, back in the archive at Callowmere, when she was a girl with ink-stained fingers and a head full of other people's dead languages. Eleven words that had sent her across half a country, that had made her lie to a charm-seller with storm in his skin, that had cost her—she was still counting those costs too, and that tally was longer.

She sat in the safehouse's back room with the prophecy book open before her, a single candle burning low, and she did what she had done every night for three weeks: she read the line wrong.

That was the only way she could think of it now. *Wrong.* Not mistranslated—she had checked every glyph against every lexicon Bren's scattered order had left behind, against the marginalia of three dead archivists, against her own memory of the tongue her grandmother had sung lullabies in before the Guild burned the songs. The translation was sound. The *reading* was wrong. There was a difference, and the difference had been sitting in front of her for three weeks like a knife on a table, waiting for someone brave enough or stupid enough to pick it up.

The candle guttered. Outside, Vell coughed smoke into the dark. Somewhere to the east, three days' ride now, the sky over the salt flats had gone the color of old pewter, and the tides had stuttered twice in a single week, and the farmers were saying the sun had forgotten how to be warm. Yssara was dying. The ember Bren had guarded for twenty years was in the hands of a priestess who wanted to eat a god, and the world was beginning to forget how to be warm, and Wren Vasska was counting words.

She turned the page again.

Here was the thing about Old Ashkarin that no lexicon in the Guild's burned libraries had bothered to record, because the Guild had never bothered to record anything about Ashkarin except how to erase it: the language had no passive voice. Not a deficient one, not an underdeveloped one—it *refused* the passive. There was no way to say *the Thorn is burned*. There was no way to say *the door is opened*. Every verb in the tongue demanded an actor. Every sentence was a hand reaching out to do something. Her grandmother had explained it once, laughing, wine on her breath: *In Ashkarin, nothing simply happens, little bird. Someone always does it. That is why they had to unmake us. A people who believe everything is chosen cannot be ruled by people who need them to believe in fate.*

Wren had known this since she was nine years old.

She had never once applied it to the final line.

She looked at the eleven words and felt her whole body go cold and then hot, the way it did before a storm broke, the way Cyrus's hands looked when the storm under his skin wanted out.

*The Thorn burns, and the burning is the door.*

She had read it as a sentence. As a verdict. *The Thorn burns*—subject, verb, doom. The Thorn, that unfortunate soul, that marked and doomed figure, burns. Full stop. The burning follows the Thorn the way night follows dusk, inevitable, impersonal, the universe closing its hand.

But there was no impersonal in Ashkarin. There was no *it happens*. There was only *someone does it*.

The verb was active. The verb had *always* been active. The Thorn was not the thing that burned.

The Thorn was the thing that *did* the burning.

Wren's hand went to her mouth. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if the room itself were holding its breath.

*The Thorn burns—and the burning is the door.* Not: the Thorn will be consumed. Not: the Thorn must die in fire. But: the Thorn *chooses* what burns. The Thorn takes the fire—the storm, the magic, the brand, the whole terrible inheritance—and decides where it goes. Into what. Into whom. The prophecy was not a sentence passed down from some dead god's mouth. It was a *job description*. It was a key cut in the shape of a hand, waiting a century for the right hand to close around it.

The book was not a sentence.

The book was a *warning*.

She was on her feet before she knew she'd stood, the chair scraping back, and she was laughing—she heard herself laughing, high and cracked and half-hysterical, in a safehouse in a pyre-lit city while the world's last dragon guttered toward the dark—and she pressed both hands flat over her mouth to stop it, because the walls were thin and the Guild was everywhere and Voss had declared martial law three nights ago, and joy, right now, in Vell, was as dangerous as magic.

But she couldn't stop her hands from shaking.

Four hundred and eleven readings, and every one of them wrong, because she had read the prophecy the way the Guild read everything: as something done *to* people. As fate. As a cage with the door welded shut. She had spent three years of her life, had betrayed the first person who had ever—she stopped that thought, put it down, picked it up again, could not put it down—had lied to and used and *betrayed* Cyrus Crane, on the strength of a misreading. She had handed a frightened, lonely man his own doom and called it scholarship.

And the worst of it, the part that made her throat close: she hadn't misread it alone. The whole world had misread it. Ilyra had misread it—or worse, Ilyra had read it *right* and lied. The cabal had misread it. Voss, in whatever black chapel of his own ambition he prayed in, had misread it. A century of terror, a country unmade, a dragon's fire stolen and hoarded and fought over—and the whole time the final line had been sitting there saying, in a language with no passive voice, *someone chooses. Someone always chooses.*

She grabbed her working copy, the transcription she'd made in the first week, and she flipped to the final page and she wrote, beneath the Old Ashkarin in her own quick hand, the true rendering:

*The Thorn burns what the Thorn chooses. The burning is the door.*

Then, underneath, because her hands wouldn't stop:

*Not a sentence. A choice. God forgive me. God forgive me. God forgive—*

The door opened behind her.

* * *

Cyrus stood in the doorway with a bucket of well water in one hand and the look of a man who had learned, over the past month, to expect the worst from every room he entered. The exile-brand at his throat had faded to a pale shine since the salt flats—Wren had noticed it, had said nothing, had filed the noticing away in the drawer where she kept everything she couldn't bear to look at directly. His eyes went first to her face, then to the open book, then to the page she'd written, and she watched him decide, in the space of a heartbeat, not to ask what she'd been laughing about.

"The well's still running," he said. "Small mercies. The east quarter's gone dry—Voss's people are saying it's the Thorn's curse." His mouth twisted around the word. "I'm cursing the city by making it thirsty, apparently. I'd apologize, but I've been rather busy not existing."

"Cyrus." Her voice came out strange. She cleared her throat and tried again. "Put the bucket down."

He did. Slowly. He'd learned that tone from her by now, the archivist's tone, the one that meant *I have found something and I don't know yet whether it's a gift or a wound.* He came and stood over the book, and the candlelight moved on his scarred hands, and Wren thought, not for the first time, of the first night she'd seen those hands in the night market, turning a luck-charm for a frightened widow, and how she had looked at them and thought: *there. The hands from the page.* She had thought she was looking at a sentence then too. She had thought she was looking at a verdict with fingers.

"Read the last line," she said. "Out loud. The way you'd read it."

Cyrus frowned, but he bent over the page. His Ashkarin was rough—three weeks of her tutoring had given him enough to survive the walls in the catacombs, nothing more—and he read slowly, haltingly, translating as he went. "The Thorn burns. And the burning is... the door." He straightened. "That's what it says. That's what it's said every night you've been up here killing yourself over it. The Thorn burns. I burn. I know, Wren. I've known since the night you showed me my own hands in a dead man's book."

"That's what it says in *Guild* Ashkarin," Wren said. "In the Ashkarin they let survive, the flattened version, the one with all the teeth pulled. But that's not what the language says. Cyrus—Old Ashkarin has no passive voice. There is no *is burned*. There is no *it happens*. Every verb needs someone doing it. My grandmother used to say—" her voice cracked, and she pushed through it, "—she used to say that in Ashkarin, nothing simply *happens*. Someone always does it. That was the whole philosophy. That was why they were erased."

Cyrus had gone very still. It was the stillness he wore like armor, the market-stall stillness, the *I am a charm-seller, I am no one, I am invisible* stillness, and Wren hated that she knew it well enough to name it.

"Say it plainly," he said. "Whatever it is. Say it plainly."

"The verb is active." She turned the book toward him and put her finger under the eleventh word. "It was always active. A hundred years of archivists read it as *the Thorn is burned* because they were reading it through a language that lets you hide the actor, and a faith that wants people to believe in fate, and a Guild that needs the marked to believe they're doomed. But the line doesn't say the Thorn will burn, Cyrus. It says the Thorn *burns*. Transitive. With an object. The Thorn burns *something*. The burning is the door—not his death. His *choice*."

The silence went on so long she could hear the candle burning, the tiny wet sound of the wick.

"You're saying," Cyrus said at last, and his voice had dropped into something she'd only heard once before, in the catacombs, when the walls had answered him in a language no living tongue should have known—"you're saying the prophecy doesn't sentence me. It *hires* me."

"I'm saying the book was never a verdict." The words were tumbling now, three weeks of them, four hundred and eleven readings of them. "It was a warning. It was—Cyrus, think about who wrote it. A keeper, in the last days of Ashkarin, watching the fire being taken, watching the country about to be unmade. They couldn't stop it. So they wrote a letter forward through time, to the one person who might be able to. *The Thorn burns what the Thorn chooses.* It's not telling you what will happen to you. It's telling you what you'll be able to *do*. The storm, the magic, the brand—all of it, the whole inheritance—it's not a debt you're paying. It's a key you're holding. And a key doesn't care what it opens. That's the warning part. That's why they wrote it in fire and hid it under a dead country's name. Because the same hand that could open the Door could burn the world, and the book can't choose for you. *Nothing* can choose for you. That's the whole point of the language. That's the whole point of—"

She stopped, because Cyrus had sat down.

Not collapsed—sat, deliberate, the way a man sits when his legs have informed him of a decision he wasn't consulted on. He put his hands flat on the table on either side of the book, and Wren watched his scarred fingers, the fingers from the page, the fingers from the night market, tremble once and go still.

"Ilyra told me the prophecy demands I burn," he said quietly.

"Ilyra told you what serves Ilyra."

"Sefa believed it. Twelve years, she believed she was choosing the world over me, because the world said the Thorn was a plague to be cut out." His jaw worked. "I believed it. Every storm I've ever called, I've called it like a man spending money he stole. Like the magic was a debt and the burning was the interest and the only question was who'd hold the bill." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and, underneath the fury, something so raw with hope that Wren had to look away from it, the way you look away from the sun. "You're telling me it was never a debt. You're telling me I've spent twelve years apologizing for a key."

"I'm telling you the book agrees with your grandmother's language," Wren said, "and your grandmother's language agrees with itself, and I have checked it four hundred and eleven times, and I would check it four hundred more if you asked me to. The Thorn chooses what burns. That's the prophecy. That was always the prophecy. Everything else—the doom, the sacrifice, the *burning is your fate*—that was us. That was the Guild's reading. That was the cage they built and then told you the cage was your bones."

Cyrus laughed. It was not a happy sound, but it wasn't a broken one either; it was the sound of a man setting down a weight so old he'd forgotten it was a weight and not a limb.

"The night market," he said. "The first night you came to my stall. You bought a protection charm and asked me to dinner, and I said yes because I was so lonely I'd have said yes to a tax collector. And the whole time, you were—" He shook his head. "You were reading my sentence."

"Yes."

"And now you've read it right."

"Yes." Wren swallowed. "Cyrus, I need to say the rest of it. The part that isn't a gift. Because there's a part that isn't a gift."

He nodded. Waited. He was good at waiting; exile had taught him that.

"If the Thorn chooses what burns," she said, "then the choice is real. Which means it can be made wrong. Ilyra thinks the prophecy makes her a god if she feeds the fire to herself—she's wrong, the prophecy doesn't *make* anyone anything, but she can still *choose* to burn a country into her own image, and the Door will open for that choice too, because the Door doesn't judge. It's a door. Voss can choose to burn the Thorn on a pyre in Coronation Square, and if he's wrong about what the burning means, the Door stays shut and Yssara dies and the world forgets warmth—but he can still make the choice, and the choice will still *do* something. The book doesn't protect anyone from choosing badly. It just refuses to pretend the choice isn't ours." She laid her hand on the page, over the eleven words, over the truth that had cost a country its name. "That's the warning, Cyrus. Not *you will burn*. *You will choose, and the burning will follow your choice, and no one else can make it for you, and no one else can be blamed for it either.*"

Cyrus was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed—settled, the way the air settles before a storm decides.

"Then Ilyra's ascension," he said. "The cabal's theft. Voss's pyre. None of it is the prophecy coming true."

"No."

"It's just people choosing. Badly. The same way they always have."

"Yes."

"And the Door—"

"Opens for whoever holds the fire when they choose," Wren said. "Whoever. However they choose. The keeper who wrote it thought they were warning the future about a tyrant. They didn't know they were writing instructions for a charm-seller with a brand on his throat." She smiled, and it hurt. "Prophecies are just letters, Cyrus. Written by people, to people, about people. We keep forgetting the last part."

Cyrus looked down at his hands. Turned them over. Studied the palms as though seeing them for the first time—not as the hands from the page, not as the Thorn's hands, but as his own. Wren watched him do it and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the night she'd first shown him the book, since before that, since Callowmere, since a girl with ink-stained fingers had first read eleven words and built her whole life inside the misreading.

"The storm I called over the salt flats," Cyrus said slowly. "When the hunters came. When that man in the street—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought that was the price. The toll. The universe's invoice. But if the choice is mine—if the *burning* is mine to aim—"

"Then the toll was never the magic," Wren said softly. "The toll was not yet knowing how to anchor it. That's what you've been teaching me to do, these past weeks. That's what the anchoring *is*. You weren't learning to avoid the price, Cyrus. You were learning to choose better."

He closed his eyes. The candle flame bent toward him, the way flames do, the way, Wren thought suddenly, flames had always done around him, and she had catalogued it as an anomaly and moved on, because she had been reading everything wrong.

When he opened his eyes, he was someone slightly different. Not a new man—the same man, the charm-seller, the exile, the lonely one who'd said yes to dinner against every instinct—but standing differently inside himself, the way a house stands differently once you learn its walls are load-bearing and not a prison.

"Then we go to the Door," he said. "Not to escape. Not to run. Ilyra has the ember. Voss has the city. Sefa—" his voice caught on the name, and Wren saw him choose, deliberately, to keep going, "—Sefa is in a cell somewhere for treason she didn't commit, and every hour we spend here the world gets colder. We go to the Door. And when it opens, I choose what burns." He put his hand over the book, over the eleven words, the way you'd put your hand on a friend's shoulder. "And I am *done*, Wren, I am finished, with letting dead archivists and live priests and grey-faced inquisitors tell me what my hands are for."

Wren believed him. That was the terrifying thing. She had spent three years believing in a sentence, and now she believed in a man, and the second faith was infinitely more dangerous.

She was reaching for the book to close it—there was packing to do, Bren to wake, a city of pyres to slip through—when she heard it.

Boots. Not one pair. Many, moving in the disciplined rhythm she had learned, these past three weeks, to dread: the Guild's cadence, iron-shod, unhurried, the walk of people who owned the streets they walked on. And beneath it, from the front of the safehouse, Bren's voice—raised, slurred with more than drink, shouting the kind of words that were less a warning than a eulogy.

Cyrus was already moving, snuffing the candle, and in the sudden dark Wren grabbed the prophecy book and held it against her chest like a child, like a weapon, like a letter that had finally, after a hundred years, been read.

"They found us," she whispered.

Down in the street, someone began to scream—and under the screaming, faint but unmistakable, rising like the first note of a storm that had been waiting twelve years for permission, Cyrus's brand began to shine.
