## 29. Voss's Coronation

The mirror in the Guildmaster's chambers had not been used in a hundred years.

Halvard Voss knew this because he had read the inventory ledgers himself—all of them, every scrap of parchment the Inquisitor's Guild had ever committed to ink, back when he was a junior clerk with ink-stained cuffs and ambitions he'd been ashamed to name even in his own head. The chamber had belonged to Grand Inquisitor Maren Solace, who had died at her desk and been carried out through a window because no door in the Guildhall was wide enough for her coffin and her honors both. After her, the room had stood empty. No one had wanted it. Successive Guildmasters had said it felt like standing inside someone else's judgment.

Voss had wanted it for thirty years.

He dressed before the mirror now in the formal whites of the Guild's founding order—the ones that hadn't been worn since the Purification of the Borderlands, since the last time the Guild had believed itself an army of salvation rather than a bureaucracy of fear. The coat fit him as though it had been waiting. Of course it had. Everything in his life that was worth having had waited for him, patient as a trap.

He looked at himself and saw what the city would see tomorrow: not a man, but an answer.

For twelve years they had whispered about him behind his back. *The one who let the storm-caller go. The one whose testimony was questioned. The one who burned the wrong villages and called it zeal.* He had heard the whispers through doors, through wine-cups tilted just so, through the particular silence of rooms that went quiet when he entered. He had endured them the way stone endures water—without flinching, without forgetting, keeping a precise account of every drop.

Tomorrow the account would be settled.

He descended the great stair of the Guildhall slowly, because a man who would be savior must never appear hurried. The building hummed around him. For three days his clerks had worked without sleep, and their exhaustion had a smell to it—candle tallow, cold tea, fear sweetened with ambition—and Voss found the smell intoxicating. This was what power actually smelled like. Not incense. Not blood. Other people's fear, working for you.

The Hall of Seals had been transformed. Where the interrogators' benches had stood, carpenters had raised a dais of white ash-wood, wide enough for forty dignitaries and high enough that its occupant would look down on the Council seats themselves. Banners of the Guild hung from every pillar—the grey flame on white field—and between them, at Voss's specific instruction, hung the old banners too, the ones from the Guild's militant century, retrieved from storage where they'd been folded away in shame. A thousand years ago the Inquisitors had marched under those colors and been loved for it. Loved. That was the word the histories used, and Voss had underlined it three times in his own copy.

"Master Voss." Corven, his senior clerk, met him at the base of the dais with a ledger open against his forearm. The man's hands were shaking. Good. "The final count for tomorrow. We have confirmation from all nine districts. The squares will overflow—we've opened the granary yards and the river terraces. Best estimate—"

"I don't want estimates."

Corven swallowed. "Forty thousand. Perhaps fifty."

Fifty thousand souls, come to watch the Thorn die. Voss turned the number over in his mind like a coin he intended to bite. When the storm-caller burned, when the crowd saw the heretic's body blacken on the pyre and heard Voss name the crime plainly—*magic, and magic's price paid in other people's lives*—the city would finally understand what he had understood alone for twelve years. That he had been right. That he had always been right. That exile had not been mercy or error but strategy: let the Thorn run, let it gather its accomplices, let it reveal every sympathizer, and then take them all at once, root and branch, before the whole watching world.

"You've received the Council's objection," Corven said carefully. "In writing. Councillor Sefa Crane—"

"She is not a councillor anymore." The words came out of Voss smooth and cold, like water over a blade. "She is a prisoner awaiting charges. Her signature has no more weight than the ash we'll sweep up tomorrow evening."

"Of course. But the objection itself—the Council formally requests that the Thorn be examined by the full bench before execution. They cite the prophecy. They say—" Corven's throat moved. "They say the book may have been mistranslated. That killing the Thorn might—"

"Might what?" Voss smiled, and watched the clerk flinch. He had practiced the smile in the mirror upstairs until it achieved exactly this effect: warmth with nothing behind it, a lamp lit in an empty house. "Might doom us? Let me tell you what I know about prophecy, Corven. I have read the Ashkarin fragments. All of them, including the pages your colleagues in the archive swore didn't exist until I showed them where Wren Halloway had hidden her copies." He stepped closer. "The Thorn is named twice in the old text. Once as savior, once as destroyer. Do you know why?"

"No, Master Voss."

"Because whoever holds the pen decides which name fits. The priests of Ashkarin wrote riddles so that clever men could rule the frightened. I am simply finishing their work." He took the ledger from Corven's arm and signed the final page himself—the execution order, the staging plan, the disposition of troops. His hand did not shake. It had never once shaken. "The Thorn dies at noon. The fire it guarded passes to the Guild at dusk. And by winter, there will be no plagues, no failing crops, no stuttering tides, because the Guild will hold the last dragon's heart and decide—as a matter of policy—who deserves warmth."

*As a matter of policy.* He savored the phrase. That was governance. That was the difference between him and every screaming prophet who'd ever promised salvation: he would deliver it on schedule, with documentation.

* * *

The cells beneath the Guildhall were older than the Guildhall itself, cut into foundations that predated Vell's oldest maps—a fact Voss had never much considered until recently, when the walls had begun to matter. Now he walked their length with a lantern held high, past the ordinary prisoners, past the debtors and the drunks, to the two cages at the corridor's end.

Sefa Crane sat on the floor of hers, back against the bars, reading. She had asked for a book and, to the astonishment of the turnkeys, he had allowed it—not out of kindness. He wanted her mind occupied. He wanted her calm. A calm woman could be reasoned with, and there remained one small use left in her.

"Councillor," he said.

She didn't look up. "I'm not a councillor. You made a point of it in front of my clerk."

"Formerly, then. I've come to discuss tomorrow."

"Now you're interested in discussing things." She closed the book on her finger and raised her eyes to him at last, and Voss was pleased to see no tears there, no begging. Twelve years on the Council had taught her composure if nothing else. "You put me in a cell, Halvard. You can't also expect me to be a gracious audience."

"The crowd tomorrow will be fifty thousand strong." He set the lantern on the floor between the cages so its light reached them both. In the second cage, the storm-caller sat motionless in the shadows, iron-cuffed, watching him with an expression Voss refused to interpret as anything but defeat. "When I stand on the pyre-steps and speak, everything I say will become history within the hour. History is written once, Sefa, and then it is only defended. I'm offering you the chance to help me write it."

"By testifying that you're a hero."

"By telling the truth as it will be remembered. That you hunted the Thorn faithfully. That your arrest was regrettable necessity—that you were compromised, deceived, that your conscience drove you to obstruct the Guild only briefly and under duress." He crouched, bringing his face level with hers. "You'll stand beside me on the dais. Pardoned. Restored. Alive. Or you'll burn on the second pyre an hour after the first, and the histories will record you as the traitor who sheltered the heretic out of—what do you imagine they'll write? Old love?" He let the word sit between them like something rotting. "Sentiment. Weakness. Is that how you want twelve years of service to end? As a footnote about sentiment?"

Something moved behind Sefa's eyes—something old and tired and, infuriatingly, amused.

"You rehearsed that speech," she said. "In front of the mirror. Don't lie, I can hear the cadence. You've been practicing your coronation for thirty years, Halvard. I used to think it was ambition. Now I think it's grief. Someone hurt you once, very badly, and you decided that if you couldn't be loved you'd be *necessary*, and now you can't tell the difference anymore."

Voss rose. The lantern light caught the seam of the old scar along his jaw—the one the storm-wind had given him, twelve years ago, on the day the village drowned anyway. He did not touch it. He never touched it.

"At noon," he said quietly, "you will watch the thing you love burn. And you will learn what I learned standing in the rain while the water rose: that love is just weather. It passes. What remains is what you build." He picked up the lantern. "Consider my offer. Corven will return before dawn for your answer."

He walked away, and behind him, faint but perfectly clear, Sefa began to laugh. Not hysteria. Something worse. The laugh of a woman who had already seen the end of the story and found his part in it small.

He did not let it trouble him. Tomorrow there would be no one left to laugh.

* * *

At midnight he inspected the pyres.

Two of them stood in Ascension Square, built of salt-hardened oak stacked fifteen feet high, ringed with the iron stakes to which the condemned would be chained. Around them the carpenters had constructed a stage, and above the stage a canopy of white silk bearing the grey flame, and beyond the stage the scaffolding for ten thousand spectators rose tier upon tier like the amphitheaters of some fallen empire. Torches burned at every corner. The night sky above the square glowed orange, and ash drifted down over Vell like dirty snow.

Voss walked the perimeter alone. He had dismissed his guards; a savior should know his own ground intimately, should feel the grain of the wood he would stand upon.

It was perfect. He permitted himself, for one unguarded moment, to feel it—to feel the whole shape of the day arriving, noon and the reading of charges, the confession he had drafted for the Thorn to make (they would draft the prisoner's voice too; the crowd would hear repentance), the lighting of the pyre, the transfer of the ember-heart from its reliquary to the Guild's new vault, and at dusk the address from the dais, the one he had written and rewritten eleven times, beginning *People of Vell, your long night ends tonight.*

And then the thought arrived, the way such thoughts always arrive—uninvited, in his own voice, wearing his own doubt.

*The crops are still failing.*

He stopped walking.

Three weeks since they had taken the ember-heart from the keeper's hovel, and the tides had not steadied. The frost had come early regardless. The fires in the eastern districts still guttered green at their edges, burning wrong, and the physicians' reports spoke of a wasting sickness spreading through the poor quarters that no poultice touched. He had told the Council these were the death-throes of the Thorn's sorcery, symptoms that would pass with its destruction. He had believed it. He had needed to believe it.

But a splinter of arithmetic lodged in him now, small and cold: if the sickness came from the Thorn, why had it worsened after the capture?

He crushed the thought with practice, the way he had crushed every doubt for thirty years. Doubt was weather too. What remained was what you built.

A sound made him lift his head—one of the carpenters, still working late on the far side of the square, driving stakes into the pyre's base. A broad, heavy-shouldered man, sleeves rolled, moving with the unhurried competence of someone who had built many things and destroyed a few. Voss watched him work for a moment. There was something familiar in the set of the shoulders, though the man's face was lost in shadow and wood-dust, and half the laborers in Vell had shoulders like that.

"Your name," Voss called across the square.

The carpenter straightened. For a heartbeat he said nothing, and in that heartbeat the torchlight seemed to bend toward him, and Voss felt—absurdly, unforgivably—the animal prickle along his spine that he had trained out of lesser men.

"Bren, master," the carpenter said, and touched his brow. "Just finishing the last stake."

"Work carefully," Voss said. "Everything tomorrow must hold."

"Oh, it'll hold," the carpenter said mildly, and bent again to his task.

Voss turned back toward the Guildhall, toward the waiting speeches and the fitting of his ceremonial whites, and did not see the carpenter pause, or glance once at the second pyre—the smaller one, the one reserved for the traitor Sefa—or press his palm flat against the salt-oak as if taking its measure, or whisper to it, very softly, in a language that had not been spoken aloud in Vell for a hundred years:

*"Hold fast, old heart. One more day. Then we burn the right thing."*

Above the square the sky churned, cloudless and yet somehow gathering, and the torches all leaned east at once, as if the wind had remembered a name.
