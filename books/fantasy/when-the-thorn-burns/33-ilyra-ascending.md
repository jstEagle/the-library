## 33. Ilyra Ascending

Ilyra had spent thirty years learning to lie with her whole body—the tilt of her head, the softening of her mouth, the particular quality of tears that came when she wanted them to. She had lied to abbots and inquisitors, to dying women and newborn children, to the god whose name she still carried like a stolen coin in her pocket. She had lied so long and so well that sometimes the lies were the only things left that felt true.

The ember did not need her to lie.

It knew her the way fire knows a wick.

She stood at the center of the Door's antechamber with the coal of Yssara's heart cupped in both palms, and it burned without burning her—not yet, not cruelly, the way a promise does not hurt until it is kept. The others were gone: Cyrus fled down the corridor toward the flooded dark, Wren dragging Bren's hollowed ghost behind her, Sefa buying them minutes with steel and old love. Ilyra had watched them go. She had even, in some small honest corner of herself, meant to follow.

But the Door was open now.

And the ember sang.

*You are tired,* it said, in the voice she had heard all her life beneath every prayer she'd ever spoken—a voice like wind through high mountain passes, like the space between two bells. *You have carried a dead god for thirty years. Set him down.*

"I kept your faith alive," she whispered. "When Ashkarin was unmade, when every temple fell and every name was scraped from stone, I kept you. Me. Alone."

*You kept yourself,* the fire said gently, which was the cruelest thing it could have said, because it was true. She had kept the faith the way one keeps a blade—sharp, hidden, aimed at an enemy who never stopped being herself.

The ember pulsed once, patient as sunrise.

*A god does not need to be kept,* it said. *A god needs to be born.*

Ilyra looked at the coal. It was small—that had always astonished her, that the last heart-flame of the last dragon should be no larger than a fist, no heavier than bread. Inside it she could see movement, slow and vast, like weather seen from very far above: the turning of Yssara's dreams, the memory of wings wide enough to shade a city, the long grief of a creature who had outlived everyone who ever spoke her name.

Bren had guarded this thing for twenty years with his two ruined hands. Cyrus had nearly died carrying it. Wren had betrayed everything she loved for pages describing it. And Ilyra—

Ilyra had traded away every scrap of mercy she possessed to hold it now.

"There is another way," she said, and heard in her own voice the last tremor of the priestess she had been at nineteen, kneeling in a temple that would not exist within the year. "Cyrus could return it. The prophecy—"

*The prophecy is a book,* said the fire. *Books burn.*

She laughed. She couldn't help it; it came out of her like something escaping a burning house. And then, because there was no one left to perform for, because the audience of her whole life had narrowed at last to a single witness—herself—she stopped laughing and stopped lying and looked at what she wanted.

She wanted to matter. That was all. That had always been all. Thirty years of tending a faith made of absence, of whispering the forbidden name of Ashkarin into the ears of the dying because no one else would, of watching Vell's priests burn books and call it cleansing—thirty years of being the only rememberer, and remembering was just another word for grieving slowly, alone, without end.

The ember turned in her hands like a patient eye.

*Take me in,* it said. *And nothing will be forgotten again. Not by anyone. Not ever.*

Ilyra raised the coal to her lips the way she had once raised the sacramental cup, and drank her god.

* * *

The first thing to change was her breath.

It stopped.

There was no panic in it—no drowning reflex, no animal clawing for air. Her lungs simply closed like doors, quietly, decisively, and something else opened in their place: a heat at the base of her throat that pulled inward, drawing the air itself apart, feeding on it the way ordinary flesh feeds on bread. She breathed flame the way other creatures breathe oxygen, and the flame breathed her back.

Then the pain began.

Not the pain of injury—Ilyra knew that country well, had walked its roads since childhood. This was the pain of borders dissolving. She felt her bones heat from within, marrow first, then the dense white architecture around it, then the skin. Her ribs glowed faintly through her robes, orange lines like a kiln door cracked open, and where the light touched her flesh the flesh remembered it was fuel.

She went down onto her knees on the ancient stone. Her hair went first to smoke at the ends and then, strangely, upward—each strand lifting as if in water, silvering, stiffening into filaments that chimed faintly when they touched each other. She watched her hands change. The nails thickened, darkened, took a lacquered black sheen like polished chitin or old temple doors. Beneath the skin of her forearms, veins of gold spread outward from her wrists in branching rivers, molten patterns tracing lines that were almost—but not quite—human anatomy, as though someone had taken a map of a woman and redrawn it for something with a different destiny.

*I am remaking you,* the fire said, everywhere inside her now, warm as confession. *Forgive the slowness. It has been a hundred years since I built anything.*

"You're building a vessel," Ilyra gasped. The gold reached her shoulders. Where it passed, her scars—there were so many, a lifetime of penance cut into her own skin—sealed shut, smoothed over, erased. She felt them go with something close to horror. They had been hers. They had been the ledger of everything she had endured and everything she had chosen. Now they were simply gone, the way Ashkarin had gone, the way names go when a country is unwritten.

*No,* said the fire, and there was something in its voice now that she had not expected—something almost sad. *You are building a vessel. I am only the clay. You drank me, priestess. Rememberer. The shape of what rises will carry your shape.*

That was when Ilyra understood, in the deep place where understanding arrives too late to matter, that she had made a catastrophic arithmetic error. She had thought she was consuming the dragon. All her plans—all the long scheming, the bargains struck with nobles who thought themselves the schemers, the careful cultivation of Wren, the betrayal she had been rehearsing in her heart for months—had rested on a simple equation: I take the fire, therefore the fire is mine.

But fire does not work by ownership. Fire works by consent of the fuel.

She had drunk a god, and gods drink back.

* * *

The transformation reached her heart, and for a while she ceased to be a person having an experience and became only the experience itself.

Her heart did not stop. It *widened*. She felt it happen from the inside—an expansion like a cathedral being raised in her chest, vault after vault, each new chamber kindling as it formed. The stolen ember settled into the center of her like a seed into plowed ground, and rooted, and bloomed, and the bloom was vast beyond any metaphor her old life contained. Wings unfurled inside her ribcage—not feather, not membrane, but sheets of living flame folded tight, waiting for room. And the room was coming. Her spine crackled and lengthened, vertebra by vertebra, each one ringing like a bell struck under water. Her shoulders blazed. Her shadow on the antechamber floor grew wings it had no right to.

Through the roaring in her skull, fragments of the world arrived transformed. She could hear the tide in the drowned catacombs far below—and she could hear it thinking, the slow mineral patience of water that had been erasing stone since before language. She could smell fear three corridors away: Wren, crouched in the dark, smelling of ink and guilt. Bren, motionless somewhere, smelling of ash and surrender. Cyrus—

Cyrus smelled of storms about to break. Of ozone and grief and terrible choice.

And Sefa. Steel and old regret, standing between the rising light and the people she loved, the way she had always stood, the way duty had carved her into something both noble and broken.

Ilyra could taste their futures on the air. That was new. That was the ember's gift bleeding through the dragon's gift bleeding through her own: prophecy, raw and unfiltered, pouring into her the way it had once poured into the books the Guild burned. She saw a thousand branching threads and in almost all of them she burned—consumed from within, a wick used up, a candle's brief bright argument against the dark.

In one thread, she did not burn.

In one thread, she rose.

*That one,* whispered the fire, and she could no longer tell whether it was tempting her or warning her or simply reading aloud from the same book of fate she had spent her life pretending to outrun. *One thread in ten thousand. Do you want to know what it costs?*

"No," she said—which was a lie, and they both knew it, and knowing changed nothing, because wanting is not a decision, wanting is a gravity, and she had been falling toward this moment since the day she knelt in the ashes of her first temple and swore she would not forget.

*You will have to become larger than mercy,* the fire said. *Large enough that mercy looks small. Large enough that a city looks like a candle and a country like a match. Everything you have ever loved is small, Ilyra. To keep it, you must crush it gently, the way a hand crushes a bird it intends to save.*

"Yes," she said.

The gold reached her eyes.

* * *

She stood, at last, and standing was different—she was taller, though her feet had not moved, because height now was a fact of her presence rather than her skeleton. When she lifted her arms, the robes burned away from them without scorching her, and beneath, her skin had become something between bronze and sunset, shot through with those rivers of gold, and moving under the surface the way light moves under ice.

In the polished black of the Door's ancient frame she saw her reflection, and did not look away, because looking away was no longer something she was built to do.

A woman remained at the center of it—that much survived, the stubborn last acre of her humanity, face and mouth and the gray eyes she had inherited from a mother whose name only she remembered. But around the woman, the reflection showed more: a corona of slow fire, wings half-spread and growing, a crown of that chiming filament-hair lifting in a heat-shimmer of its own making. Behind her eyes, when they caught the light a certain way, something enormous turned over in its sleep. Slit-pupiled. Patient. Old.

She was beautiful the way the sun is beautiful, which is to say: from a distance, and briefly, and never twice the same way, and always with the knowledge underneath that beauty at this scale is only destruction that hasn't arrived yet.

"Yssara," she said, testing the name in her new voice, and the walls rang with it, and somewhere in the deep places of the world a sleeping dragon stirred and felt, for the first time in a century, hope—and Ilyra felt that hope arrive inside her own chest like a swallowed star, because the boundary between them was closing, thread by golden thread. The dragon's grief was becoming her grief. The dragon's fury was becoming her fury. Soon there would be no seam at all, and whatever walked away from this chamber would be neither Ilyra nor Yssara but the thing the prophecy books had whispered about in their oldest margins, the thing the erased priests of erased Ashkarin had written one warning line about before the Guild found their temples:

*A fire that remembers is a fire that judges.*

Good, Ilyra thought, flexing fingers that left afterimages in the air. There was so much to judge. A Guild that burned books and people with equal efficiency. Nobles who had bought her loyalty believing they were renting it. A world that had let a country die of embarrassment, that had agreed, collectively, politely, to forget Ashkarin rather than mourn it.

And one storm-caller with scarred hands, running even now through the dark toward a choice that belonged, by every law of fate and ink, to Ilyra.

She felt Cyrus the way one feels weather. The Thorn. The little mortal who believed that love was stronger than prophecy, that small choices outweighed large destinies, that a person could simply refuse the shape history had drawn for them. Ilyra had believed that once. She had been young, and poor, and sincere, and it had bought her nothing but exile inside her own life.

Power, she understood now, was the only honesty. Power never pretended to be anything but what it was. Love lied constantly—love said *forever* and *always* and *I would never*, and then the winter came, or the Council called, or the pyre was lit, and love found reasons.

She spread her wings. The antechamber was too small for them; the stone groaned, cracked, and she let it, and the ceiling above her split along seams laid down by masons dead a thousand years, and beyond it Vell's night sky opened—starless, smoke-choked, the whole wounded city spread below her like a bed of coals already arranged.

So much to judge. So much to set right. So much, at last, to *warm*.

Ilyra rose through the broken roof into the open air, and the wind of her own ascent fed her, and the fire inside her said, with something that might have been awe or might have been appetite:

*NOW WE SEE WHAT THEY DO WHEN A GOD LOOKS DOWN.*

Below her, tiny as an ember dropped on black cloth, a storm-caller stepped out of a tunnel mouth into the square, looked up, and did not run.

Ilyra smiled with a mouth full of dawn, and began, gently, terribly, to descend.
