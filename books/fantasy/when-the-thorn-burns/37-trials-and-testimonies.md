## 37. Trials and Testimonies

The Council chamber had not changed in twelve years. That was the first cruelty of it.

Sefa stood at the witness table and let the room look at her the way it always had: as a woman made of duty, seamless and uncrackable, a blade in a scabbard of gray wool. The nine seats of the Inquisitor's Council rose in a crescent above her, and above them hung the Guild's seal worked in iron and salt-glass, and above *that*, through the high windows, the sky—still wrong, still pale at noon, still carrying that faint bruised tint it had worn since the night Yssara's fire had come back to the world and burned Ilyra's divinity out of it like tarnish drawn off silver.

The sky was better now. The tides kept their appointments. The wheat in the river valleys had come up green instead of gray. But Sefa had spent her whole life learning to read small deviations, and she knew the world was still healing, the way a bone heals: slowly, invisibly, with an ache that arrived every time it rained.

At the center of the crescent, where the Presiding Chair sat, they had put Halvard Voss.

Not in chains. The Council had debated that for two days, she'd heard, and decided against it—not out of mercy but out of theater. A man who had ruled this room for a decade would look smaller in irons than he would sitting alone in the Chair he had seized, stripped of the office but given its seat, so that everyone could see exactly what he was without it. He wore plain charcoal, no seal, no chain of rank. His hands were folded on the armrests. His face was calm in the particular way of a man who has rehearsed his own martyrdom and believes, down to the marrow, that history will apologize to him.

He looked at Sefa when she entered. He did not smile. He simply looked, the way one looks at a ledger line that has failed to balance, and then he looked away, and somehow that was worse.

"Witness Sefa Vael," read the Recorder, "Inquisitor, Third Rank, suspended pending these proceedings. Testimony concerning the events of the Ascension, the martial decree, the pyre executions at Crown Square, and the conduct of the former First Inquisitor."

"Former," Voss said mildly, to no one. The word drifted up into the vaulted dark like smoke finding a draft.

The Presiding officer—a thin, careful woman named Aldemar whose reputation for incorruptibility was itself a kind of armor—struck the sound-bell once. "The witness will proceed. The chamber will remember that testimony given here is given under truth-oath."

Sefa had sworn the truth-oath four hundred and eleven times in her career. She knew its weight the way sailors know water: it held you up, and it drowned you, depending on what you carried into it. She set her hands flat on the table. Under the table, where no one could see, her thumb found the old scar on her palm—the one from the day of Cyrus Crane's exile, from gripping the branding-iron's cooling handle because someone senior had ordered her to hold it steady and she had been too much of a coward, too much of a *believer*, to say no.

She began not with Voss but with herself.

"Twelve years ago I testified before this Council that the storm-caller Cyrus Crane was a danger to the city of Vell." Her voice came out level. She had expected it to break; it didn't, and its steadiness felt like a kind of shame. "That testimony was true in every word and false in every implication. He had called a storm. It had frightened people. What I did not testify to was why he called it—or that I had stood on the seawall beside him while he did it, or that I watched him spend himself nearly to death pulling forty-one people out of a drowning village, or that when the Guild came asking questions, I answered them in a way designed to save my career rather than his life."

Above her, nothing moved. Nine faces, still as coins. Somewhere behind her, in the public gallery they had opened for the first time in the chamber's history—the new Charter's requirement, the people's right to witness—someone shifted, and wood creaked, and Sefa felt the sound go through her like a splinter.

She kept going. She laid it out the way she had learned to lay out evidence: dates, orders, signatures. The warrant Voss had drafted within hours of the storm, citing damages that had never occurred. The witnesses he had coached—he had *coached* them, she had seen the notes afterward in his archive, pages marked *instruct* in his small precise hand. The exile sentence he had argued should be a execution sentence until three other Council members overruled him, and how he had smiled when they overruled him, because exile, for a storm-caller, was only execution conducted slowly.

"I believed him dangerous," Sefa said. "I want that understood. I wasn't lying about my fear. I was lying about everything else."

"And your fear," said Councilor Brannoch from the third seat, an old man with a voice like gravel settling, "excused your silence for twelve years."

It was not a question. She gave it air anyway. "Yes. Twelve years is mine to account for. I am here to do that too."

* * *

They gave Voss the floor after the second hour, and he rose like ink poured upward.

He did not deny anything. That was the horror of watching him work; denial was beneath him. Instead he reframed. He spoke of plagues and the years of failing crops and the slow cold creeping up from the south, and he named the Thorn as their cause with the serenity of a man reading scripture, and when Aldemar reminded him that the Thorn question had been settled—that the prophecy had been misread, that Crane had broken it, that the dragon flew whole again—he inclined his head as if conceding a point of etiquette.

"The dragon flies," he agreed. "And the country we were told never existed has reappeared on our maps like a stain returning through fresh paint. And magic walks openly in streets where children were taught to run from it. You call this restoration. I administered protection. When the fire guttered and the world forgot warmth, I gave ten thousand terrified people something to burn. You may judge the pyres. Do not pretend you would have found a kinder fuel."

"We are not here," Aldemar said, "to judge your philosophy."

"No," Voss said. "You are here to find somewhere to put me where I will embarrass you least."

Sefa watched the Council take that. She watched Brannoch's jaw tighten and Aldemar's hands go still on the rail, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost gentle, that Voss had already won a portion of this trial—not acquittal, nothing so clean, but *containment*. Half the Council owed their seats to the chaos of the past months. They needed the Voss era to have been an aberration, a single madman, a story with one villain and a tidy ending. They did not need to hear that the pyres had been fed by ordinary approvals, stamped documents, committee minutes. They did not need her to say that name.

So she said it.

"Before the Ascension," Sefa said, into the settling quiet, "the martial decree was ratified by vote. Seven in favor, two abstaining. The abstentions were mine and Councilor Edevane's." She turned her head, slowly, and looked up the crescent at Edevane, a soft-bodied man who had grown paler with every hour of this session. "I am not here to hide behind my own vote. But the record should show that the man in the Chair did not seize power alone. He was handed it, piece by piece, by people who found it convenient, and I include myself in that number, and I include Councilor Edevane, and I will name every name in my written testimony if the Charter requires it, because a trial that cleanses only one man is not justice. It is laundry."

The gallery erupted. It took Aldemar three strikes of the bell to bring them down, and even then the sound didn't die so much as go underground, a low hum of two hundred voices doing arithmetic about their own signatures on old warrants.

Voss looked at her across the chamber, and for the first time all day, something moved in his face. Not anger. Recognition. The look of a man discovering that the instrument he thought he had forged has an edge he never ground.

* * *

They broke for the evening meal and reconvened by lamplight, and it was then, in the fourth hour, that they came to the part she had been dreading since dawn: Crown Square. The pyres. The night Bren of Ashkarin walked into the flame so that a storm-caller could live long enough to end it.

She told it plainly. She had not been there—but she had signed the transport order that moved Cyrus Crane to the square, and she had stood in the Guild tower watching, and she described what she had seen: the crowd, the wood, the figure chained at the stake with their scarred hands open at their sides as if waiting to catch rain. And she described what Voss had done from the scaffold steps—how he had raised his hand for silence, and given a speech about cleansing, and touched the torch to the pitch with his own hand because he wanted the crowd to see that salvation, as he understood it, was a thing done personally.

Then she described the sabotage. The keeper's oath passed in the chaos. The old man's body going into the flames in Crane's place, buying minutes. She got through all of it without her voice failing, which surprised her, and then Aldemar asked the question she had known was coming:

"Witness. You commanded the arrest detail that night. Your orders were to prevent the escape. Did you attempt to prevent it?"

The lamp-flames leaned in the draft. Sefa looked down the length of the table at Voss, and Voss looked back, and there was nothing between them now but the truth, which was the only gift she had left to give anyone.

"No," she said. "My orders were to prevent it. I did not. I delayed the cordon by ninety seconds. Ninety seconds is what it took." She paused, and heard herself add, quietly, "I have spent twelve years telling myself that duty is what remains when love is taken from you. That night I learned the reverse is also true. Some duties you keep because they're all that's left of a love. And some you have to break, because they've become the thing preventing any other kind."

Brannoch said, heavily, "You confess to obstruction of a lawful execution."

"I confess to it," Sefa said. "And I will carry whatever sentence attaches. But I ask the Council to notice what I obstructed, and to write *that* in the verdict next to my name. Not to excuse me. To describe me accurately."

* * *

They gave Voss his closing argument on the third morning, and he used it the way he used everything: precisely, beautifully, wrongly.

He did not beg. He stood at the witness table with his hands loose at his sides and told the Council that they were about to make him the answer to their own guilt, a single grave into which they could shovel a decade of complicity, and that future historians would thank him for the convenience and despise them for accepting it. He spoke of order the way other men speak of lost loves. He said, at the end, "I did what was necessary. History will require a villain. I decline the role. Find another."

Aldemar let the silence sit for a long moment. Then she said, "The role was never offered, First Inquisitor. It was earned. The Council does not require your acceptance."

The verdict came down that same afternoon, and it was, in its way, exactly the strange, uncomfortable, honest thing Sefa had hoped and feared it would be. Not vengeance. The new Charter had no provision for vengeance, and the Council, hungry as it was for a clean story, could not manufacture one under two hundred watching eyes in the gallery. Voss was stripped of rank permanently, attainted of his property, and sentenced not to death—he had wanted death, Sefa realized, watching his face as Aldemar pronounced it; death would have completed him, sealed his martyrdom in stone—but to the Quiet Cells, the old punitive archive beneath the Guild hall where prisoners were kept among records, permitted books and paper and nothing else, forever, with the door warded so that no visitor could reach him except through the Charter's review board.

They were going to make him live inside the evidence. Every petition he wrote would be read. Every appeal examined. The rest of his life would be testimony, whether he wanted it or not.

He received the sentence standing. Only at the very end, as the guards came to walk him out, did he stop beside Sefa's table, close enough that she could smell the chalk-dust and lamp-oil smell of the chamber on his coat, and speak for the last time in her hearing.

"You were the best of them," he said. "Do you know that? Obedient, precise, incapable of corruption. I built my whole decade on officers like you." His eyes were clear and terribly calm. "And now you've built this trial on the ruins. Tell me, Inquisitor Vael—if I had never existed, who would you have become?"

She considered it honestly, because he deserved an honest answer and because the question deserved one more than he did.

"Someone," she said. "Eventually. Later than I should have. That's the whole of it, and it's yours to carry, not mine."

He looked at her a moment longer, searching for a wound to press, and found none that wasn't already hers, owned, paid for. Then he straightened his plain collar with two fingers, a small vain gesture she had seen him make a thousand times at the height of his power, and walked out of the chamber between his guards, upright, unhurried, a man being filed away rather than destroyed—which was, she thought, watching the doors close behind him, the only punishment that would actually have hurt him, and therefore the only one worth giving.

The bell rang. Session closed. The gallery emptied slowly, in the murmuring manner of people who had come expecting blood and were leaving with something harder to digest.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor outside the chamber, with the light coming amber through the tall windows and her suspension formally lifted and her resignation formally filed in the same hour—an irony she suspected some Guild archivist would appreciate a century from now—Sefa stood at the window and looked down into the courtyard where, twelve years ago, she had watched a cart carry Cyrus Crane out through the west gate.

Footsteps. Not guards; she knew guard-steps. Wren came to stand beside her, arms crossed, prophecy-book-calloused fingers drumming her own elbow, looking out the same window with the expression of a woman who distrusted institutions on general principle and had attended this one only because Cyrus had asked her to, gently, and because someone ought to witness it who loved the person it had cost the most.

"Well?" Sefa said.

"He's at the market," Wren said. "Your—the ex-Councilor sent word? He's setting up a stall. Won't sell anything. Just—" she made a vague gesture, "—sits there. Lets people talk to him. Apparently half of Vell comes by to tell him their names. Like they're introducing themselves to the country." She glanced sideways. "He asked if you'd come."

Sefa looked out at the courtyard a moment longer—at the place where a cart had gone out a gate, and a girl in gray wool had stood and let it go, and called it duty for twelve years.

"Yes," she said. She pushed off the sill. "But not today. Today I'm going to the records hall, and I'm going to start pulling every file Voss ever touched, because the trial ended and the accounting hasn't, and somebody has to do it who isn't afraid of their own name." She paused at the corridor's turn, and something almost like a smile crossed her face—the first one in days, small and unpracticed and real. "Tell him Sunday. Tell him I'll bring bread. And tell him—" she stopped, and chose the words carefully, the way she was learning to choose everything now, "—tell him I have questions. About Ashkarin. About what it means to build a country back out of nothing. Because if Vell is going to learn how to remember, I intend to be one of the ones teaching it."

Wren studied her, then nodded once, slow, and dug in her satchel and pressed something into Sefa's hand before turning to go: a slip of paper, copied in a quick sure hand, bearing a line of the old Ashkarin script and its translation beneath.

*"What is remembered is never wholly lost."*

Sefa stood alone in the amber light until the footsteps faded, then folded the paper into her breast pocket, over her heart, and went to find the archives—and, somewhere past them, the beginning of whatever she was going to be.

Outside the tall windows, far to the east, a shape crossed the afternoon sun on wide wings, trailing a ribbon of gold fire against the blue, and the whole city stopped for a moment to watch it pass—shopkeepers and clerks and inquisitors alike, faces turned upward together, remembering.
