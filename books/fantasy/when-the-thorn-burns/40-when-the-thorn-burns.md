## 40. When the Thorn Burns

The ground where Voss built his pyre had never grown anything again.

Cyrus noticed it the first spring after the storm, walking home from the market with a loaf under one arm and ash still sifted into the cracks of the square's paving stones. The Guild had been dismantled by then—its seals pried off the walls, its archives opened to anyone who cared to read them—but the square itself kept its scar. A circle of gray nothing, maybe twelve feet across, where grass refused and pigeons wouldn't land and children, without being told why, simply walked around.

Everyone in Vell knew what had happened there. That was the point of it now. The city had decided, in its slow and stubborn way, that forgetting was a luxury it could no longer afford.

Cyrus stood at the edge of that dead circle for a long time, bread going cold under their arm, and thought: *I know something about coming back from nothing.*

They came back the next morning with a shovel.

* * *

Wren thought they were mad. She said so cheerfully, perched on the low wall of the apothecary across the square, watching Cyrus work the poisoned soil with a trowel and their bare hands.

"You're planting a *rosebush*. In *that*." She pointed with her chin at the gray circle. "It's not soil anymore, love. It's a grudge."

"So was I," Cyrus said, working the blade down into the crust. "Grudges can be turned."

"That's not how grudges work."

"It's how I work." They glanced up at her—the wind pulling at her hair, ink stains still on her fingers even though she hadn't touched a manuscript in three days, because she'd promised to spend the week doing nothing but sitting with him in the sun, and she took her promises with a scholar's precision. "Hand me the compost?"

She hopped off the wall and crouched beside them instead, sleeves pushed up, and dug in with her hands. Of course she did. Wren had never once in her life watched from a wall when she could be in the dirt.

The soil fought them for weeks. It was dense and sour and smelled of old smoke no rain could wash out. Bren would have had opinions about it—Bren would have muttered about fire needing to be answered with root, about how embers and gardens were the same argument—but Bren was two years gone now, laid to rest in the salt flats among the people he'd spent his whole bitter life pretending he didn't love, and Cyrus missed him most on mornings like this one, when the work was stubborn and a voice like gravel would have made it easier.

They planted the rosebush anyway. A cutting from the wild roses that grew along the old Ashkarin road, the ones that had bloomed every year through the decades when no one remembered what country they grew in. Sefa had brought it, of all people, arriving unannounced with the cutting wrapped in wet sacking and an expression that dared Cyrus to make something sentimental out of it.

"From the borderlands," she'd said. "The refugees' camps are dissolving—they're going home. Someone should carry something back that isn't grief."

Then she'd stood there awkwardly while Cyrus thanked her, and Wren had rescued everyone by putting tea in her hands, and for one strange golden hour the three of them had sat in Cyrus's small kitchen talking about nothing—road repairs, the price of salt, whether Yssara had been seen over the eastern ranges lately (she had; the dragon flew wider circuits every year, as if measuring how much world there was to relearn)—and then Sefa had left, and Cyrus had stood in the doorway watching her go, feeling the old ache but also something newer underneath it. Something like peace.

She'd chosen her life. He'd chosen his. Twelve years of silence had taught them both that love doesn't always mean keeping someone—it sometimes just means refusing to let them be erased.

He pressed the rosebush's roots into the ash and watered it and told no one what he whispered to it, except that Wren, who missed nothing, squeezed his hand that night and said, "It heard you."

* * *

Spring came late that year, the way springs had come since the Door. Late, but *on time*, which was different—which was everything. Before Yssara's fire returned to the sky, the seasons had been drifting like drunkards, summer sliding into autumn without announcement, frost in the grain months. Now the world kept its appointments. Farmers spoke of it the way sailors speak of fair winds: superstitiously, gratefully, knocking wood.

Cyrus woke on the first true morning of spring to find Wren already gone from bed and the smell of frying onions rising up the stairs, and lay there a moment in the warm rectangle of sunlight on the floor, taking inventory.

No storm under his skin. Two years since the Door, and he still checked some mornings, the way a man who has lost a tooth checks the gap. Nothing. Just the ordinary weather of a body—breath, pulse, the faint ache in his hands when rain was coming, which any old man had and which meant nothing at all. His scars had faded from storm-silver to plain white. When he caught sight of them now, washing dishes or lacing his boots, he felt neither pride nor grief. Just history. His own, finally, instead of a book's.

Downstairs, Wren had burned the onions, as she did every Sunday, and was defending the charred ones as "caramelized" with the same rigor she brought to disputed manuscripts, and Cyrus kissed the top of her head and ate them without complaint, because this too was a kind of wealth.

"The square today?" she asked.

"If it blooms."

"It'll bloom."

"You said that in February."

"I say a lot of things. Most of them turn out to have been right eventually. It's my best quality."

They walked to the square together through streets that had learned new habits. The charm-stalls were back—not hidden in night markets now but open in daylight, little tables of knotted thread and painted river-stones, half of them frauds and nobody minding, because Vell had decided that a little harmless magic-shaped hope was better than the fear that had eaten it hollow. A girl selling woven luck-birds called out a greeting to Cyrus by name. He waved. He was known here—not as the Thorn, not as the storm-caller, not as the face in the burned book, but as the man who'd planted the rosebush in the dead circle and talked to it like a fool, and whom the city had quietly decided to adopt.

That was all the legend he wanted. It had taken him forty years of living to learn that being known for small things was not a consolation prize. It was the prize.

The circle of ash waited where it always waited. And the rosebush—

Wren saw it first. She stopped mid-stride and grabbed his arm hard enough to bruise.

"Cyrus."

One bloom. Just one, low on the western branch, where the morning light hit first—a rose the color of banked coals, deep red shading to gold at the heart, open wide as if it had been waiting all night for someone to come look at it. Its roots ran down into twelve-year-old pyre-ash and the poisoned, grudge-holding dirt above a dead man's ambition, and it had bloomed anyway. Through all of it. Because of all of it, maybe. Bren would have had something insufferable to say about that.

Cyrus crouched before it and did not touch it. Some things you let be finished.

"Hello," he said softly.

Behind them, in the market noise and cart-rattle of an ordinary morning, people had begun to notice. Not a crowd—not that, not ever again—but faces turning, a hush spreading outward like the rings around a dropped stone. An old woman crossed herself in the fashion of the restored Ashkarin faith, which Ilyra's treason had nearly destroyed and which the survivors had rebuilt smaller and kinder, the way you rebuild anything worth keeping. A child asked her mother what the flower meant, and the mother, after a moment, said: "It means it's over."

Cyrus felt Wren's hand find his.

"It was never waiting for me to die," he said. Quietly. Only for her. "Was it? All those lines, all that fire and thorn and burning. It was only ever asking."

"No," Wren said. "I read it wrong for years. We all did. It wasn't a sentence, love. It was a question with teeth." She rested her head against his shoulder. "You're the answer. That's all the Thorn ever was."

* * *

They went home by the long way, along the canal, past the flooded temple that was now a public bath and schoolhouse, its drowned archive shelves repurposed for children's slates. At the door of their house—a plain house, two rooms and a garden, chosen because the roof leaked charmingly and the neighbor's cat had opinions—Wren stopped.

"Oh," she said. "I almost forgot. It came."

"What came?"

She went inside and returned with a package wrapped in oilcloth, travel-worn, smelling faintly of salt and dragon-warm air. No return address. There didn't need to be one.

Inside, nested in straw, was a book.

Cyrus knew it before he touched it. He would have known it blindfolded, by weight alone—the prophecy book, the Ashkarin codex, the thing that had started everything with a stranger buying a protection charm and a question about dinner. It had passed through a dozen hands since: Wren's archive, the Council's evidence room, Sefa's custody, and finally, by quiet agreement, into the keeping of the rebuilt temple, where scholars consulted it and pilgrims touched its cover and no one, anymore, was afraid of it.

Except that the temple had sent it back. With a note in Sefa's blunt handwriting:

*It keeps changing. Fewer words every year. The archivists think the binding is failing. I think you should see it before it finishes whatever it's doing. —S.*

They carried it into the garden, into the sun, and set it on the table between the bean rows, and opened it together.

The early pages were intact—the histories, the warnings, the terrible beautiful verse that had named a scarred-handed stranger the Thorn. But as they turned forward, the text grew thin. Words had lifted off the parchment like mist off morning water, leaving clean blank vellum behind. Page by page, chapter by chapter, the future the book had insisted upon was quietly erasing itself.

And the last page—the final line, the one Wren had deciphered in a flooded archive while the world forgot how to be warm, the line that had changed everything—was gone entirely.

Blank. Warm-white. Empty as unplanted ground.

Cyrus sat very still, looking at all that nothing.

"It's not dying," Wren said at last. Her scholar's voice, but soft. "Look at the edges. The vellum's healthier than it's been in a century. It's not fading, it's—" She laughed, a small wondering sound. "It's finishing. It was never meant to end. Prophecies aren't stories, they're questions, and once a question is answered properly, it puts itself away."

"So what happens to a prophecy book with no prophecy left?"

"Same thing that happens to a storm-caller with no storm." She reached across the table and took his scarred hand, turning it palm-up, tracing the faded white lines with one ink-stained finger. "It becomes available. For other things."

They sat in the garden a long time, the book open to its last blank page between them, the spring air moving through the beans, the neighbor's cat supervising from the wall with the gravity of a minor official. Somewhere east, over the mountains, a dragon was flying her widening circles, learning the shape of the world she'd saved. Somewhere south, a woman who had once chosen duty was learning, year by year, to choose herself instead. Somewhere in the salt flats, under a cairn of white stones, a drunk old keeper was finally resting, his coal long since flown home to the chest it belonged to.

And here—here there was a garden, and a blank page, and a woman laughing at her own burnt onions, and a season that had arrived exactly when it promised.

Cyrus picked up the pen Wren kept behind her ear and held it over the empty final page.

"What are you doing?" she asked.

"Thinking."

"Don't write your name. Books that start with names cause trouble. I've read several."

He smiled. Then, in careful letters—letters that were only ink, that cost nothing, that bound no one and doomed nothing—he wrote four words, and closed the book, and handed it back to her to return to the archive whenever she pleased, or never.

*Plant more roses here.*

The rosebush bloomed until the frost. And the next spring, there were three.
