## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The kitchens of Thornfell Castle woke before the king did, and Quentin Ashgrove woke before the kitchens.

By the hour when the coals were first raked and the scullery boys stumbled in rubbing sleep from their eyes, he was already at his station—a narrow oak table set apart from the great chopping blocks and the roaring ovens, close enough to watch every dish pass, far enough that no cook could brush against him by accident. His table had a single chair, a single spoon, a single cup of watered wine that he never drank. Everything else on it was the king's supper, arriving piece by piece like evidence at a trial.

Quentin had learned long ago to think of it that way. Every dish was testimony. Every sauce, a suspect.

This morning began with the porridge. He took up the silver spoon—silver, because certain compounds betrayed themselves on silver with a bruise-black bloom—and tasted it the way other men read letters: slowly, twice, attending to what was written between the lines. Oats from the southern valleys, cream from the dairy, honey, a whisper of salt. Underneath it all, faint as a footstep in another room, the mineral ghost of the copper pot it had been cooked in. Harmless. He swallowed, set down the spoon, and made a small mark in the ledger beside him: *porridge — clean — sixth bell.*

The ledger was his own invention. His predecessors had kept their knowledge in their heads, and their heads, more often than anyone liked to admit, ended up in baskets. Quentin trusted paper. Paper could be burned.

He worked through the morning in this fashion. Smoked eel, clean. Bread from the third oven—the second oven's flour had a sourness he didn't like, not poison but carelessness, and carelessness killed kings too, so he sent it back. A conserve of plums that sang with sugar and nothing else. A cheese so sharp it made his eyes water, which he noted with professional approval rather than personal suffering; his eyes had stopped doing anything for him personally years ago.

That was the thing no one understood about tasting. They thought it was about the tongue. It wasn't. It was about everything else—the nose reading the steam, the skin reading the air of the kitchen, the gut reading its own quiet counsel half a heartbeat before the mind caught up. A good taster didn't taste food. He interrogated it.

And Quentin Ashgrove was very good.

* * *

He had been eight years old when they brought him to the castle, one orphan among forty, pulled from the Foundling House on a day of cold rain because the Master of Tasters had come looking for children with no family to grieve them. He remembered the selection with odd clarity—not the fear, which had come later, but the taste of the bread they'd given each child, and how the boy next to him had spat his out, weeping, saying it tasted of iron, while Quentin chewed and swallowed and said it tasted like bread.

The bread had been laced with a preparation of foxglove. Mild, the Master said afterward. Educational.

Forty children. Thirty-one went home within the year—sent back, or buried quietly, depending on how you counted such things. Nine stayed. Of those nine, Quentin alone had never once been carried to the infirmary. Whatever ran through him seemed to run through him politely, taking notes and leaving without damage, and the old Master of Tasters—a dried-up man named Hobb who smelled permanently of vinegar—had looked at him once, long and strange, and said, "You're either blessed or built, boy, and I've never seen the gods build anything."

Quentin hadn't understood the remark then. He understood its shape now, though not its meaning, the way you understand the shape of a locked door.

The training itself he remembered in flavors. That was the cruelty of it—or the gift; he had never decided which. Most men remembered their youth in images. Quentin's childhood came back to him as a sequence of tastes: the green bitterness of hemlock water administered drop by drop until his body shrugged it off like rain off waxed cloth; the metallic crawl of lead, week upon week, until his gums stopped bleeding; arsenic, always arsenic, the schoolmaster of poisons, taught in doses small enough to hide inside a grain of rice. He had learned to read them all the way a scholar reads Latin—fluently, painfully, with total attention—and somewhere along the way the pain had become grammar, and the grammar had become simply who he was.

His body was a ledger now. That was the phrase he used, privately, when he allowed himself to think about it at all. Somewhere behind his ribs, an accounting was kept: what had entered, in what measure, and what it cost. The ledger balanced. It had always balanced. He sometimes wondered, in the hollow hour before sleep, whether anything else in his life ever would.

* * *

At the fifth bell the under-cooks began assembling the king's supper proper, and the kitchen shifted around Quentin like a tide changing. He tasted as the dishes came together rather than after, because a poison slipped into a finished dish could be slipped into the taster's portion too, and dead men make poor witnesses. So he stood among the steam and the shouting with his silver spoon and his ledger and read the supper as it wrote itself.

Roast capon glazed with quince. Clean. Parsnips buttered with nutmeg—here he paused, held the flavor on his tongue a moment longer than necessary, because nutmeg in quantity was no friend to a man's head—but no, this was the honest warmth of the spice and nothing crouched beneath it. Wine from the royal cellars, decanted in his presence: he swirled it, breathed it, wet his lips only, since the cellar-master drank first anyway and the man had a liver like a cathedral.

It was the sauce for the capon that stopped him.

He had taken a half-spoonful, as always, and begun the ordinary litany—wine, stock, quince, a thread of rosemary—and then, underneath, so fine it might have been imagination: bitter almond.

He stood very still.

Bitter almond was the perfume arsenic wore when it wished to be noticed. Most preparations stripped the note away entirely—that was the whole art of poisoning, concealment—but crude arsenic, hastily refined, left its signature the way a careless thief leaves boot-marks in flour. And there it was, or seemed to be, threading through the sweetness of the quince like a dark hair in milk.

Quentin set down the spoon. Took a breath. Took a second half-spoonful, larger, and gave the sauce his full and terrible attention.

Wine. Stock. Quince. Rosemary. And almond—yes, unmistakably, a bitterness like the memory of almonds, the way a scar is the memory of a wound.

But his body said otherwise.

That was the strangest part, the part he could never explain to anyone who hadn't lived inside his skin. The sauce lay on his tongue and told him one story, and the ledger behind his ribs told him another: *nothing here, nothing owed, nothing entered.* His body had been schooled in arsenic the way monks are schooled in silence. If there were enough in that sauce to trouble a mouse, let alone a king, some deep clerk of his flesh would have stirred, would have posted its notice. The clerk slept on.

So: either the almond note was a trick of the quince and the rosemary and his own tired senses—or it was real, and harmless in this quantity, and placed there to be found.

That last possibility turned his stomach colder than any poison ever had. Because a poison meant to be discovered was not an assassination. It was a message. And messages of that kind were never addressed to the king.

"Master Ashgrove?" One of the under-cooks, a round-faced boy called Pell, hovering with the sauceboat. "Is it—is something wrong?"

"No," Quentin said, and heard his own voice do what it always did: flatten into calm, the weariness pressed out of it like water from a cloth. "The quince is excellent. Tell Cook the rosemary is a touch heavy. Carry on."

Pell beamed and hurried away, relieved, and Quentin opened his ledger and wrote, in his small precise hand: *capon sauce — almond note — likely benign — quantity insufficient — source of almonds?*

He stared at the entry. Then he added, smaller: *ask.*

* * *

The asking took him through the afternoon, and the afternoon showed him nothing, and that was its own kind of answer. No almonds had entered the kitchen that day at all. The quince conserve had been made a fortnight past and sealed. The rosemary came from the garden bed everyone used. When he traced the sauce backward through the day's labor—stock, wine, reductions, the glaze—he found hands he knew, ordinary hands, working with the ordinary sloppiness of a hot kitchen and no shadow of malice in any of them.

Which left the almond note sitting in the middle of his certainty like a stone in a shoe. Either his tongue, after twenty-two years, had begun to lie to him—and men died of less—or someone had introduced a trace he couldn't trace, in a measure too fine to harm, in a place where only a taster would find it.

He washed his spoon. He dried it. He did both things twice, which was habit and also prayer.

* * *

Evening brought the feast.

Not for the king alone tonight—the court dined in state, the long hall blazing with two hundred candles, ambassadors from the coastal principalities seated along the high tables, and the kitchens below running like a mill in flood. Quentin's work on feast nights was different in texture if not in kind: more dishes, faster tasting, and a hall full of powerful people above him whose quarrels seasoned every plate that passed through his hands.

He tasted his way up the service. Consommé, clean. Pike in green sauce, clean—though he flagged the sorrel, which had a dusty edge he didn't trust and later traced to a damp sack, nothing worse. Venison, wine, marchpane subtleties shaped like the kingdom's heraldic stag, each one broken and tasted and marked. The work steadied him. Work always steadied him. There was a simplicity to it that his life otherwise lacked: here is the food, here is my mouth, here is the truth between them. If only everything could be settled so directly.

It was during the third remove, standing at his table with the venison still warm on his tongue, that he felt the hall change.

Not saw—felt. A taster learns to read rooms the way he reads sauces, and the room's flavor had shifted: a stirring at the lower doors, servants pressing to look, guards exchanging words. Quentin glanced up from his ledger and saw the cause being carried in on a litter of cloaks—a man, ragged, mud to the thighs, snow in his hair though the passes were weeks yet from closing. Some courier off the mountain roads, by his look, half-dead already and mad with whatever had driven him down out of the fells in that season.

They shouldn't have let him into the hall. But feasts made fools of gatekeepers, and the man was waving something—papers, or a case—and crying out words that the distance ate before they reached the dais, and for a moment the whole glittering machinery of the court paused to watch, the way a household pauses over a rat that has gotten onto the banquet floor.

Then the courier collapsed.

Quentin was moving before he had decided to move. Later he would tell himself it was duty—the taster's oath extended to any soul poisoned within the king's walls, though no one had ever spoken such an oath aloud. Later still, in the bad hours, he would know better. What drove him under the tables that night, crawling through candle-grease and dropped bread while trumpets faltered above, was not duty. Duty is what fear calls itself when it wants to keep its job.

The man lay crumpled between a bench and the wall, and even before Quentin reached him he could smell it—that smell again, fainter than memory, sweeter than it should be. Bitter almond. Bitter almond, in a hall of two hundred candles, rising off a dying stranger like breath.

"I'm a taster," Quentin said, low, taking the man's wrist, feeling the pulse stutter like a moth against glass. "I can—I know what this is. Who gave it to you?"

The courier's eyes found him. They were gray eyes, going glassy at the edges, and they did the worst possible thing: they focused. They looked at Quentin's face with recognition—with *purpose*—the way a man looks at a door he has walked a hundred miles to reach.

"You—" the man breathed. His hand moved, clawing at the front of his own ruined coat, and Quentin's fingers met his and were pressed together around something small and hard: a tube of oiled leather, sealed at the end with dripping black wax, warm from the man's chest. "Vaelmoor," the courier whispered, and the word went into Quentin like a splinter, familiar and foreign at once, a word he had never heard and somehow already knew. "Take it. You're the—the blood is—" 

The rest went out of him. Not dramatically, not with a last grasping gesture; he simply stopped, mid-word, the way a clock stops, and the gray eyes fixed on Quentin and stayed fixed, and the noise of the feast came flooding back in overhead like water rushing into a emptied hole.

Quentin knelt there under the table with the tube clenched in his fist and the dead man's hand slack around his, and he did the only thing his training had ever truly prepared him for. He became invisible. He folded the tube into his sleeve, closed the courier's eyes with two fingers, rose, straightened his coat, and stepped back out into the light wearing the face he wore every day of his life: weary, competent, unremarkable, a man whose whole self was a tool for detecting danger and therefore, by universal agreement, of no interest whatsoever.

Above, the court was deciding the courier had been a beggar, a madman, a smuggler's runner dead of cold and drink. The feast resumed. Trumpets. Marchpane. The king laughed at something an ambassador said.

Quentin returned to his table and sat, and picked up his spoon, and tasted the next dish with hands that did not tremble, because his hands never trembled, because trembling was a luxury his constitution had been denied along with everything else. *Venison — clean,* he wrote in the ledger. His handwriting did not waver either.

Under his sleeve, against the pulse-point of his wrist, the little tube ticked with his heartbeat like a second heart. *Vaelmoor.* He turned the word over once, carefully, the way he turned an unknown flavor on his tongue—and found, beneath the strangeness, that same impossible familiarity. As if somewhere in the ledger of him, in a column he had never been permitted to read, an entry had been waiting all these years for exactly this word.

Around him the feast blazed on, and the bitter-almond ghost hung in the candle-smoke, and Quentin Ashgrove, invisible man, keeper of the king's tongue, understood with perfect clarity that his life had just changed flavor forever—

—and that whatever had been written into his body long ago, he was finally about to learn who had done the writing.
