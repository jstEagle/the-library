## 2. A Stranger at the Table

The king's feast for the Ambermark envoys began at sundown and, like all royal feasts, it was a slow-motion assassination attempt in which everyone pretended otherwise.

Quentin Ashgrove stood at the service door with his back against the warm oak and watched the great hall the way other men watched a battlefield. Two hundred candles. Forty courses planned, thirty-seven likely to survive the kitchens intact. The Ambermark delegation sat on the king's left, eleven men in grey-and-gold silk who smiled too easily and drank too little—a detail Quentin noted and filed away, because guests who didn't drink were guests who intended to remember everything.

His own table was modest: a small sideboard near the dais, set apart by a velvet rope no one ever crossed, bearing silver dishes under covers of hammered gold. On each cover, an embossed crest. On each dish, a portion of whatever the king was about to eat. It had taken years for Quentin to stop flinching at the first spoonful of every course. He hadn't stopped listening to his body while he swallowed.

"Roast capon, saffron glaze," murmured Cook Brammel, sliding the fourth cover into place. "Same bird as His Majesty's. Same pan drippings, same salt cellar." He said it every time, the litany of sameness, as if repetition itself could keep them both alive.

"Same knife used on both?" Quentin asked, out of ritual rather than suspicion. Brammel's face did its usual wounded dance. "Same knife, Master Ashgrove."

"I know. I have to ask anyway."

He lifted the cover. The capon smelled of honey and crocus threads, rich enough to make a starving man weep. Quentin cut a sliver of breast meat, a curl of skin glazed amber, and put it on his tongue.

Bitterness—saffron, faintly soapy, normal. Sweetness—the honey, burnt slightly at the edges, normal. Salt—yes. Fat—yes. And beneath it all, threading through the back of the throat a half-breath after swallowing, something else. Not bitter exactly. More like the memory of bitterness, a ghost note that lived behind the soft palate where most tongues never bothered to look.

Quentin chewed slowly. Swallowed again on the same mouthful, deliberately, to see if the ghost repeated.

It did. Fainter. Almond that wasn't almond. Cold metal that wasn't metal.

*Hemlock extract,* he thought, *cut with something to mask it. Foxglove honey, maybe, or that Thessian resin the guilds call widow's sugar.* Low dose. Not enough to drop the king tonight—that wasn't the design. Enough to build. Someone had been seasoning the royal kitchens for weeks, a grain at a time, patient as frost.

He set down the fork with hands that did not shake, because they never shook anymore, and that absence of shaking frightened him more than any tremor could.

"Brammel. The saffron. Where was it purchased?"

"The Spicewright's Guild, sealed tin, third of this month. Why? Something wrong?"

"No." He smiled the smile that was part of his uniform. "It's excellent. Send up the next course."

He would taste everything twice tonight. Three times for anything with sauce. And tomorrow, quietly, without telling anyone whose ears might be royal or otherwise, he would walk through the kitchen stores with a candle and a mortar and find which sack of grain, which jar of oil, which innocent-looking crocus tin had been doctored. This was his real work—not the theater of tasting before the court, but the slow forensic reading of a household that wanted its king dead and couldn't agree among itself about how.

A man's body as a ledger, his old master Vessik had called it. Every poison entered is a debt recorded. You are worth exactly what you've survived.

Vessik had died of a bad heart at sixty-two, which everyone agreed proved nothing either way.

* * *

By the second hour, the hall had reached the loud, golden stage of feasting where wine does the talking. The Ambermark envoys had loosened—one of them, a young man with a scholar's ink-stained fingers, was laughing openly at the fool's juggling—and King Aldous was holding forth about the border levies with the expansive generosity of a monarch who has been told his wine is excellent all evening.

Quentin tasted the venison pie (clean), the candied quinces (clean, though the sugar was stretched with something chalky—kitchen economy, not murder), and the mulled perry (a whisper of wormwood that made him pause, then decide it was the cook showing off). He was beginning to believe the hemlock thread was the only needle in tonight's haystack when the doors at the far end opened and the stranger came in.

Later, Quentin would understand that the man should never have gotten past the guards, and that the fact he did was itself a message. But in the moment he saw only what everyone saw: a courier, road-worn, mud to the knee, a leather dispatch satchel banging against a ribs too prominent for his coat. One of the under-stewards moved to intercept him. The courier raised a hand—some token, some pass-word—and the steward, to Quentin's distant surprise, stepped aside.

The hall barely noticed. Feasts swallow strangers whole. The courier walked the length of the room with the stiff, deliberate gait of a man who has decided precisely how far he needs to go and will spend everything he has getting there. He passed the envoys. Passed the lords. Stopped at the rail below the dais, where a servant offered him wine from a tray, because that was what servants did with travelers who got this far.

Quentin was already moving.

He couldn't have said what triggered it—later he'd assemble the evidence piece by piece, the way he assembled every diagnosis: the gray-green tint under the man's skin that candlelight alone doesn't explain; the sweat beading at the hairline in a cold hall; the way his free hand pressed flat against his stomach as though holding a lid on something; above all the smell, faint but unmistakable to a nose trained since childhood, of crushed peach kernels and old pennies. Cyanide, the crude kind, the kind that kills in minutes—or, cut thin enough, kills slowly enough for a man to finish a message.

But in the moment it wasn't deduction. It was the animal certainty of one poisoned body recognizing another.

"Water," Quentin said, taking the wine tray from the servant's hands before anyone could object. "Not wine. Water, cold, and don't let him drink until I say."

"My lord taster—" the steward began.

"He's not drunk. Look at his eyes."

The courier's eyes were pinned wide, pupils shrunk to pinheads, fixed on Quentin with terrible focus—as if Quentin were the destination the whole long journey had been aimed at. His lips were moving. No sound came out.

Then his legs went.

He folded like wet canvas, and the hall's roar stuttered into silence as forty heads turned. Somewhere a woman gasped. The king rose half out of his chair. And Quentin did the thing that would be reported, retold, and eventually used against him: he dropped to his knees and crawled under the banquet table, velvet and oak and the forest of legs of the mighty, toward the dying man sprawled on the rushes.

Because the floor was the fastest route, yes. Because crawling under the table put Quentin's body between the collapse and the king, which was, technically, his duty. Both true. But underneath both truths lay the simpler one: the courier was looking at *him*, and dying men who look at you like that have something to give, and Quentin Ashgrove had spent thirty-one years being invisible in rooms full of powerful people and knew better than anyone how much can change hands beneath a table.

He reached the man. Up close the peach-kernel smell was strong enough to taste. Quentin cradled the courier's head off the rushes—lice, road dust, blood-warm fever—and held water to his lips. The man's throat worked once, twice.

"Don't talk," Quentin said. "Save it."

"No time." The voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "No... they're in the hall. Watching. Don't look. *Don't look.*"

Quentin kept his eyes on the courier's face and let his other senses do the looking. Under the table around them: boots. Dozens, shifting, the restless shuffle of courtiers craning to see. One pair near the dais steps had gone very still—the stillness of a man standing at attention, not watching a spectacle. A guard. Or someone wearing the posture of one.

"They'll ask what I gave you," the courier whispered. Blood was threading pink through the spit at the corner of his mouth. "Tell them nothing. Tell them... bread. Say I ate bad bread on the road. Promise me."

"You're not going to die here," Quentin said, and heard the lie land hollow between them.

The courier almost laughed; it came out as a cough that shook his whole frame. "Taster," he rasped. "They told me. Find the taster. Only the taster." His hand clawed weakly at the dispatch satchel, and Quentin understood and tore the buckle open himself.

Inside: papers, official-looking, seals of the Royal Post. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a tube of dark wood no longer than Quentin's forearm, capped at both ends with wax. Black wax. Sealed with a sigil Quentin had never seen and yet recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a dream: a thorned circle, broken open at the bottom, with something falling through the gap. Ash, maybe. Or seed.

"Take it." The courier's grip closed over Quentin's wrist with shocking strength, strength borrowed from somewhere death hadn't reached yet. "Not the papers—the papers are chaff, burn them, let them burn them. The tube. It goes south. Over the Thornfell passes. There's a man—" A spasm took him; his jaw clenched; when it released he was quieter, faster, spending words like a man counting coins. "There's a place that isn't on maps. They erased it. Erased the people. My grandmother's people. I carried this for nine years because no one else would take it and now my luck's run out and it's yours, taster, God forgive me, it's yours."

"What is it?" Quentin whispered.

The courier's eyes drifted past Quentin's shoulder, to the still boots by the dais, and came back. He pressed the tube into Quentin's hand and folded Quentin's fingers over it, both his hands now, sealing it as firmly as the black wax had sealed the ends.

"*Vaelmoor*," he breathed.

The word went into Quentin like a splinter going into flesh—small, deep, and instantly impossible to ignore. He searched his memory and found nothing. No treaty, no history lesson, no drunken geographer's tale. A country-shaped hole where a word should have context.

"It's yours," the courier said again. "You're the only one it can't—"

He stopped. Not dramatically. His hands simply lost their argument, slid off Quentin's wrist, and lay palm-up on the rushes. The pinned eyes found the ceiling, the candles, something beyond both, and stayed there.

Under the table, in the shadow of kings, Quentin knelt holding a dead man's warmth in his palms and a wooden tube against his chest, and did not move.

* * *

What followed was theater, and Quentin performed his role well, because terror disguised itself in him as duty and always had.

He shouted for the physician. He turned the body with practiced gentleness, announced the obvious—"poison, taken on the road, hours ago, nothing served here"—before anyone could suggest otherwise, and planted the bread story with the smoothness of long rehearsal: the man had stumbled in half-dead, raving of bad provisions, a roadside loaf, such things happened on the northern post roads every winter. The under-steward confirmed the courier had presented valid tokens at the door. The captain of the guard confirmed the tokens were genuine Royal Post issue. The physician confirmed death by some acrid poison, probably self-administered by accident, poor wretch, couriers were careless men.

Nobody questioned why a food-taster had crawled under the table. That was, after all, his function—to put his life between the crown and contamination, even retroactively, even uselessly. Lord Chamberlain Hesse actually praised him for his quickness, clapping Quentin on the shoulder with a hand heavy as a ham. The king, pale but composed, ordered the feast to continue, because kings must, and the hall resumed its roaring as servants wrapped the corpse in a spare banner and carried it out through the same doors it had come in.

Only two people in the hall behaved strangely, as far as Quentin could tell, and one of them was himself.

The other stood among the guards along the east wall: a lean man in palace livery he wore like a costume, who had not moved during the entire commotion and had not looked at the corpse even once. He was looking at Quentin. When their eyes met—he made sure they met—he inclined his head a fraction, courteous as a chess opponent acknowledging a decent opening, and returned his attention to the hall.

Quentin spent the rest of the evening tasting dishes he no longer registered, smiling at cooks, praising wines, and keeping the tube pressed flat against his belly beneath his belt, hidden by apron and tunic, warm from his own skin. Nine years the courier had carried it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than anything Quentin had ever held.

Once—only once—he allowed his hand to brush the end cap through the cloth. The wax was unbroken. Whatever was inside had waited nine years in the dark, and it could wait a few hours more.

He repeated this to himself all night, like a prayer with a crack in it.

* * *

He did not go straight home.

That was the discipline: a man carrying contraband walks home the way he walks home every night, same streets, same pace, same tired slouch, and stops at the same places. So Quentin bought oysters from the girl at the Fiddler's Stair, because he bought oysters every feast night; argued amiably with the fishmonger about the price of eels, because he always argued; and stood a full minute outside the Lamplighters' Rest listening to the fiddle inside, because he always stood a full minute deciding whether he could afford a cup of their overpriced brown ale, and always went in, and always could afford it.

Tonight he went in. Tonight he even drank the ale, though his tongue catalogued it automatically—tannin, smoke, a sourness at the finish that meant the barrel had turned—and though every swallow felt like pouring water on a fire he needed banked, not fed.

Two men at the corner table wore traveling cloaks indoors. Neither spoke. Quentin memorized their faces the way he memorized flavors, without effort, without wanting to, and left his coin on the board, and went home through streets gone blue with midnight.

His garret sat above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, three narrow stairs and a door that locked with a latch he'd replaced himself after the third burglary attempt. He bolted it. He lit one candle, shielded it behind a book, and unwrapped the tube.

Oilcloth, then linen, then bare wood dark as old blood, dense in the hand—ironwood, or something heavier. The black wax at each end bore the broken thorn-circle, pressed deep by a seal ring worn round with use. He turned it under the candlelight. No hinges, no seams visible. Whatever mechanism hid inside had been made by someone who did not want it found.

He should burn it.

The thought arrived calm and reasonable, dressed in all his best survival instincts. A courier poisoned within arm's reach of the throne. A watcher in the guard who acknowledged him like a colleague. A name—*Vaelmoor*—that existed nowhere in any record Quentin could summon, which meant either madness or erasure, and erasure was a thing done *by* power, and power did not erase countries for reasons that left bystanders alive.

Put it in the stove. Melt the wax. Let whatever secret slept in the ironwood sleep forever, and go back to tasting capon and counting poisons, invisible and therefore immortal.

Quentin sat with the tube across his knees for a long time. The candle guttered. Below him the chandler's shop ticked and settled, and somewhere down the lane a drunk sang the same four notes of a ballad over and over, forgetting the rest.

He thought about the courier's hands folding his fingers shut over the wood. *You're the only one it can't—* Can't what? Can't hurt? Can't read? Can't kill? The sentence had died unfinished, and unfinished sentences were worse than threats, because threats ended.

He thought, too—and here was the shameful thought, the one he'd deny under torture, the one that proved Vessik right about ledgers—about how many years he had swallowed other people's poisons and recorded the debts in his own flesh, anonymous, essential, expendable. A man whose entire value lay in what he could absorb without changing. Thirty-one years old, and his body a ledger no one audited, and his heart a room no one visited.

And here, in his lap, sat the first thing in his life addressed specifically to him. *Find the taster. Only the taster.*

"Damn you," Quentin said quietly, to the courier, to himself, to whoever was watching even now in the dark between the candle flame and the window glass.

He did not burn it.

Instead he fetched his knife—the plain one, not the ceremonial blade—and with the care of a man defusing the thing that would kill him, worked the point beneath the edge of the lower seal. The black wax cracked in a clean line, all the way around, precisely scored in advance by whoever had made it, so that it would break *only* for someone who knew where to cut.

Inside the tube, rolled tight, was paper. And the paper, when he eased it out with two fingers, was covered edge to edge in fine ink: mountains rendered in hatch-strokes, rivers in flowing lines, roads marked in red—roads Quentin knew, smuggler roads, roads he had run as a young runner before his tongue earned him a place at court—and then, at the center of the map, in the white blank space where the Thornfell massif should have been, a single symbol drawn larger than all the rest.

The thorned circle, broken open. With ash falling through the gap.

Below it, in a cramped antique script, two words:

*THE DOOR.*

Down in the lane, the drunk had finally remembered the rest of his ballad, and the song floated up through the floorboards, sweet and stupid and oblivious. Quentin stared at the map until the candle burned down to a stub, and did not hear the footsteps on the stair until they stopped, softly, outside his door.

Three floors below, the chandler's dog began to growl.
