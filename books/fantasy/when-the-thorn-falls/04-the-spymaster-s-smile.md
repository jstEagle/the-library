## 4. The Spymaster's Smile

The summons came at dawn, which was the first wrong thing.

Royal business kept courtly hours. Summons came by mid-morning, delivered by a page with chapped hands and a bored voice. But this one arrived while the kitchen fires were still being lit, carried by a man in grey who did not chafe his hands against the cold, did not stamp his boots in the doorway, did not look at anything in the room except Quentin's face.

"His Lordship Corvane requests the taster's presence," the man said. "At his convenience."

Which meant immediately. Everyone knew what *at his convenience* meant when Lord Corvane said it.

Quentin set down the knife he'd been using to trim winter apples and wiped his hands on his apron with the deliberate slowness of a man who had nothing to fear. He'd learned that trick early—fear moved fast through a body trained to read it, and the fastest way to keep your own fear quiet was to make every gesture slow enough to think about first.

"Tell him I'll come directly," Quentin said. "After I've tasted the morning broth."

"He said not to wait for the broth."

There it was. Quentin untied his apron, hung it on its hook, and followed the grey man out into the blue dark of the courtyard, where the frost lay thick as spilled sugar on the flagstones. Somewhere above, in the royal apartments, the king was sleeping off last night's feast—and somewhere under that sleep, in the king's blood, was everything Quentin had swallowed for him over nine years: the arsenic in the wedding wine, the hemlock in the ambassador's honey-cakes, the slow lead of the guttered candle-wax some clever servant had shaved into the evening posset three winters back. Quentin had drunk it all so the king wouldn't have to. His body was a ledger, and every entry had been made in someone else's stead.

He had thought himself invisible. He had spent nine years making himself invisible—the plain man in the corner of the kitchens, the one whose name half the court couldn't recall. And now a stranger had died at his table with a word pressed into his palm like a coin, and invisibility, it turned out, was a garment you could be stripped of overnight.

*Vaelmoor.*

He hadn't spoken the word aloud since. He hadn't opened the tube again after that first night; he'd resealed the wax with a candle flame and a steady hand and pushed it deep behind the loose brick in his garret wall where he kept the small savings of an unambitious life. But the word sat in him like a swallowed stone. He could feel its weight whenever he bent forward.

The grey man led him not to the council chambers, where the spymaster's official work was done, but across the inner ward to a low door Quentin had passed a hundred times without ever seeing—a door in the shadow of the old armoury, unmarked, iron-banded, worn smooth at the handle by hands that had all gone through it the way Quentin was going through it now.

Inside was warmth, lamplight, and the smell of good tea.

That was the second wrong thing. Quentin had expected stone and cold and the creak of the rack in the cellar below. Instead Lord Corvane received him in a comfortable study lined with books, seated behind a desk of pale birchwood, pouring a second cup from a brown ceramic pot as though Quentin were an old friend arriving slightly late for breakfast.

"Taster Ashgrove." Corvane's smile was famous—or infamous, depending on how much of the court's gossip a man allowed himself to hear. It was a wide, warm smile that reached all the way to his eyes, and it never once changed shape, the way a painted smile doesn't change. "Sit. You'll take tea?"

"Thank you, my lord."

"Don't thank me yet." Corvane pushed the cup across the desk. "You'll want to taste it first, I imagine. Force of habit."

Quentin looked at the cup. It was a test, obviously. Everything in this room would be a test, from the tea to the chairs to the angle of the light, and the only way through a room built entirely of tests was to stop pretending otherwise. He lifted the cup, breathed over it the way he'd been taught at fourteen—nose first, always nose first—and sipped.

"Good leaf," he said. "Second flush. Kept too near the spice shelf—it's taken a ghost of cinnamon. Brewed a shade long, but not enough to bite. No poison in it, my lord, if that's the question."

Corvane laughed. It was a genuine laugh, or a perfect forgery of one. "Gods, they told me you were good, but they undersold you. The cinnamon—I put the canister beside the tin myself two days ago just to see. Nobody else has caught it." He settled back, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat. He was a compact man, silver at the temples, dressed well but not richly, the way a fisherman dresses like a fisherman even on the day he's going to sell the boat. "You've been tasting for the crown nine years, yes?"

"Nine years this midwinter, my lord."

"And before that?"

"The foundling kitchen at Saint Verity's. Then apprentice under Master Hollen, gods rest him. Then here."

"No family?"

"None living."

"No debts, no vices beyond cheap tobacco, no lovers worth mentioning, no friends who'd notice if you stopped coming home?" Corvane recited it lightly, like a man reading a shopping list. "You see, I make it my business to know everyone in this palace, Taster Ashgrove, and you are one of the easiest men in it to know. You are a very simple creature. You rise, you taste, you sleep, you save your coin, and once a month you walk to the river market and buy apples because the ones from the palace orchard bruise too easily and it bothers you. Am I close?"

"You've wasted effort on me, my lord," Quentin said mildly. "I'm the least interesting thing in this building."

"Oh, I don't agree." Corvane rose and wandered to the window, cup in hand, looking out at the frosted ward. Behind him, Quentin noticed—for the first time, and hated himself for noticing it third instead of first—that the room had a second door, half-hidden behind a bookshelf, standing slightly ajar. "I find you fascinating. Consider your profession. Every night, a man sits down to eat, and the difference between his dinner and his death is *you*. One mouthful, held on the tongue, counted slowly. What a strange, lonely sacrament that must be. Do you say a prayer over it? Some of the old tasters used to."

"I count," Quentin said. "Thirty heartbeats. Then I swallow."

"And if the poison is slow?"

"Then I die politely, my lord, sometime after the king has eaten safely elsewhere."

Corvane turned, and the lamplight caught his eyes, and for just a moment the smile was gone—not replaced by anger or menace, simply absent, the way a lamp goes out, leaving the face beneath it visible for the first time. What was underneath was patient and cold and enormously tired, the face of a man who had read too many confessions to be surprised by any of them.

"Last night," Corvane said, "a courier named Aldous Ferrin died at the feast. Collapsed at the third remove from the salt. You were serving that table."

"I was tasting that table, my lord. Different work."

"Is it?" Corvane tilted his head. "Walk me through it. What did you observe?"

Quentin counted thirty heartbeats. Then he spoke, and every word came out slow enough to think about first.

"A man eating too fast for a feast. Couriers do—they're paid by the mile, not the meal, and they forget how to sit. He went grey around the mouth first. Then his hand went to the table edge, not his throat, which told me he knew what was happening to him and was trying not to make a scene of it. He came down hard on his left side. By the time I reached him—" Quentin paused. This was the hinge, the door Corvane wanted him to walk through, and he could feel the spymaster's attention settle onto him like a hand on the shoulder. "By the time I reached him, he was past helping. I checked for breath. There was none. I closed his eyes and stepped back so the physicians could work."

"That's all you did."

"I held his head off the floor so he wouldn't strike it. It was the only decent thing available."

"Mm." Corvane drank his tea. "And in all that kneeling at his side, holding his head, closing his eyes—in all that, he said nothing to you. Gave you nothing. Passed you nothing."

*Here it is.* Quentin let his brow crease, let a little honest puzzlement into his face—the puzzle of a simple man confronted with an insinuation too large to fit in his head. "Passed me nothing, my lord. Though I confess if a dying man had pressed state secrets into my hand, I'd hardly announce it in this room."

Corvane smiled again, and the smile was somehow worse than its absence. "Honesty. Good. I get so little of it." He returned to the desk and drew a sheet of paper toward himself without looking at it. "Let me tell you what we know about Aldous Ferrin, since you've been so cooperative. He ran dispatches on the northern circuit for eleven years. Reliable, unimaginative, fond of drink and horses in that order. Three weeks ago he requested a change of route—one specific detour through the Thornfell foothills, nowhere he had any delivery scheduled. He took no dispatch case on that detour. He came back with empty saddlebags and something in his manner that made his own stable-master ask if he'd seen a ghost. Two days later he requests assignment to a palace feast—unheard of for a courier, they eat in the barracks—and spends the evening working his way toward the service tables. Toward *your* table, Taster Ashgrove. And then he dies with his lips moving, saying a word the physician swears sounded like *vale* or *veil* or—"

"*Vaelmoor*," Quentin said.

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. It pressed down on the teacups and the bookshelves and the half-hidden door, and Quentin understood that he had just done either the bravest or the stupidest thing of his life, and that both felt identical from the inside.

Corvane set down his cup with a small, precise click. "Say that again."

"I heard it, my lord. As he died. A word I didn't know." Quentin met the spymaster's eyes and lied with the whole flat calm of his profession—which is to say, he told the truth with one crucial piece carved out of its center, the way a cook carves out a stone. "It frightened me. A dead man's last word should frighten anyone. So I asked around, quietly, and the oldest servants say it's a story grandmothers tell—a country in the mountains that got erased from the treaties, back before anyone living. A fairy tale. That's all I know. That's everything, my lord, on my life."

"On your life," Corvane repeated softly. "You know, that's the one oath in this palace I actually respect. A taster who lies about poison will die of it eventually. Nature keeps her own accounts." He studied Quentin for a long moment, and Quentin let himself be studied, keeping his breathing even, keeping his hands loose on his knees, counting heartbeats. "Here is what troubles me, Ashgrove. A courier takes a secret detour. Comes back changed. Dies at *your* table, whispering a word that belongs to a country that does not exist. And within a day of his death, my riders report that a man matching your description visited the records office and asked—politely, forgettably, exactly the way you ask everything—after treaty archives from the reign of the grandfather-king. The clerk barely remembered you. Clerks never remember the invisible ones. But I remember everyone."

Quentin's stomach performed a slow, silent revolution. He had gone to the archives. Of course he had—he'd gone the morning after opening the tube, telling himself it was diligence, telling himself a taster owed the crown thoroughness, and he had been so careful, so forgettable. He hadn't imagined there was a mind in the palace that collected forgettable things.

"My lord," he said, "the man died at my table. Under my watch. In nine years, no one has died at one of my tables. I wanted to know if I'd failed. If there'd been poison in the food I cleared and missed, then every death that follows is mine. I asked after the treaty archives because the oldest servants mentioned the old wars, and I thought—if he was poisoned, someone might have used an old poison, something from before the current pharmacopoeia, something my training wouldn't recognize." He spread his hands, palms up: the gesture of a man with nothing in them. "I am a food-taster, Lord Corvane. My whole art is knowing things by their taste and smell. A dead man whispers a word I don't know—so I go looking for its flavor. That's all. That's the entire conspiracy. A frightened man consulting a library."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered across Corvane's face that looked almost like disappointment. Or perhaps it was relief. With this man, Quentin suspected, the two were indistinguishable.

"Well," Corvane said. "That is the most reasonable explanation I've heard in months, and I hate it, because reasonable explanations are how guilty men survive and innocent men hang. Do you know what I actually believe, Taster Ashgrove? I believe you're telling me the truth as far as it goes, and I believe it doesn't go nearly far enough. Someone fed Ferrin a secret, and secrets don't die with their couriers—they *pool*, like blood, wherever the body falls. Ferrin fell at your feet." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "So here is what happens next. Nothing. You go back to your kitchens. You taste the king's supper tonight as you always do, and tomorrow, and the day after. And I will be watching—not you, necessarily, but the space around you, the shape of the hole where a secret pools. If nothing moves in that space, we need never have this conversation again, and I will pour you tea myself. But if something moves, Ashgrove—if a single stone turns over anywhere in this city that connects to you—then you and I will meet again, and it will not be over tea."

"I understand, my lord."

"Do you?" Corvane's smile returned, warm and wide and painted-on. "Because here is the thing about doors, Ashgrove. Once a man knows a door exists, he starts seeing hinges everywhere." He gestured pleasantly toward the main door. "Thank you for coming at such an hour. Give my regards to the head cook. Tell her her venison marinade needs juniper, not bay—she'll know who told you."

Quentin stood, bowed the exact correct amount, and walked to the door on legs that felt borrowed. His hand was on the iron ring when Corvane spoke again, casually, from behind him, not even turning from the window.

"One more thing. The tube Ferrin carried. Wax-sealed, a hand's length, official dispatch make. It wasn't found on the body. It wasn't found anywhere in the hall. Things like that don't evaporate, Ashgrove. They get *held*. And whoever held it is carrying something worth killing for, whether they know it yet or not." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "If it surfaces, bring it to me, and this conversation becomes a pleasant memory. If I find it first—" The smile entered his voice like smoke. "Well. We'll cross that bridge when it burns."

Quentin walked back across the frosted courtyard with the sun coming up red behind the eastern towers, and the cold on his face was the only honest thing left in the world.

In the kitchens, the morning broth was waiting. He tasted it the way he always did—nose first, tongue second, thirty heartbeats—and pronounced it safe, and watched the king's breakfast go up the stairs, and stood afterward among the clatter and steam with his hands perfectly still.

They would search his garret. Not today, perhaps, maybe not this week—but Corvane was a man who counted heartbeats too, and the brick behind his bed was a door with hinges showing. Tonight, then. Tonight he would take the map out of the wall and carry it somewhere no warrant would look, sewn into the lining of the coat he never wore, or buried in the ash-bin, or—

No. Not buried. Not hidden at all.

Because Corvane had made one mistake, in all that bottomless politeness. He had said *if something moves.* He had assumed Quentin would stand still and wait to see which way the hunt ran.

But Quentin had spent nine years learning the single most important lesson a taster knows: by the time you taste the poison, it is already too late. Survival lives in the hours before—the watching, the smelling, the walking away from the table while everyone else raises their cups.

The courier had died pointing north. Corvane was watching south, east, west, and the space around Quentin's life.

Which meant the road through the Thornfell fells was open exactly as long as it took him to pack a bag.

* * *

That night, Quentin pried loose the brick, wrapped the sealed tube in oilcloth, and slid it into the hollow handle of his father's—of the foundling kitchen's—carving knife, the one tool no searcher would ever take from a taster's kit. He packed spare wool, dried apples, his savings in a belt pouch, and the small vial of bitter tonic he brewed for himself each season, the one whose recipe Master Hollen had written in a hand that shook.

He looked once around the garret—at the thin bed, the single window, the nine years of careful, invisible life stacked neatly in a space the size of a coffin.

"Sorry, Aldous," he murmured to the dark. "Looks like I'm going to find out what the word means after all."

He went down the back stairs at midnight, past the sleeping kitchens, out through the gate where the night porter knew him and didn't look up, and turned his face toward the mountains and the first real fear of his life—which was also, though he could not yet have said so, the first free breath of it.

Behind him, high in the armoury's shadow, a lamp in a low-windowed study burned on, patiently, until dawn.
