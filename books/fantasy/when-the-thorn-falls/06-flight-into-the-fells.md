## 6. Flight into the Fells

Quentin had planned his escape around a supper.

It was, he thought wryly as he packed by candlelight, the only kind of plan a taster could trust—one built on the certainty that the king would eat, and eat well, and demand his usual third cup of mulled wine afterward, and that for the length of that meal every eye in the palace kitchens would be on the roasting spit and not on the narrow man with the pale tongue who stood in the corner tasting everything first.

He worked quickly and without sentiment. His garret held almost nothing worth taking: two shirts, a wool coat that had seen better winters, a knife he was legally forbidden to carry but carried anyway, and a small tin of salt. The salt was habit more than necessity. A taster learned early that salt was the one substance no poisoner bothered to disguise, and so it became the fixed point against which all other flavors were measured. He wrapped it in cloth and tucked it against his ribs like a second heart.

The map he did not pack at all.

That had taken three days of thought. Lord Corvane's men would search him—of course they would; the courier had died at *his* table, and Corvane struck Quentin as a man who never stopped pulling threads once one caught under his nail. So the map went into the lining of his second-best coat, which went into the bundle of kitchen linens bound for the rag merchant's stall on Cooper's Lane, which was collected every seventh day by a boy named Pip who ran errands for half the underworld of the capital and asked questions about none of it. Quentin had tasted enough of the city's shadows to know which ones could be bought.

He left through the scullery, because the scullery door had no lock worth the name and because its keeper, old Hesta, owed him a debt older than her bad hip. She'd poisoned her husband twenty years ago—a slow thing, a kind thing, after the millstone took his mind and left only cruelty—and Quentin had covered for her when the king's physician grew curious about the widow's calm. He had never told her he knew. She had never thanked him. That was how favors worked between people who lived close to death: unspoken, unspent, kept warm like coals banked in ash.

"Going out again?" she asked without looking up from her pots.

"Just for air."

"At this hour." She stirred. "Mind the frost. It lies on the steps like a liar."

"I always do."

She said nothing else, and he took that silence for what it was—the closest thing to a blessing either of them knew how to give.

* * *

The city at midnight was a different country, and Quentin had spent years learning its borders.

Most men fled along the great roads: the King's Way south to the river towns, the coast road east to the harbors where ships and bribes flowed in equal measure. Those roads had eyes. Corvane's riders would blanket them within hours, checking way-stations, questioning ferrymen, holding travelers' papers up to lantern light with that particular courtesy that preceded arrest. An invisible man did not take visible roads.

Quentin took the butcher's route instead.

Every palace has its secret arteries, and the capital's oldest one was meat. Before dawn each day, carts of carcasses came up from the slaughterfields through gates the crown had forgotten it owned—narrow posterns cut into the old wall back when kings worried about famine rather than conspiracy. The drovers' guild had never surrendered those keys, and the guild had never been entirely clean, and Quentin had spent six years tasting their festival feasts for free, swallowing small indignities and smaller doses of spoiled wine, until he owned a favor in every drover's pocket worth having.

The night-drover, a squat woman named Brakka with forearms like hams, looked at him once across the reeking yard and asked nothing.

"South gate," she said. "Under the hides. There's a cart leaves before the watch changes. You'll smell worse than you've ever smelled and you'll thank me for it."

"I already do."

"You were always too polite for this work," she grunted, turning back to her hooks. "Polite men make poor runners. They apologize to the road." She paused, knife suspended over a side of beef. "You're not coming back, are you."

It wasn't a question, but it wanted an answer, and lying to Brakka felt like spitting into a soup you'd sworn to taste honestly.

"No."

"Hm." She cut. "Then run like the meat's already spoiling."

He rode out of the capital wedged beneath four layers of stinking hide, jolted over ruts in the dark, breathing through his mouth and cataloguing the smells the way he catalogued everything—tallow, blood, the ammoniac bite of old urine, and beneath it, faint as a signature, the woodsmoke of the city falling away behind him. Somewhere back there, Lord Corvane was sleeping or not sleeping, and in the morning he would send men to a garret above the chandlery and find cold ashes and a paid-up room and nothing else, and his smile would not falter, and the hunt would begin in earnest.

Quentin found, to his own surprise, that he was smiling too.

It was not joy. He examined the feeling carefully, the way he examined any unfamiliar flavor, and identified it as exhilaration—the raw, indecent thrill of a man who had spent thirty-one years being furniture discovering that furniture could walk. For six years he had made himself small. He had swallowed arsenic in measured doses and cyanide's ghost in training draughts and the slow insult of a hundred lesser toxins, building a body like a fortress wall, and he had done it all while telling himself it was merely employment, merely survival, merely the price of being the sort of man nobody remembered at parties. Now, jolted under hides that smelled of death, fleeing treason he hadn't committed toward a country that didn't exist, Quentin Ashgrove finally admitted the truth he had been tasting all along:

He had never wanted to be safe. He had wanted to be *free*, and he had mistaken the fortress for freedom because he'd never seen the sky beyond it.

The cart stopped twice before dawn—once at a toll gate where Brakka's cousin argued about tariffs, once at a stream crossing where the drover's boy climbed up and pissed against the hides not four feet from Quentin's head—and then hands dragged the layers away and grey morning light hit him like a slap, and he was lying in a farmyard forty miles south of the capital, smelling like a charnel house, alive, unfound, and grinning like a madman.

"There's a well," Brakka's cousin said, jerking a thumb. "Soap's extra."

* * *

He washed, he walked, and by the time the sun cleared the hills he had put the farmyard behind him and begun the long climb into the foothills of Thornfell.

The map—retrieved from Pip at Cooper's Lane before he left the city proper, folded now against his chest—showed what he had come to think of as the honest roads and the dishonest ones. The honest roads were marked plainly: the King's Way, the river crossings, the garrison towns with their little fort symbols. Any competent rider could follow them, and any competent pursuer could follow the rider. But threaded between them, drawn in faded brown ink as though the cartographer had been ashamed of them, ran other lines. Thin ones. Unnamed ones. Lines that crossed private land and dead land and land that appeared on no deed, connecting places whose names Quentin recognized only because he had tasted for the smuggler feasts—the Drover's Rest at Harrow Ford, the Salt Cellar below Graywatch, the chapel at Nine Stones where the congregation sang hymns with a second meaning and the priest poured wine he watered for no one but himself.

A taster who works the underworld eats at strange tables. He learns who trades in what, who can be trusted with a secret and who can be trusted only with silver. Quentin had spent years swallowing poisons professionally; it turned out the same tongue that catalogued toxins could catalogue loyalties, and he had a mental ledger of the foothills more accurate than anything the crown's surveyors possessed, because the crown's surveyors had never needed to know which innkeeper watered his ale and which one informed to the magistrates.

He followed the dishonest lines north and west, into country that grew steeper and poorer and more beautiful with every mile. Thornfell rose ahead of him like a sleeping animal, blue-grey shoulders shrugged against the sky, its high passes already white. Winter was coming down off the mountain early this year—he'd heard the talk in the farmyards, shepherds bringing flocks down weeks ahead of schedule—but the low routes would hold for another fortnight if the weather held, and Quentin had never in his life bet on the weather.

He bet instead on distance. Every mile was a flavor he catalogued: the sour tang of fear in the first hours, thinning gradually into something cleaner. By the second evening, walking a sheep track above a valley full of woodsmoke and church bells, he caught himself humming. Humming. A man with a death sentence pending, carrying a map to nowhere, humming like a schoolboy on holiday.

He stopped and laughed aloud at himself, alone on the hillside, and the sound startled crows out of a hedgerow.

* * *

They found him on the fourth night.

He should have known better than to stop at an honest inn. The Drover's Rest at Harrow Ford was exactly what it sounded like—a low stone room full of honest men drinking honest ale—and Quentin chose it precisely because it seemed too plain to be dangerous. That was the error. Plainness was a disguise, same as anything else, and he of all people should have known that the most dangerous thing in any room is the thing that looks like nothing.

He was halfway through a bowl of barley stew when the door opened and the cold came in with three men wearing travel cloaks over mail.

He didn't see the mail. What he saw was the boots—road-worn, military-issue, the left one resoled with the distinctive yellow stitching of the crown quartermaster—and his body understood before his mind did, the way it always had. Six years of tasting had trained him to read danger in traces, in parts per million, in things too small for ordinary senses. The boots were a trace. The stew went suddenly tasteless in his mouth.

The three men didn't approach him. That was the elegant part, and he admired it even as terror crawled up his spine. They simply took a table, ordered ale, and sat with the unhurried patience of men being paid by the day. One of them glanced at Quentin exactly once—the flat, inventorying look of a man memorizing a face—and then looked away, and that was worse than any challenge. They weren't sure yet. They were waiting for confirmation from someone, or waiting for him to do something confirming, and either way the net was already set and he was sitting in the middle of it eating stew.

Quentin finished the bowl. It seemed important, somehow, not to leave food unfinished—it felt like surrender—and besides, he reasoned with the detached clarity that fear sometimes granted him, a man running on a full stomach runs farther than a man running empty.

Then he stood, paid his coin with steady hands, nodded to the innkeeper, and walked out the back door toward the privy as naturally as a man could who was screaming inside.

The privy backed onto the stable yard. The stable yard opened onto the ford road, and the ford road ran along the river in both directions, and Quentin stood in the freezing dark weighing his options with the speed of a man defusing a trap he'd stepped in blind. South was back the way he'd come. East was the honest road, the garrison town, the lantern-lit checkpoints. North and west, across the ford itself, lay a sheep track the map showed threading up into the high ground—a track that would be suicide in daylight and near-suicide tonight, in the dark, with the river running loud and black beside it.

Behind him, inside the inn, a chair scraped. Just one chair. Someone standing up.

Quentin ran.

He hit the ford at a slide, the water seizing his legs with cold so total it burned, and the current shoved at him like a living thing trying to add him to its collection. Halfway across, his foot found a hole and he went down to the chest, gasping, the map's weight suddenly enormous against his chest—*the map is wet, the map will be ruined*—and he clawed upright and lunged the last yards and came out the far side dripping and half-frozen and climbing, climbing, the sheep track rising steep and slick before him while behind him torchlight bloomed in the inn's windows and voices shouted across the water.

They didn't follow immediately. The river bought him that much—they had to decide whether to ford in pursuit or ride for the bridge downstream, and either choice cost minutes. Quentin spent those minutes gaining altitude with legs that had stopped reporting pain and started reporting only movement, breath sawing white in the starlight, the cold in his wet clothes tightening like a fist. Twice he fell. Twice he got up. Once he heard hoofbeats on the far bank, parallel to him, searching for the crossing, and pressed himself flat against a boulder in a shadow deeper than night while the sound passed below him and faded.

By grey dawn he was three thousand feet up, in a hollow of rock below the snow line, shivering so violently his teeth chattered in counterpoint, and the exhilaration of the first days had curdled completely.

This was exile. Not adventure—exile. The word arrived with the sunrise and settled on him like the frost settling on the stones. Everything he had been was down there, behind him, on the other side of the river and the other side of a life: the garret, the kitchens, the ledgered tongue, even the loneliness, which he realized now had been a kind of home, furnished over years, familiar as an old coat. He had burned it all in one night for a wax-sealed tube and a dying stranger's whisper, and the burning could not be undone, and ahead of him lay only the mountain, and winter, and a country that had been erased so thoroughly that even its ghosts had no address.

He got the wet clothes off, wrung them, put them back on, and laughed at the absurdity of shaking hands doing buttons while his lips went blue. Then he dug out the map, checked it for damage with the tenderness of a man checking a wound, and studied the brown lines ahead.

The track he'd followed climbed toward a saddle between two peaks, and beyond the saddle the ink showed something Quentin hadn't noticed before, because he'd never looked at the map upside down, or perhaps because fear had sharpened his eyes: a fine dotted line leaving the main track and descending into a valley the cartographer had left deliberately blank—not unnamed, but blank, scraped clean, as though whoever drew this map had wanted to show the shape of the omission itself.

At the head of that blank valley, small as a flea, was a mark he'd seen once before and dismissed: a circle with a vertical stroke, like a door standing closed.

And beneath the saddle, drawn in the same faded brown ink, a single word in a hand tighter than the rest, almost hidden in the contour lines:

*Wren.*

Quentin stared at it for a long moment. Then he looked up at the saddle, where the first snow of the season was beginning to fall—fine, dry, indifferent snow, dusting the rocks like flour sifted over a table—and understood that the road ahead belonged to somebody, and that somebody was probably not friendly.

He folded the map, tucked it against his chest next to the tin of salt, and began to climb.

Behind him, far below, the river ran on toward the honest world, and somewhere on its banks, riders were asking questions in polite voices, and the answers were pointing uphill.
