## 7. Justice at Knifepoint

The pass above Hollowmere was called the Widow's Teeth for reasons Wren Calloway had never found it necessary to explain to anyone. The rocks on either side of the track rose in jagged white spires, fanged and close, and the road between them narrowed until a loaded cart had to fold its mirrors—metaphorically speaking—and hold its breath. Anyone coming through had to come slow, single file, and looking forward.

Which made it an excellent place to ask questions.

Wren sat on her horse in the shadow of the third tooth, wrapped in a grey cloak that matched the grey stone, and waited. Below her, maybe half a mile of switchbacks down, she could see the road unspool toward the valley: brown mud, dirty snow, a thin ribbon of river ice glinting where the trees broke apart. A good vantage. She'd held worse ones with less reason.

Her informant's words from two nights ago still sat in her ear like a swallowed coal. *A man on the south road, moving fast and badly. No pack worth stealing, no sword worth taking. But the smugglers at Fennel Cross say the crown's hounds are sniffing after him, and the crown doesn't spend silver on nothing.* Then he'd spat, wiped his nose, and asked if she was buying or just listening. She'd paid him a copper and told him to buy himself something warm. He would drink it, and by morning remember only that a woman with cold eyes had asked about a traveler, because that was how informants survived: they kept the coin and lost the details.

But Wren kept everything. It was the one thing the courts had never managed to take from her—the memory, the ledger behind her eyes where every debt and payment went down in ink that didn't fade.

She heard him before she saw him. Not hoofbeats—he was on foot—but the particular rhythm of someone trying to walk quietly and failing at it in an interesting way. Quiet walking was a craft like any other; she'd taught herself its grammar years ago, weight rolling heel-to-toe, breath timed to footfall, clothing chosen so it wouldn't whisper against itself. This man had none of it. His steps were hurried but uneven, his breathing too loud, and every few paces there came the small wet sound of boots finding mud they hadn't been invited into. A man who knew he was being followed but didn't know how following worked.

He came around the bend below her, and she studied him the way she studied everyone: hands first, then eyes, then the space around his body where a weapon should be.

Hands: soft. Kitchen-soft or clerk-soft, no calluses where a sword lived, no rope burns, no knife wear. Ink on the right middle finger, old and worked into the skin. Eyes: dark, darting up-slope every dozen steps, checking his backtrail with the regularity of a man counting his own heartbeats. Weapon-space: empty on the belt, but something flat and rigid rode inside his coat against his left ribs—a book, or a case, or a map. Something he touched every time he checked behind him, the way other men touched amulets.

And he was limping. Slightly. Left boot, and not from injury—from wearing. New boots bought in haste by a man who hadn't planned a journey.

Everything about him said *fleeing*. Nothing about him said *dangerous*, except the crown's riders on his trail, which in Wren's experience meant the opposite of harmless more often than not.

She let him get level with the third tooth. Then she stepped her horse out of the shadow onto the road, and the Widow's Teeth did what they always did: they made the sound arrive before the explanation did.

He spun. To his credit, he didn't scream. He stumbled back two steps into the mud, threw up one arm as though a hand could stop eight hundred pounds of horse, and said, in a voice pitched somewhere between apology and prayer:

"I don't have any money."

"I know," Wren said. "Money would make this simpler."

* * *

Getting him off the road took less effort than she expected, which itself told her something. A guilty man fights. An innocent man argues. This man did neither—he let her walk him, hands raised, off the track and up a deer path to a hollow beneath the fourth tooth where a fallen slab of granite made a windbreak and, conveniently, a table. She dismounted, tied her mare where the animal couldn't be seen from the switchbacks, and put her back to the rock so she could watch both the man and the road below.

"Sit," she said.

He sat. On the ground, immediately, knees folding like a puppet whose strings had been cut, and then he seemed to catch himself and try to make sitting on frozen ground look deliberate. It didn't work.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Quentin Ashgrove." The name came out fast, rehearsed, and then he flinched at having said it, which was interesting. People flinch at their own names when their names have become dangerous things.

"Ashgrove." She turned it over. "Kitchen name."

Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise exactly. Recognition that she'd read him correctly. "I'm a taster," he said. "For the king."

"And now you're running through the Widow's Teeth in borrowed boots with no sword and no money." She crouched in front of him, forearms on knees, close enough that he had to look at her or visibly turn away, and either answer told her things. "So here's my problem, taster. Three days ago I start hearing about a thief. Somebody lifted something out of the capital that the crown wants back badly enough to send riders onto my roads. My roads. And the description that comes down the smuggler lines is vague in every particular except one—that whatever was stolen is small enough to hide under a coat, flat enough to sit against the ribs, and important enough that men are dying over it." She paused. "You're touching your left side again."

His hand froze where it was. He looked down at it as though it belonged to someone else.

"That's twice you've done it since we stopped," she said. "Once while you were walking, every time you glanced uphill. Whatever's under your coat, you've been guarding it like a wound."

"It's mine," he said.

"Was anything ever yours?" She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse; she watched the question land on him like weather. "You're a palace servant, Ashgrove. You eat from a bowl you're given, sleep in a room you're given, wear livery you're given. Men like you don't own anything, which means whatever you're carrying, you're carrying for someone else—or you took it. And the crown doesn't chase servants who carry things. It chases servants who steal them."

"You don't understand—"

"Correct," she agreed. "So help me."

He was quiet. Below them, wind came up the pass and stirred the dirty snow, carrying the smell of ice and pine rot and distance. Somewhere far down the switchbacks, a crow complained once and thought better of it. Wren let the silence do her work. Silence was the oldest tool of interrogation, older than thumbscrews and cheaper than bribes: most people couldn't bear it longer than thirty breaths, and they filled it with whatever truth or lie they could reach fastest.

Ashgrove lasted nearly forty. Better than average.

"There was a man," he said finally. "At the feast. A courier. He was poisoned—I saw it happening, I *know* what poisoning looks like, it's—" He stopped, swallowed. Started again, quieter. "He died at my table. Before he died he gave me something. A message tube. Sealed wax. And he said—" The word seemed to cost him. "He said *Vaelmoor*."

The name went through Wren like a splinter finding old wood.

She kept her face still. Twenty years of tribunals and gallows and men begging at her feet had built her a face like a locked door, and she used it now, because the last thing she could afford was for this soft-handed kitchen rat to see what the name had done to her. Vaelmoor. Her grandmother's voice, low, in the dark of a cottage in the foothills, singing a song with words in no language the priests allowed, and stopping mid-verse whenever anyone else came near. *Vaelmoor* was a word her grandmother had hummed more than spoken, a shape pressed into a child's hair, a place that appeared in no charter, no treaty, no map Wren had ever been able to find—and she had looked, God help her, she had looked in every archive her disgrace still let her enter, and found nothing but blank pages and clerks who changed the subject.

"No such country," she said aloud, and heard how carefully flat her own voice was.

"No," Ashgrove agreed. "That's what I thought too."

"The courier. Did anyone see him give you the tube?"

"I don't know. Maybe. The hall was crowded." His jaw tightened. "Lord Corvane knows I had it. He summoned me. He asked me questions for hours, polite questions, and I lied to him, and I think—I think he believed me, or pretended to, but his riders were on the south road within a day, so either he believed me and followed anyway or he never believed me at all."

Corvane. Of course it was Corvane. Wren felt her opinion of the whole affair shift and settle, heavier than before. Lord Corvane did not send riders after stolen silverware. Corvane sent riders after things that could unmake kings, and he did not stop at the border of the law, mostly because he considered the border a suggestion made to other people.

"Open your coat," she said.

He hesitated. That was the moment—she'd seen it a hundred times, the last small gate a man stood in before deciding what he was. Thieves bolted at this point. Innocents argued. Ashgrove did neither. He sat in the mud with his hands half-raised and looked at her with an expression she hadn't catalogued yet: not fear, or not only fear. Something tired underneath it. The look of a man who had been afraid for so long that fear had worn through like an old boot sole, and what showed underneath was exhaustion.

"If I give it to you," he said, "will you give it back?"

"No."

"Then why should I?"

"Because if you don't," Wren said, "I take it anyway, and you'll have a cut on your hands besides. Because those riders behind you will take it without asking at all, and then they'll kill you for having seen it, because that's how Corvane ends conversations. And because—" she paused, and decided, against her better judgment, to spend a little truth, "—because I'm not the crown. I'm not going to hand it to Corvane. Whatever it is, I want to *read* it first. That's more than anyone else on these roads will offer you tonight."

Something in that landed. She watched it land—the slight easing of his shoulders, the way his breathing dropped half a register. Truth was expensive and she rationed it like hardtack, but sometimes you spent it to buy the one thing coin couldn't.

He opened his coat.

The tube was wax-sealed, black wax, and long enough to hold a rolled map. He drew it out with both hands, careful as a man lifting a hot pan, and held it out to her on his open palms—an offering, or a surrender, or possibly both.

Wren took it. It was lighter than she'd expected. She turned it once in her gloved fingers, noted the seal—unbroken, some heraldic device she didn't recognize, a tower or a door rendered in stylized strokes—and made her decision the way she made all of them: quickly, and then forever.

She snapped the seal and shook the contents out onto the granite slab.

It was a map. Thornfell, unmistakably—the massif spread across the parchment in fine inked relief, the passes labeled in a clerk's neat hand, the smugglers' routes marked in a different, rougher line that made her mouth twitch despite everything, because whoever had drawn those knew her roads, knew them intimately, knew cuts she'd only ever shown to three people living. Someone had mapped the underworld of the mountains. Someone who traveled it.

And at the heart of the range, where no road should go—where the peaks climbed into the killing snows that even the smugglers called the Devil's Parish—someone had drawn a symbol.

Not a mountain. Not a pass. A door. Drawn upright, tall and narrow, set into the inked flank of a peak, with radiating lines like heat or light or sound. Beneath it, in a script she didn't recognize—angular, dense, older-looking than anything the cartographers' guild used—ran a single line of text, and beside it another mark: a circle quartered by a cross, with a dot at its center.

Wren went cold all the way through, and the mountain had nothing to do with it.

She knew the door. Not from archives, not from maps, not from any ledger of debts and crimes. From a lullaby. From a woman's low voice humming in a dark kitchen while a pot of barley simmered, a nonsense rhyme about a door in the rock that was warm in winter, that hummed when you laid your palm on it, that kept a country safe behind it—her grandmother singing it soft and quick, always soft and quick, and once, only once, when little Wren had asked *where's Vaelmoor, Gran?*, the wooden spoon had stopped stirring and the old woman had knelt down, taken her face in flour-dusted hands, and said: *Never ask that again. Never sing that song where anyone can hear. Promise me.*

She had promised. She had been six years old.

And now the forbidden shape sat in front of her, drawn in ink on stolen state parchment, in the heart of the mountains, guarded by a line of writing in the language of a song that could get a family burned.

Her hands were steady. She noticed distantly that they were steady, and was grateful for the twenty years of practice that had purchased that particular mercy, because inside, somewhere below the locked door of her face, something six years old had gone very quiet and very frightened.

"What does it say?" Ashgrove asked. "The writing. Can you read it?"

"No." She said it too fast and knew it, and hated knowing it. "Can you?"

"No. But the courier died saying the name out loud, so I imagine the writing says the same thing." He was watching her now, and she realized with irritation that her face must have slipped somewhere along the line, because his expression had changed—less fear, more attention, the way a man looks at a lock when he notices the keyhole isn't where it's supposed to be. "You've seen this mark before."

"I've seen a drawing of it," she said. "In a children's rhyme. Which is impossible, and therefore I'm choosing not to discuss it."

"That seems—"

"Don't." She rolled the map closed with quick, precise motions, rewrapped it in its tube, and stood, putting the granite slab and four feet of cold air between herself and him, because she needed the distance to think and thinking required not looking at his face. The wind came up the pass again. Below, the road lay empty, but empty roads in the Widow's Teeth stayed empty only as long as they pleased to.

Here was her position, laid out in the ledger behind her eyes. A palace taster, fleeing the crown, carrying a map to a country that did not exist, marked with a door out of a forbidden lullaby, pursued by Lord Corvane's riders—who wanted the map, or wanted the man, or wanted whatever lay behind a door drawn warm in the heart of the Devil's Parish. Any one of those threads, pulled alone, would have been trouble. Braided together, they were a hanging offense for everyone holding the rope.

The lawful thing—the thing the courts would have ordered, back when the courts still answered to her—was to surrender the map and the man at the next garrison and wash her hands. The lawful thing was also, she understood now, with total clarity, exactly what Corvane wanted everyone on these roads to do. Every honest hand between here and the capital was a link in his chain.

And the other thing—the thing her grandmother's flour-dusted hands had sworn her to, the promise made in a kitchen twenty-five years dead—said *never ask, never sing, never let them hear of it.* The promise said bury it. The promise said this map should never have existed, and the safest place for it was fire.

But the promise had also been made by a woman who sang about a warm door keeping a country safe, and promises made out of fear were, in Wren's long experience, only half true. The other half was always love.

She looked down at Ashgrove. He was still sitting in the mud, watching her with those tired dark eyes, hands resting open on his knees in a posture of complete and perhaps practiced helplessness. A soft man. A kitchen man. A liar good enough to survive an hour of Corvane, which made him a better liar than most soldiers she'd known. Carrying a dead stranger's secret through winter mountains in borrowed boots.

"Why didn't you burn it?" she asked.

He blinked. "What?"

"The map. You had it for days. Alone, in a garret, with a candle. One document, one flame, and you're nobody again—no riders, no spymaster, no winter crossing. Why are you here instead?"

He was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice had changed—stripped of the rehearsed quality, down to something plainer.

"Because a man died giving it to me," he said. "He chose me. Out of a whole hall of people, he crawled to my table and put it in my hand and spent his last breath on the word. You don't burn that. You can't. If I burned it, I'd be—" he searched, "—I'd be deciding his death didn't mean anything. And I'm not the one who gets to decide that."

Wren looked at him for a long time.

It was the wrong answer, by the code of the courts. Evidence belonged to the evidence-locker; a dead courier's wishes were sentiment, and sentiment was not procedure. It was also, by the same measure, the right answer—by the other code, the one she enforced by lantern-light at the roadside when the crown's justice had failed some family for the last time. *A life for a life, a debt to the dead before a duty to the living.* Two moralities, hers and his, arriving from opposite directions at the same crossroads, and standing in the snow looking at each other with mutual suspicion.

She had come up this pass to rob him. That had been the plan, cleanly conceived: intercept, relieve him of the crown's property, assess, decide. Justice at knifepoint, delivered by the only court still operating on the high roads.

Instead she was standing here holding a sealed tube containing a door out of a dead language, and the thief she'd caught wasn't a thief, and the robbery had begun to feel uncomfortably like a rescue, and somewhere down the switchbacks Corvane's riders were climbing with murder in their saddlebags.

"We move," she said. "Now, before the light goes. There's a shepherd's hut over the shoulder of the fifth tooth—stone walls, one door, no chimney smoke if we're careful. We talk there, properly. And Ashgrove—" she swung up into the saddle and looked down at him, and let him see, for the first time, that her face had unlocked, "—you'll tell me everything about the courier, the feast, Corvane's questions, and exactly what you lied about. All of it. Because I know what that mark on your map is. I've known it since I was six years old, in a song I was promised I'd never hear sung anywhere but a dead woman's kitchen."

He stared up at her. "Then you know what Vaelmoor—"

"Hut first," Wren said, and turned her mare up the deer path. "Talk on the way. And pray those riders below us are worse at listening than you are."

Behind them, far down the pass, faint as a held breath, a crow started up from the third tooth—and this time it did not stop complaining.
