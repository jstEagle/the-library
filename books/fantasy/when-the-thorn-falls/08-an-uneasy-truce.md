## 8. An Uneasy Truce

The knife stayed at his throat for the better part of an hour after she read the map.

Not pressed—just resting there, a cold parenthesis against his skin, while Wren Calloway sat on a fallen birch and stared at the wax-stained vellum as though it had spoken to her in a voice she'd spent her whole life trying not to remember. Quentin knelt in the wet bracken with his hands laced behind his head and counted his heartbeats, because counting was what he knew how to do. Four hundred and eleven beats before she finally spoke.

"Get up," she said. "Slowly."

He got up slowly. His knees cracked like green kindling, and somewhere in the dark a nightjar called three times and went silent, as if even the birds were waiting to see what she'd decide.

She rolled the map closed with exaggerated care, the way one handles something that might still be alive, and tucked it into the breast of her coat. Then she looked at him—really looked, the way he imagined she looked at smugglers across a lantern-lit stump before deciding whether they'd hang or walk—and Quentin felt catalogued down to his marrow.

"You're the taster," she said. "The one from the palace. Ashgrove."

"Was." He rubbed his throat where the knife had rested. "I've been informed I'm also a thief, a spy, and possibly a regicide-in-waiting, depending on which rumor you prefer."

"I don't deal in rumors. I deal in evidence." She patted her coat, where the map lay. "And the evidence is that a man died at your table carrying state paper worth more than this whole valley, and now you're running north through smuggler country at night like a rabbit with its tail lit."

"And your evidence says you know what that mark on the map means," Quentin said. "You went white as flour when you saw it. Thieves don't go white over a door symbol, Mistress Calloway. People who've heard that symbol *before* go white."

Her jaw tightened. For a long moment the only sound was the stream below them, talking to itself in the dark. Then she did something he didn't expect: she laughed. One dry bark of a laugh, quickly strangled, like a dog yanked back by its collar.

"Sharp tongue," she said. "They say poison-tasters develop them."

"They say road-justices develop dead men's habits. You checked my boots for hidden blades twice and my collar once. That's a hanged man's ritual."

"I've hanged enough men to know how they hide things." She swung up onto her horse—a rangy grey gelding with a scarred muzzle—and looked down at him with eyes the color of river ice. "Here's how tonight goes, Ashgrove. You ride with me. We keep the map between us. And if you try to run, I'll break your ankle with a rock and carry you. Clear?"

"Perfectly," said Quentin. "And here's how *my* tonight goes. Without me, you'll be lost in the fells by tomorrow noon. Those roads you think you own—" he nodded north, toward the black teeth of the mountains, "—they end at the high passes. What comes after isn't on any smuggler's chart. It's on *my* map, and I'm the only one alive who can read the margins."

It was mostly true. The margins were alchemical notation, dense as scripture, and he could not yet read them—but she didn't need to know that. A lie told with a steady voice was just seasoning on a true dish.

Wren studied him. He watched her weigh it, watched the scales move behind her eyes, and understood in that moment why smugglers paid her tolls without argument and murderers confessed to her rather than run. She wasn't frightening because she was cruel. She was frightening because she was *fair*, and fairness is far more terrifying than cruelty when you have something to hide.

"One day," she said finally. "You navigate for one day. Then we renegotiate."

* * *

They rode north through the small hours, following a drover's track that wound up out of the riverlands into country where the trees gave way to gorse and stone. Quentin navigated by stars and by habit—the habit being that a man who has spent ten years memorizing every servant's corridor, every coal chute, every unwatched gate of a palace learns to read any human landscape the same way. Roads, he had come to believe, were just corridors that hadn't been roofed yet.

Wren rode like she'd been poured onto the saddle and left to set. She spoke perhaps six words in four hours, all of them directional. Twice she raised a hand and they stopped, motionless, while some distant sound resolved itself into wind or water and not riders. Both times Quentin's stomach performed the slow, cold roll it had learned in the days since Corvane's summons—that particular nausea that had nothing to do with poison and everything to do with being hunted.

Near dawn they came down into a hollow where a shepherd's hut leaned against the hillside like a drunk against a wall. Its owner was clearly gone for the season; the door was barred from outside with a loop of rope, and snow lay undisturbed in the lee of its walls.

"We camp here till dusk," Wren said. "Riders won't work this ground in daylight. Too open."

Quentin dismounted, and his legs nearly refused the arrangement. "You're trusting me not to walk away while you sleep."

"I'm trusting you to get lost in open moorland on foot with no food." She unsaddled the grey and rubbed it down with a handful of straw. "Also, I sleep light, and I sleep with a knife. Ask anyone I've ever arrested."

"No one you've ever arrested would speak to me. My reputation's ruined in entirely new circles now."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, haunting the ruins.

Inside, the hut smelled of lanolin and old smoke. There was a hearth, a stool, a straw tick flattened by years of a shepherd's back. Wren cleared it the way soldiers clear a room, then built a small fire—small, screened, professional—and set a dented tin kettle on it. From her saddlebags she produced hard cheese, a heel of black bread, and dried plums, and divided everything into two portions with such scrupulous equality that Quentin almost laughed. Even the smaller plum went to the larger portion until the scales balanced.

"You always split like that?" he asked.

"Everyone watches how you divide food," Wren said. "It tells you everything about a person. Who takes the bigger half. Who gives it away. Who counts." She handed him his share. "What does it tell you about me?"

"That you've fed a lot of people you were about to judge."

She didn't answer that. But she didn't stop chewing, either, and Quentin had learned long ago that silence that doesn't harden is silence that's listening.

They ate. The kettle sang softly. Through the hut's one cracked shutter, the first grey light of morning crept in and laid itself across the floorboards like a patient animal.

"The courier," Wren said at last. "Tell me how he died. Exactly."

Quentin set down his bread. This, at least, was ground he knew. "Bitter almond under the wine note. Aconite, most likely—monkshood extract, refined. Fast. He'd have had perhaps four minutes from the first swallow, less because his pulse was already up from hard riding. Someone dosed his flask, not his food. It was meant to look like a rider's collapse. Heart failure on a tired horse."

"And you crawled under a table full of nobles to reach him. Why? A taster's job ends at the swallow. Whatever kills a guest isn't your problem."

"My job," Quentin said, "is that nothing dies at the king's table that I haven't already decided about." He turned his cup in his hands. "A man dying under that table is a failure of mine whether or not I poisoned him. So I went to him. It's not heroism. It's bookkeeping."

"That's the coldest thing I've ever heard someone say about saving a life."

"It wasn't saving. He was past saving before I reached him. All I did was hold a stranger while he died and take what he gave me." Quentin looked up. "Which brings us to the honest part of the evening, Mistress Calloway, so I'll say it plainly: I don't know what Vaelmoor is. I don't know why a courier bled secrets onto my table instead of the spymaster's. I only know that Lord Corvane looked at me across his desk with that pleasant smile of his and asked questions for three hours that had no right answers, and I understood that whatever the truth was, it would kill me slower than a lie but much more thoroughly. So I ran. Call it cowardice. I've decided to call it arithmetic."

Wren was quiet for a long time. Outside, the wind worried the gorse. The fire ticked.

"Lord Aldric Vane," she said suddenly.

Quentin blinked. "Who?"

"The lord I sentenced." She was looking into the fire, not at him. Her voice had changed—not softer, exactly, but stripped, the way a room looks with the furniture taken out. "Two years ago. You wouldn't have known. Court business stays south of the passes, mostly. But you asked nothing about me all night, which means either you're very polite or you're waiting, and I'd rather talk than be waited on."

"I'm listening," Quentin said, and found, to his surprise, that he meant it.

"Vane held lands on the Thornfell marches. Timber rights, toll bridges, the usual. Three winters ago one of his foresters beat a tithe-farmer to death over a disputed pig. Openly. In front of the man's daughters. The crown court took the case because Vane was a baron's cousin and everyone assumed it would die in procedure." She turned her cup the same way Quentin had turned his, he noticed. Everyone turns their cup when they're saying something heavy. "It didn't die. Because the assizes that term fell to me. Circuit justice, lowest rank on the bench, sent up the passes because no one important wanted the posting."

"And you convicted."

"I convicted the forester of manslaughter—two years. And I convicted Vane of ordering it, which took me nine days of testimony and cost me every friend I had in the province, because Vane's people made it very clear what happened to witnesses." Her jaw worked. "Hanging a baron's cousin gets noticed, Ashgrove. Not the way you'd hope. The crown didn't want Vane freed—they wanted him *patiently forgotten*. Fines, censure, a season abroad. There are men in the capital whose fortunes sit on Vane's timber money, and my sentence sat on top of those fortunes like a crow on a corpse. Eighteen months later they found a pretext. 'Judicial overreach.' 'Conduct unbecoming.' They struck me from the rolls in a chamber I wasn't allowed to enter."

"So the road-law—"

"The road-law came after." She shrugged, but the shrug had weight in it, like armor being settled. "Out here there's no court but what you build. The crown's law stops at the last milestone. Smugglers know it. Farmers know it. So when a wrong happens on the mountain roads, they bring it to me, and I weigh it, and I sentence it, and I make them watch. Not because I love the rope." Her hand drifted, briefly, to the coil at her saddle. "Because the alternative is that strength decides, and I've seen what strength decides when nobody's watching. I've *been* what strength decides."

The fire had burned low. Quentin fed it a stick and thought about what she'd said, turning it over the way he'd turn an unfamiliar ingredient on the tongue—cautiously, attending to every note.

"You knew the sentence would end you," he said. "Before you delivered it."

"Of course."

"And you delivered it anyway."

"Of course." She looked at him then, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with the night's ride. "That's the whole job, Ashgrove. Everything else is weather."

Quentin nodded slowly. In another life—in the life he'd fled four nights ago—he would have filed this away, the way he filed everything, another entry in the ledger of people and their uses. But sitting in a shepherd's hut at dawn with a disgraced justice splitting dried plums with monastic fairness, he found the ledger wouldn't open. Some accounts, he thought, you have to keep in ink, not blood.

"I tasted for the king for ten years," he heard himself say. "Do you know why anyone becomes a taster? Orphans, mostly. Men with no family to notify. The palace pays well and the mortality rate is politely never published." He smiled, and it felt strange on his face, like wearing someone else's coat. "Every poison I survived, I survived so that some other man—the king, yes, but also the kitchen boys, the guests, the farmers sending tribute-honey to court—wouldn't. It's not courage. It's the same thing you said. It's the whole job. Everything else is weather."

Wren studied him across the dying fire, and he saw her reassess him the way she must have reassessed witnesses nine days into the Vane trial—not warmer, but *heavier*. Giving him more weight in the scales.

"You're still hiding something," she said.

"Enormous amounts."

"Good. As long as we agree on that, we can travel together." She stood, stretched until her spine crackled, and kicked earth over the embers with practiced strokes. "Sleep. Dusk comes early this time of year, and the pass above Coldmere Narrows is two days' ride if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we learn what kind of people we are faster than either of us wanted." She lay down on the straw tick facing the door, knife loose in her hand, and within minutes her breathing slowed into the shallow rhythm of the light sleepers of the world.

Quentin took the stool by the dead fire and pulled his coat around him. Sleep came for him eventually, but on its way it passed through the map—folded against Wren's breastbone now, twenty feet away, close enough that he could almost feel it humming like a second heartbeat in the room.

In the grey hour before true dawn, half-dreaming, he saw again the cramped notation crawling up the map's eastern margin. Rows of symbols he had somehow *recognized* without understanding—symbols that had snagged on something buried deep, the way a familiar smell snags on grief before memory catches up. A child's memory. A bitter taste in a small glass. A white room. Hands holding his wrist firmly, kindly, saying *again*.

His eyes snapped open in the dark.

"Mistress Calloway," he said quietly.

From the straw tick: "I'm awake."

"The map's margins. When we stop at dusk—I need to see them again. All of them." His voice sounded strange to him, scraped thin. "I think I've seen that script before. I think I've *tasted* it."

Across the dark hut, he heard Wren sit up, and heard the particular silence of a woman deciding whether the thing she'd caught had just become worth more than the thing she'd risked.
