## 10. Corvane's Hounds

The river spoke first.

Wren heard it before she saw it, a low gray murmur through the pines, and she held up her fist the way her grandmother had taught her—two fingers, knuckles out—and felt Quentin stop behind her without being told. Three days on the road together had taught him that much. He was learning. She wasn't sure yet whether that pleased her or unsettled her.

"The ford's ahead," he murmured, coming up beside her in a crouch. He smelled of woodsmoke and the dried herbs he chewed constantly now, cataloguing every plant they passed as if his tongue were writing a ledger only he could read. "The Graywash. It runs fast this time of year, but there's a crossing where it braids over stones. My runners used to use it when I carried for the kitchens."

"Your runners."

"Every palace has them." He said it flatly, without apology. "Spices come down from the mountain farms somehow, don't they? Nobody asks how."

She almost smiled. Almost. Instead she parted the branches with two fingers and looked down at the water.

The Graywash ran wide and shallow at the braid, white water stitching itself over dark stone, and the far bank rose into a wall of pine broken by one thread of trail—the only way north toward Thornfell that didn't require wings. And on the near bank, half-hidden among the alders, stood four horses.

Saddled. Tethered loosely. Steam rising off their flanks.

Wren's mouth went dry. "Quentin," she breathed. "How many men does Corvane have left to send?"

"More than we can count," he said quietly. "He had a whole company when we left the capital. Most of them will have been spread across the passes, watching roads. But if he's narrowed us down—" He stopped. Swallowed. She watched him do the same arithmetic she was doing. "If he knows we're coming to the ford, he doesn't need many. Four is enough."

"Five," she corrected. She'd counted the horses; now she counted shadows. One man stood apart from the others, upstream, where the ground rose slightly—a lookout, facing away, watching the trail behind them rather than the crossing itself. They were expecting their quarry to come up the valley road. Not to already be here, above them, inside the trees.

"Five," Quentin agreed. His face had gone the color of old tallow. She remembered him telling her, around their second fire, that he had never hurt anyone in his life—that his whole art was taking harm into himself so that others wouldn't have to carry it. She had thought then that he was describing cowardice dressed up as virtue. She no longer thought so. There was a kind of courage in a man who chose to swallow poison every day of his life, but it was not the kind that would serve him in the next five minutes.

"Listen to me," she said. She took his shoulder and turned him so he had to look at her. "When it starts, you cross. You don't fight, you don't look back, you don't stop for me. The water's cold enough to kill in ten minutes if you go under, so keep your feet under you and aim for the third braid—the big gray stone. After that, the trail climbs. You climb it."

"And leave you against five armed men."

"Four armed men and one boy pretending to be one." She'd marked the fifth earlier: young, narrow through the shoulders, holding his sword like it was a tool he hadn't finished learning. "I've put down worse odds with worse ground. This ground favors me. They're looking south. We're standing north. That's the whole battle, Quentin. Everything else is noise."

His eyes searched her face, and she saw him weighing something—not the odds, but her. Deciding whether the woman who'd held a knife to his throat a week ago was someone worth trusting with his life. Whatever he found there, he must have found enough, because he nodded once, slow.

"I cross at the third braid."

"You cross at the third braid."

They moved.

* * *

Wren went down through the alders like water finding its level, silent, each step placed on moss or root or packed needle-fall, her boots remembering thirty years of smugglers' roads so thoroughly that she didn't have to think about them anymore. Behind her she could hear Quentin doing his best and no better—heavier than he should have been, breathing through his mouth—but the river covered them both, and the wind was coming down-valley, carrying their sound away from the men below.

She counted again as she moved. The four by the horses: two sitting on the stones playing at dice, swords loose in their belts; one standing at the water's edge testing the depth with a pole, which meant they expected to ride after their quarry across the ford; and the last one walking a slow circuit of the campsite, restless, the kind of soldier who never stopped moving because stopping meant thinking. Royal livery under travel cloaks on all of them—the blue-and-silver of the crown's household guard, not mercenaries. Corvane had sent his own people. That told her two things: he wanted this quiet, and he wanted it done right.

It also told her what she'd be killing.

The thought arrived and she set it aside, the way she always did, the way her code demanded: weigh it later, when later existed. Right now there was only ground, distance, and time.

The lookout upstream was her problem. He faced south, yes, but the moment anything happened at the ford he'd turn, shout, and the arithmetic would change. So the arithmetic started with him.

She circled wide through the trees until she was above him, close enough to see the steam of his breath and the dull shine of a badge at his collar—royal, silver, stamped with the crowned thorn. Close enough to see that he was younger than she'd guessed. Nineteen, perhaps twenty. He shifted his weight and hummed something under his breath, tuneless, the habit of a boy who'd grown up somewhere with music in it.

Her grandmother's voice, dead forty years, spoke in her skull: *You'll know the line when you reach it, girl. You won't see it coming.*

Wren drew her knife.

The ambush, done right, isn't violence. It's bookkeeping. You subtract the sentry before the ledger opens, and then everything else balances. She came down the slope behind him on the last three steps and put her left hand over his mouth and her knife to the soft place under his jaw, and she felt him go rigid, felt his breath spike hot against her palm.

"One sound and you're done before you finish it," she whispered. "Nod if you understand."

He nodded. His eyes were huge and wet and very young.

"Where's your officer?"

Another nod, downstream, toward the man at the water's edge with the pole. Of course. The careful one, the one measuring the ford, was the sergeant. She filed that away.

"How long since your rider went back to report?"

A hesitation. Then, barely, a shrug—*no rider*. Which meant Corvane's hounds were still hunting blind, waiting at the one crossing that made sense, and no one knew yet that the quarry was standing behind them. Time, then. She had a little time.

"I'm going to take my hand away. You're going to kneel, and you're going to be quiet, and when this is over—if you've stayed down—you'll still be alive to hum your little song. Do we understand each other?"

He nodded again, and she felt something in him sag—not fear exactly, but relief, the terrible gratitude of a boy who had joined the crown's guard for bread and a coat and had never truly believed anyone would die out here in the gray trees. She tied his wrists with the cord from her belt-wallet, gagged him with her spare kerchief, and laid him down in the bracken like putting a child to bed.

Then she went to open the ledger.

* * *

It went wrong immediately, the way things do.

The plan had been simple: startle the horses, let the stampede break the camp's order, take the sergeant in the confusion, put the rest to flight or to sleep. Simple plans survive contact with reality about as well as eggs survive contact with cobblestones.

What actually happened was that Quentin, creeping down to the water's edge to be in position, stepped on a branch.

Not even a loud branch. A small, wet, apologetic crack, swallowed mostly by the river—and the sergeant turned anyway, because he was the careful one, the one who measured fords, and careful men turn at small sounds.

"Hold!" he called, hand going to his hilt, eyes raking the treeline. "Who's there?"

And the dice-players stopped, and the restless one stopped, and the whole delicate architecture of Wren's bookkeeping collapsed in the space of one heartbeat.

Later, she could never reconstruct the next minute properly. Memory broke it into pieces, the way ice breaks: images with gaps between them, and her body living in the gaps, doing what thirty years had trained it to do while her mind stood apart and watched with something like horror.

The horses first. She burst from the treeline on the flank of the camp, screaming—one raw, wordless shriek, the sound of a banshee of the high passes, the sound smugglers' children were warned about—and slapped the nearest horse hard on the haunch. It screamed and bolted, and the other three followed, and the camp became a chaos of hooves and shouting men and a saddlebag tearing open, spilling hardtack across the stones like dice.

The restless one came at her first. He was quick, quicker than the boy upstream had been, and he'd gotten his sword clear, and he came in low and angry with no interest in arresting anybody. Wren met steel with steel, gave ground twice to buy angle, and put her boot on the slick stone at the water's edge exactly where she wanted it, and when he lunged past his balance she simply wasn't there. He went past her, caught himself, wheeled—

—and the sergeant shouted, "Take her! Take them both!" and the second dice-player drew, and somewhere downstream Quentin was splashing wildly toward the third braid, and the world narrowed to the oldest arithmetic of all.

Two blades, one woman, bad footing.

She killed the restless one with a short stroke he never saw, because he was watching her feet and she showed him her knife-hand instead. It was clean, fast, the kind of death she had given maybe a dozen men in her years on the roads—men who had murdered, robbed, burned, who had earned it by her code and the code of every honest thief between here and the coast. He fell into the shallows and the water took him gently, turning him, carrying him off toward the sea he'd never see.

The second man hesitated. That hesitation saved his life. He looked at his sergeant, at the water, at Wren standing over a dying crown guardsman with blood on her blade and murder in her stance—and he ran. Dropped his sword and bolted up the south trail, and Wren let him go, because a running man tells no tales he hasn't run a long way to tell, and because some animal part of her was screaming that the sergeant was turning toward the river, toward the splash of a clumsy former food-taster halfway across the current.

"No—" Wren's voice tore out of her.

The sergeant had a crossbow. Of course he did. The careful one, the one who measured fords, the one Corvane trusted to do it right. He leveled it at the gray water, at the figure floundering toward the third braid with his cloak dragging him down, and Wren understood with total clarity that she would not arrive in time. Twenty yards of slippery stone lay between them, and a loaded crossbow needs half a heartbeat, and Quentin Ashgrove—who had never harmed a soul, who swallowed poison so others wouldn't have to—was going to die in the Graywash with a quarrel through his back.

She threw the knife.

She had thrown knives perhaps five hundred times in her life, at straw, at stumps, once at a man's sword-arm to save a child from a burning wagon. She had never thrown one at a royal officer wearing the crown's badge. Her grandmother's voice said something—she couldn't hear what, only that it was warning—and her arm ignored it, because her arm belonged to the roads now, to the law she'd sworn on a smuggler's grave when no other law would hold, and the law said: *you do not let the innocent die so that your hands stay clean.*

The knife took the sergeant through the throat as he loosed.

The quarrel went wild, skimming spray off the water a foot from Quentin's shoulder. The sergeant sat down slowly, as if tired, the crossbow sliding from his hands, and pressed both palms to his neck, and looked at her—not with hate, with astonishment, with the plain offended surprise of a man discovering the story he was living in was a different story than he'd thought. Then he toppled backward into the ford and the Graywash folded over him and began its patient work.

Wren stood on the bank, chest heaving, and watched the water take him.

* * *

Quentin dragged himself ashore fifty yards downstream, blue-lipped and shaking, and she made herself walk to him instead of standing there, because standing there was not survivable. She hauled him up by the collar the way she'd hauled drowning lambs as a girl, and he coughed half the Graywash onto her boots and clutched her forearm with both hands like a man clutching a rail in a storm.

"You—" cough. "Threw—"

"Yes."

"At him. For me."

"Don't make it a speech, Quentin."

But he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't bear, so she busied herself checking the camp: the scattered tack, the spilled hardtack, the dead man already fifty yards gone in the current, the fled soldier, the bound boy still lying obedient and trembling in the bracken where she'd left him. She retrieved her knife from where it had fallen. She wiped it on grass, though the river had already cleaned it. Some habits are older than sense.

Four horses had bolted; three had come back, milling at the treeline, too trained to run far. She caught the nearest by its cheek strap. A crown horse, a good one, and the irony of it tasted like copper.

The boy was crying when she came to him. Quietly, the way boys cry when they've learned that loud crying gets answered badly. He'd heard all of it—the scream, the steel, the shot—and he lay in the bracken staring at her with the badge glinting at his collar, the crowned thorn, the mark she had just spent in blood.

"You kept faith," she said. Her voice came out strange, sanded flat. "You stayed down. That buys you your life and a head start. When I cut those cords, you run south and you don't stop, and if Corvane asks you what happened, you tell him a demon of the passes took his men, and you believe it, because if you ever describe me to him I will find you, boy, and I am not someone you want found. Nod."

He nodded. She cut him free, and he scrambled up and ran, stumbling, south, and she stood listening to his footsteps fade until the river swallowed them too.

Then she was alone with the horses and the silence and the thing she had done.

* * *

Quentin found her sitting on a stone above the ford, the three horses picketed, her knife across her knees, staring at the water. He'd wrung out what he could of his clothes and built a small fire with the competence of a man who'd fed armies' worth of kitchens, and he crouched at the edge of her sight and waited, because he was learning that too.

"He wore the crown," she said finally.

Quentin said nothing.

"Thirty years, I've had one rule. One." She turned her hand over, looked at the palm as if the line of it might explain something. "The crown courts are bought and blind and cruel, fine—I've said so since I was old enough to say anything. But the king's justice and my justice were different roads to the same place, and a man wearing the king's badge was a man I didn't touch, whatever he'd done. Because the day I spill crown blood, I'm not a justiciar outside the law anymore. I'm just an outlaw with good manners."

"You saved my life."

"I know what I did." It came out harder than she meant. She made herself softer. "That's the trouble, Quentin. I'd do it again. That's what I'm sitting here learning. I keep waiting to feel like I crossed a line, and what I feel instead is—" She searched for it and hated the word when she found it. "Relieved. He'd have put a quarrel through you and ridden home to a warm fire and a commendation, and I feel *relieved* that he's dead, and there's no line left after that. A woman who kills the king's men and sleeps well afterward isn't a judge anymore. She's just weather."

Quentin sat down beside her on the cold stone. For a while the fire cracked and the Graywash talked its endless gray talk.

"My whole life," he said, "people told me what I was. A taster. A tool. A body that exists to absorb harm so important men don't have to feel it. And I believed them, because believing it was easier than asking why a seven-year-old orphan would be fed arsenic in measured doses and told it was an honor." He looked at her, and there was something in his face she'd seen only once before, on the pass, when she'd read the map and gone pale. "You're not weather, Wren. You're the only law either of us has met on these roads that was ever real. If the badge makes him lawful and the saving of my life makes you criminal, then I've learned today what the law is worth, and it's worth less than the water he's floating in."

She laughed—short, cracked, surprised out of her. "You lie well. For a taster."

"It's the first true thing I've said all week."

They buried what the river hadn't taken—the restless one, washed up in an eddy, young and stupid and dead. Wren dug; Quentin stacked stones; neither of them spoke over the grave, because there was nothing the crown's rites could offer and nothing of hers she was willing to give. She took the sergeant's badge from where it had snagged on a stone in the shallows. Silver, crowned thorn, the king's own mark. She should have thrown it in the deepest pool.

She put it in her pocket instead. Evidence, she told herself. Proof of who hunted us. She was already lying to herself and knew it. It was a weight, and weights are how you remember.

They rode north within the hour, double-mounted on stolen crown horses, leaving the fire burning deliberately—a false signal, pointing any pursuit toward the south trail. The land climbed. The pines thickened. Somewhere behind them, Corvane would learn what had happened at the Graywash, and whatever patience he had left would burn away like frost in sun.

It was Quentin, riding behind her with his arms around her waist, who finally said it, quietly, against the wind.

"There's a valley ahead. Two days, maybe three, if the map means what I think it means. My runner routes end at its mouth. Beyond that—" He hesitated. "Beyond that, everything's blank. Every map I've ever seen. Just mountains, and a symbol."

Wren looked north, where the peaks were gathering clouds around them like a jury closing ranks, and touched the badge through her pocket, and said:

"Then let's go find out what lives in the blank places."
