## 11. The Village That Isn't There

The pass had been trying to kill them for two days, and by the third morning Quentin had stopped taking it personally.

Snow came sideways off the ridge in long white lashes, and the world shrank to the width of Wren's shoulders ahead of him. She moved the way she did everything—deliberately, economically, as though the mountain were a suspect she was following rather than a force trying to bury them. Every few hundred paces she would stop, tilt her head, and listen. Quentin had learned not to ask what she heard. He'd learned instead to watch her hands: when they flexed near the knives at her belt, he stopped too.

"Smoke," she said now.

Quentin tasted the air before he thought about it—an old habit, the tongue moving against the roof of his mouth like a man checking for almond. Woodsmoke, yes, and under it something else: peat, and a faint mineral sharpness like water run over iron. A hearth-smell with an accent.

"That's wrong," he said.

Wren turned. Her face was half-lost in the hood, frost beaded on the wool where her breath escaped. "Wrong how?"

"There's no one up here." He said it with more certainty than he felt, and then realized why the certainty was there at all. He unslung the map tube from inside his coat, cracked the wax seal he'd broken and resealed so many times the wax looked like scar tissue, and spread the map against his chest to keep the wind from taking it. The mountains of Thornfell marched across the vellum in fine brown ink—every pass he'd memorized as a runner, every smuggler's cut and shepherd's stair—and here, where the smoke was coming from, the ink showed nothing at all. Not blankness exactly. The cartographer had drawn the contour lines and simply... declined to fill them. A valley shaped like a cupped hand with nothing held in it.

"Cartographers don't leave valleys empty by accident," Wren said. She'd come close enough that he could feel the warmth of her through two layers of wool, which was its own kind of alarming.

"I know." Quentin rolled the map away. His heart was doing something complicated—fear braided through with a hunger he didn't have a name for. "My grandmother had a saying. When the map is quiet, the country is hiding."

"Your grandmother," Wren said, "was a kitchen woman who told you things kitchen women shouldn't know."

"She was a lot of things I didn't understand until recently."

They went down into the valley because there was nowhere else to go—the storm behind them, the cold ahead, and smoke below, and men die of pride on mountains far more often than they die of wolves.

* * *

The village appeared the way a fish appears in dark water: suddenly, all at once, after you'd convinced yourself there was nothing there.

One moment they were descending through scrub pine and snow-blind whiteness; the next the trees opened and there were rooftops—low, turf-covered, smoke rising from vents in the sod itself as if the houses were breathing. Stone walls older than any masonry Quentin knew, fitted without mortar, lichened grey-green. A millrace, half-frozen, turning a wheel with a sound like slow applause. And people—a woman shaking out a blanket, two children chasing a dog between the woodpiles, an old man mending a net though the nearest water was the millpond—all of them pausing mid-motion to look at the strangers walking out of the storm.

Quentin counted forty souls before he made himself stop counting. Forty people in a valley that did not exist.

"They can't have seen us coming," Wren murmured. "There's no road. There's no—" She stopped herself. "You understand what this means. Either we're dead and dreaming, or everything we've been told about these fells is a lie."

"I've been assuming both since a courier died under my table," Quentin said.

An old woman came out of the nearest house without hurry, wrapping a shawl over her shoulders as she walked. She was small and straight-backed, with white hair braided flat against her skull in a style Quentin had never seen and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a tune before you place it. She stopped six paces away and looked at him—not at Wren, not at their weapons, but at him, at his face, with an expression that frightened him worse than Corvane's riders ever had.

She said something in a language he did not know, low and liquid, full of sounds like water finding cracks in stone.

"I'm sorry," Quentin began, "we only want shelter until—"

And then the old woman's face crumpled, and she said, in accented but perfectly fluent Thornic, "*Vaelmoor remembers you,*" and crossed the six paces to take his frozen hands in hers.

Her palms were warm. Everything about her was warm, impossibly warm, as if the valley kept its own season. Behind her the villagers were drifting closer, and Quentin heard the word passed among them like a coal from hand to hand—in that unknown language first, and then in Thornic, whispered: *the returned taster. The returned taster has come home.*

"I think," Quentin said, very carefully, "there's been a mistake."

"No," the old woman said. She was still holding his hands. Her eyes were wet and fierce and ancient. "There has been a great many mistakes, child. You are not one of them."

* * *

They gave them a house with a turf roof and a hearth that burned peat and pine together, and food that Quentin ate with his whole attention because that was how he did everything now—he could not look at a bowl without reading it.

Barley soup, thick with root vegetables he half-knew: a carrot's cousin, sharper; a turnip gone sweet and almost floral. Flat bread baked on a stone, dense, faintly bitter at the crust. And in the broth, an herb he did not know at all, green and resinous, leaving a coolness on the tongue like mint's northern cousin—and beneath it, unmistakable to a tongue trained his whole life for exactly this, a whisper of something medicinal. Bitter. Alkaline. The ghost-note of a compound he had swallowed in graduated doses since he was seven years old.

His spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"You taste it," said the old woman, who had introduced herself as Maren and appointed herself his shadow. She sat across the table watching him eat with an expression of profound satisfaction, like a teacher whose student has finally shown her work.

"Taste what?"

"The *selvane.*" She pronounced it carefully, teaching him. "In your tongue there is no word, because your tongue was never meant to know it. It grows only here. It grows only—" she paused, choosing, "—only for certain guests."

"What does it do?"

"To most, nothing. A flavor. To others..." Maren folded her small hands. "To others it is a key that fits a lock they were built with."

Quentin set down the spoon. Across the room, Wren stood by the window with her back to the wall and her eyes on the door—she hadn't eaten, wouldn't eat anything she hadn't watched prepared, and he loved her a little for it and hated himself a little for noticing he loved it. "Maren," he said. "I'm going to ask you questions, and you're going to want to answer them slowly and kindly, like I'm a child or a mourner. Don't. Answer them fast and true. Who am I? Why do these people call me *returned*?"

Maren studied him for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing blue behind the ice on the window.

"Your name," she said, "in the old tongue, would be Quentyn Ashgrove of the line of Vaelmoor. Your father was born in the house across the lane. Your grandfather kept the tasting-office—did you think that title was invented for kings' kitchens? In Vaelmoor, the taster was a sacred office. The one who stood between the people and the poison. The one whose body was made a fortress so that others need not be poisoned at all."

"My body was made a fortress by *orphans' homes and royal physicians.*"

"Yes," Maren said gently. "Who do you imagine paid them? Who do you imagine wrote the regimen—the doses, the years, the exact bitterness of each increment? It is all in the margins of the thing you carry, taster. We wrote your childhood thirty years ago. We have been waiting for you to grow up ever since."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and was hushed. Quentin found that his hands were steady, which surprised him, because nothing else was. He had spent his life believing himself an accident—a boy the crown happened to buy cheap, a body that happened to hold. And accident was bearable. Accident required no one. But *designed*—designed meant wanted. Designed meant someone had planned for him before he existed, had dosed him through fevers and fasts, had built a lock into his blood and mailed the key to a hidden valley.

"Why?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Why build me at all?"

Maren rose, went to the window beside Wren—who stiffened, then, deliberately, relaxed—and looked out at the darkening valley, at the turf roofs smoking softly under the snow.

"Because a country that no longer exists needed one door left open," she said. "And doors do not open themselves. Doors open for blood."

"And mine is the right blood."

"Yours is the last blood. There is a difference, child, and the difference is the whole sad history of our people." She turned back to him, and her face in the firelight was suddenly very old, older than her body, old as the erased treaties. "Eat your soup. Tomorrow I will tell you what your blood is *for*. Tonight you should sleep in a house your father's family raised, and let it be enough."

It should not have been enough. It should have been unbearable—to be claimed so completely, so suddenly, by people and a past he'd never known. Quentin understood that. He sat with the soup cooling in front of him and waited for the revulsion, the suspicion, the taster's reflex that trusted no gift and no giver.

What he felt instead was terrifyingly simple: the bread smelled like home.

He pushed the bowl away.

* * *

He found Wren later at the edge of the village, standing where the millrace vanished under ice, watching the dark shapes of the houses against the snow-glow. She'd taken first watch without being asked, because of course she had; Wren Calloway slept the way other people confessed, rarely and with difficulty.

"Well?" she said, without turning. She always knew his footsteps. "Is it a trap?"

"I've been asking myself that for three hours."

"And?"

Quentin came to stand beside her. Below the millrace, under a shelf of black ice, water moved with a sound like someone speaking just below hearing. "A trap wants something from you. It shows you the bait and hides the hook. This—" he gestured at the valley, the breathing roofs, the smoke, the forty souls who had looked at him like a returned son, "—this doesn't hide anything. They told me everything in one evening. My bloodline, my purpose, the map. Too much. Too fast."

"So you don't trust it."

"I trust that they believe it." He blew out a breath that steamed and tore away southward, toward the capital, toward the king's kitchens, toward a life that had ended under a banquet table. "Wren, I've been invisible my whole life. A taster learns to be furniture. And then I walk into a valley that isn't on any map, and forty people say my name like it means something, and an old woman holds my hands and says *welcome home*—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "Believing it would be the easiest thing I've ever done. That's what frightens me."

Wren was silent for a while. Snow ticked softly against her hood.

"When I was disgraced," she said at last, "they took my chambers, my seal, my name off the rolls. And for a year I wandered thinking I wanted it back. Then one night a farmwife I'd helped fed me dinner at her own table—me, a lawless woman, a stranger—and called me 'madam justice' out of pure habit, and I cried into the stew like a child." She glanced at him, frost-lashed and level-eyed. "Wanting to belong makes fools of better people than us. So we test it. Tomorrow, whatever the elder tells you, we test it. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And Quentin." She hesitated—a rare thing; her hesitations cost her visibly. "When they said *the returned taster*. What did it feel like? Honestly."

He looked out at the village that wasn't there, at the hearth-smoke rising from houses his family had raised, at the warm windows of a home he'd never seen until tonight.

"It felt like a door opening," he said. "And I've learned to check what's behind doors before I walk through."

Behind them, in the house across the lane, a lamp flared in a window Quentin was almost sure had been dark a moment before. Maren stood silhouetted in it, watching them, and even at this distance Quentin could see she was not smiling.

"Come inside," she called, in Thornic, in that patient, terrible kindness. "Both of you. There are things the valley must show you tonight—before the storm closes the passes. Before you decide whether you are guests..."

Her voice dropped, and the wind nearly took it, but not quite.

"...or the last of us."
