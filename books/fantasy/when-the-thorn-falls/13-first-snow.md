## 13. First Snow

The snow came in the night, without ceremony, the way the truly important things always did.

Quentin woke to a silence so complete it felt like deafness. In the capital, snowfall announced itself—the scrape of shovels, the cries of vendors, the muffled argument of carters whose wheels had frozen to the cobbles. Here there was only white. He lay in the narrow bed in Maren's guest room and listened to nothing at all, and understood, before he had even risen, that winter had closed the passes.

He dressed quickly and went out into the blue-grey light of early morning, breath smoking. The valley of Vaelmoor—or what remained of Vaelmoor, this hidden fold of earth the maps refused to name—had been transformed. The terraced fields were buried. The stone houses wore thick caps of white like men pulled into hoods. The mountain walls that ringed them on every side had gone from grey granite to blinding white, and the single gap through which they'd entered, the notch in the eastern ridge, was now indistinguishable from any other scar on the rock.

No way in. No way out.

Wren stood in the middle of the lane, arms crossed, studying that notch with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. Snow had gathered on her shoulders and in her cropped hair, and she hadn't bothered to brush it off.

"It's early," she said when he stopped beside her. "The passes don't usually close for another month. I've crossed them in late Frostmoon before."

"And?"

"And someone who wanted us trapped would be very pleased with the timing." She turned to look at him. Her eyes were the pale grey of river ice, and they missed nothing. "Or the mountains are just being mountains."

"Which do you believe?"

"I believe in what I can prove." She kicked at the snow. "Which is nothing, yet. So we wait, and we watch, and we keep our knives sharp."

She walked off toward Maren's house, leaving prints like small dark mouths in the white. Quentin stood a moment longer, looking up at the ridge, at the notch that had become a wall, and felt something strange move through him—something he was ashamed to name until he'd named it.

Relief.

Not the relief of escape postponed. Something deeper and more shameful than that. For eleven days he had been running—from Corvane's riders, from the courier's dying hand, from the map folded against his heart—and some exhausted animal part of him had heard the passes close and thought: *safe*. As if safety were possible. As if Lord Corvane, who smiled like a host and interrogated like an undertaker, had ever once lost a quarry to mere weather.

He shook the thought off and followed Wren inside, where the smell of baking bread made the whole room feel like a held breath.

* * *

The children found him on the fourth day of the snow.

He had been helping in the communal kitchen—a long, low building with a hearth wide enough to roast a lamb standing up, where three of the village women worked from dawn to dusk feeding forty souls through the winter stores. Quentin had offered his hands because his hands needed something to do, and because kitchens were the one country where he had never been a stranger. Peel, pare, render, knead. His body knew these languages even when his mind was elsewhere, turning over alchemical notations in the map's margins, the word *Vaelmoor*, his father's name spoken by Maren with a reverence that still made his skin prickle.

The first child appeared at the doorway like a wraith, all eyes and chapped cheeks, watching him work the dough with an intensity most adults reserved for executions. Then a second. Then four more, crowded shoulder to shoulder, breathing the smell of bread like it was religion.

"You're doing it wrong," said the first one, a girl of perhaps eight with a burn-scar on her forearm that made Quentin's chest tighten—he knew burns; he'd seen what happened to kitchen boys who flinched near ovens. "You have to let it rest. Grandmother says you're strangling it."

"Is your grandmother a baker?"

"She's Maren."

The other children giggled. Quentin looked down at the dough, then at the girl, and set the whole mass aside under a cloth with as much dignity as he could muster. "There," he said. "Unstrangled. Now what?"

Now what became flour on noses and a demonstration of how to test yeast with warm water and honey, and how to read an oven by holding your hand inside and counting—*one thousand, two thousand*—and how salt should never meet yeast directly because salt is patient and will kill anything it's given time to kill. The children absorbed it the way children absorb everything: greedily, chaotically, asking questions that began with *why* and did not end.

"Why does the bread rise?"

"Because the yeast eats the sugars and breathes out air."

"Air is food?"

"For yeast. Everything eats something. Everything is eaten by something else." He heard himself say it and paused, the words landing somewhere older than the conversation. That was a taster's catechism. That was the first law of his life: everything is eaten by something else, and the wise learn which things poison them slowly instead of quickly.

The burn-scarred girl—her name was Ilsabet—watched him with those too-serious eyes. "You talk like the old ones talk. Like you're saying one thing and meaning another."

"Your old ones are very perceptive."

"They say you're the returned taster." She said it plainly, without awe or accusation, the way children state weather. "They've been waiting for you since before my mother was born. They're going to be very upset if you're wrong."

Quentin's hands went still on the dough. Around him the children waited, flour-dusted, unbothered, certain that grown men answered questions directly if you simply asked them.

"I'm going to be very upset if I'm wrong too," he said finally, and Ilsabet nodded as though this were satisfactory, and the lesson resumed.

* * *

By the second week, it had become an institution.

Maren arranged it with the efficiency of a woman who had spent seventy years making decisions for others and saw no reason to stop. The children would come to the kitchen each afternoon after their letters and numbers—letters and numbers taught by an ancient, bent man named Odran who claimed to remember Vaelmoor entire, before the treaty, before the erasure—and Quentin would teach them what he called kitchen-craft and what Odran, observing one session with wet eyes, called *the tasting arts restored*.

Quentin objected to the phrase. Odran overrode him. It was that kind of village.

So he taught them. He taught them to identify herbs blindfolded, crushed between fingers and held to the nose—thyme from savory, sweet marjoram from oregano, the wild garlic that grew under the southern rocks even in deep winter. He taught them the poison-lore that was safe to teach: which mushrooms lied about their cousins, why elderberries must be cooked, how hemlock smells like mouse nests and should never, ever be tasted to check. They loved the poison-lore best. Children always did. Death was delicious to those who hadn't yet met it.

And because he could not help himself—because thirty years of being a living ledger would not stay quiet—he taught them the deeper game. How to taste a broth and know whether the cook had been sad. How bitterness at the back of the tongue meant something scorched, but bitterness at the front meant something wrong. How a taster worked: not swallowing, but holding; letting the tongue report while the mind stayed clear.

Ilsabet raised her hand. "Have you swallowed?"

The kitchen went quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Yes," Quentin said.

"What was the worst one?"

"Arsenic. Bitter almonds. You'll know it if you ever smell it—run, don't taste."

"Did it hurt?"

"Not the way you mean." He looked at his hands, flour-white, steady. "It hurt the way being useful hurts. Quietly, over years, where no one sees."

The children considered this with the gravity it deserved, and then a boy named Perrin asked if he could taste the soup base and hold it without swallowing, and normalcy resumed, and Quentin let out a breath he felt he'd been holding for thirty years.

Wren watched from the doorway sometimes.

She never came in—she stood with her arms crossed in the cold, half in shadow, wearing her road-worn coat and her justice's face—but she watched. Once he caught her mouthing along when he recited the oven-counting. Once, when Perrin dropped a crock and burst into tears, Quentin saw her take half a step forward before catching herself and retreating into the snow. She had sentenced men to hang on smugglers' roads; she had killed a crowned rider at the ford and not slept properly since; and a crying child undid her more than either.

He didn't mention it. There were mercies you offered by pretending not to see.

* * *

On the evening it happened, the evening he would measure all other evenings against for the rest of his life, the snow had stopped.

That was rare enough. The storm had blown itself out sometime in the afternoon, and by dusk the sky above the valley was the hard clean violet of high-altitude winter, scattered with stars sharp as nailheads. Someone had decided—Maren, presumably, deciding as she decided everything—that the bread baked that day belonged to everyone, and long trestle tables were dragged into the square and covered with cloths, and the whole village came out into the cold because warmth, in a place like this, was something you made together or not at all.

Forty people at two tables under the stars, eating bread and bean stew and a hard pale cheese from caves under the western ridge. Lanterns hung from poles. Breath rising like incense. The old people speaking Vaelmoori in low, musical phrases that sounded like water over stones, and the children answering in the same tongue without thinking, the language alive in them the way Quentin's immunity was alive in him—something planted by hands long vanished, growing still.

Quentin sat among them and did not speak much. He had never been good at feasts; at court he'd stood behind the king's chair with a silver spoon and an empty stomach, present and invisible, cataloguing flavors for danger. But this was different. Here no one needed him to die for their supper. Here they simply handed him bread and expected him to eat it.

Ilsabet sat across from him, tearing her loaf with great concentration. Perrin argued with another boy about whether stars were holes or fires. An old woman with a face like a walnut sang something short and looping, and half the table joined in, and the melody wound through the cold air and came back changed.

"The returned taster doesn't sing?" Maren asked, settling beside him. She smelled of woodsmoke and dried mint.

"I don't know the words."

"So learn them." She said it the way she said everything, as if the matter were already settled. "You have time. The mountain has closed its mouth. Winter is long here, taster. Long enough to learn songs. Long enough to learn who you are."

"And who am I, according to Vaelmoor?"

"According to Vaelmoor, you are the son of Aldric, who kept the Door, who stayed when staying was required." She looked at him with eyes that had seen the treaty signed, or close enough. "But I think you should decide who you are yourself. We have waited eighty years. We can wait for you to finish becoming."

She rose to help carry the stew, and Quentin sat with the lantern light moving on his hands, and around him the village talked and laughed and sang, and for one evening—one whole, unbroken evening—the thing he had spent his life pretending not to want settled over him like a second coat.

A home. Not a garret over the palace kitchens where the ceiling leaked and the guards knew his name only as *the tongue*. Not a post at a table where survival meant emptiness. A place with his craft in it, his hands needed, his silence welcomed rather than required. He looked down the table at Wren, who was listening to Odran tell some story with her guard fractionally lowered, lantern-light catching the grey of her eyes, and he thought: *she could—*

He stopped himself. He had learned long ago not to reach for things while they were still warm; reaching burned.

Later, when the tables were cleared and the lanterns guttering, he stood alone in the square for a moment, looking up. The stars over Thornfell were enormous. Somewhere beyond that eastern ridge, past leagues of closed white passes, Corvane was doing whatever Corvane did in the dark: waiting, spending, planning. Somewhere south, a court was telling itself stories about a missing taster and a dead courier. And here, in a valley that did not exist, forty people slept soundly behind doors that had kept a secret for eighty years.

*We have waited eighty years,* Maren had said.

*They're going to be very upset if you're wrong,* Ilsabet had said.

He thought of the map folded in his room, its margins dense with alchemical script he was only beginning to read, its door-symbol drawn in a hand he was beginning, terribly, to suspect he recognized. He thought of Maren's careful phrasing whenever his father's name came up—*who stayed, who kept the Door*—and what it might mean to keep a door, and whether keeping was a thing done from outside.

The last lantern in the square went out. The cold pressed close and clean.

Quentin turned toward the house—and stopped.

Down the lane, silhouetted against the starlit snow, Wren was standing at the valley's edge, facing the eastern notch, utterly still. Watching it the way she watched everything: as if it owed her answers.

As he started toward her, she raised one hand—not greeting. Warning. And in the silence of the falling night, faint and far and wrong, from beyond the closed pass, came a sound he had hoped the winter had buried:

Dogs.
