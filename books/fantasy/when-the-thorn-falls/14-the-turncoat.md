## 14. The Turncoat

Wren Calloway woke before the frost did.

That was how she thought of it now—frost as a living thing in the valley, breathing white off the rooftops of Vaelmoor's hidden village, creeping down the eaves and pooling in the wheel-ruts like spilled milk. She lay on the narrow pallet Maren had given her, listening to the house settle around her the way old houses did, wood ticking against wood, and she let herself have thirty breaths of stillness before the day demanded her spine.

Thirty breaths was a luxury. On the smugglers' roads she'd learned to sleep in slices, an hour here, forty minutes there, her body rising to every snap of brush and shift of wind. Here, in this valley that no treaty acknowledged and no map admitted to, the silence itself had become the thing that woke her. It was too complete. A silence with nothing hiding in it was a silence she didn't trust.

She dressed in the dark, buckled her knife where it had lived for eleven years—left hip, angled forward, drawn across the body—and stepped out into the blue hour before dawn.

The village looked like something remembered rather than built. Stone houses hunched under slate roofs furred with rime. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, straight columns because there was no wind, none at all, as though the mountains cupped their hands around the valley and would not let even the air leave. Somewhere a dog gave one low bark and thought better of it. Beyond the last house, the terraced fields stood fallow and silvered, and above them the slopes climbed into pine, and above the pines the peaks went up into that hard, glittering darkness that was not quite sky.

A secret country. She had walked its roads for six days now and still half-expected it to dissolve when she blinked.

She made her rounds. It was habit more than duty—she held no office here, commanded no one, and the villagers watched her the way farmers watch weather they can't decide whether to pray for or against. But Wren Calloway had spent eleven years walking circuits at dawn along roads where bandits hung their dead from trees as warnings, and a body did not unlearn that. She checked the eastern path first, the one that climbed toward the sheep-pastures and the cairn-line. She checked the granary door. She checked the well, the shrine with its soot-darkened carving of a door within a door, and finally the low stone wall behind Maren's house where Quentin Ashgrove had taken to sitting with his maps and his bitter tea, looking like a man trying to read a letter written in a language he used to know.

He wasn't there. Of course he wasn't. The sun wasn't even up.

She was turning back toward the square when she saw the boy slip out of the tannery lane.

Tam. Sixteen winters, maybe seventeen—all elbows and hollow cheeks, with that particular hungry watchfulness the orphaned and the near-orphaned carried like a second skin. He moved fast and low, hugging the wall, a bundle wrapped in sacking clutched to his chest, and he glanced over his shoulder twice in ten paces, which was twice too many for a boy doing honest errands before dawn.

Wren faded back into the shadow of the shrine without deciding to. Eleven years of road-work had made the decision for her; her feet simply knew what suspicion looked like and got out of its sightline.

She followed him.

Not quickly. Not with the direct pursuit that would spook him. She kept to the backs of houses, matched her steps to the crunch of her own breath, and tracked him by sound through the frozen lanes—the scuff of his boots on packed snow, the soft knock of the bundle against his hip. He cut west past the smokehouse, ducked through the gap in the drystone wall where generations of thieving children had worn the stones smooth, and came out on the lower pasture path, the one that wound down toward the valley's throat, where the avalanche channels Maren had loosed against Corvane's vanguard had choked the road with a hundred thousand tons of snow.

He stopped at the third marker-stone. Wren knew the place: an old waystone, older than the village by centuries, carved with the same double-door symbol that marked the map sewn into Quentin's coat. Moss grew over it now. Tam knelt beside it, dug into the snow at its base with bare fingers—he hadn't brought a tool, which meant he'd done this before, meant the ground knew the shape of his hands—and lifted out a flat tin box.

Her stomach went cold and stayed cold.

She watched him work. He unwrapped the bundle from under his arm: bread, a twist of dried fish, a small clay flask. Provisions. Payment, or toll. He tucked them into the box alongside whatever was already inside—she couldn't see what from here, but she could guess, and guessing made her jaw tighten. Then he closed the lid, pressed the snow back over it, smoothed the surface with his palm until it looked like any other drift, and stood there a moment with his head bowed like a boy at a grave.

Then he ran. Not south—he couldn't go south; the pass was buried—but east, along the contour of the slope, toward the shepherd tracks that switchbacked up toward the high col. Toward the only place in this sealed valley where a message could be passed outward: the rock-scree above the tree line, where Corvane's outriders had been seen three days ago, patient as crows, waiting for winter to do their siege-work for them.

Wren stood in the shadow of the waystone long after he was gone, and felt the cold come up out of the ground and into her bones.

* * *

By full light, she had the tin box open on Maren's table, and the room had gone very quiet.

There were four messages inside. She laid them out in a row with the care she'd give evidence at a tribunal, because that was exactly what they were. Three were letters in a careful, cramped hand—a child's hand trying to be a man's hand—addressed to no one and signed with nothing, but each describing the valley in numbers: the count of fighting men, the state of the food stores, the location of the elders' meeting-house, and, in the latest, written in ink still sharp enough to smell, a sentence that made Maren close her eyes as if struck:

*The old woman keeps the true key in the floor beneath her bed.*

The fourth item was not a letter. It was a strip of cloth, royal gray, stamped with the badge Wren knew too well—the open eye of the Crown's intelligence service, Lord Corvane's own mark. A token. A promise. Whoever collected these messages had left it as surety: *deliver, and you will be carried south. Deliver, and you will be safe.*

"Passage south," Maren said. Her voice was dry as seed-husks. She sat with both hands flat on the table, as if holding it down. "That is what they offered him."

"It's what they offer everyone." Wren kept her voice level. "Corvane's riders have been promising it since the first snow. *Bring us the key, bring us the taster, and we'll take you out of the cold.* To people shut in a dying valley with winter coming down like a lid, that's not a bribe. It's a rescue."

"He is a child." Maren's hands pressed harder. "His mother died in the fever year. His father was one of the nine we sent to hold the channel gates, and the mountain took him with the rest. Tam has been alone since he was twelve years old."

"I know what he is." Wren met the old woman's eyes. "I've known boys like him on every road from here to the capital. They're not wicked. They're hungry, and someone finally offered to feed them, and they don't ask what's in the bowl."

"And what will you do?" It was not Maren who asked. The room had filled while Wren's back was turned—villagers drawn by the gravity wells that gossip becomes in small places. Old Hesk, who kept the bees and had once, allegedly, fought at the channel gates beside Tam's father. The widow Sera with her two daughters pressed behind her skirts. Brannic, broad and slow-spoken, who had been the first to call Wren *justiciar* when she broke up the knife-fight between the Halloran brothers, using a word she had never asked for and could not put down. A dozen more crowded the doorway, breath steaming, eyes going from the letters to the gray cloth to Wren's face.

"The boy sold us," Brannic said. "Sold the valley. Sold the elder. If Corvane's men get that key—"

"They won't. I'll move it within the hour."

"That's not the point!" Hesk's voice cracked like ice underfoot. "The point is what happens to traitors! On the roads—" He stopped himself, but everyone heard the rest of it. *On the roads, Wren Calloway hangs murderers from their own cart-wheels.* They had heard the stories. That was why they'd started calling her justiciar. That was why they were standing in this room with their arms folded and their grief hardened into something with edges.

"The roads are yours now," Wren said quietly. "You asked me to sit judgment. So hear me: I will judge him. At sundown, in the square, with the whole valley present, the way law should be done—in the open, before witnesses. Until then, no one touches him. No one threatens him. No one so much as spits in his path. Is that understood?"

Nobody answered. Nobody left, either. And in the doorway, half-hidden behind Brannic's shoulder, she caught a flash of pale face and wide dark eyes and knew, with the sick certainty of eleven years' practice, that Tam had already heard.

He ran again. This time she let him.

* * *

She found him at dusk, or rather she found where he wasn't.

The whole afternoon the village had buzzed like a kicked hive. Children were called indoors. Men gathered in knots and spoke low and stopped speaking when Wren passed. Someone had thrown a stone through the window of the room where Tam slept—it was the room above the smithy, she learned, a cot lent by a neighbor after the fever year—and someone else had nailed a strip of black cloth to the smithy door, which on the high roads meant one thing and one thing only: *death promised here.* The valley had tried on its new justice and found it fit well, and the fit frightened her more than Corvane's riders ever had.

She searched the obvious places first—the hay barn, the chapel-loft, the tannery vats—then stopped searching and started thinking. Where does a boy go when the whole world has turned to faces? Not out. Out was death, in this cold, and Tam wanted to live; that was the entire tragedy of him. In, then. Down. Into the one place in the village where nobody went anymore because nobody needed to.

The old root cellar beneath the ruined meeting-house had been dug before anyone living was born, back when Vaelmoor still had reason to store for sieges. Its entrance was a collapsed-looking tumble of stone behind the shrine—collapsed-looking being the operative word, because nothing in this valley was quite what it seemed. Wren shifted three stones, dropped into the dark, and lit the stub of candle she'd pocketed on instinct.

He was curled in the far corner behind a rack of empty jars, knees to chest, the gray badge-cloth crushed in one fist.

"Don't." His voice came out scraped raw. "Don't—please—I didn't tell them about the key, I never told them where Maren keeps it, I wrote it down but I never—I didn't think they'd actually—"

"Come out where I can see you."

"No." But he came out anyway, unfolding joint by joint, a scarecrow assembling itself in the candlelight. His lips were blue-tinged. He'd been down here for hours, in the cold, and had been too frightened to build even the smallest fire. "They're going to hang me. Brannic said—they were saying in the smithy—they're going to give me to you because you've done it before, you hang people, everyone knows what you did to the Marlow brothers, they say you didn't even—"

"Tam." She said his name the way you say *halt* to a spooked horse. "Sit down. You're shaking too hard to stand."

He sat. She lowered herself onto an upturned crate opposite him, laid her knife on her knee where he could see it—not threatening, just honest—and held the candle between them.

"Now," she said. "Tell me all of it. From the beginning. And don't make it pretty, because I'll know."

It came out of him in pieces, the way confessions always did—first the frame and then the picture. It had started in autumn, before the avalanches, before the siege tightened. A man had come up the shepherd tracks alone, unarmed, smiling: a scout named Ferro, soft-voiced, with a scar through his eyebrow and a pouch of southern coin and southern sweets. He hadn't threatened anything. He'd *listened*. He'd asked about Tam's father, and Tam—who had not heard his father's name spoken aloud in kindness in four years—had talked until dark. After that it was simple, the way rot is simple. First messages for coin. Then messages for the promise. Passage south, a place in the crown's service, a life where no one looked at him like an orphan or a burden. All he had to do was write down what anyone could see. How many men. How much bread. Where the elders met.

And then, last week, Ferro had asked for one more thing. Just one. *Where does the old woman hide what matters?*

"I didn't want to," Tam whispered. He was crying now, silently, the way children cry who learned young that loud crying gets punished. "But he said if I stopped, he'd tell the village himself what I'd been doing. He said they'd do worse than the crown ever would. He said—" The boy's fist tightened on the gray cloth until the knuckles went white. "He was right. Wasn't he? Look at what they did to my window. Look at the black cloth. They decided before you even—"

"Yes," Wren said. "They decided."

"And you won't stop them."

It was not a question, and it deserved better than comfort, so she gave it truth instead.

"I'm going to stand up in that square in an hour," she said, "and I'm going to hand down judgment in front of every soul in this valley. And I will tell you now, so you can decide what to do with the time between: I am not going to kill you."

His head came up. For the first time, hope and terror fought to a draw on his face.

"You fed a wolf at your father's grave because it was hungry and someone offered you meat," Wren went on. "That's what happened here. That's all that happened here. Corvane builds engines out of exactly that—hunger, grief, loneliness—and he builds them cheap, because children are cheap. Killing you wouldn't undo one word you wrote. It would only teach this valley that justice means finding the smallest person to bleed." She picked up the candle. "Stay here. When I call your name, walk out with your head up. Whatever they shout—you let me answer it. That's what a justiciar is *for*, boy. Not for the hanging. For the standing between."

She climbed out of the cellar into the last violet light, and behind her, in the dark, she heard Tam begin to sob like the child he had never been allowed to be.

* * *

The square was full by moonrise.

Torches burned in iron brackets, throwing jumping shadows across the faces of nearly two hundred people—every soul in the village who could stand, it seemed, plus the elders on the shrine-steps and Maren among them, upright as a spear. The black cloth on the smithy door had been torn down; someone had thought better of it, or someone else had done the thinking for them. The crowd was quiet in the way crowds are quiet when they've already decided and are only waiting for permission.

Wren took the center of the square with the tin box under her arm and the evidence laid out on a plank table someone had fetched—four letters, the gray cloth, the whole ugly ledger of it. She let them look. She made them look.

"Tam of Vaelmoor," she said, and her voice carried the way voices carry in cold air, clean and hard to the back rows. "For five months, this boy passed information to agents of Lord Corvane: the strength of your stores, the number of your defenders, the place where your elders gather, and—once—the supposed hiding-place of the valley's key. For this, he was promised passage south and a place in the crown's service. These are his writings. This is the token his handler left him. Do any dispute the facts?"

Silence. Then, from the back: "Hang him." A woman's voice. Wren found her face—Sera, the widow, whose brother had died holding the channel gates—and did not flinch from it.

"Hang him," someone echoed. "Road-law. Road-law!"

"Road-law is mine to speak here," Wren said, "or it is nothing, and you should tear the word off your tongues right now and go back to being a mob, because a mob is cheaper and needs no oath." She let that land. Torches cracked. Somewhere a baby began to cry and was hushed. "So hear the law as I find it.

"First: the accused is sixteen years old, an orphan of this valley's own wars, recruited by a trained agent of the Crown's intelligence service using coin, flattery, and threats of exposure. Second: the intelligence he passed was available to any observer on the ridgeline; it hastened no attack and cost no life. Third: the single dangerous item—the location of the key—came to my hands before it reached Corvane's, and the key itself has been moved beyond reach. Fourth—" and here she paused, because the fourth was the one that would cost her, and she chose to spend it anyway, spending it the way you spend blood, deliberately, knowing you cannot take it back—

"Fourth: I have read the letters. I have read every word this boy wrote, including the words he wrote that were lies. Because in the last two months, Tam began feeding Corvane false counts—understating your stores, overstating your fighters—hoping to make the valley look poor and strong enough to be left alone. He was a traitor who tried, clumsily, late, and in fear, to become a double agent. He lacked the skill for it. He did not lack the wanting."

Murmurs—skeptical, angry, disbelieving. Hesk pushed forward. "You'd have us believe a boy outwitted the spymaster of Thornfell?"

"No," Wren said. "I'd have you believe he tried, and failed, and confessed to my face in a freezing cellar while shaking too hard to stand. Which is more than most grown men I've condemned ever managed." She turned to face the whole square. "Here is my judgment. Tam of Vaelmoor is stripped of the valley's trust for a year and a day: no vote, no share in council, no office, no honor. He will labor at the tasks no one wants—the latrines, the ice-breaking, the night watches in the worst cold—and he will do them in view of everyone he wronged. And when Corvane's men next signal for their payment, it will be Wren Calloway who carries the message to them instead—one of my choosing, one of my writing. Let the spymaster learn what it costs to buy a child. Let him choke on the purchase."

She stepped back from the table. "Judgment given. Witnessed by the valley. Who stands against it?"

For a moment—no more than three heartbeats—the square was absolutely silent, and Wren allowed herself to believe.

Then Hesk spat, eloquently, into the snow. "We brought you here for justice," he said, "and you give us chores." And the murmur that went around the square then was not agreement, exactly—it was something worse, something colder: *disappointment.* Two hundred faces recalculating her value in real time. Brannic wouldn't meet her eye. The widow Sera turned away first. And at the edge of the crowd, hood up, half a shadow, Quentin Ashgrove watched her with an expression she couldn't afford to name just then.

The crowd broke apart in knots and mutters, and Wren stood alone in the torchlight with her judgment and her empty hands, understanding with perfect clarity that she had just spent the only coin this valley had ever paid her—and that mercy, spent publicly, buys ruin at the best of rates and exile at the worst.

Maren touched her elbow. "You knew," the old woman said softly. "You knew what it would cost."

"I've known what it costs for eleven years," Wren said. "I keep paying anyway. It's the only way I know to stay—"

She didn't finish, because the night did it for her. High on the eastern ridge, where the scree shone white under the risen moon, a light appeared. One lantern, swinging in a slow deliberate arc. Once. Twice. A third time.

Signal. Answer expected.

And below it, faint but unmistakable on the wind, drifting down out of the passes like a promise, came the sound that had haunted her dreams since the river ford: the belling of hounds, many hounds, moving fast.

Corvane had stopped waiting for winter.
