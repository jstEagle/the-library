## 15. Corvane Arrives

The dogs told us before the sentries did.

They began at dawn—a low, rolling chorus from the eastern ridge where the village's three half-starved herd-dogs kept their watch—and by the time I was out of Maren's guest-house with my boots half-laced, every soul in the valley had stopped what they were doing to listen. It was not the sound dogs make at wolves. Wolves they greeted with fury. This was different. This was the sound of animals who smelled something they could not name and did not like: woodsmoke carried downwind, iron, too many men moving in one body.

I stood in the cold blue light between the houses and felt the dread arrive in my stomach before my mind caught up to it. I knew that feeling. A taster learns it early—the moment before the bitterness blooms on the tongue, when the body knows something the mouth has not yet confessed.

"They've come," said a voice behind me. Tam stood in his doorway, pale, clutching his coat closed with one fist. His eyes went to mine and slid away. He had been doing that for days now—looking at me, then not looking, like a boy rehearsing a confession he'd never give. "The riders. The ones you ran from."

"How many?"

"I can't—" He shook his head. "You can't see the valley mouth from here. But the dogs wouldn't sing like that for fewer than twenty."

Twenty. Corvane had left the capital with forty, by Wren's guess, and lost some to the passes. Twenty was consistent. Twenty was enough.

* * *

By full morning the whole village had gathered in the square below Maren's longhouse—not panicking, which frightened me more than screaming would have. These were people who had spent their lives pretending not to exist. They moved with the quiet economy of the hidden: shutters checked, livestock drawn into stone barns, the smoke of hearths banked low so no telltale column would rise above the pines. Children were herded into the undercroft beneath the shrine-house, where the walls were older than any treaty and thick as a man is tall. An old woman pressed a heel of black bread into her grandson's hands as though she were tucking him into bed.

Wren found me at the edge of the square. She had her sword already, which told me everything; she only wore it when she expected to use it or wanted others to think she did.

"The ford post signalled an hour ago," she said without preamble. "Mirror-flash from the ridge. Riders in column, royal blue under grey cloaks. Moving slow. They're not hurrying because they don't need to."

"Because they know exactly where we are."

"Because they've always known." She said it flatly, but I saw the muscle work in her jaw. Wren had spent two weeks telling herself we'd outrun the spymaster, that the winter would close behind us like water over a stone. She hated being wrong more than she hated most things, and she hated a great many things. "Corvane doesn't chase, Quentin. He herds. We talked about this."

We had. I had understood it in my bones since the day he'd let me walk away on the mountain road—*because he needs a taster's blood*. Every mile we'd fled had been a mile he'd allowed us, a gate opened ahead of us, until the only direction left was the one he wanted. And now the gates were closing, all at once, and the dog-legged valley that had hidden itself from kings for three hundred years was about to learn what it cost to shelter a key.

"Maren's called the elders," Wren said. "She wants you there."

"Why me?"

"Because whatever they decide, they'll decide around you. You're the reason the wolf's at the door." She paused, and something almost gentle moved through her voice. "And you're the only one here they trust less than they fear. That makes you important."

* * *

Maren's longhouse was dim and smelled of juniper smoke and old wool. The elders sat in a horseshoe around the central hearth—nine of them, the oldest past ninety, faces like weathered root-wood—and Maren stood at the hearth's mouth with her back to the fire, so her shadow stretched long across the floor toward me when I entered. She looked older than she had a week ago. The valley's defenses had cost her something each time she touched them, and she had been touching them for days.

"You've seen the mirror-signals," she said, in Vaelmoori first and then, for Wren's benefit and mine, in Trade Tongue. "There are thirty-one riders at the valley mouth. They have stopped there." Murmurs ran the horseshoe. Stopped—that was worse than advancing. Advancing meant battle, and battle meant blood, and blood was a thing these people understood. Stopping meant patience. Stopping meant Corvane.

"He waits," Maren continued, "because he has learned our shape. He knows the passes are closing. He knows the village cannot scatter—he has watched it too long to believe it can. So he sits at the door of the house and lets hunger do his work." Her eyes found mine, dark and level. "And he knows that inside this house stands the one thing in the world he wants."

"What are the options?" Wren asked. Blunt, as always. One elder flinched at her tone; another looked at her with something close to gratitude.

"There are none you would call options," Maren said. "But there are choices. The valley has defenses older than any of us. My grandmother's grandmother held the keys to them, and hers before her, and I hold them now." She lifted her hand, and I saw for the first time the thin iron ring she wore on her thumb, black with age, set with a chip of glassy stone that caught the firelight wrong—caught it the way the map's ink had caught candlelight, the way the Door's symbol had seemed to move when I stared too long. "Three channels were cut into the high snowfields when this valley was young. Above the western approach, above the southern scree, above the river gorge. When they were made, they were made to be used once, maybe twice in a century. Snow does not forgive. If I open the channels, the avalanches will bury the approaches under fifty feet of pack ice and broken stone. Nothing crosses. Not riders. Not wagons. Not spring messengers. Not traders. Not anyone."

"For how long?" I asked.

"Until summer thaws them. Perhaps longer. The high snows are deep this year."

"And after that?" Wren again, cutting to the bone. "After the thaw, the riders dig out, or more come, and they know where the valley is. The defenses buy time, not safety."

"No." Maren's voice did not waver, but her hands closed together in front of her, knuckles whitening. "No, justice-of-the-roads, you name it truly. What the channels buy is not safety. What they buy is the choice. Time enough to decide what must be done before the world finds us—and whether that decision is ours or Lord Corvane's."

Silence in the longhouse. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the dogs had gone quiet, which was its own kind of terrible.

An old man at the end of the horseshoe—Aldric, who mended the shrine roof and had once, I gathered, been a soldier in some forgotten company—spoke without raising his hand. "Open the channels. Bury the roads. Let them sit outside and count their frozen toes. We've starved out better men than Corvane."

"We have never starved out anyone," said the woman beside him, softly. "That is the whole of our history, Aldric. We have hidden. Hiding and starving-out are not the same trade."

"Then let us learn a new trade."

"And when the thaw comes?" The woman's voice stayed soft, which somehow made it louder. "When the meltwater runs and the roads open and the blue cloaks walk in here with their lists and their questions? What trade will we have learned then, except the grave-digger's?"

It went on like that for an hour. I will not record all of it; much of it was grief wearing the clothing of argument. What I remember most clearly is that they kept looking at me. Not accusingly—or not only accusingly. There was something else underneath, something that hurt worse: hope. These people whose whole existence was a lie maintained for centuries, whose grandparents had taught them lullabies in a forbidden tongue, looked at the king's food-taster as if I might produce an answer the way I might produce a remedy for a spoiled sauce. As if the blood in my veins came with obligations attached, and those obligations pointed toward them.

Maren ended it, as she ended most things.

"I will open the channels," she said. "Not because it saves us. Because it gives us a season in which to become something other than a secret waiting to be discovered. And because—" and here her voice did finally crack, just slightly, like ice underfoot— "because I promised your father's line that Vaelmoor would not go quietly into any man's ledger. Even the crown's. Especially the crown's."

She looked at me when she said *your father's line*. The room looked at me. I kept my face still, because a taster's face is trained to keep still, but inside me something turned over like a stone in a streambed.

* * *

I climbed with her to the watch-stone above the shrine-house, because she asked me to and because I could not bear to stand in the square and wait. The ring came off her thumb. She held it up against the grey sky and spoke words in Vaelmoori that I did not understand and yet somehow did—old words, cadenced like a prayer or a warning—and pressed the glassy stone into a hollow in the standing rock that I would have sworn a moment before had been solid granite.

For ten heartbeats nothing happened. Then the mountain answered.

I felt it through my boots before I heard it—a deep, subsonic groan rolling up from the high snowfields, the sound of a giant turning in its sleep. Then the echo arrived, doubled and trebled off the valley walls, and high above the western approach a white seam split the pristine face of the snowpack. It widened. It descended. What had been silence became a roar that swallowed thought, and a river of snow the width of a street poured down the mountainside and across the road Corvane's riders would need, taking trees with it, taking boulders, taking the path itself into a churned white grave fifty feet deep.

Then the second channel opened above the southern scree. Then the third, in the gorge, and the river itself rose white and furious, choked with avalanche debris, and the water climbed its banks and ate the ford.

Three times the mountain roared. Three times the village below flinched as one body. And when it was done, when the echoes died away into the ringing quiet that follows great violence, the valley of the hidden people was sealed inside a fist of ice and stone, and the army at its mouth was sealed out.

Maren put her ring back on. Her hand was shaking. Mine was shaking harder.

"It is done," she said. Her voice was very tired. "Now comes the part I feared."

"What part is that?"

"Now they know we are here, child. Not where—we have buried the where. But *that*." She turned her dark eyes on me. "Lord Corvane sat at the valley mouth and watched three avalanches fall precisely where he needed them to fall. Do you imagine he believes in coincidence? He has read the same treaties we have. He knows what was erased. Avalanches in three places at once, on command, is not weather to a man like that. It is architecture." She drew a slow breath. "He will stop believing we are hiding. He will begin believing we are defended. And a man who wants a treasure does not leave when the chest turns out to be locked. He goes to find the key."

We both knew what the key was. I felt it in my blood, in the engineered patience of my veins, in every swallow of poison I had ever taken for a king who never learned my name.

* * *

That evening the village did something I did not expect. They sang.

I heard it first from the guest-house window—low voices, many voices, rising from the square in a melody with no harmony I recognized, in a language that predated every border on every map I had ever tasted my life around. Vaelmoori. Sung openly, at full voice, for the first time in perhaps a hundred years, because the roads were buried and the wind was theirs and the night was dark, and because a people who had whispered for three centuries needed, just once, to fill their own valley with the sound of themselves.

I stood at the window with my arms crossed on the sill and listened until my throat ached. Wren sat behind me sharpening her sword by candlelight, and even she had stopped working to listen, the whetstone motionless in her hand.

"It sounds like mourning," she said quietly.

"It sounds like defiance."

"Those are often the same song." She set the sword down. "Quentin. We need to talk about the thaw."

"I know."

"Do you? Because they're singing like the avalanches solved something, and you and I both know they bought a season, nothing more. Come summer, Corvane comes back with twice the men and siege engines if he wants them, and no channel-cutting will stop that. Whatever happens next has to happen before the thaw. The Door. Your father's vigil. The vault." She said the word *vault* carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "We can't wait out the winter singing."

"I know," I said again. But I didn't move from the window. Below, the song swelled, and children's voices rose above the adults', clear and unafraid, and I thought: *this is what it costs to be found. And this is what it would cost to leave them.*

Tam appeared in the doorway while Wren was talking—appeared and hovered, working his cap in his hands, and I marked again how he would not meet my eyes, how he glanced at the map-case under my arm and away, at Wren's sword and away. I was too full of avalanche-roar and old songs to read him properly. That is my excuse, and it is a poor one. A taster is trained to notice what does not fit: the almond note under the honey, the courier's sweat at a feast. I noticed nothing. I was listening to singing.

"Quentin," Tam said. "The elders want you at the shrine. Maren says—" He hesitated. "She says the watch-stone showed her something, and she wants the returned taster to see it before the light goes."

Wren and I followed him out into the blue dusk. The song had ended; smoke stood straight up from the banked hearths; the air was bitter and clean. And as we crossed the square, far off beyond the western wall of ice and stone, so faint it might have been imagination, we heard it:

A single horn. Long, patient, unhurried.

Corvane's company, announcing that they had seen the avalanches, survived the edges of them, and settled in to wait.

The horn sounded once more and faded. Maren, waiting at the shrine steps, had heard it too. She looked very small against the ancient stones.

"A season," she said. "Perhaps less. Come inside. There is something you must see, and I am afraid there is not much time to see it."
