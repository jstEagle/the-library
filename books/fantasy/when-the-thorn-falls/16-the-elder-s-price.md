## 16. The Elder's Price

Wren Calloway had sat through a hundred confessions in her years on the bench, and she had learned to read them the way Quentin read the taste of wine: not for what was said, but for what was swallowed. The pauses. The dry mouth. The moment when a man's hands stopped moving because his body knew, before his mind did, that the next words would cost him.

Maren's hands stopped moving on the third evening of their confinement in the hidden village.

Snow had been falling without interruption since dawn—a soft, patient snow that meant to stay until spring—and the elder's house smelled of peat smoke and dried herbs and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a mountain. The room was low-ceilinged and warm and crowded with the accumulated weight of a life: bundles of yarrow hanging from beams, clay pots sealed with wax, a loom with half-woven cloth still in it, abandoned mid-row as if the weaver had simply decided there were better things to do with her remaining time.

Wren sat across from her on a stool by the fire. Quentin was out—teaching the village children to make bread, of all things, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, flour on his wrists like a man who had never worn chains in his life—and Maren had asked to speak with Wren alone.

That alone had told Wren everything before either of them opened their mouths.

"You've been watching me," Maren said. She spoke Vaelmoori first, then translated herself into Thornish, her accent old-fashioned, vowels rounded like river stones. "Since the night you came down from the pass. You sit where you can see the door. You count who comes in. You sleep light."

"I sleep how I sleep," Wren said. "It's kept me alive."

"It has." Maren nodded slowly, as if confirming something she'd already written down somewhere. "And you don't trust us."

"Should I?"

"No." The old woman said it plainly, without offense, the way one might say *it will rain tomorrow*. "You shouldn't trust anyone who is glad you're here. Gladness makes people stupid. We have been glad for three days now, and it has made us foolish—we've sung songs in the square that haven't been sung aloud in sixty years. We've spoken our own tongue where the wind could carry it." Her eyes, pale as skimmed milk, settled on Wren with terrible gentleness. "The boy who went south. Tam."

Wren said nothing. That, too, was testimony.

"He writes letters," Maren said. "Or he did, before you caught him. I know. I've known since the first week he came to us. He thinks an old woman doesn't notice a child counting the guard rotations." She folded her hands in her lap. They were spotted, knuckle-swollen, steady. "I let him keep writing. Do you want to know why?"

"Because you wanted Corvane to find you," Wren said quietly. It wasn't a guess anymore; it hadn't been a guess since the avalanche channels opened and closed behind them like a held breath. "You've been waiting for him."

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Wren had seen in a winter full of sad things. "A village can hide for sixty years," she said. "But hiding isn't living. Hiding is just dying slowly, in a place no one will find the body. I am ninety-one years old. There are eleven children in this valley. Eleven. When I was a girl there were two hundred souls here, and the bells rang on feast days, and we traded wool and medicine down the southern roads like any other people under any other sky." She looked into the fire. "I let Tam write because I decided, some months ago, that if the crown was going to come for us anyway, better it happen while there are still enough of us left to choose how we end. Better we be found than forgotten."

"That's not courage," Wren heard herself say. "That's surrender with good posture."

Maren laughed—a real laugh, cracked and sudden, startling in the small room. "Oh, you sound like Aldric."

The name landed in Wren's chest like a dropped stone.

"Aldric Ashgrove," Maren said, and the laughter drained out of her voice as quickly as it had come. "Quentin's father. You didn't know that name yet. I can see it. He'll have told you nothing, because he knows nothing—that was the whole design of it. But you're a justice. Were a justice. And justices hate a locked room." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed to the back wall of the house, where a shelf of clay pots stood in ranks like soldiers. She took one down, blew dust from its lid, and set it on the table between them.

"This is why I asked to speak with you alone," she said. "Not him. Him, I couldn't bear to watch. You, I owe the truth, because you're going to be the one who decides whether he survives learning it."

* * *

The pot was sealed with black wax stamped with the same mark Wren had seen on the map—the door symbol, a triangle split by a vertical line, like a blade driven into a mountain. Inside, packed in straw, was a vial no longer than her thumb.

It looked like nothing. Grey-white powder compressed at the bottom, fine as sifted flour, and above it a measure of clear liquid that caught the firelight and held it strangely, the way ice holds it.

"Ash," Maren said. "Vaelmoori ash. Not wood ash—not anything so kind. When our alchemists built the Door of Ashes, they needed a key that could not be stolen, copied, or bought. So they made it out of a person. A drop of blood from the bloodline, burned to ash in the vault fires, suspended in water drawn from the glacier above the Door. Only an Ashgrove can drink it and live. Anyone else—" she made a small, dismissive gesture, the gesture of a woman describing a spilled cup, not a death, "—the ash recognizes its own. Strangers it burns from the inside. It was very elegant. Our alchemists were very proud of themselves. That pride is why my country no longer exists."

Wren turned the vial in her fingers. It was lighter than she expected. Most things that mattered were.

"And Quentin drinks this at the Door," she said. "And then what? The door opens, you all walk north through it, problem solved, everyone lives happily in whatever's on the other side?"

"There is no other side." Maren's voice went flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice they knew was thin. "The Door opens onto a chamber. A vigil chamber. And beyond the chamber, the vault. And beyond the vault—" she paused, and Wren watched the old woman gather herself the way one gathers a heavy load before lifting, "—beyond the vault is the reason my son's son must drink ash instead of simply being told where the keyhole is."

"His father," Wren said. "Aldric."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the snow ticked softly against the shuttered window. Somewhere down the lane, one of the children laughed—Quentin's doing, Wren would have bet her last coin—and the sound was unbearably ordinary against the weight of what was coming.

"Aldric Ashgrove was born in this house," Maren said. "Forty-eight years ago. My daughter's boy. Clever, stubborn, kind in the way that kind men are dangerous—kind to strangers, hard on himself. When the treaty came, when the borders were redrawn and Vaelmoor was struck from every map and every record, the alchemists' council did what cornered people always do. They fortified. They sealed the vault with the weapons inside it and set a keeper over it. A keeper of the bloodline, whose life was tied to the Door itself. Who could hold it shut from within. Whose body the alchemists' arts could sustain—long past any natural span—as long as the seal held and the vigil continued."

The fire popped. Wren found she had gone very still, the way she went still when reading a sentence she already knew would end badly.

"He volunteered," Maren said. "He had a wife dead of fever and a son four years old, and the council needed a keeper, and Aldric said—he said, *let my dying be useful*. Those were his words. Let my dying be useful." Her hands tightened around each other. "They took him up the mountain in the autumn. Quentin was four years old. The council smuggled the boy south with a foster family, with papers that gave him a new name and no history, and with a regimen already begun—the doses, the graduated poisons, the making of his immunity. Not training. Design. You understand that by now; he does too, though he hasn't said it aloud. They built the boy into a key as carefully as they built this vial. Two keys. One to carry, one to turn."

"And the Door," Wren said. Her mouth was dry. "It opens only for him."

"It opens only for him. And once it opens—" Maren stopped. Started again. "Once it opens, the vigil ends. Whatever art has sustained Aldric these forty-four years ends with it. Either he walks out of that chamber an old man with minutes left in his body, or he is already long dead and preserved in his keeping, and the opening releases him to finally lie down." She met Wren's eyes. "Alive or dead, none of us know. No message has ever come back through. The Door does not speak. It only waits. That was the price of the design—total silence, total security. My grandson is going to climb to that Door, drink his father's ashes, open his father's tomb, and meet whatever forty-four years have left of him. And no one—no one—can tell him which it will be."

* * *

Wren walked out into the snow because the walls had become unbearable.

The village lay blue-white under the falling dark, chimney smoke rising straight in the windless cold, lamplight blooming gold in windows. Down by the bakehouse she could see Quentin still, silhouetted against the glow, surrounded by children, showing them something with his hands—the fold of dough, probably, the turn of the wrist. Even from here she could read his body language: the slight hunch of a man making himself smaller, the exaggerated patience of someone who could not quite believe he was allowed to be happy.

She thought about telling him. Tonight, immediately, before another hour passed. It was what a judge owed: prompt truth, cleanly delivered.

Then she thought about what Maren had said after—*you're going to be the one who decides whether he survives learning it*—and understood that the old woman had handed her something heavier than a secret. She'd handed her a sentencing decision. Tell him tonight, on the eve of the climb, and every step up that mountain would be a step toward his father's grave or his father's ghost, fear riding his shoulders the whole way. Tell him at the Door, and he would face the mechanism unprepared, betrayed by everyone who knew. There was no good time. There was only the least cruel time, and cruelty was measured differently depending on whether you believed a man needed time to grieve in advance or strength intact for what came after.

Wren had sent men to the gallows with more certainty than this.

"You look like my grandmother when the tax assessors came," said a voice, and Tam slid out of the shadows of the woodshed, hands jammed in his pockets, fifteen years old and trying very hard to look like he belonged anywhere at all. "She used to stand outside counting the windows before they knocked. Like if she counted fast enough, maybe there'd be fewer things to take."

"Shouldn't you be inside?" Wren said. "Given your history with doors."

"I'm always given reasons to be inside lately." He shuffled his feet in the snow. "I'm sorry. About the letters. I need to say it right, not the way I said it before, when I was crying. I'm sorry, and I'm not asking you to forgive me, I just need it said properly because—" his voice wobbled and he hated it, she could see him hating it, "—because he fed me. After. You fed me, both of you, and I keep thinking about what happens when Corvane gets here, and what he did to that prisoner, and I—"

"Tam." She said it quietly, and he stopped. Snow gathered on his shoulders. He was shivering and pretending not to. "When the time comes, you'll know what to do. That's not forgiveness. That's just information about you that I happen to believe."

He stared at her. Then nodded once, jerkily, and fled inside—which was, Wren reflected, exactly what she wanted to do.

Instead she stood in the falling snow and watched Quentin through the bakehouse window, and let herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the ford, since the frozen waterfall, since the night her hand closed around his and did not let go.

If the Door opened onto a living man, Quentin gained a father and lost the story he'd been telling himself for forty years—that he'd been abandoned, that he'd never been worth staying for. If it opened onto a dead man, Quentin gained a grave and lost even the comfort of anger. Either way, the boy in the flour-dusted sleeves was walking into a room designed to break him, and Wren Calloway, who had spent her whole life believing that justice meant putting the truth in front of people no matter how much it cost, was seriously considering lying to him by omission for the first time in her career.

Her grandmother had a saying: *inherited grief is still grief*. Wren had always thought it meant that sorrow passed down a family was sorrow all the same, deserving of the same respect. Standing in the snow, watching the son of a man entombed in a doorway, she understood it differently. Grief was inheritance in the literal sense—an estate conveyed from the dead to the living, along with the debts attached to it. Aldric Ashgrove had signed away his life and called it usefulness, and the bill had traveled south for forty-four years and arrived at his son's table disguised as a courier's dying gift.

Some inheritance. Some country.

Behind her, Maren's door opened, spilling firelight. The old woman came out wrapped in a shawl, unhurried, and stood beside her in the cold as if standing in snowstorms with disgraced justices were a normal way for ninety-one-year-olds to spend an evening.

"You haven't told him," Maren observed.

"Not yet."

"Will you?"

"When it costs least," Wren said. "I don't know when that is. Ask me again tomorrow."

Maren made a sound that might have been approval. For a while they watched the snow together, two women guarding the same silence from opposite sides.

"The vial," Wren said eventually. "There's only one."

"There is only one. There was only ever meant to be one. The alchemists did not design redundancies into their masterpieces—it offended their aesthetics." Maren's breath fogged and hung between them. "Which means you should think carefully, Justice Calloway, about what happens on that mountain if the vial breaks. Or is stolen. Or if my grandson falters at the last and cannot bring himself to drink his father's bones." She turned, and her milk-pale eyes were ancient and pitiless and kind. "You are sworn to the road, not the crown. You have killed for your code and nearly died for it, and you follow a man toward a door that may be his father's coffin. So I ask you, as one keeper to another: when the price comes due—and it will come due, prices always do—what exactly are you prepared to pay?"

Wren didn't answer. Above the valley, invisible beneath the storm, the massif called Ashfell waited with its buried door and its buried dead, patient as only mountains and secrets know how to be.

Down in the bakehouse, the lamplight flickered, and through the frosted glass Quentin raised his arms to demonstrate something to the children—how high the loaf would rise, perhaps. How large a small thing could grow.

Wren watched him, and for the first time since she'd taken the map at knifepoint, she was afraid—not of Corvane, not of the cold, but of the answer forming somewhere inside her, already true, waiting only to be spoken:

*Everything. I already know it's everything.*

Far off up-valley, where the avalanche channels cut their white scars into the mountain, a sound rolled down through the falling snow—deep, rhythmic, wrong. Not thunder. Not rockfall.

Hooves. Many of them, moving fast, coming out of the north.

Maren went rigid beside her. "That's not possible," the old woman whispered. "The passes are closed. Nothing crosses the high fells in this snow. Nothing—"

But Wren was already running for the bakehouse, already reaching for Quentin through the crowd of startled children, and the hoofbeats were growing louder in the dark.
