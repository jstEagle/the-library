## 17. Night Crossing

They left the village in the hour before dawn, when the cold is honest and the dark has not yet begun its long argument with the light.

Maren walked them to the edge of the terraces, where the last of Vaelmoor's hidden fields gave way to scree and the black shoulder of the mountain. She carried no lantern. None of them did. Light was a thing they had agreed to surrender, along with noise, along with hope of an easy road.

"The chute is there." Maren's voice was low, pitched under the wind rather than against it. She pointed with her chin toward a seam of paler darkness on the eastern face, where the mountain wept all summer and now wept ice instead. "The waterfall freezes from the bottom up, but it never freezes clean. There are hollows behind the ice—air pockets, old summer channels. The smugglers used it before your grandfather was born, taster. It will hold you."

"*Will*," Wren said quietly, "is not *did*."

Maren turned her hooded head toward Wren, and though I could see almost nothing of her face, I felt the look she gave her—the look of a woman who had buried a country and did not intend to bury two more people for the sake of a coward's arithmetic. "It will hold you if you climb like men who mean to live. Nothing holds those who climb like they've already died."

I said nothing. I had spent my whole life climbing like I'd already died, in the small ways—in kitchens, at tables, swallowing what was put before me with a steady hand and an empty face. But this was different. This was stone and glass and a drop measured not in inches but in stories, and my hands already ached at the thought of it.

"Corvane's pickets?" I asked.

"Gone to their fires by now. Two on the western rim where the valley narrows—they watch the road, because the road is what a soldier watches." Maren's breath fogged and vanished. "No one watches the waterfall. It looks like death, so they let it be death. That is the oldest defense Vaelmoor ever had, boy: looking like something not worth taking."

She pressed something into my hands then—a twist of oilcloth, heavy for its size. Inside were three strips of dried meat and a lump of dark bread, and beneath the food, wrapped separately, a coil of braided hair-rope, fine as anything out of a capital rope-walk.

"From the lofts," she said. "Old work. Better than anything your king's army buys." Her fingers found mine in the dark and squeezed once, hard. "You carry our name now, whether you want it or not. Climb well."

Then she was gone, back down into the valley that would not be found, and Wren touched my elbow, and we began.

* * *

The first hour was scree, and scree is honest too—it shifts, it slides, it tells you plainly that it does not care about your plans. We went up through it in the gray half-dark with the rope between us, Wren leading, testing each stone with her boot before committing her weight, moving the way water moves, finding the line of least resistance as if she'd been born reading mountains instead of ledgers.

She had been quiet since the valley. Quiet since Tam, since the trial she'd refused, since the villagers' eyes had followed us out of the gates with something worse than anger in them—disappointment, which is anger's patient cousin. I had watched her pack in silence, check her blades in silence, and when she'd finally spoken, standing at the gate while the snow came down soft and indifferent, all she'd said was:

"They wanted me to kill a boy. So much for justice."

And I had wanted to tell her what I knew about refusing—about the cost of it, about how the refusal doesn't make you free, it just makes you someone who has to live with having been right alone. But I hadn't known how to say it without it sounding like pity, and Wren Calloway would sooner take a knife than pity. So I'd only said, "Come on, then. Let's go be right somewhere else," and after a moment she'd almost smiled.

Now she climbed, and I climbed, and the valley fell away below us, and somewhere behind and below, the fires of Corvane's company burned in the valley mouth like a wound that wouldn't close. I counted them once—one, two, four, more, too many—and stopped counting. Numbers like that don't help. They just sit in your stomach like bad meat.

At the base of the cliff the chute began, and I understood why no one guarded it.

It was a waterfall frozen mid-fall—a hundred feet of ice poured down the rock face in ropes and curtains and hanging blades, white where starlight caught it, blue-black in the shadows, groaning faintly to itself in a voice like a ship at anchor. The sound went through me somewhere older than thought. Ice makes that sound when it is alive, when it is working, when it remembers being water.

"That," Wren whispered beside me, "is the most beautiful thing I have ever wanted very badly to avoid."

"Maren says there are hollows. Channels behind it. Air pockets from the summer flow."

"Maren also says the mountain will hold us." Wren uncoiled the hair-rope, tested it with a sharp jerk, grunted approval, and tied one end around her own waist with quick, economical knots. "Give me your end. When I pull twice, come up. Once, freeze. Three times—" She paused. "Three times means run. Down or sideways, anywhere, just not toward me, because by then whatever's happening won't care about you."

"And if I hear nothing at all?"

Her teeth showed briefly in the dark. "Then climb faster."

She went first, and I learned in those first minutes that everything I thought I knew about watching Wren Calloway had been wrong. On the road, at knifepoint, at the ford, she had been iron—hard, certain, finished. Here on the ice she was something else entirely. She moved like the mountain had written her. Every placement of her boot was a decision made and unmade a dozen times a second; every swing of her knife, striking a step into the ice, landed exactly where she meant it to land, and she never struck the same place twice if she could help it, because—she told me later—ice keeps score.

I climbed behind her, hands screaming, following her steps because making my own was beyond me. The cold got into the cracks of everything. My palms split early—I felt the skin go, a hot bright sting swallowed immediately by numbness—and after that I stopped feeling my fingers at all, which was either mercy or a warning.

Fifty feet up, the character of the wall changed. The falls here hadn't fallen so much as crawled, spreading into sheets, and behind the sheets—if Maren was right—ran the old summer channels. Wren found the first one by accident. Her knife-strike cracked a pane of ice and the crack breathed: a soft exhalation of warmer air, smelling of wet stone, coming from a gap barely wide enough for a hand.

"Hollow," she breathed. "Gods above, the old woman was right."

We went into the wall.

Inside the ice was another world—or the ghost of one. The summer channel ran upward at a steep angle, a throat of smooth stone perhaps three feet across, roofed over with ice thick enough that the starlight came through it greenish and strange, so that we climbed through what felt like the belly of a wave caught forever mid-break. Water still trickled somewhere below us, deep in the mountain's plumbing, and the sound of it was the only warm thing for miles.

It should have been easier. In some ways it was—footing sure, walls to press against, no wind. But it was close, and dark, and every twenty feet the channel narrowed and I had to turn sideways and exhale and push through a pinch of stone that seemed designed by someone with a grudge, and always overhead the ice groaned its slow ship-sound, and I thought: *this mountain is talking in its sleep, and we are crawling through its throat.*

Twice Wren stopped and held up a fist and we waited, motionless, while something shifted far off in the dark—wind in the upper passes, probably, or the mountain resettling its shoulders. Both times the sound passed and we went on.

An hour inside, maybe more—we couldn't know, time goes strange in the dark—the channel opened into a wider chamber, tall enough to stand in, and Wren called the first halt since the base. We ate a strip of meat each, chewing slowly because chewing fast invites cramps, and drank snowmelt from a seep in the wall, cupping it in hands too numb to feel how cold it was.

"How are yours?" she asked. Meaning the hands.

"Still attached."

"That's the standard I'm holding you to tonight. Attached." She flexed her own fingers in front of her face, checking. In the green ice-light I saw that hers were cut too, blood frozen in brown lines across both palms, and that she had not mentioned it and would not. "After this ridge the channel turns east. Maren's map said the upper chute breaks open near the top—we come out on the shoulder above the tree line, north-facing slope, and from there it's a traverse to the col and the high camp."

"You've read her map?"

"I've read everything you've read. You talk in your sleep, taster." A beat. "You don't, actually. But you mutter over maps like a priest over scripture, and I stand behind you. Old habit. Reading over shoulders is how you catch liars."

"And did you? Catch one?"

"No." She said it simply, and somehow it landed harder than any reassurance. "That's the trouble with you, Ashgrove. You're the worst liar I've ever had to trust."

We climbed again.

* * *

It happened in the third hour, in the place they would later call the Narrows, though neither of us named it anything at the time. Names came afterward. At the time there was only the event, arriving the way disasters do—without announcement, without mercy, in the space of a single held breath.

The channel pinched. Harder than any pinch before it—a slot where the summer water must have plunged as a true fall, carving a vertical throat no wider than my shoulders. Above the slot the ice had grown fat over centuries, bulging inward from both sides until the gap was barely a body's width, and the ice there was old, and thick, and heavy, tons of it, hanging above our heads like a held verdict.

Wren went through first. I watched her turn sideways, wedge herself, work upward with her back against one wall and her boots against the other, chimneying through the narrowest part, and she made it—her boots clearing the lip, her weight coming onto the shelf above—and then it was my turn, and I was halfway through the slot when the mountain cleared its throat.

The sound was not loud. That's what I remember most. Not a roar, not a crack like a cannon—just a deep, wet *groan*, rising from everywhere at once, from the ice above and around and beneath, the whole frozen fall waking at once, and then a shudder ran through the wall like a shiver through a horse, and somewhere above us a mass of ice the size of a haywain sighed, tilted, and began to come apart.

"Wren—"

"I feel it! Move—UP, Quentin, UP!"

I went up. I don't remember deciding to. My body simply committed itself the way it had committed itself a thousand times at a thousand tables—swallow, keep your face empty, survive—and I hauled myself through the slot with my elbows and my fear while the world above me filled with a sound like the sky tearing linen. Chunks of ice the size of my torso broke loose from the chamber roof and came down the channel behind me, bouncing, shattering, filling the throat I'd just climbed with thunder and shards.

A piece the size of a loaf took me across the shoulder blade and spun me half around. Another hit the wall an inch from my skull and burst into stinging gravel. I scrambled, blind, hands finding holds by luck and prayer, and the channel below me was gone—filled, choked, erased by the collapse—and above me the ice was still moving, still letting go, the whole upper wall peeling in slow terrible sheets—

And then her hand.

It came out of the chaos above me, out of the white roaring dark, and closed around my wrist—not my fingers, not a courteous grip, but her whole fist locked around the bone of my arm with every ounce of what she was—and she threw herself backward onto the shelf and *pulled*, and the mountain tried to keep me and lost.

I came over the lip of the shelf and onto solid ice, gasping, and she dragged me farther, both hands on me now, hauling me away from the edge while the collapse thundered itself out below and behind, dusted us with powder, and slowly, slowly, went quiet.

Silence after. The particular silence of high places after violence—the mountain holding its breath to see what it has done.

We lay side by side on the shelf, chests heaving, frost in our lashes, and I became aware, gradually, of two things. First, that I was alive. Second, that her hand had never left my wrist.

Not my wrist anymore, actually. Somewhere in the dragging it had migrated, and now her fingers were laced through mine, gripping hard, and she seemed to notice it at the same moment I did—and I felt her start to let go, the reflex of a woman who touches nothing she hasn't decided to touch.

She didn't let go.

She looked at our hands for a long moment, there in the green dark, and then she looked at me, and her face was stripped of everything she usually wore—all the iron, all the code, all the careful distance—and what was underneath was young, and fierce, and frightened, and utterly unwilling to pretend otherwise.

"Your grip slipped," she said. "On the slot. Your left hand."

"Yes."

"If it slips again, I need to be already holding it." She said it like a tactical assessment, like a rule of the road, and her eyes dared me to make it anything else. "So I'm going to keep holding it. For the rest of the climb. That's all this is."

"All right," I said.

"It's practical."

"It's practical," I agreed.

But neither of us laughed, and neither of us looked away, and after a moment she turned and began climbing again, and my hand stayed in hers—rope slack, knife sheathed, the two of us moving up the dark throat of the mountain like one creature with four limbs and one intention—and I thought, with the strange clarity that comes after near-death: *so this is what it feels like, trusting someone with your actual weight.*

I had spent thirty years being trusted. Kings trusted me with their food. Corvane trusted me with his suspicion. An entire dead kingdom, apparently, had trusted me with its door. Everyone trusted the taster, because the taster's whole purpose was to absorb harm meant for others.

Nobody had ever held on.

* * *

We came out of the chute an hour before dawn, through a final gap in the ice, onto the north shoulder of the fell—and the world opened.

Below us, the hidden valley lay drowned in pre-dawn blue, smoke rising thin and straight from the houses of people who had chosen to stay in a country that didn't exist. And beyond the valley's mouth, ringing the low ground, the fires of Corvane's siege—dozens of them now, strung along the narrows like beads on a wire, with the dark shapes of tents and picket lines visible even at this distance. Too many to fight. Too many to slip. The valley was found, and everyone in it knew it, and the smoke rose straight because the air was perfectly, terribly still.

Wren stood beside me with my hand still in hers, and we looked down at it together.

"He'll tighten it within days," she said quietly. "Once he decides the village matters more than patience. And he will decide that, Quentin. Men like Corvane always do. Patience is just cruelty waiting for permission."

"I know."

"And we can't go back down. You understand that too." She wasn't asking. "There's no way through that ring, and even if there were, going back would just add two more names to whatever he does next. The only road left is up." She nodded at the massif looming above us, white and vast and indifferent, the Door of Ashes somewhere in its high places like a word at the top of a page. "Over the top of the world, taster. In winter. With what we're carrying."

I looked at our joined hands, and then at the mountain, and I found—to my considerable surprise—that I wasn't afraid. Or rather, I was afraid the way you're afraid in a fever dream, distantly, knowing it can't touch the thing underneath. Because I had swallowed poison for a king who never learned my name, and I had fled my whole life like a man with no destination, and I had crawled through the throat of a dying glacier, and the sum of all of it was this: a woman who had every reason to let go of me, holding on.

"Then we go up," I said.

She squeezed my hand once—briefly, hard, the same way Maren had squeezed mine at the valley's edge, and I wondered if she knew she'd learned it—and turned toward the heights.

We had taken maybe fifty steps across the shoulder when she stopped so suddenly I walked into her back.

"What—"

She raised her free hand for silence, head cocked, staring back down the slope we'd climbed. Following her eyes, I saw it: far below, at the mouth of the shattered chute, a pinpoint of orange light. Then another. Lanterns. Moving along the base of the cliff, deliberate and unhurried, sweeping the ice wall the way a man sweeps a room he knows something is hiding in.

Corvane's pickets had noticed the avalanche.

Or—worse, and I knew it in my bones even before Wren said it, her voice flat and cold as the slope beneath us:

"They're not searching," she murmured. "They're marking. He knows exactly where we came out."
