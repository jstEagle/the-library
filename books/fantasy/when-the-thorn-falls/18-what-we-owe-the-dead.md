## 18. What We Owe the Dead

The storm had been circling them for two days like a wolf deciding whether to commit.

Wren Calloway could read weather the way she read men—by what it held back. The clouds above Ashfell's shoulder weren't threatening yet. They were *considering*. And so they made camp early in a fold of granite that the wind couldn't quite reach, and Quentin built a fire small enough to hide from God, and for a while neither of them spoke.

She watched him work. That was becoming a habit she didn't examine too closely—the way he moved through a camp as though it were a kitchen, everything ordered, nothing wasted. He'd cut strips of dried mutton into equal pieces with a knife that had once, she suspected, only ever touched bread. He'd found some kind of wild herb under the snow crust and crumbled it over the pot with an authority that bordered on arrogance. The man tasted poisons for a living and he still cooked like flavor mattered.

"Eat," he said, handing her a tin cup of stew. "It's not poisoned. Probably."

"That's your idea of reassurance?"

"It's my idea of honesty." He settled across the fire, knees drawn up, steam rising off his cup into his face. "Everything's poison. It's just a question of dose."

Wren ate. The stew was good, which annoyed her, because she'd decided somewhere on the ice chute to stop being grateful to him and hadn't managed it yet. Since the waterfall—since her hand around his wrist and the groaning ice and the moment afterward when neither of them let go until the solid ground forced the issue—something had shifted between them. Not softened, exactly. Settled. Like silt after a flood.

The fire popped. Above the fold of rock, the sky went the color of old pewter.

"Tell me about him," Quentin said.

She didn't pretend not to know who. She'd told him the shape of it already, back in the low country: Lord Aldric Vane, condemned by her hand before the crown could pardon him. But she'd told it like a report. Dates, charges, sentence. He wanted the rest, and she'd known since the river ford—since the royal badge on the dead hunter's chest and the line she'd crossed there—that someday she would have to say it out loud properly or it would keep eating her from the inside.

"You'll think less of me," she said.

"I've thought every possible thing about you since you put a knife at my throat on the smugglers' road. Try me."

Wren turned her cup slowly. The firelight caught the scar along her knuckles—the first one, earned at fifteen breaking up a fight between two drovers who both deserved worse than they got.

"Vane was a grain magnate," she said. "Owned half the lowland mills. During the famine winter—eight years ago—he bought up relief granaries and resold the grain at siege prices to his own countrymen. Perfectly legal. Every signature in order. The crown courts looked at it for three years and concluded no crime had occurred because the law defines murder narrowly." Her voice stayed level. She'd practiced that. "Forty-one people starved within sight of his storehouses. I know the number because I counted the graves myself. I was still a crown justice then. Still wearing the gray."

"What happened?"

"The case collapsed. A witness recanted—bought, everyone knew it, provable by no one. Vane walked out of the courthouse smiling and his lawyers filed for costs against the families." She took a breath. "So I stopped being a crown justice in the middle of a road between here and the capital, followed him to his hunting lodge, and held a tribunal. Lantern-light, four witnesses, sworn testimony. My code. My verdict. His life."

Quentin said nothing. He was good at that—not filling silences the way most people did, like they were afraid of what lived in them.

"He begged," Wren went on, because the worst part should be said plainly; anything less was vanity. "Not for himself at first. For his daughter. He kept saying, *she doesn't know what I did, don't make her know.*" The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through stone like breath through teeth. "I hanged him anyway. And his daughter learned what her father was, because that's how it works. You can't cut out the rot without showing the whole wound. I told myself that for eight years. Some nights I even believe it."

"Do you?" Quentin asked quietly. "Believe it?"

She looked at him across the flames. His face was half-lit, half-shadowed, and he wasn't judging her—she'd have seen it, she always saw it, she'd spent ten years reading guilt on faces in courtrooms. What she saw instead was something more unsettling. Recognition.

"No," she admitted. "Some nights I believe I liked it. That's the honest answer. The law failed and I got to be the law, and there is a part of a person that—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "My grandmother used to sing me songs about a door. Forbidden ones, though I didn't know why then. One verse goes: *the just blade rusts quickest in its own hand.* She never explained it. I think about it every day now."

"Your grandmother was Vaelmoori?"

"Half. On my mother's side. Which is probably why I'm sitting on a frozen mountain chasing a door out of a children's lullaby instead of drinking myself to death in some border town." She almost smiled. Almost. "There. Now you know the worst of me. Lord Corvane thinks I'm a dangerous fanatic. The crown struck my name from the rolls. Both are right."

Quentin set down his cup. When he spoke, his voice had changed—stripped of the kitchen-wryness he wore like armor.

"You counted forty-one graves," he said. "I've never counted mine. I don't know the number."

Wren waited.

"When I was six years old, they took me into the palace orphan wing and started feeding me things." He stared into the fire. "Small doses. Nothing that would show. Bitter almond in the morning broth—I can taste arsenic at concentrations that would kill a horse twice over. Hemlock derivatives. Metals. Things without names that the alchemists wrote down in ledgers and adjusted week to week, watching how my blood answered. I was told it was training. Tasters need tolerance; that's the trade; every taster before me earned it the hard way, dose by dose, year by year." A muscle worked in his jaw. "Except we found the ledgers in the map's margins, and Maren confirmed it, and I've had two weeks on this mountain to turn it over. It wasn't training, Wren. It was design. Someone bred my family for this. Someone chose me as a child and built a lockpick out of me, and the lock is a door in a mountain, and I'm walking straight toward it like a good little key."

"That's not what you meant about counting."

"No." He rubbed his face with both hands. "What I meant is this. Everyone assumes a food-taster is a victim. Press-ganged, poisoned against his will, poor wretch. And the first years, maybe that was true. But somewhere around my twentieth birthday I understood exactly what I was doing—and I kept doing it. Voluntarily. Because I'd done the arithmetic." He looked up, and his eyes were very clear and very tired. "Every meal I taste is a meal where if there's poison, it finds *me* first. Me, with a body they built to survive it. Not some chambermaid carrying a tray. Not some envoy whose death starts a war. There are perhaps thirty people alive today who aren't dead because I swallowed what was meant for them. I used to tell myself that made it worth it. That it was sacrifice."

"And now?"

"Now I'm on a mountain learning that my sacrifice was purchased in advance. That my nobility was engineered. That the choice I was so proud of making might have been made for me before I could walk." He laughed—a short, bitter sound with no humor in it. "You want to compare sins? You broke the law because the law was wrong. I served it faithfully because it gave me a reason to exist. I don't know which of us should feel worse. I genuinely don't."

* * *

The wind rose through the night, but the granite fold held, and the fire burned down to a red heart they fed piece by piece.

Wren lay on her bedroll staring at the stars through the gap in the clouds, listening to Quentin breathing a few feet away, and turned their two confessions over like stones found on a beach.

They were not the same, what he'd done and what she'd done. She needed that clarity; it was the last thing she owned. She had taken a life the law refused to take, and whatever the justification, the taking was hers alone—her hand, her rope, her grave-count, her sleepless nights. He had given his body, meal by meal, decade by decade, to a machine that consumed him and called it duty. But the longer she sat with it, the more she saw they were two answers to the same question—the only question, really, that mattered on a road like theirs:

*When the world is unjust, what do you owe it?*

She had answered: resistance, even bloody, even ruinous. He had answered: service, even corrosive, even self-erasing. And both answers had hollowed them out into these two particular people lying on a mountain, exiled, hunted, bound for a door that shouldn't exist.

"Quentin." His name came out quieter than she intended. "Are you awake?"

A pause. "Define awake."

"If we find this door. If it opens. What do you actually want to be on the other side?"

The silence stretched long enough that she thought he'd gone to sleep. Then, low, in the dark: "Someone who chose something. Once. With clean hands and full knowledge. Even if it's just—" she heard him gesture vaguely at the darkness, "—what to cook for supper. Even if it's small. Everything I've ever chosen was chosen for me first. I'd like to know what I'm like when that isn't true."

Wren looked at the stars. "That's not small," she said.

"What about you? If we get out of this. After."

"After," she repeated, like a word in a foreign language. "I don't have an after. I had a code, and I've broken every clause of it since I met you—harbored a fugitive, killed a crowned man, abandoned a tribunal mid-route. Whatever I rebuild after this has to be built from scratch. Maybe that's the point. Maybe codes that can't bend deserve to break." She rolled onto her side, facing his silhouette against the faint glow of coals. "My grandmother's songs say the door keeps what must be kept and releases what must be released. I used to think that was nonsense. Comfort-nonsense for people who couldn't afford justice."

"And now?"

"Now I think whoever wrote those verses had stood exactly where I'm standing." She pulled her blanket higher. "Sleep, taster. Tomorrow we climb."

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which was worse than wind. Wind meant the air was moving; stillness at this altitude meant the sky was holding its breath.

Wren woke first and got the fire going, and by the time Quentin stirred she had the maps spread on a flat stone, weighted with pebbles at the corners. The smuggler's routes ended here—everything beyond was blank vellum except for the inked symbol at Ashfell's heart, the same mark her grandmother had sung about, the same mark now seared into her memory alongside a dead man's badge and forty-one graves.

Quentine crouched beside her, rubbing sleep from his eyes, and traced the route upward with one finger. "Through the col, then the scree field, then—" His finger stopped.

Wren saw it at the same moment. The eastern horizon, behind the shoulder of the massif, had gone the color of a bruise—and it was moving. Not clouds. Clouds didn't move with that uniformity, that *purpose*, a white wall swallowing the peaks one ridge at a time and leaving nothing behind it but white.

"Storm?" Quentin asked, but his voice already knew.

"Avalanche front, maybe. Or a whiteout riding a gale." Wren was already rolling her bedroll, hands moving fast. "Either way, it will close the passes for days. Possibly a week. We're two hours from shelter if there's shelter to be had—the map shows ruins up-slope, a shepherd station, maybe."

"And if we're caught in the open?"

She met his eyes. Neither of them said it. They didn't need to; the mountain would say it for them.

They struck camp in under ten minutes, and climbed into the silence before the storm.
