## 20. Tam's Redemption

The storm broke on the third morning the way a fever breaks—suddenly, and leaving everything behind it changed.

Wren knew it first by the silence. For three days the wind had screamed against the bothy's dry-stone walls like something trying to get in, filling every pause between words, making conversation an act of shouting. Then, in the grey hour before dawn, it simply stopped, and the silence that followed was so complete she could hear her own blood moving in her ears.

She lay in the dark under two blankets and Quentin's coat—he'd insisted—and listened to the bothy settle. The shepherd's hut was older than any kingdom she could name, its walls mortared with something black and glassy that never quite warmed to the touch. Vaelmoori work, Quentin said. Everything out here was Vaelmoori work. A country erased from every treaty, and still its stones held up strangers' roofs three hundred years later.

There was a lesson in that. Wren wasn't sure she liked it.

Beside the dead hearth, Quentin slept sitting up, his back against the wall, chin on his chest, one hand curled loosely around the ash-vial at his throat. He'd taken to wearing it like a relic. She supposed it was one. His father's country, sealed inside a stopper of wax.

She rose quietly, fed the last of their wood to the stove, and went out into the white.

The world had been remade overnight. The pass they'd climbed through two days ago—a nightmare of wind-scoured ice and exposed rock where Wren had roped herself and Quentin together and told him not to think about the drop—was now a single smooth sweep of unmarked snow, blue in the early light, running down toward the tree line like a road poured from milk. No tracks. No birds. The kind of clean that made her feel like an intruder just by standing in it.

They were down to hardtack, a fistful of salt pork gone waxy with cold, snowmelt, and the dregs of the tea Quentin hoarded like a dragon. Three days of rationing had stripped something out of both of them. Wren's hands were steady—her hands were always steady—but there was a hollow behind her sternum where her certainty used to sit, and it had been growing since the river ford, since the man with the royal badge had gone down under her knife and stayed down.

She had killed before. That was the truth of road-justice; you didn't hold law over smugglers' roads without spilling what needed spilling. But she had always killed within the shape of her code. This man had been fleeing, half-armed, more afraid than dangerous, and she had put her blade through him because stopping meant answering questions about who she was and what she carried, and the map mattered more than he did.

*The map mattered more than he did.* She turned the sentence over the way Quentin turned a spoonful of stew, testing for poison.

It tasted of arsenic.

She heard the sound as she was turning back to the bothy—a sound so small and wrong that her body reacted before her mind did, hand going to the knife at her hip, weight dropping low. A scrape. A stumble. Something alive in a world where nothing should be.

It came out of the rocks below the bothy on hands and knees, dragging one leg, and it took her far too long to recognize it because her mind refused the answer her eyes were offering.

"Tam."

The boy collapsed face-down in the snow ten paces away.

* * *

He was fourteen years old, and when Wren knelt and turned him over, she saw that frost had gotten into his cheeks and left them mottled grey-white, and his lips were split and crusted, and his eyes were sunken into his skull like stones settling in mud. His breath came in shallow, whistling pulls. One glove was missing; the bare hand was the worst thing, the fingers swollen and discolored, the nails gone dark.

"Quentin!" she shouted over her shoulder, and then, because shouting was useless against stone walls, she got her arms under the boy and hauled.

He weighed almost nothing. When had he weighed almost nothing? In the village he'd been all elbows and appetite, stealing honey-cakes off Maren's sill and grinning about it, quick-eyed and quicker-tongued, asking Quentin endless questions about the capital while pretending not to care about the answers. He'd been the one who brought them food the night Corvane's riders reached the valley mouth. He'd been *theirs*, or near enough.

And then he'd sold them. Fed the spymaster their route, their numbers, their faces, in exchange for passage south. Wren had caught him at it with the evidence in her hand—the scrap of charcoal notes, the boy's face going grey as the confession spilled out of him—and the villagers had gathered with torches and old hungers, and they had asked her for justice, *road-justice, Calloway-justice*, because she was the one whose name meant the code would be kept.

She had looked at a child in the lantern-light and refused to kill him, and the village had looked at her like something broken.

Now she carried that same child up the slope to the bothy, and her arms knew exactly how much he weighed, and she hated how well she remembered it.

Quentin had the stove roaring by the time she laid Tam on the sheepskins near it. He moved around the small space with a taster's economy—no wasted motion, no wasted heat—and when he took the boy's frozen hand in both of his, Wren saw him do the thing he did without thinking: cataloguing. Reading damage the way he read flavor.

"Frostbite, second degree, maybe third on the two smallest fingers." His voice was flat and professional, which was how she'd learned to know when he was frightened. "We need warm water, not hot. Snow, melted slow." He was already packing snow into their one pot. "And he needs to drink before he eats, small sips, or his stomach'll reject everything."

"You've done this."

"I've been poisoned eleven ways and nursed back twice. You learn what the body forgives and what it doesn't." He glanced up at her, and something passed across his face—not quite a question. *Do we save him? Is that decided?*

"We save him," Wren said, because it was true, and because saying it aloud made it harder to unsay later.

Tam surfaced from unconsciousness twice in the next hour. Both times his eyes fixed on Wren's face and both times he flinched, hard, a full-body spasm, and tried to push himself backward across the floor. The second time he spoke.

"Don't—" His voice was a ruin. "Don't make me go back. I won't tell you anything, I don't know anything anymore, I swear it, but don't send me back to him."

"No one's sending you anywhere," Wren said.

"You don't understand." He was crying now, silently, the tears freezing at the corners of his eyes. "You don't know what he does. I *saw*—"

"Later," Quentin said gently, pressing a cup of meltwater into the boy's good hand. "Small sips. Later."

Tam drank. And then, because he was fourteen and half-dead and had clearly been holding something behind his teeth for days longer than his body could bear, he talked anyway, between sips, in fragments, staring into the fire.

"He had a man. From the valley. One of Maren's runners." Tam's breath hitched. "Corvane wanted to know another way into the valley besides the main track, some path the defenses didn't watch. The man wouldn't say. So Corvane—" The boy swallowed. His hand shook; water slopped over the rim; Quentin steadied it without seeming to notice he'd done it. "He had methods. He called them questions. It took most of a night. I was supposed to stand watch outside the tent. They make the local boys stand watch so the soldiers can't hear and can't leave."

"And you ran," Wren said.

"After. After they finished." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "After he was done screaming. I couldn't—I stood out there all night listening and I thought, this is what I sold them for. This is what passage south costs. And in the morning Corvane comes out wiping his hands like he'd eaten a meal, and he smiles at me, and he says, 'Well done, lad,' and gives me a strip of dried meat, and I took it." Tam's whole body shuddered. "I took it. I ate it. What does that make me?"

The fire popped. Outside, the silence of the high snow pressed against the walls.

"It makes you a boy who was hungry and scared," Quentin said quietly, "in the power of a man who has made cruelty into a profession. I've seen lords do worse over card games."

"That's not—" Tam started.

"He's right," Wren said.

Both of them looked at her. She hadn't planned to speak. The words had come out the way judgments come out of her sometimes—already formed, already weighted, needing only to be spoken to become real. She sat down on the bench across from the boy, deliberately, and let him see her whole face, because hiding your face was the first tool of a liar and she wanted no part of it in what she said next.

"In autumn I sentenced a man to hang for killing a shepherd over a debt of four silver marks," she said. "I did it by the code. The code says a life taken is answered with a life given, no matter the reason. And the code is right, because the day you start weighing lives against circumstances, every rich man in the lowlands finds a circumstance that weighs more than a poor man's neck." She paused. "But I have thought about that man every night since. Not because I doubt the sentence. Because the code worked *for* me that night, and I have watched it fail other people, and I know the difference is only which side of it you're standing on."

Tam stared at her.

"You betrayed us," Wren said. "That's plain fact, and I won't pretty it. If you'd come to my tribunal in the lowlands and confessed it, the code would ask a price. It still might." She leaned forward. "But I caught you once already, and I didn't take your life, and everyone told me mercy was weakness. And now you've walked through a killing storm, alone, half-frozen, with nothing to offer us and everything to fear from us, to warn the people you sold. That's not nothing, Tam. That's the price being paid. I've seen grown men too proud to pay it."

"I'm not brave," Tam whispered.

"No," Wren agreed. "Brave is overrated. I want reliable." She held out her open hand, palm up, the way she did when administering an oath. "So here's what happens now. You eat. You sleep. Your hand gets tended. And tomorrow, if you can stand, you tell us everything Corvane knows, everything he plans, everything you heard in that camp—every scrap, however small. Not because you owe it. Because it's the work in front of us, and you're going to do the work."

Tam looked at her hand for a long moment. Then he put his ruined one in it—the good fingers curling, the bad ones lying stiff as twigs—and gripped as hard as he could, which wasn't very.

"I swear it," he said. "By the road. By whatever you want."

"The road will do," Wren said, and felt something in her chest shift a half-inch, like a beam settling in an old house. Her code, bending. Not breaking. There was a difference, and she was staking everything on knowing it.

* * *

Quentin fed the boy the way he did everything—precisely, patiently, in stages. Broth first, thin as fog. Then bread softened in it. Then, hours later, a sliver of salt pork rendered soft in the pan. Wren watched him work and recognized the liturgy of it: a man who had spent his whole life feeding bodies back to themselves, doing the only thing he'd ever been allowed to be good at, and doing it as an act of tenderness he probably couldn't name even to himself.

While Tam slept, they stepped outside into the blue afternoon to talk where the boy couldn't hear.

"He said Corvane tortured one of Maren's runners," Quentin said, keeping his voice under the wind. "Which means Corvane has prisoners from the valley. Which means he breached the avalanche lines somehow, or found a way around them."

"Or someone showed him." Wren said it before she could stop herself, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Quentin's face went carefully blank in the way that meant she'd cut close to bone. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean—"

"You meant Tam," Quentin said. "Before he ran. It's fair. It occurred to me too." He looked out over the white sweep of the pass. "But it doesn't fit. Tam fled *after* the torture. Corvane learned the valley's approaches from the runner, yes, but the runner died—Tam said he was past speaking by morning. Whatever Corvane knows now, he paid for it in full and it's already stale. We've come higher and colder than anything he mapped."

"So we're ahead of him."

"For now." Quentin turned the ash-vial over in his fingers, a habit now, worry worn smooth as a rosary bead. "Wren. There's something else. Tam said Corvane gave him meat and called him 'lad.' Kept him close. Made him stand watch." He hesitated. "Spymasters don't keep local boys close unless the boys are useful. And a boy from the valley, known to us, trusted by Maren—that's useful in only one way."

Wren felt the cold go through her coat before the wind did. "Insurance."

"A hostage he hasn't claimed yet. Or bait." Quentin's breath smoked. "Corvane doesn't chase, remember? He herds. He let me walk out of that ravine because he wants my blood at the Door. What if the boy isn't a spy anymore? What if he's a message?" He met her eyes. "*Follow the boy and you follow me. I'll be waiting where you're going.*"

The wind picked up, keening down from the col above them, and somewhere in it Wren imagined she could hear the sound of boots on snow, though she knew it was nothing, she knew it was only weather.

"Then we move tonight," she said. "Full dark, before the moonrise. Cold travel is fast travel." She looked back through the doorway at the sleeping boy, at his bandaged hand, at the firelight on his face. "All three of us."

Quentin raised an eyebrow. "You trust him?"

"No," Wren said. "I *chose* him. Trust is for people who haven't given you reasons. Choice is for everyone else." She pulled her collar up. "That's not the code talking. That's better than the code. I'm still learning what it is."

They went in to wake Tam and break camp.

And neither of them saw, in the deepening dusk below the bothy, far down the pass where the snow lay unbroken and blue—neither of them saw the single line of prints that had appeared at the edge of sight, climbing steadily upward, patient as winter itself.
