## 21. The Spymaster's Bargain

The trap was set with salt.

Quentin noticed it the way he noticed everything now—too late to matter, early enough to be ashamed of himself. He had gone down from the shepherd's bothy at first light to melt snow at the stream below the tree line, Wren asleep above with her knife under her hand and Tam's frozen boots drying by the fire. The water came up clean and cold and tasted of nothing, which should have been his first warning. Nothing was a flavor he had spent thirty years learning never to trust.

He was halfway back up the slope when the man stepped out from behind a wind-bent pine, and Quentin understood that the stream had been the least of it. He had been walking toward this moment since the courier died at his table in the capital, maybe since the day an orphanage matron first put a bitter spoon between a child's lips and told him it was medicine.

"Lord Corvane," Quentin said.

The spymaster looked worse than the last time Quentin had seen him, and better, in the way that a blade looks after sharpening. The journey through Thornfell had stripped the court off him—the furred cloak gone, replaced by a hunter's grey wool crusted with frost; the manicured hands cracked at the knuckles. But the eyes were unchanged. They were patient eyes. Eyes that had watched men eat for years without ever once being hungry.

"You've lost weight, Ashgrove," Corvane said pleasantly. "Mountain air disagrees with you. Or perhaps it's the company."

Quentin set down the water skin slowly. His hands were steady. Thirty years of tasting had taught his body one lesson above all others: *do not spill*. "Wren is twenty feet above us."

"I know. She's also pretending not to have heard, because she wants to see what you do." A thin smile. "She's learning about you. I'm afraid she'll be disappointed. You're a very simple man, Quentin. That's not an insult. Simplicity is a virtue in your profession. It's why they chose you."

"Who chose me?"

Corvane tilted his head, and for a moment—and this was the thing Quentin would remember afterward, the thing that made the whole encounter obscene—the spymaster looked almost kind. Almost tired. A man at the end of a long hunt who would rather talk than kill.

"Walk with me," Corvane said. "Ten minutes. No knives, no riders—I sent them home weeks ago, or most of them; you buried two by the ford, I believe, so my compliments to the justice on her throwing arm. Ten minutes of truth, taster to poison-eater, and then you can go back up the mountain and pretend this conversation never seasoned your supper."

Every instinct Quentin owned said run. Every instinct he owned also knew running was pointless; Corvane had found him here, at the roof of the world, alone at a stream, which meant Corvane could find him anywhere. And beneath the fear, quiet as arsenic in wine, sat the other thing. The thing Corvane had come all this way to feed.

*Ten minutes of truth.*

Quentin had been starving for truth since the wax tube split open in his garret. He hated himself for it even as he picked up the skin and walked.

* * *

They followed the stream downstream, into a fold of rock where the wind dropped away and the pines stood thick enough to swallow sound. Corvane moved well for a courtier. Better than well. There was a sureness to his footing on the ice-glazed stones that no desk-bound spy should possess, and Quentin catalogued it the way he catalogued everything: *field-trained, mountain-born or long posted, comfortable with cold.* A ledger entry against a man who already knew every page of Quentin's own book.

"Do you know what your immunity cost the crown?" Corvane began, conversationally, as if discussing the price of pepper. "Not in coin—in patience. Twenty years of feeding you doses disguised as orphan rations. Tasters are expensive, Ashgrove. Trained tasters who survive their first decade are rarer than rubies. And then one day the palace's best asset simply walks out the gate carrying state property in his coat pocket, and I'm asked to explain it to a king who does not like explanations."

"It wasn't state property. It was a dying man's map."

"It was a Vaelmoori cipher keyed to a Vaelmoori bloodline," Corvane said, "and the dying man was a Vaelmoori courier who had spent forty years walking in circles across three kingdoms waiting for someone with your blood to sit down near him. You didn't receive that map at random, Quentin. You were *chosen*, the same way you were chosen for the kitchens. Everyone chooses you. Hasn't that ever struck you as strange? The alchemists chose you before you could walk. The courier chose you in his last breath. The justice on the road chose you the moment she read that door-mark and went pale. Even I—" he spread his cracked hands, mock-apologetic— "I am choosing you right now. You are the most chosen man in Thornfell, and you still think of yourself as invisible."

The words went in deep, which was, of course, their design. Quentin kept walking. Snow squeaked underfoot. Somewhere above, a crow argued with the wind.

"You said truth," he said finally. "So say the true thing. Why does the crown want the Door?"

And there it was—the pause. Quentin had watched ten thousand faces take their first taste of a lie, and Lord Corvane's pause was a masterwork, barely a heartbeat long, just enough room for honesty to fit inside if it wished.

"Because Vaelmoor did not die," Corvane said. "It was archived. Your hidden valley, your old people singing their forbidden lullabies, your elder Maren with her ash and her rituals—all of it preserved, all these years, because the treaty that erased Vaelmoor required its *arsenal* to remain intact. Sealed. Guarded." His voice dropped half a register. "Vials that make a phalanx unkillable. Draughts that turn a besieged city untouchable. The work of alchemists who solved death the way brewers solve thirst. The old king sealed the Door rather than let them loose. The new king intends to open it—not out of curiosity, Quentin. Out of necessity. The eastern powers have their own alchemists now. Crude ones. If we don't open the vault and take what's ours by conquest-right, someone else opens something worse within the decade."

"That's not truth," Quentin said quietly. "That's a speech. I've heard speeches over soup my whole life. Speeches are what powerful men use instead of seasoning—they make lies go down easier."

For the first time, something flickered across Corvane's face that was not control. It might have been respect.

"The truth, then, without the sauce," he said. "Yes, the crown wants weapons. Kings always want weapons; it is the single honest appetite they have. But *I*—" and here he stopped walking, turned, and looked at Quentin with those patient eyes, and Quentin saw, underneath everything, the thing that had driven the man over a thousand miles of winter mountain alone: hunger. Real hunger. The kind Quentin recognized because he had starved it in himself for decades. "I want the truth itself. I have spent my life inside other people's lies—the court's, the crown's, my own. Vaelmoor is the only lie I've ever found that was worth more than the truth it hid. A nation erased so thoroughly that its own survivors speak it only in whispers. Do you understand what that took? What it *cost*? I need to see it, Ashgrove. I need to stand in front of that door and know that erasure has a shape, a mechanism, a seam. Because if a kingdom can be unwritten, then so can a man. So can *I*."

The confession hung in the freezing air between them. Quentin realized his breath had gone shallow, and made himself breathe.

"You're asking me to feel sorry for you."

"I'm asking you to recognize yourself," Corvane said. "That's what I came here to offer. Not a rope, not a cell. A mirror."

* * *

They reached a shelf of granite overlooking the valley's northern throat—the route they would take toward Ashfell, toward the Door, toward whatever waited warm behind the stone. Below, the world was white and silent and utterly indifferent to kings and keys.

"Here is my offer," Corvane said. "Hear all of it before you spit."

"I haven't spat in thirty years. Bad habit for a taster."

That earned him the smile again, thinner now. Genuine, almost. "First: full pardon. Not the grudging kind—a written restoration, signed and sealed, your name cleared of the courier's death. Second: rank. Master of the Royal Kitchens, yes, but more than that. My office needs a man who can taste truth the way you do, Quentin. Poisons, yes—but also poisons of another kind. I want you inside my service. Third—" and here his voice changed, softened, became something almost gentle, and Quentin felt the hook slide past his guard and into flesh he hadn't known was exposed— "third: purpose. You have spent your entire life swallowing other people's intentions and calling it survival. I am offering you the chance to *matter*. To stand at the opening of a door that has been shut for a century and decide, as the last taster of the old blood, what comes through it. Restoration, rank, purpose. Everything you never dared want, because wanting was the one appetite your profession forbade you."

Quentin looked out at the white silence and found, to his horror, that his eyes were stinging.

Because it *was* tailored. Stitch by stitch, seam by seam, cut to his exact measure the way a master tailor cuts to a lord. Pardon—yes, he wanted his name clean. Rank—God forgive him, some small vain chamber in his heart had always imagined walking back into those kitchens as more than furniture. Purpose—he had lain awake in the bothy three nights ago listening to Wren breathe and thinking: *she knows what she serves. Her code, her roads, her law. I have never served anything. I have only endured.*

Corvane watched him feel all of this, and waited, because Corvane was a man who understood that temptation does its own work once planted.

"There's a flaw in your offer," Quentin said at last, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved. "You keep saying *decide*. Stand at the door and decide. But you don't need my decisions, my lord. You need my blood. Maren told us—the Door drinks from the old line. You didn't cross a kingdom to recruit me. You crossed it to *harvest* me. Every word of this beautiful speech is a shepherd's whistle, and I'm the lamb."

"Yes," Corvane said.

Quentin blinked. Of all the responses in the world, he had not priced in honesty.

"Yes," Corvane repeated, mildly. "Of course I need your blood. I needed it the day the courier died; I'll need it the day we stand before the stone. Does that make the rest untrue? The pardon will be honored—I keep my instruments sharp, and a pardoned taster is sharper than a fugitive. The rank is real. The purpose—" he shrugged, a small, terrible gesture. "Purpose is real whenever a man accepts it. Quentin. Listen to me. You have two futures. In one of them, you keep running up this mountain with a woman whose code broke the day she killed a crowned man at a ford, carrying a map toward a door that will demand pieces of you neither of you can predict, pursued by nothing, protected by nothing, ending nowhere good. In the other, you walk down with me, warm, fed, forgiven, and you open the door as a free man instead of a bleeding key. Same blood either way. Same door either way. The only difference is whether anyone thanks you."

The crow called again, closer. Or it wasn't a crow. Quentin didn't turn to look.

"And the arsenal?" he asked. "The vials that make armies unkillable? When the door stands open, who decides what comes out?"

"The crown decides."

"The crown decided to erase a country."

"The crown decides," Corvane said again, without apology, "because that is what crowns are *for*. Someone must hold the leash, Ashgrove, or the dog eats the village. You of all people should appreciate containment—you've been a contained substance your whole life."

"And if the crown's hand slips?"

"Then the crown's hand slips," Corvane said, "as all hands slip, and history writes down what survives to write about it. I don't deal in forever, taster. I deal in next year."

There it was. The whole man, laid out on the granite shelf like a dish at a feast: brilliant, honest in the ways that mattered least, blind in every way that mattered most. Quentin had poisoned rats with more imagination.

He thought of Maren's face when she handed over the vial of ash. He thought of his father's name spoken like a prayer in a village that shouldn't exist. He thought of Wren at the frozen waterfall, her hand locked around his, refusing to let go even when letting go would have been easier, safer, saner.

He thought of the children in the hidden valley, learning to knead bread from a man who had believed himself unlovable, laughing at the way flour dusted his nose.

"No," Quentin said.

Corvane sighed—not disappointed, exactly. Something more like a craftsman watching good clay refuse the wheel. "You haven't heard the alternative."

"I've seen the alternative. It's named after you and it's standing in front of me."

"The alternative," Corvane continued, as if Quentin hadn't spoken, "is that I don't actually require your consent. Consent is cleaner. Blood taken willing opens faster—or so the archives suggest. But unwilling works. Unwilling has worked before." He glanced north, toward Ashfell, toward the Door, and his voice went quiet and factual and infinitely chilling. "Your father, Quentin. The last taster of the old line. The records differ on whether he walked in or was carried. Either way, the door drank."

The world tilted. Quentin caught himself on the granite shelf, snow crunching under his palm, and for one vertiginous second he was eight years old again in the orphanage yard, holding a spoonful of bitterness, being told by everyone, always, that it was for his own good.

"You know what happened to him," Quentin whispered. "You've known all along. You've known since the capital."

"I know what the archives say. The archives are incomplete. Which is precisely why I intend to open the door myself and read the primary source." Corvane straightened his frost-crusted collar with two cracked fingers, a small courtly gesture absurd and awful in the wilderness. "Take a day, Ashgrove. Take two. I'm camped in the throat of the valley you'd need to pass through, and I've grown quite patient—mountains teach patience better than courts do. When you're ready, send the justice down with a white cloth, or come alone. I'll even provide the wine." The thin smile returned. "You can taste it first. Old habits."

He turned and walked down into the pines, sure-footed on the ice, unhurried, a man leaving a dinner party rather than a siege line, and the snow closed behind him without a trace, as though the mountain itself had agreed to keep his secrets too.

Quentin stood alone on the granite shelf for a long time, the water skin forgotten at his feet, freezing solid by inches.

Above him, faint on the wind, he heard the bothy door creak—Wren, coming down at last, having heard everything, having waited to learn what he would do. Below him, in the valley throat, woodsmoke rose from a camp that had appeared out of nowhere, marking the pass they needed like a brand on a steer.

They were not being chased anymore. He understood that now, standing in the killing cold with his heart hammering out the oldest rhythm he knew—*swallow, endure, survive.* They were being herded. Every route, every choice, every mercy Corvane had shown them since the capital had been a fence post, and the last fence was the Door itself, and behind the Door, alive or dead or something the archives couldn't name, his father was keeping vigil over an arsenal that the man downstairs intended to drink the mountain dry to reach.

Quentin picked up the water skin, cracked the ice skinning its surface with his thumb, and climbed back up toward the bothy and the woman waiting in it.

They had until he decided. And the worst of it—the thing that made his stomach turn like bad shellfish—was how long the list of reasons to decide *for* Corvane had grown, and how short, how terribly short, the list of reasons against had become.

All except one. One reason, one name, one father behind a door.

He climbed.
