## 23. The Ascent of Ashfell

The mountain did not want them.

Wren had climbed enough of Thornfell's shoulders to know the difference between a slope that merely tested you and one that refused you outright, and Ashfell was the second kind. It rose out of the white like a black tooth, its upper reaches scoured bare of trees, the wind coming off it in long flat gusts that carried ice crystals fine as flour. Every hour the air grew thinner and meaner, as if the mountain were rationing its breath on purpose.

"Charming place," Tam said, and immediately bent double coughing, because at this altitude even sarcasm cost something.

"Save your wind," Wren told him. "You'll want it for turning around."

She said it lightly. She didn't feel light about it. She'd made the decision two nights ago in the bothy, watching the boy sleep with his frostbitten fingers curled against his chest like a beggar's hoard, and she still hadn't found a way to say it plainly: *you cannot come where we are going.* So instead she said cruel small things wrapped in kindness, and hated herself for the cowardice of it.

Quentin came up the trail behind them, moving with the deliberate economy of a man who had learned that wasted motion was paid for later. He'd been quiet since the whiteout—quieter than usual, which was saying something—and there was a new way he carried the ash-vial against his breastbone, not hidden but *kept*, the way other men kept a relic or a debt.

"How far to shelter?" he asked, nodding at the black mass above.

"There's a shelf under the west face. Old shepherd's work, if the shepherds ever worked this high." Wren squinted into the wind. "Three hours. Four if the weather turns."

"And if it turns?"

"Then we learn what we're made of."

*We already learned,* she thought. *In the chute. On the ice.* Her right hand still remembered the shape of Quentin's fingers in hers, the groan of the frozen waterfall above them, the moment when the whole white world had leaned inward to listen and decided, for reasons of its own, to let them live. She hadn't let go of his hand until they reached solid rock, and neither had he, and neither of them had mentioned it since, which was its own kind of conversation.

They climbed.

The wind was the worst of it—not the cold, which was honest and could be fought with wool and motion, but the wind, which lied. It came from behind on the switchbacks so that you felt warm and forgot yourself, then swung around the shoulder of the massif and took everything back at once, breath and heat and certainty. Twice Wren called a halt just to press her back against the stone and let the mountain shout itself out over her head. Tam shook between halts. He tried to hide it. He was fourteen; hiding things was the whole of his profession.

By midafternoon they reached the shelf, and it was there—a shallow cave under an overhang of gray basalt, floored with old straw gone to dust, its walls blackened by fires lit by people whose names no living tongue remembered. Wren got a flame going with hands she couldn't feel, and the three of them crowded the small heat while the sky outside went the color of a dead man's lips.

Tam slept first, the way the young do, dropping mid-sentence into darkness. Wren watched him breathe and made her decision again, the way you test a knot.

Quentin sat down beside her, close enough to share warmth without asking permission. That was another thing they didn't discuss.

"You've been rehearsing something," he said. "All day. Your jaw does this thing."

"My jaw does nothing."

"It does. Like you're chewing a verdict." He turned the vial over in his fingers, firelight sliding along the glass. "Is it me?"

"It's him."

Wren looked at the sleeping boy and finally said the plain thing, because Quentin had a way of pulling the plain thing out of her like a splinter.

"He can't climb the last of it. Look at his hands. Look at his breathing. Above the shelf there's no shelter, no wood, nothing but killing cold and whatever waits at the top. He's carried us as far as his body will allow, and if I take him higher I'm not being kind, I'm spending him." Her voice stayed level. She'd sentenced men to hang with this voice. "So he goes back."

"Alone?"

"With letters."

Quentin frowned. "Letters?"

"That's the part I've been chewing." She drew from inside her coat the oilskin packet she'd been building since the ford—the day she'd killed a man wearing a royal badge and crossed her own final line. "If we don't come down from this mountain, someone should know what happened up here. Not Corvane's version. Not the court's version. The truth, written by the only people who saw it." She tapped the packet. "Mine's in there. All of it. The tribunals. The lord I hanged. What I did at the ford. Why. If I die on Ashfell, that paper is my last act of justice, and it needs to get south."

Quentin was silent a long moment. Then he said, quietly, "You're trusting a boy who sold us to Corvane."

"I'm trusting a boy who walked away from Corvane through thirty miles of winter because he couldn't stomach what the man did to a prisoner." She met Quentin's eyes. "I've hanged people for less than Tam did. I've pardoned people for more. Mercy isn't trust, taster. It's a wager. And I've decided the odds."

Quentin looked at her for a moment longer, and something in his face shifted—not agreement exactly, but recognition. Then he nodded, slowly, and said, "Then I'll write too."

He wrote by firelight that night, hunched over the page with his coat around his shoulders, and Wren deliberately did not read a word of it. Some confessions belonged to their addressees alone. Hers she had written in three drafts and burned twice before letting stand; his, judging by the number of times he stopped and stared into the flames, was costing him more.

Near dawn, she woke Tam.

He came awake the way road-children do—all at once, hand already seeking the knife he'd lost at the ford. Wren put her own hand flat on the straw between them, empty palm up, and waited until his eyes focused and he understood.

"We need to talk," she said. "You're going south."

* * *

He argued, of course. She'd expected that. He argued with his voice cracking and his frostbitten hands shaking and all the furious dignity a half-grown boy could muster, and Wren let him spend it, because the argument wasn't really about going south. It was about being sent away. There was a difference, and she knew it, and she let him have it anyway.

"The passes—" he said. "You can't find the Door without—"

"Without you? No. But we found the valley without you, and Maren drew us the way, and the way is the way whether I carry it in a boy's memory or my own." She crouched in front of him, forearms on her knees, close enough that he couldn't look anywhere else. "Listen to me, Tam. This isn't exile. It's an errand, and it's the hardest one on this mountain."

She put the oilskin packet in his hands.

"These go south. Quentin's and mine. You deliver them or you burn them, but you don't read them, and you don't lose them. There are names in mine—families in the capital that Lord Corvane broke on his way up. If we don't come back, those families deserve to know who owed them what. If we *do* come back—" she shrugged, "—then you've carried paper through a winter for nothing, and you can laugh at us forever."

Tam looked down at the packet. His throat worked.

"I ran once," he said. "From Corvane. Everyone will say I'm running again."

"Everyone will say whatever's easiest," Wren said. "That's what everyone is *for*." She let that sit, then softened her voice by a degree, which for her was nearly a confession. "I know the difference, Tam. Between running and choosing. Running looks behind. Choosing doesn't." She tipped her chin at the black bulk of Ashfell visible through the cave mouth, its summit lost in cloud. "Going down that mountain alone, through Corvane's country, carrying our sins in your pocket—that's not running. That's the bravest thing anyone on this peak will do this week, and I include myself."

"Why me?" His voice was very small. "Why not send the taster?"

"Because the taster's blood opens doors, and yours doesn't. Because you know the southern roads better than either of us. And because—" she paused, and decided to give him the true reason, since he'd earned one true thing, "—because I promised myself I would never spend a child to buy my own way. Corvane spends children. Kings spend children. I don't. That's the whole of my code, boy. It's short, but it holds."

Tam cried then, briefly and furiously, wiping his face with the heel of his hand as though the tears were an insult he intended to prosecute. Wren looked away to give him privacy, which was the only courtesy she knew how to offer.

When he'd finished, Quentin was standing at the cave mouth with his pack already half-stripped, adding to Tam's load: salt pork, the good flint, a strip of dried venison for each day of descent. He worked without ceremony, and when he finished he held out one last thing—a small knife with a bone handle, the one he'd carried since the capital.

"For the roads," he said simply. "And for after. When you're grown and somebody needs feeding, come find us. I'll teach you to make soup that makes weak men weep."

"I thought you were a poisoner," Tam said, wet-eyed and grinning despite himself.

"A taster," Quentin corrected. "There's a difference. Poisoners kill carelessly. Tasters pay attention." He glanced sideways at Wren, and she caught the ghost of something in his face—pride, maybe, or grief wearing pride's clothes. "It's the paying attention that saves people."

* * *

They left at first light, if a light that gray and sourceless could be called first anything. The storm had spent itself in the night, leaving behind a world scoured clean and blinding, the snow squeaking underfoot like new leather. At the point where the shelf ended and the true ascent began—a spine of bare rock climbing toward the cloud deck—Wren called the halt she'd been dreading.

This was where the mountain split them.

She checked Tam's bindings herself, tightening knots with fingers that wanted to fumble, refusing to let them. She checked his rations, his flint, the letters pressed flat against his chest beneath two layers of wool. She gave him the bearings south in the flat reciter's cadence she used for sentencing, because it was the only voice she had that never shook.

"Do you have them?" she asked, one final time.

"The letters. The bearings. The knife." Tam patted each in turn. "And my life, which you gave back twice now, so don't expect me to waste it."

"Good." Wren stepped back. The words she wanted—*be careful, boy, come spring, find us*—sat in her throat like stones, and she swallowed every one of them, because this was the trade: you let go of what couldn't be carried, and you didn't weep about it on the mountain. "Go."

He went. Three steps, five, ten. Then he turned around, because of course he did, because he was fourteen and the world was ending the way it always ended for the young: completely, personally, for the first time.

"Wren!" he shouted into the thin air, cupping his hands. "The lord you hanged! In your letter—tell his family I helped carry it. Tell them it got there!"

And then he was gone, a small dark figure descending the white spine, getting smaller, until the glare took him and Wren Calloway stood alone on the roof of Thornfell with a taster and a silence.

She did not cry. She had decided years ago that the mountain could not have that, and it occurred to her, distantly, that this was the first rule she'd ever kept out of love rather than fear.

* * *

Above the cloud line the world simplified terribly. Sky above, rock below, wind everywhere, and somewhere ahead a door that had no business existing. They roped together—Wren leading, Quentin following at the length of rope she'd chosen because it was long enough to save him and short enough to feel him breathe. The cold had passed through pain days ago into something stranger, a vast indifference, as though her body had submitted papers of surrender and was merely awaiting processing.

At the second rest stop Quentin spoke for the first time in hours.

"What did you write to them? The families?"

Wren worked her jaw inside her scarf. The wind stole warmth from every word before it finished forming. "Names. Dates. What I took and why. An apology that doesn't ask forgiveness, because I haven't earned the right to ask." A pause. "You?"

"My son."

The words fell into the wind and Wren nearly lost her footing—not on the ice, but inside. Quentin had never spoken of a son. Had never spoken of anyone. He was the most self-erased man she had ever known, a man who had made himself invisible so thoroughly that even his confessions traveled anonymously.

"He died," Quentin went on, when it was clear she wouldn't ask. "Eight years old. Fever. I was tasting for the king the week it happened—I couldn't leave, there was a state dinner, and I told myself duty, I told myself *someone has to stand between the crown and the poison*, and I stood there and swallowed arsenic in front of three hundred courtiers while my boy—" His voice did not break. It simply stopped, the way a clock stops, and resumed elsewhere. "My letter isn't to him. He can't read it. It's to whoever finds it. It says what I did and why, and it says that *why* is not the same thing as *excuse*, and that if anyone ever offers a lonely man a great purpose, he should check what it costs the people who love him." He looked up at her across the rope. "You asked once why I ran instead of staying to face Corvane honestly. That's why. I've already been honest too late once. I won't be late twice."

Wren stood in the killing wind and understood that she was looking at the only honest man she'd ever met, and that honesty, at this altitude, weighed more than rope or iron, and that she had tied herself to it willingly.

"Then we climb," she said. "Both of us. Whatever's at the top—we owe it to the ones we couldn't carry."

They climbed.

The last pitch took them four hours the map said should take two, and it took everything. The basalt spine narrowed until the drop yawned on both sides, and the wind came up the face in long rhythmic shoves, patient as a torturer, and Wren counted her own breaths aloud because counting was the oldest trick she knew for staying inside her body. Once, near the top, Quentin slipped—an entire heartbeat of weight hitting the rope, his boot scraping stone—and she hauled him back with arms that screamed, and they lay against the rock afterward, cheek to cold rock, laughing in gasps that had nothing to do with humor.

And then, abruptly, there was no more up.

The spine opened onto a broad saddle below the summit cone, sheltered from the worst of the wind, and the silence that fell when they crawled into its lee was so total Wren could hear her own heart, could hear Quentin's, could hear the mountain's old slow creak of ice settling into itself. She unroped with fingers that barely obeyed and looked up—

—and stopped.

Set into the summit cone's flank, facing south like a watcher turned homeward, was a shape in the rock. From here it might have been geology—a seam, a shadow, a fold of darker stone. But it was too regular. Too deliberate. And as the clouds thinned and the low sun struck the face of Ashfell full-on, the shadow resolved into lines, and the lines resolved into geometry, and the geometry resolved into a symbol she knew.

Wren had seen it before. As a child, in her grandmother's kitchen, traced in spilled flour by an old woman who sang lullabies in a language the crown had outlawed and drew this mark on the hearthstone every solstice night, whispering *so we remember, so we remember.*

The mark of Vaelmoor.

The same mark burned into the wax seal of a dying courier's message. The same mark inked at the heart of a stolen map. Here, carved into the roof of the world, larger than a house.

"Quentin," she said, and her voice came out strange, flattened by awe and altitude and something older than either. "Do you feel that?"

He had gone very still beside her, one gloved hand hovering an inch from the rock face. When he answered, his breath came out in a long silver plume that the wind pulled apart and scattered.

"It's warm," he said. "Wren—the stone is warm."

And beneath their feet, faint as a sleeper's pulse, deep in the bones of the mountain, something hummed.
