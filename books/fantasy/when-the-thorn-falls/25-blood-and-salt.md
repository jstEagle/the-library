## 25. Blood and Salt

The Door did not open.

Wren had known it wouldn't, not yet, but knowing did nothing to soften the blow of standing before a wall of basalt carved with the answer to her grandmother's lullabies and finding it mute as any other stone. The frost on the mountain lay thick enough to muffle their footfalls, and their breath hung in ragged columns, and the Door—warm to the touch, faintly humming beneath her palm like a sleeping animal—simply waited.

"It knows we're here," Quentin said.

"Then why won't it let us in?"

"Because it's waiting for something." He ran his fingers along the carved symbol, the seven-pointed star with its single broken ray, tracing grooves that were worn smooth at their centers by hands long dead. "There's a mechanism. Look—" He crouched, brushing snow from a shallow basin set into the rock at knee height, ringed by channels that spiraled inward like water seeking a drain. The basin was stained dark, and Wren felt her stomach tighten when she saw the color of the stain. Not rust. Not lichen.

Old blood, dried into the stone.

"There," Quentin said quietly. "Blood and salt."

Wren looked at him. His face was gray with cold and something else—the particular pallor of a man who has just understood what is being asked of him and has not yet decided whether to pay it. She'd seen that look on condemned men. On smugglers deciding whether to run. She had never expected to see it on Quentin Ashgrove, who faced down poisoned suppers the way other men faced down breakfast.

"The vial," she said.

He drew it out of his coat. Maren's parting gift: a stoppered glass phallus no longer than his thumb, filled with a powder so fine it moved like liquid when he tilted it, gray-white, glittering faintly, smelling even through the glass of burnt rosemary and rain. Vaelmoori ash. Salt from the burned country, ground from the bones—or so the elder had implied, and neither of them had asked twice.

"And your blood," Wren said. "A taster's blood. That's what she told us."

"That's what she told us." He turned the vial over. Powder shifted, settled, glittered again. "Wren. If this goes wrong—if it's not a key but a lock, if it seals instead of opens—"

"Then we've climbed a mountain for nothing and I'll owe you an apology." She kept her voice flat because flat was what she could manage. "You don't have to do this."

"Yes I do." He said it simply, the way he said most true things, and she hated how much it cost her to hear it. "I've been drinking poison my whole life for someone else's table. Let me drink this one for mine."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the Door itself, which radiated that impossible warmth like a hearth built into the mountain's ribs. Wren would not have believed stone could be warm at this altitude if she hadn't pressed both palms against it. It made no sense. Almost nothing since the courier died at Quentin's table had made sense, and she had learned to stop demanding explanations and start demanding survival instead. But warmth was warmth, and they were alive because of it.

Quentin ate little. He sat with the vial balanced on his knee, turning it, turning it, and watched the light die off the western peaks.

"Tell me what you're thinking," Wren said.

"I'm thinking about my mother." He didn't look up. "She died when I was six. Fever, they said. My father went into service at the palace two years later and took me with him into the kitchens, and Master Corbin started my regimen before my ninth birthday. Bitter almond every morning. Hemlock extract on feast days. A drop of something in my evening porridge that Master Corbin never named and I never asked about, because asking questions in the king's kitchens is how you end up in the river." He paused. "I thought it was training. I thought—I built my whole life around the idea that I was being made strong. Hardened. That all those years of sickness and shaking and vomiting into a bucket while other boys slept were *worth something*."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in front of a door my father's people built, holding a key my father's people brewed, and I'm thinking: they never stopped dosing me. Not once. Twenty years. Someone kept the ledger of my body current the whole time, which means someone was always planning for me to come here." He laughed, short and bitter, a sound that steamed in the cold air. "I used to think my immunity was the one thing that belonged to me. Something I earned. Turns out it's a coat somebody sewed onto my skin before I could walk."

Wren reached across the fire and put her hand over his where it gripped the vial. His knuckles were white. She didn't say anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie or a platitude, and she had spent too many years sentencing liars to tolerate either.

After a while he said, "It might hurt."

"Probably."

"It might kill me."

"Also probably not. Maren wanted you to open the door, not die in front of it." She squeezed his hand once and let go. "But if you do die, I'll write it down honestly in my book. *Died as he lived: swallowing things that should have killed him.*"

The corner of his mouth twitched. "That's almost tender, Calloway."

"Don't tell anyone."

* * *

He drank the ash at dawn.

They mixed it first—he insisted on mixing it—with melted snow, in his tin cup, because the powder alone would scour the throat raw. The salt dissolved reluctantly, clouding the water gray, and the smell filled the hollow between the mountains like something ancient exhaling. Burnt rosemary. Rain on hot stone. Underneath both, faintly, something sweet that made the back of Wren's teeth ache.

Quentin held the cup in both hands. His breath came fast and shallow.

"To Vaelmoor," he said, and drank.

For three heartbeats, nothing happened. Wren counted them out of habit, the way she counted everything—a justice's reflex, measuring the world in beats and intervals—and then Quentin's knees buckled.

She caught him before he hit the stone. He was heavier than he looked, and burning up, his skin going from winter-gray to flushed red in the space of a breath, and his eyes were open but rolling, seeing nothing that stood in front of him. The cup rang away across the rock. She lowered him down gently, cradled his skull off the basalt, and shouted his name twice with no answer.

Then the Door began to sing.

Low, at first—a hum she'd felt rather than heard before, now rising to an actual tone, a chord of two notes that wound around each other and refused to resolve. The warmth coming off the stone intensified until the snow around them slumped and slid, retreating in a widening circle, and the carved channels of the basin began to glow a dull ember-red.

"Wake up," Wren snarled at Quentin, gripping his shoulders. "Wake up, damn you, I can't carry you down this mountain—"

His back arched. His mouth opened. And he screamed—but it wasn't a scream of pain, or not only that. It was the scream of a man seeing something, and Wren, kneeling beside him with no idea what else to do, leaned close and listened to what he was saying, because he was saying things, fragments spilling out between clenched teeth.

"—the stair, there's a stair behind the wall—"

The Door's chord climbed higher.

"—he's carrying the seal, he's carrying it up, why is he carrying it up—"

And then, clear and quiet, in a voice gone suddenly calm, gone suddenly *other*, as though someone else were speaking through his mouth:

*"Father?"*

* * *

Wren did not leave him. Whatever was happening—visions, alchemy, the dead country reaching up through his blood to take hold of him—she stayed, one hand on his chest, feeling his heart hammer and slow and hammer again, and she talked to him because talking was the only tool she had. She told him about the weather. She told him about her grandmother's kitchen, the wood stove and the smell of yeast, the forbidden lullabies sung low after dark. She told him she was still there, still watching, and that if he died she would be very annoyed, because she had carried his pack up half a mountain and he owed her the weight of it back.

The visions held him for what she later judged to be the better part of two hours. When he finally surfaced, it was slowly, like a drowning man rising through deep water—his eyes focusing past her, then on her, then through her again, and his face doing something terrible and private that she had to look away from, because whatever grief was moving through it was not hers to witness.

"He stayed," Quentin said. His voice was scraped raw. "He's still staying."

"Who?"

"My father." He pushed himself upright against the Door, and Wren saw that he was crying without seeming to notice it, tears freezing in the wind on his cheeks. "He sealed the Door from the inside, Wren. Twenty-three years ago. And he's *alive*. He's alive inside the mountain, kept that way by the same alchemy that's kept me dosed since I was a boy, and he's been standing vigil at the vault ever since, and he knew—" His voice broke. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "He knew I'd come. He saw my name in the ledger. He said—"

He stopped. Breathed. Started again.

"He said he chose the country over me. He said it plainly, like a confession, like he'd rehearsed it for twenty years waiting to say it to my face. *Someone had to stay. Someone had to keep the door.* And the Door opened for me anyway, because the mechanism doesn't care about his vigil, it only cares about the blood, and the ash, and—" He fumbled in his coat and pulled out the map, unrolled it against his knee.

The symbol at the heart of Thornfell—the seven-pointed star, the broken ray—was fading. Not ink-fading; it was sinking into the paper, each line dimming like a coal going out, and as Wren watched, the last point of the star vanished entirely and left the map blank at its center. Blank, and then not quite blank: new lines surfacing, fine as spider silk, sketching the outline of a passage, a stair, a chamber.

"It heard you," Wren said quietly. "The map's showing the way in."

"It's showing me my father." Quentin folded the map with hands that shook only a little. "Twenty-three years, Wren. He let me grow up an orphan in the king's kitchens. He let me believe I was nobody's son. Because the vigil needed him, and the key needed to be raised far away and ignorant, and I—" He swallowed. "I understand it. That's the worst part. I understand exactly why he did it. I'd have done the same, standing in his place, weighing one boy against a nation's grave."

"But you weren't standing in his place," Wren said. "You were the boy."

"Yes." He looked up at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly steady. "Yes, I was the boy."

* * *

The Door stood open.

Somewhere in the singing, in the hour when Quentin lay raving and Wren talked to him about bread, the basalt had parted along a seam no eye could find, drawing back into the mountain like a curtain, leaving a threshold of smooth black stone and a breath of air flowing out of it—warm, mineral, tasting faintly of the same burnt sweetness as the ash. Beyond the threshold, a passage descended into the mountain's heart, its walls glowing with veins of that ember-light, bright enough to see by, warm enough to live in.

Neither of them moved toward it for a long time.

Wren checked her knives. Checked Quentin's pack, redistributed the weight, tightened the straps herself because his hands still weren't right. She lit no torch—the passage didn't need one—and stood at the threshold breathing the mountain's warm air and trying to make peace with what her gut was telling her.

Her gut said: *this is the moment everything changes.*

Her gut had been right about every ambush, every betrayal, every bad road since she was nineteen years old. She'd learned to trust it the hard way, over graves, and it had never once lied to her.

"What's wrong?" Quentin asked.

"I don't know yet." She looked down the descending passage, at the ember-veins pulsing slow as a sleeper's heartbeat, and felt the hair rise on her arms despite the warmth. "Something's down there. Something old. Your father, maybe. Or maybe just the weight of everything that's been buried."

"You could wait here."

"No." She said it before she'd thought about it, and then, having said it, found she meant it completely. "No. You'll want a witness. Whatever's down there—whoever your father is now, after twenty-three years alone with a secret—you'll want someone who saw it. Who'll remember it truly. That's what a justice does." She stepped over the threshold onto stone that hummed faintly underfoot. "Besides, you still owe me the weight of your pack."

Quentin followed her in. Behind them, the Door began to close—not quickly, not with grinding menace, but with the unhurried certainty of a thing that had done this before—and Wren turned to watch the strip of daylight narrow, and narrow, and go out.

In the last sliver of white mountain light, she saw something move at the bottom of the passage. Far below, deep in the ember-glow, a figure was climbing the stairs toward them. Slowly. One step at a time. As though it had been climbing for twenty-three years and did not know how to hurry anymore.

"Quentin," she said.

"I see it."

The Door sealed shut behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released, and the figure below came up out of the glow, and Quentin made a sound Wren had never heard from him before—half sob, half snarl, all wound—and started down the stairs to meet his father.
