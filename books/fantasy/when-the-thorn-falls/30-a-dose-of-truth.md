## 30. A Dose of Truth

The vault breathed.

Quentin had felt it from the moment the Door of Ashes swung wide—a faint warmth rising off the shelves like bread out of an oven, like a body under blankets. The air tasted of copper and cold stone and something older underneath, something that reminded him of the orphanage infirmary where he'd swallowed his first measured dose of arsenic at seven years old and been praised for not crying.

Now he stood among the shelves with his hands full of that inheritance, loading a satchel for a man he intended to kill.

"Carefully," Corvane said.

He said it the way he said everything—politely, reasonably, as though they were two clerks discussing the filing of documents. He stood near the chamber's entrance with one hand resting on Tam's shoulder, the boy's arms bound behind him, a knife held low against the lad's ribs in the casual grip of a man who had done this often enough to stop thinking about it. Wren knelt on the far side of the vault floor, wrists lashed, her face bruised from the exchange gone wrong. She was watching Quentin with an expression he couldn't afford to read.

"Carefully," Corvane said again. "You have handled three of them without incident. Continue exactly so."

Three of them. Three vials of Vaelmoor's arsenal lifted from their niches of black velvet, each one wrapped in waxed linen and nestled into the satchel's padded cells. Amber liquid. Green. One that was neither color at all but a slow silver swirl, like moonlight caught in oil. Quentin's hands were steady. That was the horror of it—they were steady because his body had been built for this, built by the same alchemists who had built these vials, and somewhere in the marrow of him a voice whispered *you know these, you've known them all your life.*

He did know them. Not individually—he'd never seen them before tonight—but he knew their grammar. The regimen they'd fed him as a child had a vocabulary: bitter for metals, sweet for venoms, salt for the things that lived in blood. He could hold a vial to the lantern light and read its intent the way other men read letters.

And one of them, back on the shelf behind him, had a signature he recognized the way you recognize your own name written in someone else's hand.

The destabilizer. Maren hadn't named it that—the old woman had called it *the unbinding*, spoken of it only once, in a whisper, when she'd pressed the ash-vial into his hands and told him what the Door required. But she had described its look: a gray-green liquor that separated and rejoined itself, over and over, patient as tide. *It does not kill,* she'd said. *It unmakes what was made. It takes back every gift.* And a body engineered against poison, stripped of its gifts, standing inside a vault saturated with ambient alchemy—

He didn't let himself finish the thought. Finishing thoughts got men killed. He finished the wrapping instead, folded the linen corners neat as a cook folding a pastry, and set the fourth vial into the satchel.

"Good," Corvane murmured.

Tam made a small sound. Quentin glanced up despite himself. The boy was gray-lipped, shaking, his eyes fixed on Quentin with a terrible question in them. *You're going to get us out of this. Aren't you. Aren't you.*

Quentin had fed that boy kitchen-craft through a whole winter. He'd taught him to test flour between his fingers, to listen for the change in a boiling pot's voice. He had never taught him how to lie with his whole face while his heart tried to climb out of his throat.

So he didn't try. He kept his eyes down and his hands moving and gave Tam nothing to hope with, because hope was loud, and loud things got people killed in rooms like this.

* * *

Corvane made him work through the night.

That was the spymaster's method, Quentin understood now—had understood since the capital, since the polite bottomless interrogation across a table laid with wine. Patience as an instrument. He would not simply take the vials and go. He catalogued. He asked questions in a soft voice, walking the aisles of the vault with his fingers trailing along the stone, reading the Vaelmoori inscriptions aloud in badly accented syllables, making Quentin correct him. Every correction was a small surrender. Every surrender was noted.

"This shelf," Corvane said, tapping a niche near the vault's heart. "Empty. Why?"

"I don't know."

"You know everything else. You read the margins of a map no living scholar could parse. You navigated a valley that isn't on any map. You opened a door sealed for thirty years." He smiled. It was a good smile. Quentin remembered thinking, in the capital, that it was the smile of a man who found the world interesting, and had mistaken it for warmth. "So. Why is this shelf empty?"

"Because something was taken from it," Quentin said. "Or because something in it was used up."

"Which?"

"I don't know."

Corvane studied him for a long moment. Then he turned to Tam and pressed the knife a half-inch closer, not cutting, just announcing, and said, "Guess."

Quentin's stomach turned over. He thought of Maren's confession, of the treaty, of a nation erased because its alchemists had learned to make armies that could not die. He thought of his father behind the inner door, hollow-eyed and ancient and alive past all reason, keeping vigil over exactly this. "Used up," he said. "I think—there are records in the vigil chamber. Ledgers. If a dose was drawn, it would be recorded."

"It would," Corvane agreed pleasantly, and left the shelf alone.

Hours passed. Quentin loaded eleven vials. His back ached. His mouth had gone dry as ash, and he kept thinking about water and not letting himself drink, because drinking meant swallowing, and swallowing meant his throat might remember how much it wanted to refuse everything currently passing through this room.

Near the middle of the night—or what he judged was the middle of the night; the vault had no windows, only the lantern and its own warm breathing dark—Wren spoke for the first time in hours.

"The satchel won't hold more than twelve," she said. Her voice came out flat and rough. "If you're planning to carry it down a mountain, Corvane, you'll want a second bag. Or fewer ambitions."

"Justice Calloway." Corvane turned, delighted, as if she were a dessert course arriving. "Still advising. Even trussed. They broke you out of the crown's courts for impertinence exactly like this, I imagine."

"They broke me for sentencing a lord you were paid to protect."

"I wasn't paid anything. I was ordered. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire architecture of civilization." Corvane crouched in front of her, knife still loose in his grip, head tilted. "Do you know why I let you live back at the ford? Before, I mean—before the Door, before all this. I could have taken the map from your corpse."

"Because dead justices make martyrs and live ones make examples."

"Because you're useful," Corvane said. "Martyrs are useless. Examples are decorative. Useful people—" he straightened, gesturing at Quentin, at the satchel, at the whole warm humming dark, "—useful people build things. Look at this. Thirty years of erasure, and in one night we've undone it. Because a taster ran, and a justice chased him, and both of you were too stubborn to die on the way. That's not treason, Calloway. That's providence."

Wren spat blood onto the vault floor. It was dry-mouthed and mostly gesture, but Quentin loved her for it with a fierceness that frightened him.

* * *

It was near dawn, or the vault's version of it, when Quentin saw his opening.

Corvane had the satchel open on a flat stone, checking each cell, re-wrapping two vials whose linen Quentin had folded too loosely on purpose—not enough to damage, just enough to draw the spymaster's eye and his hands, to keep him occupied with the cargo rather than the carrier. Tam had slumped against the wall, half-asleep upright, which was its own kind of agony to watch. Wren's eyes tracked everything.

And Quentin stood beside the shelf where the unbinding waited, gray-green and patient, separating and rejoining itself like a tide that never tired.

His hands were steady. His hands were always steady. That was the first thing they'd ever taught him about himself, back in the infirmary with the measuring spoon: *the boy doesn't shake.* He had spent forty years believing it was courage. Then believing it was damage. Now he knew it was design—and design could be aimed.

But there was a problem, and the problem was that Aldous Corvane had not survived twenty years as the crown's spymaster by drinking things offered to him by men he was robbing.

Quentin had watched poisoners work. He'd sat at the king's table for a decade and catalogued every attempt: the slow metals in the wine, the quick alkaloids in the sauce, the fool who dusted the sugar. And he knew the single law that governed them all. No poisoner ever offers a draught openly. Openness is the one costume poison cannot wear.

So he did the thing that went against every instinct his survival had ever taught him.

"Lord Corvane." He kept his voice level, tired, a workingman's voice. "Before you carry those down, there's a problem you should know about."

Corvane's head came around slowly. "A problem."

"The vault isn't passive." Quentin touched the nearest shelf, felt the warmth of it, let his hand linger as if reluctant—as if confessing something costly. "The ambient alchemy. My immunity protects me—I was made for it, you know that now, it's why you need me. But you've been breathing this air for hours. And these vials—" he nodded at the satchel, "—they're not inert in transit. They interact. With each other. With carriers."

"Go on." The pleasantness had thinned. Underneath it was the sharp attention of a man who had built a career on hearing the sentence underneath the sentence.

"There's a reason the old records specify handlers," Quentin said. "Vaelmoori handlers. Trained, dosed, protected. An unprotected man who carries this stock risks—it's in the ledger in the vigil chamber, I can show you the notation—but the short form is that the compounds leach. Through the skin, through breath. And a man carrying them who hasn't been prepared..." He let the silence do its work, then finished quietly: "There's a preparatory draught. The handlers drank it before every handling. It buffers. It buys maybe six hours of safe carriage."

"And where is this draught?"

Quentin turned and took down a plain clay flask from a lower niche—one he'd noticed hours ago and marked, one that held nothing worse than a bitter mineral infusion, the vault's ordinary handler's tonic, harmless as tea. He set it on the flat stone between them.

"I can drink first," he said. "You'll watch me handle the rest of the stock either way. If I'm lying, you lose nothing but me—and you don't need me alive once the satchel's packed. Do you?"

The lantern hissed. Somewhere in the vault's deep stone, that faint hum shifted pitch, like a house settling.

Corvane looked at the flask for a long time.

Then he smiled, and it was the worst smile Quentin had ever seen, because it was the smile of a man agreeing, and men like Corvane only agreed to traps they'd already turned inside out.

"A reasonable offer," Corvane said. "From a reasonable man. Which is precisely what worries me." He hooked a boot around Tam's ankle and dragged the boy awake, upright, stumbling. "But we'll refine the protocol. The taster drinks. And then—" the knife came up, rested flat against Tam's collarbone, "—the boy drinks. And then I'll consider it."

"No—" Tam's voice cracked apart on the word.

"He's a child," Wren said, and her voice came off the floor like a blade leaving a sheath. "Whatever code you serve, Corvane—"

"I serve results." Corvane uncorked the flask himself, sniffed it with the delicacy of long practice, poured a measure into the clay cup beside it. He held it out to Quentin first. "Your health, taster."

Quentin drank.

Bitter mineral, iron-tinged, cold. Harmless. He let none of that reach his face—let his throat work, let him grimace slightly at the taste, because a truthful man gags at bad medicine and a liar performs it. He set the cup down. "There."

"Mm." Corvane poured again and held the cup to Tam's lips. "Slowly, boy. Choking wastes everyone's time."

Tam drank. His eyes stayed on Quentin the whole time, huge and wet and asking, and Quentin gave him the only thing he could: the smallest nod, a cook's nod, *it's fine, eat it, it's fine*—the nod he'd given a hundred nervous new kitchen boys tasting their first unfamiliar dish. Tam swallowed. Shuddered. Lived.

"Harmless," Corvane observed.

"As I said."

"As you said." Corvane set down the cup. He looked at Quentin for a moment too long—that bottomless politeness again, the interrogation that never ended—and Quentin felt the whole night balance on the point of that gaze. *He suspects. He suspects everything. He has suspected everything since the courier died at my table, and suspicion is his native weather, and still—*

Still, Corvane wanted this. That was the flaw in him, the flaw Quentin had counted on since the moment Wren had whispered *we only get one chance* through split lips: want. Twenty years of serving a crown that would never love him, and here at last lay a prize the crown itself would kneel for. Men who want greatly audit poorly. It was the one law of the king's table that had never failed.

Corvane picked up the flask and drank.

Not a sip. A handler's measure—three swallows, deliberate, wiping his mouth afterward with the back of his hand like a soldier. He set the flask down, exhaled, and rolled his shoulders as though shrugging off a long day.

"Bitter," he said. "Like everything in this country." He crouched and began checking the satchel straps. "We move within the hour. Taster—you'll carry the second bag. Justice—" a glance at Wren, almost fond, "—you'll walk between us, and you'll continue being useful by staying alive. The boy walks ahead of me. If anyone grows heroic on the descent, remember that I fall slowly, and heavily, and onto whoever is nearest."

No one answered. The lantern burned. The vault breathed its warm copper breath.

And Quentin turned back to the shelves to pack the final vials with hands that were, as always, perfectly steady, and a stomach that was churning like the sea.

Because he had watched Corvane swallow, and he had counted—three swallows, yes, but Corvane had drunk from the flask's lip, and the unbinding clung to clay, and more than that, more than any trick of dosage, Quentin had seen the man's throat move and understood with sick clarity that it was done. There was no taking it back. In six hours or less, the gray-green patience in the spymaster's blood would meet the vault's ambient alchemy and begin its quiet work of unmaking—and until then, they had to get a murderer, two prisoners, a child, and a satchel full of the world's most dangerous mercy down off this mountain without letting Corvane realize he was already dying.

Corvane straightened, shouldered the satchel, and paused.

"Taster," he said lightly. "One more thing. The draught—the real handler's draught. How long did you say it lasts?"

"Six hours," Quentin said. "Perhaps eight."

"Good." Corvane smiled, and pushed Tam toward the passage, and the lantern-light bent and shivered along the walls of the Door of Ashes as they filed out into the killing cold of the high mountain—and behind them, unnoticed by everyone but Quentin, the empty niche where the unbinding had sat was already growing cold.
