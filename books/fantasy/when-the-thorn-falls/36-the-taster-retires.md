## 36. The Taster Retires

The first sign was the smell.

Quentin had woken every morning of his life to a world muted at the edges—the kitchen smoke that never quite stung his eyes, the raw onion he could chew like an apple, the nightshade berries Maren's grandchildren kept daring each other to eat while he watched them the way other men watched children play near cliffs. His body had been a fortress for thirty years, garrisoned by doses he'd never chosen, walls raised in him before he was old enough to know what they defended against.

On the fourth morning after the descent from Ashfell, he smelled the tannery smoke from across the valley and it burned his throat like a brand.

He stood in the doorway of the cottage Wren had claimed for them—claimed being a generous word for what she'd done, which was walk in, set her boots by the door, and say *this one has a good hearth*—and breathed the winter air and felt it scour him raw. Smoke. Pine sap. The iron tang of the creek where the village women broke the ice each dawn. All of it arriving in his nose with the blunt, unmediated force of a stranger's fist.

His eyes watered. He laughed—a short, cracked sound that startled a hen off the woodpile—and wiped his face with the back of his hand, and found his cheeks wet, and could not for the life of him stop.

"Quentin?" Wren's voice from inside, sharp with the particular alarm she reserved for him. She still half-expected assassins behind every closed door. He suspected she always would.

"I'm fine," he called, and his voice broke on the word, and he laughed again, helplessly, standing in the snow in his shirtsleeves with tears freezing on his jaw, weeping over the smell of tannery smoke like a man greeting a long-lost brother.

Because that was what it was. That was exactly what it was.

* * *

Maren understood before he finished explaining, which was her way; she had understood most things about him since the day he walked into her valley, before he had understood them himself.

"The vault is gone," she said. They sat in her low-ceilinged house among the hanging herbs, bundles of yarrow and vervain turning slowly in the draft, and she poured him a tea he now had to let cool because he could feel its heat properly again, could feel everything properly, the way ordinary men did. "The alchemists' work was not only in the vials, Quentin. It was in you. Every dose they gave you as a child was anchored to the Door—sustained by it, renewed by it, the way a lamp is fed by its oil. You were a lamp lit from Vaelmoor's fire." She wrapped her knotted hands around her own cup. "You snuffed the fire."

"So I'm—" He turned the cup in his fingers. It was strange, holding something without knowing precisely what was in it. Thirty years of knowing. Gone. "I'm just a man."

"You are just a man," Maren agreed, and there was such gentleness in it, such deliberate kindness, that his throat closed all over again. "Welcome to the rest of us. We die of things. We suffer. We taste our food and are sometimes fooled by it." Her eyes crinkled. "And none of us have ever once been used as a key."

He had wept that first morning over tannery smoke, and he wept a little now, quietly, into his tea, and Maren pretended not to see, which was its own form of grace. Because it was relief—he needed someone to understand that it was relief. Any sane man would call it terror: to go from invulnerable to mortal overnight, to drink water without testing it, to sleep without the low hum of engineered certainty in his blood. Corvane would have called it weakness. The crown had built an empire on men who could not be poisoned, and called the building strength.

But Quentin had spent his whole life on the wrong side of a wall. He had eaten arsenic while kings dined. He had swallowed hemlock so a courtier's toast could proceed. He had been a piece of palace equipment—a sieve, a filter, a thing that absorbed harm so that better men needn't—and no one, in all those years, had ever asked whether the sieve wanted to be anything else.

Now he could be hurt. Now he could be killed by a careless cook or a vengeful rival or bad shellfish, same as any farmer or fletcher or fool. Now his body belonged to himself alone.

He cried into his tea, and Maren refilled it, and outside the snow went on falling softly on a country that officially did not exist, keeping its secret under white.

* * *

By the second week, the village had begun to organize itself around Wren, though neither of them had planned it. It happened the way weather happens. A dispute over a slaughtered goat; two brothers, a widow, and a disagreement about which family's beast had wandered whose pen. Someone fetched Wren Calloway the way you'd fetch a magistrate, except there was no magistrate within three weeks' travel and the crown's law had never reached this valley and never would now.

Quentin heard the proceedings through the cottage window while he worked: Wren's voice, level and unhurried, asking questions in her road-court cadence. Who saw the goat last? When did the pen gate stick, and who mended it? She listened the way she did everything—completely, without interruption, as if the answer were owed to the question—and when she ruled, she ruled simply: the widow was owed the goat's worth in spring lambs, and both brothers would mend her fence before thaw, and if either man grumbled about it he could bring his grievance to her directly, and no one brought their grievance to Wren Calloway directly unless they enjoyed losing.

That evening Quentin cooked.

It began, as these things did, with habit. There was a store of roots in the cellar—turnips, parsnips, a sack of dried beans, salt pork from the autumn slaughter—and a village that had spent a hard season hiding from history and deserved, in his opinion, a meal that wasn't survival rations. He had cooked for kings. He had cooked under threat of death, tasting each course twice, cataloguing bitterness against bitterness, his tongue a ledger where every entry was an attempt on someone's life. Cooking had been the shape his fear took. It had been the only language the palace ever let him speak.

But this kitchen was his. This small black stove, this scarred table, this window looking out on snow and woodsmoke and children sledding down the burying-mound hill with shrieks that carried across the whole valley. No king would eat this food. No poisoner would touch it. Nobody here had ever heard of arsenical wine or the bitter-almond note, and God willing nobody ever would.

He peeled turnips with a knife Wren had sharpened for him, badly, out of love, and the peels came off in long ribbons, and the onions made him cry a second time that month—for the ordinary reason, the human reason, the reason every cook in history had wept—and he stood there laughing and crying at once, stinging-eyed over a cutting board, happier than he had ever been in a royal kitchen with gold on the tables and death in the cups.

He tasted everything first.

Of course he did. Habit dies slowly; a tongue trained for thirty years does not forget its office. He dipped the spoon into the bean pot and held the broth on his tongue and read it automatically—salt, fat, the green ghost of the bay leaf, no bitterness, no almond, no metallic whisper beneath the warmth—and realized halfway through that he wasn't testing anymore. He was tasting. Just tasting. Just a man, standing in his kitchen, enjoying his own soup.

He tasted the pork drippings and thought of his father. Not the vigil-thing behind the Door of Ashes, the pale keeper sustained past all mercy—but the memory underneath, the one the visions had given back to him: a younger man in a small kitchen, lifting his son onto a stool so he could watch, saying *always taste first, Quentin. Always know what you're putting in your mouth.* He had thought, for twenty years in the capital, that the regimen had stolen that lesson from him—that the alchemists' needles had turned tasting into a sentence. But it hadn't. The lesson had been love first. The poison had come later. And now the poisons were gone and the lesson remained, and it was his, freely held, and he seasoned the soup until it was right.

The whole village came. That was the thing about a hidden valley in deep winter—news of a feast traveled faster than cold, and by dusk his little cottage and Maren's great hall next door were full to the rafters, every bench dragged out, every bowl in the settlement commandeered. He cooked in shifts, the pots going all evening, and between shifts he stood in the doorway watching people eat his food and felt something rearrange itself permanently in his chest.

A king had once told him—waving a fork, not even looking at him—that a taster's work was nothing, a dog's work, that any man could swallow. Quentin had bowed and said nothing, as he always said nothing, and gone back to the kitchens.

But no man could do this. No man could feed forty people in a famine winter and stand in the door and watch them laugh. That was the work. That had always been the work; he'd simply been doing it for the wrong table.

Wren found him there near midnight, scraping the last pot, the hall emptying, children asleep under benches. She leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed—she still crossed her arms like a woman expecting trouble, and maybe she always would too—and looked at him with an expression he was learning to read, which was the softest one she owned, the one she'd deny under oath.

"They're already calling it the Taster's Feast," she said. "Maren says they'll do it every year. Whether you like it or not."

"It'll be Tam's Feast by spring," Quentin said. "They'll rename everything by spring."

"No," Wren said, and there was something odd in her voice—not softness now but attention, the road-look, the look she got when the trees moved wrong along a trail. "No, I don't think they will."

He set down the spoon. "What is it?"

She hesitated. In the months he'd known her, Wren Calloway had hesitated perhaps four times, and every one of them had preceded news he didn't want.

"One of Maren's watchers came down from the pass at dusk," she said. "There's a traveler on the south road. Alone, on foot, half-dead by the look of the track he's leaving. Young, she thinks. And carrying something bundled against his chest like it matters more than breathing."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the snow kept falling on the passes they had sealed behind them, on the Door that no longer was, on the blank page where a country used to be.

"Describe him," Quentin said quietly, though he already knew, the way you sometimes know a dish is spoiled before you've lifted the lid.

"Small," Wren said. "Dark-haired. Limping favoring the left leg—the one Corvane's hounds worked over at the ford." Her eyes met his across the ruined kitchen, across the empty bowls and the dying fire and the whole warm wreckage of the best night the valley had known. "Quentin. It's Tam. And whatever he's carrying, he didn't stop running until he got here."
