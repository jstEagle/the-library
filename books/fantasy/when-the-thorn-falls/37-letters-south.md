## 37. Letters South

The thaw had come to the foothills in fits and starts, the way spring always came to the high country—apologizing for winter one day, withdrawing the apology the next. Wren Calloway stood at the edge of the new village's eastern path with her arms crossed against the morning chill, watching the road where it bent around a shoulder of granite and disappeared into pine shadow.

Tam was late.

She told herself it meant nothing. Roads were soft with melt; streams were running high; a boy traveling alone through country that still remembered Corvane's riders had a hundred good reasons to be slow. She told herself this while her thumb worried the edge of her belt knife, back and forth, back and forth, until she caught herself doing it and stopped.

He'd been gone eleven days. He'd promised seven.

*He also promised me he wasn't selling us to the spymaster,* she reminded herself, *and that promise held right up until it didn't.*

But he'd come back from that too. Half-frozen, hollow-eyed, carrying the news of what Corvane did to prisoners—and carrying, folded against his chest, their letters. Quentin's letter to no one. Her own letter to no one. Words written for families who might never receive them, entrusted to a boy whose loyalty had been bought once already and had cost them nearly everything.

She heard him before she saw him: the particular rhythm of a tired horse picking its way over loose stone. Then he rounded the bend, mud to the girth, leading the animal more than riding it, and Wren let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

"You're late," she called down.

"I'm alive," Tam called back. "You want punctuality or survival? Pick one."

It was Quentin's humor, worn like a borrowed coat. The boy had spent too many evenings by their fire on the descent. She didn't smile. She waited until he'd dismounted and handed her the reins before she said, quietly, "Well?"

Tam looked past her, toward the village—the scatter of timber houses going up along the stream, the smell of cut pine and woodsmoke, children hauling kindling with the solemnity of soldiers. He looked like a boy deciding how much truth a morning could hold.

"There's news," he said. "And there's news."

* * *

They talked in Maren's old house, or what remained of Maren's household now—Maren herself gone into the mountain with her king's vigil, her kitchen inherited by Quentin and his pots. But this room had been Wren's since they'd come down off Ashfell: a table, two chairs, a window facing south. She'd chosen the window deliberately. She liked knowing which way the capital lay.

Tam sat across from her, hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea he wasn't drinking, and gave it to her straight, because she'd taught him that much if nothing else.

"Corvane is dead," he said. "Officially. 'Lost on assignment in the Thornfell passes, presumed taken by avalanche.' There's a memorial plaque in the Hall of Records. I saw it. They carved his name in marble." His mouth twisted. "There were flowers."

"Of course there were," Wren said. "The dead are always better than the living. Especially when the death is convenient."

"The court's already rewritten everything else, too. I sat in taverns. I listened. The story is that Corvane uncovered a foreign plot—a Vaelmoori conspiracy, remnants of an erased nation trying to smuggle weapons across the border—and died heroically stopping it. The courier who died at your table, Quentin's flight, all of it—they've folded it into the same tale. Treason, foiled. Order, preserved. The crown sleeps safe." He shrugged, a boy's shrug that couldn't quite hide how much the words had sickened him. "By summer, nobody will even remember there was ever another version."

Wren stood and walked to the window. South. Somewhere down there, in the capital's gray sprawl, clerks were filing Corvane's name among the honored dead, and somewhere else—she thought of specific streets, specific doors—families were still waiting for answers about people who had never come home.

The merchant Aldous Renwick, taken off the King's Road three winters ago for a debt his brother owed, found floating in the canal with his ledgers burned. Corvane's work. The brother hanged for it on thin evidence, and Corvane had signed the warrant himself—she'd seen the signature years later, when she still believed signatures meant something.

The Vessic family. Mother, father, and the daughter who'd been a witness to something she shouldn't have seen—all three gone in a single night, house burned, ruled accidental. Wren had ridden past that ruin. She'd been a justice then. She'd read the report and found it clean, and moved on, because the reports were always clean when Corvane wrote them or had them written.

Eleven names she could swear to. Eleven she knew for certain. And behind those eleven, a shadow-list of dozens more—disappearances, accidents, suicides—that she would never be able to prove, because the proof had been Corvane's craft, and Corvane's craft had died with him in a vault under a mountain that officially contained nothing.

"They'll never reopen a single case," Tam said. "Not one. The story's too useful. A dead hero and a buried conspiracy—it explains everything and costs nothing."

"No," Wren agreed. "They won't."

"So what do we do?"

She turned from the window. On the table between them lay the letters—the ones Tam had carried south and brought back undelivered, because there had turned out to be nowhere lawful to deliver them. Quentin's pages, careful and sad. Hers, written in the hard slanting hand that had sentenced men on mountainsides.

"We tell the truth," she said. "Even if it changes nothing. Even if the marble plaque stays up and the flowers keep coming. The families get to know. That's not vindication. That's just—" She searched for the word and found it. "That's just accounting."

* * *

Quentin came at midday, as he seemed to come everywhere now: carrying something. Today it was bread, still warm, wrapped in cloth, set on the table like an offering at a shrine. He'd been cooking for the village for weeks, and the habit had settled into him so deeply that he arrived at every conversation bearing food, as if no discussion should be conducted empty-handed.

He listened to Tam's report without interrupting, which Wren had learned was his way of taking things seriously. When the boy finished, Quentin looked at the letters on the table for a long moment.

"You're going to send them anyway," he said. Not a question.

"One letter," Wren said. "Anonymous. To each of the eleven families—or to whoever's left of them. No signature. Nothing traceable. Just the truth: what happened to their people, who did it, and that he is dead."

"And the crown's version?" Tam asked. "Do we contradict it directly?"

"No." Wren sat down slowly. "That's the trap, don't you see? If we write 'the court lies,' it becomes sedition, and sedition gets the letter burned unread—or worse, it gets some clerk hunting the handwriting. We say nothing about the court at all. We say only what we know: the date, the place, the method. What we saw. Let the families hold our account beside the official one and draw their own conclusions. People aren't fools. They know what marble plaques are for."

Quentin lowered himself into the third chair—the one he'd started keeping at her table without either of them remarking on it—and tore a piece of bread, but didn't eat it. Turned it over in his fingers.

"When I was a taster," he said, "I learned that most poisons can't be detected by taste alone. Arsenic, certain alkaloids—you need training, you need the ledger in your head, you need to compare what you taste against what you know should be there. A lie works the same way. The court's story tastes almost right. Heroic death, foreign threat, order preserved." He looked up. "Almost right. But someone who knew Corvane—who knew what he actually was—would catch the note underneath. Bitter almond in the wine."

"That's why the families," Wren said. "They knew him. Some of them, anyway—knew what he took from them, even if they never knew his name for it. They'll taste it."

Tam frowned. "And if they write back? If they start asking questions, digging—if one of them goes to the capital and makes noise?"

"Then the crown will call them grief-mad cranks," Wren said, "and the story will hold, because stories held up by marble always hold. We can't fix that. We were four people against a kingdom, and the kingdom won the telling." She met Quentin's eyes. "All we can give them is the one thing the kingdom didn't take: the knowledge that they weren't wrong. That the world really did swallow their people whole, and it wasn't their fault, and somebody, somewhere, knows."

The bread went cold between them. Outside, a hammer rang against a nail, patient as a heartbeat.

"All right," Quentin said at last. "One letter. But we write it together. All three of us. If my hand's in it anywhere, I want the rest of you to have chosen it."

* * *

It took them two days.

Not because the facts were long—eleven deaths could be told plainly in a page—but because every sentence had to survive three readers with different fears. Tam kept striking anything that sounded like accusation; Wren kept striking anything that sounded like apology. Quentin, who had spent a lifetime reading faces across tables, read their drafts aloud in different voices until the words sat right.

They settled on a form at last: plain, dated, unadorned. For the Renwicks:

*"Your son and brother Aldous was taken on the fourth of Thawmonth, three years past, by agents of Lord Corvane, First Spymaster to the Crown, in settlement of a debt owed by no member of your family. He did not owe money. He witnessed a shipment he was not meant to see. He was killed the same night. His ledgers were burned to make the killing look like fraud. It was not fraud. He was innocent, and he is mourned by strangers who learned the truth too late to help him."*

For the Vessics, harder:

*"Your mother and father and sister were killed on the night of the nineteenth of Harvestmonth, five years past. The fire was not an accident. Your sister saw something she should not have seen, and Lord Corvane ordered the house burned to bury what she knew. There was no chance to run. It was quick. This is a small mercy and we do not pretend it balances anything. We only want you to know that the man who did it is dead—not honored-dead, not plaque-dead, but truly dead, and he died afraid."*

Wren had fought over that last line. *"He died afraid"*—it was vengeance dressed as information, and she knew it. Quentin had argued for keeping it. "They'll want to know that," he'd said. "Not for justice. For sleep. Grief keeps its own hours, and 3 a.m. is easier with that sentence in the house."

She'd let it stand. She was learning, from him, that mercy sometimes wore strange clothes.

Tam copied the final versions in a round merchant's clerk-hand, learned during his year of selling secrets—his penmanship, he observed grimly, was the one skill from his treason that proved worth keeping. Eleven letters. Eleven envelopes, addressed in the same neutral hand. No seal; seals invited questions.

"What about the twelfth?" Quentin asked quietly, when the stack was done.

Wren looked up. He was looking at the table, not at her, but she knew exactly whom he meant.

Maren. Taken by Corvane's men in the last days, tortured for the valley's location—Tam had carried that news down the mountain with the rest. Dead now, or so they believed; the snows had closed before anyone could go back and confirm, and the passes wouldn't open for weeks yet. Maren, who had fed them, sheltered them, given them the ash-vial and the truth of Quentin's father, and paid for it in ways Wren refused to imagine in detail because imagining it made her hands shake.

"There's no family to write to," Wren said. "Her family was that valley, and the valley knows what happened. They were there."

"Somebody loved her before the valley," Quentin said. "Somebody taught her the old songs. Somebody waited for letters that stopped coming forty years ago." He was quiet a moment. "When I opened my father's map, the first thing I felt wasn't wonder. It was anger—at everyone who'd decided, decades ago, what I was allowed to know about my own blood. Being erased is its own wound. Don't hand it to whoever's left of her."

So they wrote a twelfth letter, and it was the hardest, because they had almost nothing to put in it. Where Maren had gone. What she had guarded. That she had not broken quickly, and that the secret she died protecting was now buried so deep that no spymaster would ever dig it out again. *She won*, in essence. They wrote it without saying it, because saying it would sound like consolation, and consolation from strangers is salt in the wound.

Wren sealed none of the envelopes. Instead she melted plain wax over each flap and pressed it flat with her knife's spine—unmarked, anonymous, the wax of a person with no name and no allegiance. As she worked, Tam watched her with an expression she recognized from tribunal nights on the pass: the look of a boy watching law being made in front of him, and wondering whether he was witnessing order or its opposite.

"You've done this before," he said. "Sentenced people with less ceremony than a letter."

"Yes."

"And it doesn't bother you? Sending these out like—like arrows into the dark? You'll never know what they do. Whether they help or just reopen wounds that were scarring over."

Wren set down the knife. She considered the question seriously, because Tam deserved serious answers now; that was part of what she owed him, for the knifepoint and the mercy both.

"When I held tribunals on the roads," she said, "I always told the accused the same thing: I'm not giving you what you deserve. Nobody can weigh what a person deserves—I've tried, and the scales don't balance. What I'm giving you is the truth about what you did, spoken aloud, in front of witnesses, so it exists somewhere outside your own head. That's all judgment ever really is. Making the true thing exist." She tapped the stack of letters. "This is the same. I'm not healing anyone. I'm making the true thing exist. What they do with it belongs to them."

Tam nodded slowly. Then, with the blunt honesty that was his best feature and had very nearly gotten him executed: "You've changed. On the pass, you'd have said it was justice. Now you're calling it accounting."

"On the pass, I hadn't watched the crown carve a monster's name in marble," Wren said. "Accounting is what's left when you learn how little justice weighs."

* * *

They sent the letters south in threes, by three different hands, over the course of a week—Tam carrying four, a drover named Sela carrying four, and a tinker who traded through the foothills carrying the rest, all paid from the small fund of coin they'd scraped together and all sworn to nothing more than delivery. The letters would arrive in the capital scattered across a month, from different directions, in the same anonymous clerk-hand. Untraceable. Unprovable. Indisputable, to the only people who mattered.

On the evening the last of them went out, Wren walked to the southern edge of the village alone and stood a long time in the blue dusk, watching the road.

Spring was winning now, mostly. The pines had come back green along the lower slopes, and somewhere below, the river ran loud with snowmelt. In a few weeks the high passes would open—the same passes that had sealed behind them last winter, that had swallowed the Door of Ashes and the vault and Quentin's father and everything Vaelmoor had been. By summer, travelers would cross Ashfell again and find nothing but rock. The map was ash. The immunity was fading out of Quentin's blood day by day—he'd described it to her once, with tears in his eyes, as the sweetest illness he'd ever had. Even the heat of the Door was gone from the stone. The world had closed over the secret like water over a dropped coin.

Except for twelve envelopes, moving south at the speed of horses.

She heard Quentin's footsteps before she saw him—he still walked like a kitchen man, soft-footed, apologetic—and he came to stand beside her, and for a while neither of them spoke. That was another thing she'd learned from him: silence could be shared without being filled.

"Do you regret it?" he asked eventually. "Sending them. There's no taking them back. If any letter falls into the wrong hands—"

"Then the wrong hands will read the truth and have nowhere to act on it," she said. "Corvane is marble. The vault is buried. Who's left to punish? Us?" She almost smiled. "We're ghosts, Quentin. Ghosts can afford to tell the truth."

"Not ghosts," he said. "People. We're people. It's the whole point."

She looked at him then—at this man who had once made his living being invisible, tasting other people's dinners and swallowing other people's poisons, and who now stood in the open air of a village he'd helped build, insisting on visibility as if it were a discipline he practiced daily. Maybe it was.

"The court will never acknowledge any of it," she said. "No pardon. No correction of the record. Corvane stays a hero in the histories. The Vaelmoori stay a myth. We stay traitors, if anyone ever thinks of us at all." She paused. "I used to think that would be unbearable. Serving a code no court ratified, enforcing a law no king signed. Now—" She turned her palm up, feeling the cold coming down off the fells, smelling pine and woodsmoke and someone's supper. "Now it feels like the first honest work I've ever done."

Quentin reached over and, without ceremony, tucked a strand of hair behind her ear that the wind had pulled loose. Neither of them remarked on it. They were getting very good, the two of them, at not remarking.

"Come inside," he said. "The bread's ready, and Tam's telling the children about the capital, and someone has to make sure he doesn't teach them to gamble."

She followed him. But at the door she stopped and looked back once, south, down the darkening road, and let herself imagine it—one envelope opening on a table in a narrow room, a woman's hands trembling as she read, the bitter-almond taste of truth arriving years too late and exactly on time.

*You were not wrong,* the letters said, in their plain anonymous hand. *It happened the way you feared, and worse, and now you know.*

Behind her, the village lamps were lighting one by one, and above the fells the first stars were out, and spring was coming on like a promise the world intended to keep. Wren Calloway, disgraced justice, elected justiciar of a country that existed on no map, went in to supper.
