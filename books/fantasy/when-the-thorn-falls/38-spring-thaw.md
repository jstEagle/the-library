## 38. Spring Thaw

The thaw came to the foothills the way forgiveness comes—slowly, then all at once, and never quite complete.

Quentin heard it first. For a man whose whole life had been built on listening to what things said beneath their words, spring was deafening. The snowmelt spoke in every gutter and gully, a thousand small voices running off the high fells, and under that ran the deeper sound of ice letting go of stone, the groan of the pass roads shedding their white silence. He stood in the doorway of Maren's old house—which had become his house by no decision anyone remembered making—and simply listened until his chest hurt.

Three winters he had known here now, or nearly three. The first had been grief. The second had been work. This one had been something so unfamiliar it took him most of the season to name it: ease.

Behind him, the hearth ticked as it cooled. On the table lay the morning's bread, proofed and scored, waiting for the oven's temper. A pot of barley soaked in whey. A knuckle of salt pork he'd been saving for a soup worth saving it for. These were the ledgers he kept now—not poisons catalogued in the blood, but flavors noted in the memory: how the wild garlic came up earlier on the south-facing slope; how the village's hard little apples made a cider that bit back; how honey from hives set near the thorn-fell bloomed faintly bitter at the finish, like a word left unsaid.

He had been a taster for nineteen years. He was learning, at forty-something and slow about it, to be a cook.

The distinction mattered more than anyone who hadn't lived both lives could guess. A taster ate alone, always, even in a crowded hall—alone because his death was the point of him, and death keeps its own table. A cook fed other people. A cook stood in the steam and the noise and the small quarrels of a kitchen and watched strangers become less strange over a shared dish. A cook's body was not a ledger but a ledger's opposite: a thing written on by everyone who sat down hungry.

Quentin Ashgrove had spent half his life being swallowed for other men's safety. He was spending what remained learning to feed them instead.

* * *

The messengers came up the low road eleven days after the pass opened.

He knew they were coming before anyone told him—the way you know a storm, or a spoiled broth, or the particular quality of attention a stranger carries when they've been sent. Tam brought the word, actually, breathless from the switchbacks, still wearing the capital's mud on his boots. Tam went south and came back more often than anyone; he'd made himself the village's courier, its one trusted thread to the world that had tried to erase it. He carried letters both ways, news both ways, and he had learned, in his redemption the hard way, exactly which news to bring fast.

"Riders," he said, leaning in the door with his cap in his hands. "Four of them. Livery under the travel cloaks—I saw the colors when they watered at the ford." He hesitated. "Court colors, Quentin."

The name still landed strangely. In Vaelmoori he was *ash-kehl*, the returned taster; to the children he was Master Quentin, who taught them to read maps and make bread; to Wren he was—well. What Wren called him varied by the hour, and she'd have skinned him for repeating any of it. But *Ashgrove* was the name the crown had given an orphan boy it decided to use, and hearing it approach up the valley in four pairs of riding boots put an old taste in his mouth. Bitter almond. Always that note, underneath everything from those years.

"They're asking for you by name?"

"Asking politely. Which is worse."

It was worse. Quentin had survived Corvane's interrogations and Corvane's hunters and Corvane himself, in the end, in a vault of impossible weapons where the spymaster had died begging—and polite had been Corvane's native tongue. Politeness was what power wore when it wanted something.

But the winter had taught him things too. He wiped his hands on his apron, looked at the bread waiting on its cloth, and made a decision that would have been unthinkable once: the loaves could wait. The oven could wait. Whatever the court wanted from him could wait, because Quentin Ashgrove's time belonged to Quentin Ashgrove now, and to the people who'd chosen to share a valley with him.

"I'll meet them at the moot-ground," he said. "Alone."

"You sure? I could get Wren—"

"Wren's holding assizes at Thornbrook ford." He smiled despite himself. "And if you tell her the crown sent riders for me, she'll come back up that road at a pace that scares the horses. Let her finish her law. This is mine to settle."

Tam gave him a look that said he thought this was idiocy, said it eloquently, and obeyed anyway—a skill he'd developed under Wren's tutelage and one Quentin privately admired.

* * *

They waited for him by the standing stones, four riders sitting their horses with the careful stillness of men who'd been told exactly how to behave. Their leader dismounted when Quentin arrived—an honor, or a performance of one—and threw back his hood.

Quentin recognized the face. Not the man; the type. Younger than Corvane had been, smoother, with the same court-trained warmth that never reached the eyes. He wore his courtesy like livery.

"Master Ashgrove." A bow, precise to the degree. "I am Ser Aldous Renwick, envoy to His Majesty's household. I'm told—" and here his gaze traveled, taking inventory of the valley with a professional's eye: the terraced fields, the smoke of chimneys that appeared on no chart, the children watching from behind the woodpile with the frank stares of kids raised free—"I'm told you might be found here."

"You were told correctly." Quentin kept his voice level, his hands visible and easy. Old habits. "You've ridden a long way for a dead man."

"Not dead, Master Ashgrove. *Missing.* There is a difference, in law." Renwick produced a document case, wax-sealed with the royal signet, and held it out. "A full pardon, signed under the privy seal. Restoration of your office. Your old quarters in the palace, your stipend trebled, and a place at the king's own table." He let the pause do its work. "His Majesty remembers your service. He remembers that in twenty years, no poison ever reached him past your tongue."

*Twenty years, no poison reached him past my tongue.* As if the ledger of what Quentin had swallowed to make that true counted for nothing. Arsenic and nightshade, cantharides and lead-water, the slow metals and the quick ones—each dose a debt his body had paid so some crowned stranger could eat unafraid. And now the crown wished to resume payments, with interest.

Something in Quentin's chest that had been clenched for decades finally, quietly, let go.

"No," he said.

Renwick blinked. Whatever script he'd memorized, the refusal wasn't in it. "Master Ashgrove—"

"It's just Quentin now. Or the taster, if you like, though I don't taste anymore. Can't, in fact." He turned his hand over, almost showing it, the way you'd show an old scar. "Whatever was done to me as a child—it ended, in the vault, with everything else Vaelmoor kept. I can be poisoned like any man now. Like any man, I assure you, is plenty poisoned already." He nodded at the sealed case. "Keep your pardon. I committed no crime worth pardoning, and accepting one would say I did."

"The king's offer is generous—"

"The king's offer is an apology shaped like a bribe." Quentin surprised himself with the steadiness of it. "Your master lost a spymaster in these mountains and gained nothing, and the story you'll tell at court will be that Corvane died bravely on assignment, because stories are the only arsenal a crown can admit to owning. Fine. Let the story stand. But don't dress it as a gift to me."

For a moment Renwick's mask slipped, and Quentin saw beneath it—not malice, merely bewilderment. The envoy genuinely could not compute a man refusing restoration. In Renwick's world, rank was gravity; nobody stood outside its pull.

"What do you want, then?" the envoy asked, and it was almost honest. "Every man wants something."

Quentin looked past him—at Tam, hovering by the well, pretending not to listen; at the children by the woodpile, one of whom, little Sefa, waved when she saw she'd been caught watching; at the smoke rising from ovens warming for the day's baking, from houses that had no business existing on any map and flourished regardless.

"A recipe," he said.

"...What?"

"I want a recipe. Well—several." He began counting on his fingers, enjoying Renwick's expression enormously. "There's a barley soup from the southern marches that uses burnt onion for depth, and I've never gotten the char right. There's a way of preserving cherries in wine and honey that the old palace kitchens knew, and the last cook who made it is either dead or retired, and I'd very much like the method before it dies entirely. And there's a bread—rye, long ferment, baked on a stone—that they serve in the low city, and if your clerks can find me the formula, I will consider us square."

Renwick stared at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, the smoothness cracked into something humaner: disbelief shading toward reluctant amusement. "You're serious."

"I have never been more serious in my life. Poison I understood. Bread is harder."

The envoy looked at his three silent companions, at the impossible valley, at the impossible man in the flour-dusted apron. Then he laughed—one short bark, quickly suppressed, the laugh of a man realizing he'd walked into a play with no idea of his part. He tucked the pardon case away.

"I'll see what the archives yield," he said. "Though I warn you, the cherry recipe may cost you."

"Everything worth eating costs something. That's how you know it's worth eating."

Renwick mounted, paused, and looked down. "For what it's worth, Master Ashgrove—the court thinks you mad. Living in the fells, baking bread, sending back a king's favor."

"Good," Quentin said. "Madmen are left alone. It's the useful ones they keep coming for."

* * *

That evening, Wren came home.

She always announced herself badly—boots on the porch like a summons, door thrown wide like an arrest—but Quentin had learned to read tenderness underneath the noise of her. Tonight she stamped the snow-melt off her boots, hung her coat, accepted the bowl of soup he pressed into her hands without a word of thanks because thanks between them had long ago been replaced by the passing of salt, and only then said:

"Tam says the crown sent men."

"Sent and gone." He sat across from her. The fire ticked. Outside, meltwater ran in the dark like a conversation the mountain was having with itself. "A pardon. My old office. Trebled stipend. All of it."

"And you said—"

"I asked them for recipes."

Wren Calloway, who had faced down smugglers and hunters and the worst winter in living memory without flinching, put down her spoon and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes.

Then she grew quiet, in the particular way she did when something needed saying and she was going to say it whether it was comfortable or not. That was the thing about her: she never let a hard truth sit unpunished overnight.

"They'll ask again," she said. "Next year, or the year after. Crowns are patient with things they think they own. You were theirs a long time, Quentin."

"I know." He stirred the fire. "But here's what I learned in that vault, kneeling next to a man I hated while he died. Ownership ends where choice begins. I chose this." He gestured—at the kitchen, the valley, the dark window beyond which the hidden village slept, at her, sitting across his table with her boots off and her guard down, which was rarer and more precious than any royal favor. "I choose it again tomorrow, and the day after. They can send riders until the mountains wear flat. The answer's already written."

Wren studied him across the firelight, and whatever she saw made her nod once, the way she nodded when a verdict satisfied her code.

"Good," she said. "Because I've been asked to swear the full oath in the spring council. Justiciar of the roads, sworn and elected. And I didn't want to swear it wondering whether the man across my table was planning to be a king's servant again."

"The man across your table," Quentin said, "is planning tomorrow's baking. Rye. Long ferment. You'll hate it—you hate everything with character."

"I hate everything that argues back," Wren agreed, and took up her spoon again, and the fire settled, and the mountain went on talking to itself in meltwater, and neither of them said anything else, because nothing else needed saying.

Later, after she'd gone up to her loft and her breathing had slowed into sleep, Quentin sat alone with the banked coals and did the thing he did on such nights: he took out the book with no title on its cover, the one in which he pressed flowers and copied recipes and, occasionally, wrote down truths too large for speech.

On a fresh page, beside a sprig of the first coltsfoot of spring, he wrote:

*They offered me everything I used to be. I sent them back a shopping list.*

He read it twice, smiled, and closed the book.

Tomorrow there would be rye to knead and children to teach and a woman upstairs whose snoring, he had decided years ago, was the single most comforting sound in the world. Tomorrow the thaw would run a little further down the slopes, and the thorn-fell would climb another week closer to its white blooming.

And somewhere south, in an archive smelling of dust and ink, a clerk would find a dead cook's cherry recipe, and a king's envoy would shake his head over it, and the world's great business would go on without Quentin Ashgrove—which was, he reflected as the coals dimmed, precisely the point.

He banked the fire, climbed the stairs past Wren's loft with the care of a man passing a sleeping bear, and slept the whole night through without tasting anything bitter at all.
