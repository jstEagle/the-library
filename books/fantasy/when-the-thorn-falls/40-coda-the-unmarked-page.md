## 40. Coda: The Unmarked Page

The schoolhouse smelled of chalk dust, tallow, and the particular sweetness of children who had run through snow to get somewhere warm. Quentin Ashgrove stood at the great slate board he had asked the village carpenters to build twice the size of any ordinary board, because maps wanted room, and drew the last stroke of a coastline he had never seen with his own eyes but knew the way a man knows the shape of his own hand.

"There," he said, and stepped aside so the light from the windows fell across it. "The Kingdom of Thornfell. Capital at Carreth. The king's road runs south from the citadel to the river, and the river runs down to the sea, and everything the sea touches, the crown calls its own."

Twenty-three children sat on benches built for smaller bottoms than theirs, because the village was young and its furniture was borrowed from three different generations of other villages. In the front row, a girl named Sera—Maren's granddaughter's daughter, all elbows and impatience—raised her hand before he had finished speaking, as she always did.

"Master Ashgrove. Where's Vaelmoor?"

Quentin smiled, because she asked it every year, every class, the way children ask the question adults have agreed not to ask. He picked up the chalk again and turned to the board, to the space east of the mountains he had just drawn, the space where the map of the world simply stopped, the way a road stops at a cliff edge, the way a sentence stops when the speaker dies.

"Where is it?" he said. "Show me what your grandmothers told you."

Sera stood, came to the board, and pointed her whole arm at the blankness. "There. But there's nothing there."

"There's nothing *drawn* there," Quentin said. "That's not the same thing. Come sit down, and I'll tell you the difference, and then I'll tell you why it matters more than anything else on this board."

They settled. Outside, the wind came down off the fells carrying the smell of the white-flowering thorn that gave the mountains their name, and the light through the windows went soft and silver the way it did every afternoon in the season the village called the blooming, the few weeks when the passes were open and the world remembered they existed.

Quentin set down the chalk and folded his hands, and his joints ached the way they had ached for twenty years now, honestly, like any man's joints, like any ordinary body's. He had come to love that ache. It was the ache of a man who could be hurt, who could be worn down, who could be poisoned like anyone else if someone cared to try—which no one did, because no one had reason to, because the small life he had built was a life no one needed to kill him out of.

"Once," he said, "there was a country where the blank space is. It had rivers and harvests and songs, and it had alchemists who learned things about the human body that no one should learn, and because of what they learned, the great powers around it grew afraid, and the great powers did what the great powers always do when they are afraid."

"They erased it," said a boy in the back—Tam's nephew, though the boy did not carry the name, the family having retired it with honor, the way you retire a sword that has been broken in good service.

"They erased it," Quentin agreed. "Not with fire. Fire leaves ruins, and ruins leave questions. They erased it with paper. Treaties. Signatures. A line of ink where a line of mountains should be, and an agreement among kings that the ink was stronger than the stone. And it worked. For a hundred years it worked. Cartographers drew the blank. Chancellors taught the blank. Children in the capital learned that east of Thornfell there was nothing but rock and weather, and their children learned it, and it became true in every way that matters to the powerful."

He paused. Sera's hand was up again. He nodded at her.

"But it wasn't true," she said.

"It wasn't true," Quentin said. "There were people. There were always people. And the people remembered, and they passed the memory down in lullabies and kitchen-songs and the names they gave their daughters, and they waited a hundred years for someone to walk out of the blank space and say *we were here*."

"Was that you?"

Quentin laughed. It still surprised him, the laugh, how easily it came now, how it had once been a thing he rationed. "No. I was a food-taster. I swallowed poison for a living and kept my head down and told myself that was enough. The person who walked out of the blank space was a courier, and he was dying when he arrived, and he put a map in my hand because his hands had no one else's to put it in." He looked at the children, at their upturned faces, at the particular seriousness children bring to stories they know are not entirely stories. "And I want you to understand something, because you are the first generation being taught the truth on purpose, in a room, with a slate board, and that has never happened before in the history of this valley. Are you listening?"

They were listening. He had them the way a good broth has salt—completely.

"The map had a blank space on it too," Quentin said. "Right here." He tapped the board. "And I spent a winter learning that a blank space is never empty. A blank space is a room with the door shut. Sometimes it's a grave. Sometimes it's a vault. Sometimes—" and here his voice did the thing it always did, the small catch he had stopped fighting decades ago, "—sometimes it's a country, waiting."

* * *

He dismissed them at the bell that Tam's son rang from the meeting-house roof, and they poured out into the silver light like water finding its level, and Quentin stood a while at the board, looking at what he had drawn and what he had left undrawn.

Wren found him there. She still moved like a woman half her age, which was a lie the mountains had taught her body and her body had agreed to tell, and she leaned in the doorway with her arms crossed and her gray-streaked hair tied back with the same kind of plain cord she had used on the roads thirty years ago, because some things, she said, were not sentiment but uniform.

"They'll ask you again next year," she said.

"They ask every year. It's the right question. It's the only question worth building a school around, if you think about it." He wiped the board clean with long strokes, and the kingdom of Thornfell dissolved into gray haze, and he did not redraw it. "Corvane's people never asked it. That was his whole genius, you know. Not cruelty—cruelty is common. He simply never asked what was in the blank. He assumed the blank was there for him, the way a man assumes a locked door is there for him to open."

"And you assumed it was there to stay shut."

"I assumed nothing. That was my whole genius." He set the chalk in its tray. "I kept my head down for thirty years, Wren. Assumption is a luxury for people who've been seen."

She came to him and stood beside him, and they looked together at the empty board, which was, he thought, the truest map either of them had ever drawn.

"Maren's granddaughter wants to put up a stone at the pass," Wren said. "A marker. *Here stood the Door of Ashes.* She's collecting names for it. She asked if we'd sign."

Quentin was quiet a moment. Outside, a child shouted something about a goat, and another child shouted back, and the ordinary music of a living village played on.

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her I'd sign when you signed. I told her you'd have a reason, and I wanted to hear it, because I've been married to your reasons for thirty years and they're usually better than mine."

He smiled and looked out the window at the fells, white above the tree line, the thorn blooming along the lower slopes like snow that had decided to live. Somewhere up there was a scree field and a cliff face and a seam in the rock that no longer hummed, no longer warmed, no longer was anything but stone. He had made sure of it with his own blood, and his father had made sure of it with the last of his unnatural life, and the vault beyond the Door had gone into the mountain like a seed going into winter ground—buried, finished, never to sprout.

"No stone," Quentin said.

Wren nodded slowly. "That's what I told her you'd say."

"A stone is a question you leave standing in the landscape. *What was here? Why does this matter? Who died?* And those are good questions for graves. But the Door wasn't a grave. It was a promise. The whole point—" he tapped the blank board, gently, "—the whole point of what we did was that the questions would finally get to rest. You don't put a marker on a resting thing. You let it sleep, and you teach the children why it's sleeping, and you trust them to keep it asleep."

"Some of them will dig," Wren said. "There's always one."

"There's always one. And when that one is old enough, I'll tell that one the whole story, every terrible beautiful piece of it, and I'll watch their face while I tell it, and I'll know by supper whether they're a digger or a keeper." He turned from the board. "That's the only monument I believe in. A face, listening."

* * *

That evening he cooked.

He cooked the way he had cooked for thirty years, since the night the village had gathered in the meeting-house and someone—Maren, it had been, old Maren with her failing eyes and her iron voice—had said, *the returned taster should make the feast*, and he had stood in a kitchen that was not the king's kitchen, with ingredients no steward had counted and no spymaster had sampled, and he had understood that he was free.

He cooked simply, because the village ate simply and because he had learned in the high passes that simplicity was not poverty but discipline. Barley and lamb from the high pasture. Roots roasted in their skins. Bread that Wren had learned to make from him and now made better than he did, which he mentioned often and resented never. And a small dish at the center of the table that he made only in the blooming season, when the thorn-flowers came down the mountain on the wind: a honey cake, dense and dark, flavored with the dried white blossoms, the recipe his own invention and his alone, the one recipe he had ever sent south instead of an insignia, all those years ago, when the king's messengers had come with pardons and he had written back with flour and sugar and a flower no cartographer had ever mapped.

The children called it taster's cake. They did not know all the reasons why. They would, someday, some of them.

He tasted everything before it went to the table. He did it out of habit, and then—he caught himself doing it, as he caught himself every evening, and smiled at himself, as he smiled at himself every evening—he did it out of love. The first taste of the stew, held on his tongue, catalogued by a body that had once been a ledger of poisons and was now only a body, an old man's body, honest and vulnerable and his: barley, lamb, thyme, the iron tang of the valley water, nothing else, nothing hidden, nothing waiting. Safe. He had wept the first time he had tasted food and found it merely safe. He suspected he would weep at the last taste too, and he had made peace with that.

Wren came in with an armload of split wood and found him standing over the pot with a wooden spoon and an expression she had learned to read in the dark, in a frozen waterfall chute, at the mouth of a door in a mountain.

"Safe?" she said.

"Safe," he said.

"Then feed them. Sera's been at the window for ten minutes pretending she isn't starving."

* * *

Later, when the house was dark and the wind had settled into its nighttime murmur among the fells, Quentin sat by the banked fire with a book in his lap.

The book had no title on its spine. It had no spine to speak of anymore, really—thirty years of hands had worn it down to a soft brown block of leather and paper, swollen with pressed things, its stitching repaired twice by Wren, its cover scarred by a burn along one edge that Quentin had never explained and Wren had never asked about, because they had both been there the night of the burn and some nights belong to the two people who survived them.

He opened it to the place he always opened it. The pages there were stiff and pale, and between them, flat as a blessing, lay a single white flower.

It was a thorn-bloom. Not the cultivated kind that grew in the village gardens now, fat and fragrant from thirty years of good soil, but a wild one, small and hard and five-petaled, picked on the day the snows broke the first spring after the sealing, picked by a man who had walked down out of the killing cold with nothing left of his engineered body but an ordinary heart, and had seen the lower slopes of Ashfell gone white with blossom, and had understood—standing there, forty years old and finally mortal, with Wren's hand in his—that the mountain was not mourning. The mountain was forgiving. Or if not forgiving, then at least continuing, which in the end was the same thing and better.

He had pressed it that night between the pages of a book he had bought blank in the foothills, a book with no title because he had not yet known what the story was, and he had never written the title in. He had filled the book instead with other things: a scrap of Maren's handwriting, a Vaelmoori lullaby copied out by a child's careful hand; the stub of the wax seal from the courier's tube, broken and dark, the first object of the whole story; a pressed sprig of the gray herb that grew only on the Door's threshold, or had grown there, before the Door was stone again; a lock of hair that needed no explaining between him and Wren; and on the last written page, in his own younger hand, the words he had said to the schoolchildren that afternoon, the words he had been saying for thirty years, the words that were the closest thing the book had to a title:

*Some countries end so their people can begin.*

He looked at the flower a long time. His eyes were not what they had been. The flower was not what it had been either—faded to the color of old bone, its petals thin as the skin of a memory—and he found that he loved it more for the fading, the way he loved his own hands more for their spotted backs, the way he loved Wren more for every gray hair she refused to mourn.

"You're doing it again," Wren said from the bed, without opening her eyes.

"Doing what?"

"Sitting up with the book like it's going to answer you."

"It did answer me. Thirty years ago. That's the trouble with answers—they keep being true after you've gotten used to them." He closed the book gently, the flower sleeping between its pages, and set it on the shelf where it had always lived, spine out, blank, unremarkable to any eye but two. "Sera asked about Vaelmoor today."

"Mm."

"And I told her about the blank space. And I thought, as I was telling it—she's the one. The digger or the keeper. I think she's the one I'll tell everything."

Wren opened her eyes then, and looked at him across the dark room, and her face in the firelight was the face from the ice chute and the vault and the founding festival, older, all of it older, and none of it less.

"Will that be safe?" she said. "Telling her everything?"

Quentin considered it honestly, the way he had considered every taste of every dish of his life—carefully, completely, with respect for what might be hidden in it.

"No," he said at last. "It won't be safe. Truths that matter never are. But it will be *hers*, and that's better than safe. That's the whole point of ending a country, Wren—so that what it was stops being a secret and starts being a story. Secrets need guarding. Stories only need telling." He banked the fire and lay down beside her, and her hand found his in the dark the way it always did, the way it had not let go since a frozen waterfall half a lifetime ago. "And she'll tell it to her children, and they'll tell it to theirs, and one day some cartographer will ask a Vaelmoori grandmother what lies east of Thornfell, and the grandmother will say, *people*—and that will be the whole map, and it will be enough."

The wind moved in the fells. The white thorn bloomed in the dark, thousands of blossoms opening on the mountainside where a door had been, and in the village below, in a house that smelled of bread and woodsmoke, an old taster and an old justiciar slept the unguarded sleep of people whose country was finally, at last, only a story—

—and on the shelf, in a book with no title, a flower kept the memory, and the memory kept the peace, and the peace, like spring, came back every year.
