## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison in the broth tasted of almonds and burnt sugar, which meant arsenic cut with something floral — foxglove, maybe, or oleander if the cook had been feeling ambitious. Beatrix Marchetti held the spoon on her tongue for the full count of ten, because ten seconds was the difference between a taster who lived and a taster who guessed, and then she swallowed.

Bitter at the root. Sweet on the finish, like a lie told kindly.

"Foxglove," she said to the kitchen, her voice steady as a scale. "Two parts in a gallon, steeped too long. Whoever brewed it wanted Her Majesty drowsy before her heart stopped. Sloppy."

Head cook Maren Ostrow crossed herself with flour-white fingers and began, immediately, to cry. She was the third cook this season to weep over a poisoned dish she swore she hadn't poisoned, and Beatrix had stopped believing any of them around the second year. Kitchens were rivers; everything flowed through them, and anyone could drop anything into the current.

"Sit down, Maren." Beatrix set the bowl aside with the care one gives a sleeping animal. "You'll make a fresh pot. I'll taste that one too. Nobody dies tonight."

*Except me, slowly, by appointment.*

She kept that part behind her teeth, where she kept most things.

The palace kitchens of Vhalenne ran hot even in autumn, great iron cauldrons breathing steam, forty bodies moving in the choreography of feeding three hundred. Beatrix moved through it like weather — acknowledged by nods, never greeted. Six years in the queen's service had taught her that a taster was a tool, and tools weren't embraced. They were checked for rust.

She took her station at the side table while Maren rebuilt the broth from scratch: bone stock, leeks, saffron — the queen's comfort dish, requested on nights when the court's knives came out. Tonight was such a night. The envoy from Kestreach arrived within the hour, and Queen Ilseva wanted to appear calm, generous, unafraid. Which meant someone, somewhere, wanted her actually dead, because fear was the only honest thing left in this palace and there were men paid well to cure it.

Beatrix pulled the little tin from her apron pocket. Inside, six vials in a felt roll, each labeled in her own cramped hand: *nightshade, diluted. hemlock, third distillation. aconite — do not exceed four drops.* Her morning ritual, performed every day for six years, so regular now that her body expected its poisons the way other women expected breakfast. A sip of death before dawn, another at midday, a final one at dusk, each dose a hair stronger than the last, climbing the ladder of lethality rung by rung until her blood had become a locked door with no key anyone alive possessed.

It wasn't courage. She wanted that understood, even if only by herself. It was arithmetic. Every dose bought her another day of being useful, and usefulness was the only currency a bastard girl from the salt docks could spend in a royal house.

Maren brought the new broth. Beatrix tasted it clean — just leeks and saffron and the long patience of good stock — and carried it up the servants' stair herself, because tonight she didn't trust the food runners. Tonight she didn't trust the walls.

* * *

Queen Ilseva received her in the antechamber off the private apartments, where the light came amber through honey-colored glass and made even the queen look young. She wasn't young. She was fifty-one and tired in the way mountains are tired, holding herself up out of pure geological habit.

"You've brought the broth yourself," the queen observed. Her eyes went past Beatrix to the covered bowl, then back. "Is there news in it?"

"Foxglove in the first pot, Majesty. Handled."

Ilseva closed her eyes briefly — not gratitude; accounting. One more attempt logged against the ledger of her reign. "Kestreach sends an envoy tonight and my soup arrives pre-poisoned. The timing is almost elegant."

"The envoy didn't brew it."

"No. Someone inside did." The queen said it without heat, the way one notes rain. "You will stand at my shoulder through the dinner. If the envoy's aide offers you wine — and he will, they always test the taster — you will drink it and smile."

"And if it's keyed?" Beatrix asked quietly. Keyed poisons were the new fashion out of the empire: compounds built to slip past common immunities, tailored like assassins' gloves to a specific hand.

"If it's keyed, you'll tell me tomorrow, won't you." It wasn't a question. It was also, Beatrix noted, not an apology.

She bowed. In the amber light her own shadow stretched long across the floor, thin as a blade.

* * *

There were two hours before the banquet, and Beatrix spent them the way she spent all her stolen hours: writing letters that didn't exist.

Not literally. Nothing written, nothing carried, nothing found. But in the ledger of her mind she drafted the week's contraband — a widow in Millbrook whose son had been conscripted into Kest's levies needed word passed to him that his mother lived; a chandler on the Salt Road owed a message to his brother in the free cities; and there was the usual trickle of small treasons, names and safe-houses and dates, that flowed along the smuggler roads of the Thornfells because Beatrix Marchetti fed them there, drop by drop, the way she dosed herself. Six years of favors banked in mountain villages, in the pockets of mule-drivers and hedge-witches, interest compounding quietly in the dark.

The Thornfells. Even thinking the name loosened something in her chest. Three passes the crown's maps didn't show. Three roads only she knew, learned from a father who'd died running them and a mother who'd never once asked her daughter what she carried or why. Out there, people were poor and honest about their crimes. In here, everyone was rich and dishonest about their souls.

A knock at her door — the servant's knock, two soft raps — and a page stood there holding a folded note sealed with plain wax. No crest. That alone made her pulse tick upward.

*For the Lady Taster, from the map-room.*

She broke the seal. Elio Vane's handwriting, all ink and intent:

*The survey is finished. The crown gets its map Thursday. Come see the margins before I surrender them. — E.V.*

Beatrix read it twice, and despite everything — the foxglove still ghosting her tongue, the banquet waiting, the aide who would test her with wine — she smiled. It happened every time, that involuntary treachery of the face. Six years of training her body to hold its ground against poison, and one cartographer could undo her with a sentence.

The margins. Of course. He drew the Thornfells for the crown with meticulous, obedient precision — every ridge, every river, every fort the usurper's engineers would want catalogued. And in the margins, in a cipher disguised as decorative annotation, he wrote to her. Wind-speeds that were really meeting times. Compass roses whose needles pointed not north but toward the passes he'd left off the official map. Three blank spaces on the royal chart, deliberate as held breath, that only she could fill.

He was committing treason with a ruler and compass, and he did it beautifully.

She burned the note in her candle flame and watched the ash curl. Then she looked at herself in the small mirror above the washstand: twenty-six years old, gray-eyed, unremarkable by design, dressed in the plain livery of a servant whose whole art was to be forgotten between courses. The mirror showed her what the court saw. What the court didn't see was the fortress — the blood that had eaten six years of poison and grown walls instead of scars.

What the court didn't see, increasingly, was how tired she was.

That was the thing no one warned you about immunity. Not the doses. Doses were easy; they were discipline, and discipline suited her. It was the solitude. You cannot share a meal with anyone. You cannot drink what friends drink, eat what lovers eat, accept so much as a honeyed fig from a friendly hand without performing the full ritual — the tasting, the counting, the wait. People thought poisoning was the lonely trade. Poisoning was nothing. Poisoning was chemistry. The loneliness was the *living* — the way her body had become a country with closed borders, and every kindness offered across it had to be inspected, weighed, refused.

Everyone in the palace called her fearless. *The Taster's Tongue*, they said, half legend already: the woman who couldn't be killed by cup or plate. They said it with respect, the way you'd praise a well-made lock.

Nobody ever asked whether the lock got tired of being locked.

* * *

The banquet hall blazed with two hundred candles and one lie: that Vhalenne was still a power and not a vassal-in-waiting. Beatrix stood at the queen's shoulder in her formal grays, tasting spoon in hand, invisible and essential, as Aurel Kest's envoy processed down the length of the hall — Lord Cassian Merle, silk over steel, bowing at precisely the depth that said *we honor you and we own you.*

Behind him came the aide.

Beatrix marked him the way she marked everything: automatically, professionally. Younger than his lord. Plain clothes, excellent boots. He walked like a man who'd been taught to walk like a man who wasn't watching anything, which was itself a kind of watchfulness she recognized, because she performed it daily.

He watched her.

Not obviously — never obviously — but Beatrix had spent six years learning to feel eyes the way sailors felt weather. When the first course came up she tasted it before the queen, counted her ten seconds, nodded. And the aide's gaze rested on her mouth through the whole performance, patient and faintly amused, like a man reading a document he'd already memorized.

Wine, as promised. He sent it over himself during the fish course — a silver flagon, a single glass, delivered by a footman with a message: *With the envoy's compliments to Her Majesty's famous taster.*

The hall's noise dimmed around her. Two hundred faces, and she felt every calculation in the room pivot on the glass in her hand.

She drank it. All of it, because refusal was confession. Almond again, faint, under a good southern red. Arsenic base — but threaded with something else, something she didn't have a name for, a bitterness shaped wrong, like a key cut nearly right.

Her heart did not speed up. Six years of training. But her mind laid the sensation on the table and turned it over twice, and filed it under a heading she didn't like: *someone is studying me.*

Across the hall, the aide raised his own glass a fraction — salute or experiment — and smiled.

Beside her, Queen Ilseva murmured, without turning her head: "Well?"

"A vintage worth drinking, Majesty," Beatrix said. "I may need to test my tolerance further tonight."

The queen's fan paused, one beat, then resumed. She understood. She always understood, which was somehow worse than if she hadn't.

And then trumpets, and the chamberlain's voice lifting over the candles: *"His Majesty's court presents Elio Vane, Royal Cartographer, whose surveys of the Thornfells shall guide the armies of the realm—"*

Beatrix went very still.

Elio crossed the floor in his best coat, ink-stained cuffs hastily scrubbed, bowing to the envoy, to the throne, to the glittering apparatus of a court that had no idea the man it was honoring had just been handed the instruments of its undoing. His eyes swept the high table once, professional, empty —

— and caught hers for exactly half a heartbeat. Long enough to say *I know. Long enough to say* tonight.

Then he was kneeling, and the chancellor was pinning the cartographer's chain at his throat, and the hall was applauding a man it intended to use, and Beatrix stood at the queen's shoulder smiling her competent, forgettable smile while somewhere beneath her ribs, quiet as wet ink on old paper, dread began to write its date.

She didn't know yet why the evening mattered. She wouldn't know until a bookseller pressed a century-old codex into her hands and she read her lover's name in ink that hadn't dried.

But the tongue, which had tasted ten thousand poisons and been fooled by none, tasted something in the air of that golden hall that it could not name — and never lied to her.

Something had begun.
