## 3. A Date Written in Ash

The rain came down on Vhalenne like a verdict, and Beatrix Marchetti walked through it without hurrying, because hurrying was a thing innocent women did.

She had three hours before the evening tasting. Three hours in which she was not the queen's taster, not the palace's quiet instrument, not the woman whose tongue had been ruined for pleasure and remade into a weapon. For three hours she was only a buyer of books — or so the story went, and stories were the only currency she'd ever been allowed to keep.

The shop was where it always was: down a stair that smelled of wet stone and old glue, behind a door with no sign, past a curtain of beads that clicked like teeth. Maddox kept his lamp turned low. He always did. A man who sold forbidden things learned to love the dark.

"You're late," he said, not looking up from the ledger he was defacing.

"Your street is watched."

"My street is *always* watched. That's what I pay for." He set down his pen at last. He was older than the war, thinner than the famine that followed it, and his eyes had the flat shine of a man who had seen every kind of customer and been surprised by none of them. "You want the codex."

It wasn't a question, which meant someone had already told him what she wanted, which meant her request had passed through more hands than she liked. But that was the price of wanting anything rare in Vhalenne these days. Everything touched too many palms on its way to you, like a coin worn smooth by strangers.

"I want to see it first," she said.

"Of course you do." He drew a key from around his neck — one key among forty, each on its own thread, and she had never once figured out his system — and unlocked a cabinet behind the counter. From it he lifted a book wrapped in oilcloth, and even before the cloth came away she knew it was old. Not old the way palace books were old, all gilt and ceremony. Old the way bones are old.

The binding was grey leather gone soft as skin. The pages inside were ragged-edged, hand-pressed, the kind of paper they hadn't made since before Aurel Kest burned the free cities' presses and called it peace. Along the spine, someone had stitched a strip of vellum bearing a single word in faded ink:

*Thornsong.*

Her breath did something small and stupid in her chest. She kept her face still. Six years of tasting had taught her that much: nothing shows on your face if you've practiced dying there often enough.

"A century old," Maddox said. "Give or take a decade. It surfaced in the Thornfells — a shepherd found it in a cave after the spring floods, if you believe shepherds, which I don't. Passed through four hands before it reached me. Two of those hands are dead now." He said this the way other men mentioned the weather. "The buyer who commissioned me to find a purchaser wants no name attached."

"And you?"

"I attach no names to anything. That's my whole religion." He slid the book across the counter between them. "Look. Touch. Don't lick your fingers, I know how tasters are."

She opened it.

The first pages were prophecy, or something shaped like it — verses in an old dialect of the free cities, the letters cramped and certain, the margins crowded with annotations in at least three different hands. She read slowly, translating in her head the way Elio had taught her over one long winter of coded letters, when they'd used dead languages as their cipher because no inquisitor alive bothered to learn them.

*When the crown is stolen and the thief sits warm,* the first verse began,

*and the mountains forget the names they bore,
a book will wake beneath the thorn,
and choose a bearer for the door.*

Prophecy. Everyone's favorite lie. The free cities had drowned in prophets during the war; half the smugglers she ran letters for carried some scrap of verse in a locket and swore it would bring Kest down. Prophets were cheap. Hope was cheaper. She turned the page with the professional detachment she brought to suspect dishes.

Illustrations followed — ink drawings, fine-lined, obsessive. A mountain cut open like a dissected heart. A door of green stone set in living rock, carved with vines that looked less decorative than *alive*, tendrils spilling off the page's border as if the artist couldn't contain them. A figure standing before the door, faceless, hands raised.

And then, near the end, a page that stopped her cold.

It was dated.

Not in the vague way prophecies dated themselves — *in the last days, in the reign of thieves*. Dated precisely, in numerals, in the calendar the usurper had imposed on all his conquered territories. And beside the date, in the same cramped hand as the verses, two words:

*The bearer.*

Below that, a line of text. She read it once. Read it again. Felt the kitchen-floor sensation of the world tilting a degree she hadn't authorized.

*Tonight,* the page said. Tonight's date. Today's. The day she had woken in her narrow bed above the kitchens, dosed herself with foxglove tincture, tasted arsenic in the queen's morning broth, and complained about the rain.

Beside tonight's date, in ink a century dry, was a name:

*Elio Vane, bearer.*

The shop went very quiet. Rain against the shutter. The click of beads as a draft moved through the room. Maddox watching her the way he watched everything — flatly, completely, missing nothing.

"That name," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "That's specific."

"Prophecies get specific," Maddox said. "That's how forgeries work. You write the prediction after the fact and backdate it. Oldest trick in the trade. Half the codices in the capital's vaults were 'discovered' within living memory of their own miracles."

"So it's a forgery."

"It might be." He shrugged, a slow movement like something settling. "I've had it examined. The leather's right. The paper's right. The ink oxidation is consistent with age — that's the part forgers always botch, they can fake the writing but not the years in the ink. Every test says a century old. Every test but one." He leaned forward, lamplight catching the hollows of his face. "The name. Elio Vane. There's a cartographer in the royal court by that name. Young. Talented. Recently promoted, I'm told. If the book's genuine, it named him before his grandfather was born. If the book's forged, someone went to extraordinary expense to point a hundred-year-old weapon at a mapmaker." He paused. "Which do you find more likely?"

Neither, she wanted to say. Both. She found it most likely that the world had finally noticed her and chosen the cruelest possible way to say so.

"I find it likely someone wants the crown nervous," she said. "A prophecy naming a royal cartographer? Show it to the wrong official and Vane hangs for treason by lunchtime. That's not prophecy. That's assassination with better penmanship."

"Mm." Maddox didn't sound convinced. He didn't sound anything; sounding things was for amateurs. "Then you won't be buying it."

"I didn't say that."

"No. You didn't." He wrapped the book back in its oilcloth with the tenderness of a man handling something that had already bitten him twice. "Price is forty crowns. Non-negotiable, and before you object — you're not paying for paper. You're paying for the four dead and disappeared people between that shepherd and me. Rare books have body counts, taster. This one's just getting started."

She paid him. Forty crowns was two months of her salary and all of her smuggling margin for the season, and she paid it in silence, because the alternative was leaving the book in his cabinet, and she could not — she understood this suddenly, completely, the way you understand a locked door — she could not leave it there.

Some things you carry out of burning buildings whether or not they're yours.

At the top of the stairs she stopped under the awning, rain hammering down around her, the codex heavy and damp-warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat. She should go straight back to the palace. She should burn it in the kitchen ovens, where the fires ran hot enough to consume anything, even prophecy.

She should tell no one, especially not Elio, especially not Elio, especially not—

She opened the book again, right there in the rain, sheltered by nothing but her own stubbornness and the awning's thin lip. Turned to the final page. Checked the date again, as if the numbers might have rearranged themselves out of mercy.

They hadn't.

Tonight's date. Elio Vane, bearer.

"Forgeries," she said aloud, to the rain, to herself. "It's a forgery. Someone spent a fortune faking a hundred years of ink decay to frighten a taster who buys books she can't afford."

The theory held. The theory was sound. She repeated it all the way back through the wet streets, past the guard posts, through the servants' gate where the porter nodded at her without seeing her, up the back stairs to her narrow room where the foxglove waited in its brown bottle and the palace below hummed with its thousand small cruelties.

The theory held until she sat on her bed with the lamp lit and the door bolted, and opened the codex a final time to commit the thing to memory before destroying it.

And saw the ink.

Here is what six years among poisons teaches you. It teaches you colors. Arsenic leaves a particular dullness in the whites of the eyes. Foxglove stains the gums a certain grey-violet. Hemlock has a smell like mouse nests, sweetish and wrong. And ink — ink, she knew ink, because half the contraband she carried was letters, because she had spent six years learning to read documents by lamplight while checking them for dusts and coatings that could kill the reader — ink has a life cycle anyone in her trades learns young.

Fresh ink shines. It sits *on* the paper, wet-black, still movable, still capable of staining a careless thumb. Within hours it dulls. Within days it settles into the fiber. Within years it browns, cracks, fades, sinks so deep into the paper that the paper itself seems to have grown the letters, the way a tree grows a scar.

The date on the final page shone.

She told herself it was the lamp. She tilted the page. The shine tilted with it, obedient to light the way only moisture is obedient to light. She brought the page close, closer than reading distance, close enough that she could feel the faint coolness rising off it, and there — unmistakable, undeniable, the one thing her trained senses could not be lied to about — the smell.

Ink dries from the outside in. Fresh ink smells of itself: iron gall, gum arabic, a ghost of vinegar. A century-old page smells of nothing at all, or of leather, or of the cave it slept in. This page smelled freshly made. This page smelled like the moment after writing.

She sat very still for a long time.

Somewhere below her, the palace went about its evening. Carts in the yard. A cook shouting about eels. The ordinary machinery of a conquered city grinding toward another ordinary night, and in three hours she would stand behind Queen Ilseva's chair and taste her soup, and everything would be exactly as it had been yesterday and the day before, except that a book written a hundred years ago had a date on its last page that was still wet.

Wet ink means recent. Wet ink means someone wrote it recently — days ago, perhaps hours — and made it look a century old everywhere else. Which meant a forger of obscene skill had aimed this thing at Elio like a drawn bow.

Or.

Or the book was exactly as old as every test said, and the ink had stayed wet for a hundred years, waiting. Writing itself, or being written, in a time that ran differently than hers. Prophecies were supposed to arrive already finished. Nobody had warned her they might arrive *ongoing* — a sentence still being set down, a hand somewhere in the dark still moving, still spelling out tomorrow.

Both possibilities ended the same way: with Elio Vane's name on a page that powerful people would kill to control. Kest's inquisitors burned prophets and their congregations both; the queen's chancery wasn't gentler with inconvenient texts. A book like this didn't need to be true to be lethal. It only needed to exist, and to name him.

She thought about the illustration — the faceless figure before the green door, hands raised, vines crawling off the edge of the world. She thought about Elio's hands. She thought about how he drew maps the way monks illuminated psalms, with a devotion that looked, from certain angles, exactly like prayer.

"No," she said quietly, to the book, to the century, to whatever patient thing had written itself a page in her lifetime. "You don't get him. Whatever you are. Whatever game this is — you don't get to spend him."

The book said nothing. Books that say nothing are the most frightening kind, because they leave all the answers to you.

She wrapped it back in the oilcloth. She did not burn it. Burning it required believing it was only paper, and she no longer believed that, and a taster survives by never pretending about what's in the cup.

She hid it instead — inside the false bottom of her herb chest, beneath the monkshood and the foxglove, where no searcher would rummage casually and where any searcher who did would assume the worst thing present was the poison. Let them find the poisons. Poisons explained themselves.

Then she dressed plainly, took the servant stairs down, and slipped out through the laundry yard into the blue dark, heading for the quarter where the mapmakers lived, moving fast through streets that had begun, very subtly, to feel less like streets and more like the corridors of a trap she'd been walking through all her life without noticing the walls.

Elio answered his door with ink on his fingers and surprise on his face, and she loved him so violently in that moment that it frightened her more than the book had.

"Beatrix?" He glanced past her at the empty lane. "What's wrong?"

She stepped inside. Bolted the door behind her.

"We need to talk about your dreams," she said — because Maddox had mentioned, in his flat inventory of facts, that the codex had surfaced in the Thornfells, and because some instinct older than sense told her that a man whose name appeared in wet century-old ink had been dreaming of doors.

Elio went pale in a way she'd never seen.

"What dreams," he said.

And she knew, before he said another word, that the book had beaten her here.
