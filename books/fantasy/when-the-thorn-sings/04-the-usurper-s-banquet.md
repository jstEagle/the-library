## 4. The Usurper's Banquet

The pheasant arrived under a silver dome, and Beatrix knew before the lid came off that something in it was wrong.

Not poison. She had learned to hear poison the way a musician hears a flat note — a small wrongness in the smell of the steam, a shadow in the glaze. This was different. This was rosemary where the recipe called for thyme, a substitution no cook in Vhalenne's kitchens would dare make on a banquet night, because Queen Ilseva had hated rosemary since childhood and everyone knew it, and everyone knowing it was the point.

Someone wanted her to notice.

She dipped the small gilt spoon anyway, lifted a sliver of breast meat glazed in quince, and held it on her tongue while the hall watched. They always watched. That was the theater of it — the little ritual of the royal taster, the girl who stood at the queen's shoulder in dove-gray silk and swallowed the kingdom's dangers one spoonful at a time. Six years, and she had made an art of the pause: long enough to seem thorough, short enough to seem unafraid.

She let the meat rest against her palate. Rosemary, quince, the coppery ghost of a marinade that had been changed twice to disguise the first change. And beneath it all, faint as a whisper through a wall, something bitter and green that did not belong to any kitchen in the palace.

*Wolfsbane,* she thought. *A whisper of it. Enough to make a queen ill by midnight, not enough to kill. Enough to make a point.*

She swallowed. Smiled at the table. Turned to the queen and gave the smallest nod — the one that meant *safe*, and tonight also meant *I see it, and I will deal with it after*.

Queen Ilseva's eyes flickered acknowledgment and nothing else. Twenty years of a stolen throne had taught her to keep her face like a locked drawer.

At the far end of the high table sat the reason for the rosemary and the wolfsbane and the theater of it all: Lord Cassian Merev, envoy of Aurel Kest, Emperor of the Seamed Lands, Usurper by the free cities' counting and His Radiance by his own. He was eating the pheasant with evident pleasure. He was not dying. Of course he wasn't. The dish had never been meant for him.

Beside him sat his aide — a woman with iron-colored hair cropped short as a soldier's, hands folded neatly beside her plate, who had not touched her wine since the first course and who was watching Beatrix the way a jeweler watches a stone she suspects of being paste.

Beatrix had counted her glances all evening. Eleven so far. Twelve, as Beatrix returned to her station behind the queen's chair. The aide didn't look away when caught. That was the trouble. Everyone in Vhalenne knew the etiquette of watching a taster — you glanced, you looked away, you pretended the whole apparatus of survival was beneath mention. This woman watched openly, patiently, like someone taking notes behind her eyes.

Beatrix filed her under a name: *the Ledger*. It was how she managed fear — give it a shape, give it a habit, learn its rhythm until it was only another dish to taste.

"Your majesty honors us," Merev was saying, his voice carrying the oiled warmth of a man who had practiced charm on people who could have him flayed. "Emperor Kest sends his regards, and his admiration. Vhalenne's independence has always been a — what is the phrase — a charming anachronism."

"It has been a fact for twenty years," the queen said pleasantly.

"Facts," Merev said, lifting his glass, "are so often just maps that haven't been redrawn yet."

Laughter, polite and brittle, moved down the table like wind through wheat. Beatrix kept her eyes lowered and her hands still and thought about Elio's margin note from three days ago, inked tiny in the corner of a survey chart she wasn't supposed to have seen: *the marmalade is gone. eat dinner with me tuesday. — v.* As if Tuesday were a country they could both reach.

Then the herald's staff struck marble three times, and the noose drew tighter.

"By command of Her Majesty Ilseva, First of Her Name, Sovereign of Vhalenne and the Thornfell Marches — Master Elio Vane is named Royal Cartographer to the Crown."

Elio rose from the third table, where the scholars and surveyors ate, and crossed the marble floor in his good coat with the ink stain still on one cuff, because of course he'd forgotten the cuff, because he was Elio. He knelt. The queen touched his shoulder with the thin ceremonial rod. The hall applauded with the measured enthusiasm of people doing arithmetic — *why does the crown honor a mapmaker at a banquet for the usurper's envoy?*

Beatrix applauded too. Her heart was doing something complicated and stupid, pride and terror braided together, because she knew why Elio had been named publicly, tonight, in front of Merev and the Ledger and every listening ear in Vhalenne.

Because Kest wanted maps of the Thornfells. And the crown, which owed Kest debts no ledger admitted to, had just handed him its best cartographer on a silver salver.

Merev raised his glass toward Elio, smiling like a door opening onto darkness. "Master Vane. The emperor has heard your name already. He does love a man who knows where things are."

Elio bowed from the knee where he still knelt. "I know where things are, my lord," he said. "That's rarely the same as knowing what they are."

Clever, Beatrix thought. Too clever. She saw the Ledger's pen actually move — a real pen, a slim black notebook open beside her plate, into which she wrote four words Beatrix could not read but could, by the set of the woman's mouth, feel.

Under the noise of the toast, Beatrix tasted the next course before it left the pass — a chilled sorrel soup — and found nothing in it but soup. Then she tasted the honey cakes. Nothing. Then, when the footmen turned their backs, she slipped into the service corridor and did what no taster was asked to do: she went to Merev's table setting, the one already cleared, and ran her tongue along the rim of the envoy's abandoned wine cup.

Bitter green. Wolfsbane again, fainter, a signature rather than an attempt. A message in the only language she truly spoke.

*I know there is a taster here. I know how tasters think. I am speaking to you.*

She set the cup down with a hand that did not shake, because six years of practice had purchased exactly that, and walked back into the light with her face arranged into pleasant nothing, while somewhere beneath her ribs the old loneliness shifted in its sleep.

* * *

They gave Elio a chamber off the map room with his name painted on the door in gold leaf, which was either an honor or a leash depending on how far the chain reached. He stood in it after the banquet broke up, still wearing the coat, holding the deed of appointment with its heavy wax seal, and trying to feel like a man who deserved a door with his name on it instead of a man who had spent the evening lying by omission to a room full of murderers.

"You shouldn't have said that to Merev." Beatrix's voice, from the doorway. She'd come without being summoned; she always knew. "The clever reply. About maps and things."

"He laughed."

"Merev doesn't laugh. Merev files." She shut the door behind her and crossed the room, and for a moment she simply stood close, forehead nearly touching his chest, both of them too tired for the version of each other that performed composure. In this room they were not the taster and the cartographer. In this room she was Bea and he was the only person alive who knew what her silences cost.

"The aide," Elio said. "Iron hair. She wrote something when I spoke."

"I saw. I've named her the Ledger. Don't ask me why. It helps." She pulled back and looked up at him. "Elio. Why tonight? Honors like that don't happen by accident. The queen put you on the table tonight like a dish."

"I know." He set the deed down. "There's more. Two days ago I was ordered to begin a full survey of the Thornfells — interior passes, water sources, everything. Crown seal, top priority." He hesitated, and then, because this was the room where they didn't lie, he took the draft map from his desk and laid it out, and Beatrix read it the way she read everything: fast, hungry, missing nothing.

"Three passes are missing," she said quietly.

"Yes."

She looked up at him. For six years she had carried the smuggler roads alone — the Cold Larch, the Hollow Stair, the Singing Thorn — memorized in her feet, never drawn anywhere, never spoken aloud except along the trails themselves. She had assumed her secret was hers. Now she understood it was theirs, plural, shared without ever having been agreed to, and the tenderness of that almost undid her.

"You left them out of a crown commission," she said.

"I left them out of every map I've ever drawn." He shrugged, awkward, honest. "You told me once you liked having somewhere the empire couldn't find. I decided the empire didn't need to know where you were."

Beatrix kissed him then, briefly, hard, the way you drink water in a drought — and then stepped back, because the drought was the point, because tomorrow morning the Ledger would be taking notes and tonight was all they had.

"There's something else," she said. "Something about tonight you need to hear, and I need you not to decide anything until sunrise."

She told him about the wine cup. The wolfsbane, dosed to speak rather than to kill. Merev hadn't come to dine; he'd come to leave a note, addressed to whoever in the palace was fluent in bitterness. *We know your blood. We are watching your blood.*

Elio was quiet a long moment. "Speaking to you," he said. "Why you?"

"Because I'm the most dangerous person in that dining hall," Beatrix said, "and nobody claps for me."

He laughed despite himself, and the laugh cracked something loose, and they stood in the lamplight in the room with his name on the door, holding each other like people on a deck that was very slowly beginning to tilt.

Outside, rain started against the windows. Somewhere across the city, in a guest wing kept warm by imperial coin, the Ledger was writing by candlelight — patient, neat, unhurried — and Beatrix, with her cheek against Elio's shoulder, listened to the rain and thought: *she isn't recording what happened tonight. She's recording what will happen next.*

She was right. But the truth was worse than she imagined, and it would take a bookseller's stall, a codex bound in ash-gray leather, and one page of ink that had not yet dried to show her exactly how much worse.

Two days. That was all the polite part of the noose had left.
