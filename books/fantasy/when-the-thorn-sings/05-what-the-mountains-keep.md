## 5. What the Mountains Keep

They left the capital before the fourth bell, when the fog still owned the streets and even the rats were sleeping.

Beatrix had dressed for it: no palace silk, no taster's grey. Wool trousers, a man's coat three sizes too big, boots with heels worn to the shape of her stride. She'd kept these clothes in a cache behind a dyer's shop on Candle Lane for six years, and every time she pulled them out she felt like a woman putting on a body she'd outgrown and never quite left.

Elio met her at the dyer's back door with his satchel and a look of open disbelief.

"You own trousers."

"I own many things you don't know about." She handed him a hooded cloak. "Keep that up. In the Thornfells, faces are currency. Spend yours carefully."

"Beatrix." He said her name the way he did in bed, low and unhurried, and it was unfair of him, because they were about to do something that could get them both hanged and he was making her want things. "Where are we going?"

"Somewhere the crown has never mapped." She pulled the door shut behind them and set off down Candle Lane at a smuggler's pace — quick, quiet, eyes moving. "Which, if I've done my job right, is most of it."

* * *

The city gave way to terraced vineyards, then to pine, then to nothing but dark. They walked the old drover road until it forked, and where any map in Vhalenne showed the road ending at a rockfall, Beatrix turned left through what looked like solid stone.

It wasn't. It never was.

The pass was a seam in the mountain, six feet wide at the mouth, narrowing to a slot you had to turn sideways for. Smugglers called it the Needle. Beatrix had first come through it at nineteen, half-starved, carrying letters sewn into the lining of her mother's coat, guided by a woman named Ostra who had died on this exact stretch of trail two winters ago and been buried under a cairn that Beatrix rebuilt every spring.

She lit no torch. The Needle was best done blind, by feel, because light leaked and leaks got people killed.

"Put your hand on the wall," she murmured. "Left side. There are handholds cut into it — old ones, older than the war. Count your steps. Two hundred and forty to the widening. If you lose count, stop walking and wait for me."

"And if I fall?"

"Then I'll carry you," she said, "and I'll be insufferable about it for the rest of our lives."

Behind her, in the black, Elio laughed — a short, startled sound, swallowed by the mountain. Then his footsteps, careful, counting.

She listened to him count under his breath and thought about how strange love was. Six years she had built herself into a fortress, dosed herself nightly, told no one anything, and here was one man walking blind through a crack in the world because she asked him to, trusting her with his whole weight. It terrified her more than the poison ledger ever had.

Two hundred and forty steps. The walls fell away and cold starlight spilled in through a chimney of broken rock overhead, enough to see by. Elio emerged from the slot with dust on his cheek and his hair wild and his eyes enormous.

"That was—" He stopped. Started again. "How long has this existed?"

"Longer than the empire. Longer than the free cities. The Thornfells don't belong to anyone. That's rather the point." She took the codex from her pack and pressed it against her chest, feeling its weight like a heartbeat. "Come on. The hard part's over. Now we just walk uphill for four hours."

"Four hours," he repeated faintly, and followed her up into the singing dark.

* * *

The safehouse had been a shrine once — before Kest, before the war, maybe before the kingdom. A hollowed chamber under an overhang, its walls carved with vines and faces so weathered they'd gone featureless, a spring running cold through a trough cut into living rock. Smugglers used it as a waypoint and mostly left it alone; there were rules about the old shrines, unspoken ones, and even men who moved contraband for a living didn't like sleeping where the carvings could watch them.

Beatrix banked the fire small, hung the kettle, and watched Elio walk the chamber slowly, trailing his fingers over the carved vines.

"These aren't decorative," he said. "Look. The vines all lead somewhere. Downward." His finger traced a line from a carved face, along a tendril, into a spiral at the base of the wall. "This is a map."

Her stomach tightened. "It's a shrine."

"It's both. Shrines are maps, Beatrix. Maps are prayers. Half the cartographers in Vhalenne would laugh at me for saying that, but—" He crouched at the wall, head tilted, and she saw the thing in him she'd seen the night they met: a mind that couldn't leave a pattern alone, that had to follow every line to its root. "Whoever cut these knew the mountains from the inside. Not from above. From below."

"Elio."

He looked up.

"We came here to hide a book. That's all. We hide it, we go home, we forget tonight ever happened." She heard how thin it sounded the moment she said it, and hated herself for saying it anyway. "Please."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, and unpacked the bread and cheese she'd brought, and let it go — which was worse, somehow, because she could see him not letting it go.

They ate sitting shoulder to shoulder against the warm stone. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like distant voices. Beatrix realized she was starving — real hunger, the kind she never allowed herself at court, where she tasted everything and ate almost nothing. She ate three pieces of bread and let her head tip onto his shoulder.

"Tell me something true," she said. "Something you've never told anyone. I'll trade you one of mine."

Elio was quiet long enough that she thought he might refuse. When he spoke, his voice had changed — flatter, more careful, the voice of a man handling something fragile.

"I've been dreaming about a door."

She lifted her head.

"For months now. Always the same dream. I'm underground — deep, deeper than any mine — and there's a door in the rock. Green stone, with vines carved on it, except the vines are moving. And there's a lock, and the lock isn't metal, it's—" He laughed, embarrassed, rubbing the back of his neck. "It sounds mad. Say it sounds mad."

"It doesn't," she said quietly. "What's the lock?"

"A cup." He stared at the fire. "A chalice. And in the dream I'm always holding it, and I always wake up before I drink."

Cold moved through Beatrix that had nothing to do with the mountain air. She set down her bread. Her hands were steady — six years of training — but inside she was falling.

"The codex," she said. "Show me the last page."

He fetched it from his satchel. She held the book close to the fire and read the passage again, the one she'd read forty times since the bookseller's stall, the one she'd been refusing to understand:

*Beneath the roots of the Thorn the Gate stands shut, and it shall stand shut while the vessel holds. One shall bear the key written in his own hand. One shall pour the cup. What the mountains keep, the mountains keep — until a bearer walks down, and a drinker kneels.*

Below it, inked a century ago beside tomorrow's date: *Elio Vane, bearer.*

"The door in your dream," she said. "Green stone?"

"Jade, maybe. Or serpentine." He frowned. "Why are you looking at me like that?"

Because the carved vines on the shrine wall led downward too. Because the prophecy hadn't just named him — it had described his dreams, dreams he'd been having before either of them knew the book existed. Because she had spent six years believing she understood exactly how much danger she was in, and she had been wrong by an entire continent.

"Beatrix." He touched her chin, turning her face to his. "You're trading truths. You owe me one now. Something you've never told anyone."

And there it was. The choice she'd been avoiding since the bookseller's stall: keep the fortress closed, or open one gate and trust him not to sack the place.

She opened her mouth to give him something small. A half-truth. *I take poisons for the queen, you know that already.* Something that would cost her nothing.

Instead she heard herself say: "Every night, before I sleep, I dose myself. Arsenic, then antimony, then a dozen others in rotation. I've done it for six years. My blood is a fortress, Elio. I am the single most poisoned human being in Vhalenne, possibly in the world, and I've never told a living soul." Her voice didn't shake. She was proud of that, and ashamed of it, in equal measure. "Not the queen. Not my handlers. No one. Until you."

Elio didn't recoil. That was the thing about him she still couldn't believe — he leaned in. He took her hand and turned it over and looked at her palm as if the poisons might show there, as if he wanted to know every one of them by name.

"Does it hurt?" he asked. "The doses."

"Some nights."

"Why?" Softly. Not horrified. Asking, genuinely asking, why she had chosen a lifetime of small deliberate suffering. "You're the taster. You only need to survive long enough to taste. Why build a fortress?"

"Because tasting tells me what's in the cup," she said. "Immunity lets me decide whether to tell anyone. There will be a night when the queen is poisoned and the truth gets people killed — or the lie does. On that night I want to be the only person alive who can choose which." She looked at their joined hands. "That was the idea, anyway. Lately I think I just wanted one thing in the world that belonged entirely to me."

The fire cracked. Wind sang over the shrine mouth, a high thin note, and beneath it — or perhaps she imagined it, perhaps the mountain itself was dreaming with Elio — a lower sound, like a door settling in its frame.

"When we go back," Elio said, "we need to talk about the date on that page."

"Tomorrow's date."

"My name under it. *Bearer.*" He exhaled. "Beatrix, I don't believe in prophecy. I map coastlines. Coastlines don't care about destiny. But I dreamed that door before I ever saw this book, and I can't map my way out of that."

"No," she agreed. "Neither can I."

She should have felt afraid. She did feel afraid. But underneath the fear was something else, something warmer and more dangerous: relief. The fortress had a second soul inside it now, and instead of feeling breached she felt, for the first time in six years, less alone.

She kissed him, slow, tasting woodsmoke and cold air and the particular salt of him, and he pulled her close against the old carved stone, and for a while neither of them spoke about doors or dates or cups.

* * *

They slept in shifts, wrapped in one blanket against the shrine wall. Beatrix took the first watch and let Elio sleep, watching the fire and thinking.

One shall bear the key. One shall pour the cup.

*A drinker kneels.*

She flexed her poisoned blood in her veins like a fist. Whatever was coming for them — Kest's inquisitors, the prophecy, the door beneath the mountains — there was one role in that old verse she could play better than anyone alive. She didn't tell Elio that. Some maps you didn't share until you had to.

Near dawn, Elio woke suddenly, gasping.

She was beside him instantly. "The dream?"

He nodded, still half in it, sweat on his brow despite the cold. "It changed. This time the door was open." His voice dropped. "And someone was standing in it, holding out the chalice. Beatrix — it was you. It was you, kneeling, offering me the cup."

Outside, the thorn thickets below the overhang stirred in a wind that hadn't been blowing a moment before, and from far beneath the mountain — impossibly far, a sound more felt than heard — something answered.
