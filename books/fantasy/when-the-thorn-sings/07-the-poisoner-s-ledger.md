## 7. The Poisoner's Ledger

The master poisoner's workroom sat at the end of the palace's oldest corridor, past the cold larders and the chapel no one used anymore, in a wing where the stones sweated even in high summer. Beatrix had walked past that door six hundred times without once going through it. Tasters and poisoners occupied adjacent professions the way a lamb and a wolf occupied adjacent pastures — technically neighbors, structurally doomed.

Tonight she went through it.

She told herself it was curiosity. She told herself it was diligence. She told herself a great many things while she lifted the latch with her sleeve over her hand, because she had spent six years learning what poison could do and only recently begun asking what people who handled poison all day might do with the knowledge, and the answer had arrived three nights ago in the shape of a broth that tasted like nothing she'd ever been dosed with before.

The room was lit by a single lamp turned low. Shelves climbed every wall, jarred and stoppered and labeled in a cramped hand. Dried foxglove hung from the rafters in bundles like funeral wreaths. There was a stillness to the place that felt less like absence and more like held breath.

Master Corvane was not there. He rarely was, after the ninth bell. He was old now, old enough that the court had stopped remembering what exactly he did behind this door, which was precisely how he liked it. A man who brewed the palace's poisons was safest when he seemed like furniture.

Beatrix closed the door behind her and stood in the dark a moment, letting her eyes adjust, letting her pulse slow. Fear was a flavor. That was the first thing they taught you, except nobody taught tasters anything; you learned or you died. Fear tasted like copper at the back of the throat. She swallowed hers and got to work.

* * *

She wasn't sure what she was looking for. That was a lie too, but she hadn't admitted it yet, so she moved through the shelves reading labels — nux vomica, queen's sorrow, widow's kiss, the whole litany of names that sounded like ballads and killed like butchers — looking for the one thing that would make sense of the broth.

It had been a Tuesday service, ordinary in every way. The queen's table, the third course, a pheasant broth the color of late honey. Beatrix had taken her spoonful in front of the assembled court as she always did, smiled the small smile she always did, and waited for the bitterness that was her nightly tax.

There was no bitterness.

There was something else. Something she had no name for, because she had never tasted it: a sweetness so faint it was almost a memory of sweetness, followed by a numbness on the tongue that faded within a minute and left nothing behind. No burn. No ache. No symptom she could report to the physician, because there was nothing wrong with her, except that everything about it was wrong. Poisons announced themselves to her. They had to. Her body met them like an army meeting invaders at the wall — there was always a skirmish, always a taste, always some cost she paid privately in the hours after, sweating out her doses alone in her narrow bed.

This one had walked through the gate unchallenged. This one had been *expected*.

She had spent two days telling herself it was nothing. Then she had spent one night not sleeping, and by dawn she had admitted what her instincts had been shouting since the third spoonful touched her tongue: someone had built that compound the way a smith builds a key. Measured against a specific lock.

Measured against her.

The ledger lived in the second drawer of Corvane's desk. She knew this because she knew everything about this palace that mattered — which servants carried keys, which guards drank, which corridors had sightlines and which were blind — knowledge she'd gathered not for espionage but for survival, back when she was a nineteen-year-old smuggler's daughter pretending to be a kitchen girl pretending not to notice what went into the royal wine. Six years of paying attention. It kept you alive right up until it didn't.

The drawer was locked. Of course it was locked. But locks were just opinions, and Beatrix had learned hers from people who thought borders were suggestions. Two minutes with a sliver of iron from her sleeve and the drawer surrendered.

The ledger inside was plain black calfskin, unmarked. Unmarked was its own kind of message. Everything in this palace bore a seal, a stamp, an ownership; this book wore nothing because it belonged to no office. It belonged to a purpose.

She opened it by lamplight and her stomach dropped through the floor.

* * *

It was her.

Not her name. Her name appeared nowhere, which was somehow worse — she had been reduced to a cipher, a subject of study, a specimen. The entries began six years ago, almost to the week. Almost to the day she'd first been dosed.

*Subject received 4 grains henbane in morning broth. Emesis. Recovery within the day.*

*Subject received 6 grains. No emesis. Pulse elevated. Note: adaptation faster than expected.*

*Subject received compound 12 (foxglove base). Subject reports "bitterness." Continue.*

Her own words, recorded here. Every taste she'd ever taken, every reaction she'd hidden or shown, transcribed into a neat crabbed hand and reduced to data. Six years of her life laid out in columns. The night she'd doubled over in the servants' stair after a dose of wolfsbane and sworn no one had seen — entry dated, symptoms noted, *subject conceals distress well*. The month she'd quietly increased her own private doses, building her walls higher because the political season was coming and she couldn't afford weakness — noted, analyzed, *subject self-dosing beyond required protocol. Interesting.*

Interesting.

She read on, and the entries changed. Somewhere around year four the language shifted from observation to construction. Formulas appeared. Ratios. Corrections. And beside each formula, a notation that made her blood run colder than any poison ever had:

*Trial 3. Insufficient affinity. Adjusting base.*

*Trial 9. Closer. Subject tolerated full cup. Taste profile: sweet? Confirm with next serving.*

*Trial 11. Subject detected nothing. Compound passes fortress. Awaiting authorization for escalation.*

Trial eleven. The broth. The sweetness. The nothing.

Someone had been building a poison keyed to her immunity the way a lock is keyed to a single key — and they had succeeded, and the proof of their success had passed through her body three nights ago leaving no trace, and somewhere in this palace a page awaited an authorization that would turn *passes fortress* into *ends subject.*

She stood very still in the dark workroom and let the truth arrive in pieces, because if she took it all at once it would break her.

Someone knew.

Not suspected. Knew. You could not build a poison against a taster's immunity unless you understood the immunity — every dose, every adaptation, every wall she'd raised brick by agonizing brick. The ledger proved it: whoever wrote these pages had watched her for six years with more attention than any lover, any friend, any enemy. She had thought her secrecy was a fortress. Here was its blueprint, drawn in someone else's hand.

And there was only one reason to build such a weapon. A regular poison killed queens and inconvenient ministers alike; the palace had dozens for every purpose. But a poison that could defeat Beatrix Marchetti's blood could only have one target. It meant removing her was the goal — and removing her mattered only if what she knew, or what she could do, or whom she could protect, stood between someone and something they wanted.

Elio's name surfaced in her mind unbidden, and she pushed it down hard, the way you push down panic, knowing it will bob up again.

* * *

She copied what she needed. Not everything — no time, and the risk of being caught with pages in hand was its own death sentence — but the formulas for trials nine through eleven, the dates, and above all the final line, written in fresh ink that still shone faintly in the lamplight:

*Awaiting authorization for escalation. Court of His Majesty Aurel Kest sends envoy within the fortnight. Recommend trial twelve coincide with state occasion. Maximum witnesses. Maximum consequence.*

Kest's envoy. The banquet. She counted backward and felt the floor tilt. The dinner where Elio would be named royal cartographer. The dinner where she would stand behind Queen Ilseva's chair with a silver spoon and a lifetime of practiced calm. The dinner where, apparently, someone intended to test whether six years of her life amounted to anything more than a long, slow education in dying.

She put the ledger back exactly as she'd found it, relocked the drawer, and wiped her prints from the lamp handle. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside she was a struck bell, ringing and ringing, but her hands had been trained too well to tremble where anyone might see.

At the door she paused, and looked back at the shelves, at the hanging wreaths of foxglove, at the little glass kingdom of measured deaths — and she understood, finally, why the room felt like held breath. It was waiting. Everything in it was waiting. Poison is patience in a bottle, and patience was about to be rewarded.

* * *

The corridors were empty at this hour except for ghosts, and Vhalenne's palace kept plenty. She moved through them on autopilot while her mind raced ahead, cataloging suspects. Corvane himself? He had the access, the skill, the anonymity. But the handwriting in the ledger wasn't his — she'd seen his apothecary marks on the poison labels often enough to know his letters, and the ledger's hand was younger, sharper, someone who wrote fast and thought faster. An apprentice, then. Or someone who merely used the workroom. Someone with rank enough to command the palace's master poisoner and authority enough to await *authorization* — that word again, that chilling bureaucratic modesty, as if her murder were a requisition form.

The queen?

She rejected it immediately, then un-rejected it, then hated herself for both. Ilseva had been good to her. Had trusted her, promoted her from kitchens to high table, called her *my shield* at court with a warmth that felt real. But shields existed to be spent, and queens were not sentimental about equipment. If Kest's shadow had fallen across Vhalenne already — if the queen were negotiating, appeasing, trading pieces — then a taster who knew too much was a piece like any other.

No. She wouldn't believe it yet. She needed the queen. More than that — she needed to warn someone, and the list of people she could trust had gotten so short she could count it on one hand with fingers left over.

One name. One name on the whole list.

Elio.

Which meant the thing she had been protecting him from — her other life, the letters she moved along smuggler roads, the whole architecture of lies she'd built between them and the truth — was about to come down whether she liked it or not. Because if someone in this palace was building poisons against her blood, then whatever came next would touch everyone near her, and Elio stood closer than anyone. He had drawn her a map once, early on, of everywhere he wanted to take her — the free cities, the coast, a house with a blue door. She had kissed him and said nothing and gone back to drinking poison for a living.

She reached her own narrow room, bolted the door, and sat on the edge of her bed with the copied pages against her chest, listening to the palace breathe around her.

Somewhere down those stone corridors, a trial twelve was being authorized. Somewhere a compound was being refined, patient as winter, tailored to the exact shape of her survival. She had spent six years believing her blood was a fortress, and fortresses made you feel safe right up until you learned that every fortress ever built had been besieged by someone holding its plans.

But plans cut both ways. Whoever wrote that ledger thought they knew her completely.

They didn't know about the Thornfells. They didn't know about the passes. They didn't know about the codex hidden in a smuggler's cache beneath the mountain, inked with tomorrow's date and Elio's name. They knew the fortress.

They didn't know what it was guarding.

Beatrix blew out her candle and began, in the dark, to plan.

Three days until the banquet. Three days to find who held the pen, warn the one person she couldn't bear to lose, and decide — with a killer's patience working against her — whether the time had come to run.

Then came the knock at her door. Soft. Twice. A rhythm she knew.

Elio — pale, breathless, wild-eyed in the corridor dark — pressed a folded paper into her hands before she could speak.

"Beatrix," he whispered. "There's someone in my workshop. They went through my maps. And they left this."

The paper bore a single line, in the same sharp young hand as the ledger:

*The bearer should choose his allies carefully.*
