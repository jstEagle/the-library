## 8. Kestreach

### BEATRIX

The summons came by silver rider, which meant it came by order of the crown, which meant — in the sixth year of Aurel Kest's occupation of the capital's foreign policy — it came by Kest.

Beatrix read it twice by the kitchen window while the morning bread cooled behind her. The parchment was heavy, cream-colored, sealed with the double-headed thorn of the Kestreach court. It named three names: Queen Ilseva of Vhalenne, her taster Beatrix Marchetti, and the royal cartographer Elio Vane. It requested — *requested*, in that way that made the word a blade wrapped in silk — their presence at the winter court of the usurper, for the formal commissioning of a great expedition into the Thornfells.

*Expedition into the Thornfells.*

She read it a third time and her hands were perfectly steady, because her hands were always perfectly steady, because she had trained them to be steady the way other women trained their daughters to sew.

"You've gone pale," said Mistress Odile, the undercook, passing with a tray of quinces.

"I'm always pale. It's the profession."

"You've gone *paler*."

Beatrix folded the summons along its original creases. "The queen will want her gray traveling gown. The one with the silver at the cuffs. And pack the blue vial from my cabinet — the one I don't label."

Odile's eyebrows climbed, but she went. That was the thing about the kitchens: they asked nothing and saw everything, and they kept what they saw the way a cellar keeps wine.

Beatrix stood alone for a moment with the summons in her hand and let herself feel it — just for a moment, just the one breath — the cold weight of what it meant. Kest knew about the Thornfells. Kest knew about *something* in the Thornfells. And he wanted the man who had drawn its maps standing in front of him when he asked the right questions.

She had spent six years learning to detect poison by the tenth of a grain, by the color of an oil slick on wine, by the way a broth's steam bent. She had never once been able to detect the poison that arrived by parchment.

* * *

### ELIO

The road to Kestreach took nine days, and Elio spent six of them pretending to sleep.

He lay in the swaying dark of the queen's traveling coach with his eyes shut and his sketchbook open on his knee, working from memory, because memory was the one archive no inquisitor could seize. The Thornfells from the west, in section. The three passes that did not exist on any crown map — the Hollow Stair, the Widow's Tooth, the Singing Cut — each one drawn small and true, then blotted out again with a thumb-smudge of graphite. He could draw them blind. He had drawn them blind, once, in the margin of a letter he'd pressed into Beatrix's hand in the palace orchard, disguised as a complaint about the quality of the palace ink.

*The ink is thin here,* the letter had said, in the coded idiom they'd built over three years of stolen evenings. *It pools in the low places. I'll thicken it where it matters.*

She had written back: *The low places are where the best water gathers. Keep your ink thin where the crown can see it.*

He had not known, then, that he was practicing for this — for the art of showing a tyrant a map with holes in it while his heart hammered like a boy's.

Across the coach, Queen Ilseva sat upright and gray as her gown, her hands folded, her eyes on the curtain. She was fifty-one years old and had been a hostage of politics since she was nine, and she wore her captivity the way other queens wore pearls. She had not spoken to Elio directly in four days.

On the fifth day she spoke.

"Cartographer." Her voice was dry as old vellum. "When the usurper asks you what lies beneath the Thornfells, what will you tell him?"

Elio kept his eyes on his sketchbook. "Mountains, majesty. Stone, mostly. Stone all the way down."

"Mm." A pause long enough to drive a cart through. "I had a tutor once who said that all maps are confessions. The cartographer tells you what he believes the world is for." Her eyes came off the curtain and settled on him, and they were pale and patient and terrible. "I wonder what yours confesses."

"That the world is larger than the people who want to own it."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile. It might have been the ghost of a warning. With Ilseva the two had always shared a face.

"Be careful in Kestreach," she said, and turned back to the curtain. "The court there flatters the way a spider wraps. You will be complimented until you cannot move."

The coach rocked on. Elio looked down at his sketchbook, at the smudged ghosts of three passes only one living person besides him knew, and thought: *She already knows I'd lie. She's telling me to lie better.*

* * *

Kestreach did not look like a usurper's capital. That was the first obscenity of it.

It looked like what it had always been — the second city of the old empire, built on a river bend with the confidence of a place that had never once been besieged — except that now the old imperial green of the banners had been over-dyed in Kest's black and gold, and the dye job was imperfect, so that in certain lights the old color bled through like a bruise under powder. The palace gates stood open. The guards wore polished black and did not check papers. Everyone, Elio thought, was already known.

They were housed in the east wing, in rooms too fine, with fires already lit and wine already poured, and Beatrix appeared at his door within the hour in a servant's gray, having charmed or bribed or quietly poisoned her way past three corridors of Kest's household staff.

"We need to talk," she said, "and we need to do it while I taste your wine, because that's the only conversation this palace will believe."

He poured. She sipped, held it on her tongue with the professional stillness he'd watched a hundred times and never tired of, and swallowed.

"Rowanberry and something under it. Iron, maybe. Not poisoned — just cheap." She set the cup down. "Elio. The codex page. The date. His name beside yours. *Bearer.*"

"I remember."

"Kest summoned us the same week that page surfaced. Either he's read the codex, or he's read something older, or someone told him. And he wants you in front of him, in his own hall, where he can watch your face when he says the word *Gate*."

"Then I'll watch mine too."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke." He took her hand, there in the lamplight with the cheap wine between them, and her fingers were cold. "Beatrix. Whatever he offers me, I refuse."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her do the arithmetic she was always doing, the cold beautiful arithmetic of survival. "Refusal is a door with only one handle," she said finally. "You don't get to choose which side of it opens. If you refuse a usurper to his face, he doesn't kill you — killing you confirms the secret's worth. He keeps you. Politely. In a comfortable room. Until you're useful or until I can get you out, and I don't know yet which I can do faster."

"Then what do I do?"

"You listen. You flatter. You're the best-drawn map of a loyal man he's ever seen." Her thumb moved once over his knuckles. "And you let me stand behind you with the wine."

* * *

### BEATRIX

The commissioning was held in the Hall of Rivers, so named for the mosaic floor that ran the length of it in blue and silver — a river with no source and no mouth, flowing eternally toward a throne.

Aurel Kest received them standing.

That was the second obscenity. Usurpers, in Beatrix's experience of reading about them, liked thrones; they climbed them early and often. But Kest stood at the head of his river, in a plain black coat with a single gold chain, a broad, gray-templed man of perhaps fifty-five with the open, weathered face of a farmer and the stillness of a man who had once, by all accounts, held a besieged city with four hundred soldiers and a lie about reinforcements. He did not look like a tyrant. He looked like someone's kind uncle. Beatrix had tasted enough poisons to know that the dangerous ones were never the ones that announced themselves.

"Queen Ilseva." His voice was warm, unhurried, pitched to carry no farther than the first three rows. "Vhalenne honors me. And this is the famous cartographer."

"Elio Vane, majesty." Elio bowed exactly the right depth — deep enough for a king, shallow enough for a proud man. Beatrix, two steps back with the queen's cup in her hands, could have kissed him.

"Vane." Kest turned the name over like a coin he was checking for clipping. "I have your map of the Thornfells on the wall of my study. The northern approaches. Extraordinary work — the contour lines alone. Do you know what I like about it?"

"The contour lines, majesty?"

"The honesty." Kest smiled. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Most mapmakers flatter the passes. Make them look gentler than they are, because their patrons want gentle. Yours look exactly as murderous as they are. It's the work of a man who respects the mountains." He let that sit. "And yet."

And yet.

"And yet I'm told there are places even your honest map declines to go. Gaps. Blank spaces, here—" he touched his own collarbone lightly, as if pointing at a spot on an invisible chart over his heart "—and here. Now, a less trusting man would call those omissions. I call them modesty. Every artist has places he keeps for himself." The smile widened by a degree. "I should like to buy them from you."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere in the gallery a page shifted, and the sound was enormous.

Elio said, "Majesty, the interior of the Thornfells hasn't been surveyed. The gaps aren't modesty. They're ignorance. No one has mapped what's under the central massif — the rock is folded, the passes move with the frost, and the old roads—"

"I know about the old roads." Still warm. Still gentle. "The roads the smugglers use. The roads your queen's household has, perhaps, found useful from time to time." His eyes did not move to Beatrix. That was the mastery of it — everyone in the hall knew exactly where his eyes were not looking, and the not-looking landed on her like a hand on the back of her neck. "I'm not angry, Vane. I'm an emperor — I'm told I must call myself an emperor now, the free cities are so tedious about titles — and emperors are not angry at modesty. We commission it. I want you to map the depths of the Thornfells. All of them. Every gap on your beautiful honest map, filled in. Under crown seal, with crown soldiers, and my own inquisitor to keep the frost off you." He paused. "You'll want to see the commission terms. I'm told they're generous."

He gestured, and an aide brought forward a leather case. Elio opened it with steady hands. Beatrix, reading upside down at twenty feet the way she'd been trained to read everything upside down, caught the figures and felt the trap close with a soundless click: the sum was not generous. It was *ruinous*. It was the kind of sum that, refused, looked like treason, and, accepted, made a man the usurper's instrument in the one place in the world that could still unmake him.

"And the object of the survey, majesty?" Elio asked. His voice was level. Beatrix marveled at it. She could hear, under it, the heartbeat.

"Minerals," said Kest comfortably. "Timber. Roads for trade. The usual reasons emperors give." He stepped closer — one step only, down from the dais, into the river of the mosaic, so that he stood among them rather than above them, a man among men, and Beatrix understood suddenly that the informality was the performance, that every inch of this man's kindness was load-bearing. "And, Vane — between us, since you're a man who respects honesty — there is a door."

The hall did not breathe.

"Under the Thornfells. The old texts call it the Verdant Gate, though I suspect that's a translator's romance. It was sealed at the end of the last war, by the treaty powers, and the location was — deliberately — lost." Kest's voice stayed at that warm, uncle-ish register, and that was the most frightening thing yet: he spoke of a weapon that could crack the world in the tone of a man discussing drainage. "I don't want it opened. Let's be clear. I want it *found*, and mapped, and guarded. An emperor's job, in the end, is to keep doors shut. You of all people must understand the value of a well-kept secret."

*He knows,* Beatrix thought, and her blood, her fortress blood, her six-years-built blood, went cold in a way no poison had ever made it go. *He knows the name of it. He knows it's real. He knows someone can find it, and he thinks that someone is standing in front of him being modest about his gaps.*

Elio bowed. "Majesty honors me with his trust."

"Trust is exactly the word." Kest beamed at him, then — the pivot so smooth it was nearly invisible — at the queen. "Ilseva. Your household does you credit. The taster especially — I'm told she's the finest in the nine cities. Perhaps the finest in a century." And now his eyes did come to Beatrix, mild and interested, the way a man looks at a wine he intends to cellar for a few more years. "I hope you'll all dine with me tonight. I've taken the liberty of designing the menu myself."

* * *

That night, in the east wing, Beatrix sat on the floor of Elio's room with her back against his bed and the day's menu unrolled on her knees, reading it the way other people read battle plans.

Seven courses. She knew the poisoner's grammar of it: the cold courses were where you hid alkaloids, the sauces were where you hid the heavy metals, the wine was where you hid everything else. Kest had designed it himself. Which meant the menu was a message, and she read it twice before she understood it.

Every dish was one she had cataloged in six years of tasting. Every single one. The white soup with its trace of bitter almond. The quail in a sauce that would take a trained palate to know carried a whisper of lead-salt. The honeyed pears that were, she'd have staked her life — had staked her life, many times, on lesser odds — dosed with a slow cumulative poison that killed on the fortieth serving, not the first.

He wasn't trying to kill her.

He was *testing* her. Serving her a menu built entirely of things a normal taster would flag and a master would survive, to see which she was. To see how deep the fortress went.

"Elio," she said quietly. He looked up from the commission papers he was pretending to study. "He knows what I am."

"Beatrix—"

"He built a dinner out of my own ledger. Every dish is a dose I've taken. He's not feeding the queen tonight — he's feeding *me*, and he's watching what I swallow without flinching." She folded the menu along its creases, very carefully, the way she'd folded the summons. "He knows I'm immune. He knows what that means. And he's about to send you into the mountains with his inquisitor to find a door, and the only person in the world whose blood is keyed to old poisons will be standing in his capital, drinking his wine, smiling at his uncle's face."

The fire popped. Down in the Hall of Rivers, faintly, they could hear the court dancing to music Kest had commissioned, in a river that flowed nowhere.

"So we don't refuse," Elio said slowly. "And we don't comply."

"We do something better." Beatrix looked up at him, and for the first time since the silver rider, something in her chest was not cold. "We go into his mountains. We let him watch us find his door. And then we show him exactly how honest a map can be."

She did not say the rest. She did not need to. Somewhere under the Thornfells, in the dark the old treaty had made, a door was waiting — and now two empires, one usurper, and one woman with a fortress for blood were all walking toward it, and only one of them knew all the passes.

Three days later, the expedition's muster list was posted in the east wing, and Beatrix read it by candlelight with her heart going like a drum: Elio Vane, royal cartographer. Inquisitor Sarel Voss, crown observer.

And at the bottom, in Kest's own hand, an addition that had not been requested, was not explained, and could not, by the letter of the commission, be refused:

*Beatrix Marchetti, taster — to safeguard His Majesty's investment.*
