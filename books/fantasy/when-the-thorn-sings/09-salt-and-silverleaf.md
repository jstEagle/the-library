## 9. Salt and Silverleaf

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a delivery of lamp oil that no one had ordered.

*The house on Candlerow. The blue door. Come hungry.*

Beatrix read it twice by the light of the palace corridor, then fed it to the flame of the nearest sconce and watched the ink curl and go. Elio had never once asked her to come hungry in six years of coded margin notes and stolen hours, and she understood immediately what he was telling her: this was not a night for maps or codices or the war pressing down on them like weather. This was a night for something else, and he was frightened of it, and he wanted her to arrive with an appetite anyway.

She finished her shift. That was the discipline of her life — finish the shift. She tasted the queen's evening consommé, held the bitterness on her tongue for a full count of sixty, bowed, withdrew. In the servants' stair she pressed two fingers to her wrist and found her pulse steady as a metronome, which was either health or habit; after six years she could no longer tell the difference.

Then she changed out of the taster's grey into a smuggler's brown, pulled a hood over hair she'd pinned up plainly, and walked out through the coal gate where the guard owed her a favor she intended never to collect.

Candlerow Street smelled of rain on hot stone and someone's supper — onions, thyme, fat hitting a pan. Ordinary smells. The city pretending, for one more night, that it was only a city.

The blue door opened before she knocked.

"You look like a burglar," Elio said.

"I am one." She slipped past him into the narrow hall. "You look like a man who's about to do something stupid."

"I'm about to cook." He closed the door and shot the bolt, then the second bolt he'd installed himself, then dragged the heavy cabinet across it — three locks' worth of caution for a rented room above a chandler's shop. He'd been busy. He'd been afraid. Both were written on him like annotations. "I've been told it's the same thing."

"Who told you that?"

"A very dangerous woman who tastes poison for a living."

Beatrix laughed, and the sound surprised her — it came out rusty, underused, like a hinge in a room no one entered anymore. She stood in his kitchen while he lit the lamps, and she took inventory the way she always did: exits (window over the sink, roof access through the loft hatch), risks (the shop below kept oil, oil kept fire), him (thin around the eyes, hands steady, watching her the way he watched a coastline he was charting for the first time).

On the table lay provisions arranged with cartographic precision. Flour. A knuckle of salt pork. Eggs. A bundle of silverleaf greens, still wet from some black-market garden. Dried chilies dark as dried blood. And a small clay pot of salt — not the palace's fine white salt but the coarse grey kind, scraped from tidal stones, the kind her mother had bought from a fishwife who sang off-key.

She touched the clay pot and couldn't speak for a moment.

"How," she said finally.

"I bribed a harbormaster." Elio shrugged, too casually. "And threatened a fishmonger. And paid a boy to run to the coast and back twice. I've been planning this since Voss's name went on the muster list." He looked at her, and the casualness fell away like a mask slipping its string. "Since I understood we might not get another night that isn't watched."

* * *

Her mother had cooked the dish on the first cold night of every year, in a kitchen the size of a coffin, in the free-city quarter of Marrow's Reach before Kest burned it. The dish had no name, or rather it had a family name — *cucina povera*, poverty's kitchen — salt pork rendered slow, silverleaf wilted into the fat until it gave up its bitterness, chili broken over the top, all of it folded through flatbread cooked on an iron plate. Her mother had said it was food for people the world had already taken everything from except their tongues. *They can burn the houses,* she used to say, kneading dough with flour to her elbows, *they can't burn the recipe.* Then they had burned the houses, and the recipe lived only in Beatrix's hands, unmade for six years because making it meant tasting it, and tasting meant remembering, and remembering was a poison she had no immunity against.

Now she stood at Elio's stove with her sleeves rolled and her hands moving without instruction, as if the six years between had been a door she could simply walk back through.

"Render it slower," she said, when he reached for the heat.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You're a mapmaker. You want everything legible." She moved his hand away from the flame. "Some things have to stay illegible a while. That's how the flavor gets out."

He stepped back and let her lead, which for Elio Vane was nearly a religious act. She showed him how to score the pork so the fat ran even, how to wash the silverleaf three times because grit was the honest tax on wild greens, how to know when the flatbread was ready by listening to it — a whisper, then a sigh, then silence, and you flipped it in the silence.

"Your mother taught you to cook by ear?"

"She taught me everything by ear." The chili cracked between her fingers, and the smell rose up sharp and clean and hurt somewhere behind her ribs. "She said eyes can be fooled. Tongues can't. Which is ironic, given what I do now."

"It isn't ironic," Elio said quietly. "It's inheritance. You taste truth for a living. She just got there differently."

Beatrix kept her face toward the pan. Fat spat. Silverleaf collapsed from bright green to deep olive, surrendering its bitterness into something richer. Outside, the rain began again, soft on the shingles, and the chandler's shop below breathed its waxy smell up through the floorboards, and for a few minutes there was no empire, no muster list, no prophecy book hidden in the loft wrapped in oilcloth, no door beneath the mountains waiting in Elio's dreams. There was only a kitchen, and a man learning her childhood one ingredient at a time.

* * *

They ate sitting on the floor, backs against the bed, plates on their knees, because Elio's table was covered in maps he'd spread out to burn if anyone came. The bread was imperfect — she'd rushed the last round — and the pork had caught slightly at the edges, and it was the best thing she had eaten in six years, possibly ever.

"So," Elio said. He turned his plate slowly, not eating so much as arranging. "Truths we've withheld."

"That's a strange way to start a pleasant evening."

"We don't have many evenings left that are merely pleasant. Tomorrow the wagons roll and Voss rides with us and every word we say within fifty feet of each other will be inventory for her ledgers." He set the plate down. "So tonight I want the whole ledger. Mine first, if it helps."

Beatrix looked at him across the lamplight. His face had the openness he brought to blank parchment — that terrible, generous readiness to be drawn upon. She thought of all the things she had not told him, arranged them by weight, and chose the one that came first chronologically, because it always had.

"When I was fourteen," she said, "I stole a peach."

He blinked. "That's your great confession?"

"Let me finish. It matters. There was a fruit seller in Marrow's Reach, a woman named Odile, and she was cruel to beggar children, and I was hungry, and I took a peach. She caught me. And instead of calling the watch she made me eat the whole thing in front of her stall — the pit, the skin, everything, while everyone watched. And then she said, *now you'll never steal from me again, girl, because you'll remember what taking costs.*" Beatrix turned her cup in her hands. "And she was right. I remembered. But what I actually learned wasn't her lesson. What I learned was that my body would keep me alive through almost anything, and that everyone else assumed the opposite, and that the gap between those two facts is where I've lived ever since. Every poison since has just been that peach again. Bigger peaches. Same lesson."

Elio was silent a long moment. "What happened to Odile?"

"Died in the burning. With most of the quarter." She shrugged, and hated how practiced the shrug was. "That's the other half of the ledger. Everything I'm good at surviving, I learned from people who didn't."

"And the queen." He said it carefully. "Do you trust her?"

The question hung there. Beatrix thought of Ilseva's cool gray eyes, the way the queen had watched her at the banquet — not with suspicion but with *recognition*, which was worse. She thought of the master poisoner's ledger she hadn't yet shown Elio, the doses logged in a hand too neat, the poison being built like a portrait of her blood.

"No," she said. It was the first time she'd said it aloud, and saying it felt like setting down a weight she'd mistaken for part of her body. "No. I think the crown has known what I am longer than I've known what I am. I think I've been useful precisely because they knew. And I think—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "I think when this expedition goes into the mountains, I may not be able to protect you from the people paying me."

"Then don't protect me from them," Elio said. "Protect me from everything else, and I'll handle the crown."

"You can't handle Aurel Kest. No one handles Aurel Kest."

"I handled the Thornfells' passes for six years without giving up yours." He smiled, tired and crooked. "I'm good at holding illegible lines."

* * *

His truths came slower, coaxed out between bites of the second round of bread and the last of the wine he'd bought with the bribed harbormaster's change.

The dreams, first — the door beneath the Thornfells, green light through stone seams, the pull he'd confessed once in the dark of a pass and never spoken of since. He described it now in daylight terms, which somehow made it worse: how the dream recurred on a schedule he could almost chart, how it had sharpened since the codex came into their lives, how sometimes he woke with dirt under his fingernails though he'd slept in a clean bed.

"And the other thing?" Beatrix asked.

Elio refilled both cups. His hand was not quite steady.

"The illustrations in the codex. The ones I said were in a similar style to my work." He looked at the flame. "They weren't similar. They were mine. My hatching. My habit of drawing water as parallel curves. I told myself forgery, coincidence, anything. But there's a mistake I make — I've always made it — I draw north arrows with seven points instead of four. Decorative. Useless. Nobody does it. It's in three of the codex illustrations, Beatrix. A century old. Ink older than my grandfather's grave, in my hand." He drank. "So either I'm mad, or the book is lying, or—" He gestured vaguely at himself, at all of it, at the impossible shape of it. "Or there's a third thing I don't have words for yet."

"Vane," Beatrix said. "The name. Where's it from?"

"The Marches originally. Before Kest. There was a village — Vanesford. It's gone now, off the maps. My grandmother used to say we came from a place that stopped existing." He laughed shortly. "I thought it was just something old women say."

"What else did she say?"

Elio was quiet. Rain ticked on the shingles. When he spoke, his voice had gone strange and careful, like a man reading coordinates he didn't trust.

"She said our family kept a door. That we didn't open it and we didn't lose it, and that the day someone in the blood forgot the difference, the singing would start." He met Beatrix's eyes. "*When the thorn sings.* I thought it was a lullaby. I've been humming it my whole life."

Beatrix set down her cup. The prophecy page surfaced in her memory — tonight's date, his name, *bearer* — and beneath it the line she'd dismissed as poetic filler: *the gate knows the hand that drew it.* She had thought it meant a king's hand. A general's. Some grand instrument of history.

It meant a pencil. It meant a man who drew water as parallel curves and got his compass roses wrong.

"Elio." She reached across and took his hand. "Whatever the door wants from you — whatever the book says you're for — listen to me. You are not a vessel. You are not a function. Prophecies are just old gossip with better penmanship, and I have survived every expert who ever told me what my body was for." Her thumb moved over his knuckles. "You get to choose. Whatever else happens. You get to choose."

"Do I?" he asked softly. "Did you? With the queen? With the poisons?"

"Yes," she said. "Every single time. That's the whole secret of it. I chose every dose. Not freely — nothing's free — but chosen. It's the only ownership I've ever had and I would not trade it for safety." She squeezed his hand. "So keep yours. Promise me."

"I promise," he said, and then, because he was Elio, precise even in surrender: "Ask me again at the Gate."

* * *

Later, the plates washed and the lamps trimmed low, they lay in his narrow bed with the rain coming harder and the city curfew bells ringing the quarter hour. Beatrix traced the scars on his forearm — the burn from a cartographer's mishap, the knife nick from the pass at Sorrel Gap — mapping him the way he mapped coastlines, patiently, feature by feature.

"I need to say one more true thing," she murmured, "and then I'll stop ruining the evening."

"Say it."

"I love you." The words came out level, unadorned, stripped of all her usual armor of irony. "Not the way I love surviving. Not the way I love a job well done. I love you the way I loved that kitchen tonight — like something that was burned down and came back anyway." She felt him go still beside her. "I've been carrying that sentence for two years and rationing it out in margins and glances because saying it whole felt like leaving it where the wrong person could find it. But tomorrow Voss rides with you into mountains that want you dead, and I decided I'd rather say it and be damned than die with it still inventoried."

Elio turned and took her face in both hands, and for a moment he didn't speak at all, which from him was the loudest possible answer. When he did speak, his voice broke slightly on the middle of it.

"I love you. Whole sentence. No margins." He kissed her forehead, her mouth, the pulse point at her throat where six years of poison sat sleeping. "And tomorrow, when Voss watches us, we give her nothing. We're colleagues. We're professionals. We save every true thing for nights like this."

"Nights like this," Beatrix repeated, and memorized it: the rain, the salt smell still in the air, his heartbeat under her palm. Because she was a taster and a smuggler and a liar in three languages, and she knew better than anyone that you hold onto the true things hardest right before the lying starts.

They slept in snatches. Near dawn, boots on the stairs woke them both — but it was only the chandler opening his shop below, whistling off-key, an ordinary man starting an ordinary day in a city that had maybe three ordinary days left in it.

By full light, the royal wagons were loading in the palace yard, and Sarel Voss stood among them in her dove-grey coat, consulting a ledger, looking exactly like a woman who already knew how the story ended.

Beatrix pulled her hood up, shouldered her kit, and went to find out how much of it she already knew too.
