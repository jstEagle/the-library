## 10. Down the Thorn Road

The expedition left Kestreach at dawn, thirty riders and a cartographer who had spent the whole night redrawing his own conscience.

Elio rode fourth from the front, close enough to hear Sarel Voss's voice without seeming to listen to it. She rode a grey mare that matched her coat, and she spoke rarely, and when she spoke it was always in questions.

"You've surveyed these passes before, Master Vane?"

"Twice," Elio said. "Once for the Admiralty, once for the Queen's Works."

"And both times for crowns." Voss turned her head. She had the kind of face that made you want to fill silences — pale, patient, unlined, as though expression had been sanded off it years ago. "Never for yourself?"

"I'm a cartographer. Maps are never for yourself."

Something moved behind her eyes — not amusement, exactly. Recognition, maybe. "That's the saddest thing anyone's said to me all year."

Ahead of them the Thornfells rose out of the morning mist like the backs of sleeping animals, blue-grey and immense, their lower slopes stitched with pine. Somewhere up there were three passes that did not exist on any crown map. Three passes only Beatrix knew. He had drawn this expedition's route himself, and he had drawn it wrong, and the wrongness was the truest work of his life.

He thought about Beatrix's hands on his map two nights ago, tracing the false route while the candle guttered. "Here," she'd said, tapping the pass he'd invented out of thin air. "The Serpent's Stair doesn't exist. Good name, though. It sounds real." And then, quieter: "They'll figure it out by the third day. Voss will figure it out sooner. Don't lie better than you have to. Liars who lie well get promoted, and promoted men can't be spared."

So the plan was not to lie brilliantly. The plan was to lie like a tired man doing his job — competently, unimaginatively, with just enough error to be forgivable.

Behind him, someone coughed. One of Kest's men, a broad-shouldered soldier named Dren with a voice like gravel in oil, riding escort on the supply mules. There were six of Kest's men embedded among the forty, and Elio had spent three sleepless nights identifying them by small things: how they checked their saddles, how they watched Voss when they thought no one noticed, how none of them had ever once complained about anything.

*Betrayal performed as loyalty.* He turned the phrase over in his mind as the column climbed into the trees. He had written it in the margin of his working copy of the codex, in cipher, before leaving the capital — then burned the page. Some truths you couldn't even keep in ink.

The dream came back to him as they climbed, as it had come every night for a week now, with the fidelity of a memory rather than a dream. A door beneath the mountain, green-veined stone, older than the empire, older than the free cities. His hand on it. The door knowing his hand.

He had told Beatrix about it in the dark under the Thornfells, on the night she showed him the smuggler trails, and she had listened the way she listened to everything — completely, as if nothing else existed — and then said, "Then we already know where you're going to lead them. The trick is making it look like an accident."

*Them.* Not *us*. He'd noticed that. She still said *them* when she meant the expedition, herself included in it or not. As if some part of her had already stepped outside the crown's story and was only walking through it.

Voss reined her mare alongside his. "You've gone quiet, Master Vane."

"He's been quiet since Kestreach," Dren called from behind, cheerful. "Cartographers. They live in their heads."

"So do inquisitors," Voss said, without looking back. "It's the company I keep."

She let the silence sit between them for a hundred yards of switchback before she asked, lightly, the way one asks about weather: "Do you believe in prophecy, Master Vane?"

The question landed in his chest like a dropped stone. He kept his eyes on the trail. "I believe in terrain. Terrain is honest."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer a cartographer gives."

"Hm." Voss looked ahead at the mountains, and for a moment something almost human crossed her sanded-down face — longing, maybe, or its cousin. "When I was a girl in the Salt Marches, a seer told my mother I would serve a great man. My mother wept with pride. The great man turned out to be a grain merchant who beat his clerks." She clicked her tongue and the grey mare picked up pace. "Prophecy is a mirror, Master Vane. People see what they're already carrying."

Elio said nothing. In his saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth beneath his survey instruments, the codex pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat. He had not opened it since Kestreach. He didn't dare. Last time, he had found his own handwriting in a book a century old — his own hand, older than his grandfather — and the world had tilted and never quite righted itself.

*The bearer walks the thorn road twice,* the last legible line read. *Once to show the way. Once to close it.*

He was fairly sure they were on the first walk.

* * *

They made camp the second night in a hanging valley where a stream ran cold and loud enough to cover conversation. Elio set his tent apart from the others, as befitted a royal officer, and spent an hour correcting his survey notes by firelight — corrections that were themselves misdirection, elevation readings nudged, a waterfall placed half a mile east of where it stood.

A shadow fell across his fire.

Dren. The big man crouched, held his hands toward the flames without invitation, and sat. For a while neither spoke. Then Dren said, "You take your bearings off the stream bend, you'll be wrong by a degree. Stream bends wander. Ridge lines don't."

Elio's pen stopped moving. "Thank you."

"My father herded sheep in the Greymarch. Forty years. I know mountains the way you know ink." Dren smiled, and it was a warm smile, which was somehow worse. "Lord Voss says you're the best mapmaker in the empire. Says the usurper trusts you with the deep roads."

"The usurper," Elio said carefully, "has a name."

"So does everyone he's hanged." Dren shrugged, easy as a man discussing rain. "I don't much care. Crowns are weather. But the deep roads—" He leaned closer, firelight carving his face into planes. "My grandmother used to sing a song about a door under the Thornfells. Green door. Sealed door. She said her grandmother's grandmother had seen soldiers go in and never come out, and the mountain drank the war whole." He held Elio's gaze, and his eyes were flat and black as a well. "Funny thing to think about, up here. Don't you find?"

"Funny isn't the word I'd use."

"No," Dren agreed pleasantly, and rose, brushing off his knees. "Sleep well, Master Vane. We've hard climbing tomorrow. Lord Voss wants to reach the high shelf by the third day."

He walked away whistling. Elio stared after him, his pulse loud in his ears.

*Dren knows.* Or Dren suspected, or Dren was bait, or Dren was exactly what he appeared to be — a shepherd's son who'd found the only honest work left in an empire built on lies. That was the poison of living inside a conspiracy: you could no longer tell a trap from a kindness. Both arrived wearing the same face.

He looked at the fire and saw, superimposed over it, green-veined stone.

That night he dreamed again, but this time the dream changed. This time there was a woman standing before the door, dark-haired, arms crossed, shaking her head at him with an expression that managed to be exasperated and terrified at once, and her mouth formed words he couldn't hear, and the shape of them — he knew this even asleep — was *not yet.*

He woke with frost on his blanket and the certainty that Beatrix was within a mile of the camp.

He didn't know how he knew. He knew the way the door knew his hand.

* * *

On the third day they climbed out of the treeline, and Elio began the real work.

The route he'd charted bent east along what his maps called the Grey Shelf — because he had invented the Grey Shelf — a high traverse that would add four days and deliver them, eventually, to a dead-end cirque full of scree and disappointment. It was a good lie. It was the kind of lie that cost an empire money and patience and nothing else.

Except that by noon, Voss had stopped consulting her copy of his map.

She rode at the head of the column now, and she rode looking north — not along his route, but across it, toward a notch in the ridgeline that Elio recognized with a sick lurch of the stomach. The Widow's Tooth. Above the Widow's Tooth lay a smuggler pass so old the free cities' own maps barely marked it. Beatrix's maps. The ones she kept in her head.

"She has another source," he told himself. "She must. Everyone has sources."

But his hands had gone cold on the reins, and when they halted at midday to water the mules, he found himself studying Voss the way he studied unfamiliar coastline: for the feature that revealed the whole shape beneath.

He found it, or thought he did. She carried no copy of his map at all.

"Master Vane." She had come up beside him without sound, which should not have been possible in boots on scree. She handed him a strip of dried meat, an oddly domestic gesture. "Tell me about the Verdant Gate."

The world went very quiet. The mules stamped. Somewhere above, wind worried at the rocks with a sound like distant singing.

"I've never heard the term," Elio said.

"No?" Voss chewed her own portion slowly. "It appears in a customs ledger from the free city of Amareth, sixty years ago. Sealed by order of the Amarethi council. Two pages torn out. The surviving page mentions a tariff exemption for 'pilgrims to the Verdant Gate.'" She swallowed. "Empires keep excellent records, Master Vane. It's the one thing we do better than anyone. We remember everything, and we understand none of it, and eventually we send someone to look." She brushed crumbs from her coat. "Someone like me."

"There are a thousand valleys in these mountains," Elio said. "Any of them could hold a gate. Or a shrine. Or a customs clerk's bad poetry."

"Mm." Voss looked north again, at the Widow's Tooth, and the wind pulled at her grey coat. "Tomorrow we leave your route, Master Vane. I'm sorry. I know you drew it carefully." She turned those pale eyes on him, and for one unguarded instant he saw what lived underneath the sanding: a hunger so clean and total it was almost admirable. "But you see, I read your margin notes."

His blood stopped.

"To the Queen's Works surveys. Six years of them. Little jokes in cipher. *The third pass sleeps. Ask the nightingale.*" She smiled — a real smile, small and terrible. "I broke it in nine days. Your nightingale sings beautifully, by the way. Whoever she is. Wherever she is."

The wind sang in the rocks. The mules breathed. Somewhere below them, on a trail no crown map admitted existed, Beatrix was watching, and Elio understood with total clarity that he had perhaps a day — perhaps hours — before Voss closed her hand around everything he loved.

"Your cipher," he heard himself say, and was astonished at how steady his voice came out, "is incomplete. The nightingale changes keys. You'll have decoded last spring's messages and believed you'd caught the song."

Something flickered in Voss's face. Doubt? No. Interest. The interest of a scholar shown a deeper text.

"Then tomorrow," she said softly, "you'll teach me the new key. On the road. As we ride. Consider it a commission." She patted his shoulder, once, the way one pats a good horse, and walked away down the scree toward the tents.

Elio stood alone in the wind with his heart hammering and his mind racing faster than it had ever raced.

She hadn't broken the cipher. Or she had, and was testing him. Either way, the clock was running now, openly, and the plan — the careful, patient, lie-like-a-tired-man plan — was dead.

There was only one move left, and it was the one Beatrix had forbidden: stop leading them astray. Lead them true. Take them to the door, ahead of her schedule, on ground he chose, at a hour he chose — because the only thing more dangerous than an inquisitor finding the Verdant Gate was an inquisitor finding it while Beatrix was somewhere else.

And the door knew his hand. Whatever waited behind it, whatever the sealed armies were, whatever the burden was — it would open to *him.*

He looked north past the Widow's Tooth, into the blue haze beyond which the pass ran down and around and home, and he let himself imagine — just for a breath — that he could feel her there. Watching. Waiting for the signal they'd sworn they'd never need.

Below the camp, faint under the wind, he thought he heard it anyway: a birdcall, three notes, repeated.

*Nightingale.*

Not yet, her sleeping mouth had said.

But the night had other plans, and the thorn road was singing, and tomorrow they would leave his map behind forever.
