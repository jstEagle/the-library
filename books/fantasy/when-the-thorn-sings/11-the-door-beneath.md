## 11. The Door Beneath

**ELIO**

The dream stopped being a dream on the fourth day.

That was when the trail the expedition followed — Sarel Voss's trail, drawn from her inquisitor's maps and Kest's archives — bent away from the mountain, and Elio felt it like a hook behind his sternum. The pull went left. The column went right.

He had spent three days performing obedience. Marking waypoints. Reading the false passes off his own crown-commissioned map with a straight face while Voss watched him over the campfire, her gray eyes doing their patient arithmetic. He had even led them half a mile up a dead ravine and let them find nothing, so that his eventual discovery would look earned rather than guided.

But tonight the pull was no longer something he could argue with. It was a sound below hearing, a door knocking from the inside of his own ribs.

He waited for the second watch change. He knew the sentry rotations better than Voss thought he did — Beatrix had drilled him on smuggler habits until they lived in his hands. Then he took the codex, his satchel of inks and instruments, and the little lamp, and he walked out of camp into the dark like a man going to piss, casual as sin.

The Thornfells at night were not silent. They creaked. The frost worked at the stone all hours, prying, whispering, and somewhere below there was water moving through channels older than any kingdom. Elio followed the creaking down.

The pass Beatrix had shown him — the one that didn't exist on any map but hers — opened onto a shelf of rock above a hanging valley, and the valley was wrong. No trees grew in it. No snow lay in it, though snow rimmed every edge. And in its center, where a glacier's tongue should have been, the mountain had been carved.

Not built. Carved. A facade the height of ten men, cut directly into the living rock, wearing centuries of scree like a disguise. His lamp found edges: pilasters, a lintel, a relief so worn by weather it might have been decoration or might have been writing. Two great doors met in the middle, sealed by a seam so fine he couldn't have found it if he hadn't already known it was there.

Elio stood in the cold with his breath smoking and understood that every map he had ever loved was a child's drawing compared to this.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

He didn't turn around. He'd known she was coming since the third switchback — heard her the way you hear your own heartbeat, which is to say constantly and without alarm.

"I was," he said. "It woke me up."

Beatrix came to stand beside him, hood back, frost in her hair, six days of shadowing the expedition written into the lines of her face. She looked at the doors and then at him, and what crossed her expression wasn't wonder. It was grief arriving early, like a guest who knows the party will end badly.

"It's real," she said quietly. "Gods. I half hoped it was forgery all the way down."

"The codex?"

"Everything." She touched his sleeve. "Elio. If this is what I think it is —"

"Then it's what the book said." He turned to face her. In the lamplight her eyes were the brown of strong tea, and he wanted very badly to spend forty more years looking into them instead of whatever came next. "Bearer of the secret. Blood of the first wardens. I used to think prophecy was just history with better penmanship."

"And now?"

"Now I think my hands have been trying to tell me something for years." He lifted them — cartographer's hands, ink-stained, steady — and looked at them like tools belonging to someone else. "Every map I've ever drawn, I drew from here first." He tapped his chest. "I just thought it was memory. Some childhood thing. My mother singing songs nobody else knew. Words in a language the scholars couldn't place."

Beatrix went still in the particular way she went still before tasting poison — total attention, body braced.

"Elio," she said. "Don't touch it yet."

"Why not?"

"Because everything we've done for six years has worked because we moved *first*. Because someone always knows more than we do, and the only advantage we've ever had is a head start." Her breath clouded between them. "Once you open that door, we don't get a head start on anything ever again."

He wanted to tell her she was right. He was, in fact, almost certain she was right.

Instead he heard himself say: "It opened in my dream. Every night this month. And in the dream, Beatrix, there are armies behind it. Sleeping ones. Standing ones, ranks deep, in the dark, waiting for someone to either wake them or keep them asleep — and I am the only person alive who knows which hand does which."

Somewhere far below them, through a hundred fathoms of stone, something shifted. Not an earthquake. A settling. The sound a house makes when its owner comes home.

Beatrix closed her eyes. "Then we have minutes, not choices," she said. "Do it fast. Touch it, see what happens, and we go. We don't explore. We don't read inscriptions. We *leave*."

"*Leave* —"

"Voss has been counting heads at every watch change for two days. She's not suspicious yet, but she's *counting*, Elio. Counting is how it starts."

He kissed her then — quick, hard, tasting of frost and fear — because he was about to do the one thing she asked him not to do, and he wanted the taste of her to be the last ordinary thing.

Then he walked to the doors.

Up close, the carving resolved. The worn relief wasn't decoration. It was a procession — figures marching toward the seam, carrying something between them, and the something had been deliberately chiseled away by hands that wanted it forgotten. Above the lintel, in that language his mother had sung: a single word, repeated twice.

He knew it the way you know your own name.

*"Verdant."*

His palm rose without his permission.

The stone was warm.

Warm, in a hanging valley where frost ruled everything — warm like skin, like a fever, like a hand held too long. And under his palm the seam brightened, a hairline of green light leaking out like dawn under a door, and the light *recognized* him. There was no other word for it. It ran up his arm the way water runs up a wick, and it tasted — he had no better sense for it — it tasted like his mother's voice, like a lullaby sung in a tongue that predated the empire, like being *known*.

The doors did not swing. They *withdrew*, sliding into the rock with a groan that Elio felt in his teeth, exhaling air that hadn't moved in a century — air that smelled of green things. Growing things. Soil and sap and spring.

Beyond the threshold: darkness, and within the darkness, at the limit of the lamplight, the faintest suggestion of ranks. Standing figures, shoulder to shoulder, receding beyond sight. Still as furniture. Patient as geology.

Sealed armies. Exactly as dreamed. Exactly as written.

Elio stood on the threshold of the Verdant Gate with his hand still raised and the green light crawling lovingly up his veins, and he felt — for three full heartbeats, three of the purest heartbeats of his life — chosen. Found. *Home.*

Then he heard the boot scrape behind him, and the wonder curdled.

* * *

Sarel Voss stood twenty paces back with her lantern raised, alone, her two escort soldiers dismissed on some errand he would later realize she'd invented herself. Snow gathered on the shoulders of her gray traveling cloak. Her face, in the mixed light of green and flame, showed nothing at all.

"Cartographer," she said pleasantly. "You've been holding out on the crown."

Elio's mind, trained by six years of Beatrix's paranoia, did four things at once: cataloged the distance (twenty paces, uphill, her side), noted her weapon (a long knife, sheathed — inquisitors preferred knives, close work), registered the camp behind her (too far to raise anyone quickly, and she'd made sure of that), and located Beatrix (not visible; gods knew where; hopefully gone to ground).

"Lord Voss." He kept his voice level. "I could ask the same. You followed me."

"I follow everyone. That's the profession." She drifted closer, unhurried, studying the open doors with the frank appetite of a woman reading a rival's ledger. "Four days I've watched you lead us in circles. Very good circles. Convincing circles. And then tonight you sleepwalk out of camp like a man called home to dinner." She stopped at ten paces. "The emperor will want to know why."

"The emperor can read my report."

"The emperor," Voss said softly, "cannot touch what you're touching. Look at your hand, cartographer."

He looked. The green light still threaded his fingers, faint, faithful, and he understood with a lurch that it was visible — that it announced itself — that whatever he was, he now wore it where anyone could see.

"In the old texts," Voss continued, conversational, terrible, "the Gate answers only the warden line. Kest's archivists have hunted that bloodline for a decade. Married into families. Bought parish records. Burned villages that didn't cooperate." Her gray eyes lifted to his. "And all along it was hired help drawing our maps."

"Voss—"

"Don't." She raised one finger, almost gentle. "Don't lie to me. Lying is work, and we're both tired, and I'm offering you something better than lies." She nodded past him, into the dark, at the standing shadows. "Do you know what sleeps down there?"

"No."

"Liar," she said, without heat. "But you'll learn. Here is my offer, cartographer, and consider it carefully, because it expires when my patience does. Come to Kest willingly. Open what the emperor wants opened. Teach us the rite. In exchange, the woman you've been meeting on smuggler trails lives out her natural life in comfortable obscurity." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Yes. We know about her too. Six years of letters, cartographer. Six years of a taster playing courier. The palace has files thicker than your codex."

The ground tilted. That was the only way Elio could describe it — the whole world tilting, because if they knew about Beatrix, then the broth, the doses, the immunity, the entire architecture of their survival was already mapped by the enemy, and everything they'd built was a fortress with the blueprint in hostile hands.

"You're lying," he managed.

"About which part?" Voss smiled thinly. "That's the trouble with being us, isn't it? Everything we say might be strategy." She unclasped her lantern and set it on a rock between them, a small island of light in all that ancient dark. "Take the night to think. Dawn, you give me your answer. Refuse, and I write to Kestreach tonight, and what comes for you both won't be one polite inquisitor with a knife." She turned to go, then paused, her back to him, her voice dropping to something almost human. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I read the codex over your shoulder for six days. Whatever's behind that door, it chose you before you were born. Men have killed empires for less certainty than that." She resumed walking. "Sleep well, cartographer."

Elio stood alone at the threshold, hand burning green, watching her lantern bob away down the pass — and only when the dark swallowed her did he let himself shake.

* * *

**BEATRIX**

She reached the shelf of rock as Voss's lantern vanished below, and she arrived the way she arrived everywhere — too late, having counted every step of a journey that was always going to end one step short.

She had been fifty yards away. Fifty yards, flat against stone with her knife out, listening to an inquisitor offer her lover a bargain priced in her life. She could have put the knife in Voss's back. Fifty yards downhill, snow muffling everything, an easy throw, an easier approach —

Except Voss had walked out alone and come back alone, which meant soldiers somewhere above or behind, which meant killing her bought them a night and cost them everything. Except the doors were open and wouldn't stay shut on command. Except Elio's hand glowed like a struck match and there was no version of this where they simply walked away.

Too late. Always exactly, precisely too late. Six years of being early to everything — poisons, letters, exits — and here, at the center of it, late.

She stepped out of the shadows.

Elio spun, hand flaring green, and for one horrible instant she saw him ready to defend himself against *her*, and then his face broke and he crossed the snow to her in four strides and held her hard enough to bruise.

"They know," he said into her hair. "Beatrix, they know everything. The letters. The courier routes. Your immunity — Voss said the palace has files —"

"I heard." Her voice came out flat and calm, the kitchen voice, the professional voice, and she hated how steady it was when everything underneath was screaming. "Six years of work, mapped by Kest's archivists while we congratulated ourselves on our tradecraft."

"We run. Tonight. Now —"

"Where?" She pulled back to look at him. "They know the routes. They know the contacts. They know me, Elio. Running is what they've prepared for." She glanced past him at the doors — at the green seam, the standing dark, the smell of growing things pouring out of the world's oldest cellar. "And we can't leave this open. Anyone could find it. *Kest* could find it."

"I don't know how to close it."

"Then we learn faster than they can march."

They stood together in the hanging valley, two liars with one truth between them, the Gate breathing its green light against their backs. Far below, the expedition camp slept on, dreaming imperial dreams, one inquisitor among them already composing her report in her head — or not composing it, which was worse, which meant she was waiting, weighing, deciding what the Verdant Gate was worth and to whom.

Beatrix looked at the open doors and felt the future narrow to a corridor.

"Dawn," she said. "She gave you until dawn. So we have until dawn to decide what kind of lie we tell her — and Elio?" She turned to him, and her tea-brown eyes were hard as coin. "Whatever we decide, we decide it knowing the palace already sold us. The queen's files are in Kestreach. Which means when we go home, we aren't going home."

Below them, faint through miles of frozen air, a single bell began to ring in the expedition camp.

Not the watch bell. The alarm.

Someone had counted heads — and come up one inquisitor short of where she should be, or one cartographer long gone. Down in the dark behind the doors, so soft Elio felt it more than heard it, something ancient shifted its weight, and waited.
