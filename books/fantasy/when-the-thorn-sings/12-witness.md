## 12. Witness

Sarel Voss had learned long ago that the most useful lies were the ones you told yourself while looking directly at the truth.

She stood in the dark of the mountain's throat and watched a cartographer put his hand against a door that had not opened in a hundred years, and she felt her faith — the faith that had carried her from a fisher-town on the Greywater to the inquisitor's black coat — shift inside her like ballast in a storm.

The Gate opened.

Not all at once. It opened the way a decision opens: first a seam, then a certainty. Green light bled out along the stone veins of the door, and Elio Vane's hand was flat against it, fingers spread, and he was weeping without seeming to know it. The light climbed his wrist like water finding its level.

*Bearer*, said the codex in her memory. *The blood remembers what the empire burned.*

Voss did not move. She had been trained never to move first. Inquisitors of the Kestreach Order were instruments of patience; their whole art was standing still while other people revealed themselves. So she stood in the shadow where the passage bent, three men behind her with lanterns shuttered to slits, and she watched the light, and she did what she always did with wonder: she took its measure.

Wonder, properly weighed, was just information.

* * *

She had been nine years old when Aurel Kest came to Saltmere, the grey fishing town where the sea ate one house a winter and called it rent. She remembered it precisely because it was the first time she had seen a man make the truth obey him.

Saltmere had belonged, on paper, to no one — a freeholding, one of the last, its taxes paid to itself. Kest arrived with forty soldiers and no decree, only questions, and he asked them so gently that by the time the town understood what he was asking, they had already answered. He sat in the harbor tavern with the town elders and drank their thin beer and said, *Tell me who holds your loyalty,* and old Maren Ostrow said, *No one but ourselves,* and Kest smiled as if that were the loveliest thing he'd heard all year.

Then he burned the tax rolls. Not seized them — burned them, publicly, in a barrel, saying that a people's debts should die with the paper they were written on. The town cheered. Voss, nine years old, perched on a crate, cheered too.

It took her another ten years to understand what she'd watched. Kest hadn't freed Saltmere. He had made it *indebted* — to him personally, for the burning. Every favor creates a ledger. Every act of mercy is a hook baited with gratitude. The empire wasn't built on fear. Fear was cheap; every tyrant in history had fear. Kest built his empire on the far more durable material of *obligation*. People defended him because they believed they owed him their freedom, and no chain binds tighter than one a man has thanked you for.

At fifteen she wrote to the Kestreach Order unprompted, laying out this analysis in a child's careful hand. At seventeen they came for her. At nineteen she was the youngest sworn inquisitor in the Order's history, kneeling before the First Questioner while he touched her shoulder with an iron stylus and asked the question every initiate must answer:

*What is truth worth?*

And she had said — because she had worked it out years before, alone on the seawall watching the nets come in empty — *"Whatever someone will pay for it."*

He had laughed. She had passed.

* * *

Now she stood before an open door older than any empire, and the truth in front of her was priceless, and everyone who could pay was already bidding.

Elio Vane stepped back from the threshold. Behind him, through the widening seam, Voss saw what the century-old codex had only sketched: a hall. A vast green-lit vault of a hall, and within it —

She counted. She couldn't help counting; numbers steadied her. Ranks of shapes in the deep gloom, armored, mounted, standards hanging motionless in air that should have been moving. The sealed armies. The old war's unfinished business, locked behind treaty-stone by the free cities and the drowned kingdoms both, because neither side had been able to win and neither would stop.

They were still there. A thousand years, and they were still there.

"Close it," she said.

Her voice came out flat and quiet, and Elio turned as if she'd struck him. One of her men — Brannic, young, devout, the kind who thought the black coat meant something holy — shifted beside her. "Inquisitor—"

"I said close it." She walked forward into the green light, boots ringing on stone that hummed faintly, like a struck bell fading over centuries. Elio didn't move away from the door. Interesting. Most men, caught, would have fled or groveled. This one planted himself between her and the opening like a man guarding a hearth.

"Inquisitor Voss." His voice shook. Good. Honest fear she could work with. "You don't understand what—"

"I understand perfectly." She looked past him, into the hall, at the nearest of the sealed ranks — a rider whose armor was green-black bronze, whose horse's head was bowed as though asleep. As though *waiting*. "The Verdant Gate. The treaty-lock. The weapon your prophecy book says you're fated to carry." She let that last word sit between them. "I've read the codex, Master Vane. I read everything. That is rather the point of me."

"You'll report this to Kest," he said. Not a question. She admired that — he wasn't begging her to do otherwise. Begging wasted time and revealed weakness. He simply named the consequence, the way a man names weather coming over a ridge.

"Yes."

"And he'll open it."

She considered lying. It would have been easy; it was almost always easy. But she had learned something else in twenty years of questioning: a lie told to buy cooperation is a debt you owe, and she kept her ledgers clean. Besides — she wanted to see his face.

"He'll try," she said. "Yes."

Elio Vane closed his eyes. When he opened them again, something had settled in him — some weight accepted, some line drawn. She catalogued it the way she catalogued everything: *the bearer accepts the burden. Note it. It matters later.*

"Then I can't let you leave here," he said quietly.

Behind her, steel whispered from scabbards. Brannic and the others. Four blades against a mapmaker's soft hands.

"No," Voss agreed. "You can't."

* * *

Here is what Sarel Voss believed, and had believed since the barrel-fire at Saltmere: that order was the only kindness large enough to matter. Chaos killed more children than cruelty ever had — famine, rout, plague, the long lawless winters when the strong ate and the rest prayed. Every age of terror she'd studied began the same way: not with a tyrant, but with a vacuum. Kings dead, borders broken, truths multiplied until none of them held weight. The free cities called themselves free. She had walked their markets. Freedom there meant the freedom of whoever could afford the largest guard. The empire, whatever its sins, fed the provinces. The roads were safe. The coin was honest. Kest had ended a war that had eaten a generation.

That had been enough, for years. That had been her creed, worn smooth as a riverstone from handling.

But creeds are tools, and tools wear, and lately — she could name the day; it was the day she'd found the poisoner's ledger cross-referenced against a taster's dosing records — the riverstone had developed a flaw she kept putting her thumb against.

Aurel Kest wanted the Gate open.

She had known it since Kestreach, since the private audience where he'd unrolled Elio Vane's maps and run one finger down the blank spaces beneath the Thornfells and said, softly, the way he said everything that mattered: *There is something under my mountains, Inquisitor, sleeping. Empires are built by those who wake such things first.*

He had framed it as defense. The free cities would find the Gate eventually; better it be his. He had framed it as justice — the sealed armies had marched under free-city banners once; their descendants had no right to sleep behind a lock bought with other men's blood. Kest could frame anything. That was his genius and, Voss had begun to suspect, his rot. Because framing was not the same as weighing, and somewhere in thirty years of winning arguments, the emperor had stopped being able to lose one even privately. There was no one left in Kestreach who told him no. There was no one left who *could*.

An emperor who cannot hear no is not an emperor, Voss thought, watching the green light breathe in the dark hall. He is a fuse.

If the Gate opened, the sealed armies woke. If they woke, the old war resumed — and the old war had not ended in victory. It had ended in exhaustion and a lock. Both sides had spent themselves to the marrow. The armies behind this door were the last full strength either world had ever fielded, and whichever master held their leash held the continent.

Kest believed he would hold it. Of course he did. He had never lost.

But Voss had read the campaign histories the way the Order taught — not as glory but as risk assessment — and she knew what Kest refused to know: that waking a weapon is a wager, and wagers made by men who cannot conceive of losing are the ones that burn the world.

And beneath that calculation, deeper, where she rarely looked: the sealed armies had been locked away by *treaty*. By agreement. By the last moment in history when exhausted enemies had chosen a door over a grave. Everything since — Kest's empire, the free cities' pride, the whole grinding machinery of crowns and coats — had grown up in the shadow of that one mature decision, like weeds around a fallen column, none of them half so grand.

Maybe the door was wiser than everyone who wanted through it.

She put the thought away. Not destroyed — filed. Inquisitors did not destroy inconvenient thoughts. They archived them, dated and cross-referenced, for the day the archive became evidence.

* * *

"Stand down," she said.

Brannic stared at her. "Inquisitor, the Order's charge—"

"The Order's charge is the empire's security," Voss said, without turning, "and I am determining what secures it. Stand down, Sergeant. That is not a discussion."

Steel slid home. Elio Vane's shoulders dropped half an inch. She noted that too — relief, unguarded, immediate. An honest man. Useful. Dangerous, for exactly that reason; honest men made other men honest, and honesty was contagion in an empire built on framed truths.

She approached the Gate slowly. Up close, the stone was warm. That unsettled her more than the armies had — warmth meant something tended it, or something *was* it, and either way the categories she'd brought into these mountains were failing her one by one.

"Do you know how to close it?" she asked.

Elio hesitated. She watched the lie assemble behind his eyes and then, interestingly, get abandoned. "I think so. The rite is in the codex. Blood keyed to the old poisons — the ones used to seal it. But I don't—" He stopped.

"But you don't have that blood," Voss finished. "No. You have the key that opens. Someone else holds the key that shuts." She thought of the taster's records, the fortress-blood, the woman who drank poison nightly for six years and called it duty. Beatrix Marchetti, who watched dishes like a hawk and lied like a lady-in-waiting. The pieces had been assembling themselves for weeks, patient as sediment. *Someone else holds the key.* Oh, yes. She would bet her iron stylus on it.

"That's why you need him alive," Elio said, following her silence somewhere she hadn't invited him.

"That's why I need several things alive," Voss said. "Master Vane, I am going to say something once, and you will decide whether you heard it. Do you understand?"

He went very still. "Say it."

"Aurel Kest intends to open this Gate and aim what's behind it at the free cities. I intend to report that I found nothing but empty tunnels, because a weapon that wakes the world is worth more than my master's gratitude." She met his eyes and let him see — truly see, for the first time — that the black coat contained a mind, and the mind was not his enemy's, merely not yet his friend. "You are going to walk out of this mountain, return to the capital, draw your maps, and tell no one what stands behind this door. And in exchange, I am going to forget your face. For now. Leverage keeps, Master Vane. Bodies don't. Choose which of us proves right about the other."

"And if I refuse?" he said. "If I go to the queen? To the free cities?"

"Then I report everything tonight, and the empire comes for this door with ten thousand picks, and you will have traded the only quiet version of this story for the loud one." She stepped back from the warm stone. "I am offering you time. Time is the only currency anyone in this war actually has. Spend it well."

For a long moment the only sound was the Gate breathing its green light, and far below, faint as a heartbeat through floorboards, the stirring of things that had slept a hundred years and dreamed of marching.

Elio Vane looked at the door, and then at her, and she watched him arrive at the arithmetic she'd designed for him: a live secret was a movable secret, and a dead man carried nothing anywhere.

"I'll walk out," he said.

"Good." She turned to her men. "We found ruins. Old, minor, collapsed. The cartographer surveyed them; they were of no strategic value. You'll each swear to that in writing when we reach the valley, under seal." She paused. "Anyone who finds that oath burdens his conscience may bring it to me privately, and I will release him from it and from my service simultaneously. Are we understood?"

Four faces, four nods, four different qualities of fear. She catalogued each.

* * *

They camped that night above the treeline, and Voss sat awake with her back to cold rock, writing her true report — not the one she'd file, the one she'd keep — in cipher in the back of her prayer-book, where the Order never thought to look, because inquisitors were assumed to have no doubts and therefore needed no confessional pages.

*I have served the empire for twenty years,* she wrote. *Tonight I witnessed the empire's founding premise fail its own test. Kest would open the door. The door was closed by wiser men. Therefore the empire serves me, and not I it, and a servant who discovers this owes his master nothing further except the courtesy of timing.*

She looked up. Across the dying fire, Elio Vane lay staring at the stars, awake, guarding whatever lived behind his ribs now.

*The bearer lives,* she wrote. *As leverage. As insurance. As — I lack the word. Mirror, perhaps. I looked into the thing I am hunting and it had my face.*

Below them, miles down the black flank of the Thornfells, a single lamp moved along a trail no crown map showed — small, steady, climbing toward them through the dark.

Voss watched it for a long time, and smiled, and did not wake her men.
