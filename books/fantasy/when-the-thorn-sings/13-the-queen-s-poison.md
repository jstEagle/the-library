## 13. The Queen's Poison

The capital smelled different when you came back to it. That was the first thing Beatrix noticed, riding through the Saltgate with dust still on her boots and the Gate's name folded against her ribs like a knife she couldn't put down. Vhalenne always smelled of brine and tallow and the green rot of the harbor — but it had never before smelled like a held breath.

Curfew banners hung from the gatehouse. Soldiers in Kest's gray-and-gold checked carts with a boredom that was worse than zeal. Zeal could be bribed or outrun; boredom just waited for something to happen to.

She kept her hood up, her papers loose in her hand, her face arranged into the particular blankness of a woman who had been sent on an errand and wanted nothing more than for the errand to be over. It wasn't difficult. Six years of tasting for a queen had taught her that blankness was the most useful expression a body could wear.

The guard barely glanced at her seal. "Taster Marchetti. Her Majesty will be pleased. There's been trouble with the kitchens."

"There's always trouble with the kitchens," Beatrix said, and rode on.

* * *

The palace took her back the way a body takes back a swallowed thing — without comment, without welcome.

Her old rooms above the tasting chamber were exactly as she'd left them: the narrow bed, the ledger shelf, the row of stoppered vials she'd built one bitter drop at a time across six years. Wolfsbane. Arsenic in three gradations. A distillation of marsh-elder that had taken her a month of fevers to master. The little pharmacopoeia of a woman who had made her own blood into a locked door.

She stood in the doorway and felt, for the first time, like the door might have been built from the outside.

Later, she told herself. Later she would think about what Sarel Voss had seen at the Gate, about the ledger she'd left burning behind her, about Elio hidden in the Thornfells with a secret that could unmake an empire. Tonight there would be the evening service, and the evening service required nothing of her but the oldest discipline she knew: sip, wait, catalog, survive.

The kitchens were quieter than she remembered. Half the undercooks were gone — conscripted, fled, or simply not replaced — and the ones who remained worked with their heads down, the way people work when they've decided not to know things.

Master Corvel stood at the spice table, and Beatrix felt the wrongness of him before she could name it.

He'd been the palace's second poisoner since before her time — not a maker of toxins but a keeper of them, a dry precise man who catalogued the crown's arsenal the way a priest catalogues relics. He had never once, in six years, prepared a dish. Poisoners didn't cook. Poisoners watched cooks.

"Marchetti." He didn't look up from the scale he was balancing. "You're back."

"The queen summoned me."

"Mm." He weighed something invisible onto the pan, then swept it into a mortar. "The queen has been asking after you. Daily." Now he looked up, and his eyes were the color of watered milk. "She'll be glad to see you whole. The road is dangerous these days."

"It is."

"For some more than others." He tapped the mortar twice, a small dry sound, and turned back to his work. "The evening broth is nearly finished. You should rest first. You look tired."

"I'm never too tired to serve."

"No," Corvel said softly, to the spices rather than to her. "I don't imagine you are."

* * *

Queen Ilseva received her in the private solar, alone, which had never happened before. Six years, and every audience had included at minimum a lady-in-waiting, usually a steward, once a Kestish observer who had sat by the window pretending to read. Alone meant either trust or trap, and Beatrix had long ago stopped believing the queen trusted anyone.

"You went to the mountains." Ilseva stood at the window with her back turned. She was fifty-one and had been beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, and the years of Aurel Kest's slow strangulation of her kingdom had worn her down to the tang. "To the Thornfells. With the cartographer."

"I was sent to retrieve a runaway mapmaker, Majesty."

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip on ice. Ilseva turned. Her face was composed, but her hands, clasped at her waist, were white-knuckled. "Don't lie to me tonight, Beatrix. I haven't the strength left for it, and you haven't the need. I know where you went. I know what's under the mountains. I have known for longer than you would find comfortable."

Beatrix kept her breathing level. Below them, faintly, she could hear the kitchens beginning the evening service — the clatter, the calling of orders, the ordinary music of a court that believed it would see tomorrow.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

"I want you to listen." The queen crossed the room and, to Beatrix's astonishment, took her hands. They were cold. "When your predecessor died — do you remember? Old Marta, who choked at midwinter feast?"

"She was poisoned. I found the residue myself."

"You found what I asked you to find." Ilseva's grip tightened. "Marta drank what was meant for me because I switched the cups. I did it deliberately, Beatrix. She was dying anyway — the lung-rot — and Kest's envoy was watching, and if the cup had reached me untouched, the war would have started nine years early, before Vhalenne had a single ally. So I chose. And then I chose again, when I saw a kitchen girl who didn't flinch at the corpse. I chose you."

The floor seemed very slightly tilted. Beatrix heard herself say, "You built my immunity. The doses—"

"Were mine. Every one. Corvel kept the ledger; I set the ladder. Wolfsbane, arsenic, marsh-elder, the silverleaf compounds no one outside this palace has ever distilled." The queen's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Six years of small poisons, so that someday, someone could drink a large one and live. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I did not make you a taster. I made you a vessel. I made you the only person in Vhalenne who can stand at the Verdant Gate and do what must be done there."

The room went quiet in a way that rooms shouldn't. Somewhere below, a pot lid clattered, absurdly ordinary.

"You knew about the Gate."

"I knew there was a door my grandmother's grandmother sealed with blood, and that the prophecy named a vessel keyed to poison, and that when Kest came looking for it — as he has been looking for twenty years — I would need someone standing between him and it whose blood was a fortress." Ilseva finally let go of her hands. "I did not know it would be you. But I confess, Beatrix — I hoped. From the first day, when you sipped the hemlock broth and lied to my face about its taste, I hoped."

"And Elio?" The name came out rougher than she intended. "Was he also part of your design? His dreams, his bloodline, the codex with his name in it?"

"No." For the first time the queen looked tired — genuinely tired, the mask slipping off the bone. "That was the mountains' doing, or fate's, or the codex's. When I learned the bearer's name, I sent you after him because I thought you'd bring him to heel. Instead—" She gestured vaguely, helplessly. "Instead you fell in love with him, which I should have foreseen, since I engineered everything else about you except the one thing that mattered."

* * *

The evening service proceeded as services do: trays assembled, seals checked, the line of dishes carried up through the antechambers toward the queen's table. Beatrix walked beside them out of habit, though her mind was elsewhere — in a mountain pass, at a door of green stone, in a bed she'd shared three nights ago with a man whose name was inked in a century-old book.

Corvel met her at the solar doors with the tray.

"Evening broth," he said. "And the queen's cup." He lifted the small silver lid from the cup, and Beatrix looked down into wine the color of garnets, and every hair on her arms rose.

She had catalogued ten thousand poisons. She knew them the way a mother knows footsteps on stairs. This one she had never tasted — but she recognized its architecture instantly, the way you recognize a face in a stranger's child. Marsh-elder base. Silverleaf alkaloid, the compound only Corvel could distill. And beneath both, threaded through them like a signature, something new — something built, patiently, over months, against a specific blood. Against hers. The keyed poison. The one the ledger had promised. The one meant to kill the fortress by learning its walls.

It sat in the queen's cup, and it was not aimed at the queen at all.

Because the queen's cup was the one cup in Vhalenne that Beatrix Marchetti always, always tasted from.

"Go on," Corvel said quietly. Not unkindly. That was the horror of it — he said it the way a doctor says *this won't hurt*, knowing perfectly well that it will.

Behind him, down the corridor, two guards she didn't recognize stood with their faces carefully forward. Watching. This was the test, then. Or the execution. Either way, refusing the cup announced that she knew; drinking it announced whatever it announced.

*Beatrix.* Elio's voice in her memory, laughing over a smuggler's fire. *You always taste like you're daring the world to try something.*

She picked up the cup.

The wine was smooth going down, and for three heartbeats nothing happened, and she allowed herself one wild flicker of hope that she'd misread it.

Then the marsh-elder opened like a flower in her chest.

It was worse than any fever she'd trained through, worse than the month she'd spent convulsing over the silverleaf distillate, because those had been doses measured to teach and this had been measured to end. Her vision doubled, then whited. Her knees hit stone. Distantly she registered the cup rolling away, red spreading across pale flagstones like a wound in the palace itself, and Corvel crouching beside her saying, "Hold. Hold. Breathe through it," in the tone of a man timing a patient rather than mourning one.

*They're counting,* she thought, from somewhere far inside the fire. *They're counting how long I last.*

So she lasted. She dragged every year of stubbornness up out of her marrow — the wolfsbane winters, the arsenic summers, the girl who had decided that if her life belonged to the crown, her blood would belong to her — and she breathed, and breathed, and the fire crested, and broke, and receded the way a tide recedes, leaving her shaking on the stones with her heart stuttering but beating, beating, beating.

Silence in the corridor. Then Corvel straightened and offered his hand, and she took it, because pride was a luxury and her legs were not currently hers.

"Eleven minutes to steady pulse," he said, consulting a watch. "Better than projected."

"How long," she rasped, "have they known?"

"About the vessel?" He closed the watch. "Since the beginning. Since her Majesty began building you." He paused, and something almost human moved behind the milky eyes. "The keyed compound was not the queen's order, if that matters to you. It came down from Kestreach eight months ago. Specifications for a poison calibrated to your recorded tolerances — all of them, every dose in the ledger. Someone in this palace has been selling your history south for years." He glanced at the solar doors. "Someone close."

The doors opened. Queen Ilseva stood in the gap, alone, her face gray, and Beatrix understood in one cold wash that the queen had known this cup was coming — had perhaps even approved its arrival as the price of her own survival — and had chosen to learn the result from the corridor rather than warn her.

Every allegiance is a debt, Beatrix thought. And debts get called.

"Ilseva—" she began.

"*Your Majesty*," the queen whispered, glancing at the guards, at Corvel, at the wine-dark stain on the stones. "In front of them, always *Your Majesty*." She stepped closer, and her voice frayed to a thread only Beatrix could hear. "They'll report that the taster survived the queen's poisoned cup — that's the story now, it's already moving through the palace like blood through water, and I cannot stop it. Kest will know what you are. He may already have known. Which means you have days, Beatrix. Perhaps hours. When the next cup comes, it will come with soldiers behind it."

"Then give me a reason to stay," Beatrix said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, scraped raw and steadier than she felt. "Tell me you didn't let them pour that cup. Tell me the debt runs both ways."

Ilseva's eyes filled, and Beatrix realized with a distant surprise that the tears were real. "I begged them to wait until after the coronation delegation leaves. That's all I could buy you. One week." She gripped Beatrix's wrist hard enough to bruise. "Run. Take the cartographer and run, and when they ask me where you've gone I will lie to Aurel Kest's face, because it's the only thing I have ever given anyone in this palace freely. But hear me—" her voice dropped to ash, "—the Gate must not open. Whatever he offers you, whoever asks — the sealed armies stay in the dark. Swear it on the only thing you love."

Down the corridor, the guards shifted. Corvel studied his watch. Somewhere beyond the walls, the city settled into curfew, and beyond the city, in the high passes, a man with a cartographer's hands was waiting for her with no idea what had just been poured in his name.

"One week," Beatrix said. And turned toward the stairs, already counting the favors she had left to call in, and how many of them would cost her the only secret she had left to sell.
