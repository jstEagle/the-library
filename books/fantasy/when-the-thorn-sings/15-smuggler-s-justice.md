## 15. Smuggler's Justice

The smugglers of the Thornfells had a saying: *the mountain keeps no ledgers.* It was a lie they told each other around cookfires and in the backs of wagons, a comforting fiction that debts could dissolve like breath in cold air. Beatrix knew better. She had spent six years building her ledger with them — one vial of contraband antidote here, one sealed letter there, one night spent sitting upright in a waystation with a knife across her knees while a stranger's cargo slept three feet from her hands. Every favor was written down somewhere, even if only in the memory of a woman whose blood remembered every poison ever poured into it.

Tonight she intended to spend all of it.

She crossed into the Thornfells through the Hollow Stair, the pass she'd first shown Elio, moving by moonless dark with the codex's warnings ringing in her skull. The keyed poison had taught her the shape of the court's knowledge. The queen's trembling apology had taught her the shape of its mercy, which was to say its absence. There was nothing left in Vhalenne for her but a title on a register and a body someone was patiently learning how to kill.

Elio was hidden two passes deeper, in a shepherd's bothy that appeared on no map he had ever drawn. He had wanted to come with her. She had kissed him and lied — *stay, rest, I'll be back before dawn* — because whatever happened tonight, he would only be a liability at the negotiating table. A branded man. A bearer. The mountains were old-fashioned about their debts, but they were older still about omens.

The meeting place was a charcoal-burner's clearing where three trails knotted together, marked by a birch tree stripped of bark in a spiral. Someone had hung a lantern in its branches, shuttered to a single blade of light. Beatrix stood beneath it and waited, hands empty, sleeves loose, every concealed blade left behind on purpose. Among smugglers, an armed arrival was not protection. It was punctuation — and it usually ended a sentence rather than beginning one.

They came out of the dark the way they always did, as if the trees had been holding people all along and finally exhaled. Seven of them. She knew five faces. The sixth belonged to Maren Kostiva, who ran the salt road and owed Beatrix for the winter three years past when Beatrix had drunk a slow arsenic meant for her cousin and kept walking afterward, smiling. The seventh was young, armed, and nervous, which made him the most dangerous thing in the clearing.

"Six years," Maren said. No greeting. Smugglers didn't waste words on greetings; greetings cost candle-oil.

"Six years," Beatrix agreed. "Eleven favors outstanding. Two of them life-debts."

"I count nine."

"Then your ledger's shorter than mine, and mine has names attached." She let the silence do its work. "You can check with Danya at Greywater. With the ferryman at Sorrel Ford. With the widow Hesse, if she's still breathing."

Maren's mouth twitched. "Hesse is breathing. Barely. She says you saved her sons from the press-gangs and never asked coin."

"She asked me to say that?"

"No." Maren stepped into the lantern light. Up close she looked older than her reputation — grey coming in at the temples, a scar Beatrix didn't remember splitting her chin. The mountains collected tolls from everyone. "So. The taster wants to buy something big enough to spend eleven favors on. Or nine. Speak it."

"A crossing. One man. All the way through, no relay points, no one knows the destination but the guide. Out of the mountains entirely, to the free cities."

"That's not a favor." Maren's voice flattened. "That's a caravan. That's papers, that's bribes at three checkpoints, that's men who don't come home if it goes wrong. You know what's marching up the salt road? Kest's banners. Whole columns. Whatever your man is carrying, half the empire is looking for it."

"He isn't carrying anything."

"Then why is his name burned into proclamation posts from here to the capital?" Maren let the question hang. "Bearer, they're calling him. My grandmother used that word. It wasn't a compliment in her stories either."

Beatrix had prepared for this — prepared for greed, for fear, for refusal — but not quite for the steadiness of Maren's contempt, and it took her a moment to find her footing. "It doesn't matter what he carries."

"It always matters." The young one spoke for the first time, voice cracking on the second word. Maren raised a hand without looking at him and he went quiet, but the damage was done; Beatrix saw the flicker pass through the other faces. They had all heard the proclamations. They had all done the arithmetic of what Kest paid for a name.

"Milo thinks we should sell you," Maren said pleasantly. "I think Milo should be quiet. But I'd be lying if I said the thought hadn't crossed my mind wearing very expensive boots." She circled Beatrix slowly, boots crunching on charred ground. "Here's my problem, taster. Everything you've done for us, you did while wearing palace livery. Your blood is crown property. Your letters moved under a royal seal forged or otherwise. Six years of favors, and every single one of them bought by a woman who belongs to Queen Ilseva — who, word travels fast, is herself belonging to Kest these days. So when you come asking us to move a hunted man against the empire, I have to ask: whose errand is this really? Because if it's hers, then you're asking us to carry our own heads to the block and thank the headsman for the ride."

*There it is.* Beatrix breathed out slowly. This was the real price, and it had never been going to be paid in silver.

"Not hers," Beatrix said. "Not anymore."

"Words."

"I burned my credentials."

"I heard. I also heard the queen begged your silence instead of your head, which means you're still useful to her, which means you're still hers whether you've admitted it or not." Maren stopped circling, directly in front of her now, close enough that Beatrix could smell woodsmoke and pine tar. "You want to know what the Thornfells actually are, taster? Not passes. Not roads. We are everyone the crowns threw away. Press-gang runners. Tax-fleers. Heretics. Bastards of kings who didn't send for us. Every soul on these trails is someone an empire decided didn't count. And you — " she tapped Beatrix's sternum with one scarred finger — "you counted. For six years. You drank poison so a queen could eat safe. You're the best-loyaled woman in Vhalenne, and you come down here with your ledger thinking it buys you a place among people who were never allowed to have one."

The words landed truer than any blade could have. Beatrix let them.

"You're right," she said. "Every favor in that ledger was spent by a servant. So tear the page out."

Maren's eyebrows rose a fraction.

"I'm not here as the queen's taster. I'm not here as anyone's anything. Here's what I have instead." She held up one hand and counted. "One: I know every pass your network uses and three it doesn't, and I'll die before Kest's inquisitors learn them from me — you've seen my blood hold, Maren, you watched me drink arsenic at Greywater and ask for seconds. Two: the man I'm asking you to move is the only person alive who can close what's opening under the Thornfells, and if Kest reaches it first, there will be no salt road, no smuggling, no mountains worth hiding in. There will be soldiers from one end of the range to the other and free cities burning behind them. Three—" her voice caught, and she let it, because among thrown-away people a lie of composure was worth less than a truth that trembled — "three: he is the person I love, and I am choosing him over every crown that ever claimed me. That's my offer. It's not a purchase. It's a defection."

The clearing went very still. Somewhere upslope, wind worked through the pines, and beneath it — faint, absurd — Beatrix thought she heard something almost like singing, the high thin whistle of wind through hollow birch, and refused to give it a name.

Maren looked at her for a long time. Then she laughed, short and hard, and turned to the others. "Well. There it is. Six years of watching her, and it turns out she's one of us after all. Just late."

"We want terms," said a broad woman near the rear — Petra, who ran the river fork. "Favors are one thing. Defection is another. What does she bring us besides her famous throat?"

"The Gate," Beatrix said.

The name dropped into the clearing like a stone into a well. Even Milo stopped fidgeting.

"You've heard the stories," she went on. "Everyone in these mountains has heard the stories — the door beneath, the treaty-lock, the thing the old kings sealed and the new maps pretend is a cave-in. I can tell you where it is. Exactly. To the pace and the bearing. That's the price you asked for, and yes, I know it's the price Kest would pay a kingdom for."

"And once we know it?" Maren asked softly. "What stops us selling it ourselves?"

"Nothing. Except that you've spent your whole lives keeping things out of empires' hands, and this is the largest thing there is." Beatrix met her eyes. "And except me. I'll swear it however your network swears anything — blood, salt, whatever old words you use. My loyalty to no crown. To the trails, and the people on them. That's the oath you actually want. You know it is. Anyone can promise a location. I'm promising myself."

Maren was quiet long enough that the lantern guttered once in the wind. When she spoke again, it was to the group, not to Beatrix, and her tone shifted into something formal, older, cadenced like a litany learned in childhood.

"The mountain keeps no ledgers," she said, "but we keep them for the mountain. Beatrix Marchetti, called the Taster, called Ironblood by the Greywater folk: you come to us crownless and asking kinship. Kinship is given, not bought — so it will be given, and witnessed, and it cannot be taken back by you or by us, living or dead, tasted or untasted." She drew a knife. Not threateningly; ceremonially, turning it flat across her palm. "Salt and silverleaf, the old way. Do you take the trails as your country and the thrown-away as your people?"

Beatrix reached out and pressed her palm flat against the flat of the blade. Cold iron, an edge she trusted more than any throne.

"I take the trails as my country and the thrown-away as my people. My loyalty to no crown. My blood to whoever bleeds beside me."

"Spoken," Maren said, and the others echoed it — seven voices, low, and the wind took the words up through the birches as if delivering them somewhere for filing. Then the ceremony ended the way smuggler ceremonies did: Maren sheathed the knife, and everyone started talking logistics at once.

It took two hours. Petra would run the false trail south toward Sorrel Ford, loud and visible, drawing inquisitor eyes. Milo — redeemed, chastened, apparently an excellent climber — would lead the actual party over the Shale Teeth, the route so brutal no soldier had bothered garrisoning it since the war. Papers would be forged in Greywater by a man who owed Maren a debt so old it predated the usurpation. And the Gate's location, Beatrix gave them piece by piece, bearings recited from memory while Maren scratched them onto waxed slate with a stylus, checking each figure twice.

When it was done, Maren dismissed the others and stood alone with Beatrix under the spiral-barked birch.

"One more thing," Maren said. "Kinship means I can say this plainly. The man you love — the bearer, the door-opener, whatever he is — moving him keeps him alive. It doesn't keep him safe. Nothing in these mountains will keep that boy safe until whatever's under them is settled. You know that. You're planning around it. I saw your face while you gave me those bearings; you weren't giving away a secret, you were laying a trail for yourself." She paused. "So I'll tell you what an old smuggler tells family: don't make yourself the map, girl. Maps get folded. Get yourself out too, when it's done."

"I intend to."

"Liar," Maren said fondly. "But you're our liar now. Go home — go to wherever you're keeping him. Party moves in four nights. Keep him off the roads, keep his face out of firelight, and if inquisitors come early—" she shrugged, and the gesture contained an entire philosophy of survival— "well. You've drunk worse than what we'd give you to handle it."

Beatrix walked out of the clearing the way she had come, alone, unarmed, and lighter than she had felt in six years. Behind her the lantern went dark. Ahead the Thornfells rose black against the stars, and the wind moved through them in that thin, singing register she could no longer pretend was only wind.

Four nights. Then the mountains would carry Elio out of the empire's reach — and carry her, in the opposite direction, toward the one door everyone was willing to kill over.

She had sworn her loyalty to no crown. It remained to be seen whether the mountain would accept it, or whether it, too, kept accounts of its own.

* * *

The bothy was dark when she reached it, but the signal stone was turned — the flat side out, *all well* — and she smelled woodsmoke banked low, and under it, rosemary. Elio was cooking. Of course he was cooking; the man faced exile and imperial death squads and his answer was to make soup.

He looked up when she came in, and whatever was in her face must have shown in the firelight, because he set down the spoon before he asked.

"They said yes."

"They said yes." She let him pull her into the warmth of him, cheek against his chest, and felt the day's tension begin to leave her body one muscle at a time. "Four nights. Over the Shale Teeth. A boy named Milo leads you — trust him, he's terrified of you and that makes him careful."

Elio laughed into her hair, then sobered. "And you? What did it cost?"

Beatrix closed her eyes. Outside, far off, the thorns sang.

"A page from the ledger," she said. "And everything after it."
