## 16. The Long Night of Vhalenne

**Beatrix**

The curfew bell rang at dusk, and the city answered with silence.

From the kitchen window Beatrix watched the last of the lamplighters hurry home, their flames swallowed one by one behind shutters. Six years she had stood at this window. Six years of tasting death in small measures, of learning every corridor and servant's tread, and never once had the palace felt like this — like a held breath.

Kest's armies had crossed the Serein two days ago. Not marching on Vhalenne's capital — marching past it, south toward the free cities, a hundred thousand boots drumming the lowland roads. The queen had received the news at breakfast without setting down her cup. That was the first thing Beatrix had tasted wrong: not the food, but Ilseva's calm. Grief has a flavor. So does complicity.

Now the kitchens were empty except for her and the banked ovens, and the silence had teeth.

She was cataloging the night's stores — salted eel from the delta, honey that smelled faintly of smoke because everything did now, the city burning its documents in courtyards — when the side door opened and Mistress Corvel entered with a lantern and two guards.

Corvel was the keeper of keys, the woman who signed for every lock in the palace. She had never once come to the kitchens after dark.

"Her Majesty requires you," Corvel said.

"At this hour?"

"Her Majesty requires many things at this hour." The guards flanked Beatrix without touching her, which was worse than touching. "Bring your cloak."

They walked the long gallery where the old kings hung in oil and gilt, faces Beatrix had memorized out of boredom during slow tastings. Kest's portrait had been added last year, hung slightly higher than the rest, as if proximity were an honor rather than an insult. His painted eyes followed her the way his soldiers' boots followed the roads south.

The queen's private study smelled of beeswax and something sharper underneath — ink burned fresh, or paper burned fresher. Ilseva stood at the window in a dressing gown the color of drowned things, watching the city go dark below her.

"You sent for me," Beatrix said.

"I did." Ilseva didn't turn. "Lock it."

The guards withdrew. The door clicked. Beatrix counted the seconds between the click and the queen's next word — four — and understood that whatever came next had been rehearsed alone, in this room, for longer than four seconds. Longer than four days.

"Do you know what they're saying in the wine shops?" Ilseva said. "Before the curfew emptied them. They're saying I opened the Serein bridges to Kest's engineers. That the flood defenses failed because someone gave him the survey charts. That I—" she laughed, a small dry sound with no floor beneath it, "—that I sold the free cities the way a merchant sells grain. Cheap, and before the famine, so the buyers think themselves clever."

"Are they saying true things?"

The queen turned then. Up close, Ilseva looked older than her thirty-one years, older than her mother had at fifty. There were shadows under her eyes like bruises pressed into wax.

"They're saying true things," she said quietly. "And you know it, because you taste my cups, Beatrix, and you have known since the envoy's dinner that something in this palace turned rotten. You've been carrying letters out through the Thornfells for six years. Did you think I didn't know? Did you think a woman can be poisoned into wisdom for a decade and not learn to read what passes through her own house?"

The floor did not move, but Beatrix felt it tilt. Every careful route. Every smuggler who owed her, every sealed packet slipped into flour sacks and map tubes. She had built her double life on the assumption that power was blind to anything smaller than armies.

"And you let me continue," Beatrix said.

"I let you continue because your letters went to people I needed placated, and because a taster who lies is more useful than a taster who doesn't." Ilseva crossed to her desk and lifted a single sheet of paper. It bore no seal. "But tonight the arithmetic changed. Kest's marshal sent terms under a parley flag. One page. One demand." She set the paper down and slid it across the desk with two fingers, the way one slides a dish that might be poisoned.

*Deliver the cartographer Elio Vane to the vanguard at the Serein crossing within three days.*

"The bearer," Beatrix said. Her voice came out level. She had trained it to, in rooms full of poison.

"The bearer." Ilseva studied her face the way Beatrix studied broth — looking for what floated beneath. "You love him. Don't perform surprise; it's unbecoming in both of us. Yes. I've known that too. I know everything that happens in my house, even the parts I pretend are beneath notice." She sat, suddenly, as if the standing had cost her something. "Here is what I cannot make you understand until you sit in this chair someday, if you ever do: I did not choose Kest. Kest chose me — bought me, blackmailed me, married my fears to his ambitions — and by the time I understood what I'd signed, the signature was already history. Now the free cities will burn whether or not I weep about it. The only question left to me is whether Vhalenne burns too."

"So you'll give him to the vanguard."

"So I will give *you* the order," Ilseva said, "and see what kind of woman six years of poison made. Bring me Elio Vane. Do that, and when the empire settles over these mountains like weather, there will be a place in it for you. Refuse—" she spread her hands, an elegant little gesture of helplessness that Beatrix recognized as the most honest thing the queen had done all evening, "—and I will be forced to find another hand to do it. A less gentle hand. And you will watch it done, because you will still be alive, because you are very difficult to kill. I'm told that on good authority."

Silence. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere below, a dog barked once against the curfew and was silenced.

Beatrix thought of Elio's hands stained with ink, drawing borders he despised. Thought of the codex with its wet century-old ink. Thought of the Gate standing open somewhere beneath the Thornfells, holding back armies older than any of the crowns squabbling over them, and how every man who wanted it open wore silk.

"You knew my blood," she said. "All along. The keyed poison. The ledger. You let them build a poison shaped to me and served it through your own cup."

"I permitted it," Ilseva said, "because refusing would have told Kest's people you mattered more than a taster should. Sometimes protection looks like permission, Beatrix. That is the whole education of a throne." She rose, smoothed her gown, and became once more the woman from the coins. "Three days. Bring him to the crossing, or bring me your resignation and your silence, and I will assume the worst of you. Dismissed."

Beatrix bowed — the precise depth, the exact angle, six years of muscle memory — and walked out of the study without letting her spine bend.

In the gallery, Kest's painted eyes watched her pass.

She did not look back at them. But she thought: *I am already gone.* She had been gone since the moment the queen said *bring me Elio Vane* and something in Beatrix's chest closed like a door, quietly, permanently, the way a fortress closes.

She just hadn't yet lit the fire that would prove it.

**Elio**

Elio heard the curfew bell and kept working.

There was something obscene about it — hunched over his drafting table by candlelight, inking coastlines while the city outside locked itself against its own king — but the maps wouldn't draw themselves, and the free cities would need accurate charts of the delta channels if anyone was going to move troops, or refugees, or a certain smuggled cartographer, through what was coming.

He had been redrawing the Thornfells again. Not the crown version — the true one, with all three passes restored, the ones he'd omitted from the official survey because only Beatrix knew them and some knowledge should stay in trustworthy hands. He worked small, in the margins of a larger sheet, so that anyone glancing in would see only mountains being dutifully rendered.

A knock. Three soft raps — the rhythm Beatrix used, the one that meant *alone*.

He opened the door and she slipped in wearing her cloak up, and the sight of her hit him the way it always did, sideways, in the ribs. But something was wrong with her face. She was holding herself the way she held a cup she suspected.

"You've heard," he said.

"I've heard." She pulled back her hood. Candlelight caught the gray at her temple — new, he realized, since autumn. Poison-gray, she'd called it once, laughing. "Elio. The queen summoned me. She knows everything — the letters, us, all of it. And Kest has offered terms." She laid the folded paper on his drafting table, next to the true map of the mountains, and he read it twice while his heartbeat climbed into his throat.

*Deliver the cartographer.*

"They want the bearer," Elio said. "Not dead. Delivered."

"Alive. Because a dead bearer can't open the Gate, and Kest doesn't want it closed." She sank onto the stool across from him, elbows on knees, the posture she took when the truth required sitting down. "Ilseva gave me three days. Hand you over, or she finds another hand to do it. And here's the part that should frighten you more than it frightened me: she wasn't threatening. She was grieving. She thinks she's already lost, and she's just choosing which pieces survive."

Elio looked at the map. At the three passes drawn in his own careful hand, the secret architecture of their whole life together — concealment as tenderness, tenderness as concealment. "Then we run tonight. Through the high pass, down to the delta road—"

"Every road is watched. Kest's marshals bought half the garrison before the curfew was declared; the other half is pretending not to notice which half took the money." She shook her head slowly. "Running makes us fugitives. Fugitives get hunted. Hunted men get caught, and caught bearers get opened like letters." Her eyes came up to his. "There's one way out that isn't running. But it costs everything I am, and I need you to hear it before I decide, because I decided the moment she said your name, and I don't trust decisions I make that fast."

"Tell me."

"The smugglers will move you. All the way to the free cities, safe, through passes no army can follow. But their price is the Gate's location, and my oath — sworn to no crown, to no queen, to nothing but the network. My credentials, my position, my name in the palace rolls. Everything six years built." She paused. "And it means I die."

The candle flame leaned in a draft that hadn't reached him.

"Not truly," she said quickly, seeing his face. "On paper. The taster Beatrix Marchetti has to stop existing, or the queen hunts her and the inquisitors use her to reach you. But the woman who walks out of the Thornfells with you—" she smiled, and it cracked in the middle, grief and freedom fighting for the same ground, "—she gets to be someone new. Someone who never tastes another cup meant for another person's survival. Someone whose only duty is a choice."

Elio stood. Crossed the small room. Took her hands, ink-stained and steady, and found them trembling only now, only here, only for him.

"You understand," he said, "that if you burn your life down for mine, I will spend the rest of mine making sure it was worth the ash."

"That's the vow?" she said. "I've heard better drafted."

"It's the honest version. The pretty one comes later, somewhere with better light."

She laughed — wet, real — and then straightened, and he watched her gather herself the way she gathered herself before tasting, all that fear compressed into competence, except this time there was no bitterness to swallow. This time the poison was her own name.

"Then don't watch," she said. "It's easier if you don't watch."

* * *

**Beatrix**

She did not go to the credential office. There was no need; she carried everything that defined her in a flat tin case she'd kept for six years — her commission signed in Ilseva's hand, her seal, the ledger pages listing every poison her body had survived, dated and dosed like a physician's record of a war fought entirely inside her.

The courtyard behind the taster's quarters had a brazier. It was meant for warmth on cold mornings, for the servants who waited on deliveries. Tonight it was empty, and the coals from the evening shift still glowed.

Beatrix stood before it with the tin case in both hands.

Behind her, at a respectful distance, Elio waited with two packed satchels and the codex wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. The curfew silence pressed down on the palace like snowfall. Somewhere beyond the walls, south, the empire marched on the free cities, and above the mountains a Gate stood open in the dark, and none of it — not the armies, not the prophecy, not the thrones — could reach this small square of flagstones where a woman was deciding.

She opened the case. The commission on top, Ilseva's signature elegant even in treachery. Beneath it, the seal of the royal household, warm wax gone cool. Beneath that, the ledger. Six years. She remembered each entry the way other women remembered birthdays. Wolfsbane, aged nineteen, half a dram. The river-toxin that had put her in bed for nine days. The slow arsenic of her first winter, building her blood's walls grain by grain, brick by brick, until she was a fortress no cup could breach — and a prisoner no wall could free.

*You are very difficult to kill,* the queen had said, offering it as a threat.

Tonight it was a key.

"You could keep the ledger," Elio said softly. "It's proof. Of what they did."

"It's proof of what I survived. Different thing." She weighed the tin in her palms one last time. "Proof belongs to the person who paid for it. And I'm done paying."

She tipped the case over the coals.

Paper catches slowly at first, then all at once — the commission curling, Ilseva's signature blistering and going brown, then black, then nothing. The seal softened, slumped, ran like a wound. The ledger burned longest, page after page of dates and doses, six years of small stubborn acts of survival feeding a fire too small to warm anyone. Sparks climbed the dark. Beatrix watched every one of them, and felt — she searched for the word and found it, astonishingly —

*Lighter.*

Not free, exactly. Freedom was a country she hadn't entered yet. But lighter, the way a courier feels setting down a load at journey's end, muscles remembering their own weight.

Behind her, Elio exhaled.

"There," she said, to the ashes, to the queen asleep upstairs behind locked doors, to the empire grinding south. "No taster. No vessel. No one left in this palace for you to command."

The brazier ticked. The last page whitened and lifted on the heat and was gone.

When she turned, Elio held out his hand, and she took it, and together they walked out through the kitchen garden where the herb beds slept under frost, past the wall she'd climbed a hundred times on errands no crown authorized, into the streets of a city under curfew where every shadow might be an informer and every bell might be the end of everything.

At the mouth of Tanner's Row, a lantern blinked twice — green glass, then dark, then green. The smugglers' signal. *Come. Now. Quietly.*

Beatrix blinked back the answer with her own shuttered light, once, twice, and thought of the queen waking tomorrow to an empty room and a cold brazier and a choice already made for her.

Let history call it treason. Let the chronicles write *betrayal* in whichever hand held the pen. She had spent six years swallowing death for strangers, and she had exactly one loyalty left, and it was walking beside her with a forbidden book against his heart and the whole dark mountain ahead.

The green lantern swung forward, leading them down into the sleeping city, toward the passes, toward the free cities, toward whatever remained of the world after crowns.

And far behind them, faint through the curfew stillness, the palace bell began to ring — not the curfew pattern, not the alarm, but something older, a rhythm Beatrix had never heard in six years within those walls.

Elio stopped. Listened. Went pale in the lantern light.

"Beatrix," he said. "That's the bell from the codex. The one that rings when the Gate is found."
