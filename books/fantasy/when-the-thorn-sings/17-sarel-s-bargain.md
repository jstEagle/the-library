## 17. Sarel's Bargain

Sarel Voss had learned early that the best place to conduct treason was a room where treason was expected of everyone.

The safehouse was a tannery loft in the Salt Quarter, three streets from the river, chosen because the smell of lye and rotting hide made even inquisitors reluctant to linger. She had arrived first — she always arrived first; arriving second meant surrendering the shape of a conversation before it began — and spent forty minutes arranging it. One lamp, low. Two chairs, angled so that neither woman would have to look directly at the other unless she chose to. A decanter of wine that Voss had tasted herself, in front of the vintner's boy, with theatrical care.

She did not expect Beatrix Marchetti to drink it anyway without hesitation. But the gesture mattered. Everything mattered. That was the whole disease of espionage: you could not pour water for a guest without it becoming a message.

Below her, the tannery floor was silent. The workers had been paid to stay away until dawn. Above her, pigeons muttered in the eaves like old men disagreeing about weather. And somewhere across the city, Kest's armies were counting their days to the free cities, and Queen Ilseva was learning what it cost to be a collaborator when your collaborator stopped needing you.

Voss sat, folded her hands, and waited for the most dangerous woman in Vhalenne to come up her stairs.

* * *

Beatrix came the way Voss had known she would — through the window, not the door. She dropped from the roof beam into the lamplight without a sound, which should not have been possible for a woman carrying the weight of a burned life on her back, and straightened slowly, hands visible, empty.

"You're alone," Beatrix said. Not a question. She'd counted heartbeats or breath or some smuggler's arithmetic of the dark.

"I'm alone."

"That's what an ambush would say."

"Yes," Voss agreed. "It is." She nudged the second chair out from the table with one boot. "Sit down, taster. Or don't. I've been sitting in this chair for an hour and I can tell you it's no great honor."

Beatrix didn't sit. She never would, Voss suspected; standing people could leave, and leaving was the only luxury either of them had left. "You killed my contact at the fish market."

"No."

"He was found with his throat—"

"Cut by my own knife, yes, I know. I put the body there myself." Voss let that settle, watched Beatrix's eyes narrow into something cold and quick as a scale-weight. "He was selling you, Marchetti. To Kest's quartermaster corps, through a broker in Gildport. He'd already sold the shape of your network once. I bought his ledger before he could sell the rest. Then I closed his account." She lifted one shoulder. "You're welcome. You may thank me whenever the shock wears off."

"And I'm supposed to believe the inquisitor who watched Elio open the Gate — who reported everything to her emperor — is now murdering informants to protect me?"

"Believe whatever keeps you alive." Voss poured two cups of wine, pushed one across the table, and drank from her own first, again, deliberately. "But believe this much: if I wanted you dead, you'd be dead. If I wanted Elio Vane dead, he'd be dead. I had him in my sight under the mountain for the length of three breaths, and I am very good with three breaths. He is still breathing. Draw your conclusions."

"The last time we spoke under that mountain," Beatrix said quietly, "you were writing dispatches about him."

"The last time we spoke under that mountain," Voss said, "I was still a believer."

That was true, and truth was the only currency she had left that anyone might accept, so she spent it carefully, coin by coin, and watched the other woman weigh each piece.

* * *

Here was what Sarel Voss had learned in the weeks since the Verdant Gate stood open under the Thornfells, since she had knelt in the green dark and felt the sealed armies shift in their sleep like something turning over in bed:

Aurel Kest did not want to close the Gate. He wanted to own it.

She had gone back to Kestreach expecting orders to seal it. Instead she had been given a team of engineers, a requisition for enough chain and iron and blood-rite paraphernalia to outfit a cathedral, and a private audience in which the Emperor of the Ashen Throne had looked at her with those patient gray eyes and said, "Inquisitor, why lock a door when you can hold the key?"

Kest intended to open it fully. To wake what slept behind it. The old war's armies — the ones the treaty-lock had buried in the dark for four hundred years — were not, in the imperial reading, a horror to be contained. They were an arsenal. A final argument. When the free cities refused to kneel, as they would refuse, as they had always refused, Kest meant to open the Gate and let the argument speak for itself.

And Sarel Voss, who had given twenty-two years to the Ashen Throne, who had buried her doubts the way other women buried lovers, one at a time and with increasing efficiency, had sat in that audience chamber and understood that she was no longer serving an empire.

She was serving a man who intended to loose the end of the world on his enemies and call it law.

There were words for that. Traitor was the kindest of them.

So here she was, in a tannery loft, committing the highest treason of her career: offering it to someone else first.

* * *

"You want the rite," Beatrix said.

It wasn't a question either, but Voss answered it anyway, because watching Beatrix work things out was half the pleasure of this whole doomed enterprise. "I have it. Most of it. I copied what I saw under the mountain, and I've spent every night since cross-referencing it against the codices in Kestreach's sealed archive — the ones even the archivists need two emperors' seals to open." She turned her cup slowly on the table, a small orbiting motion, thinking. "The opening rite is incomplete. What I witnessed was the Gate responding to a bearer's blood, not the full sequence. The closing rite, though — the closing rite I have entire. Every word. Every order of operations. What I don't have—" she looked up, met Beatrix's eyes across the lamplight, "—is the only thing that makes it worth anything."

"Me."

"Your blood. Yes. The Gate keys itself to poison-blood. Old poisons, old bloodlines. The prophecy called it a vessel, and vessels are made, not born, and you—" Voss allowed herself the ghost of a smile, "—you have spent six years making yourself into the most qualified vessel in the world. Do you know how rare that is? There are perhaps three living people whose blood could seal that Gate. One is Elio Vane, who cannot do it without dying. One is a nun in Ilvaren who doesn't know what she is and whom I will never find. And one is you."

"So sell me to the free cities yourself. Walk into their embassy, hand them the rite, collect whatever traitors collect."

"With what proof? With what guarantee?" Voss set the cup down. "The free cities are not fools, but they are frightened, and frightened men buy from whoever stands closest. Right now, closest is Kest. If I approach them openly, they'll assume it's a trap — because it would be, if I were still his. No. The rite needs a courier they trust. It needs the smuggler roads. And it needs to arrive wrapped in a story they'll believe." She spread her hands. "It needs you."

"And what does it cost me?"

"Nothing you weren't already going to spend." Voss leaned forward. This was the part. She kept her voice level, almost bored, because urgency read as desperation and desperation read as leverage against her. "Elio Vane leaves Vhalenne alive. Through the Thornfells, along your passes, past my colleagues, past the checkpoints, past the cordon Kest has drawn around this city tighter than a garrote. I give you the route timings. I give you the patrol rotations. I give you the name of the officer commanding the eastern gate garrison and the fact that he owes me eleven years of quiet favors. In exchange, the closing rite goes to the free cities in your keeping, and the Gate gets sealed before Kest can open it." She paused. "And I ask for nothing else. No oath. No service. No future claim."

Beatrix stared at her. "No one asks for nothing."

"Everyone asks for nothing," Voss corrected. "The trick is meaning it."

* * *

They circled for an hour after that. Beatrix probed at the edges of the offer the way she must have probed at poisoned dishes all her palace career — a touch here, a hesitation there, testing for the bitterness underneath.

Why did Voss turn? Because she'd seen what was behind the Gate. Because faith, like any long marriage, ends either in contentment or in flight, and hers had ended in flight. Why trust her? Don't. Verify everything; here are documents, here are names, here are three separate ways to confirm each one within a day. Why keep Elio alive instead of using him as bait for the whole network? Because a bearer who dies is a martyr and martyrs start wars, and Voss had had her fill of wars started by men who liked them.

"Why tell me about the nun?" Beatrix asked at one point, sharp. "That's a vulnerability. Now I know you have another option."

"Because if you think this offer is unique, you'll negotiate badly. And because—" Voss hesitated, then gave her another coin of truth, "—because I want you to understand I'm not choosing you. I'm choosing the outcome. Whoever can deliver it. That's the only honest thing about me, and you deserve honesty more than flattery tonight."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow I go to the free-city embassy myself, alone, with worse odds, and probably die in a cellar in Gildport, and the Gate stays open, and Kest wakes his arsenal, and everyone you love learns what the end of the world smells like." She shrugged. "Refuse. It's your right. I'm not holding anything over you except the obvious."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, the river barges groaned against their moorings. Beatrix walked to the window — the one she'd entered by — and stood looking out at the city with its curfew lanterns burning sullen orange, and Voss watched the back of her head and understood that she was watching a woman perform an act of translation: converting an enemy's ambition into something a human being could carry.

"Alliances are knives," Beatrix said at last, without turning around. "My mother used to say it. You hand someone a knife handle-first, and you hope they take it by the handle. But you never really know, do you? Until the moment arrives. And by then your back is turned."

"Your mother sounds like she survived something."

"She didn't." A beat. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Voss laid out the terms formally, because formality was a kind of armor and both of them needed armor tonight.

The rite, transcribed in her own hand, sealed with her personal cipher — the one that would damn her if Kest ever saw it, which was precisely the point; it was collateral, hostage to her own good behavior. The route: three passes, two river crossings, the timing of the eastern garrison's changeover, the blind spot in the checkpoint rotation that lasted ninety seconds twice a night and had lasted, unreported, for eleven years, because Voss had built it herself and no one above her rank knew it existed. Safe houses marked in a cipher Beatrix would recognize — Voss had cracked the smugglers' old Thorn-code years ago and had never told anyone, another coin of truth spent face-up on the table.

"In exchange," Voss said, "the rite reaches the free cities. The Gate closes. And Sarel Voss disappears off the face of the earth, which, if you're feeling generous someday, you might arrange. I hear the Thornfells are good at making people vanish."

"You want asylum."

"I want," Voss said, "to stop being what I've been. That's all asylum is, when you strip it down."

Beatrix finally took the cup of wine. Drank it without ceremony, without checking it — and Voss realized, with something that was almost admiration, that she hadn't checked it because she didn't need to. Six years of poison had made her the one person in Vhalenne for whom a stranger's cup held no fear.

"That's the difference between us," Beatrix said, setting the cup down. "I checked everything my whole life. Every dish. Every letter. Every friend." She looked at Voss, and her eyes in the lamplight were tired and hard and younger than her years. "You're asking me to stop checking. At the worst possible moment. With the worst possible person."

"Yes."

"It's insane."

"It's Tuesday," Voss said. "Insanity is the local weather now."

And Beatrix Marchetti laughed — a short, cracked, genuine laugh, the first sound of its kind Voss had heard from her — and something in the room changed pressure, the way air changes before rain.

* * *

She took the knife.

Not literally — though Voss slid the transcription across the table wrapped in oilcloth, and Beatrix took it and weighed it in her palm like exactly what it was, a blade offered handle-first.

"I'll verify your documents," Beatrix said. "Every one. If a single name is wrong, a single timing, I burn the rite and you never see me or him again, and I will spend the rest of my short life making sure the free cities know your cipher and your face."

"Reasonable."

"If you betray us at the gate — if there's a squad waiting in that ninety-second blind spot —"

"There won't be."

"—then I want you to know something." Beatrix leaned in, and her voice went soft and terrible. "I have survived every poison this kingdom knows. I have carried letters through mountains full of men who'd skin me for the ink. I have drunk death for a queen who sold me. There is nothing left in me that can be threatened. So don't betray us for gain, or fear, or duty. If you betray us, it can only be for nothing. Remember that. Nothing is a poor price."

Voss held her gaze and said, evenly: "I have betrayed one master already for less than nothing. I'm not in the market for a second."

Something passed between them then — not trust, nothing so clean, but recognition. Two women who had spent their lives as instruments deciding, in the same hour, to become something else. It wasn't friendship. It might never be. But it was a handle, and it was being taken, and in the trade they both lived in, that was as close to grace as the world offered.

Beatrix tucked the oilcloth packet inside her coat, against her ribs, where Voss imagined she carried everything that mattered.

"One more thing," Beatrix said at the window, one leg already over the sill, the city's orange glow outlining her. "The officer at the eastern gate. The one who owes you favors."

"What about him?"

"When this is over — when the Gate is shut and your emperor is answering for what he tried to wake — I'll make sure the free cities know whose knife opened the road." Her mouth curved, thin and real. "Handle-first, Voss. Both directions."

Then she was gone, down into the dark, and Sarel Voss sat alone in the tannery loft with an empty cup and a heart hammering like a fist on a door, understanding that she had just bet her life on the mercy of a woman trained never to extend it — and that somewhere across the city, in a borrowed room, Elio Vane was packing his maps and did not yet know that by dawn he would be running.

By dawn, everything would be moving. The armies. The Gate. The girl with the fortress blood and the cartographer with the burden in his veins.

Voss blew out the lamp and began, in the dark, to write the letter that would set her escape in motion — and paused, pen hovering, as somewhere far beneath the Thornfells, faint as a heartbeat through stone, something ancient shifted in its sleep.

The Gate knew. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming.

Three nights, she thought. Three nights, and then the door closes forever — or opens.
