## 21. The Woman Who Wasn't Tasted

They buried her twice before they buried her at all.

Elio read the proclamations in a border town called Ashfall, pinned to the customs house door in three languages, the wax seals still soft from the courier's ride. *Beatrix Marchetti, royal taster of Vhalenne, dead of fever contracted in service to the crown.* The first kingdom had her dying quietly. The second, the one whose seal he didn't recognize, had her dying heroically — *in service to the realm, her sacrifice unrecorded by decree* — which was the crown's way of saying they knew exactly how she'd died and needed everyone else to stop asking. The third proclamation was newer, cruder, nailed up over the other two: *By order of Aurel Kest, any person harboring or concealing agents of the fallen court will answer to the empire.*

She stood beside him reading it, hood up, hands in her pockets like any traveler waiting out the rain.

"They gave me a fever," she said. "I've never had a fever in my life. Six years of drinking poison and they give me a *fever*."

"Would you have preferred something dramatic?"

"I would have preferred accurate." She tapped the second seal. "This one's the interesting lie. *Unrecorded by decree.* That means Ilseva signed it herself. She let them declare me dead so no one would look for me alive."

"Isn't that what you wanted?"

"It's what I *needed*." She pulled the hood lower. "Wanting is different."

He understood then that she was grieving, and that what she grieved for was not the woman on the paper but the woman who had existed before the paper — the one with a salary, a title, a place at the queen's left hand, a name that opened doors in the palace kitchens. That woman had served six years and been repaid with a forged death certificate. And Beatrix Marchetti did not weep about it. She just stood there in the rain reading her own obituary and cataloging its bitterness, the way she once cataloged poison.

"Come on," Elio said. "The ferry leaves at dusk."

---

The change had happened slowly enough that he'd stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a scar.

In the first days after the Gate, she had been gray-faced and shaking, carried half-conscious across the Thornfells while the smugglers argued over whether to leave her. The rite had burned through her blood like fire through a dry field. Her immunity — six years of small doses, every poison the palace master could devise, keyed now into something vast and old beneath the mountain — had held. Barely. The Gate had sealed. The armies stayed in the dark. But when she woke on the far side of the pass, something had gone out of her face.

Not beauty. He wouldn't insult her with that. Something structural. The way a river changes course and leaves the old bed visible but empty.

At the border crossing into Ashfall, a customs officer had looked directly at her — looked at the description circulated through every checkpoint in three kingdoms: *dark hair, olive skin, a scar along the left jaw* — and waved her through. Because the scar was gone. The rite had taken it, along with two shades of color from her skin and whatever quality it was that made people remember her after she left a room. The palace physicians, if any had seen her, would have sworn on their mothers' graves that this was not Beatrix Marchetti.

"You're mourning her," Elio had said one night on the trail, when he caught her staring at her reflection in a smuggler's tin flask.

"I'm inventorying," she'd said. "There's a difference."

But there wasn't, quite. Not yet.

---

Ashfall smelled of coal smoke and river mud, and it was the last town where either of their faces meant anything. They took a room above a dye-shop because the dye-stink covered everything, including fear. Elio spread his maps on the floor and worked by candlelight redrawing the Thornfells — not the crown version, with its omissions, but the true one, all twenty-three passes, the smuggler roads inked in green because green was the color the network used and he had learned to think in their colors now.

Beatrix spent the days learning how to be anonymous.

It was harder than either of them expected. She kept reaching for habits that belonged to a dead woman — checking sightlines for the queen's men, tasting the first spoonful of every meal with a professional's attention, angling her body in a doorway the way a bodyguard does. In the market she caught herself examining a fruit-seller's knives for poison residue and had to stand there, laughing at herself, until the laugh turned into something else and Elio put his arm around her shoulders and steered her away before anyone saw a stranger cry over pears.

"The taster's tongue doesn't retire," she said that night. "You know that? It just goes hungry."

"Then feed it something honest."

So he cooked. Badly, at first — he was a cartographer, not a kitchen man — but he learned her childhood dishes one by one, the ones she'd traded him truths for back in the capital, and he watched her taste each one the new way: not testing, not guarding, just eating. Like a person. Like someone whose only obligation at a table was appetite.

On the ninth night in Ashfall she tasted a stew he'd made from her grandmother's recipe and set down the spoon very carefully, and said, "It needs more salt," and her voice broke on the word *salt*, because salt had been her whole life — the preserving of things, the bitter duty of keeping others safe — and here it was just salt again, seasoning, nothing carried in it.

He crossed the room and held her while she shook, and neither of them said anything about queens or crowns or the woman in the proclamations, because grief and freedom were wearing the same face and there was no point trying to tell them apart.

---

The smuggler caravan left for the free cities on a morning of thin fog.

Maren of the Thornfells came for them personally — Maren, whom Beatrix had paid in letters and favors for six years, who had once said *you're the only crown servant I've ever liked, which worries me*. She looked at Beatrix's changed face for a long moment and then embraced her anyway.

"You don't smell like her," Maren said. "But you argue like her. Good enough."

"What's the price?" Beatrix asked, because with the network there was always a price, and she had already paid most of hers beneath a mountain.

"Nothing." Maren spat, ceremonially, toward Kestreach's direction. "The Gate's closed, Vane's maps are ours, and you drank the cup nobody should have had to drink. As far as the free cities are concerned, you're pilgrims going to thank the Verdant Mother. Which, incidentally—" she grinned— "is even true."

The caravan moved north through country Elio had only ever known as ink. That was the strange gift of it: he had drawn these mountains for the crown, drawn them precisely, lovingly, and never walked a single mile of them until he became a fugitive. Now he filled his sketchbooks with what ink couldn't hold — the sound the fog made moving through pine, the way Beatrix rode with her weight forward like she expected the horse to betray her, the little shrines travelers built at crossroads, each one holding a cup of clean water instead of wine. Offerings to the Verdant Mother, Maren said. For safe passage. Nobody remembered anymore that the water was originally for washing blood off hands.

Elio drew one of the shrines and, without deciding to, drew the cup full of dark liquid instead of clear, and stared at it a long time before tearing the page out and burning it in the cookfire.

Some things were carried, not drawn.

---

They crossed the border at a place with no name, just a cairn and a customs shed manned by an old man who took their toll in copper and asked no questions, because the free cities asked no questions; that was the whole point of them, and also the reason empires hated them.

On the far side, Beatrix reined in and looked back at the mountains. The Thornfells stood gray and enormous behind them, the passes invisible from here, the Gate sealed somewhere in the deep stone with its terrible cargo, silent as it had been silent for a hundred years before a cartographer dreamed of a door.

"It's really quiet," she said. "I keep listening for it."

"What would it sound like?"

"I don't know. Something under everything. A hum." She shook her head. "There's nothing. Just wind. Six years I listened for poison in every cup, and now there's no cup, and I can't make my ears believe the quiet is real."

"It'll take time."

"Everything takes time. That's the one thing I learned in the palace." She turned her horse south, toward the low golden hills of the free cities, and something in her posture changed — loosened, fractionally, like a knot worked almost free. "Come on, Vane. I want to see a city where nobody knows my name."

"And if they learn it?"

She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the kitchens, sharp and warm at once. "Then they'll learn the new one."

---

The city they came to was called Serevan, and it sat where three rivers met, and it had never in four hundred years been successfully besieged, mostly because it was built like a rumor — layered, contradictory, impossible to pin down. Elio fell in love with it professionally within the hour. Every mapmaker's fantasy: a city that refused to be mapped, streets that doubled back on themselves, districts stacked on districts like sediment, bridges that connected places that had no business connecting.

They took rooms in a lodging house run by a widow named Odile, who asked their names and Beatrix said, "Vesna and Elior, travelers," without a flicker, and Odile wrote it in her ledger and never thought about them again, which was the entire miracle of it.

That evening they climbed to the roof of the lodging house, because Beatrix had developed a habit of needing sky after years of kitchens and corridors, and they ate bread and hard cheese and cherries bought from a boy who had no idea he was serving a dead woman and a branded fugitive.

Below them the city glowed. Somewhere a fiddler played badly. Boats moved on the dark rivers carrying cargo and secrets, as boats always had.

"Three kingdoms think I'm dead," Beatrix said. She was working on the cherries with grim concentration, spitting pits into her palm. "Two of them are right."

"One isn't."

"One is *lying*, and it signed the lie in wax." She tipped the pits over the roof edge into the canal. "Ilseva knew. About my blood, about the keyed poison, about all of it. She declared me dead to close the account. You understand what that means? It means I was never a servant. I was a line item. An expenditure written off."

"Do you hate her?"

Elio expected anger. What he got was a long silence, and then, quietly: "I pity her. She's sitting in a palace that belongs to Kest now, drinking from cups she has to taste herself, and she knows exactly what that costs. I did it for six years and I had somewhere to walk afterward. She has nowhere." Another pit, arced into the dark. "No. Hate's too expensive. I'm done buying it."

The fiddler below stumbled through the same phrase three times and gave up. Beatrix leaned her head on Elio's shoulder.

"Elior," she said, trying the name out. "Do you know what the strangest thing is? I keep waiting to feel guilty. Alive, free, eating cherries on a roof while the world burns down south. I keep waiting for the guilt, and it doesn't come. There's just this—" she gestured at the glowing city, the dark rivers— "*this*. And it feels like theft. And it also feels like the first honest thing I've ever owned."

"It's not theft. You paid."

"In blood."

"In blood," he agreed. "Paid in full."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Say my name. The new one."

"Vesna."

"Not the dead one?"

"No. Never the dead one."

She kissed him then, cherry-warm, and below them the fiddler found the phrase at last and carried it, ragged but whole, across the water.

---

Later — much later, when the city had gone soft and the lamps were guttering — Elio woke to find Beatrix sitting upright in bed, listening.

He knew that listening. It was the taster's listening, the one that heard what wasn't there.

"What is it?" he whispered.

"Nothing." But her head was tilted, her whole body strung like wire. "That's the thing. It's nothing. It's *quiet*."

And then, fainter than wind, fainter than memory — a hum. Under the floorboards. Under the rivers. Under everything. Coming not from the mountains behind them but from somewhere ahead, deep in the free cities, deep in the earth itself, and it was not the sound of a Gate opening.

It was the sound of another one, waking up.

Beatrix reached for the lamp, and her hand — steady as it had ever been, steady as a woman who had drunk death nightly for six years — was shaking.
