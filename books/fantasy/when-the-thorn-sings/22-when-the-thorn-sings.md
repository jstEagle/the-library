## 22. When the Thorn Sings

**Beatrix**

The kitchen smells of garlic and salt and nothing else.

That's the whole miracle, and I have to remind myself of it every morning, standing at the stove in a house that has no name in any ledger: no one is trying to kill anyone at this table. There is no cup to sip first, no broth to hold on my tongue while my heart counts to ten, no queen waiting behind a screen for the small nod that means *live*. I cook the way other people cook — badly, generously, with too much rosemary because rosemary was free at the market and the woman selling it told me a story about her grandson and I couldn't say no.

I still taste everything. Old habit. But now it's only to know whether the stew wants more salt, and when I set the spoon down, my mouth doesn't go numb, and my hands don't shake, and nobody dies.

Six years I ate poison so that a stranger could eat bread. Three years before that I learned to swallow bitterness without flinching, back when my mother's kitchen in the Salt Quarter had a hole in the roof and we counted coins by candlelight. I used to think the tasting was the worst of it. It wasn't. The worst was the competence — being good at something that was slowly killing me, and being praised for it, and calling the praise a life.

This morning I burned the flatbread. Both sides. Elio ate it anyway and said it tasted like ambition, which is his way of saying he loves me without saying it, because some habits from the old country survive even when the countries don't.

We live above a chandler's shop in Vessa Maré, third city of the coast, where the harbor bells ring off-key and nobody checks papers at the fish market because half the fishmongers arrived here the same way we did — over the mountains, through the passes that aren't on any map anymore, carrying whatever they could hold and leaving the rest to burn. Our neighbors know us as the mapmaker and his wife who cooks. They do not know my blood could seal a door beneath the world. They do not need to. That is the entire point of a life like this one: the point is that it is boring.

Some mornings I wake up reaching for a cup that isn't there. Some nights I dream of the palace kitchens, the long steel tables, the master poisoner Corvane with his little book of doses, and I wake with my pulse hammering and Elio's hand already finding mine in the dark, because after two years he knows the shape of that dream by the way I breathe. He never asks what it was about anymore. He just says, "The bread will be better today," and somehow that helps.

The rite changed me. I don't talk about it much, even to him, because there isn't language for it yet. My hair came in darker at the temples, ink-black where it used to be brown. Scars I've carried since childhood have faded to whispers. When the physician in the free cities drew my blood last winter — a kindness, checking that the keyed poison hadn't left anything sleeping in me — she looked at the vial for a long time and said, quietly, "What are you?" And I said, "Retired," and she laughed, and we left it there.

But some nights, when the wind comes down off the Thornfells and rattles the shutters, I swear I can feel it — the Gate, far inland, sealed and silent, holding its armies in the dark the way a fist holds a coin. Not pulling at me. Just *there*, the way an old injury is there when the weather turns. Elio says I imagine it. Elio also talks to his maps, so I take his medical opinions with a grain of salt. A large grain. A fistful.

The codex sits on our shelf between a cookbook and a water-stained atlas of the southern isles. The new one — the one we started the week we arrived, two refugees with ink-stained fingers writing on paper bought from a stationer who asked no questions. Its last page is blank. We agreed it would stay blank until we knew what kind of story we'd earned.

Some days I think I know. Some days I think the page should stay empty forever, as a monument to the fact that we got out — that prophecy is a trap dressed as a gift, and the best ending is the one you refuse to write.

And then some days, like today, I stand at the stove with steam on the window and the harbor bells going their tuneless rounds, and I catch myself humming, and I realize the song is one I learned in the Thornfells from a smuggler named Ossa who died so we could cross the last pass — and the thorn song sounds different here. Warmer. Like it's learning to be about something other than survival.

Elio is at the table behind me, drawing. I can hear the pen. It's the sound I fell asleep to for two years in the capital, muffled through walls and fear; now it's just the sound of Tuesday.

"Bea," he says. "Come look at this."

"Flatbread first."

"The flatbread is a lost cause and we both know it."

I bring it over anyway, burnt side up, defiant. He kisses my flour-dusted wrist — the wrist that held a hundred cups, the wrist they would have bound if they'd ever caught us — and turns his slate toward me.

**Elio**

Here is what I know about maps that I didn't know when I had a title: a map is an argument. Every line you draw is a claim, and every claim is a small violence against someone who lives on the other side of it. I spent six years drawing arguments for crowns. I was very good at it. The royal cartographer of Vhalenne, they called me, right up until the night the royal cartographer of Vhalenne ceased to exist in three kingdoms simultaneously, which remains the proudest thing on my record.

The maps I draw now have no borders.

It started as an exercise, the first winter, when my hands shook too badly to work and Beatrix suggested — gently, the way she does everything, like setting down a cup — that I draw somewhere I loved without measuring it. So I drew the Salt Quarter from memory: the fish stalls, the chapel with the crooked bell, the alley where she taught me to pick a lock when we were nineteen and both too stupid to be afraid. No scale bar. No compass rose. Just the place, the way love holds a place, which is inaccurately and completely.

Then I drew the Thornfells. All of them, every pass, including the three I once omitted from the crown's survey — the ones only she knew, the ones that saved our lives. On this version I marked them all, because secrets kept us alive but secrets nearly killed us too, and there is a difference between a secret and a wound that hasn't been cleaned.

Now I draw coastlines and rivers and roads and the small marks where people live, and I leave the edges open. Merchants buy them. Teachers. One old woman bought three and gave them to her grandchildren with instructions to draw their own borders on top in red, "so they can see how silly it looks." She paid double. I think about her often.

Today's map is different, though. Today I've been working on it for a month and telling no one, not even Bea, which is difficult, because we made a vow of no more concealment and I've been technically violating it with cartography. But I wanted it finished first. Finished, or as finished as anything I make.

It's a map of everything we crossed. The capital. The river. The banquet hall where Kest's envoy watched her too closely. The bookseller's stall where the codex waited with its wet ink and its century-old patience. The pass where Ossa died singing. The door beneath the mountain — drawn not as I saw it but as I dreamed it, before I ever stood in front of it, because the dreams were true first and the stone was only catching up. And at the far edge, past the border that isn't a border, Vessa Maré, small and unimportant and ours.

A map of a love story, if a love story were a country. Which I am beginning to suspect it is.

Bea leans over my shoulder with the ruined flatbread and studies it, and I watch her face do the thing it does — the taster's discipline, cataloging, measuring, except now what she's tasting is memory, and I can see each one land.

"You drew the kitchen," she says. Her voice goes strange. "In the palace."

"I drew all of them. Look."

She looks. The palace kitchen, tiny, rendered in six strokes of the pen. Her mother's kitchen in the Salt Quarter. This kitchen, the one with the bad shutter and the good light. Three kitchens, one woman, twenty years.

"When did you have time—"

"Nights. When you were asleep. When you were dreaming about cups and I was awake anyway." I shrug, which is inadequate, but I've never found a gesture big enough for what I mean. "I wanted you to have a map where every place you've been is still connected. Where none of it got cut off. In the empire's maps, your life would be three separate territories with wars between them. On mine it's one road."

She sets the flatbread down. She puts her arms around me, flour and all, and holds on the way people hold on when they've both been to the bottom of something and come back up breathing.

"The last page," she says into my shoulder. "I've been thinking about it."

"I know. You hum when you're thinking. You've been humming the thorn song for a week."

"It's supposed to be a chronicle, isn't it? That's what the old codex was. What happened, and what's coming, and who carries it." She pulls back and looks at me, and her eyes are doing the thing where they're laughing and grieving in the same breath, which took me years to learn to read and now I can't unsee it. "But maybe a chronicle is just a map of time. And maybe the mistake was thinking someone else holds the pen."

"So we hold it."

"So we hold it." She picks up the pen — my pen, the one I drew the crown's lies with, the one I drew our escape with — and weighs it in her fingers like she's testing a blade. "Not tonight. Tonight there's stew. But soon. And when we write it, we write all of it. The Gate. The rite. What my blood cost and what it bought. The names of everyone who helped us, starting with a smuggler who sang off-key." Her jaw sets, and I recognize the expression from a hundred state dinners: it's the face she makes when she has decided something irreversible. "Because if we don't write it down, then someday some bookseller sells a codex with wet ink, and some kid reads a date beside their own name, and thinks destiny is a thing that happens *to* them instead of a thing you argue with."

"That's the lesson?"

"That's the lesson. Destiny is real. It's also negotiable." She smiles, and the scar-faded corner of her mouth lifts, and I fall a little further in love, which at this point should be geometrically impossible and yet here we are. "Also the lesson is: always taste the stew. Even retired. Especially retired."

* * *

**Beatrix**

Evening comes down over the harbor like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. We eat at the table by the window — the stew, the resurrected halves of the flatbread that Elio insists are fine, the cheap wine that tastes of nothing but itself, which is the finest flavor I know.

Afterward he washes, I dry, and we argue about whether the chandler's cat is plotting against us (he says yes; I say cats lack the institutional patience; we have been having this argument for a year and neither of us will concede). Through the open window: gulls, bells, a fisherman cursing the tide in an accent from a city that no longer appears on imperial maps because the empire renamed it and the rename didn't take.

Kest is still on his throne, last we heard. The war he wanted didn't come — the Gate stayed shut, the sealed armies stayed sealed, and empires rot faster from the inside than from the front. The free cities grow fat and loud and free. The queen sends letters sometimes, unsigned, through channels we pretend not to notice, and we burn them unread, or rather I read them and then burn them, because old habits include mercy.

Sarel Voss, they say, advises the free cities' admiralty now. Traitor to one crown, servant of none, the most useful knife in the western seas. Last spring a package arrived with no return address: inside, a single pressed sprig of silverleaf and a card that read *You chose well. So did I.* I keep the sprig in the cookbook. I have not decided what it means. I may never decide. Some debts you carry the way the mountains carry the door — closed, silent, and yours.

The codex waits on its shelf. Blank at the end, full everywhere else: our handwriting tangled together across a hundred pages, his maps folded into my accounts, the recipe for my mother's stew annotated in two languages, the names of the dead written carefully and the names of the living written carelessly because the living will forgive us.

Tonight, drying the last dish, I catch him looking at the shelf. He catches me looking at him looking. We laugh, quietly, the way you laugh in a house where no one is listening for laughter.

"Tomorrow?" he says.

"Tomorrow," I say. "After the market. After the bread. After the day."

Because that's the whole of it, in the end — the secret under the mountain, the prophecy, the poison, the crowns, all of it distilled to something a child could understand and an empress never could: love is not what you fall into. Love is the duty you choose, every morning, with your eyes open, knowing exactly what it costs. I spent six years tasting death for strangers. I will spend the rest of my life tasting bread for one man, and the second life is the braver one, and no codex, no king, no ink wet or dry will ever convince me otherwise.

Outside, the thorn wind comes off the fells one last time before dawn, rattling the shutter, carrying — or so I will always believe — the faintest thread of Ossa's song, off-key and unbroken.

Inside, the last page waits.

Tomorrow, we begin.
