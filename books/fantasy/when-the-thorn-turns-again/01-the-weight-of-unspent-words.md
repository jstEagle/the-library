## 1. The Weight of Unspent Words

The winter ledger smelled of tallow and old iron, and Dominic Thackeray had been reading it since the second bell after midnight.

She sat in the counting room at the back of the Guildhouse of Saint Verre, where the guild's business was conducted in whispers and its accounts were kept in a hand so small it looked like insect tracks. Three candles burned at her elbow. A fourth had drowned in its own wax an hour ago, and she had not replaced it. The dark at the edges of the room felt honest. The numbers did not.

*One memory of a first kiss, sold by a widow in Copperlane to a man who wished to know what he'd missed. Paid: eleven crowns and a jar of pickled eels.*

*One year of good luck, drawn from a miller's daughter and transferred to a racehorse owner. The girl has been unlucky ever since; the horse has never lost. Both parties satisfied. No refunds.*

*One name, unspoken for forty years, purchased by a woman who wanted to say it aloud before she died. Delivered in a sealed box. The box was returned empty.*

Dominic wrote each entry twice — once in the guild cipher, once in plain figures for Master Quillon's private copy — and her pen moved with the flat competence of fifteen years' practice. That was what she was now. Not a swordspeaker. Not the last of anything. A woman with good penmanship and a talent for arithmetic, who kept her sleeves buttoned to the wrist even in summer, because the oath-scar there still itched when the words came up.

And they were coming up tonight. They always did on audit nights, when the ledger made her handle other people's impossibilities one by one, turning them over like coins bitten between the teeth. Every entry was a small door left ajar, and behind each one something hummed.

She pressed her tongue against the roof of her mouth, where the pressure gathered, and kept writing.

It was not that the words were loud. That was the cruelty of it. They were quiet — a hum beneath the breastbone, a warmth behind the teeth, like a kettle not yet boiling. If she let herself listen, she could feel whole sentences standing in line behind her sternum, patient as debtors. *Open.* *Burn.* *Stay.* Words her grandmother had used to split a river in flood. Words her mother had used once, only once, and then never spoken of again. Words that had been bred into the Thackeray line the way other families bred for height or temper, and which Dominic had starved for fifteen years with the same discipline she brought to everything: completely, and without mercy.

A swordspeaker's gift did not die from disuse. She had checked. In the early years she had tested it the way you test a broken leg, cautiously, in private, hating herself for hoping. She would stand in the stockroom among the crates of bottled dreams and whisper a word against her own palm — nothing dangerous, nothing that would leave her skin — and feel it land like a struck bell, ringing through bone. The gift was alive. It was only waiting. That was worse than dead. Dead things stopped wanting.

She turned the page.

*One door, six inches tall, carved from peach wood. Opens onto a garden that does not exist. Sold to a childless couple in Vell Street. They have been seen leaving bread and honey beside it every morning for three years. No further trade requested.*

Dominic's pen paused above the entry.

That was the trouble with the ledger. Quillon thought of it as inventory. But inventory remembered. Every impossible thing the guild had ever brokered sat in these pages wearing its little coat of figures, and some nights the pages felt less like accounting than like a census of everything the world had lost and someone had dared to want back.

Her grandmother would have loved this book. That thought arrived uninvited, the way the hum always did around ledgers, and Dominic set down her pen and pressed both palms flat against the desk until the wood bit into her skin.

Not tonight. Not any night.

* * *

The delivery came at dawn, because deliveries always came at dawn, when the free cities were grey and the harbor fog had not yet decided whether to be weather or merely mood.

Dominic took it herself, as she took most routine deliveries. This was deliberate. Fifteen years ago, people had looked at her twice — once because she was young and armed, and once because something in her voice made them look again without knowing why. Now nobody looked at her at all. She had built the invisibility plank by plank: the drab wool cloak, the ink-stained fingers, the stoop she'd taught herself, the habit of speaking just under the volume of ordinary conversation. She had made herself a woman the eye slid off, and she maintained her craft the way a swordspeaker once maintained a blade.

The parcel was small and wrapped in oilcloth, addressed in a merchant's hand to a chandler's shop on Rope Walk. Inside, according to the manifest, was a jar of preserved lemons. According to the guild's true ledger, it was the last hour of a dead man's courage, purchased from his widow by his son, who could not face his creditors without it. Routine. The guild moved perhaps forty such parcels a week through the free cities, tucked inside shipments of ordinary goods, and no customs officer in a hundred years had ever opened one, because the guild paid its bribes the way rivers paid the sea: constantly, and without being asked.

Dominic walked the parcel down through the waking streets, and the city performed its morning around her.

Fishwives crying the night's catch. A beggar outside the Shrine of the Ninefold Path who was, if you knew how to read him, a retired assassin collecting alms for the guild's pension fund. Two children playing at dragons on the church steps, roaring at each other in voices pitched too high, and Dominic felt the hum stir at the sound of their shouting — because children's voices carried the language underneath all languages, the one every speaker's gift woke in, and for half a heartbeat she wanted to crouch down and answer them in it.

She did not. She adjusted the parcel under her arm and walked faster.

At Rope Walk the chandler's boy took the jar without meeting her eyes, exactly as trained, and gave her the receipt token, a lead disc stamped with the guild's sigil — a key crossed with a quill. Dominic pocketed it and stood a moment on the step, letting the fog wet her face.

From the chandler's doorway she could see down the length of the street to the salt market, and beyond it the low wall, and beyond the wall the salt road running east toward countries that still existed. Carts were coming in along it, oxen and mules, the ordinary traffic of an ordinary season. Nothing about the road looked wrong.

But she had been a swordspeaker once, and though she had buried the gift, she had never buried the attention that came with it — the way a speaker's ear lay open to the world even when the voice was shut. And the road sounded wrong. Not loudly wrong. Quietly wrong. Too few carts for the hour, and the ones that passed rode in formation spacing, wheel-ruts overlapping, drivers sitting straight the way soldiers sit when they're pretending to be teamsters.

Dominic told herself it was grain moving ahead of the winter tariffs. She told herself the usurper's quartermasters drove like that everywhere; discipline was cheap. She told herself a great many reasonable things while standing on the chandler's step with a jar of lemons and a dead man's courage, and then she went back the long way, through streets she hadn't planned on using, and hated herself a little for the relief she felt doing it.

Fifteen years of silence, and still her body flinched toward war like a dog toward a whistle.

* * *

Back at the Guildhouse, the day's trades were already queuing in the front parlor, and Dominic resumed her place behind the appraisal desk with the relief of a woman returning to a fortress.

Appraisal was the part of the work she permitted herself to love. People brought their impossibilities in off the street, wrapped in shawls and shame, and set them on the scarred oak table, and Dominic weighed them. Not their price — Quillon handled price. Their weight. Whether a thing was safe to hold, whether it was what the seller claimed, whether it would keep. It was work that used the buried half of her without ever waking it fully, the way carrying water uses an arm without asking it to sing.

The morning's queue was ordinary. A man selling his grief — too fresh, she told him, come back in a year, grief thins to something portable if you wait. A woman buying back a promise she'd sold in desperation two winters ago; Dominic fetched it from the vaults, a small grey knot of light in a locket, and watched the woman press it to her lips like a relic. A boy of maybe ten who tried to sell a dream he swore was prophetic, and which Dominic examined and found to be only a dream, but a beautiful one, and she paid him fairly for it anyway because the guild had rules about feeding children who were hungry enough to sell their sleep.

Then, near midday, the old woman came.

She was nobody. That was the first thing Dominic noticed, professionally. Grey shawl, cracked boots, hands like tree roots, the face of someone the city had been chewing for eighty years and had grown tired of. She shuffled to the desk and stood there breathing, and Dominic waited, because the old ones sometimes took a while to say what they'd come to sell.

"I've got a word," the woman said at last. "Just the one. Been keeping it sixty years."

Dominic's pen stopped.

"We don't deal in those," she said, and her voice came out level, which cost her more than the sentence cost most men a day's wages. "Words aren't tradeable. Guild policy."

"Not a power-word." The old woman's eyes were milky but they fixed on Dominic with terrible precision. "Just a name. My mother's name. Nobody living remembers it but me, and my memory's going. I can feel it going, like ice going out of a pond. I want it written somewhere that'll outlast me. I'll pay whatever you ask. I've got four coppers and a silver spoon."

The hum rose so fast it was almost in her throat before Dominic caught it — a full breath's worth of Alderman rising behind her teeth, the old tongue that named things truly, that could carry a name across centuries without dropping it. She swallowed it down. It went down like a fishhook.

"Four coppers and a spoon," Dominic said, when she trusted her mouth again, "is fine."

She took a slip of vellum and wrote the name in the old woman's careful spelling — letters that bent the wrong way, letters that belonged to a script from a country that was no longer on any map, though the old woman plainly didn't know she was writing in it, or didn't remember that she knew. Dominic copied it into the permanent register with hands that did not shake, because fifteen years of practice will hold a hand steady through nearly anything.

"There," she said. "It's written. It'll outlast us both."

The old woman touched the page with one root-knuckled finger, the way you touch a sleeping child, and smiled, and shuffled out into the fog.

Dominic sat looking at the name for a long time.

*Aldermere,* said the hum, softly, treacherously, in the voice of her grandmother. *You wrote Alderman letters today, little thief. You held a piece of the country in your hand and you filed it under inventory.*

"It isn't the same," Dominic said aloud, to the empty parlor. Her voice sounded rusty. She cleared her throat. "It's a name. Names are safe."

The hum did not argue. It never argued. It only waited, the way the ledger waited, the way the gift waited, patient as interest accruing on an old debt.

* * *

Quillon found her at dusk, as she knew he would. He came into the counting room carrying two cups of mulled wine and the expression of a man about to discuss money, which for Master Quillon was the expression of a man discussing love, fear, God, and death, all four being matters he believed were ultimately financial.

"Winter audit done?" he asked.

"Done. Forty-one entries. Two disputes flagged — the luck transfer is souring, the horse owner wants a refund, and I've told him the guild doesn't refund luck." She slid the ledger across. "There's also a new standing order in the register. A name, held in perpetuity. Fee waived."

Quillon's eyebrows climbed. "Waived? Thackeray, if we waive fees for every sentimental—"

"She paid four coppers and a spoon. It covered the vellum." Dominic took the wine because refusing it would invite conversation. "It's done, master. Audit's clean."

Quillon read. He read the way he did everything, thoroughly and greedily, lips moving slightly, and Dominic watched him over the rim of her cup and felt the old familiar distance open between them — the distance of a woman who had spent fifteen years being useful to a man whose entire soul was a shop, and who had never once asked herself what that made her, because the answer might require words, and words were the one currency she refused to spend.

"Salt road's busy," Quillon said, not looking up. "Odd traffic for the season. Armed odd traffic."

"You've heard it too."

"Heard it, seen it, and been billed for it — three of my usual couriers have been stopped and turned at the tolls. Warrants, they said. Looking for smugglers." He snorted. "Usurper's men don't call anyone smugglers unless they mean scholars, priests, or speakers. Smuggler's the polite word. There's a purge shape moving east to west, Thackeray, and I don't yet know what it's shaped like, and I dislike not knowing. It's bad for trade."

"Bad for trade," Dominic agreed, and heard how the words fit her mouth like a glove fitted to a hand with the fingers cut off.

Quillon closed the ledger and tapped its cover once, a little drumbeat of ownership. "You've been with me fifteen years. Best appraiser I've had, and I've had appraisers who could smell a forged miracle at forty paces. You never ask questions, you never take holidays, and you flinch at fireworks." His small sharp eyes came up. "I've never asked what you flinch from. That's been our arrangement, and it's been a profitable arrangement for us both."

Dominic said nothing. The candles guttered. Somewhere below, the guild's night porter was locking the front doors, and the sound of the bolts going home ran up through the floorboards like a pulse.

"But arrangements change," Quillon said mildly, "when roads change. Keep your ears open, Thackeray. Whatever's coming down that salt road, I want to know about it before it knows about us. That's not a request. That's an entry in next quarter's ledger."

He took his wine and his ledger and left her alone with the drowned candle and the dark.

Dominic sat for a while without moving. Then she rolled back her left sleeve and looked at the oath-scar — a thin white line circling her wrist like a shackle worn smooth, the mark she'd taken at nineteen when she'd sworn the vow of silence before her mother's cold body and three witnesses who were now all dead. *I put down the sword of my voice. I will not speak what sleeps in me. Let the last house of Thackeray go dark rather than burn bright again.*

Fifteen years, and the scar had never once faded properly. Scars faded. This one gleamed, faintly, in certain lights, as if the words beneath it polished it from below.

Outside, the fog had thickened until the window showed nothing but grey, and the city beyond it had gone silent in the particular way cities go silent when everyone is listening.

Dominic pulled her sleeve down, banked the candles, and climbed the stairs to her narrow room above the counting floor, where she slept every night with her back to the wall and her right hand loose and empty, the way soldiers sleep, the way speakers sleep, waiting for a knock.

She did not know, yet, that the knock was three nights away. She only knew, lying in the dark with the hum humming its one patient note beneath her sternum, that the silence she had built her life inside was not a wall.

It was a dam. And far off, past the fog, past the salt road, past the countries that still existed, something had begun, very quietly, to rain.
