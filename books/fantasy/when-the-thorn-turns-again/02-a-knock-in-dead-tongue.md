## 2. A Knock in Dead Tongue

The storm came off the water three nights later, exactly as the fog had promised, and it came the way debts come: all at once and with interest.

Dominic heard it first as a change in the sound of the harbor. The free city of Vaelport made noise even at night—the groan of moored ships, the bell on the breakwater, the endless argument of gulls—but beneath it all there was a low tidal breathing that she had lived beside for fifteen years without ever consciously hearing. That breathing stopped. Then the rain arrived sideways, flung against the guild's shutters like handfuls of gravel, and the whole building shuddered on its foundations as if something enormous had put its shoulder to the coast itself.

She was awake for all of it. She had been awake most of the week.

The ledger sat open on her desk under a single candle, and she had been staring at the same entry for an hour without reading it. *One winter's silence, purchased by the widow Anselm of Copper Lane, price: her late husband's unbought forgiveness.* She had written that entry herself, four days ago, in her careful auditor's hand, and every time she looked at it the words rearranged themselves in her mind until they were not about the widow at all but about her—about the silence she had bought for herself, and what she had paid, and whether the price had been fair or whether, like so many guild trades, the true cost had simply been deferred to a later page.

The hum had not quieted since the audit. If anything it had thickened, a note held too long by a singer who would not breathe, and twice today it had risen far enough up her throat that she had tasted words—not meaning, just texture, syllables like river stones rolling over each other—and she had pressed her tongue to the roof of her mouth until they dissolved.

*Not tonight,* she told herself. *Not ever, if you're careful.*

Outside, the storm climbed another register. Somewhere below, the old building ticked and settled, its bones remembering wetter centuries. The Guildhouse of Vaelport had been a counting-house before it was a guild, and before that—Ferro the mapmaker claimed, when he was drunk enough to talk—a customs hall for a country that no longer appeared on any chart. Dominic had always assumed that was Ferro's romantic nonsense. Buildings did not outlive their countries. Nothing outlived its country. Countries were like names; you could strike them from the record and after a generation no one remembered they had ever been spoken.

She knew better than most how long a name could be made to stay dead.

The candle guttered. She trimmed it, and in the small violence of that motion caught herself listening—listening past the rain to the street below, the way she had listened every night this week without admitting it, the way soldiers listen, the way speakers sleep.

Nothing. Wind, water, the bell. The salt road empty between tides.

She closed the ledger. She banked the candle down to a red bead of light. And then, because sleep was going to be one more thing the night refused her, she took the audited pages and went downstairs through the dark house to file them in the strongroom, because order was a kind of prayer, and filing was the only liturgy she still practiced.

* * *

The ground floor of the guild at night was a different country than the ground floor by day. By day it rang with brokers' voices and the scrape of chairs and Quillon's laugh, which he deployed like currency, spending it where it bought the most. By night it was all shadow and grain-smell and the soft industrial ticking of the great clock above the trading floor, and the impossible things slept in their alcoves like patient animals: the jar of last year's summer that some farmer had traded away to survive a bad harvest; the mirror that showed you your mother's opinion of you rather than your face (Quillon kept it draped; he said it was bad for morale); seven keys to rooms that no longer existed, sold individually to collectors who never asked why the locks were gone.

Dominic moved through them without looking. Fifteen years will teach you to walk past anything.

The strongroom door stood at the back of the counting floor, behind the rostrum where the auctioneer called trades. It was iron-banded oak older than the guild itself, and it opened to her key and to her hand laid flat against the cold plate beside it, because the guild trusted flesh more than metal—flesh could be bound, and bound flesh keeps faith better than any lock.

Her palm touched the plate. The ward woke along her arm, that familiar cold crawl, the guild's own magic threading through her skin to read her—

—and the knock came.

Three blows on the outer door of the guildhouse. Soft ones. Small ones. The kind of knocks a fist the size of a pear makes when it has been knocking a long time and has almost given up hope.

Dominic stood absolutely still in the dark with her hand still flat on the ward-plate, and every animal instinct she owned went silent at once, the way a forest goes silent.

Because it wasn't only a knock. Under the wind, under the rain, thin as thread, she heard a voice. A child's voice, cracked with cold, saying something over and over to the closed door. Words she could not understand.

Words her body understood perfectly.

The hum beneath her sternum tore upward like a hooked fish. It wasn't pain, exactly—it was recognition, vast and involuntary, the feeling of hearing your own name shouted across a crowded market when you have spent fifteen years being someone else. The language reached her through three feet of oak and a storm, and every buried syllable in her answered it, rose screaming toward her throat, and hit the oath.

The oath-bond woke the way she imagined a scarred-over wound wakes: white heat, a flare of old fire along paths she had not let herself feel since she was twenty-two years old. It ran from her sternum down both arms and pooled in her palms, and her hands—her traitor, faithful hands, the hands that had signed ten thousand honest entries—closed into fists so hard her nails bit blood.

*Sealed,* she told herself. *You are sealed. You swore it. It holds.*

It held. Of course it held; it had been paid for dearly enough. But holding was not the same as quiet. Her teeth ached with unspoken words. Her jaw trembled around them. And the voice outside kept talking, kept knocking, kept saying whatever it was saying in the dead tongue of Aldermere, the language that existed nowhere in the world anymore except in her, sealed and rotting like a coin in a dead man's mouth.

No one else could hear it. That was the first coherent thought she managed. The night porter, Tam, slept two doors from the entry hall; if the words were loud enough to carry through a storm, he'd have woken. No one heard Alderman and stayed asleep. No one heard Alderman and felt nothing.

Which meant that whatever was knocking on the guild's front door in the middle of a salt storm, speaking a language fifteen years extinct—

—it had come for her specifically. Or it was her, dreamed, mad, finally cracked open by forty years of ledgers and one week of hum.

Dominic Thackeray had faced down debt-collectors with knives, guild rivals with worse than knives, and once, memorably, a broker who had tried to sell the guild a bottle containing his own death. She had never once in her adult life been afraid the way she was afraid standing in that dark counting floor, listening to a child's voice speak the tongue of a drowned country.

Slowly, deliberately, the way you move when you're trying to convince your own body the danger is manageable, she took her hand off the ward-plate. The bond's heat sank back to embers. The hum settled—not gone, never gone now, but crouched, watchful, like a dog that has scented its homeland on a stranger's coat.

She crossed the counting floor. She lifted the bar on the inner door of the entry vestibule, and stood in the gap between the two doors, in the cold draft that poured through the letter-slit, and listened.

The voice stopped. Then started again. Closer now. Right at the base of the outer door, she realized. Not standing. Crouched. Or sitting.

"...sennet halleth, sennet halleth, dommaru—" The words dissolved into coughing, small and wet and terrible.

And Dominic's body translated for her, the way it always would, the way a drowned man's lungs translate water. *Open the hall. Open the hall. Please.* Nursery cadences. A beggar's formula, the kind children in Aldermere had chanted at feast-day doors a hundred years ago, asking for honey-cakes. She knew it. She had chanted it herself, at her grandmother's knee, before she knew what the words meant or what they would cost her.

Her hand was on the outer bolt before she decided to move it.

She stopped it there. Forced it still. *Think,* she ordered herself, in Quillon's voice, in her own voice, in the cold auditor's voice that had kept her alive and invisible for fifteen years. *A child speaking Alderman at your door in a storm, three nights after the ledger hummed. Either it is bait, and the hook is set for whoever answers in kind—or it is real, and real is worse, because real means the country isn't finished striking names from the world, and the usurper's men are already this far west.*

Either answer ended the life she had built. There was no third option. There was no version of this night that ended with her ledger balanced and her silence intact.

The voice coughed again. Weaker.

Dominic thought about her grandmother's kitchen. About honey-cakes. About the specific weight of a promise made to a dying woman in a burning year, the last oath of the last swordspeaker of a guild that had traded in impossible things: *I will bury it so deep no one digs it up, and I will be no one, and I will keep the words behind my teeth until my teeth are done.* She had kept it. Through fifteen years of audits and anonymity, of making herself a woman so gray that even her reflection seemed to file her under miscellaneous, she had kept it.

But oaths, she had learned in the guild's oldest folios, are contracts. And every contract has clauses. And hers had never once said *let a child freeze.*

"Who's there?" she called through the door—in Trade-Tongue, common, safe. Her voice sounded strange to her, scraped raw though she hadn't spoken above a murmur all day. "Name yourself."

Silence. Then a scramble, a small body hauling itself up the door, a thump as something—someone—leaned against the oak. And the voice again, and this time it wasn't the nursery formula. This time it was slower, deliberate, shaped carefully the way a foreigner shapes words in a new alphabet:

"Dominic." A pause, a cough. "Dom-ma-rik. Thack-er-ay." The surname mangled almost past sense. But the first word—

The first word was her true name. Not Dominic-of-the-ledgers, not the gray auditor of Vaelport. Her swordspeaker's name, the one her grandmother had given her at the hearth, the one that had not been spoken aloud anywhere in the world for fifteen years. Spoken in Alderman, by a child, through a storm, correctly, with the stress on the second syllable and the breath-hitch in the middle that marked a speaker of the eastern valleys.

The oath-bond detonated.

White light behind her eyes. Heat down both arms to the fingertips. Every sealed syllable in her rose at once like a flock startled off a marsh, and for one endless half-second Dominic Thackeray stood in her own doorway with a dead country shouting inside her chest, and understood with perfect clarity that she had never buried her gift at all.

She had planted it. And something had just knocked on the soil from underneath.

"No," she whispered, to the bond, to the child, to the night. "No, no—you can't know that name. Nobody living knows that name."

"Nobody living," the small voice agreed through the door, in accented but functional Trade-Tongue now, and there was something in its tone that stopped her heart colder than the storm had: a weariness, an ancient practicality, entirely wrong for a child. "That's why I knocked here. Please. I have a key."

"A key." Her voice came out flat, professional, the auditor's voice. Good. Cling to the auditor. "To what?"

"To everything," the child said. "I'm sorry. I don't know how to say it smaller. They burned our village and I ran until the sea, and everyone said go to the guild in Vaelport, the guild trades in things that aren't possible anymore. Please. My hands won't work. I can't hold the key much longer."

Every rule she had. Every wall. Every hard-won, expensive, fifteen-year lesson in survival screamed at her to walk away, to wake Tam, to fetch Quillon, to do anything—anything—other than open that door alone, in the dark, with her gift clawing at its seals and her name hanging in the storm air like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

Dominic Thackeray drew back the bolt.

The door swung in six inches, caught against the chain she'd set without remembering deciding to, and the storm reached through the gap and hit her full in the face—salt and cold and the smell of the sea gone feral—and by the light of the harbor lantern across the lane she saw what had been knocking.

A girl. Eight years old, maybe nine. Soaked to the skin and blue-lipped, hair plastered to her skull, barefoot, both arms wrapped around something she hugged to her chest the way drowning sailors hug wreckage. Her lips were moving, shaping the nursery formula again—*open the hall, open the hall*—but no sound came out anymore. She had spent her voice on the storm.

Her eyes found Dominic's through the gap. And in them Dominic saw the thing that undid the last of her resistance: not fear. Recognition. The child looked at her the way people look at a door they've been promised exists.

"The key," the girl rasped. She worked one hand loose from her bundle and held it up between them, and the lantern light caught it.

Iron. Old iron, black with age, heavy as sin, stamped with a sigil pressed deep into the bow: a circle broken by a single vertical stroke, and within the broken circle, a thorned branch bearing one closed bud.

Dominic hadn't seen that mark since she was eleven years old. Hadn't seen it since the night her grandmother pressed her thumb to it, said the sealing words, and told her granddaughter that the sight of it should make her afraid for the rest of her life.

It did. God help her, it did—and beneath the dread, rising through it like spring through frost, unmistakable, unbearable, came the other thing. The recognition. The homecoming.

The hum in her chest sang one clear note, and this time Dominic did not press it down.

She pulled the chain free and opened the door wide, and the storm and the child came in together.
