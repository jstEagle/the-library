## 3. Wren

The stockroom behind the guild's salt cellar had not been used for anything honest in forty years. Dominic knew this because she had audited its contents twice—once when she took over the winter ledgers, and once when she suspected Ferro of hiding contraband maps among the casks of pickled eel. What it held now was dust, three broken looms, a crate of candle stubs gone soft as tallow-fat, and one child.

She had put Wren there at dawn, wrapped in a sailcloth that smelled of tar and old voyages, with instructions to be quiet as the dead and to eat nothing she hadn't seen Dominic touch first. The girl had looked up at her with those grey eyes—eyes the color of the sea before weather—and had said nothing at all, which was the first thing about her that made sense.

Everything else made no sense whatsoever.

Dominic climbed the stairs from the cellar level with a tray balanced on one hand: bread still warm from the kitchen ovens, hard cheese, a cup of watered wine she'd swapped for cider at the last moment because the child couldn't have been more than nine or ten. She moved through the guild's morning rhythms like a woman wading through familiar water—the clerks' corridor already loud with argument over a disputed delivery of bottled thunder, someone laughing too near the reliquary shelves where they weren't supposed to laugh—and let the noise close over her head the way it always did. Fifteen years of practice. She was furniture here. She was the ledger-keeper, the woman who counted things, who never raised her voice because she never needed to.

The words were still humming in her chest. They had been since the night the child scratched at the door.

She set the tray down outside the stockroom door, knocked twice—a pattern, low-high-low, so the girl would know it was her—and slipped inside.

Wren was awake. Of course she was awake. She sat cross-legged on the sailcloth in the grey light that came through the loading grate near the ceiling, and she had done something to the space that made Dominic's skin prickle: she had arranged the broken loom shuttles in a ring around herself, each one turned so its grain ran the same direction, and she was watching them the way other children watched fire.

"What are you doing?" Dominic asked, and then, because the question felt too much like an accusation, softened it: "Those aren't for playing with."

"They're listening." The girl said it simply, without self-consciousness, the way you'd say *the sky is blue*. "Things hold what's said near them, if you ask right. These heard a lot of talking before they broke."

Dominic stood very still in the doorway. In fifteen years of deliberate silence she had met hedge-witches and word-mongers, men who sold blessings by the ounce and women who could talk a lock open, and none of them—not one—had ever spoken of language as though it were a thing objects could *do*.

"You're hungry," she said instead, and put the tray down within reach.

Wren ate the way small creatures eat when they've learned hunger can last: fast, watchful, one hand always curled around the iron key on its cord at her throat. Dominic sat down on an upturned crate across from her, far enough away to be polite, close enough to catch her if she bolted, and studied her properly for the first time in daylight.

Small. Thin in the way that wasn't poverty exactly but flight—someone had fed this child, recently, but someone had also taught her to sleep sitting up. Dark hair cut raggedly, as if with a knife. A burn scar along her left forearm, old enough to have healed smooth. And the eyes. Dominic had spent fifteen years refusing to look at mirrors of any kind, metaphorical or otherwise, but there was no refusing this one: those eyes were her grandmother's eyes, and her grandmother's mother's, and every Yarrow who had ever signed their name in a book Dominic was not supposed to know existed.

"Your name," Dominic said. "You gave it to me last night. I want to hear it again."

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

The girl considered her over the bread. "Just Wren," she agreed, in the tone of someone agreeing to a lie both parties understand. Then, unexpectedly: "It's short for something. But the long name is—" and she said a word.

Not Alderman. Not quite. It was a name-shape, a sound that bent in the middle the way Alderman words bent, and Dominic's oath-bond flared hot under her breastbone like a coal breathed upon, and she had to press her fist against her sternum and breathe until the burning settled into ache.

"Don't do that," she said, sharper than she meant.

Wren flinched. Set down the bread. "I'm sorry. I didn't—"

"No." Dominic dragged a hand down her face. "No, I'm sorry. That was my fault, not yours. Just—" She searched for words that wouldn't cost anything. There were fewer of those in the world than people thought. "Some sounds hurt me. Old habit. Tell me about your village instead. The one you walked here from."

The girl pulled her knees up. For a long moment Dominic thought she'd lost her, that the wall would come down and she'd get nothing but silence until the child decided otherwise. Then Wren began to talk, and her voice changed—it went flat and careful, the recitation of something rehearsed to survive the telling.

"Marshedge. On the coast past the Salt Road fork. Everyone spoke it—all of us, even the ones who only knew the prayers and the counting songs. My gran said we'd kept it alive two hundred years by pretending we hadn't. We fished. We mended nets. When tax-men came we sounded like everyone else on the coast, and when they left we sang." Her fingers found the key and turned it over, over. "Soldiers came at harvest. Not tax-men. They had lists. Names written down—how did they have names? Gran said someone sold us, but I don't think you can sell a whole village." She looked up. "Can you?"

"I've seen smaller things sold," Dominic said quietly. "Go on."

"They took the elders first. Made everyone say words while they wrote them in a black book, and if your words matched the book you went in the cart, and if they didn't match you went in the—" Wren stopped. Started again, flatter. "They burned the granary with some people locked in. The ones who tried to run got cut down at the tide-gate. I got out because my gran pushed me down a well and told me to swim the outflow channel and not stop swimming until the sea tasted different." Her chin lifted a little. "I swam two days. There was a boat. The boat man didn't want me but I had the key and he thought it was worth something, so he brought me to the city and then he tried to take it off me at the dock and I—" she hesitated, and something flickered behind her eyes that made Dominic glad she hadn't finished the sentence, "—he changed his mind. Then I asked the stones which door would take me in, and they said this one."

*Asked the stones.* Dominic filed it away with all the other impossible things stacking up in her chest like cargo in a flooding hold.

"The soldiers," she said. "Did they wear grey? Black sashes? Whose colors?"

"Grey coats. Red thread at the collar." Wren's mouth twisted. "And they had a speaker with them. One of ours—or he used to be. He read the black book aloud and the words made the doors open and the fires burn hotter and nobody could run because running is a word too, and he'd taken it." She said it without drama, without tears, and somehow that was worse than weeping. "Gran said if I ever met a grown-up who spoke, I should check their hands first. Speakers who work for soldiers always have ink on their fingers, from writing down what they take."

Dominic looked at her own hands. Clean. Ink-stained, actually—from ledger work, harmless arithmetic—but clean of that particular stain. Fifteen years of keeping them clean. And yet here sat a child who had watched speakers do with words what Dominic had sworn never to do again, and the sight of her made something move in Dominic's chest that she refused to name, because naming it would mean it was real, and if it was real she would have to act on it, and acting on it would end the careful invisible life she had built plank by plank for a decade and a half.

"I'm going to keep you here," she heard herself say. "For now. A few days. You don't leave this room, you don't speak—that language—in the corridors, you don't tell anyone your name or show anyone that key. If anyone asks, you're my sister's girl, come from upcoast, slow of speech and slower of wit, and I'm stuck minding you. Can you remember that?"

"Slow of speech," Wren repeated solemnly. "Slower of wit."

"And don't enjoy it so much."

The ghost of a smile crossed the girl's face, there and gone, and Dominic felt it land somewhere beneath the oath-bond's dull burn and lodge there like a splinter.

The forbidden folios lived in the guild's deepest archive, behind a door that required three keys and a lie. Dominic had inherited access to two of the keys with her rank; the third she had taken from Master Quillon's own ring during the chaos of the spring inventory, and neither of them had ever acknowledged it. The lie was the easy part. The archive ward asked, in the old formula, *what do you seek*, and the correct answer—the answer that opened the door—was *nothing*. Every guildsman who passed that threshold had to stand in the dark and honestly claim to seek nothing, which meant the door only opened for liars, which Quillon had once told her, laughing into his cups, was the single most honest design decision the founders ever made.

Tonight she claimed nothing, and the door believed her.

The oldest folios were chained to their shelf—not to prevent theft, but to prevent removal, because paper that old crumbled if it traveled. Dominic carried the lamp along the racks until she reached the case marked only with a blank brass plate, and lifted down the volume she had touched exactly once before, at nineteen, on the night she forswore everything: the Founders' Register, bound in hide she preferred not to identify, its pages soft as cloth and brown as tea.

She had come looking for the sigil. The child's key bore it—stamped deep in the iron bow, a thorned circle broken at the top by an open gate—and Dominic had spent the day telling herself she was wrong, that grief and exhaustion were drawing patterns in coincidence, that a thousand sigils must look like that.

She turned pages. Trade records, mostly, in the crabbed hand of the first guildmaster: *one miracle, minor, exchanged for harbor rights; one curse, lifted, payment deferred; one door, sealed, terms below.*

Terms below.

The entry occupied a full leaf, and it was written in a different hand than the rest—larger, more formal, the letters of someone trained in scriptorium discipline. Dominic read it standing, because her legs had informed her they were no longer reliable.

*Item: the Door of Aldermere, being the last working passage between this world and the country of that name.*
*Condition: sealed by compact of the undersigned, at the request and joint expense of the free cities' founding councils.*
*Terms: the Door shall not be opened by any party, at any price, in perpetuity. The sealing sentence is divided among nine signatories, each holding one clause, none holding the whole. The full opening sentence is known to no living person.*
*Price paid: see annex. The annex has been removed by order of the councils.*
*Sigil of record:* —and there it was, drawn in faded iron-gall ink, the thorned circle with its broken crown. The gate at the top was drawn open in the sigil, and someone, long ago, had scratched a line of ink across it, closing it. The scratch was older than the page's other marks. Someone had wanted even the *drawing* shut.

And beneath, the signatories. Nine names, each followed by a clause-mark—a fragment of notation, half-word, half-sigil, meaningless alone. Dominic's eye went down the list the way a finger goes down a blade.

Seven names she didn't know. An eighth that might have been a founder of Harrowgate. And the ninth—

*Yarrow, Maren, of the Swordspeakers' Guild. Clause held: the seventh.*

Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother, who had sung her lullabies in two languages and taught her letters from a book with no title, whose hands had smelled of iron and lavender, who had died saying *never speak it, little bird, never, whatever knocks*—her grandmother had been one of the nine who sealed a whole country behind a door, and had handed the seventh clause of the opening sentence down her bloodline like an inheritance of debt.

Dominic set the lamp down because her hands were shaking, and stood in the archive dark breathing the smell of old vellum and older secrets, and understood, with the slow terrible clarity of a floor giving way, that the child sleeping in her stockroom had not come to her door by accident. Stones had told her which door would take her in. Stones remembered. And somewhere in the wreck of Marshedge, or in the black books of the usurper's soldiers, someone knew exactly what the last Yarrow was carrying in her blood.

The key at Wren's throat bore the sigil of the Door. Which meant either the girl's family had guarded it for two hundred years—

—or the key was not a key to the Door at all, but a key *from* it, and something on the other side wanted it back.

Dominic closed the Register. Chained it. Stood a moment longer in the dark with her palm flat against the blank brass plate, feeling the cold come through the metal as if from a great depth, and made herself a promise she already knew she'd break: just a few days. Just until the roads were safe. Just until she could hand the girl off to someone whose life wasn't built on a buried oath.

Above her, through six floors of guild, she could almost believe she heard it—faint, patient, unmistakable—a small voice asking the stones whether the tall sad ledger-woman would come back in the morning.

The stones, presumably, answered yes.

They were, Dominic reflected grimly, climbing back toward the light, exactly as honest as the archive door.
