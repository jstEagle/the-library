## 4. The Guild of Impossible Things

Master Quillon kept three ledgers on his desk, and only one of them was made of paper.

The first was the winter ledger, the honest one, the one the assessors from the Coinwrights' Compact were permitted to squint at each quarter: quantities of saffron and stormglass, contracts for memory-tinctures diluted to legal potency, the guild's entirely fictitious specialty in "rare perfumes." The second was the true ledger, kept in a cipher of his own devising, which recorded what the guild actually sold—impossible things, one per season, never more, priced so high that only princes and the occasional desperate archbishop could afford them. And the third ledger was the oldest, the forbidden one, chained shut with iron that hummed faintly when you touched it, and Quillon had not opened it in eleven years because the last time he had opened it, it had cost him two fingers and most of his certainty about how the world worked.

He was thinking about those fingers now, turning the third finger's knuckle absently as he listened to his factor report on the salt shipments, because somewhere above him, six floors up, in a stockroom full of bottled fog and unripe thunder, a child was hiding. And children did not hide in the Guild of Impossible Things unless someone very good at hiding things had put them there.

"—and the Vesseri buyer wants delivery by the thaw," Factor Ondrey was saying, "but the fog won't be decanted until—"

"Ondrey." Quillon said it mildly, and the factor stopped as if he'd walked into a wall of glass. "Who is in the north stockroom?"

The factor's face performed a small, complete collapse of composure. It was, Quillon thought with distant professional appreciation, rather well done—the way the man's eyes went first to the door, then to the window, then to the middle distance where liars store their courage. But Quillon had been reading faces for forty years, and had bought and sold the confessions behind them for thirty.

"No one, Master."

"Mm." Quillon rose, took his cane—it was mostly affectation, but affectation was armor, and armor was the guild's native language—and limped toward the stairs. "Come along. Let's go meet no one."

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a building older than the free cities around it, though the cities pretended not to notice. Its foundations were foundation-stones in the literal sense—quarried, according to the oldest leases, from "the first walls," whatever that meant—and its corridors did not behave. The sixth floor was farther from the fifth on some days than the fourth was from the street. Stairs occasionally declined to arrive. New hires were told simply: *count your footsteps, not your floors.* The building had moods. Quillon had long ago stopped pretending this was ordinary. He had also, crucially, stopped asking why, because the one time he had asked—in writing, in the forbidden ledger's margins—he had received an answer in handwriting identical to his own that said: *You know exactly what sleeps under the trade routes. Leave it sleeping.*

He had left it sleeping. That was the whole art of guildmastership, as he practiced it: leave everything sleeping, charge rent on the dreams.

Now he climbed toward the north stockroom with Ondrey sweating at his heel, and with every step he catalogued what he knew, because knowledge was inventory, and inventory was the only scripture he trusted.

Item: his best auditor, the Thackeray woman, fifteen years silent and reliable as gravity, had spent last night in the archive and had this morning requested access to the restricted folios through channels she must have known would reach his desk by noon. She had never once requested anything in fifteen years. Item: the restricted folios contained the old trade routes, the ones from before the Compact, before the prohibition, back when the guild's impossible things had included—

Quillon's hand tightened on the cane.

Back when the guild had trafficked in doors.

That was the word nobody wrote anymore. *Doors.* Not gates, which were architecture; not portals, which were poetry; doors, which were commerce. Two hinges, a threshold, terms and conditions. His grandmother's grandmother had grown rich on them, or so the second ledger's earliest pages implied, before some catastrophe Quillon could not reconstruct had turned the whole trade into a single line item, closed out with terrible finality: *paid in full, sealed by oath.* Sealed by whom? Paid to whom? The page didn't say. Pages that don't say are the most expensive pages in any ledger, and Quillon had spent eleven years telling himself he didn't want to know, the way a man tells himself he doesn't want to know what's making the noise under his floorboards, while quietly knowing all along.

Item: three nights ago, a child had scratched at the guild's front door during a salt gale, speaking a language that had made two of his porters drop a crate of bottled fog and made the third—one Herrick, who owed the guild nine months of wages and therefore discretion—come directly and secretly to Quillon, pale as milk, and say: *"Master, the words off that girl burned my ears like frost. Like frost going the wrong direction."*

And item, the last item, the one Quillon had been carrying in his chest like a coin too heavy for any pocket: the child had carried an iron key stamped with a sigil that Herrick, who could not read his own mother tongue reliably, had drawn accurately in spilled salt on the counting-room floor.

Quillon had looked at that sigil drawn in salt, and had felt eleven years of careful ignorance come apart like wet paper.

Because he had seen it before. Chained inside the forbidden ledger. Stamped into the iron band that sealed it. And once, long ago, in the margin of a shipping manifest from a country that was not on any map, written in a hand that signed itself *Thackeray.*

* * *

The north stockroom smelled of cedar and cold lightning. Bottles lined the walls in graduated sizes, each one breathing its slow weather; a cask of unripe thunder ticked in its corner like a patient egg. And between two shelves of decanted twilight stood Dominic Thackeray, arms crossed, positioned—with what Quillon recognized instantly as deliberate, insulting precision—directly in front of the gap between the shelving and the wall.

Behind her, in the shadow of that gap, two eyes caught the lamplight.

"Well," Quillon said pleasantly. "Master Thackeray. I see the guild's inventory has grown."

"Master Quillon." Her voice was flat as a settled account. "You're up past your floor."

It was the closest thing to insolence she'd offered in fifteen years, and Quillon found, to his surprise, that he almost admired it. He limped forward, leaned on his cane, and looked past her shoulder into the dark.

"Come out, child," he said. "I've bought and sold scarier things than you, and I let most of them retire."

A pause. Then she came—a small girl, thin as a winter reed, salt-matted hair, clutching something against her chest with both hands the way drowning folk clutch driftwood. She looked at Quillon with the particular expression he had seen on the faces of everything he had ever appraised: the look of something that knows it is being valued and fears the number.

She spoke then. One word, maybe two, in that language Herrick had described, and Quillon felt it happen to him exactly as it had been described: a cold that entered through the ear and went *inward*, toward places cold should never go. The cask of unripe thunder shivered. Every bottle on the western wall clouded over at once, fogged from inside, as if the weather within them had flinched.

The building, Quillon thought, six floors of impossible stone around him—the building *recognized* that language.

"Stop," he said quietly, and the girl stopped, looking to Dominic with a trust that answered many questions Quillon had not yet dared to form.

He lowered himself onto the stockroom's one stool, cane across his knees, and arranged his face into the expression he used for negotiations that mattered: kindly, reasonable, utterly without mercy.

"Her name is Wren," Dominic said.

"I didn't ask yet."

"She'll want to know eventually whether you asked."

Quillon studied his auditor. Fifteen years of ledgers and silence, and here she stood guarding a child like a wall built overnight on ground everyone thought was empty. He knew what that cost. He had watched her bury something in herself the day she arrived—young, gifted, hollow-eyed—and he had prudently never asked what it was, because the guild ran on the principle that you do not dig near other people's foundations.

"The sigil on the key," he said now. "Where is it from?"

Wren's hands tightened around the iron in her arms. Dominic's jaw set. And Quillon sighed, because he already knew, and because knowing made everything worse.

"Let me tell you a story instead," he said. "Since apparently we're all being honest tonight, which I consider a serious failure of management." He turned his stool slightly, gesturing with the cane at the bottles, the casks, the breathing inventory of the guild. "You think we deal in trinkets. Fog, thunder, love-philtres cut with rosewater for bishops with more guilt than gold. That's the shop window. But the guild is older than the Compact, older than the free cities, and in the beginning we dealt in one commodity only. The largest thing there is."

He let the pause do its work.

"Doors," he said. "Passage between countries that had no passage between them. Not smugglers' routes—not tunnels, not ships. *Doors.* You walked through a doorway in Aldermere and came out under the founders' stones of Harrowgate. The guild brokered them. We took a percentage of every crossing. We were, for two hundred years, the richest institution on the face of the earth, and then—" He stopped. The old anger, or fear, or whatever it was, moved behind his ribs. "And then something happened, and the trade ended in a single night, closed out in our oldest book as *paid in full, sealed by oath*, and every door in the world went cold, and the country called Aldermere was struck from every map by people who were very thorough, and I have spent eleven years not thinking about it. Eleven years, Master Thackeray. Do you know how much discipline it takes to not think about something? I ought to bill myself for it."

Dominic had gone very still. "Sealed by oath," she repeated. Her voice did something strange on the word *oath.*

"Yes." Quillon's eyes narrowed. He filed her reaction where he filed everything: inventory. "Now. Here is my problem. Three nights ago, a child knocks on my door speaking a dead tongue, carrying a key stamped with the seal from my most dangerous book. Yesterday, my most reliable auditor—who has never in fifteen years asked me for so much as a fresh inkwell—requests the restricted folios. Today I find the two of them hiding in my inventory like contraband." He leaned forward. "So let us speak plainly, since plain speech is apparently the fashion. There are soldiers on the salt road. My contacts in the harbor offices say they carry warrants for linguists, and—I quote the warrant—*'their harborers.'* Harborers, Master Thackeray. That word includes me. This building. Everything I have spent forty years building and eleven years keeping asleep. And you have put the reason for those warrants six floors above my vault."

"They burned her village," Dominic said. Flat. Final. A fact like a stone laid on a table.

"I don't doubt it." Quillon rubbed his thumb along the cane. "I believe you, which is precisely the problem. Because if I believe it, then I must decide what she is worth, and everything is worth something. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic. The usurper will pay a kingdom's ransom for the last speaker of Aldermere—or he will burn this city to get her, and either way the guild ends, unless the guild does what the guild has always done, which is to sell the inevitable at a profit instead of dying of it for free."

The girl—Wren—said something soft in the dead tongue. It wasn't directed at anyone. It sounded, Quillon thought with a lurch he refused to acknowledge, like comfort. She was comforting *them.*

"You're talking about selling her," Dominic said.

"I am talking out loud, which is different, and the difference is the whole of civilization." Quillon stood, joints announcing their grievances, and held out his hand. "The key. Give it to me."

Neither of them moved.

"Child," he said to Wren, gently, and it was even genuine, which annoyed him. "No one takes anything from you in this room while I draw breath. But I cannot help you—or price you, or price *this*—blindfolded. The key, please."

Wren looked up at Dominic. Dominic, after a moment, nodded once, a small grudging motion, and Wren crossed the space and laid the iron key in Quillon's palm.

It was heavier than iron had any right to be. Cold, but a living cold, the cold of deep cellars and deeper water. The sigil sat stamped in the bow: a circle broken by a thorn, and Quillon's mouth went dry, because he knew—had always known, in the cellar of himself where he kept the things he didn't think about—what door that seal belonged to. Not one of the two hundred. The one. The first door, the master door, the one the oldest ledger recorded not as a route or a percentage but as a debt *paid in full*—and paid by whom, and with what, the ledger declined to state, though the signatory's name was there in ink gone brown as old blood:

*Thackeray.*

He closed his fingers around the key and looked up at his auditor, at her white-knuckled stillness, and understood with terrible clarity that she did not know. Or half-knew. Or knew in the way we know all our inheritances, which is to say in nightmares.

"Well," Quillon said lightly, because lightness was also armor. "It appears, Master Thackeray, that your family has done business with mine before. Long before."

"What do you mean?" Flat. Too flat.

"I mean," Quillon said, tucking the key into his coat like a man pocketing a live coal, "that this object requires appraisal, and there is exactly one book in this building qualified to receive it, and it has been eleven years since anyone opened it, and the last person who opened it lost two fingers to the iron band." He flexed his right hand; the missing finger's ghost ached, as it always did, in weather like this. "Tomorrow, at first bell, you will meet me in the archive, and together we will open the forbidden ledger, and we will find out what that seal is, what that key opens, and what it costs. And then, and only then, Master Thackeray, will I decide what your Wren is worth—and to whom."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll both be gone from this building by dawn, and I will genuinely grieve you, and the soldiers on the salt road will find you anyway, because I am the kindest man who will ever have this conversation with you." He turned for the stairs, leaning hard on the cane, and paused at the door without looking back. "One more thing. The language she speaks. When it touched the air just now, every bottle in this room clouded over. Do you know why?"

"No," Dominic said.

"Neither do I," said Quillon. "But I know this building, better than anyone alive, and buildings remember their foundations. And for one moment, Master Thackeray—for exactly as long as that child's voice was in the air—this entire guild leaned toward her. Six floors of stone, listening." He limped out into the corridor, and his voice drifted back down the stairwell, mild as a bill presented.

"So tomorrow we open the book. And pray, both of us, that it stays asleep."

* * *

Alone in the north stockroom, among the bottled weather, Wren asked Dominic a question in the dead tongue.

Dominic didn't answer. She was watching the doorway Quillon had gone through, and her hand had risen unconsciously to her own throat, to the place where, beneath fifteen years of silence, an oath lay coiled like a banked fire—and for the first time since she was a girl, it had warmed.

Above them both, below them both, all through the founders' stones the guild stood upon, something old turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to listen for its name.
