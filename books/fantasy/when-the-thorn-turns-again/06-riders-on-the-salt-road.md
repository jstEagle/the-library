## 6. Riders on the Salt Road

The salt road ran straight as a sentence from the eastern passes to the gates of Vessengard, and Sergeant Ilva Kett had spent eleven days marching it in the company of four hundred men who no longer spoke to her.

Not because she'd done anything wrong. Because she'd done nothing at all.

She rode at the head of the third file, her mare picking through the ruts where the autumn rains had chewed the road to porridge, and behind her the vanguard moved with the particular silence of soldiers who had learned that their officers' ears were everywhere now. Two weeks ago they had sung on the march—filthy songs, old songs, songs about girls in Vessengard and wine that tasted like regret. Now they marched like a funeral for someone nobody would name.

The warrants had come down from Colonel Ashe on the ninth day, read aloud at dawn in a voice pitched flat as slate. *By order of the Lord Protector, all persons practicing or harboring the forbidden tongue are to be detained. Linguists and their harborers. No exceptions. No appeals.*

Linguists. Ilva had turned the word over in her mouth all morning, tasting its strangeness. In her village—a mud-splashed nothing of a place three days south of the pass—they had called such people talkers. Old Maren who could hush a fever with a phrase. The ferryman who sang his boat across the flooded river in a language his grandmother's grandmother had carried out of a country that wasn't on any map anymore. Nobody had called them dangerous. Nobody had called them anything at all. They were just part of the texture of things, like weather, like bread.

Now there was a word for them, and the word came with a seal stamped in red wax, and the seal came with four hundred men.

"Kett." Captain Dreev reined up beside her, close enough that she caught the smell of him—leather, horse sweat, and under it the sweetish rot of whatever he chewed to stay awake on night rides. "Scout report."

"Tanner's mill is burned. Half a day back." She kept her eyes on the road. "Nobody in it when it went up, or the smoke would've smelled different."

"And beyond?"

"The salt flats. Then the free cities." She said it without inflection, but she felt the shape of what she wasn't saying press against her teeth: *and everything between us and those walls is people, Dreev. Farmers. Ferriers. Maybe an old woman who sings.*

Dreev grunted. He was not a cruel man, as far as she had ever been able to tell. That was the thing about this army that frightened her most—it wasn't built out of cruelty. It was built out of men like Dreev, men who followed orders the way water follows a slope, without malice and without thought, and somewhere above them all sat the one man who did the thinking, and he thought only of a door.

She'd heard the rumors like everyone else. That the Lord Protector himself was born east of nowhere, a refugee brat from a drowned country, and that everything—the burning villages, the red-waxed warrants, the long grey column of them crawling west—was so he could go home again. Home. As if home were a place you could take by force. As if you could burn your way back into your mother's arms.

"Colonel wants the vanguard in sight of Vessengard by week's end," Dreev said. "We'll billet at the crossroads village tonight. Marrow's Ford."

"I know it," Ilva said, and then wished she hadn't.

"You do?"

"My sister married a cooper there. Before." Before the war, before the conscription, before the word *linguist* entered the language. Before.

Dreev looked at her for a moment too long. "Then you'll know where the well is," he said, and spurred ahead.

* * *

Marrow's Ford was smaller than she remembered, which was how she knew how much she herself had grown, and sadder than she remembered, which was how she knew what the last two years had cost everyone. They rode in at dusk with the light going copper on the wet cobbles, and the villagers stood in their doorways watching them come with faces like closed shutters.

Ilva gave the orders she was given to give. The granary for the horses. The inn for the officers. The square for the men. And then the fourth order, the new one, the one printed on paper that crackled like frost: houses to be searched for written matter in foreign scripts. Persons of interest to be reported to the sergeant of the watch.

*Sergeant of the watch.* That was her. She was the person persons got reported to.

She walked the village streets as the searches began, and she made herself look at everything. A boy of perhaps eight peering around his mother's skirts. An old man mending a net by lamplight, hands steady, eyes down. The cooperage with its hoops stacked outside her brother-in-law's door—her brother-in-law, Tam, who had once held her upside down over the ford until she laughed so hard she cried, standing now in his own doorway while a corporal pawed through his cupboards.

"Nothing here," the corporal reported, and moved on, and Tam looked at Ilva across the width of his own threshold, and she could not hold his gaze, and he saw that she couldn't, and something passed between them that was worse than anger. It was disappointment. It was the specific, quiet grief of a man watching a child he loved turn into a stranger.

She kept walking.

It was near midnight when the knock came at the guardhouse door—not a knock, really, more a hammering, and Corporal Vesh answered it with his hand already on his knife. Outside stood two riders from the second file, mud to their thighs, and between them, bound at the wrists, a man in a ferryman's oilskin coat.

Ilva knew him. Of course she knew him. Joss Marlow, who poled travelers across the swollen river every spring, whose grandmother's grandmother had come out of the east, who sang while he worked in a language that rolled like river stones. She'd crossed on his boat twice before the war. Once he'd taught her the song's first line, laughing, saying she butchered it beautifully.

"What's the charge?" she asked, and her voice came out level, and she hated how level it came out.

"He talked," said one of the riders. "At the checkpoint past the flats. Soldier asked him his business, and he answered in the old tongue first. Didn't mean anything by it, probably—habit, or stupidity—but Corporal Renk heard it. Reported it."

"Answered in the forbidden script?" Ilva asked, because procedure required the question.

"Just spoken. One word. Maybe two."

One word. Maybe two. She looked at Joss Marlow, and he looked back at her, and his face was the terrible calm of a man who has already understood everything and is waiting only for the rest of the world to catch up. There was blood at the corner of his mouth where the rope had jerked him.

"Put him in the storeroom," Ilva said. "Colonel Ashe arrives tomorrow with the interrogator. He'll want to see this one personally."

She didn't know why she said that last part. It wasn't in any warrant. It simply fell out of her, the way a stone falls—because it was heavy, and because holding it had become impossible.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself it was the watch rotation, and then she stopped telling herself anything, and sat in the guardhouse doorway with her cloak around her shoulders watching the moon move over the salt flats. The flats were silver and endless and utterly empty, and somewhere beyond them lay the free cities, glowing in the dark like coals banked for morning, full of people who did not yet know what was coming down the salt road toward them.

Near the second hour, a sound made her turn her head.

Singing. From the direction of the storeroom. Low, barely more than breath, threaded through the gaps in the door planks. Joss Marlow, bound in a room that smelled of tallow and rat droppings, was singing.

Ilva rose before she understood she had decided to rise, crossed the yard, and stood at the door listening. It was the song he'd taught her. The crossing-song. She knew now—it had taken her years, and a war, to learn it—that the words weren't nonsense, weren't just pretty sounds. They were a blessing for the far shore. *May the water be kind. May the far side be waiting.* Something like that. Her Alderman was nothing; she'd only ever owned that single line, borrowed, mispronounced, given freely by a man on a boat.

He stopped singing when her shadow fell across the gap in the door.

"Sergeant Kett," he said through the wood. His voice was wrecked but steady. "You still say it wrong."

Her throat closed. She put her hand flat against the door plank, feeling the grain of it, feeling the absurd warmth of the wood as if the man on the other side were heating it with his body, his life, his one or two careless words.

"Why did you sing it first?" she asked. "At the checkpoint. Why not just answer the soldier?"

A silence, long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Because it's my mouth, girl. My tongue lives in it. A man answers with what he has." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Your colonel thinks a language is a weapon. It's not. It's a house. You don't burn a house because you're afraid of what might be hiding in the cellar."

*What might be hiding in the cellar.* The words went through her like cold water, though she couldn't have said why. Somewhere in the free cities, right now, there were people speaking that tongue—old women, children, maybe—and somewhere in the dark of the world there was a door that a whole army had been built to find, and she was standing on the wrong side of a wooden door in a village she used to love, holding the key to a man's death in the simple act of doing nothing.

"Is there someone coming for you?" she asked quietly. "Family. Anyone."

"No one living speaks it whole anymore, not out here." Another pause. "There's a child, I heard. Down the coast road. A little girl who talks in dreams." He said it lightly, a rumor tossed off, but something in his tone snagged—something careful, deliberate, a coin slipped into her palm disguised as small talk. "They say the soldiers burned her village for it. Say she ran. Say anyone who shelters her—" he let it hang.

"Harbors her," Ilva finished. "I've read the warrant."

"Then you know better than me what happens next."

She took her hand off the door. Stood there another moment in the cold, hearing her own breath, hearing the sea-silence of the flats. Then she went to the duty desk, took the ration of bread and the skin of water that was issued to prisoners, and carried them back, and pushed them through the gap in the planks without a word, and he took them without a word either, and that transaction—bread through a crack in a door, in the dark, unwitnessed—felt like the largest thing she had ever done, and the smallest, and both at once.

* * *

Colonel Ashe arrived at midmorning with a waggon and six and a passenger.

The interrogator came off the waggon like something poured rather than stepped—a tall, narrow man in civilian grey, soft-shoed, with a clerk's posture and a face so unremarkable that Ilva forgot it twice in the first minute of looking at him. He introduced himself as Master Corvin, attached to the Office of Restoration, and he smiled at Colonel Ashe with exactly the right amount of deference, and then he asked to see the prisoner.

They set it up in the tithe barn because the storeroom was too small for witnesses. Ashe wanted witnesses. Witnesses meant the record would show proper procedure had been observed. Ilva stood at the back among the noncoms, close enough to see, positioned—she noted with a shame that arrived late and settled deep—where she could not be seen clearly from the front.

Joss Marlow knelt on the barn floor with his wrists bound before him. Master Corvin circled him slowly, hands clasped behind his back, and spoke in a reasonable, almost gentle voice about cooperation, about how the Lord Protector bore no hatred for any people, about how the old tongue was not being destroyed but *collected*, preserved, brought safely home.

"All we require," Corvin said, "is the word."

Joss said nothing.

"The word you spoke at the checkpoint. We have the report. We have the sound of it described—two syllables, rising tone, ending open. But description is not the thing itself, is it? Language dies when it's described. It lives only in mouths." Corvin crouched beside him, and his voice dropped into something warmer, more intimate, the register of a confessor. "Say it for me. Just the one word. And you walk out of this barn, and I write in my ledger that the man was mistaken, confused, loyal even—loyal enough to answer his own country's call first. No one will ever know. Not your neighbors. Not your customers. Just a word, given back to its own country, and freedom for you."

And Ilva, watching from the shadows at the back of the barn, felt the trap close around her own chest, because she understood—with the sick clarity of a woman seeing her own reflection in a window she'd thought was a mirror—that the offer was not really aimed at Joss Marlow at all.

It was aimed at everyone in the barn. At Ashe, at the corporals, at her. *This is how easy it is,* the offer said. *This is how reasonable. One word. One signature. One small compliance, and you remain a good person. Complicity doesn't arrive as evil. It arrives as paperwork.*

Joss Marlow lifted his head. Blood had dried at the corner of his mouth. He looked past Corvin, past the colonel, past all the armed and witnessing men, and found Ilva at the back of the barn the way a compass finds north, and he smiled—tired, unhurried—and he opened his mouth.

"Vessha," he said.

The word filled the barn like a struck bell. Ilva felt it in her sternum, in her teeth, in some locked room inside her she hadn't known she had. It meant, she somehow knew without knowing how, *the far shore*. Or possibly *home*. Possibly both. Some words are like that.

Corvin exhaled slowly, reverently, the way a man exhales over treasure, and nodded to the guards. "Take him to the waggon," he said. "He rides with us to Vessengard. And Sergeant—"

Ilva's spine went rigid. He hadn't turned around. He couldn't have seen her.

"—Sergeant Kett," Corvin continued, addressing the air, smiling faintly, "you'll be pleased to know the Office of Restoration values observant soldiers. Your name came up in dispatches. Compassionate, thorough, local knowledge of these villages." Now he did turn, and his unremarkable face found hers across the barn with the ease of long practice. "We'll have use for someone like you in the city. Someone who understands that people are not the enemy—only the words are. Report to me when we make the walls."

He walked out. The barn emptied. The light through the slats lay in pale stripes across the trampled straw where a man had knelt, and Ilva stood alone in it, holding the shape of his smile, the weight of the bread through the door-crack, the memory of a word that meant *the far shore*, and beneath all of it, rising like water into a hull with a slow, patient, inexorable pressure, the understanding of what she had already become—and of how narrow the remaining distance was between becoming and being.

That evening, the column broke camp for Vessengard. Ilva mounted, checked her file, gave the order to advance.

Behind her, lashed into the prison waggon, very softly, Joss Marlow began to sing.
