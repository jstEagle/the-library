## 8. Found Company

Dominic had always imagined that if she ever ran, she would run well. Fifteen years of invisibility had been, in its way, training: she knew which alleys behind the guild took the cart traffic and which took the watch, which doors were painted shut and which merely looked it. She had a satchel packed under a loose floorboard since her third year—coin, a whetstone, two shirts, and nothing else, because a person who expected to flee did not let herself own anything worth weeping over.

She had never imagined she would be running with a child's hand in hers and the taste of burned paper still in her mouth.

Wren kept up without complaint, small boots slapping wet cobbles, but her grip on Dominic's fingers was ferocious, and every time a lantern swung past an alley mouth the girl flinched like something struck.

"They're not following," Dominic said, for the fourth time. "I'd hear them."

"You'd *hear* them?"

"I have good ears." It was true, though not for the reason Wren thought. The oath-bond had quieted to an ember since the burning of the page, since Quillon's face had gone grey and his voice had said *you are no longer of this guild* with the finality of a man closing a ledger. But it was not silent. Somewhere beneath her breastbone it sat like a coal raked to the edge of the grate, and it warmed when danger came near, and it had been cold as river stone all night.

"Where are we going?" Wren asked.

"To people who owe me," Dominic said, and then, because lying to this child had begun to feel like a stone in her boot, "and to one person I've never met, and one person everyone says is dead."

They went first to Ferro.

The mapmaker kept rooms above a sailmaker's loft on Cordage Street, three flights up a stair that smelled of pitch and old hemp. Dominic had bought maps from him twice a year for a decade—harbor charts, road surveys, the dull honest work of a dull honest trade—and had never once asked about the rumor that followed him through the cartographers' quarter like a smell of smoke: that Ferro had once drawn a country into existence, or tried to, and been struck from the rolls for it.

She knocked the way you knock when you might need to run again: quick, twice, thumb covering the knocker's crest.

Footsteps. A bolt. The door opened a hand's width on a chain, and one eye appeared—brown, bloodshot, ringed by skin gone soft and folded, the eye of a man who worked by candlelight too many nights running.

"Dominic Thackeray." The eye narrowed. "At my door at the hour when honest people are asleep. Either the guild has finally collapsed, or you want something impossible."

"Both," Dominic said.

The chain rattled off and the door swung wide, and Ferro stood revealed: a broad, rumpled man in a coat stained with ink at every cuff, his beard trimmed by no instrument more precise than a knife. He looked past her shoulder, down at Wren, and something moved across his face like weather.

"Come in before the street sees you," he said. "Both of you. Quickly."

His rooms were a paper avalanche—charts pinned to every wall, rolled vellum standing in barrels like kindling, a long table buried under a half-finished survey of the northern coast. He cleared two stools with a sweep of his arm that dislodged a decade of drafts, put water on a little spirit stove, and then stood with his arms folded, waiting, in the manner of a man who had learned that questions asked too fast got answered with lies.

So Dominic gave him the truth, or as much of it as could survive being spoken aloud: a child speaking a dead tongue, soldiers hunting language itself, a page burned out of the guild's oldest ledger, and a key stamped with a sigil she had last seen as a girl.

While she talked, Wren wandered—not rudely, but with the gravity of someone walking through a church—among the pinned charts. Her finger rose toward one near the window, a coastal survey like a hundred others, and stopped.

"This isn't right," Wren said.

Ferro went very still. "What isn't?"

"The coastline." Wren traced it, frowning. "It bends wrong here. There should be—" she made a sound, a word in Alderman, low and rounded, "—there should be a place where the river meets the sea, and the reeds grow taller than a man, and there's a shrine on a mound where you leave your troubles so the tide can carry them away."

"That's—" Ferro's voice cracked and he started again. "That's the Merefen. That's Aldermere."

"It's home," Wren said simply.

Dominic watched Ferro the way she might have watched a man take a wound he'd been carrying for years and only now notice the bleeding. He crossed the room slowly, as if the floor might object, and stood looking at his own chart—at the smooth invented coastline he had drawn because the real one had been erased and someone, somewhere, had to keep drawing it.

"Twenty-two years," he said quietly. "I've been drawing that bend wrong for twenty-two years, because I only ever saw it from the seaward side, and no survivor would talk to me, and the maps they burned didn't show the rivers." He turned to Wren, and his big ink-stained hands hovered near her shoulders without touching, as if asking permission even to gesture. "Child. Would you tell me? All of it? Every river, every road, every—"

"Ferro." Dominic put a warning in his name.

He rounded on her, and for a moment the mild ruined cartographer was gone and something older and harder looked out. "You come to me at midnight with a child who speaks Alderman and ask me for help, and you think I will help you less because she can give me back a country?" His voice dropped. "Do you know what they took my license for? Not falsification. *Speculation.* Drawing places that don't exist on official parchment. They said I'd gone mad. Maybe I had. I drew Aldermere forty times from memory and rumor and the shape of shipping routes, and every time it came out different, and every time it was wrong, and I kept doing it anyway because the alternative was agreeing that a million people had never existed."

The spirit stove hissed. Wren tugged Ferro's sleeve.

"The shrine mound," she said, "is called the Troubles' Rest, and if you draw it, you have to draw the willows leaning east, because the wind comes off the fen and never changes its mind."

Ferro sat down slowly on his stool, like a man whose legs had decided the matter without consulting him.

"I'll need paper," he said hoarsely. "A great deal of paper."

"We're not staying," Dominic said. "Quillon sold us out or will soon, and when the usurper's men come asking about a disgraced swordspeaker and a child, your neighbors will remember everything. We need a place outside the walls. Somewhere no warrant reaches."

Ferro looked at her for a long moment, and then at Wren, and then at his wrong-bent coastline.

"I know a lighthouse," he said. "Disused. On the Salt Road, north of the tollstones. The keeper drowned eleven years ago and the Guild of Lights never replaced him because no ship needs a light where no ship goes." He was already pulling a waxed satchel from under his bed, already sweeping instruments into it with the economy of a man who had rehearsed leaving. "I've used it before. For storage. For—" a pause, "—for things I shouldn't have kept."

"What things?" Dominic asked.

"Maps," Ferro said. "Of countries that aren't."

* * *

Sable found *them*, which was the first thing about Sable that anyone needed to understand.

They had made perhaps two miles along the dark spine of the Salt Road, Ferro limping cheerfully and refusing to admit it, Wren riding his shoulders with the sudden boneless trust of children who decide quickly and completely, when a voice said out of the hedge-shadow beside them:

"She's prettier than the description."

Dominic's knife was in her hand before the sentence ended. Ferro yelped. Wren, unnervingly, only turned her head with interest.

A woman stepped out of the hedgerow as if hedges were a place she lived—a narrow woman in a man's oilskin, hood back, hair cropped close as a convict's, hands bare despite the cold. She was perhaps forty and looked younger until you reached her eyes, which had the flat patience of deep water.

"Sable," Dominic said. The name tasted like the guild's cellar. Everyone in the free cities knew the stories: a thief who didn't steal purses. Who put two fingers to your temple while you slept and left you lighter by a summer afternoon, or a first kiss, or the name of your mother's killer, whatever you could least afford to lose. Half the stories said she worked for the guild. The other half said the guild was terrified of her.

"The same." Sable looked at Wren on Ferro's shoulders with an expression Dominic couldn't read, and didn't like that she couldn't. "You're loud, do you know that? Both of you. You burned a page out of Quillon's forbidden folio tonight, and grief like that carries. And the little one—" she tilted her head, "—the little one dreams in a dead language, and dreams leak. I've been able to find you since the fish market, if I'd wanted to."

"Why haven't you?" Dominic kept the knife low and loose. "And how do you know about any of this?"

"Because I stole it." Sable said it the way other people admitted to eating breakfast. "Two nights ago I lifted the location of a certain iron key out of a guild clerk named Master Ellery, who appraised it before Quillon locked it away. Very tidy memory, very well indexed. I know what the key opens. I know what the sigil means. And I know that by tomorrow noon, the usurper's men will have taken the same knowledge out of Ellery, because they caught him at the tollhouse this evening and he doesn't have my discipline."

The road was very quiet. Somewhere behind them, faint as a thought, the bells of the free cities rang the second hour.

"You're telling me we're already followed," Ferro said.

"I'm telling you that you're slow." Sable smiled, and it was not entirely unkind. "There's a lighthouse north of the tollstones. Don't look surprised; I told you I'm thorough. I can get you there ahead of the warrants, and I can teach you what Ellery knew, and in exchange—"

"In exchange for what?" Dominic asked. "Coin? I have coin."

"Coin." Sable examined her as if pricing her. "No. Here is my price, swordspeaker, and you may refuse it. When this is finished—if it finishes—I want to be there. I want to see the door." Her flat eyes flicked, for just a heartbeat, toward Wren. "Because twenty years ago I stole a memory out of a dying woman's head, and it was of that door, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever held in my life, and I sold it to buy my freedom out of a prison cell, and I have regretted it every day since with a completeness that frightens me. I want to see it with my own eyes. That's the price. My eyes."

Nobody spoke. The hedge rustled. Wren slid down from Ferro's shoulders, walked forward through the frost-killed grass until she stood directly in front of Sable, and looked up at her with the frank appraisal of the very young.

"You're sad," Wren said. "All the way down. Like a well."

Sable went absolutely still—the stillness of a thief caught, though there was nothing to catch.

"Yes," she said at last.

"My grandmother was like that. Before the soldiers." Wren reached up and patted her hand, once, matter-of-fact. "It gets better if you stop being alone."

Dominic closed her knife. Later she would examine the fact that a seven-year-old had negotiated her alliance for her; tonight, on a cold road with warrants blooming behind them like mold, she found she didn't have the strength to argue with it.

"Lighthouse," she said. "Dawn. Don't steal anything of mine on the way."

"No promises," said Sable, and melted back into the hedge.

* * *

Nan Hollis was not hard to find, because Nan Hollis had never once in eighty years done anything that made herself hard to find.

Her cottage stood alone where the Salt Road bent toward the marshes, wattle and daub and a roof of reed thatch gone silver with age, a garden of herbs gone leggy and wild around a doorstep swept cleaner than any nobleman's hall. Smoke curled from the chimney at an hour when every sensible household banked its fire. A lamp burned in the window. And as they came down the lane—Dominic, Wren, Ferro limping, and Sable appearing out of the dark beside them as if she had been there the whole way—they heard, thin and clear over the winter fields:

singing.

It was an old tune, worn round as a river stone, in a language that made the ember under Dominic's breastbone flare so suddenly she staggered and caught the gatepost.

*Alderman.* An old woman alone on the marsh edge, singing Alderman lullabies to nobody, at midnight, with the usurper's edicts nailed to every market cross in the free cities.

Wren broke from them all and ran up the path.

The door opened before the child reached it. Nan Hollis stood framed in lamplight—a tiny woman, stooped, wrapped in three shawls, with a face like a walnut carved by someone fond of walnuts and eyes that had seen everything twice and been impressed by very little of it. She looked at Wren. Wren looked at her. And then Nan finished the verse she'd been singing, deliberately, all the way to the end, and Wren—voice trembling, word-perfect—answered with the line that came after it.

Nan Hollis sat down right there on her own doorstep, shawls and all.

"Sixty years," she said. Her voice shook. "Sixty years I've sung that song and never once had the answer sung back to me. My mother taught me before we ran. She said, *keep it in your mouth, Hollis-girl, even if it's only a song, even if they laugh, because a language lives as long as one mouth holds it.*" She looked up at Wren, and laughed, and wept, both at once, without embarrassment. "Look at you. Look at the size of you. Come in, all of you, before you freeze, and don't stand there gawking. Yes, I know why you've come. Word travels faster than horses when the word is *they've found a child who speaks.*"

Inside, the cottage was warm and dense with living: dried herbs hanging from beams, a cat with an opinion, a kettle already on because Nan Hollis had apparently known they were coming since roughly Tuesday. She fed them barley soup and brown bread without being asked twice, and while they ate she talked, in the unhurried way of the very old, laying out sixty years of hiding like cards on a table.

She had been nine years old when Aldermere fell—or was closed; she used both words and spat slightly on neither. Her family had come out through the last days of the exodus, among the thousands who scattered into the free cities and learned, within a generation, that survival meant silence. Parents stopped teaching children. Grandmothers sang their lullabies only to themselves, or disguised the words, or buried them so deep in nonsense syllables that even the singers forgot what they carried.

"But songs remember," Nan said, tapping her temple. "Songs are stubborn. I've forgotten my mother's face and I remember every verse she ever sang me, and it was only last winter, sitting alone here with my feet in the oven like a fool, that I understood what the verses *were.*"

"What were they?" Ferro asked, pen already in hand, ink already on his fingers because the man could not be within ten feet of information without committing it to paper.

"Directions." Nan smiled with fierce satisfaction. "Every lullaby I know is a map wearing a nightgown. Which roads led to the door. What stones marked it. What words the door liked to hear." She turned to Wren, and her voice gentled into something almost frightened. "But here's my trouble, lamb. There are verses in those songs I've never been able to sing straight through—phrases that tangle my tongue, lines that go somewhere my mouth won't follow. Sixty years I thought it was just age, or fear, or that the old singer had garbled them. Then this child walks in and sings the answer-line back to me like it was nothing."

She reached across the table and took Wren's small hand in her two wrinkled ones.

"Tell me, love. When I sing the tangled parts—can you hear where they go?"

Wren listened while Nan sang, head tipped, brow furrowed with the effort, and twice she stopped the old woman and corrected a vowel, gently, the way you'd correct someone's grip on a hammer. And on the third song—the one about the thorn hedge and the sleeping king—Wren went pale, and set down her spoon, and said:

"These aren't just directions. These are instructions. This one says—" her lips moved, translating silently, and then aloud, in the common tongue, haltingly: "*The door wakes for a full sentence, and a full sentence takes two voices, and the voices must be—one that stayed, and one that came back.*"

The fire popped. The cat stretched. Nobody breathed.

"One that stayed," Ferro repeated, writing. "One that came back. Stayed where? Came back from where?"

"I don't know," Wren said. "The song knows, but it's in the part that's broken. The part nobody remembers whole."

Nan Hollis looked around the table—at the disgraced mapmaker, at the memory-thief sitting very quietly by the fire with an expression like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like, at the disgraced swordspeaker who hadn't touched her soup—and her old eyes gleamed wet and sharp.

"Well," she said briskly, wiping her face with a shawl-corner. "Then we'd best start remembering, hadn't we? There are more of us than you'd think, dearies. Old ones, scattered ones, half-speakers who only hold a word or two apiece like coins in a torn pocket. I've kept track. It's what I do instead of dying." She stood, joints crackling like green wood, and began ladling out more soup whether anyone wanted it or not. "Eat. Sleep. Tomorrow we make a list."

* * *

Later, past midnight, Dominic stood in the cottage doorway and watched the winter stars wheel over the marshes, and listened to the sounds behind her: Ferro's pen scratching, relentless, transcribing songs; Sable humming something she probably didn't know she remembered; Nan crooning to Wren, who had fallen asleep against her shawls mid-verse; the cat purring like a small engine.

Four days ago she had been a ledger-keeper with a packed bag and no reason to unpack it. Now she was the center of something with weight and warmth and terrible fragility, and every instinct honed by fifteen years of hiding told her that things like this were exactly what got people killed—that love was just another thing the usurper's men could use as a handle.

And underneath that voice, quieter, older, the ember said: *this is what the oath was for.*

A light was moving on the far road. Not lantern-light—lanterns bobbed. This was steady, white, disciplined: torches carried in formation, a long way off yet, coming up the Salt Road from the tollstones.

Counting them was habit. There were too many to count.

Dominic stepped back inside and shut the door softly on the forming family, and on the list they would make at dawn, and on the broken song waiting in Nan Hollis's stubborn memory—and began, quietly, moving furniture against the walls.

"Everyone," she said, "wake up. We have company."
