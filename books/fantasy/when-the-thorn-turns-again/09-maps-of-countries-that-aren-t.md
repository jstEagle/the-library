## 9. Maps of Countries That Aren't

The room at the top of Ferro's lodging house had no windows worth mentioning—two of them, both cracked, both painted over by a previous tenant who had feared the sea—and this suited him fine. He did not want the world looking in. He had spent thirty years making sure the world could not see what he kept here, and old habits held like pitch.

He heard them coming up the stairs before they knocked: Dominic's measured tread, the lighter, quicker one beside it that belonged to the child, and Sable's near-silent glide behind. He had known Dominic since she was a junior clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a gift she pretended not to have, and in all those years she had never once come to his rooms without reason. She came now with a child who spoke a dead language, a thief who stole memories, and the look of a woman who had burned her own ledger page and was still smelling the smoke.

Ferro opened the door before they knocked.

"You knew we were coming," Dominic said.

"I heard you on the stair." He stepped back to let them in. "Also, everyone in the free cities is coming to someone's door eventually. Mine was due."

The room was small and crammed to the rafters. Shelves bowed under folios; rolled charts stood in barrels like sheathed swords; brass instruments hung from hooks on every wall—astrolabes, dividers, a sextant whose glass had gone cloudy with age. And covering nearly every flat surface, pinned and layered and overlapping like scales on some enormous patient fish: maps.

Wren stopped just inside the door and stared. The child had been staring at everything for days—the guild stockrooms, Nan Hollis's kitchen, the fish market where they'd nearly died—but she stared at maps the way other children stared at sweetshops.

"Are these all real places?" she asked.

"Most of them." Ferro closed the door and shot the bolt. "Some of them aren't."

"That's the interesting answer," Sable said from the corner, already drifting along the shelves with a thief's idle hands folded ostentatiously behind her back. "Which ones?"

"The ones I've spent my life drawing."

* * *

He made tea because it gave his hands something honest to do while he decided how much truth to pour out. It was not a decision he made lightly. Thirty years ago, when the guild had stripped him of his credentials—he could still recite the charge verbatim, *unauthorized surveying, fabrication of geographic claims, bringing the Guild into disrepute*—he had understood that no one would ever again pay him to draw a coastline. What he had not understood then, young and furious as he was, was that it didn't matter. A mapmaker who cannot be hired draws anyway. The hand wants what it wants.

So he drew. He drew the coasts he could verify and the rivers he could infer, and then, when those ran out, he began drawing the country that appeared in the gaps.

Aldermere.

It started, as most obsessions do, with an error. He had been apprenticed then, copying a merchant's route chart from the salt roads east of the free cities, and he noticed that three separate source documents each contained a small impossibility: a toll recorded in a currency that matched nothing minted anywhere; a distance between towns that only worked if there were four days of road where the charts showed open moor; and, in the margin of the oldest document, written in a cramped clerk's hand, a single word he could not read. Not corrupted. Not smudged. *Written*, deliberate and confident, in an alphabet that appeared nowhere else in any archive he searched.

He had brought it to his masters. They had looked at the word, and he had watched something pass across their faces—not confusion but recognition, and beneath the recognition, fear—and they had told him to burn the margin and copy the rest clean. He burned the margin. He copied the word first.

That was the beginning. Everything after was thirty years of the same motion: find, copy, hide, connect.

"They'll want to know why," Dominic said now, settling onto the one stool that wasn't buried under paper. She said it to Sable and Wren, but her eyes were on him. "Why a disgraced mapmaker spends decades reconstructing a country that every government in the world agrees never existed."

"Because they're wrong," Ferro said. "And being wrong at that scale offends me."

Sable laughed. Wren didn't laugh. The child had gone very still, the way she went still around anything that touched Aldermere, and her eyes were moving across the walls of maps with an expression Ferro had seen once before—on the face of a man reading a letter from a son reported dead.

"It's real," Wren said. Not a question.

"It's real," Ferro agreed. "Come here. All of you. This will go easier if you see it the way I built it, piece by piece, or you'll think I'm mad, which—" he shrugged, "—I may be, but I'm mad with evidence."

* * *

He cleared a space on the great table by lifting off forty pounds of charts and setting them on the floor, which pained him physically, and laid down a single sheet: the free cities themselves, drawn at large scale, accurate to within a pace. Every street, every foundation stone of the old quarter, every seawall and cistern, annotated in his own tight script.

"This is home. This part no one disputes. Now watch."

From a drawer lined in felt he took a second sheet, older, foxed with damp stains, and laid it over the first, aligning the edges. It was a trade ledger transcription—tolls, weights, tariffs—from two hundred years past. On its face, boring beyond bearing. But Ferro had spent years marking it up, and now he traced the marks: little crosses where a toll seemed to be charged twice, circles where a caravan's stated mileage exceeded the possible, arrows where goods appeared in a city's records without ever having entered any port or gate.

"Goods don't walk," he said. "Caravans don't gain a day of travel on open moor. Twice-toll means two jurisdictions, and you can't tax what doesn't exist. Someone was trading *through* something. Regularly. For generations. Look at the pattern."

The marks clustered. Not scattered—clustered, densest at the oldest quarter of the free cities, thinning outward like ripples from a dropped stone.

"Now the songs." He nodded toward Wren. "Nan Hollis has been singing to you. The counting song, the one about the miller's nine daughters?"

Wren blinked. "How did you—"

"She sang it in a tavern on Salt Row twenty years ago, thinking no one listened. Everyone listens to me; I look harmless and I tip well." He pulled a third sheet, covered edge to edge in musical notation interleaved with syllables transcribed phonetically. "I've collected eleven versions of Alderman songs from refugees' grandchildren, wet-nurses, sailors' shanties, things people sing without knowing what they sing. And here's the thing about songs: they survive better than books. You can burn a library. You can't burn a lullaby—it lives in mouths, and mouths make more mouths. So the exiles hid things in songs. Distances. Directions. Names of places. Listen—"

He hummed the counting song's fourth verse, then tapped the notation. "The melody drops here, always drops, in every version, even the ones sung by people who've never met. A melodic drop at the same point isn't accident, it's structure. And when you map the drop-points against the toll anomalies—" his finger moved between sheets, "—they align. The songs are bearings. Someone turned a whole country into music so it couldn't be confiscated."

Silence in the room. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor.

"The songs are maps," Wren whispered. Her voice had changed—something underneath it, a tremor. "Nan said—I thought she meant it the ordinary way, that old songs hold memories—"

"Nan Hollis knows more than she lets on, or less than she thinks, or both," Ferro said gently. "But yes. And there's one more layer. The marginalia." He took down a folio bound in grey leather, unremarkable, deliberately so, and opened it to pages of copied scraps: fragments of letters, ledger notes, inscriptions, all in the same unfamiliar alphabet he'd first seen thirty years ago. Beside each, his transliterations, guesses, cross-references.

"I can't read Alderman. Nobody living can, supposedly." He looked at Wren. "Except apparently a child from a village of speakers that soldiers burned, which tells me the usurper's war has been going longer and more thoroughly than anyone in the free cities admits. I have thirty years of fragments and no key." His voice roughened despite himself. "I have a country drawn in pieces—a coastline inferred from tariff disputes, roads recovered from arithmetic errors, cities named in half a chorus—and I have never once been able to read its name written plainly. Do you understand what I'm asking?"

Wren stepped forward to the table. She looked at the alphabet for a long moment, lips moving, and then she read aloud.

"*Here the road goes down and does not come up again.*"

Her voice in the quiet room sounded like water finding an old channel.

Ferro sat down heavily on his stool. Thirty years. Thirty years of fragments, and the first plain sentence of Aldermere he had ever heard read aloud was about a road going down.

"Where," he managed. "In the fragments—which fragment, where was it found—"

Wren pointed. "On the stone. Under the words about the toll. It says the road goes down beneath the founders' stones, and—" her brow furrowed, reading slower now, "—and it says *the door keeps the ninth hour*. That part's strange. Doors don't keep hours."

"No," Ferro breathed. "They don't." He was already pulling the great chart of the old quarter back to the center of the table, his hands shaking slightly, thirty years of discipline failing him all at once. "The founders' stones. The oldest quarter. When the free cities were founded, they say the first stones were laid on bedrock—but the bedrock under the old quarter has never been surveyed because the founding guilds forbade it, and I always assumed the ban was about property rights, because everything is about property rights, but what if—"

He spread the toll-anomaly overlay across the city chart, and the cluster of impossible crossings sat directly over the founders' quarter like a dark star.

"What if it was about what's *under* the property."

Dominic rose slowly from her stool. She had gone pale in the lamplight. "The door," she said quietly. "You're telling me the Door of Aldermere isn't across the sea somewhere. It's not in ruins in some lost valley. It's been under our feet the entire time. Under the oldest quarter of the free cities."

"Beneath the founders' stones," Ferro confirmed. "Where the first stones were laid, someone laid a threshold first. The free cities weren't built next to Aldermere's door. They were built *on top of it*—a lid, or a lock, or maybe just neighbors who moved in above the cellar and agreed never to speak of the cellar." He laughed, short and unsteady. "Thirty years I thought I was reconstructing a country. I was mapping my own basement."

Sable, silent until now in her corner, spoke. "Then the usurper's armies aren't marching on the door. They're already standing on it."

The words settled over the room like cold air through a cracked window.

"And the child," Sable went on softly, watching Wren, "has been reading us our own foundations."

* * *

Later—much later, after Wren had read him six more fragments and he had filled twelve pages of fresh notes with a hand that would not quite steady—they stood alone by the window, Dominic and Ferro, looking out at nothing through paint-cracked glass.

"You could have shown me years ago," she said.

"To what end? A clerk with a buried gift and a mapmaker with a banned obsession? We'd have gotten ourselves killed separately and uselessly." He rubbed his eyes. "No. It needed a speaker. It needed—" he glanced back at the table, where Wren was carefully turning the pages of his grey folio with reverent fingers, Sable hovering protectively near, "—it needed her. And it needed you willing to burn something for her. I heard what you did at the guild, Dominic. Burning your own rank to keep one child. That's the kind of act countries get founded on. Or ended."

"Ferro."

"Hm?"

"The fragment she read. *The door keeps the ninth hour.* What does that mean?"

He was quiet a moment. "In the oldest toll records, the twice-charged tolls—the crossings—nearly all bear a date notation. Not a calendar day. An hour. The ninth hour, evening. Whatever the door is, whatever state it's in, it seems to keep time like a tide." He met her eyes. "And Dominic—if the usurper's linguists are translating the same fragments I am, and they have better archives and worse intentions—then they know about the ninth hour too. We're not racing to find the door anymore."

Downstairs, faintly, a bell began to ring. Not the harbor bell. The old quarter's watch bell, ragged and urgent, the sound it made when something was happening below the streets.

"We're racing to reach it first."

* * *

By the time they reached the street, the bells had multiplied—every quarter answering every quarter, a ragged peal rolling across the rooftops—and people were pouring from their doors with lamps, asking each other questions no one could answer. Ferro locked his rooms behind him with hands that finally, blessedly, had stopped shaking. Obsession, he thought, following Dominic and Wren down toward the old quarter through the gathering crowd, was a lonely country, and he had held citizenship there for thirty years.

Tonight, for the first time, it had borders that opened. Tonight it was hope, and hope walked fast, and the crowd carried them all downhill together toward the founders' stones—where, far below the lamplight and the shouting and the ringing bells, something old was keeping its ninth hour, and waiting to hear whether the voices that came for it came gently or with iron.
