## 10. The Usurper's Edict

The proclamation went up in Ashfall Square before dawn, and by the time Sergeant Ilva Kess reached the square with her squad, the crowd had already gathered to be afraid.

She knew how to read a crowd. Fifteen years of soldiering taught her that fear had a texture — it moved through a market like weather, low and damp, and people bent with it without quite knowing they were bending. This was different. This was fear with a nail in it. The free cities' crowds did not bend; they braced.

The edict itself was pasted to the customs house in three languages and one enormous sheet of vellum, the usurper's black boar stamped at the bottom in wax thick enough to survive a fire. Ilva read it twice while her squad pretended not to.

*By decree of His Radiant Majesty Aldous, First of That Name, Sovereign of the Reunified Marches:* the free cities of the coast were declared to be in violation of imperial harmony. They harbored *linguists*. They sheltered *the last tongue* — that diseased remnant of the erased state of Aldermere, whose very syllables carried sedition, plague, and the memory of a war the empire had generously won. All persons speaking, teaching, or concealing said tongue were subject to arrest. All persons aware of such persons and failing to report them would be treated as speakers themselves. House-to-house searches would commence in three days, conducted by the Fourth Vanguard under—

Her own name was there. *Sergeant Ilva Kess, to command search operations in the Saltrow and Founders' quarters.*

Someone in the crowd had already thrown mud at the lower corner of the page. It had dried to a brown crust over the word *harmony*, which struck Ilva as the most honest piece of political commentary she'd seen all winter.

"Reading it again, Sarge?" said Dren, the youngest of her squad, breath steaming. "Thought you'd have it memorized by the second go."

"I'm checking for typos."

"Find any?"

"The whole thing's a typo." She turned away from the wall. "Fall in."

They fell in. Twelve soldiers, eight of them conscripts like herself, four of them regulars transferred from the interior who laughed too easily at jokes about burning. Ilva marched them back along the harbor road and felt the edict on her back like a hand between her shoulder blades.

She had come to the free cities the way most conscripts came: because the alternative was worse, and because the war had been officially over for so long that soldiering had softened into policing, and policing into paperwork. For six years she had checked cargo manifests and broken up knife fights and been paid, poorly but reliably, to stand in places where standing was required. She had told herself it was an honest trade. Standing in places where standing is required. There were worse creeds.

The edict had just made the creed complicated.

* * *

The briefing came that afternoon in the customs house's map room, delivered by Captain Voss of the interior regulars — a man built like a well-fed falcon, all hooked nose and hooded patience, who wore his warrant officer's chain the way other men wore jewelry.

"Three days," Voss said, tapping the map of the Saltrow quarter. "Street by street, door by door. The quarter has eleven hundred registered households. You'll have forty men and the city watch's cooperation, which will be grudging, so plan accordingly."

"And we're looking for what, exactly?" Ilva asked. "Speakers? The quarter's full of immigrants. Half of them mumble prayers in tongues their grandmothers taught them. We going to arrest every old woman at a funeral?"

Voss smiled without warmth. "You're looking for fluency, Sergeant. Not mumbling. Fluency." He slid a paper across the table. On it was a single line of script, angular and strange, each letter built like a fence against something. "This is Alderman. His Majesty's philologists believe fewer than two hundred living persons can write it. Fewer can speak it. Your men have copies. If anyone in the Saltrow recognizes this script — hesitates over it, flinches from it, tries to hide correspondence written in it — you bring them to me."

Ilva looked down at the letters. Something in her chest tightened, though she couldn't have said why. The script looked old the way driftwood looks old — worn smooth by long travel.

"And if they can't read it at all?"

"Then they're citizens and we thank them for their time." Voss gathered his papers. "But understand this, Kess. Intelligence out of the capital says a speaker crossed into the cities last month. A child, possibly traveling alone, possibly harbored. The warrants name 'linguists and their harborers,' but between us—" he lowered his voice, and the room seemed to lean in, "—the priority target is the girl. Everything else is noise. The old speakers are dying out anyway. The child is the future, and His Majesty takes a keen interest in futures."

"A child," Ilva repeated.

"Nine or ten years old. Dark-haired. May be using a false name." Voss straightened his chain. "Dead or alive is not the phrasing I want used in reports, Sergeant. Alive. The philologists are very insistent. Apparently the language dies with its last fluent throat, and His Majesty wants to be holding that throat when it happens."

He left. Ilva stood over the map of the Saltrow, eleven hundred households drawn in tidy ink, and thought: *a child.* Nine or ten. Somewhere behind those tidy lines was a little girl who knew a forbidden way of saying *water* and *mother* and *please*, and forty men with warrants were going to walk door to door until they found her, and Ilva Kess was going to lead them there.

Dren appeared at her elbow, squinting at the sample script. "What happens to the ones we find? The speakers?"

"You've seen the interrogations, Dren."

"Yeah, but that's adults." He said it lightly, the way young men say things they haven't finished thinking. "Nobody's going to hang a kid, right? Not even the interior lot."

Ilva looked at him. Nineteen, farm-born, still wrote letters home. She remembered being nineteen. She remembered believing that institutions contained some floor beneath which behavior could not sink, and she remembered, later, learning that floors are just where the digging starts.

"Get your kit ready," she said. "Searches start tomorrow."

* * *

They started in Saltrow because Saltrow was poor and poor quarters resisted less, or resisted in ways that didn't reach the magistrate's desk. Ilva organized it with military precision and civilian mercy: knock at reasonable hours, two men visible at the door and the rest back off the step, no boots inside unless invited, no roughing unless roughed first. Her squad called it *Kess's courtesy*. The interior regulars embedded among them called it weakness, quietly, in the way of men who hadn't yet been ordered to test it.

The first day was almost boring, and the boredom frightened her more than violence would have.

Doors opened. Families stood in their doorways in the gray morning light, holding the printed notice with the strange angular letters, most of them genuinely baffled. An oyster-woman squinted at the script and said it looked like chicken tracks. A rope-maker's son tried to sound out the sample aloud and produced nonsense that made his mother cuff him. Eleven hundred households, and by midday Ilva had learned to read the micro-expressions: the honest blankness of people seeing Alderman script for the first time, versus —

versus the flicker.

She saw the flicker at the house of a widow named Maren Ostrow, salt-curer, sixty-odd, hands like knotted nets. Maren took the notice, looked at it for exactly half a heartbeat too long, and then arranged her face into bafflement so smooth it could only be rehearsed.

"This?" Maren said. "No, sergeant. Never seen it."

It was perfect. It was flawless. It was also, Ilva realized with a lurch, the performance of a woman who had spent decades practicing bafflement, who had buried a whole country inside herself and kept the grave tidy. And standing just behind Maren's skirts, half-hidden, a granddaughter of maybe seven watched Ilva with wide, unblinking eyes.

"Your granddaughter," Ilva said. "Pretty child. What's her name?"

"Sela," said Maren Ostrow, and Ilva heard the pause before the name, the tiny hitch of a lie chosen rather than spoken. Real names come fast. False names come after a silence you could sail a barge through.

Ilva looked at the child a moment longer. The child looked back. Then Ilva nodded, marked the household *clear* in her ledger, and walked down the steps into the street, where the cold wind off the harbor smelled of fish and tar and nothing at all.

That night she lay awake doing arithmetic.

*A child, possibly traveling alone, possibly harbored.* Voss had said it like a clerical detail. But Ilva had spent six years watching the free cities absorb refugees the way a beach absorbs tide, and she knew how the hunted traveled. A nine-year-old speaker didn't cross three hundred miles of occupied territory alone. Someone had carried her. Someone was feeding her. Which meant somewhere in these streets — Saltrow, Founders', the fishmarket warrens — there wasn't one fugitive. There was a web. Grandmothers like Maren Ostrow, hiding fluency behind chicken-track faces, ready to pass a stranger's child from stairwell to stairwell the way smugglers once passed brandy.

And the empire meant to pull the web apart thread by thread, in front of everyone, slowly, publicly, until the whole city learned what happened to people who kept other people's secrets.

*Harborers,* the warrant said. Ilva turned the word over. To harbor. To give shelter to. She thought about the word too long and found she couldn't make it sound like a crime.

* * *

The second day brought the interrogation.

She wasn't supposed to attend — her role was search command, not processing — but Voss sent for her specifically, and specific summonses from Voss were orders wearing polite clothing. So she stood at the back of the customs house cellar while two philologists in grey put an oyster-dredger named Piet Halloran in a chair under a good lamp and asked him, with terrible gentleness, to pronounce a single word.

The word was written in the angular script on a card. The philologists pronounced it first, carefully, their accents mangled but recognizable as attempts. *Aldheris.* Then they slid the card across the table.

"We believe you know this word, Master Halloran. Your late wife's family came from the northern coast. Trade records show Alderman surnames three generations back. Now — it's only a word. A place-name, actually. Say it for us, and you can go home to your supper."

Piet Halloran was fifty, broad as a door, with forearms that could wring a mooring line. Ilva watched his hands. They were shaking.

"It's just sounds to me," he said. "Sounds."

"It's a place-name," the older philologist said softly. "The name of a place that doesn't exist anymore. Saying it harms no one. Refusing to say it, on the other hand — refusal is a kind of speech too, Master Halloran. It tells us things."

They worked on him for two hours. Ilva stood at the back and counted the cracks in the ceiling stones because looking at the chair was unbearable and leaving was impossible. They never raised a hand. That was the horror of it — the new methods didn't need hands. They had time, and lamplight, and the patient certainty of men who knew the empire always, eventually, got what it catalogued. Toward the end Halloran was weeping, great helpless sobs, saying over and over that he didn't remember, he was never taught, his mother only sang sometimes, songs, just songs —

"Sing one," said the younger philologist, leaning forward, pen poised. "Just one song. For the record."

And Piet Halloran — big, broken, terrified Piet Halloran — shut his mouth with a snap, and looked up with red-rimmed eyes, and said in a voice like gravel: "My mother's songs aren't yours."

They took him to a cell anyway. *Pending further inquiry.* Ilva signed the transfer docket because rank required her signature, and her signature on that docket felt like mud drying on her own skin. *Kess, Sgt. I., witnessed.* Two words in her own handwriting, filed in triplicate, evidence for a tribunal that hadn't been convened yet — against whom, she wondered, and when.

She walked home through the night streets and stopped on the bridge over the canal, and let herself think the thought she'd been outrunning since the square.

The hunt was for a child. A child who spoke, and grandmothers who remembered, and dredgers who wouldn't sing, and a whole scattered country of people whose crime was that they existed in a grammar the empire had outlawed. She had sworn — when? Where? Conscripts swore everything, oaths like rain — to uphold imperial harmony. Harmony. She said the word to herself on the bridge and it sounded like a cell door.

Her grandfather, dead thirty years now, had come from somewhere north that no one ever named precisely. He used to hum, working his nets. When she was small she'd asked him what the tune was called and he'd laughed and said, *just a song, girl, just a song* — and she had heard, all these years later with terrible clarity, the exact shape of a man declining to hand his mother's voice to someone who might write it down.

Maybe it wasn't Alderman. Probably it wasn't. But the not-knowing was its own verdict: the empire had made it so that no one could be sure of their own grandmother's humming, and so everyone was guilty, and so everyone waited for the knock.

*House-to-house searches,* she had commanded. Day three began tomorrow. Founders' quarter. The oldest streets, the deepest cellars, the best hiding places in the city.

Dren found her on the bridge — he had a knack for finding her when her thoughts got heavy, which she suspected meant she wore them on her face. He leaned on the rail beside her and watched the black water.

"They'll find her eventually," he said. "The girl. Won't they? If she's real."

"Probably."

"What happens to her? Honestly. Not the report version."

Ilva was quiet a long moment. Below them the canal carried a lantern's reflection apart and let it reassemble downstream, none the worse.

"Honestly? If the philologists get her, she becomes an instrument. They'll keep her alive and lonely and teach her that her own mouth belongs to the crown. And when they've wrung the language dry, they'll dispose of what's left, because a speaker who's outlived her usefulness knows too much." She said it flatly, factually, the way she'd once read cargo manifests. "That's the report version too, Dren. That's what it says between the lines."

Dren stared at the water. "I joined up because the recruiter said we'd be keeping the peace."

"So did I."

"Is this peace?"

Ilva looked east, toward the dark line of the sea, toward the drowned horizon where a country used to be. Somewhere behind her, in the sleeping city, a grandmother was lying awake rehearsing bafflement, and a child who might be nine or ten was breathing somewhere in the dark, unaware that forty men with a list were coming to the oldest quarter at first light.

She had led searches for two days. Tomorrow she would lead another. Unless.

Unless a sergeant's ledger developed a smudge. Unless a household marked *clear* stayed clear. Unless the woman paid to find fugitives became, quietly, in small illegible ways, the sort of person the warrants were also written for.

*Harborers.* She tried the word on herself, the way you try on a coat that fits better than the uniform over it.

"Dren," she said. "Tomorrow, when we work Founders' quarter, you're with me at every door. Whatever I mark, you witness. Understood?"

"Yes, Sarge." A beat. "Sarge — why me?"

Because you still ask questions like that, she thought. Because you haven't learned to stop hearing yourself sign things.

"Because somebody should see clearly what we're doing," she said, "and I don't trust my own eyes anymore."

He frowned, not understanding, and she didn't expect him to yet. Understanding would come the way conscience always came to soldiers: late, all at once, and expensive.

She turned up her collar against the harbor wind and started for barracks. Behind her the city slept on top of its secrets — and somewhere in its oldest quarter, beneath the founders' stones, Ilva would have sworn she could feel it waiting: a door, a child, a tongue the empire couldn't kill.

At first light, she decided, she would begin keeping two ledgers.

One for the crown.

One for the truth.
