## 11. First Word, First Blade

The fish market smelled of brine and betrayal.

Dominic had chosen it for exactly that reason. No one watched a woman buying mackerel. No one remembered her. She moved through the stalls with her hood up and her ledger-face on—that flat, unremarkable expression she had spent fifteen years perfecting—and she let the noise of the crowd wash over her like gray water.

Wren walked beside her, one hand hooked in Dominic's belt loop, small fingers gripping through the wool. They had agreed on the rules that morning in Nan Hollis's back room: no talking in Alderman, not even whispers, not even in their sleep. Wren was to be a mute child from the eastern hills if anyone asked, frightened into silence by soldiers. It wasn't even a lie worth telling. The girl simply had to be nothing, the way Dominic had been nothing for half her life.

But Wren kept humming.

It was barely audible—a thread of melody under her breath as they passed the ice carts, the tune rising and falling with a shape that made the hair on Dominic's arms lift. She knew the song. She didn't know how she knew it, but she knew it the way you know your own heartbeat: *the thorn sleeps under the snow, the thorn sleeps, the thorn waits to turn.* An Alderman counting song. Her mother had sung it while darning socks.

"Wren." She crouched at a stall, pretending to inspect a tray of silver herring, and put her mouth close to the girl's ear. "No songs."

"I'm sorry." Wren's voice was very small. "It helps me not be scared. If I sing inside my head it gets out a little, like steam."

Dominic's chest did something painful. She bought the herring she didn't want, because the fishwife was watching, and because buying things was what invisible women did.

They were halfway down the row of stalls, between a man selling eels from a barrel and a woman arranging oysters in ranks like little armored mouths, when Dominic felt it.

Not heard—*felt*. A pressure behind her sternum, cold and bright, like a coin left out all night in frost. Fifteen years ago she would have named it instantly: someone near was speaking the tongue, or something near was *made* of it. Now the sensation arrived strange and foreign, a letter in an old friend's handwriting.

She turned.

Three men stood at the mouth of the lane behind them. Not soldiers—their coats were plain, their boots were wrong for marching—but they held themselves the way soldiers hold themselves when they've been told to look like civilians and haven't quite managed it. One of them carried a long iron rod banded with rings, and the rings were etched with characters Dominic recognized with a lurch: Alderman script, stolen and nailed into a tool, the way you'd nail a hawk's feathers to a snare.

A wordcatcher's rod. She'd seen one only once before, as a girl, in her mother's hands.

"Down," she said quietly, and set the wrapped fish on the oyster-seller's table as though she'd merely stopped to rest. "Wren. Behind the barrels. Walk, don't run."

The girl obeyed, which was worse than screaming. Children who'd been hunted before knew how to walk instead of run, and every step of Wren's obedience told Dominic more about those burned villages than any confession could have.

The three men split without hurry. That was the professional part, and it frightened her most. Two drifted along the outer edge of the market; the third came straight down the aisle toward her, unhurried, browsing. He stopped at the eel barrel two paces away and looked at her with polite interest.

"Madam." His voice was mild. "Have you seen a child? Small, dark-haired. Speaks oddly."

"Half this city speaks oddly," Dominic said. Ledger-voice. Flat as slate.

"True enough." He smiled. He reached into his coat and drew out a slip of paper—not a warrant, something smaller, a printed bill such as fishmongers tacked to posts—and smoothed it on the eel barrel's rim. A crude woodcut of a child's face stared up from it, the features generic enough to be anyone. Beneath, in three languages, one of which made the frost-coin behind Dominic's sternum flare white-hot:

**SPEAKERS AND THEIR HARBORERS. THE USURPER PAYS IN SILVER AND SILENCE.**

"We're told," the man said conversationally, "that speakers feel each other. Like toothache in a shared jaw. Is that true, madam? Have you ever felt anything... odd, this morning?"

Every instinct Dominic owned said *lie, deflect, become furniture.* Fifteen years of instincts. She opened her mouth to do it—

—and Wren sneezed.

It was a tiny sound, a child's sound, lost in the market din. But the man's head turned toward the barrel stack with the smooth snap of a weathervane finding wind, and the iron rod in his companion's hands across the lane began, faintly, to sing—a thin harmonic whine, like a wet finger on glass.

The rings lit up one by one.

Dominic moved without deciding to move. Three steps, shoulder low, and she drove the eel-seller's heavy brass ladle—it had been sitting on the barrel's rim, greasy and solid and real—into the first man's throat. He folded with a strangled croak. The second was already running, shouting something about *"here, HERE"* in a voice pitched to carry over the whole market, and the third swung the singing rod at her head.

She caught it. Bare hands. And the moment her skin touched the etched iron, the world went silent and enormous.

* * *

Later, she would try to describe it to Ferro and fail, because there was no describing the moment the dam broke.

Fifteen years she had kept the words down. She had swallowed them daily, hourly, like stones, until her throat was a well full of drowned bells. She had built a life above the waterline—ledgers, appraisals, quiet rooms, careful silence—and she had believed, truly believed, that the gift below was dead or sleeping or at least loyal to its chains.

Her hands closed around the wordcatcher's rod, and every bell in the well rang at once.

The rod was a cage for language. She could feel it—feel the stolen script in its rings writhing against her palms like netted fish, feel the intent coiled in its making, the whole ugly cleverness of a tool built to *hear* a tongue so it could be killed. And underneath her terror, underneath the roar of the crowd and the man's shout and Wren's small gasp behind the barrels, something in Dominic rose up with fifteen years of buried pressure behind it and *answered.*

She spoke one word.

She did not choose it. That was the thing she could never explain afterward, the thing that kept her awake for nights after: the word chose itself, the way a river chooses the sea. It came up through her chest, through her teeth, out into the salt air of the fish market, and it was old and round and hard, a single syllable in the dead tongue of a country that no longer existed—

*"BRÆKAN."*

Break.

The sound did not travel the way sounds travel. It arrived everywhere at once. Fishwives clutched their ears. Gulls fell off the rigging of the harbor cranes as though struck. And every blade of iron within thirty feet—every knife, every hook, every cleaver, the wordcatcher's rod in her own hands, the hidden sword beneath the second hunter's coat as he came around the ice cart—*shattered.*

Not bent. Shattered. Steel burst into gravel with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and shards rained across the oysters and the herring and the packed mud of the lane, glittering, ringing on stone, settling into sudden profound stillness.

For one heartbeat the entire market hung suspended. A hundred faces turned toward her. The second hunter stood holding a hilt with nothing attached to it, staring at the falling dust of his own weapon.

Then the pain hit her.

It came up from the base of her spine like a spear of white fire, following the same road the word had taken, but backward, and it took her legs out from under her. She went down onto her knees in fish guts and broken steel, and the world went gray at the edges, and somewhere very far away Wren was screaming her name—"Dom! DOM!"—and the taste of copper flooded her mouth so thickly she thought her lungs might be bleeding.

*That,* said some distant, lucid corner of her mind, *is what fifteen years of interest looks like.*

The gift hadn't died in its chains. It had grown. And it had eaten.

Through the gray she saw the second hunter drop the ruined hilt and flee, and she understood, dimly, that this was the worst possible outcome. A dead witness tells no tales. A fleeing one runs straight to every garrison, every barracks, every bounty office in the free cities, and tells them a woman in a gray hood unmade steel with her voice in the fish market by the harbor cranes.

By nightfall, every hunter in the city would know.

*Get up.* She pressed her palms into the mud. *Get up, get Wren, get gone.* But her arms shook like a drunkard's, and blood ran warm from her nose over her lips, and the crowd—God, the crowd—was beginning to make sounds. Not panic yet. Worse. *Wonder.* Somewhere a fishwife whispered, "Did you see—did you see her *eyes*—" and somewhere else a child laughed with pure delight at the shining wreckage, and both sounds were knives.

Small hands gripped her shoulders. Wren's face swam into view, pale, tear-streaked, furious with fear.

"You said no songs," the girl whispered, "and then YOU—"

"I know." Blood on her teeth. She got one foot under herself. "I know. Help me stand."

Wren hauled at her with the desperate strength of the small, and Dominic rose by degrees, the market spinning slowly around her. The first hunter lay groaning in the eels, alive, clutching his bruised throat. The third was scrambling backward on all fours, eyes fixed on her with the expression of a man who has seen the sea stand up and speak.

She should say something. She should threaten them, or lie, or—

What came out instead, hoarse and cracked and entirely involuntary, was: "Forget."

Not the deep word. Just a push, a feather-touch of the gift, aimed at two terrified men—and even that feather cost her. Fresh agony lanced behind her eyes; her vision whited at the rim; she tasted blood again. But the hunters' faces went slack and confused, the way men's faces go when they can't remember why they stood up. The one in the eels sat down heavily among his catch and began, absently, to sort fish.

It wouldn't last. She could feel the edges of it fraying already, feel how shallow the working was, a cup of water thrown on a house fire. Minutes, perhaps. Then they would remember everything, and the hunt would begin in earnest.

"Wren." She gripped the girl's hand. The world tilted and righted. "We're leaving. Don't look at anyone. If I fall down, you keep walking east along the harbor until you see the blue door with the anchor carved on it, and you tell Ferro I said—"

"You're not going to fall down."

"I might."

"You're not *allowed* to." Wren's chin was trembling, but her voice had iron in it, and Dominic thought, absurdly, through the blood and the roaring in her skull: *where did you get that, little one? Who taught you to be brave in a burned village?*

They walked. Out of the market, past the ice carts, past the gulls regaining their perches and screaming their complaints, past the fishwife who crossed herself with one wet hand and the child who waved. Dominic kept her spine straight through sheer hatred of collapsing in front of strangers, and each step was a coin of pain paid out of a purse that was nearly empty.

At the mouth of the lane, where the shadow of the harbor crane cut the street like a sundial, she stopped and made herself look back.

The market was already recovering—stalls reopening, voices resuming, the great indifferent machinery of commerce grinding on over the gravel of shattered steel. But at the edge of it, three figures in plain coats were converging on the eel barrel, moving fast, and one of them carried another wordcatcher's rod, and even at this distance Dominic could feel it humming, hungry, listening for echoes.

They would trace the word. She knew it the way she'd known the pressure behind her sternum: *brækan* had been spoken aloud, raw, unmasked, in the open air, and every instrument built to hear that tongue had just been pointed at this district like compass needles finding north.

"Ferro will need to move us tonight," she said. Her voice sounded like gravel dragged over gravel. "All of us. Tonight, before—"

"Dominic." Wren tugged her sleeve. Her face was turned upward, toward the crane, toward the rooftops beyond it, and her eyes were wide—not with fear now, but with something older and stranger. "Listen."

Dominic listened. Above the harbor noise, above the gulls and the auction bells, she heard it: thin, scattered, rising from doorways and alleys and upper windows across the waterfront quarter.

Whistling. Ordinary dockhands' whistling, ordinary street tunes—except that here and there, woven into them, were fragments. Four notes of a counting song. A phrase of melody shaped like *the thorn sleeps under the snow.* Snatches that no one in this city had any business knowing, hummed by people who had hidden them for years the way she had hidden her voice.

They had felt it. All the hidden ones, the broken remnants, the half-speakers and their children—they had felt *brækan* roll across the city like weather, and they were answering the only way the hidden dare to answer.

Or—her stomach turned cold—someone was whistling bait into every alley, drawing the frightened out to see.

Either way, the sound would carry. Either way, the hunters with their singing rods were already climbing the rooftops to find its source.

"East," Dominic said, tightening her grip on Wren's small, steady hand. "Now. Before they learn the song is a map."

Behind them, faint and getting fainter, the wordcatcher's rod began to sing.
