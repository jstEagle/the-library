## 12. Nan Hollis Sings

The kettle had a crack in it shaped like a river delta, and Nan Hollis had long ago decided this was fitting, because everything she loved best in the world was cracked somewhere and still held water.

She sat in the kitchen of Ferro's safehouse—the mapmaker's house, all of it smelling of ink and old paper and, now, of the fish stew Dominic had left simmering before she went out to walk the rooftops with Ilva's warning folded into her sleeve—and she watched the child across the table try not to stare at her hands.

"You can look," Nan said. "They're only hands."

Wren looked. They were old hands, spotted like thrush eggs, knuckles swollen with sixty years of kneading dough and hauling nets and, once, for one terrible summer, burying what the soldiers left. The right hand was missing half its little finger. A cleaver, when she was nineteen. She'd told a hundred stories about that finger and none of them were true.

"My gran had hands like yours," Wren said at last, in Alderman.

Nan's chest did the thing it always did now, the tight sweet ache, hearing the tongue come out of a child's mouth whole and unbroken, the way water comes out of a spring, without the silt of decades that the adults carried. "Did she," Nan said, also in Alderman. It came out rusty but true. "And what did your gran do with them?"

"Bread. And she'd—" Wren stopped, and her face did something complicated, grief arriving and being set aside like a parcel too heavy to carry through a crowd. She was getting good at setting it aside. Nan hated that she was getting good at it. "She'd fold them together when she sang. Like this." The child laced her fingers, turned her joined hands over, thumbs up—a shape like a small roofed gate.

The kettle began to sing.

Nan got up because her knees made her, poured two cups of willowbark and honey, and did not let her own face do anything complicated at all. Because that hand-shape, the little roofed gate—that was the fourth figure of the *Lullaby of the Nine Stones*, the one every Alderman mother sang over cradles from the salt coast to the high moors, and here was a child doing it unprompted, the way you breathe, the way you flinch from heat.

*Oh, you clever dead country,* Nan thought, stirring honey into nothing. *You hid yourself in us so deep we didn't even know we were maps.*

"Drink," she said, in Common, because some habits were load-bearing. Then, softer, in the other tongue: "Will you sing for me, little wren? Anything your gran gave you. I want to hear what's in it."

* * *

What was in it was a road.

Nan had known the lullabies were more than lullabies—had suspected it since she was young herself, since her mother had sung *Nine Stones* with a strange gravity on certain nights, touching the bedframe at each verse as though counting rafters. But suspicion is a fog, and it took Wren's voice to burn it off. The child sang the first verse plain, the way children sing, no ornament, and then at the second verse her voice bent naturally around a note that no adult speaker could hold anymore—not because their throats had forgotten, but because the note required a word that had been split, in the old tongue, into a rising and falling pair, and every surviving adult rendered it as one sound or the other. Never both. The language had broken along its fault lines when the country broke, and the adults had healed crooked.

Wren hit both halves of the note. The room changed.

It was not dramatic. That was the uncanny part. No light blazed, no wind moved. But the shadows in the corners leaned a fraction toward the girl, like grass bending under a weight of birds, and Nan felt—in her teeth, in the scar tissue of the finger she'd lost—something turn over, far away and far below, like a great animal shifting in its sleep.

"Stop," Nan said, and Wren stopped, mid-breath, obedient and frightened.

"What happened?"

"Nothing." Nan sat down slowly. Her tea trembled against the saucer. "Nothing yet. Sing it again—but slower. And watch my hands. I'll show you the figures."

Because there were figures, of course there were figures; the mothers' hands had never been idle in song, and Nan had spent forty years thinking it was just something hands did. Now she watched Wren's small fingers move through the shapes—gate, bridge, plough-furrow, door—and heard, laid over the melody like thread over thread, the words that went with them, and understood that the child was singing directions.

Verse one: the harbor stones. Verse two: the salt road east, and where it forked. Verse three: the count of steps down beneath the oldest quarter—nine verses, nine descents, or nine turns, or nine names, the song refusing to say plainly which, because songs that say things plainly get their singers burned. Verse four, the little gate made of two hands. Verse five—

"I don't know verse five," Wren said, faltering. "Gran said she only had the first four from her mother, and her mother had six, and between them they were missing pieces, because—" the child's jaw set— "because the soldiers took grandmothers too."

Nan reached across the table and closed her old hands over the child's small ones.

"And that," she said, "is why they could never kill it, do you see? They burned the books and the speakers both, and the song survived anyway, because they couldn't burn it out of everyone, and because no one person ever had it whole. It's been living scattered in us. In bits. In grandmothers." Her voice caught and she let it, this once. "In me."

"You know the middle verses?" Wren leaned forward, hunger in her face, and Nan recognized the hunger because she'd worn it herself for forty years, the hunger of someone holding a fragment of home and knowing it was a fragment.

"I know the ones my mother sang. Six through eight." Nan began, low, and Wren's eyes went wide and wet, and the two of them sat in a kitchen smelling of fish stew and ink, an old woman and a child stitching a country back together out of lullabies, one torn seam at a time.

* * *

By the third hour Ferro had drifted in and was pretending to inventory his ink pots while writing down everything on the backs of failed maps, and Sable had appeared on the windowsill the way she appeared everywhere, cat-silent, chin on her knees, listening with the particular intensity of a woman who stole memories for a living and knew exactly which ones were worth taking.

"It's a survey," Ferro breathed, when Nan and Wren had run the assembled song through twice. He spread a ruined chart of the free cities' oldest quarter—his own work, twenty years old, annotated past legibility—and began marking. "Here—the harbor stones, that's the Founders' Ring, the first seawall. The fork in the salt road, that's still there, it's called Cutpurse Corner now, nobody knows why—" his pen flew— "and the count of steps, if it's steps, down beneath the old mint, that would put the end of it—" He stopped. His pen stopped. He looked up at Nan with the expression of a man who has just seen his life's madness confirmed by a nursery rhyme.

"The Founders' Stones," he whispered. "Under our feet. Under the city. It was never across any sea at all. We built on top of it."

"We built on top of a lot of things," Sable said from the windowsill. "That's what cities are." But her voice was careful, and Nan saw her looking at Wren with an expression the thief probably believed was neutral and wasn't.

Wren wasn't listening to them anymore. She was frowning at her own hands.

"Nan," she said. "There's a last verse. Gran hummed it but she never gave me the words. She said the last verse isn't for one mouth."

Nan went very still.

The fire in the grate popped. Out in the street a cart passed, wheels grinding salt-crusted cobbles, and somewhere above, faintly, the bells of the Harbor temple rang the third hour of the afternoon, and Nan Hollis sat with sixty years of a dead country in her lap and felt the last piece of the puzzle slide into view—and it was shaped like a person. Two persons.

"Not for one mouth," she repeated. Her mother had said those exact words, hadn't she? On the nights of strange gravity, when she touched the bedframe. *This verse needs two voices, little bee. One to ask and one to answer.* And Nan had thought it meant a call-and-response, the way harvest songs worked, the men's line and the women's line. But Wren's gran had said it differently, apparently. And the child was looking at her now with that terrible directness children have, the directness adults spend their whole lives learning to avoid.

"What does it mean, then?"

"It means," Nan said slowly, working it as she spoke, testing each stone before she stepped on it, "that the song isn't only a map. Maps you can read alone. This last verse—" she turned her cup, watching the dregs spin— "a lock you can't pick alone either. Two keys. Turned together. Or maybe—" and here her stomach dropped, because sixty years of living teaches you that the simplest reading is usually the trap— "maybe it means the door itself answers to a sentence, a real one, spoken by two voices at once. Not a password written down anywhere. A thing that has to be *said*, jointly, by two people who mean it."

Ferro set his pen down very carefully. "A sentence spoken by two voices," he said. "To open a door that's been shut for fifteen years."

"Longer," Nan said. "Shut longer than that. Sealed." She didn't say *by oath*. She didn't say *by whose family*. Some knowledge was not hers to spill; some belonged to a swordspeaker who was currently walking the rooftops rather than sit in a warm kitchen, and who would have to be the one to say it.

Sable unfolded herself from the sill and stood. For once she didn't joke. "Two voices," she said. "And how many whole speakers does the world have left?"

Everyone looked at Wren.

The child looked back at them, and Nan watched her understand it—not the mechanics, children grasp mechanics easily, but the weight. One fluent tongue in a broken country. Whoever stood opposite her at that door would have to carry the other half of the sentence. Would have to mean it. And there were perhaps a dozen people alive, scattered and hiding, who even had fragments enough to try.

"Then we need the others," Wren said simply. "The ones like me. Gran said there were others. She said, *find the ones who hum.*"

The kettle cracked a little further along its fault line, and did not stop holding water.

* * *

They sang until dark.

Nan taught Wren verses six through eight, and Wren taught her the corrected notes, the unbroken ones, and where the two versions disagreed the truth revealed itself like a bone under skin—Wren's way made the shadows lean; Nan's way made only music. It should have been bitter, discovering that forty years of faithful memory had been keeping a wounded version of the song, but it wasn't. It was like seeing a photograph of her mother as a girl. *So that's who you were. Before the breaking. You were whole once.*

At the eighth verse Wren's voice cracked on a word for *homecoming*—there was no clean Common equivalent; the Alderman word contained doors and thresholds and the smell of bread waiting—and she stopped, and pressed her fists against her eyes, and Nan gathered her in, chair and all, the way she had once gathered in daughters, and then granddaughters, and then, after, no one at all for a very long time.

"They're not sad songs," Wren said into Nan's shoulder. "Why do they make me cry if they're maps?"

"Because they're both, love." Nan rocked her, slow, the rhythm older than language. "The best hiding places are inside the things people can't bear to search. Nobody burns a lullaby. Nobody interrogates a grandmother's humming. They put the country in the cradle songs because the cradle songs are the last thing anyone would touch—and the first thing every child keeps." She swallowed. "Your gran kept hers. My mother kept hers. And now it's in you, whole, and the soldiers who burned your village—they lost. Do you understand me? Whatever else happens, whatever comes through that door or doesn't—they lost. The song got out."

Wren cried for a while, properly, the way children are allowed to and adults forget how, and Nan held her and looked over the small bowed head at the window, where the evening had gone the color of pewter, and where—if she let herself imagine it, and tonight she let herself—somewhere far beyond the smoke of the free cities, a grey country waited under the same pewter sky, starving, and humming.

* * *

Dominic came back after full dark, salt wind behind her and trouble in her stride, and found the kitchen transformed: charts everywhere, Ferro radiant, Sable uncharacteristically quiet, and Nan and the child sitting hip to hip at the table with a single candle between them, going over the ninth verse—or the absence of one.

"There's no ninth verse," Wren reported, with the gravity of a magistrate delivering a verdict. "The song has eight. Gran was right. The last verse isn't a verse at all."

"No," Nan agreed. "It's a sentence. Spoken by two voices, at a door, meaning what they say." She met Dominic's eyes across the candle flame, and let the silence stretch just long enough.

Dominic Thackeray—who had spent fifteen years teaching her face to be a ledger page, flat and balanced and revealing nothing—did not quite manage it. Something moved behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. But Nan Hollis had raised daughters and buried friends and sung over three generations of cradles, and she knew a person hearing a verdict about themselves when she saw one.

"Two voices," Dominic said. Neutral. Perfectly neutral. Her hand, resting on the back of a chair, had gone white at the knuckles.

"At least two," Nan said gently. "Perhaps the sentence wants more than two, once you start assembling it—half-speakers with fragments, the ones Wren says we must find. The ones who hum." She rose, joints announcing themselves, and crossed to the swordspeaker, and did the thing she had been wanting to do since the night a child scratched at the guild door: she took Dominic's scarred sword-hand in both of her soft old ones, the missing finger and all.

"When the time comes," Nan said, in Alderman, so the child wouldn't follow every word, "you'll know whether you're the second voice. You'll have known a long while, I think. Your mother's hands did the same thing mine did, in the figures—did you know that? Gate, bridge, furrow, door. Somebody sang the map over your cradle too, Domi. However hard you've tried to put the country down, it never put you down."

Dominic looked at her for a long moment. The candle guttered. Somewhere out in the besieging dark, a drum began—far off, patient, the usurper's army keeping time with the tide.

"I sang a word once," Dominic said at last, barely above the drumbeat, "that I swore never to speak again. If the sentence needs me, Nan—it may need *that* kind of word. Words with edges."

"Then we'll teach you new ones," said Nan Hollis, and squeezed the white knuckles until some of the blood came back. "Songs with maps in them. Songs your grandmother's grandmother used to open doors, not close them." She nodded at Wren, already half-asleep upright, fingers still moving faintly through the figure of the little roofed gate. "Besides. The song says the door answers two voices. It doesn't say which two. Maybe it's been waiting fifteen years not for a swordspeaker's oath at all."

"Then for what?"

Nan smiled, and it was the smile she saved for the moment the bread came out of the oven right, rare and warm and slightly astonished at itself.

"For a child," she said, "to ask it nicely."

Out in the street, the drum stopped. And in the silence that followed, from under the floor—from beneath the founders' stones, from nine verses and nine turnings down—came a sound so faint that only two people in the room heard it, and both of them had Alderman in their ears: a long, slow exhale, like a door remembering it had once been open.

Or like something on the other side of it, drawing breath to speak.
