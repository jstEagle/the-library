## 13. The Memory Thief's Bargain

Sable had stolen her first memory at nine years old, and she had been paying for it ever since.

She sat in the back room of Nan Hollis's boarding house on Candlewick Lane, turning a small tin box over in her hands the way a gambler turns a coin he already knows is cursed. Inside the box: three wax-sealed vials of grey glass, each holding a curl of something that wasn't quite smoke and wasn't quite light. Memories, decanted and stored. Her trade goods. Her savings.

Her sins.

Across the room, Ferro was arguing with Nan about whether it was safer to move Wren before or after the curfew bells. Dominic stood by the shuttered window with her arms crossed, listening the way stone listens—completely, silently, missing nothing. And in the corner, on a cot too big for her, the child slept with the iron key clutched against her chest like a talisman, her breath whistling faintly through a healing cold.

Sable had been watching that key for two days.

She knew what it was. Not because anyone had told her. Because twelve years ago, in a vault under the Guild of Impossible Things, she had sold away a memory for her freedom, and what remained of that memory—its shape, its absence, the hole where it used to live—ached whenever the key came near. Grief has an outline even when the grief itself is gone.

"You're brooding," Nan said, without turning around. Old women always knew.

"I'm pricing," Sable said.

Ferro stopped mid-argument. "Pricing what?"

"The clerk." She let the word sit there. "Marrow. The guild's second ledger-keeper. Quillon's man. They took him this morning when the raid went wrong—Ilva's runner told us before dusk. Corvane has him in the customs house on Harrow Quay."

"And?" Dominic said. Just that. One syllable, but Sable had spent fifteen years reading people for a living, and Dominic Thackeray read like large print.

"Marrow knows where the door is."

The room went still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"He carried the appraisal folios," Sable went on, "before Quillon burned the sensitive ones. He handled the survey contracts for the Founders' Quarter restoration—that's not coincidence, that's a guildmaster keeping his options open. Quillon always kept his options open. Marrow saw the surveys. He walked the tunnels with the masons. If the door is under the city, Marrow doesn't just suspect it—he can draw you a map with his eyes shut."

"Then we get the map from him," Ferro said. "Bribe him. Or—" he made a gesture that meant the uglier thing.

"Marrow would die before betraying the guild," Sable said. "That's not loyalty, that's arithmetic. Corvane will have promised him his life for the location, and Marrow believes the promise, because Marrow is the sort of man who believes promises. You can't torture truth out of a man who's decided to be brave. You'd just get lies, and we don't have time for lies."

"So what do you propose?" Dominic asked quietly. She already knew. Sable could see it in her face—the same terrible arithmetic happening behind those grey eyes.

"I go in tonight," Sable said. "Corvane keeps prisoners on the third floor of the customs house; Ilva confirmed the rotations. I take Marrow's memory of the door. Not the map—a map can be wrong, misremembered, planted. The memory itself. What he *saw*."

Nan turned fully now, drying her hands on her apron, and her face had gone very old and very kind. "Girl," she said, "you know what that costs."

"I know what it costs."

"Say it anyway. Say it so they understand what they're letting you buy."

Sable looked down at the tin box. The vials inside shifted like sleeping things.

"When I take a memory," she said, "I have to hold it. Carry it out through the wards in my own head, wrapped in my own recollection so the theft reads as remembering instead of reaching. That's the craft. You can't cut a page out of a book without putting another page in its place, or the book screams. So I put one of mine in." She tapped her temple. "One of mine goes into him. It fills the hole where his memory was, and by morning he doesn't know anything was taken. But the page I gave up—" her voice caught, only slightly, only where she allowed it to catch— "the page I give up is gone. Permanently. I don't get it back. Nobody gets it back. It lives in Marrow now, wearing his life like a borrowed coat, and someday he'll remember a birthday party that never happened to him, and think it was his."

Silence. Wren stirred on her cot but didn't wake.

"How many times have you done it?" Dominic asked.

"Does it matter?"

"Yes."

Sable smiled the smile she used at card tables. "Forty-one times. Give or take. I stopped counting when I ran out of fingers and started running out of *reasons*." She set the tin box on the table between them. "The first one was my mother's face. I was nine. The client wanted a memory of a woman named Liss, and my mother fit the description, and I didn't understand yet that the trade ran both directions. I walked home that night knowing I loved someone whose name I couldn't find. There was a shape in me shaped exactly like her, and nothing inside it." She shrugged, casual, practiced. "So. You want to know if I'll flinch? I haven't flinched since I was nine years old. Tonight I need to pick which page of myself to spend. That's all. It's bookkeeping."

"It isn't bookkeeping," Dominic said. "It's amputation."

"Everything is bookkeeping if your hands are steady enough."

* * *

She chose the memories alone, up in the attic, by candlelight, the way she chose everything alone.

This was the part no thief talked about: not the taking, but the choosing. Every person is a library, and most people never learn which volumes they actually live in until someone asks them to burn one. Sable closed her eyes and walked her own shelves.

Childhood: mostly gone already. Forty-one trades will do that. She had her ninth year intact—the year of the theft—and almost nothing before it. There was a summer she'd spent on a river barge, age eleven or twelve, learning to read water; she'd offered that one twice before and both buyers had found it too ordinary. Ordinary was worthless in the memory trade. Everyone wanted first loves and dying words.

Adulthood: richer stock, worse cost. The night she'd escaped the guild, twelve years ago, buying her freedom with the memory that now ached in its absence—she'd paid with the single worst day of her apprenticeship, and she still didn't know what that day had been. The hole had a shape. The shape was a door. Of course it was. She'd known, dimly, for years, that whatever she'd traded away had something to do with Aldermere, with doors, with the reason a girl from nowhere ends up apprenticed to a guild that traffics in impossible things. Fate had a sense of humor like a knife had a handle.

What could she afford tonight?

Not Wren. She examined that shelf and stepped back from it deliberately—the child asleep downstairs, the weight of the girl's head against her shoulder two nights ago on the stairs, the way Wren had said her name like it was worth saying. No. She would not spend that. A woman had to keep something, or the trades stopped being trades and became suicide by installments.

Not the barge summer. Not yet. That was her reserve, her last ordinary joy, and she'd learned long ago to always keep one ordinary joy in reserve.

Which left—

She laughed, alone in the attic, a short ugly sound. Which left the obvious. Which left the one memory she'd been saving without admitting she was saving it, the one she told herself she kept for sentimental reasons, the one any appraiser in the free cities would price highest:

Dominic Thackeray, four years ago, laughing.

It was nothing. It was less than nothing—it was a Tuesday. Sable had come to the guild hall to fence a stolen ring of recollections off a dead man's estate, and Dominic had been in the counting room doing figures, and Ferro had said something idiotic about a shipment of bottled thunderstorms, and Dominic—who never laughed, who everyone said couldn't laugh—had laughed. Really laughed, head tipped back, hand flat against the ledger, surprised by her own mouth. Sable had been standing in the doorway with contraband in her coat, and she had stood there far too long, memorizing it the honest way, and then later, alone, she had decanted it properly, because a thief archives everything valuable, and she had told herself the archiving meant nothing.

Four years she'd kept that vial. Never sold it. Never would have sold it. The one piece of inventory in her whole crooked life that wasn't for sale.

She held it up to the candlelight. Inside the grey glass, something glowed warm gold, the way happy memories did, and she thought: this is what love looks like in my line of work. A vial you refuse to price.

And then she thought: Marrow has a daughter. He does; she'd seen the miniature on his desk, a gap-toothed girl in a blue dress. When Corvane finishes with Marrow—if Marrow breaks, if Marrow dies—the daughter loses a father either way. But if Sable spends her one unpriceable thing tonight, maybe the father walks out of the customs house alive, maybe he makes it to a boat, maybe the gap-toothed girl grows up.

Love was a currency. Everyone pretended otherwise. Sable had simply stopped pretending earlier than most.

She sealed the vial into the transfer case, along with a lesser one—the taste of honey bread, some small sweet thing, padding for the wrapping—and blew out the candle.

* * *

Harrow Quay at midnight smelled of tar and dead fish and the particular salt-grease fear of places where men were interrogated. Sable crossed the yard as shadow-work, hood up, gait changed, the walk of a dockside drunk looking for a warm wall. The customs house guard rotated on the half-bell; Ilva's information was good, as it had been every time, which frightened Sable more than bad information would have. A defector who was right was a defector who existed. A defector who existed could be caught. And caught defectors talked, and Sable was currently standing in a yard full of the people they'd talk about.

Third floor. The window latch was rusted but the frame was older than the lock, and old frames forgive a thin blade. She poured through the gap like smoke given opinions.

They were holding Marrow in a records room, of all things—chained to a chair among shelves of confiscated manifests, a candle burning down beside him. He looked terrible. Three days of Corvane's questions, and Corvane hadn't even raised his voice; Corvane never raised his voice. Marrow's lips moved constantly, soundlessly, the muttering of a man rehearsing his refusals.

"*Don't,*" Marrow whispered when her hand touched his shoulder, and then his eyes focused and went wide. "Sable. Saints' teeth. You're—Quillon said you were dead. Quillon said—"

"Quillon says a lot of things." She crouched in front of the chair, checking the chains out of habit. "Listen fast. Corvane is going to break you. Maybe tomorrow, maybe the day after. Not because you're weak—because everyone is weak, it's just a question of how much time they're willing to spend. He's willing to spend time. So here's what happens instead: I'm going to reach into your head and lift out the memory of the door, and I'm going to leave something in its place, and tomorrow when Corvane asks, you won't know anything. Not because you're brave. Because there'll be nothing left to surrender. Do you understand?"

Marrow stared at her. Then, horribly, he began to cry—quietly, the crying of a clerk who had always suspected the ledgers would end this way. "You'll take a piece of yourself," he said. "Everyone knows what your kind pays. I won't—I can't have that on—"

"You can and you will." She softened it, barely. "Marrow. Your daughter has your chin and your stubbornness and, if Corvane gets what he wants from you, a very short future. This is the cheapest rescue you will ever be offered. Hold still."

She opened the transfer case. Two vials: the gold one, and the honey-bread one. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook.

The craft itself was simple and awful. She pressed her fingertips to Marrow's temples, breathed out the opening phrase of the thief's grammar—a dead language too, though nobody sang songs about hers—and felt the walls between minds go thin as paper. Marrow's memory rose toward her like a fish toward bait: the Founders' Quarter, the survey, descending a stair beneath the oldest stones with a lantern, masons' chalk marks, a threshold carved with a thorn-and-circle sigil, cold pouring off it like winter off a lake, and—she took it, all of it, the whole bright page—

—and slotted the gold vial into the gap.

For one suspended moment she stood inside her own best memory from the other side. She watched Dominic laugh. She felt what it had felt like to be the woman in the doorway, contraband forgotten, heart doing something unscheduled. She felt the exact temperature of loving someone quietly for four years without once saying so, because saying so would have made it a transaction, and Sable Voss had never once in her life made a gift she couldn't pretend was business.

Then it left her.

The hole opened where it had lived. She knew the trick of holes by now: they didn't hurt immediately. They hurt later, at odd hours, when her hand drifted toward a shelf that no longer held the book. Right now there was only a strange new warmth in her chest that belonged to no one—a residue of feeling without a cause, like the smell of a fire in a cold grate. She would spend years catching herself smiling at Dominic for no reason she could name. Some part of her, deep in the arithmetic, understood that too, and paid gladly.

She filled the remainder of the gap with honey bread and pushed back from Marrow's mind.

The clerk sagged in his chains, blinking, and looked at her with mild, empty courtesy. "Sorry—do I know you? Only, I've been ill. They've been questioning me, but there's nothing, I keep telling them, there's nothing I—" He frowned, groping. "There was a door. Wasn't there? Under the stones. No. No, that's not right, that's a story my mother—" He stopped, puzzled, one hand rising vaguely toward his own temple, touching a memory that had never been his: a woman laughing, a doorway, a thief who loved him enough to bleed herself dry and call it bookkeeping.

"There's nothing," Sable agreed gently. "Tell them there's nothing. They'll believe you, because it's true."

She was three streets gone, moving fast through the curfew dark, when the shaking finally started—not her hands, never her hands, but everything underneath them. She leaned in a doorway between a shuttered chandler's and a shrine to some minor sea saint, and pressed her fist against the new hole in her chest, and made herself say it aloud to the empty street, because a debt unnamed is a debt unpaid:

"Her name was Dominic Thackeray. She laughed on a Tuesday. I loved her for four years and I just spent it on a clerk with a gap-toothed daughter, and it was worth it, and I don't remember why I'm crying."

A cat regarded her from a wall with the contempt of its entire species. Somewhere across the city, bells tolled the half-hour.

* * *

They were all awake when she got back to Candlewick Lane—of course they were; nobody in that house slept anymore—and she laid the transfer case on the kitchen table and cracked the seal, and poured the memory out onto a slate Ferro had dusted with chalk-dust to receive it.

It bloomed into images above the slate the way dreams bloom: the stair, the lantern-light, the chalk marks, the carved thorn-and-circle, and beneath the sigil, in old Alderman characters that made Wren—awake now, wide-eyed, wrapped in Nan's shawl—whisper their meaning aloud without seeming to decide to:

*"Beneath the founders, beneath the flood, the door that is not a door."*

And below that, scratched into the stone by some desperate hand decades ago, coordinates. An entrance. A way down.

Ferro was already sketching, cross-referencing, his ruined mapmaker's hands flying. "The old cistern gallery. Under the Tanner's Walk. Saints—we've walked over it, I've *mapped* over it, I thought it was a collapsed well—" He looked up, and his face was lit with the particular madness of a man whose obsession has just been vindicated. "Tomorrow night. We can be beneath the Founders' Stones tomorrow night."

Nobody thanked her. People rarely did; thanks implied knowledge of the price, and Sable kept her prices confidential as a matter of professional pride.

But Dominic looked at her across the table—looked at her a moment too long, the way you look at a room you feel you've forgotten something in—and something in her grey eyes snagged, snagged like a sleeve on a nail, some ghost of recognition with no root to grow from. "Sable," she said slowly. "Are you all right?"

And Sable Voss, who had forty-two holes in her library now, who had just spent her one unpriceable thing, who could not remember why her chest ached or why looking at this woman felt like reading a letter addressed to someone else—Sable smiled her card-table smile and lied beautifully.

"Never better," she said. "When do we leave?"

Behind her, unnoticed by anyone, Wren was staring at the fading image above the slate—at not the door, but at the edges of the memory, the margins where stolen things always blurred—and the child's face had gone pale as milk. Because children who spoke dead tongues sometimes saw what adults couldn't, and what Wren saw, clinging to the memory's rim like frost on a window, was a shape. Watching. Patient. On the *other side* of the door, with its attention already flowing through the crack, toward the city above—

Toward the child who could speak its name.
