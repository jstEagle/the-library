## 14. Under the Founders' Stones

Ferro had spent forty years learning to read the ground, and the ground beneath Saltmere's oldest quarter had been lying to everyone for three hundred years.

He stood at the mouth of the cistern stair behind the tanner's row, lantern held low, and felt the old thrill rise in him like water in a well. Above them, the city went about its evening—fishwives arguing, watchmen lighting their lamps, the usurper's criers reading edicts at every corner. Below, under six feet of foundation stone and thirty feet of older stone that no one bothered to count, something waited that appeared on none of his maps.

Not yet.

"Say it again," Wren whispered beside him. "The way Sable said it."

Ferro looked down at her. The girl had appointed herself his shadow since they'd left Nan Hollis's kitchen two nights ago, trailing him through Ferro's own borrowed rooms while he inked the route from Sable's stolen memory onto paper no larger than his palm. She watched everything. She remembered everything. It was, he suspected, how she'd survived soldiers who burned villages for their languages.

"The clerk's memory," he said. "Sable took it whole, before the fever took her for a day. He was a vault-keeper, forty years in the guild's lowest cellars. And what he knew—" Ferro unfolded the scrap of paper again, though he no longer needed to. He could have recited it in his sleep. He *had* recited it in his sleep, twice, waking both times with his heart hammering like a debt collector's fist. "*Third cistern behind the tanners' row. Descend past the flood line. The founders did not build their city on rock. They built it on a door, and they built their laws on top of it so no one would ever look.*"

Wren nodded solemnly, as if he'd quoted scripture. In a way, he supposed he had.

"You're smiling," she said.

"I am not."

"You are. You smile like my grandfather did when he found a word he'd lost." She said it simply, without accusation, and it landed anyway, sharp under the ribs. Her grandfather. Another speaker of a country that wasn't. Another name on another list of the dead.

Ferro closed the paper. "Your grandfather sounds like a wise man."

"He cried when he sang," Wren said. "Papa said it was because songs are just maps of places you can't walk anymore." She looked up at him, guileless, devastating. "Is that why your maps cry?"

"I don't cry over maps."

"No," she agreed. "You cry over countries."

Behind them, Dominic cleared her throat—the soft, deliberate cough she used instead of saying *stop talking, child, you're flaying him*. Ferro heard Ilva shift at the stair's head, keeping watch on the street above, one hand resting easy near the knife at her belt. The deserter sergeant still moved like a soldier even out of uniform: weight forward, eyes moving, always cataloguing exits. There were only two here. One of them led down into the dark.

"Well," Ferro said. "Shall we go meet the founders?"

* * *

The cistern stair was older than the city's oldest charter, and Ferro loved it immediately and completely.

The steps were cut in a style no mason used anymore—each tread slightly concave, worn by centuries of feet, but laid in a herringbone pattern that predated the free cities' founding guilds by generations. His fingers itched for charcoal and paper. Every architecture student in Saltmere was taught that the first quarter rose on bedrock. Every textbook repeated it. And every textbook was wrong, and Ferro had known it was wrong since he was nineteen years old and disgraced, and knowing it had kept him alive in the way a splinter keeps a body awake.

"Watch the third step," he called back softly. "It's false. Drops a hand's depth to catch rainwater—or anything else coming up."

"Why would anyone catch things coming *up*?" Sable asked. She was moving better now, the stolen memory settled into her like a swallowed stone, but there was a new hesitancy in her stride. Twice already she'd stopped mid-step, staring at nothing, her lips moving around words that weren't hers. Ferro understood the cost of what she'd done. He'd seen her rub her temples and reach for a childhood memory—a dog's name, a mother's face—and find the shelf empty. She hadn't spoken of it. She didn't need to. It was written all over the careful way she touched the walls as she descended, as if confirming each stone was real.

"To keep things down," Dominic answered from the rear. Her voice was flat, professional, the voice she used for appraisals. But Ferro had spent enough weeks traveling with her now to hear what lived underneath it: the hum, the pressure, the fifteen years of unspent words. Being this close to whatever waited below must feel to her like standing next to a struck bell.

They passed the flood line. Ferro marked it with chalk—an old habit—and noted, with a cartographer's detached delight and a living man's dread, that the damp stopped in a perfectly straight line. Not water. *Boundary.*

Below the flood line, the stair changed.

Above, it had been workmanlike, functional, the bones of a city doing its business. Here the walls were dressed stone, close-fitted without mortar, and carved. Ferro raised his lantern and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

Thorns.

The walls were covered in thorn-work—a climbing briar rendered in low relief, spiraling down the stairwell in an unbroken line, and threaded through every third coil, unmistakable even after three centuries of darkness: the sigil. The same sigil stamped on Wren's iron key. The same sigil Ferro had traced a thousand times in the margins of a hundred forbidden documents, wondering, always wondering, what country had been confident enough to carve itself into the foundations of someone else's city.

"It's everywhere," Ilva breathed. She had come down against her better judgment, leaving the street above to luck and a bribed bread-seller. Her soldier's eye was reading the carvings differently than Ferro's—looking for defenses, chokepoints, murder holes. "This isn't decoration. This is a warning. *Turn back.* Written in a language nobody down here could read."

"They could read it once," Wren said.

She stepped past Ferro, and her small hand came up and touched the carving, and she spoke.

One word. Soft, almost conversational, the way she spoke when she was greeting something rather than commanding it. The word fell through the stairwell like a coin down a well, and the lantern flames all leaned toward the wall at once, and for half a heartbeat Ferro saw the relief glow faintly from within—not light exactly, more like attention, like the stone remembering it had once meant something.

Then dark again. Then silence.

Nobody moved. Somewhere far above, muffled through thirty feet of stone, a church bell rang the evening hour, absurd and ordinary.

"What did you say?" Sable whispered.

"*Good evening,*" Wren said. "I think it's polite to say good evening when you visit someone's house." A pause. "It said thank you. I think. It sounded very tired."

Ferro looked at Dominic. Dominic looked at the wall. Whatever negotiation happened between them happened without words, and ended with Dominic nodding once: *down.*

* * *

The chamber at the bottom was not a chamber.

That was Ferro's second thought, arriving hot on the heels of his first, which had been *it's real, oh gods, it's real, I was right, I was right all along.* Because the room—if room it was—was less a space than a refusal of one. The founders' stones formed a rough oval, maybe sixty feet across, pillars at intervals holding up a ceiling lost in shadow, and every surface carried the thorn-sigil, dense near the far wall, thinning toward the entrance like frost retreating from a warm window.

And in the center of the far wall stood the threshold.

Ferro approached it the way he'd once approached a sleeping bear he'd surprised on a mountain pass: slowly, obliquely, with tremendous respect. Up close it resolved into a doorway perhaps nine feet tall, cut from a single slab of grey stone that matched nothing else in the chamber—greyer than granite, smoother than river glass, cold enough that he could feel it on his face from four steps away. No hinge. No handle. No seam. Just the shape of a door, carved with the sigil at its crown, and sealed.

Sealed shut. Sealed *shut*, in the way that a wound shut is shut—with intention, with violence, with scar tissue. Ferro could see it now that he was close: hairline lines running across the stone's face, radiating from the sigil like cracks in ice, except they weren't cracks. They were letters. Thousands upon thousands of tiny Alderman characters packed edge to edge, covering every inch of the door, a sentence or ten thousand sentences, an entire library compressed into a seal.

"It's writing," Wren whispered. Her voice had gone strange. She pressed both palms flat against the cold stone, and Ferro watched her lips move as she read, faster and faster, and then stop altogether.

"Wren?" Dominic was beside her instantly. "What is it?"

"It's not a lock." The girl's hands stayed on the door. "Locks say *no*. This says—" she frowned, hunting for the word in the free cities' common tongue, finding it inadequate, trying again. "This says *stay*. Like—like when you tell someone to stay safe. But also like when you tell a dog to stay. Both at once. It's very confused about which one it means."

"That," said Sable quietly, "is the most honest description of a seal I've ever heard."

Ferro circled the threshold, sketching by lantern-light, his pencil flying. Proportions, carvings, the orientation of the sigil relative to true north—he recorded everything, because recording was what he did, because a thing written down could survive anything, because he had spent his whole life rebuilding a murdered country out of marginalia and this, *this*, was the margin where the country itself still lived. His hand shook. He let it shake and drew through it.

"We should go," Ilva said, from the chamber's mouth. She hadn't crossed into it. Ferro noticed that; filed it away. The ex-sergeant, who'd faced cavalry charges, wouldn't step inside. "We've seen it. We've confirmed it. Every minute we spend—"

"Ilva." Ferro didn't look up from his sketch. "When did you last check the street?"

"A quarter hour ago."

"Check again."

She hesitated, then climbed back toward the stair. And in the silence she left behind, Ferro set down his pencil and did the thing he had promised himself he would do if he ever found this place.

He knelt.

The stone floor was cold through his trousers. Forty years of obsession, of disgrace, of maps drawn in secret and burned in panic, of a career destroyed because he insisted a country existed that every atlas said did not—it all funneled down to this: a middle-aged man kneeling on a founder's floor in the dark, saying nothing, because there were no words in any language he spoke that would be adequate.

"Ferro." Dominic's voice, gentle in a way he'd rarely heard from her. "Get up, old friend. Floors like this remember knees."

"Let it remember mine, then." He looked up at the sealed door, at the sigil crowning it, at the vast compressed library of a language that had refused to die. "Do you know what I thought I'd find? All these years? I thought I'd find proof. Evidence. Something to hold up and say *see, see, it was real.*" He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "I never thought it would be *cold*. I never thought it would be waiting."

"It's not waiting," Wren said.

Ferro turned. The girl had stepped back from the door, and her face in the lantern-light was pale and older than her years.

"What do you mean?"

"It's listening." She wrapped her arms around herself. "There's something on the other side. It's not asleep. It's—" She shuddered. "It's hungry, Ferro. Not for food. For *words*. Nobody has talked to it in so long." Her eyes came up to Dominic's, and Ferro saw the question in them before either of them voiced it. "Can we open it?"

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her hand drifted to her own throat—to the oath-bond, Ferro realized, the scar-tissue of her forsworn gift.

"Not tonight," she said. "And not alone, and not without understanding what we're opening. Quillon's ledger said *paid in full, sealed by oath*. Oaths like that aren't locks, Wren. They're promises. And I need to know what my family promised before I decide whether to keep it or break it." She looked at the door, and Ferro watched fifteen years of buried grief cross her face like weather. "But yes. Eventually. I think it was always going to come to eventually."

* * *

Ilva's boots came down the stair fast—too fast, the rhythm wrong, urgency in every strike.

"Light," she said, before she'd even reached the bottom. "Two lanterns, maybe three, entering the tanner's row from the east. Moving door to door. That's not a patrol—that's a search pattern."

The warmth drained out of the chamber as if a door had opened onto winter. Dominic moved first, sweeping Wren behind her with one arm while the other hand went to her blade. "How long?"

"Minutes. Less." Ilva's jaw was tight. "If they have the clerk's confession too—if they know about the cisterns—"

"They can't," Sable said. But her voice wavered, because they both knew what memory-stealing looked like from the other side, what interrogation looked like, what a man would give up to make pain stop.

"Doesn't matter." Dominic was already moving, snuffing lanterns, her voice dropping into the clipped cadence of command that Ferro had heard only twice before and hoped never to need again. "Ferro. Your map. Which way out?"

Ferro's mind snapped into the geometry he trusted more than his own heartbeat. "West passage. It runs under the dyer's lane and comes up through a well shaft—narrow, but it bypasses the street entirely. The east stairs are lost to us."

"Then west. Wren, stay touching me. Sable, you know the dark best—you're our eyes. Ilva, rear guard, and if anyone follows us down—" Dominic paused.

"I'll introduce them to the third step," Ilva said grimly. "Repeatedly."

They moved. But at the chamber's mouth Ferro stopped—one last look back, because he was who he was, because some part of him might never leave this place even after his body climbed into the night air. The sealed door stood in the dark, cold and patient and covered in its ten thousand tiny letters, crowned with the sigil of a drowned country.

And as the last lantern swung away, he heard it—or thought he heard it, or would swear until his dying day that he heard it—faint as breath, deep as foundations:

a single word, in a language only two people in that chamber could understand,

spoken by something that had been told to *stay* for three hundred years.

Ferro ran.

He caught the others at the west passage, lungs burning, and didn't tell them what he'd heard, because some knowledge is a burden you carry so others don't have to. But Wren looked back at him from Dominic's side, her hand small and fierce in the swordspeaker's grip, and her eyes said she'd heard it too.

Behind them, far above, the searchers' lanterns began to descend the east stairs.
