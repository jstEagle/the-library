## 15. Ilva Turns Her Coat

The orders came at dawn, sealed with the black wax of the Ninth Column, and Sergeant Ilva Rask read them three times before her hands stopped shaking.

*By command of His Radiance Aldous, rightful heir of Aldermere: all entrances to the undercity of Vhalsport are to be located, mapped, and sealed with masonry and iron. No passage is to remain. Any found within the foundations are to be taken alive. Warrants extend to linguists, their harborers, and their harborers' kin.*

Alive. That word was doing more work than it appeared to. Ilva had been a soldier long enough—eleven years, first in the border levies and then, after the conscription rolls swallowed her village's second sons and daughters whole, in the usurper's regular columns—to know when *alive* meant *alive for now*. The interrogators preferred them breathing. What happened after was not her department.

She folded the order along its crease and set it on the map table of the garrison house, where the morning light came through salt-fogged windows and turned everything the color of old bruises.

"Sergeant?" Corporal Denna Marsh stood in the doorway, helmet under her arm, nineteen years old and still carrying herself like the army might notice if she slouched. "Is it true? We're going under the city?"

"It's true."

"They say there's people living down there. Under the Founders' Quarter. Whole families that never come up." Denna said it the way children say things about the sea—not quite believing, not quite daring not to.

Ilva looked at her for a moment too long. "There's people everywhere, Corporal. That's the trouble with cities."

* * *

They spent four days finding doors.

It was, Ilva had to admit, work she was good at. She had a surveyor's patience and a smuggler's instinct for architecture; she could look at a cellar wall and see where the stones didn't match, could smell the difference between a dead cistern and a live one by the draft. Her squads found seven entrances in the Founders' Quarter alone: behind a wine merchant's rack, beneath a chapel that had been a granary before it had been anything else, down a well so old its rope-marks had worn grooves a hand deep into the lip of stone.

At each one, the procedure was the same. Mark it on the map. Post a watch. Report to Captain Verrek, who reported upward, and whose reports came back stamped and filed somewhere Ilva would never see.

On the fourth day they found the eighth entrance, and everything changed.

It was under a chandler's shop on Tallow Row, a trapdoor beneath the rendering floor, and when Ilva pried it open and lowered her lantern, she saw not a tunnel but a stairway cut with deliberate care, each step worn concave by centuries of feet, and at the bottom—forty feet down, at the edge of the light—a door. Not a cellar door. A door of grey stone carved with a spiral sigil like a thorn coiled into itself, and around the frame, in letters older than any alphabet she knew, words that seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at them.

Her lantern flame bent toward the door. Not away from it. *Toward.*

"Close it up, Sergeant?" asked Denna, already reaching for the chalk to mark the wall.

Ilva stood there longer than she should have. The air rising from below smelled of dust and cold water and something else—something faintly sweet, like hay dried in sun she'd never felt. It reminded her, absurdly, of her grandmother's chest of linens, of the smell of things kept folded and waiting.

"Sergeant?"

"Yes," she said. "Mark it."

And then, in the margin of her survey sheet, in her own cramped hand, she wrote what she would later understand was the first sentence of her new life:

*Entrance unstable. Stair collapsed in antiquity. No access. Recommend no further survey.*

She looked at the lie for a long time. Then she blotted it, rolled the map, and carried it up the stairs into the grey morning like a man carrying a lit fuse through a powder store.

* * *

Captain Verrek took the report without reading it closely, which was the first mercy. He was a Vhalsport man himself, promoted past his competence because the usurper trusted outsiders least and therefore promoted them most, and he had the harried look of someone drowning in paperwork from three directions. He stamped her survey, initialed the margin, and said only, "The Tallow Row one matters. The envoy's people think there's a threshold under the Founders' Quarter. Corvane wants every approach catalogued before the week's out."

"Catalogued," Ilva repeated. "Not sealed?"

"The sealing crews arrive with the main force. When the walls come down." He said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. Everyone in the garrison knew the siege train was coming; you could hear the roads humming with wagons day and night. But hearing it named, attached to this particular door, made Ilva's stomach drop through the floor of her.

"And until then?"

"Watch it. Nothing goes in or out." Verrek finally glanced up. "You've gone pale, Sergeant. Eat something."

"Yes, sir," said Ilva, who had not been pale, and went to eat nothing, and instead walked out onto the seawall where the wind off the flats could scour the thought out of her head.

It didn't work. The thought stayed. *Nothing goes in or out.* Except that something already had. A swordspeaker and a child and whatever ragged company the woman had gathered—and Ilva knew, had known since the fish market, since she'd seen the aftermath of that impossible violence and heard the whisper run through the ranks like fire through dry grass: *a woman spoke and steel died.* Knew it the way you know a debt.

She owed them. Or the city did, or the language did, or simple decency did—it hardly mattered which. What mattered was that she had just written *no access* over the one door that mattered, and Captain Verrek had stamped it, and now the lie existed in official ink, protected by the very bureaucracy that would hang her if it unraveled.

Treason, it turned out, was mostly paperwork.

* * *

The bread-seller's name was Ottoline, though everyone called her Mama Crust, and her stall sat at the mouth of Cooper's Lane where the garrison foot traffic passed twice daily. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a flour sack, with forearms that could crack walnuts and eyes that missed nothing on either side of her counter. Half the garrison bought her rye loaves. The other half bought her honey buns and pretended they hadn't.

Ilva had been buying bread from her for two years. In that time she had learned three things: the loaves were honest, the prices were not negotiable, and Ottoline had opinions about the occupation that she kept folded away as carefully as a knife in a bodice.

On the evening after she falsified the Tallow Row report, Ilva joined the queue with her heart hammering like a drummer boy who'd found the wine.

"One loaf," she said.

Ottoline wrapped it without looking up. "Rye's dense today. Keeps better."

"I'm marching tomorrow."

"Mm." The old woman tied the cloth with a practiced twist. "Where to?"

"Tallow Row. Watch duty."

Something flickered across Ottoline's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "Long nights on that street. Cold, too. You'll want something warm in you." She reached beneath the counter and brought up a second loaf, smaller, darker, split and stuffed with a paste of fig and pepper that Ilva had never seen her sell. "House gift. Don't tell the tax man."

Their eyes met. Ilva understood, in the way you understand a bridge holds weight only after you've stepped onto it, that she was being offered more than bread.

"There's a message inside," she said quietly. It wasn't a question.

Ottoline's hands never stopped moving, wiping the board, squaring the loaves. "There's bread inside, Sergeant. Whatever else a customer finds in her supper is between her and her digestion." She leaned an inch closer. The smell of yeast and woodsmoke. "I don't know what you're carrying and I don't want to. But I'll say this much for free: my sister's girl married into the Founders' Quarter thirty years back. Good family. Quiet people. And if soldiers start pulling up floorstones looking for quiet families—" she straightened, loud again, cheerful, a vendor once more, "—then I say let the buyer beware, and good evening to you, Sergeant, and mind the step."

Ilva walked away with two loaves and a heartbeat she could hear in her ears.

In the privacy of her billet, an attic room over a rope-maker's shed, she broke open the dark loaf. Inside the fig paste, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a strip of paper no wider than her thumb, covered in a fine, slanted script:

*If you have something to say to the swordspeaker, say it Thursday, market bell, and buy the honey buns. — O.*

Ilva sat on the edge of her cot for a long while, holding the note, listening to the ropes creak in their loft above her head. There was still a door back. She could burn the note. She could eat the evidence. She could go to Verrek in the morning and report a seditious baker, and be thanked for it, and sleep at night, or whatever passed for sleep these days.

Instead she fed the note to the stove, ate the bread—both loaves, all of it, destroying the evidence the way traitors presumably had since the first traitor—and lay awake until the bells rang the small hours, staring at the rafters, rehearsing sentences.

That was the thing no one told you about treason. The terror wasn't in the moment of betrayal. It was in all the ordinary hours afterward, when your body insisted on being a body—hungry, tired, needing bread—and every ordinary act became a performance, and every performance had one audience: the future version of yourself who would judge whether you'd done the right thing, and the present version of Captain Verrek, who would hang you if he ever read the margin of a certain survey sheet.

She slept maybe two hours. She dreamed of a grey stone door with a spiral thorn carved on it, and the dream-door was breathing.

* * *

Thursday, market bell. Cooper's Lane was crowded with the last market before the roads closed—refugees already trickling in from the eastern villages had doubled the price of everything, and the stalls were picked over like bones. Ilva wore no uniform; she had leave until noon, and a plain coat, and the sensation of walking around without her skin.

Ottoline's stall. Honey buns, golden and glistening.

"Two," said Ilva.

"For sharing, are we?"

"That depends on the company."

Ottoline boxed the buns with maddening slowness. "Company's been asking after you, dear. Quiet-like. Through channels." Her chin indicated, minutely, the alley beside the chandlery next door—the chandlery, Ilva noted, on Tallow Row's approach, the chandlery whose cellar held the world. "Says a soldier helped her once, whether she meant to or not. Says she'd like to thank her proper."

"In the alley?"

"In the alley. Two minutes. My buns go cold and my reputation goes with them."

The alley was narrow and smelled of tallow and cat. Ilva stepped into it, and the shadows resolved, and there she was: Dominic Thackeray, the ledger-keeper turned fugitive, thinner than the last time Ilva had seen her, a fresh scar on her jaw, standing with her back to the wall in the stance of a woman who had learned, recently and permanently, what ambush felt like.

"You got my message," Ilva said.

"I got Ottoline's message. Which was apparently also yours." Dominic's voice was low, roughened. "Why? You're wearing the black column's pay. Why risk your neck to reach me?"

"Because I falsified a military survey, and I need you to know it before someone un-falsifies it for me." Ilva kept her eyes on the mouth of the alley, scanning the street beyond. Talking fast, talking low. "The Tallow Row entrance. Under the chandlery. Your door, or near enough—I saw the sigil, the spiral thorn. My orders were to catalogue it for the sealing crews. I wrote it up as collapsed. Unusable. Verrek signed it."

Dominic went very still. "You lied in an official report. For a door you've never used."

"I lied for a door that leads to people who shouldn't be found. Yes." She made herself meet the other woman's eyes. "I'm not asking for thanks. I'm telling you what's coming, because what's coming is worse than you know. The main force arrives within days. The whole siege train. They're not just investing the walls—they're coming for the undercity specifically. Corvane has maps. Old ones. He knows the Founders' Quarter sits on top of something, and he means to take it apart stone by stone until he finds it."

"How long?"

"Days. Four, five, if the wagons don't break axles. After that, every entrance gets walled and watched, and anyone below gets flushed out with smoke and iron." Ilva hesitated, then made herself say the rest. "There's more. Envoy Corvane keeps a list. Names. Linguists, harborers, and—he calls it this, in writing—*'sympathetic municipal assets.'* I've seen the list. Ottoline's on it. So am I, I suspect, though I haven't confirmed it, and I'd rather not confirm it by asking."

"He'll raid the guild," Dominic said softly. Not a guess. A reading.

"He'll make an example of it. The guild's the biggest name on the list. When the army's in position, they'll hit Quillon first, loud, public. Everything he's got in those vaults will end up in Corvane's hands—every record, every debt, every secret anyone ever traded away." Ilva finally let herself look at Dominic directly. "Including, I'd wager, things about you. Things you paid to have forgotten."

The scarred face didn't move, but something behind the eyes flinched, and Ilva understood that she'd struck bone.

"So move," Ilva said. "Whatever you need from the guild, whoever you can pull out of Quillon's orbit—do it before the wagons arrive. And get your people clear of Tallow Row. Once the sealing crews come, I can't protect that entrance. Nobody can. All I can do is lie on paper for a few days longer than they expect me to."

"Why?" Dominic asked again, quieter now. "Truly. I need to know I can trust this."

Ilva thought about the answer for longer than the alley allowed. She thought about the prisoner in the camp outside Marrow Ford, the old man executed for a single word spoken in his sleep, and how she'd stood at the edge of the torchlight and done nothing, and how *nothing* had a weight that compounded daily, like interest. She thought about the stairway under the chandlery, and the lantern flame bending toward the door, and the smell of hay dried in an impossible sun.

"Because eleven years ago they put a rifle in my hands and told me it was a career," she said. "Every year since, they've asked me to hold it against somebody, and every year the somebody gets smaller. First armies. Then villages. Now a child who speaks a dead country's tongue." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than any parade ever had. "One of these days the somebody is going to be me. I'd rather choose my side while I still remember there was a choice."

Dominic studied her for a long moment. Then she extended her hand—not a courtier's handshake but a soldier's, forearm to forearm, grip hard.

"Thursday after next," she said. "Market bell. If there's another message, it comes the same way. And Ilva—" a ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile, "—when this is over, if there's an *over*, I keep a ledger of debts. Yours is going in it."

"Don't bother. I'm not doing it for credit."

"No," Dominic agreed. "That's why it counts."

Then she was gone, dissolved into the market crowd with a skill that must have taken fifteen years of practice, and Ilva stood alone in the tallow-smelling dark with two boxes of honey buns and the knowledge that she had just, irrevocably, become the thing her oaths were written to hang.

* * *

She went back to work.

That was the unbearable part, she discovered. Treason did not excuse you from drills, from ration inventories, from Denna Marsh asking earnest questions about load-bearing masonry. On Friday she inspected two more cellars and lied about neither, because neither mattered, and the discipline of lying *selectively* was somehow worse than lying wholesale—it required her to keep perfect books in her head, a double ledger, the true city and the reported city running in parallel columns that must never touch.

Saturday, Verrek summoned her.

For one white-hot instant, standing before his desk, Ilva was certain. This was how it ended: not with a chase but with a folder, a margin-note, a sergeant's collar taken in a bare office while the garrison went about its morning. She arranged her face into blankness and waited.

Verrek pushed a dispatch across the desk. "Corvane's advance riders. They'll reach the walls in three days, ahead of the main body. Envoy's orders." His finger tapped the page. "He wants the Tallow Row entrance opened. *Before* the sealing crews. Says there may be persons of interest already using the approaches, and he doesn't want to wall them in with the evidence. He wants them taken first."

Ilva read the order twice. Her hands, she was proud to note, did not shake. Somewhere in the last week she had burned through all her shaking and arrived at the far shore, where the terror went quiet and cold and clear.

"When?"

"As soon as his engineers arrive. Three days." Verrek rubbed his eyes. "Pick your best people, Sergeant. And Rask—" he looked up, and for a moment there was something almost human in his tired face, "—the men are saying things. About the fish market. About what spoke there. I don't believe in ghosts in languages. But Corvane does, and Corvane's beliefs have rifles. Whatever's down there under our feet, I want it handled clean. No heroics. Understood?"

"Understood, sir," said Sergeant Ilva Rask, who had already composed, in her head, the message she would fold into a loaf of rye before the hour was out:

*Three days. Corvane comes early, with engineers, for the door under the chandlery. Move now or lose it. And whatever you left in Quillon's keeping—take it with you, because the guild will not survive the month. — A friend of the house*

She saluted, she marched, she bought bread.

The queue at Ottoline's stall was long that morning, full of frightened people stocking up before the roads closed, and Ilva stood among them holding her place like any honest soldier, feeling the lie in her pocket warm as a coal, waiting her turn to commit the smallest, largest crime of her life: buying a loaf.
