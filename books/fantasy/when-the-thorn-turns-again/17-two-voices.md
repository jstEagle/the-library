## 17. Two Voices

The undercity smelled of rain that had fallen before the city had a name.

Dominic felt it in her teeth as they descended the last of Ferro's stairs—a cold mineral breath rising from the dark, older than the free cities, older than the guild, older than the ledgers she had spent fifteen years pretending were her life. The lantern light wobbled in Sable's hand. Behind her, Nan Hollis leaned on Wren's shoulder, and the old woman was humming something low and circular, a tune with no beginning that Dominic's bones recognized before her mind did.

*The door answers only to a full sentence spoken by two voices.*

Nan had said it three nights ago, over a fire built from broken cart wheels, while the siege drums above them counted out the hours like a slow heart failing. The sentence was stitched through the lullabies, through Ferro's marginalia, through the fragments the half-speakers carried like splinters of a shattered mirror—each one holding a piece of sky. They had spent two days assembling it, laying word against word the way Ferro laid his maps, arguing over stresses, over the fall of a syllable, over whether the fourth phrase began on an exhale or a held breath.

And now the sentence stood complete in front of her, written in chalk on the underside of Sable's forearm because paper could be taken from a corpse and skin, apparently, could not—and Dominic could not read it without her throat closing.

"Say it again," Wren said. "The whole thing. Slow."

Sable recited it. Eleven phrases, some in Alderman, some in the older tongue beneath Alderman, one line that was not language at all but a rhythm, a knock-pattern, *three and pause, two and hold*. The half-speakers who had come down with them—Marta with her merchant's grammar, the twins Osk and Ilse who spoke only in rhyme because rhyming was all their grandmother had kept alive, gray-bearded Tam whose fragment was a single word for *threshold* that he repeated like a prayer—each mouthed their own pieces along with her, and when the sentence ended, the silence afterward had weight.

"It wants to be said here," Marta whispered. "You can feel it wanting."

Dominic could. That was the problem. The words pulled at her the way a hook pulls at a fish that has seen other fish take the bait. Fifteen years of silence had not killed the gift; it had only taught it patience, and now it rose in her chest like water finding a crack, and she pressed it down the way she always did, the way she'd done ten thousand times, except this time pressing hurt more than speaking would.

"You don't have to say it," Wren said quietly. She was standing close, close enough that Dominic could see the salt-sting scars on the girl's knuckles from the night she'd first scratched at the guild door. "I heard you in the market. When you broke their swords. I know what it costs."

"That wasn't cost," Dominic said. "That was interest. The principal comes due later."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'm putting off a debt." She looked up at the threshold.

It filled the end of the chamber like a wound fills a wall. Twice the height of two men, carved into the living stone of the free cities' oldest foundation, ringed with the sigil—the interlocking thorn-and-key that Dominic hadn't seen since childhood, that had been stamped into the iron key Wren carried, that appeared on page after forbidden page of the guild's oldest ledger beside her family name. The stone around it was veined with pale seams, like frost on glass, like something trying to grow through from the other side. It was cold. Not winter-cold. *Refusal*-cold.

Ferro had gone quiet the way he went quiet in front of maps he loved. He stood off to the left with his lantern raised, sketching even now, charcoal scratching, because obsession does not stop for awe. Sable hovered near the rear with her arms crossed, watching the stair behind them more than the door ahead, because Sable trusted doors the way sailors trust reefs.

"All right," Dominic said. "Positions. Like we practiced. Wren and I center. Everyone else back past the second pillar—if it opens, I don't want anyone standing in the frame."

"Why not?" Osk asked.

"Because thresholds are promises," Nan said from the dark, "and promises bite."

They moved. Lanterns were set in niches. Tam began, unbidden, to murmur his single word—*dorath, dorath, dorath*—and no one told him to stop, because it seemed less like a habit than a kind of ringing bell.

Dominic stepped up beside Wren.

Up close, the door was worse. The carving was fine enough that she could see tool marks finer than anything the free cities' masons produced, and between the strokes of the sigil there were letters, thousands of them, so small they might have been grain until you looked properly and understood that the entire surface was text—that the door was not decorated with writing but *made* of it, compressed, bound, a country's whole tongue hammered flat into a slab. And threaded through every line ran the seal-phrase, repeating, repeating, the oath her family had spoken:

*Aldermere sleeps behind us. We do not look back. We do not call. What is shut for love stays shut for love.*

Her mother had signed that. Her mother's hand, in the ledger, in ink gone brown as old blood. *Sealed by Yarrow, in sorrow, in full.*

"Dominic." Wren touched her wrist. "Your hands are shaking."

"They do that."

"Liar."

Despite everything, almost despite herself, Dominic smiled. "Yes. Liar."

"We say it together," Wren said. "You start. Nan said the elder voice starts. The one who—" She stopped. Even now, kind even now, she wouldn't say it plainly: *the one who sealed it must be the one to open it.* "You start."

Dominic looked at the first phrase chalked on Sable's arm. Translated into sense it meant something like: *We who were scattered, we who remember, stand at the root of the road.* In Alderman it meant more than that—it meant it the way a key means a lock, with a click of rightness that went through her sternum. Her gift strained toward the syllables like a dog toward a door.

"If this goes wrong," Dominic said, loudly enough for the others, "you run. Up the stairs, left at the cistern, out through the chandler's cellar. Ilva's bread-seller will be waiting at the third arch with a cart. You don't wait for me. You don't come back for me. Am I understood?"

A ragged chorus of assent. Sable alone said nothing; she just looked at Dominic with those eyes that had seen too many people trade pieces of themselves away, and gave the smallest nod, which from Sable was a eulogy and a blessing both.

"Wren. Together on my mark."

Wren squared her small shoulders. Thirteen years old, village burned, tongue hunted, and she stood at the door of a buried country like she'd been born for it—which, Dominic thought, perhaps she had.

"*Mark*," Dominic said, and spoke.

---

The first phrase came out of her whole.

She had expected rust, resistance, the grind of fifteen unused years. Instead the words poured as if they had been waiting in her the whole time, patient as seed, and the sound of Alderman in her own voice nearly undid her—it was her mother's cadence, her grandmother's, the kitchen-language of her earliest years, and hearing it leave her mouth was like seeing her own face in water. Wren caught the phrase on the second beat and braided her voice in, higher, brighter, and where the two voices crossed the air itself seemed to tighten.

The door warmed.

Not much. A degree. But the frost-veins in the stone shifted, and somewhere inside the slab something turned over, slow, enormous, like a sleeper changing position.

"*Second phrase*," Nan called softly, and they took it—*the road remembers every foot that walked away from it*—and this time the warmth spread visibly, pale light crawling along the carved lines like dawn coming up a valley. Tam was weeping openly and still saying *dorath*. The twins had joined hands. Marta was whispering her merchant's fragment over and over like counting coins that had suddenly become priceless.

Third phrase. Fourth. The rhythm-knock, which they struck together on the stone with the flats of their palms—*three and pause, two and hold*—and on the *hold*, the whole chamber exhaled. Dust lifted off the floor in a slow spiral. The lantern flames bent toward the door, all of them, uniformly, like grass leaning in wind.

"Ninth phrase," Dominic said, and stopped.

Because the ninth phrase was the hinge. Nan had known it, Ferro's maps had shown it, the assembled sentence made it plain: the opening clause required the sealing clause answered. *What was shut must be named, and what named must be released.* To open the door, the voice of the house that closed it had to stand in its own words—not just the opening sentence, but the oath renewed and then deliberately broken, spoken aloud, witnessed. *We do not look back* — renounced. *We do not call* — renounced. *What is shut for love stays shut for love* — and then the terrible, necessary inversion, the words that had to follow if the door was ever to move again:

*We release what we sealed. We open what we shut. Whatever follows us out, we will meet it with open hands.*

The past as lock. The past as key. Both at once, and both made of her family's voice.

She opened her mouth. Nothing came.

It wasn't stage fright. It wasn't fear of the thing behind the door, though Quillon's last ledger page had told them enough to be afraid—*sealed not to imprison Aldermere, but to keep something from following the exiles out.* It was simpler and worse than that. The words were *bound*. She could feel them sitting in her chest like stones at the bottom of a well, wrapped in the same oath that wrapped them, and the oath would not let her speak its own unraveling. Her mother had sworn it *in sorrow, in full*, and oaths sworn in full do not fray. They pass down. They wait. They sit in the blood of the signatory's children like a coin sewn into a coat lining, and they do not come loose because a girl in a burned village needs them to.

"Dominic?" Wren's voice, very small. "The door's waiting."

The door was waiting. The light had pooled in the sigil's center and hung there, trembling, expectant, and Dominic realized with cold clarity that the door knew exactly what she was trying to say and exactly why she couldn't. It was patient. It had been patient for sixty years. It could be patient while her lungs forgot how to work.

"I can't," she said. The words came out flat and ugly in common-tongue, the language of ledgers, the language of hiding. "I can't say it. It won't—I'm trying and it *won't*—"

"Say what?" Marta asked. "We have the whole sentence. Sable has it all—"

"She doesn't have this part." Dominic pressed her fist against her own sternum as if she could reach in and break the binding manually. "This part isn't in the songs. This part is in me. My mother swore the sealing oath in full and it came down the blood, and an oath sworn in full can't be broken by the heir unless—" She stopped.

Unless what? Unless the original swearer released it. Unless her mother's voice, wherever voices went, spoke the loosening word. Unless—

"Unless you mean it," Nan said.

The old woman had come forward despite everything, past the second pillar, past every rule Dominic had set, one hand on the wall, humming stopped at last. Her eyes in the lantern light were wet and fierce.

"When your mother sealed that door, girl, she meant it. Every word. Love and terror both, in full measure. That's why it holds you like iron—you're carrying an oath that was true." She reached up and gripped Dominic's jaw, gentle and immovable, forcing her to meet her eyes. "You cannot counterfeit meaning. The door will taste the lie. So the question isn't whether you can say the words. The question is—" her grip tightened— "*do you believe them yet? Do you believe it should stay shut? Because if any part of you does, any part at all, the oath will use that part like a foothold, and you will stand here until the usurper's army digs down to us and kills us all in front of a locked door.*"

The chamber was silent except for Tam's whisper and the far-off, muffled heartbeat of the bombardment above, transmitted through sixty feet of stone as a shiver in the floor.

Did she believe it?

She thought of Quillon dying among his ledgers, sending her a key and a note: *Some things were never for sale.* She thought of Ilva's smuggled warnings folded into loaves. She thought of Ferro's life spent reconstructing a country everyone else agreed to forget, and Sable selling pieces of her own past to buy other people's futures, and twelve half-speakers who had hidden their mothers' language in their mouths for decades the way you hide a jewel from thieves, and Wren—Wren, who had knocked on a guild door in a dead tongue because it was the only living thing she had left.

And underneath all of it, older than all of it, she thought of her mother's face on the last morning, the morning before the soldiers came to the Yarrow house, when her mother had knelt and taken little Dominic's hands and said, *Whatever happens, whatever you hear about our name, remember that we shut it out of love. Remember that, even if you hate us for it.*

She had hated them for it. For fifteen years she had hated them, and hidden, and hated the hiding, and told herself the hatred was grief.

But did she believe the door should stay shut?

"No," she said aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle. "No. I don't. Not anymore. Not since I read the ledger and found out my family paid for this country's prison with their name. Not since I met her." She looked at Wren. "I want it open. God help me, I want it open, and I don't know what's behind it, and I want it open anyway."

"Then say it," Nan said.

Dominic turned to the door. She planted her feet the way she'd been taught at eight years old, swordspeaker stance, ground the heel, unlock the breath—and she reached for the ninth phrase, for the renunciation, for the breaking of her blood's oldest bond.

The oath rose to stop her.

She felt it the way you feel a hand on your shoulder in the dark—vast, familiar, sorrowful. *In sorrow, in full.* Her mother's voice layered under her grandmother's under great-aunts and signatories back through the generations of Yarrows, all of them saying *we do not look back, we do not call,* and the weight of them was not cruelty, that was the worst of it; it was love, love calcified into law, love that had decided once and for all and could not bear to be reconsidered. It pressed down on her chest. It filled her mouth with the taste of iron and old ink. It said, in a voice that was half her own: *if you open it, everything they suffered was for nothing.*

And there, in that pressure, in that exact moment, Dominic understood the lock at last.

It wasn't the words she couldn't say. It was the forgiveness she couldn't give. The oath held because she still believed, somewhere below belief, that her family had been wrong—that they had betrayed a country, sold a door, chosen safety over honor—and a person cannot break an oath they secretly despise. Renunciation requires respect. You cannot release what you have never fully held.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you clever, frightened woman. You weren't imprisoning them. You were *grieving*, and you built the grief so strong it outlived you."

"Dominic?" Wren's hand found hers. Small fingers, scarred knuckles, laced tight. "Say it with me. Whatever part you can. Two voices. That's all it's ever needed. Maybe it doesn't need yours to be finished. Maybe it just needs yours to be *trying*."

The light in the sigil pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a patient heart.

Dominic gathered everything she had—gift, grief, guilt, the ache of a lifetime's unspent words—and spoke the first word of the renunciation.

It tore loose like a hook from flesh.

Half a phrase. That was all she got—half the ninth phrase, her voice cracking across the middle syllables, the oath clamping down hard enough that she tasted blood—and Wren caught the falling sentence and carried it, voice ringing pure and furious and thirteen years old, and for three full phrases they flew together, the door blazing now, the whole carved surface lit from within, the frost-veins running with gold—

And then it stopped.

Not failed. *Stopped.* The light drew back into the sigil's center and hung there, and through the stone came a sound none of them had heard before and all of them understood: a deep, tectonic exhalation, almost a sigh. Almost a question.

And in the silence after it, in the marrow of her bones, in the dead tongue she had sworn off for fifteen years, Dominic heard the door speak for the first time.

Two words. Addressed to her alone, in her mother's cadence, in the voice of every Yarrow who had ever signed:

*"Not yet."*

The light went out. The cold rushed back. Somewhere far above, muffled by sixty feet of history, the siege drums changed their rhythm—from the slow count to the quick one, the assault rhythm, the one that meant the walls above the Founders' Quarter were about to be tested.

And from the darkness of the stairwell behind them, breathless, boots hammering stone, the bread-seller's boy came shouting the news that split the night in half:

"The east wall's breached—they're in the streets—the usurper himself is coming, and he's brought speakers of his own!"
