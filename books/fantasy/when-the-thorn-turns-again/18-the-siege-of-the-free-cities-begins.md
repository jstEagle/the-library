## 18. The Siege of the Free Cities Begins

Ilva had stood on the Salt Road wall twice in her life before this. Once as a girl, when her father lifted her onto his shoulders to watch the festival barges come up from the delta with lanterns strung along their masts. Once at her conscription ceremony, when Sergeant-Major Dree told the assembled recruits that the walls were not stone but promise.

She thought about Dree's words now, watching the horizon fill with firelight.

The usurper's army did not march so much as accumulate. For three days it had been growing out of the eastern haze—first a smudge, then a stain, then a tide of cookfires and horse-lines and siege engines dragged by oxen teams that lowed plaintively in the cold, as if even the beasts resented what they were being made to do. By the fourth morning the plain below the walls was a city of its own, canvas and iron, orderly in the way that only very large violence could be orderly.

"Count the ladders," said Captain Vess beside her, without looking away.

"Ladders they haven't raised yet," Ilva said. "They're building more."

"That's an answer for someone who wants to be thanked. Count them anyway."

Ilva counted. Forty-one visible. Probably sixty behind the ridgeline. Enough for three faces of the wall at once, which meant the usurper intended to spend men like water, which meant he believed he had water to spare, which meant the free cities' famous neutrality had finally run out of road.

"They'll parley first," Vess said. "Corvane will want the city to open its gates politely. He hates messes. Messes leave witnesses."

"And if we refuse?"

Vess finally turned to look at her. He was a heavy man gone grey early, with a face like a dropped plate—cracked but still recognizably whole. "Then we find out how many of our defenders are willing to die for a child they've never met." He let that sit between them for a moment. "You've been quiet since the market order came down, Ilva."

"The market order?"

"The one authorizing house-to-house searches in the lower quarters. Linguists and their harborers. You wrote the distribution list yourself."

"I did my job," Ilva said.

"Mm," said Vess, and walked on down the rampart, leaving her alone with the counting.

* * *

By noon the refugees were coming.

They had been trickling in for weeks, ever since the first warrants crossed the borderlands—a cart here, a family there, farmers with their livestock sold off cheap and their eyes already hollowed by the road. But today the trickle became a river. Ilva stood at the Marsh Gate and watched it swell: carts stacked with bedding and grandmothers, children driving geese, a man carrying a doorframe on his back because, he said, his father had built it and he would not leave it for the fires.

"Names?" the gate clerk asked each head of household, scratching entries into the register. "Trade? Point of origin? Any contact with foreign speakers, any documents in unknown scripts—"

"You're registering people against the usurper's warrant," Ilva said quietly, when the clerk paused to dip his pen. "In a book."

The clerk looked up at her. He was perhaps nineteen. "Then I'll write small," he said, and bent back over the page.

That was the thing about the free cities that the usurper had never understood, Ilva thought. You could burn a village of speakers in Aldermere and the silence afterward would hold, because terror is patient. But here were ten thousand people who had never heard an Alderman word in their lives, standing in line to be registered, and every single one of them had decided, somewhere on the road, that they would rather carry the risk than hand over a neighbor to satisfy a distant crown. The language wasn't just words. It was contagious. It made people remember they had spines.

A woman reached the front of the queue with a baby slung across her chest and a boy of six clutching her skirt. Her lips were cracked, her knuckles split from cold. When she spoke, her accent was borderland, but underneath it Ilva caught something else—a lilt, a rise and fall in the vowels that she had heard exactly once before, in a fish market, in a word that had shattered steel.

"Name?"

"Maret Sova. Weaver. From Kellin's Ford."

"Any contact with—"

"My grandmother sang songs nobody knew the words to," Maret said, flat and fast, staring straight ahead. "I don't know if that counts. I can't sing them anymore. She's dead."

The clerk's pen hovered. Ilva stepped forward. "It doesn't count," she said. "Register them. Quartering assignment, east granary annex."

Maret's eyes flicked up to hers—quick, sharp, measuring—and Ilva understood that the woman knew precisely what had just happened, and would say nothing, and would remember everything. That was the other thing about refugees. They learned to read soldiers better than soldiers read them.

* * *

The rumor arrived before the sun set.

Ilva heard it first as a murmur moving down the refugee lines like wind through barley: *the speaker-child is here. In the city. The one who stopped blades with a word. They say the door of Aldermere is under our feet.* By evening it had names attached—the fish market, the thorn-seller's street, a red-haired woman soldier who'd turned her coat—and by full dark it was no longer a rumor but a faith, whispered in the granary annex where Maret Sova's family slept among two hundred others.

Ilva went there herself, ostensibly to check sanitation arrangements, actually because she could not stay away.

The annex was a cathedral of hay and huddled bodies. Oil lamps burned low. And in the corner farthest from the door, half a dozen people sat in a rough circle, and one of them—an old man with a millstone scar across his scalp—was humming.

Not singing. Humming. A melody with no words, or rather, words worn down to their bones until only the shape of them remained. Ilva recognized the tune the way you recognize a face from a dream. Nan Hollis had hummed it once, in a kitchen, years ago, before any of this started, and old Nan had laughed and said it was nonsense syllables, nothing, a children's game.

But the scarred man's granddaughter, a girl of maybe nine, was not humming. She was mouthing something beneath the melody, soundless, her lips shaping syllables with the unconscious precision of someone doing arithmetic. And when the old man faltered, she picked the thread up without hesitation and carried it further than he had, further, it seemed, than he knew it could go.

The circle around her had gone utterly still. Maret Sova stood at its edge with her hands pressed over her mouth.

Ilva should have reported it. It was, in the most literal sense available to her, her duty. *Linguists and their harborers.* She stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind her and felt the report forming itself in her mind—the neat military sentences, the location, the numbers—and watched it dissolve like sugar in rain.

Instead she cleared her throat.

Every head turned. The humming stopped. The little girl looked up at her with wide, unafraid eyes, and Ilva thought: *she doesn't know yet what she is. Nobody's taught her to be afraid.*

"There's a woman called Nan Hollis," Ilva said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Thorn Street. She knows the songs. All of them, all the way through." She pulled off one glove and scratched at the back of her neck, feeling absurd, feeling like a traitor in both directions at once. "If anyone here has pieces. Of the old tongue. Half-remembered things. Grandmother's words, cradle fragments, anything—" She stopped, started again. "There are others. In this city. More than you'd think. They're trying to make something whole out of all the broken parts."

Silence. Then the scarred man said, in a voice like gravel poured slowly: "And who do we tell them sent us? What name does a soldier give?"

"Ilva," she said. "Sergeant Ilva Renn. Third Wall Company." She hesitated. "Formerly of the vanguard. I know what the warrants say. I carried them long enough."

The old man studied her for a long time. Around him the circle waited, breath held, and Ilva realized she was standing in front of the exact tribunal she deserved—one assembled from the wreckage her own army had made—and whatever verdict they handed down, she would accept it.

"Renn," the old man said at last. "There was a Renn family in the river valleys. Long ago. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before the maps changed." He smiled without warmth. "Go home, Sergeant Renn. We'll find Thorn Street. If your Nan Hollis is what you say, then maybe—" He glanced at his granddaughter, still watching Ilva with those unafraid eyes. "Maybe the broken parts remember how to be a pot."

* * *

At midnight the parley horns sounded from the eastern wall, and Ilva ran the battlements with the rest of the Third Company to see Corvane's herald ride forward under a white banner that was, she noted, silk—real silk, dyed expensive white, because even surrender offers from the usurper came with a price tag.

Herald Ossen read the proclamation in a voice trained to carry: the free cities had harbored enemies of the realm; the realm offered amnesty to all who surrendered the last speakers and opened their gates; resistance would be answered with fire; the usurper was merciful; the usurper was patient; the usurper remembered.

"He remembers," Ilva murmured to Vess, who had come up beside her.

"Remembering is cheap," Vess said. "Everyone remembers. The question is what he remembers *for*."

Below, on the dark plain, forty-one ladders waited in their stacks, and beyond them the cookfires burned in patterns too regular to be anything but intention. Ilva looked at the fires and saw, superimposed over them, another night fifteen years ago—or so the veterans said—when a different wall had faced a different army, and a country had died not in battle but in paperwork, warrants, lists, registers of tongues.

History repeating, her grandmother used to call it, though she'd meant it about marriages and bad harvests. Now Ilva understood the phrase properly. History didn't repeat like an echo. It repeated like pressure—the same weight applied to the same place until something gave. And everything, everyone, was gathered now in one place to feel it: the refugees in their granaries, the half-speakers with their broken syllables, the child somewhere in the city whose name she didn't even know, and above it all the walls, stone pretending to be promise.

"Herald's withdrawing," a lookout called. "They're giving us till dawn tomorrow to answer."

"Dawn," Vess repeated. He rubbed his jaw. "Get some sleep, Renn. Tomorrow the council meets, and after the council—" He shrugged, eloquently.

Ilva didn't sleep. She walked down through the sleeping city instead, past shuttered shops and lamp-lit shrines, to Thorn Street, where a light still burned in Nan Hollis's window. Through it she could see silhouettes: the old woman, a tall swordspeaker woman she'd seen only in wanted-sketches, and a small figure that could only be Wren, sitting cross-legged on the floor with her mouth open around a word Ilva couldn't hear through the glass.

But she saw what the word did. Every candle in the room leaned toward the child, all at once, like flowers toward sun.

And on the doorstep, waiting in the cold with bundles at their feet, stood seven strangers—refugees, travelers, a woman in weaver's clothes, a man with a millstone scar—drawn through a besieged city's streets by the same gravity that bent candleflame.

Ilva stood in the shadow of the opposite wall, a soldier off-duty outside a house full of treason, and knocked anyway.

The door opened. Nan Hollis peered out, took one look at her uniform, and said, "Well. Either you're arresting us or you're the sergeant from the gate. Come in out of the cold either way, girl, and bring your conscience with you—it looks frozen."

Behind her, in the warm room, the small figure turned, and Wren looked at Ilva with eyes that had already seen too much, and held out her hand as if welcoming her home.
