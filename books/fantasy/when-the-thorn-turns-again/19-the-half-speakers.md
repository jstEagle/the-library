## 19. The Half-Speakers

They came in ones and twos at first, as rumors do.

The siege had been on for nine days when the first of them appeared at the fish-cellar door — a woman with river mud dried to her knees, carrying nothing but a loaf she refused to eat because, she said, you didn't eat the bread that bought your passage; you gave it back to the house that sheltered you. Nan Hollis took the loaf in both hands like something consecrated, and the woman wept without sound, the way people weep who have spent years learning not to be heard.

"Name," Sable said from the shadows, because someone had to.

"Maren Ostrevich." The woman said it like an apology.

"And what are you, Maren Ostrevich?"

Maren looked at her feet, then up, and her voice changed — dropped into a register none of them used, low and round, vowels worn smooth as river stones. "I am the daughter of Aldermere."

Dominic, standing at the top of the cellar stair with Wren's hand tight in hers, felt the words go through her chest like a bell struck under the ribs. She had not heard the language spoken aloud by anyone but a child in fifteen years. It was not that she'd forgotten it. It was that hearing it hurt, the way weight hurts a limb long splinted — proof of everything the bone still remembered how to carry.

*Come in,* Wren answered, before Dominic could stop her, in the same tongue. *You're safe. We're keeping the light low.*

And Maren Ostrevich crossed the threshold like a woman walking into water she'd been told was cold and found warm.

* * *

By the twelfth day there were eleven of them, and Ferro had started calling them the Remnant, which Sable said sounded too much like a military unit and Nan said sounded about right.

They came through the flooded cisterns and over the rooftops where Ilva's defectors kept watch, each one drawn by the same rumor, mutated differently in every mouth: *a child in the free cities speaks the old tongue whole.* Some came because they wanted to hear it again before they died. Some came because they were afraid of what it meant, and wanted to see the danger with their own eyes. One man came because his grandmother had made him swear an oath on her deathbed — *find the door, Toma, find the door* — and he had spent thirty-one years finding nothing, and had almost stopped believing he'd ever sworn anything at all.

Toma Vetch was a cooper by trade, sixty if he was a day, with hands like knotted rope. He arrived carrying his oath the way other men carried knives, and he did not smile for the first four days.

There was also:

— Petra Ilyen, a midwife from the salt marshes, who knew only three words of the language but sang them while she worked, and whose singing made grown men sit down suddenly on crates.

— The twins, Ansel and Odile Marchand, nineteen years old, whose mother had taught them the language only in whispers after dark, half a word each so neither child alone would know enough to hang them, and who finished each other's sentences out of habit so deep it was indistinguishable from love.

— Old Gaspard, who was not old at all but had gone grey at thirty-five, who had been a clerk in the usurper's own customs house and had copied warrants against speakers for eleven years before he fled with a stolen seal and shaking hands. No one spoke to him for two days. Then Petra sat beside him and asked him, gently, in the old tongue, whether he was hungry, and he put his head down on the table and cried into his arms until the candles guttered.

— A boy called Finch, maybe fourteen, who claimed to be a full speaker and was not, and who had walked ninety miles on the claim, and whom Wren caught out within an hour — kindly, privately, teaching him instead of exposing him — and who stayed anyway, sweeping floors, guarding doors, hungry for anything that smelled like belonging.

Eleven. With Wren, twelve. Nan counted them each night like a woman counting sheep that might scatter.

* * *

It was Ferro who saw it first, because Ferro saw everything as geography.

He had spread his maps across the floor of the map-room — the real ones now, the life's work, the marginalia-and-song reconstruction of a country that existed nowhere but paper — and he had laid the Remnant's fragments alongside them like pieces of a shattered plate held up to the pattern.

"Say it again," he said to Ansel. "What your mother gave you."

Ansel glanced at Odile, got her nod, and spoke: four words, whispered, the way whispers get passed down — worn at the edges, slightly wrong, a word missing from the middle that no one alive remembered putting there.

Ferro turned to Petra. "Yours."

Petra sang her three words, the ones she sang at births.

Ferro turned to Toma. "The deathbed oath. All of it. Word for word, as she said it."

Toma recited it like a man reciting a grievance, which was close enough to the truth.

Ferro stood very still over his maps. Then he began to move the fragments — writing them on slips of paper, laying them along the inked rivers and roads of dead Aldermere, and Dominic watched his hands shake as the shape emerged.

"It's not a sentence yet," Ferro said. "But look." He laid the slips end to end, gaps yawning between them like missing teeth. "Look at the *shape*. Each fragment sits in a different place. Your mother didn't give you half a word, twins — she gave you a *quarter* of a sentence, and gave the other quarters to other families, or split it across siblings, or buried it in a lullaby." He looked up at Nan. "The songs. The songs are full of these."

Nan had gone very quiet. "My mother sang me verses I never understood. Thought they were nonsense syllables to help a child sleep."

"They're not nonsense." Wren had been standing at the edge of the lamplight, and now she stepped forward, and when she spoke the old language everyone in the room went still the way rooms go still around a struck match. "They're grammar wearing a melody. That's why the door answers songs. That's why—" She stopped herself, and Dominic saw her decide to say it anyway. "That's why the soldiers burned the villages instead of taking prisoners. You can't torture a song apart. You have to kill the singers."

Nobody moved for a moment. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the siege guns spoke their slow dull syllables, and dust sifted from the ceiling beams like patient snow.

"The opening sentence," Dominic said at last. Her voice sounded strange to her, scraped thin. "The one the door demands. It's scattered across a dozen families like — like a will divided among heirs."

"Like a key cut into pieces," Sable corrected softly, "and hidden in people. Because paper burns, and stone can be broken, but a grandmother makes her granddaughter memorize a melody, and who searches a lullaby?"

* * *

The trouble was not the sentence. The trouble was each other.

It showed itself on the fifth night, over soup. Gaspard the clerk ladled bowls for everyone, working-man efficient, and Toma Vetch knocked his bowl out of Gaspard's hands and onto the floor.

"You wrote the lists," Toma said. His voice was gravel and old grief. "Eleven years. My cousin's name was on those lists. My cousin's *children*."

"I copied names." Gaspard stood very straight, grey and brittle. "I did not choose them."

"No. You just made the choosing possible. There's a word for men like you in the old language. My father taught it to me. Want to learn it?"

"Toma." Dominic's voice came flat and hard across the cellar, swordspeaker's voice, the one she hated using because of what it cost her to have it. "Sit down."

Toma sat. But the room stayed broken along the crack he'd opened, and Dominic saw it plainly: eleven people who had survived by hiding, now asked to stand together in a room with a lamp lit, each one certain — with the certainty of the hunted — that someone else's fear would be the death of them all.

That night she found Nan Hollis shelling beans by candlelight, and sat down across from her.

"My mother used to say," Nan offered, without looking up, "that Aldermere didn't fall to armies. Armies just finished it. It fell to neighbors deciding they couldn't afford to trust each other anymore. Every family sealed its own little door." She flicked a beanpod open. "Then the big door was the only one left, and we sealed that too, and then there was nothing left of us but what fit inside our own skulls." She looked up, eyes bright and fierce in the candle-glow. "You want to assemble a sentence out of twelve frightened people, girl, you'll need more than grammar. You'll need them to hand each other the pieces and *turn their backs while holding them*."

"How do you build that?"

Nan smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "You don't build it. You spend it. Trust is coin, Dominic. Somebody has to pay first, and it's always the one who can least afford it."

Dominic thought about the page she had burned. About Quillon's note, folded now inside her shirt like a second heartbeat. *Some things were never for sale.*

"I know," she said.

* * *

She paid first, the next evening, in front of all of them.

She brought out the forbidden ledger — her family's ledger, the folio with the Door of Aldermere recorded as *paid in full, sealed by oath*, with the sealing signatory written in faded iron-gall ink: **Yarrow**. Her name. Her blood. She laid it on the table where Gaspard sat, and let them read it.

"My family sealed the door," she said. "Whatever your grandmothers hid from, whatever villages burned, whatever hunger drove your people out — the lock on the far side of it was signed by a Yarrow. By my grandmother's hand, and her oath, and her gift. I am the last of that line." She turned the pages so the lamplight caught the signature. "If any of you wants to leave tonight rather than trust a jailer's heir, the cistern gate is open, and I will not follow, and I will not blame you."

Silence. The siege guns punctuated it, distant and obscene.

It was Toma Vetch who broke it. He rose from his bench, crossed the room, and stood over the ledger a long time, reading his grandmother's grief in a stranger's handwriting. Then he reached into his shirt and drew out a small oilcloth packet, unwrapped it, and set a scrap of parchment on the table beside the signature — a fragment of song-verse, copied out in a careful dead hand, carried thirty years against his skin.

"Half of a verse," he said gruffly. "The other half my cousin had, and my cousin is ash. So it's worthless unless—" he gestured around the room, at the twins, at Petra, at Gaspard, at all of them, jaw working, "—unless it isn't."

Wren picked up the scrap with both hands, the way you hold a bird. And somewhere in the room, softly, Petra began to sing.

* * *

They worked for six nights, and it was the strangest labor any of them had ever done: a dozen fugitives in a cellar beneath a burning city, stitching a country back together out of scraps.

Wren was the loom. She could hear a fragment and know instantly where it sat in the architecture of the sentence — *no, that clause bends upward, it belongs near the beginning, it's a naming-clause* — and the others learned to bring her their pieces without shame, one by one, each surrender its own small ceremony. Ansel and Odile spoke their quarter together, hands linked, and Wren listened with her eyes closed and then opened them shining: "Your mother didn't split it between you to keep you ignorant. She split it so that the sentence would be *impossible to speak alone*. So no soldier could ever make one of you open anything." Odile cried. Ansel held her hand tighter.

Gaspard contributed the thing no one expected: not words but structure. Eleven years in the usurper's archives had taught him how the enemy catalogued, and cross-referencing his memory of seized documents against Ferro's maps, he identified three fragments already in imperial hands — transcribed, filed, mislabeled as decorative verse. Which meant the usurper's scholars were assembling the same sentence, slower, blind to the music, but assembling it.

"We're in a race," Ferro said grimly, pinning a red thread across his map. "They have archives. We have grandmothers. Pray grandmothers win."

And through it all Nan Hollis sang, and taught Wren the verses she'd slept inside for eighty years, and grew visibly, gently smaller, as though the songs were leaving her the way water leaves a cup poured out — and she seemed, Dominic thought, entirely unafraid of being emptied.

On the sixth night, they had it. Not complete — a gap remained, a clause none of them carried, a silence shaped exactly like a word — but assembled enough to see its whole terrible beauty: a sentence that named the door, greeted it, and asked it to remember. Spoken entire, by voices true, it would open what fifteen years of oath had shut.

They stood around the table looking at the slips of paper laid end to end, and nobody cheered. It felt less like victory than like standing at the edge of something very deep.

"There's a piece missing," Wren said quietly. "One voice."

"Then we find the one who carries it," Dominic said.

As if summoned, boots sounded on the cellar stairs — Ilva, coming down fast, still in her sergeant's coat, snow in her hair, her face doing something complicated.

"News?" Dominic asked.

"Two kinds." Ilva braced herself against the doorframe. "Bad news is, Corvane's envoys posted a proclamation at every gate this morning. Pardon and restoration, offered by name, to one Dominic Thackeray, last swordspeaker — in exchange for the delivery of 'the child who speaks whole.'" Her eyes flicked to Wren, then away. "Good news is harder to say."

"Say it anyway."

Ilva hesitated. "A bread-seller I trust says Corvane requested the meeting himself. Not the usurper. Corvane. And Dominic — the man who delivered the request—" she exhaled, "—he had an Alderman accent. Under the free-city polish. Old, and careful, but I've been listening for that accent my whole life." She met Dominic's eyes. "Whoever they sent to buy you... he's one of us. Or he was, once."

The candle flames leaned in the draft from the stairs, all together, like a dozen small heads turning.

Above them the guns went on speaking, and below them, on the table, the scattered pieces of a country lay waiting for their final voice.
