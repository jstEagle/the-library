## 20. What Dominic Forsook

The dream always began with the well.

Not a nightmare, exactly—Dominic had long ago stopped flinching from it—but a return, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. She stood in the yard behind her mother's house in the Weepers' Quarter, seventeen years old and certain of everything, and the well stood where it had always stood, its stones slick with moss, its rope frayed to a whisper of itself.

In the waking world she knew what was coming. In the dream she never did.

*Dom. Dom, come look.*

Her cousin Tam's voice. Tam, who was fourteen that summer and all elbows and borrowed bravado, who dared her to climb the guild-house wall and dared her to steal plums from the harbor-master's cart and now, on this last ordinary morning of her old life, dared her to lean over the well and shout down into the dark to hear her echo come back wrong.

She could still feel the rope in her hands. Still smell the green rot of the water below, and above it the sweeter rot of the plums Tam had stolen from the cart, his pockets bulging, juice running down his wrist as he laughed at her.

*Say something loud enough to wake the dead, Dom.*

And she, being a swordspeaker's daughter and proud of it, being fifteen years from understanding what that pride would cost, leaned out over the dark and spoke.

The word is gone now. That is the mercy and the horror of it both. Her mother saw to the mercy, and the horror is that Dominic has had to build a life around an absence, like a woman walking on a leg that isn't there. But she remembers the shape of the moment: how the word left her throat not as sound but as *weight*, how the well-water went still beneath her—not rippled, *still*, still as glass, still as held breath—and how the silence afterward was not silence at all but a listening.

Then Tam's hand slipped on the wet stones. Or the stones shifted. Or the word reached up through the dark and took him; she has never known which, and never knowing is its own wound. He fell the length of the rope, past the frayed end, into water too shallow for the depth of the fall, and when they pulled him out he was not drowned and not broken in any way the bone-setters could name. He was simply *empty*, the way a house is empty after the family has gone, and by nightfall he was gone too, and the whole Weepers' Quarter said what quarters always say: *a terrible accident, poor girl, poor mother, no one's fault.*

Everyone said no one's fault. Everyone but Dominic.

Because she had felt the word leave her. Because she had heard the water go still. Because some part of her, deep in the marrow where the gift lived, had *known*, even before Tam's fingers slipped, that the dark below had woken—and that it had woken because she called it.

* * *

She woke in the safehouse above Ferro's workshop with Wren asleep in the next room and the siege-quiet pressing against the shutters, and for a long time she lay still and let the old grief sit on her chest, familiar as a cat that comes every night to the same spot.

Fifteen years since she had spoken a word of power. Fifteen years since her mother had knelt before her in the ashes of Tam's funeral fire and taken her daughter's face in both hands and said, *I can take this from you, or it will take you. Choose.*

She had chosen. Of course she had chosen. What else does a child choose, offered a knife or the sheath?

But it was only now, lying in the grey hour before dawn with Wren's breathing soft through the wall, that Dominic understood there had been a second choice made that day, and it had not been hers.

Her mother had not only taken the gift. Her mother had taken the *memory*—the true memory, unsoftened, unedited—the memory of exactly what Dominic had said and exactly what it had done. And her mother had done what swordspeakers' mothers did in the last desperate generation, what the scattered Aldermen had learned to do when hiding meant survival: she had carried the memory to the guild of impossible things, and she had sold it. Sealed it in a lead-lined folio in the vaults beneath the guildhouse, filed among the other unspeakable trades, purchasable by no one, returnable to no one, because the seller had paid with the one thing she had left.

Her own recollection of her daughter's face when the word left her mouth.

Dominic sat up in the dark. Her hands were shaking, and she watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to the girl at the well.

Sable. It had been Sable's guild even then—or Sable's mistress's, before Sable bought her freedom with a memory Dominic was only beginning to suspect was connected to her own. The thief had told them about the vaults. About the folios. About the price of taking things back.

Somewhere under the ruined guildhouse, in a room full of other people's surrendered yesterdays, there was a box with her mother's handwriting on it, and inside the box was the answer to the question Dominic had carried for half her life: *what did I say?*

She found she was not sure she wanted to know. She found, also, that it no longer mattered whether she wanted to.

* * *

Three days later, waiting for Corvane's promised answer—for envoys did not knock twice, they *loomed*—she went to Nan Hollis.

The old woman sat by the window of the safehouse mending a child's coat three sizes too big for any child they had, her needle rising and falling like a slow bird. She did not look up when Dominic sat across from her. Nan never looked up anymore unless she chose to; her eyes were going the way of her knees, and she spent her sight the way a miser spends coin.

"You've got your thinking face," Nan said. "The one where you look like you're doing sums about yourself."

"Nan. You knew my mother."

"I knew a lot of people." The needle paused. "That's not a question."

"She came to the guild once. Before I was born, maybe. Or—" Dominic stopped. Started again. "Did she ever talk to you about sealing memories? About what the vaults do?"

The needle resumed, slower. Outside, the bombardment had lapsed into its daytime hush, the usurper's gunners resting their barrels the way oxen rest between furrows, and in that hush Nan's silence was very large.

"Your mother," Nan said at last, "sat where you're sitting, more or less, twenty years ago, and asked me the same thing you're asking. Whether a thing given up could be lived without." She set down the coat. Folded her hands on it. "I told her what I'll tell you. A sealed memory doesn't die, girl. It waits. It grows roots in the dark, same as anything buried. And one day the ground opens—because ground always opens, that's the one promise the earth keeps—and what comes up isn't what went in. It's what went in *plus* all the years of waiting."

"Then why seal anything?"

"Because the waiting is the cure." Nan's clouded eyes found her, or found near her. "You think your mother sealed that memory to punish you? To hide from you?" A dry sound, almost a laugh. "She sealed it because a swordspeaker who remembers killing carries the word in her mouth forever after. It sits on the tongue. It *wants*. Every fight, every fear, every moment of rage, it whispers: say me, say me, and be finished with this. Your mother put it underground so it couldn't whisper. So you could grow up around it without leaning toward it."

Dominic looked at her hands again. They had stilled.

"And if I open it now," she said quietly. "If we go into the vaults—if Sable finds it—"

"Then you'd best already know who you are," Nan said. "Or the thing in the box will tell you instead." She picked up the needle again. "Which is why you came to an old woman instead of to Sable. You wanted to know if you're ready." A pause, the thread pulled tight. "Are you?"

Dominic thought of the well. Thought of the stillness of the water. Thought of Wren, asleep down the hall, whose language woke doors and whose existence woke hunters, and thought of the sentence they were assembling piece by piece, the final clause waiting like an empty chair.

"No," she said honestly.

"Good," said Nan. "'Ready' is what people say right before they do a stupid thing. 'No' means you'll think first." She bent back to her mending. "Now get out. This coat won't sew itself, and neither will your answer."

* * *

That evening she found Sable on the roof, as she had known she would. The thief liked heights and disliked company in equal measure, which made roofs her natural parish, and tonight she sat on the ridge tiles with her knees drawn up, watching the usurper's campfires ring the city like a necklace of orange beads.

Dominic climbed up beside her. Sable didn't startle; she had heard her coming from the third tile, probably, maybe from the street.

"You talked to Nan," Sable said. "You have the face."

"Everyone keeps telling me I have faces."

"It's a family trait. Guilds breed for it." Sable turned her head. Firelight moved in her eyes. "Ask me, then."

Dominic looked out at the fires instead. It was easier than looking at Sable, who had a way of receiving questions the way a blade receives a whetstone.

"My mother sold a memory to your guild. Twenty years ago. A woman named Yarrow—she'd have used the name Yarrow, or maybe nothing at all, she was careful—it would have been sealed special. Lead folio. No buyer listed."

"I know the entry."

Dominic turned. Sable's face was unreadable, which from Sable meant it was very readable indeed.

"You *know* it."

"I catalogued the vaults when I bought my freedom. All of them. It's how I learned what the guild really was—a bank for cowardice, Quillon called it, fondly, the way you'd call a shark a good swimmer." Sable hugged her knees tighter. "Yarrow, D. Sealed by maternal authority. One memory. Contents withheld even from the ledger—that costs extra, and she paid extra. There are four entries like that in the whole archive. Yours is the only one with a child's name attached."

"What did it cost her?"

"That's not written."

"Sable."

"*What did it cost her,*" Sable repeated, gently this time, "is not written. That's the point of withholding. If I tell you she gave up her wedding day, or her mother's voice, or the smell of bread—you'll build a shrine to it. If I tell you nothing, you'll build nothing, and maybe you'll ask her yourself someday, if your dead talk to you the way mine do." She looked away, back at the fires. "Here's what I can tell you, since you're going to make me. The folio is small. Small folios are the worst ones—one memory, whole and entire, none of the shattering that happens when you cut pieces. Someone wanted you to be able to have it back someday *whole*. That takes craft. That takes love, frankly, and I don't say that word about paperwork often."

The wind off the siege lines carried woodsmoke and, faintly, singing—some soldier's song in the usurper's tongue, tuneless and vast, a thousand men forgetting they were men together.

"If I open it," Dominic said, "I might learn I killed my cousin deliberately. That I *chose* the word. That some part of me wanted—"

"You didn't."

"You don't know what's in it."

"I know what's in *you*," Sable said. "I steal memories for a living, Dom. I've stood inside better liars than you and watched them lie to themselves, and you don't. It's your single most inconvenient quality. Whatever word you spoke, you spoke it the way a child speaks—without knowing what speech can do. The guilt you carry is real. The guilt you're *afraid of finding* is invented. Fear invents. That's its job." She unfolded, finally, and put one cold hand over Dominic's fist. "But I'll tell you the actual danger, since nobody else will. Taking it back doesn't just give you the memory. It gives you the *word*. Whole. On your tongue. Ready for the door, or ready for anything else that asks. Your mother sealed it because she was afraid of exactly this moment—of the day when opening the door and speaking that word would look like the same act."

Below them, in the street, a lantern bobbed. A bread-seller making her rounds at an hour no bread-seller kept, pausing beneath the safehouse window, leaving something wrapped in cloth on the sill, moving on. Ilva's channel. Dominic's chest tightened; news from Ilva was either salvation or a countdown, and lately it had been mostly countdown.

"Corvane will come tomorrow," she said. "Or the day after. With an offer dressed as a pardon. And whatever's in that box, I'm going to need to be standing in a doorway soon, holding it open, with that word in my mouth and every hunter in the city aiming at the light." She exhaled. "So yes. I want it back. Not to know what I did. To know what I'm *capable of*—and to decide, awake and armed, whether I use it."

Sable studied her for a long moment. Then she smiled the thin, sharp smile she saved for jobs worth dying over.

"Vaults are rubble," she said. "But rubble keeps. Give me two nights."

* * *

Two nights became one, because the city ran out of patience.

They came at dusk—the bread-seller's warning, breathless, half a step ahead of the drums: *the envoy crosses the square at moonrise, and he's brought the usurper's own half-speakers to translate whatever you're hiding.* By lamplight the safehouse filled with the whole strange congregation of it: Wren clutching Nan's finished coat, too big and warm; Ferro rolling his maps with hands that wouldn't quite steady; the half-speakers pressed shoulder to shoulder, each carrying their shard of a dead country; and Sable, pulling on her dark coat, checking the lockpicks in her sleeve with the calm of a woman setting out for market.

At the door, Nan caught Dominic's arm. Her grip was still strong. Everything else about her was going soft as ash, but the grip held.

"When you stand in front of that box," the old woman said, "whatever's in it—remember it waited twenty years to be forgiven. Don't make it wait longer than it has to. Least of all by you."

Down in the square, torches bloomed around Envoy Corvane like a wound opening, and his voice rolled up through the streets, smooth as oil on water, offering the free cities a bargain in the name of a king who had once been a boy who ran.

Dominic drew her sword out of habit, then left it sheathed, and followed Sable down into the dark toward the vaults—toward the box with her name on it, and the word sleeping inside, and the answer that had been waiting, patient as ground, all this time.
