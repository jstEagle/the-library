## 21. The Usurper's Envoy

The bread-seller's stall smelled of cardamom and smoke, and Envoy Corvane had been standing in front of it for an hour without buying anything, which was the first thing the fishwives noticed and the last thing he wanted them to notice.

He had dressed down for the occasion—grey wool, no sigil, boots scuffed at the toe by his own hand with a whetstone the night before. A man could buy a scuffed boot in any city in the world; a polished one announced itself. He had learned that lesson in Aldermere, before there were no more boots in Aldermere at all.

The message he had sent ahead was simple, as good messages are: *To the swordspeaker Dominic Thackeray, formerly of the Guild of Impossible Things, from a friend of her family's family. A parley, unarmed, at the old customhouse on Tanners' Walk, at the turn of the tide. Come alone or don't come. Either answer is information.*

It was not strictly true that he was unarmed. Corvane had been carrying the same knife for thirty-one years, and it had never once drawn blood, but he carried it the way other men carried their mothers' rings—as proof that something of the old country had survived. It was a thin blade of grey Alderman steel with a hilt wrapped in eel-leather, and its maker had been a woman named Thackeray.

That was the point of the parley. That was the whole point of everything.

* * *

He heard her before he saw her—or rather, he heard the absence of her. Tanners' Walk emptied the way water drains from a lock when a door opens somewhere below. The market noise didn't stop so much as bend around a single set of footsteps, and then she was there in the doorway of the customhouse, backlit by the grey afternoon, and Corvane understood why they still told stories about swordspeakers even though there were none left.

Dominic Thackeray looked like a woman who had been awake for three days and had decided, calmly, not to mind. She wore a sword. That surprised him—he had expected the guild to have sanded her down into pure ledger by now—but the way she wore it told him more: low and loose, a working tool, not a decoration. Her eyes went to his hands first, then the windows, then the rafters, and only then, briefly and dismissively, to his face.

"Thackeray," he said, in Alderman.

She did not flinch. He had counted on a flinch. Fifteen years buried, and the language should have hit her like cold water. Instead she answered in Common, flat as slate. "You're the envoy. Corvane."

"Corvane," he agreed, "in these clothes. In another life I was called something else." He gestured to the room behind him—a customs hall gone to rot, dust sheets over furniture like a house holding its breath, one table cleared, two chairs. "I took the liberty."

"You took a lot of liberties. Your soldiers burned a village full of children because of what they said."

"My lord's soldiers," Corvane said. "I burn nothing. I am the opposite of burning. I am the thing that comes before the burning, offering terms, so that afterward everyone can say the offer was made." He smiled, and let the smile be tired rather than sharp. Honesty, oddly deployed, disarmed better than any blade. "You know how armies work, swordspeaker. I'm the part of them that hates them."

"Then you're here to make an offer."

"I'm here to make an apology, an inheritance, and an offer—in that order, if you'll allow me."

She didn't sit. She stood with her back to the wall where she could see both doors, which told him she'd trained under someone from the old school, someone who'd known the wars. He sat anyway, alone at the cleared table, deliberately making himself the smaller thing in the room.

"The apology," he said. "My lord's warrants name 'linguists and their harborers.' I wrote those words. I chose them because they were narrow enough to be denied and broad enough to be used, and I told myself that narrowness was mercy. It wasn't. It was just a smaller cage. For that, I'm sorry—not to you, you can look after yourself, but to a girl named Wren, whom I have never met, whose name I put on a list."

Dominic's jaw moved once. "And the inheritance?"

Corvane reached inside his coat—slowly, telegraphing every inch, watching her weight shift onto her back foot—and drew out not the knife but a folded square of oilcloth, older than either of them. He unwrapped it on the table between them.

Inside lay a child's practice sword. Wooden, edge-battered, the grip worn to the shape of two small hands.

"Do you know it?" he asked.

"No."

"That's honest. You wouldn't. But your mother would have." He turned it so the pommel faced her. Burned into the wood, small as a freckle, was the sigil—the interlocked thorn-and-key that Dominic had seen again after fifteen years on an iron key clutched by a salt-soaked child. "This belonged to a boy in the last class your mother ever taught, in the last year of Aldermere. She taught us the forms on wooden swords because steel was already being requisitioned. There were eleven of us. I was the youngest. And when the order came down—when the first fires started—this boy ran. He got out through the marsh gate while his classmates held it." Corvane laid his own hand flat on the table, beside the little sword. "The boy was my brother. My mother called him clever. Everyone else called him something less kind, in the years after, and he stopped using our family name entirely, and eventually he stopped using any name except the one he gave himself out of a conquered province's tongue, which is a very Alderman joke if you think about it long enough."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, the tide was turning; he could hear the harbour bell counting it.

"Your lord," Dominic said quietly, "is Alderman."

"Aldous. Yes. Though he'd be furious to hear you use the old name for him—he prefers the new titles, the usurper business keeps him fed." Corvane leaned back. "He is the boy who fled first. He has spent thirty years building an army out of the shame of that, and now he wants to go home, and he cannot go home, because home is behind a door that only opens for people who speak the whole sentence, and the sentence was scattered on purpose, broken among families, hidden in lullabies, so that no one man could ever open it alone."

"And now there's a child who speaks fluently."

"There is now a child who speaks fluently," Corvane agreed. "And a dozen half-speakers crawling toward her like iron filings to a lodestone, and a disgraced mapmaker whose obsession has finally become useful, and an old woman singing songs that are maps. Yes, swordspeaker, we do read reports too. My lord built his intelligence service out of exiles. We are very good at recognizing each other."

* * *

He watched her take that in. He watched the particular way she took it in—not the soldier's assessment, though that was there underneath, but the ledger-keeper's: weighing entries, looking for the false column.

"You want the door opened," she said at last.

"I want what my lord wants, which is not quite the same thing, and the difference matters, so let me be precise." Corvane folded his hands. "Aldous wants the door opened and an army marched through it. He wants to reclaim Aldermere as territory—crown himself, raise the thorn-banner over whatever's left, call it restoration. He has spent thirty years telling himself the story where he returns in glory instead of running away, and the story has calcified into policy." His voice dropped. "What I want is simpler and worse to say out loud. I want to see it. The hills. The eel-weirs. The rowan trees along the marsh road. I am fifty-three years old and I have not seen a single thing that grew in the country that made me, and some nights that fact sits on my chest like a wet cloak, and I would trade a great deal—one pardon, one career, one conscience—to stand on Alderman soil once before I die."

"And if what's on the other side isn't hills anymore? If the country itself changed? If—" She stopped herself. He noted the stop, filed it. *They've found something about the door. Something that frightened them.*

"If," he allowed. "Which is why I am here talking instead of besieging. My lord believes knowledge is weakness and hesitation is treason, and so he sends envoys only to betray them, and I am aware that I am likely betraying myself right now simply by being reasonable where he can hear echoes." He tapped the table once. "But hear the rest, because this is the offer, and I'd rather deliver it cleanly than cleverly."

"Go on."

"Aldous knows the Thackeray name. Not from me—from the old rolls, from the sealing of the door. Your family signed. Your blood is written into the threshold, whether you like it or not, and the door remembers signatories the way a wound remembers the knife. If anyone alive can stand in that doorway and hold it open—or hold it shut—it is you. So." He slid a document across the table. Heavy paper, heavy seal, red wax stamped with the crossed spears of the new regime. "Full pardon. All warrants withdrawn—for you, for the mapmaker Ferro, for the thief Sable, for the watchwoman Ilva whose falsified reports I have personally kept out of three separate inquisitions, because I liked her handwriting. Restoration of rank in the rebuilt guild, with a charter under my lord's seal, funding, protection. The free cities keep their walls and their markets and their grubby independence. No searches. No more villages burned. And in exchange—"

"Wren."

"Wren comes to the palace. Ungagged, unharmed, tutored, honored. She becomes what she already is—the last fluent speaker—under a roof that will keep her alive, because I promise you, swordspeaker, the moment my lord stops needing her alive, half his generals will discover reasons why a dead witness is safer than a live one. With me she eats well and learns her own language properly, from adults who remember it. Without—" he spread his hands, taking in the rotting customhouse, the city under edict, the army assembling on the salt road like a tide that had learned patience, "—without, she is a nine-year-old girl hiding in stockrooms while ten thousand men search house by house, and every day the search gets closer, and every day my lord gets less reasonable, and eventually someone less sentimental than me finds her."

He let the document lie there between them, red seal up, like something freshly killed.

"That's the offer. Pardon and restoration for the surrender of a child. And I'll tell you the truth about it, since no one else in this war will: it is a good offer, and it is a trap, and both of those things are true at once. The trap is not for you. The trap is for the sentence. Aldous doesn't need Wren's goodwill. He needs her voice. Half-speakers can be broken into giving up fragments—they always break, fear finds every crack in a scattered language. Give him enough fragments and a fluent throat to assemble them, and he can speak the opening clause without consent, without oath, without anyone standing in the doorway at all. He doesn't want a key. He wants a crowbar." Corvane met her eyes. "And he thinks, because I share his blood, that I will feel nothing about handing him a child. He is wrong, but he is my lord, and I serve him anyway, because the alternative to serving monsters is usually being eaten by them, and I am a coward in the old style—I run early and live long."

* * *

For a long time Dominic said nothing. She picked up the little wooden sword, turned it over, ran her thumb across the burned sigil as if reading braille.

"Who taught you the forms?" she asked suddenly. "On the wooden swords."

"Your mother." He watched the name land—her mother, the signatory, the woman whose signature he knew was eating at her since the forbidden folios—and saw her knuckles whiten around the practice blade. "She was patient. She had a way of correcting your grip without touching you, just words, quiet, until your hand fixed itself out of shame." He almost smiled. "She told us once that a word spoken rightly is stronger than any blade, and a word spoken wrongly is worse than any blade, and that most people spend their whole lives doing neither, and that this was the tragedy and the mercy of the world. I was seven. I thought she meant it about swords. I have spent forty-six years finding out what she actually meant."

"Why are you telling me all this?"

"Because temptation works best wearing a familiar face, and I am the most familiar face you're going to get from that army, and I would rather you refuse me knowing everything than refuse me on instinct and spend the rest of the siege wondering." He rose, slowly, showing empty hands. "Also because I need you to understand what happens next, so you can prepare, so fewer people die, including possibly me."

"Which is?"

Corvane walked to the window. Below, the street had refilled; the tide had turned; the ordinary commerce of a frightened city resumed its noise. Far off, past the rooftops, past the walls, the salt road ran white toward the horizon, and on it, invisible at this distance but perfectly visible to his mind's eye, the vanguard of the usurper's host was striking camp in ordered ranks.

"Three days," he said. "In three days my lord loses patience with parley. He has begun assembling his own half-speakers—exiles like me, found in the conquered provinces, each carrying their fragment. They're translating the song-maps. They have maybe half the sentence. When they finish, he won't need the child, and he won't need the doorway held, because he intends to force it—there are ways, old ways, sealed ways, and the things your group found in Quillon's last ledger page, yes, we know about that page, grief makes men talk—those ways exist, and they are worse for everyone, including him, and he no longer cares." He turned back. "So here is my last honesty, freely given, off the record, costing me my neck: whatever you're going to do at that door—do it in three days. Open it, seal it, sing to it, marry it, I don't care. Do it before he does it badly."

Dominic set the wooden sword down on the table between them, gently, the way one sets down something that belongs to the dead.

"And you?" she asked. "When the door opens—or when it's forced—whose side does the envoy stand on?"

Corvane considered the question honestly, which was more than he had done for anyone in thirty years, and found the answer waiting where he'd left it, worn smooth as river stone.

"With whoever lets me smell the rain on Alderman hills," he said. "Even if it's only once. Even if it kills me. Especially then."

He bowed—an Alderman bow, deep, old-fashioned, the kind that hadn't been seen in the free cities in a generation—and left the little wooden sword on the table as payment for her time. At the door he paused.

"For what it's worth, swordspeaker: my lord sent me with two documents. The pardon you've seen. The second authorizes me, upon refusal, to give the signal." He tapped the doorframe twice, softly, like a knock. "I haven't given it yet. I'm walking out the long way. You have exactly as long as that walk takes."

Then he was gone into the market noise, and Dominic Thackeray stood alone in the rotten customhouse with a dead child's sword, a traitor's pardon, and perhaps four hundred heartbeats in which to decide the fate of two countries—while somewhere beneath the city, in the dark under the Founders' Stones, the Door of Aldermere waited, cold as a held breath, for the sentence to be spoken.
