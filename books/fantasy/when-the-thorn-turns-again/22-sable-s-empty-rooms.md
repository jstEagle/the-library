## 22. Sable's Empty Rooms

Sable had a rule about her own memories: she never counted them.

Counting was how you learned what was missing. She kept the inventory of other people's pasts with perfect precision—every stolen hour catalogued in wax and vellum down in the vaults, every recollection labeled by year, by grief, by market value. But her own head she left uncounted on purpose. It was easier to live inside a house if you never drew its floor plan.

Tonight, sitting cross-legged on Ferro's floor with a candle guttering between them and Nan Hollis snoring gently in the corner chair, she finally drew the floor plan. And found a room missing.

"It's not there," she said.

Ferro looked up from his maps. His hands were stained three shades of ink, and there was a smudge of it high on his cheekbone where he'd pushed back his hair. "What's not there?"

"The door." She turned the memory over in her mind the way she'd turn a coin suspected of being clipped. "I told you I'd seen one. A door between countries. When I was apprenticed, before they struck me off the rolls. I stood in front of it. I remember standing in front of it."

"You remember *standing*," Nan said, without opening her eyes. Her voice came out dry as old paper. "That's not the same as remembering the thing itself, girl. Any thief worth her salt knows that."

Sable did know that. That was what made her stomach go cold. A memory didn't erode from the middle out. You lost the edges first—the color, the smell, the name of the street—and the center held like a stone in a river. What she had was a smooth oval absence shaped exactly like the center of something important. She could feel the shape of the hole when she pressed her mind against it: a doorway, cold air, a voice reading terms aloud.

She had sold it. Of course she had sold it. That was the whole ugly arithmetic of her life. At nineteen, caught red-handed in the guild's own counting room with eleven years of debt against her name, she'd been given the choice every apprentice thief was given: the cells below the harbor, or the counter. Pay your debts in the only currency you carried.

She'd paid. She'd sold the guild the one thing no fence would take off her hands because no one else wanted it—the night she'd been taken into the undercity as part of an appraisal party, the night she'd seen the sealed threshold beneath the Founders' Stones, the night she'd heard the guildmaster of that era read out the price of Aldermere.

The price. The *terms*. Whatever had been traded, whatever had been promised, whatever kept the door shut—that was the room missing from her house.

"I have to steal myself back," she said.

Ferro set down his pen. Nan opened one eye.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its vaults the way a spider keeps a web: everything in reach, nothing visible, and the builder always at the center. Except Quillon was dead now—killed for his ledgers, made an example of in the guildhall square—and the web still held, because webs don't need their spiders alive to be sticky.

Sable walked the salt road to the guildhouse at dusk, wearing the face of a woman named Marta Voss who collected rents for a shipping concern and whose memory she'd bought fair and square from a man drowning in dice debts. Marta had the kind of face that made clerks want to be somewhere else. It was useful.

The guildhall gates stood open. That was wrong. In fifteen years of trading around that building, she had never once seen those gates open after dark. Two of the usurper's soldiers flanked the entrance, bored, their warrant-ribbons fluttering in the sea wind, and beyond them the entry hall smelled of ash and wet plaster where something had burned.

"Business?" one of the soldiers asked.

"Inventories," Marta Voss said, in Marta Voss's flat voice. "My principals hold paper against the guild. With the master dead, we want our assets counted before someone else counts them for us."

The soldier waved her through with the weary contempt of a man who had been posted to guard a corpse and knew it. Inside, the great hall was stripped bare. Quillon's counting tables were gone; the wall of pigeonholes where trade-slates hung had been pulled apart, slates scattered across the floor like a flock of dead birds. Someone had tried to burn the forbidden folios and done it badly—a black scar on the flagstones, half-burned pages shovelled into an iron bin, the rest simply gone. Looted, probably. Or hidden by clerks with more loyalty to the archive than to the crown.

Good. Let the usurper's men hunt a language in a building full of empty shelves.

The stairs down to the vaults were guarded by no one, which meant they were guarded by something better. Sable paused at the top step and let her gift open, just a crack, the way you test wind with a wetted finger.

Every thief in the free cities had some small uncanny knack—it was practically a hiring requirement—but Sable's was the reason the guild had taken her in the first place, and the reason they'd later thrown her out with instructions never to come back. She could walk into a room and feel the memories in it. Not read them—reading required contact, skin to skin or mind to mind, and cost her what it cost—but *feel* them, the way you can feel heat through a door. A room where someone had died hummed low and sour. A room where children had grown up felt warm at the edges. And a room full of stored memories, bottled and shelved and waiting for buyers—

The stairwell below her felt like standing outside a hive.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every stolen afternoon and buried grief the guild had ever warehoused, humming behind stone at a pitch only she could hear. It should have been comforting. It had been, once, when she was young and thought of the vaults as a library of second chances. Now it sounded like a choir singing in a language made entirely of things people wished they'd never said.

There was a new note in the chord, though. Something sharp. Recent. She followed it down.

* * *

The vaults ran three levels beneath the guildhall, older than the guild itself, older than the free cities' walls—the Founders' Stones above ground were foundations here, vast blocks worn smooth, and between them the guild had racked its inventory like a chandler racks candles. Level one was common stock: petty secrets, embarrassing letters, the memories of minor scandals, all priced to move. Level two was serious goods. Level three—

Level three was hers.

Not legally. Nothing down here was legally anything anymore; the guild was a carcass and the crows hadn't finished arguing over it. But the deep racks on level three, the ones carved into the living stone behind a door that took two keys and a spoken word, held the guild's founding inventory. The oldest trades. The impossible ones.

And somewhere in those racks, filed and sealed and gathering dust for twenty-two years, was a small grey vial with her name scratched into the wax.

She stood before the level-three door and pressed her palm flat against it. The lock wanted a key she no longer had and a word she'd sworn never to say again. But locks were just memories of their makers, hammered into metal, and Sable had spent her whole life learning what rooms remembered.

This door remembered being built by a man named Corvin Hale, who had been proud of the mechanism and insecure about his hands. It remembered the word spoken over it at its making—not as sound but as intention, the way a nail remembers the hammer. And it remembered, with a small mechanical vanity, that it had been designed to fail gracefully: break the wrong tumbler and the bolt withdrew rather than jammed, so that no thief would ever be trapped inside with the inventory.

Guildsmen thought of everything except thieves thinking back.

It took her forty minutes and both her knives, working by touch in the dark with her ear against the mechanism like a physician listening to a heart. When the last tumbler surrendered, the bolt slid home into the doorframe with a click that sounded, in the hive-hum of the vaults, almost like approval.

Inside, the deep racks stretched away into blackness, and the hum changed key.

These were not petty secrets. These were the trades that had built the guild's fortune and its damnation together: a king's forgotten crime, purchased so he could be blackmailed into peace; a saint's last doubt; forty years of a harbor pilot's knowledge, sold twice by the pilot himself. Sable moved down the aisles with her fingers trailing the rack edges, reading the shelf-marks by feel, following a filing system she'd helped design and then been forced to forget as part of her expulsion. They'd even taken the memory of the catalogue. Thorough people, guildsmen.

Except they'd filed her under her own name. There was a certain poetry in that. Arrogance, too—they'd never imagined she'd come back, so why hide her?

Row nine. Shelf fourteen. Between a jarred memory of a drowned village (the water still moving inside the glass, somehow) and a lead-sealed box marked with a sigil she pretended not to recognize, there it was:

A grey vial, small enough to swallow. Wax cap, scratched with a knife in a clerk's neat hand: *SABLE. SOLD VOLUNTARILY. TERMS OF SALE ATTACHED.*

Her hands shook. She noticed them shaking with a strange detachment, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

Twenty-two years ago she'd sat in this vault on a stool too tall for her, nineteen years old and terrified, while a guild appraiser named Mistress Ondrey had explained the exchange rate. Debts forgiven, rolls struck clean, passage to any free city she chose—in return for the night of the appraisal, complete and unblemished. "You'll not miss it," Ondrey had said, in the gentle voice they used. "It's only fear and cold stone. We're doing you a kindness."

And Sable, who already understood that the guild's kindnesses were invoices, had asked the question that mattered: "What was the door's price? The real terms. I stood there. I heard the guildmaster read them."

Ondrey had smiled the smile of a woman watching inventory appreciate. "Then that's exactly what we're buying."

They'd known even then. Even then, twenty years before Wren scratched at the guild door speaking a dead tongue, the guild had known that someday someone would come asking about the Door of Aldermere, and that the girl who'd stood in the undercity during the last appraisal was a loose thread. So they'd bought the thread and spooled it onto a shelf.

Sable lifted the vial. Through the grey glass she could see nothing—stored memory looked like fog and starlight mixed, or like nothing at all unless you knew how to look—but she could *feel* it, the way she felt all the vault's stock. Cold. Old. Herself.

A younger self, anyway. Nineteen and frightened, standing in a doorway of cold air, hearing terms read aloud in a voice like falling gravel.

She weighed it in her palm and understood, with the particular clarity that comes in the moment before you do something irreversible, that she was about to meet the person she had been before she sold herself piecemeal to survive. Everyone talked about what selling memories cost the seller—the gaps, the wrongnesses, the way your history developed holes like moth-eaten cloth. Nobody talked about what it cost to buy yourself back. To put on, all at once, a piece of yourself you'd amputated. To find out whether the person who grew up in the gap could carry the weight of the person who'd been cut away.

Nan Hollis would say something wise about it. Ferro would say something practical. Dominic—who had spent fifteen years burying a gift and calling it prudence—Dominic would understand best of all, and say nothing at all, and that silence would be the kindest thing anyone offered her this week.

But they weren't here. This was a theft, and theft was a solitary sacrament, and Sable had always performed her sacraments alone.

She broke the wax seal with her thumbnail.

* * *

Cold air. Torchlight going blue at the edges. The smell of wet stone and old iron, and underneath it something stranger—something like the smell before lightning, a green-metallic tang that made her teeth ache. She was nineteen. She was holding an appraiser's lantern for Mistress Ondrey, and her arms were tired, and she was trying very hard to be invisible, which was funny, because invisibility was the one trade she'd never managed to learn properly.

Ahead of her, in a chamber carved between the Founders' Stones, stood a door.

Not a door like doors. It was shut, and it was dark, and it was *listening*. She had no other word for it then and she had no better one now. The surface was stone worked with the sigil she'd later see stamped on an iron key in a child's fist—the thorn-and-root, the mark of Aldermere—and though nothing moved, the whole chamber had the quality of a held breath.

The guildmaster of that era stood before it with a ledger open on a stand, reading aloud, and Sable heard the terms.

Not all of them. Memory is a thief too, and it kept only what it could carry. But she heard the shape of it: the door had not been locked from this side out of cruelty, nor greed, nor conquest. The sealing oath was a *payment plan*. Aldermere had been dying—not conquered, dying, hollowed by something that came after the exiles fled, and the exiles had begged the guild of that age to shut the door behind them so that what emptied their country could not follow them into the world. The price of the sealing was memory. The door fed on remembering. Every generation, someone had to stand at the threshold and *remember Aldermere aloud*, wholly, completely, giving the country back its own past in the only coin it could still taste—

—and the last signatory of that payment, the last keeper of the schedule, the family bound to renew the oath when the door grew hungry again, was a name Sable had known all her life without knowing its weight.

Thackeray.

The memory ended there, cut clean at the edge of the chamber, because nineteen-year-old Sable had been dragged backward out of the undercity before the reading finished, and what came after belonged to Mistress Ondrey's memory, not hers.

Sable came back to herself kneeling on the vault floor, both palms flat against cold stone, breath sawing, tears she had no memory of starting running freely down her face. The vial lay empty beside her, grey glass gone clear as water. Spent vessels went clear. Somewhere in the guild's accounting, an asset had just been consumed rather than returned, and some distant clerk would write *depleted* next to her name.

Let them.

She knelt in the dark and let the two selves finish merging—the woman who'd survived twenty-two years on cunning and gaps, and the girl who'd stood in a listening dark and heard the truth and been robbed of it before she could tell anyone. It hurt. It hurt the way setting a bone hurts: necessary, structural, a pain with a purpose. When it was done she stayed kneeling a moment longer, forehead against the stone, and said quietly, to the girl who no longer existed separately from her:

"You were right to ask. They shouldn't have taken it. I'm sorry it took me this long to come back for you."

The vault hummed around her, ten thousand borrowed lives murmuring behind glass, and none of them answered, and that was fine. That was the job. You stole what was owed and you got out.

But she rose with more than the vial's contents in her head. She rose with the shape of the terms, and the name, and the understanding that rewrote everything Dominic had been tearing herself apart over since the night the child knocked.

The door wasn't sealed *against* Aldermere. It was sealed *for* it—or rather, for the world, at Aldermere's own desperate request, and the payment had run out, and the keepers of the payment were one burned-out swordspeaker and a child who didn't know she'd inherited a debt.

And the final clause—the clause the reading had reached before they dragged her out—had not been about opening the door at all.

*When the thorn turns again, let the keeper stand within the turning, and pay what remains.*

Someone had to stand in the doorway. She hadn't understood it at nineteen. She understood it now, and her blood went cold, because she also understood who the sentence would choose.

Sable pocketed the empty vial, checked the dark aisles one last time out of professional habit, and climbed toward the night air with the vault's hive-hum fading behind her. Above, the city lay under curfew, occupied streets silvered by a thin moon, and somewhere out there Dominic was keeping watch over a sleeping child while soldiers hunted the language itself.

Sable stepped out through the broken gates, wearing Marta Voss's forgettable face and carrying a dead girl's testimony, and began to run.
