## 23. The Vault Beneath the Vault

* * *

Sable had stolen a hundred memories in her life, and every one of them had cost her something she could not name until it was gone. Her mother's face. The smell of the harbor in the town where she was born. The name of her first teacher of locks and lies. She had learned to live in the hollows those thefts left behind, to furnish them with other people's recollections like a squatter furnishing an abandoned house.

Tonight she was going to steal from herself.

The guild vaults lay beneath the burned quarter, under three floors of collapsed archive and the ash of Quillon's ledgers. The usurper's agents had come through eleven days ago with fire and writs, and what they had not taken they had broken. But Sable knew vaults. Vaults were promises buildings made to greedy men, and greedy men always believed their own promises too much.

"You're sure the memory is still there?" Ferro crouched beside her on the rooftop across Ashen Lane, his maps rolled tight under one arm like weapons. In the dark he looked older than his years, gray stubbled, eyes red-rimmed from weeks of drawing countries that no longer existed.

"The guild never destroyed a memory it could sell twice," Sable said. "Quillon kept everything. That was his whole religion—nothing is ever really gone if someone will pay for it." She swallowed. "He'll have catalogued mine. *Item: one memory, female thief, age nineteen. Contents: the Door of Aldermere, its price, its purpose.* I sold it to buy my way out of a debt. I didn't even read the label before I handed it over."

"And now you want it back."

"Now I need to know what I knew." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Isn't that the joke? I've spent fifteen years being the woman who knows things. And the most important thing I ever knew, I traded away for coin and a clean slate."

Below them, Ashen Lane lay empty except for the wind pushing ash along the gutters like gray snow. Somewhere in the ruined quarter a beam settled with a groan, the sound of a building giving up.

Dominic's voice came soft through the speaking-tube Nan Hollis had rigged from a length of copper pipe—a smuggler's trick, old as the salt roads. *"Two patrols passed in the last quarter hour. They're circling. Whatever the usurper wants down there, he wants it badly."*

"Then we'd best be quick," Sable said.

She went over the edge first, because that was the order of things: Sable down the wall, Sable through the window, Sable into the dark while the others held the rope and prayed to gods they only half believed in. She dropped four stories in silence, landed in a crouch among the char, and stood a moment letting the dead building speak to her.

It spoke of water damage and bad mortar and, beneath all of it, of stone far older than the guild above. The free cities were built in layers, each generation paving over the last, and the oldest layer went down further than anyone admitted in daylight. Down to the founders' stones. Down, if Ferro's mad beautiful maps were right, to doors.

The way to the sub-vault was through the record room, or what remained of it. Sable moved through shelves of fused paper and slagged iron fittings, her boots raising small ghosts of ash. At the far end, behind a shelf that had once hidden contraband brandy, was the door she needed—and it was already open.

Her heart stopped, then started again, faster.

No. Not open. *Broken.* The lock hung in splinters, and the splinters were days old at most. Someone had been here before her.

*Sable,* said a voice in her ear, thin through the copper pipe. Dominic. *What do you see?*

"Company," she breathed. "Recent."

*Pull out. We find another way.*

"There isn't another way. There's never another way; that's why they hire me." She slid through the broken door and down the spiral stair beyond, one hand on the wall, counting steps the way she counted everything—locks picked, debts owed, pieces of herself missing. Forty-one steps. At the bottom, the air turned cold and mineral, and she lit no lamp because her eyes had long ago learned to feed on almost nothing.

The sub-vault opened around her like the inside of a bell.

It was not the guild's real vault—that was upstairs, or had been, full of coin and deeds and bottled weather. This was older. The ceiling arched in dressed granite no guild mason had cut, and the walls were lined not with shelves but with niches, hundreds of them, and in each niche sat a jar of smoked glass, and in each jar floated a curl of silver light.

Memories. Hundreds of them. The guild's deepest trade: not impossible objects but impossible pasts, bought from the desperate and the dying and resold to anyone who wanted to have lived another life. Sable walked between the niches and felt them whisper against her skin like moths.

And there, third row, marked with a lead tag stamped with her own mark—the thief's knot she'd used since girlhood—sat a single jar.

She lifted it with both hands. Inside, the silver light coiled and uncoiled, and she knew it the way you know your own reflection even when it's been years. Herself, nineteen, kneeling in this same cold dark, pressing the memory out of her skull and into glass so that a man named Corvane—who had not yet become an envoy, who had then been merely a buyer with soft hands—would let her walk away alive.

"I'm sorry," she told it. "I'm late."

Footsteps on the stair.

She killed her breath, folded herself into the shadow between two niches, and watched a lantern descend. The man who carried it wore no uniform, but he wore the uniform's absence the way soldiers do—hair cropped short, boots too good for a laborer, a sword worn apologetically at his hip. He went straight, without searching, to a niche near the back. He knew exactly which jar he wanted.

Sable weighed her options and found them all heavy. Then the man reached up, took down a jar, uncorked it, and pressed it to his temple—and screamed.

It was a terrible sound, muffled behind his teeth, the sound of a mind taking in more than it could hold. He staggered, caught himself on the niche, and when he raised his head his eyes were wrong. Wet. Wide. Full of something that had happened to someone else.

"No," he whispered, in a voice not entirely his own. "No, it wasn't supposed to—it was sealed, it was *sealed for a reason*—"

Sable moved. She had a knife at his kidney and her hand over his mouth before he finished the second word, and she said, very quietly, "You're going to walk back up those stairs and forget you came down here. That's not a threat. That's arithmetic. You're holding a soldier's memory of Aldermere, and your body doesn't know how to carry it. One more minute and it will carry you instead."

He trembled against her. Through her palm she felt him trying to speak, and after a long moment she eased her hand just enough.

"It followed them out," he said. His voice was layered now, two men speaking at once, the soldier underneath and the clerk on top. "That's what the memory says. The door was sealed from both sides. Not to keep the Aldermen in. To keep—" His face convulsed. "I can't hold it, I can't—"

"Yes, you can. Give it to me."

He stared at her. And then, because men at the end of their strength will do anything they're told by someone who sounds certain, he pulled the jar from his temple and pushed it into her hands, and fled up the stairs two at a time, weeping.

Sable stood alone in the bell-dark with two jars: her own past, and a dead soldier's last knowledge.

She unstoppered hers first.

* * *

The memory poured into her like cold water into a hot throat.

Nineteen again. The debt, the soft-handed buyer, the desperate bargain. And then—because the guild had made her read the provenance before accepting payment, guild rules, absurd and absolute—the memory of being *shown* the original: a memory taken from a swordspeaker on her deathbed, sixty years gone, whose dying confession Sable had once skimmed like a merchant skimming a bill of lading.

The swordspeaker's name had been Maren Yarrow.

And Maren Yarrow, bleeding out in a room that smelled of tallow and rain, had spoken these words to the guild's recorder:

*"We did not seal the door to imprison our country. Understand that, whoever hears this. We sealed it because the exiles were followed. Something crossed with us—or after us—I never saw it whole, none of us did, we only saw what it did. It wears hunger like a coat. It eats remembering. By the end, half of Aldermere had forgotten their own children's faces, and the thing walked among us wearing our love for them like a mask. So we closed the door with it on the far side, and we paid for the sealing with our oaths, and our children's oaths, unto the seventh generation. If the door opens, hear me: it must be guarded. It must be witnessed. What is remembered cannot be eaten. What is loved aloud cannot be worn. Guard it. Witness it. Or burn the threshold stone to slag and damn us all."*

The memory ended. Sable found herself on her knees on the cold granite, the empty jar rolling from her fingers, her own returned past sitting in her chest like a swallowed star.

She had known this. At nineteen, she had known it, and she had sold it for her life, and she could not even hate herself properly because she would have done it again. That was the filth of desperation: it never offered you choices you could be proud of.

But oh, the shape of it now. The plan—they had all been so careful, so clever—Wren's fluency, Nan's songs, the half-speakers' fragments assembling sentence by sentence toward an opening. And all of it built on the belief that behind the door lay a country, starving, waiting, wanting only to be remembered.

Maybe that was true. Wren's dreams said it was true.

But Maren Yarrow's dying voice said something else too. Said that hunger could wear the shape of wanting. Said that the country itself might be the mask.

*The door was sealed not to imprison Aldermere,* Sable thought, climbing the stairs on legs that felt borrowed, *but to keep something from following the exiles out.*

And they were about to open it.

* * *

Dominic met her at the rope, and one look at Sable's face told her everything before a word was spoken.

"What did you find?"

"Us." Sable handed her the soldier's jar. "And the reason there's a 'us' to find. Read it. I can't say it twice tonight."

They read it together in the shell of the record room, by shielded lamplight, while Ferro stood watch at the broken window and Wren sat cross-legged on the floor with her arms around her knees, watching the grown-ups' faces the way children watch weather.

When Dominic finished, she was silent for a long time. The lamp flame bent in some draft from below, and in its bending her face seemed to change—older, then younger, then simply tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Maren Yarrow," she said at last. "My great-aunt. My mother's sealing signatory was her sister. They were all Yarrows, the ones who paid." She looked down at the jar in her hands as if it might answer questions the memory hadn't. "I thought my family's sin was locking a country in a cellar. All these years, that's what I couldn't forgive. And it turns out—"

"It turns out they may have been right," Sable said.

"It turns out they may have been *right*," Dominic agreed, and laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what that does to a person? Fifteen years of shame, and now the shame might be the only decent thing my blood ever did."

Ferro came away from the window, moving fast and low. "Patrol's doubled. We have perhaps twenty minutes before they sweep this block. Whatever we've decided, we decide walking."

"Then walk and talk," Dominic said.

They went out through the brandy-shelf door, along the ash-choked lane, Ferro leading them through cuts and courtyards only a mapmaker would know, while the argument unspooled between breaths.

"We don't open it," Sable said. "That's the simple answer. We take Wren and the half-speakers north, over the mountains, somewhere the usurper's writ runs thin, and we let the tongue survive the way tongues always have—in kitchens, in lullabies, in secret. Alive but shut."

"And Aldermere?" Wren's voice, from between them, small and steady. "The people still in there. The country still in there. You'd seal them in with it. With *it*."

Sable flinched. From a child, the objection landed differently than it would have from an adult—from an adult it would have been politics, but from Wren it was arithmetic of another kind, the kind where the numbers are people.

"We don't know what's true," Sable said gently. "We know a dying woman was afraid sixty years ago. Fear makes big shadows."

"So does hunger," Wren said. "I've dreamed it. Both of it. The country and the—other thing. They're tangled together like roots." She kicked a stone off the cobbles with unnecessary force. "My village burned because soldiers think my language is a weapon. Now you're telling me my own allies think it's a trap. Everyone keeps deciding what my words mean without asking me."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing honest to answer it with.

* * *

They reconvened an hour later in Nan Hollis's kitchen, the safest room in the free cities because no soldier on earth wanted to be scolded by an old woman over her own stove. Nan fed them barley soup and listened with her hands folded while Sable recounted the memory, and the soldier's breakdown, and Maren Yarrow's dying charge.

Nan was quiet for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her voice had the cadence of song about it, the cadence she used when she was quoting without meaning to.

"*What is remembered cannot be eaten,*" she said. "*What is loved aloud cannot be worn.*" She tapped the table. "Those aren't just dying words, girl. Those are the last two lines of the Sealing Verse. The verse I've been teaching Wren since the day she knocked on our door. We always thought it was a lock-chant. A closing formula." Her old eyes went around the table, and there was something terrible in them—not fear exactly, but the particular grief of a woman whose songs keep turning out to be true. "It's instructions. It's always been instructions. For the guarding."

"Then the sentence we assembled," Ferro said slowly, "the opening sentence—"

"—opens the door fine," Nan said. "Opens it beautifully. Opens it wide as a barn door in harvest. But nothing in it guards what comes through. Because the guarding was supposed to be a separate work. A separate oath." She looked at Dominic, and everyone else looked at Dominic too, because in every company there is eventually a moment when all eyes go to the person least likely to want them there.

Dominic set down her spoon.

"You want a swordspeaker's oath renewed," she said. "From me. The woman who broke hers. Who buried the gift so deep it nearly died. Whose gift, when I finally used it again in that fish market, nearly killed me outright."

"I want," Nan said, "whatever's on the other side of that door to be met by someone who has already lost everything once and chose to keep going anyway. That's the only credential that matters for this work. And I happen to be looking at her."

Silence, except for the soup pot ticking as it cooled.

It was Wren who broke it, quietly, in the tone of a child stating the obvious to slow adults: "There's something else. In the dream. The country—when it talks to me, sometimes it says sentences I don't understand, and I write them down." She dug in her pocket and produced a scrap of paper, folded soft as cloth from handling. "I wrote this one down three nights ago. Before any of this. I didn't know what it meant."

She unfolded it and laid it on the table. The Alderman script marched across it in her careful child's hand, and Ferro leaned in to transliterate, lips moving, and then stopped moving altogether.

"Read it," Dominic said.

"'*I am not behind the door,*'" Ferro read, and his voice cracked on the last words. "'*I am the door.*'"

The draft moved through the kitchen, lifting the scrap of paper, turning it over. On the reverse side, Wren had written a second line, smaller, in a hand that looked—everyone saw it at once—less like a child's writing and more like something that had written itself through her.

Nan read it aloud, though her voice shook doing it.

"*Ask the keeper what she is willing to become.*"

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to ring. Not the usurper's alarm—something older, the harbor bell, rung only for ships in distress or for the founding-day procession. It rang once, paused, rang again. Three times. Paused.

Then it did not stop.

Dominic rose from the table, and her hand went to the hilt of a sword she had sworn never to draw, and her other hand went to the iron key stamped with the sigil she hadn't seen since childhood.

"That's not the harbor pattern," she said. "Somebody's ringing it from the *founders' stones*."
