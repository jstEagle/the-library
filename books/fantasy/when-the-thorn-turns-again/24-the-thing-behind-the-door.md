## 24. The Thing Behind the Door

Wren dreamed in Alderman, and that was how she knew something had found her.

She had dreamed in the old tongue before—everyone who still carried it did, Nan said, because a language lives longest where no one is listening. But those dreams were ordinary ones: her mother's kitchen, smoke rising straight from the chimney on cold mornings, the particular creak of the third stair. This dream was different. This dream was *listening back*.

She stood in a field of grey grass that moved without wind. The sky above was the color of pewter left out too long, and there were no birds in it, and no clouds either—just a flat, patient grey, like the inside of a closed door seen from underneath.

"Wren," said the field.

Not the wind. Not an echo. The field itself, every blade of it at once, a voice worn thin as old cloth but unmistakably alive. It pronounced her name correctly—in the Alderman way, with the soft *r* rolling through it like water over stones—and Wren's whole body shivered with recognition. No one in the free cities said her name right. Even Nan, bless her, softened it into something comfortable. This voice said it the way her mother had.

"Who's there?" she asked. Her own Alderman came out rough, rusted at the edges from weeks of speaking free-city trade-talk. She was suddenly ashamed of it, the way you're ashamed of muddy boots in a clean house.

"I am here," said the field. "I have always been here. That is rather the trouble."

The grass parted ahead of her—not trampled, just *yielding*, folding aside like a curtain drawn by careful hands—and revealed a road she knew. She knew it the way you know a face in a dream: not from having walked it, but from having heard about it so many times it had grown into her bones. The Long Mile, the road between her grandmother's village and the sea-cliffs, the one the lullabies sang about. *Walk the Long Mile, my darling, walk it to the grey water, and whatever calls your name from the foam, do not answer twice.*

"You know this place," the voice said. It was closer now. Or perhaps it hadn't moved at all, and closeness was simply a thing that happened to her when it spoke. "Of course you do. You are the one who carries it properly."

"I don't carry anything," Wren said. "I'm just—" She stopped. She had been about to say *just a girl*, but standing here, in a country made of listening, the lie would not form. "I remember things," she amended. "Songs. Words."

"Remember," the voice repeated, and the word came out strange, stretched, as if it were tasting something it had almost forgotten existed. "Yes. Remember. Do you know what has not remembered me, child? Anything. Anyone. For fifteen years I have been on the far side of a shut door, and the far side of a shut door is very much like being nothing at all."

The grey grass rippled. And then, all around her, the country began to show itself—or tried to.

It came in flashes, half-formed, like reflections in disturbed water: a harbor town with white walls and blue shutters, except the walls flickered and lost their color mid-glimpse. A wedding procession moving down a lane, the dancers' faces smooth as eggs. An orchard heavy with fruit that cast no shadow. A market square full of people whose voices, when they laughed, made no sound at all. Everything was *almost* Aldermere. Everything was Aldermere the way a portrait is a person—the shape correct, the life gone out of it.

"We had ten thousand songs," the voice said quietly. "We had a game played with knucklebones on the church steps every saints' day, and the winner got to ring the bell. We had a way of saying goodbye that took four sentences, because we believed parting quickly invited sorrow to stay. I had rivers with names older than any kingdom you have ever heard of. I had children." A pause, long enough for Wren's heart to beat six times. "I had children who learned to walk on my roads and grew up and buried their parents in my soil and taught *their* children my words. Do you understand what I am telling you, Wren?"

"No," she whispered. And then, honestly: "I think I might."

"I am not a man behind a door. I am not an army or a god or a monster waiting in the dark, though I know that is what they tell you." The grey grass bowed flat, all of it, as if the whole country were kneeling. "I am the country. And a country that is forgotten does not die, Wren. It only gets *hungrier*. Hunger is what forgetting turns into, on the far side of a door. Hunger is all a memory can feel when no one will hold it."

* * *

She woke with salt on her lips and didn't know if she'd been crying or if the dream had followed her out.

The undercity storeroom they'd made home was dark except for the guttering stub of a candle Ferro had scrounged. Somewhere above, muffled by thirty feet of founders' stone, the bombardment went on—soft rhythmic thuds, like a giant's heartbeat, so constant now that Wren barely heard it anymore. Dominic sat against the far wall, awake as always, whetstone whispering along a blade she never seemed to sharpen so much as talk to. When Wren stirred, the whetstone stopped.

"Dream?"

Wren nodded. Then, because lying to Dominic felt like lying to a wall that could see: "It talked to me."

Dominic crossed the room without hurrying and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. She smelled of lamp oil and iron. "What did it say?"

"That it's hungry." Wren wrapped her arms around her knees. "That it isn't a monster. It's the *country*, Dom. The actual country, like—like Aldermere is alive, and it's been shut behind the door with everything else, and nobody remembers it, and forgetting turned into hunger." She looked up. "Is that true? Can a country be alive?"

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. In the candlelight her scarred face was unreadable, which meant she was choosing carefully among true things.

"The guild ledgers say the Door was sealed 'paid in full,'" she said finally. "My family signed the seal. I used to think that meant we locked people in." Her thumb traced the old burn-scar across her palm, the swordspeaker's mark. "Maybe we locked something else in too. Something that was already starving when we shut it."

"It showed me things," Wren said. "The harbor town. Weddings. An orchard. But they were all wrong somehow. Faded. Like it couldn't remember them properly anymore and was showing me its best guess." Her throat tightened. "Dom, it sounded so *sad*. It sounded like Nan sounds when she sings the verse about the boats that never came back. It asked me to remember it. That's all it wanted. It said remembering was all anyone had ever needed to give it."

"And you believed it."

"Yes." Then, smaller: "Shouldn't I?"

Dominic looked at her for a long time. Wren had learned to read the silences of adults the way other children read letters, and this one contained, clearly, *I believe you believed it, and that frightens me more than the thing itself.*

"Eat something," Dominic said at last, pressing half a loaf into her hands. "And tomorrow you'll tell Nan exactly what it said. Word for word, in Alderman. If it spoke to you in our tongue, there may be more in its words than its meaning." She rose, then paused. "Wren. Whatever it shows you next—and there will be a next—you tell us before you decide anything. Promise me."

"I promise," Wren said, and meant it.

But promises, she was learning, were doors too, and things could come through them.

* * *

The second visit came three nights later, and this time the country had prepared.

The grey field was still there, still listening, but now there was a table set in the middle of it—a long harvest-table of dark Alderman oak, laid with food that shimmered like heat-haze: brown bread, honeycomb, fish fresh from the grey water, apples that gleamed like lanterns. Chairs waited. One of them was small, sized for a child.

"This is rude of me," the country said, and its voice had changed—it had spent three nights, apparently, remembering how to be gentle. "You cannot eat a dream. I know that. But I wanted you to see what setting a table used to mean, here. Every guest fed. Every stranger given bread and salt before a single question was asked. We were not a warlike people, Wren. We were a *feeding* people."

Wren didn't sit. She had promised Dominic, and promising Dominic had teeth in it now. But she walked the length of the table, looking at everything, memorizing everything, because that was the gift the country wanted and she found—to her own surprise—that she wanted to give it.

"Tell me about the bell," she said. "The saints'-day bell."

The grey air shivered with delight. Around them the dead orchard bloomed into a memory sharp enough to sting: a stone church with a squat round tower, children scrambling over its steps, a gap-toothed boy hauling the rope while the whole village counted aloud in Alderman, and the bell—

The bell made no sound. It swung and swung and the sound had been eaten somewhere between then and now, and watching it, Wren felt grief move through her like weather.

"That's the hunger," she said quietly. "Isn't it? You can keep the shapes. You can't keep the sound."

For a long moment the country said nothing, and its silence was worse than any wail.

"Yes," it said finally. "You understand faster than the last one who visited me. Faster than any of them."

Wren went very still. "The last one?"

"A boy." The orchard dimmed. "He came to me the night before the burning started. He was afraid—he was always afraid, that one, fear was the strongest thing he carried—and he asked me to save him. To open the door for him alone. He offered me anything." The grey grass flattened in a slow wave, like something breathing. "I told him I could not open it alone. I told him a door needs two voices and a willing heart, and he said, *then I will find another way through*, and he left, and he did not look back at me, and I have wondered for fifteen years whether he ever once thought of what he left on this side of the wall."

The candle-flame of Wren's attention bent toward it. "What was his name?"

"He had a name he was born with and a name he chose. He kept the first secret even from me." A pause. "He calls himself Aldous now. I believe the free cities have another name for him. They say it in the same breath as the word *usurper*."

The ground did not tilt; dreams never let you fall when you most need to. But Wren felt the whole architecture of the last months shift beneath her—the soldiers, the warrants, the hunt for speakers, the siege hammering above their heads. All of it, driven by a frightened boy who had asked the country to save him and been told the truth: *not alone.*

"He wants to open you," she said slowly. "Not to let people home. To take you. To claim what lies beyond."

"He wants to wear me," the country corrected softly. "There is a difference, and it is the whole difference. A homesick man opens a door to walk through it. A hungry man opens a door to swallow the room on the other side." The table between them flickered; for one instant Wren saw it not laden with food but bare, rotted, standing in a field of ash. "I know hunger, child. I have studied nothing else for fifteen years. I know it when I see it wearing a crown."

"Then why tell *me*?" Wren's voice cracked. "Why show me weddings and bells and tables? If you're dangerous—if forgetting made you hungry—how do I know you won't eat us too? How do I know you're not just hungrier than him, and better at waiting?"

The silence that followed was enormous. The grey sky pressed down. And Wren realized, with a lurch, that she had hurt it—that a country could be wounded by a child's honest question, and that this proved nothing either way, because monsters could be wounded too, and the most dangerous things in every story were the ones that suffered beautifully.

"You cannot know," the country said at last. "That is the truth, and I will not dress it. Fifteen years of hunger live in me, Wren, and hunger does not make promises well. Perhaps I would pour through that door and forget myself entirely. Perhaps I would take and take until the free cities looked like this field." The grey grass shivered. "But hear the rest. There is one thing hunger cannot counterfeit, because I have tried, in the long dark, to counterfeit everything else."

"What thing?"

"To be witnessed is to be fed." Its voice dropped to almost nothing, and every blade of grass leaned toward her. "You have spoken with me twice now. You have named my bell and my table and my Long Mile. Each time you did, the edges of me grew *sharper*. Clearer. I can feel the harbor town again—I can nearly hear the gulls. You feed me by seeing me, Wren, and I cannot fake how that feels. Hunger only knows how to take. What I feel when you remember me is not taking." A pause. "It is being given to."

Wren stood in the grey field with her fists clenched, fourteen years old, carrying the weight of a language on her small shoulders, and understood that she was being asked to do the hardest thing anyone had ever asked of her: to pity something that could destroy everyone she loved, and to do it with her eyes open.

"I'll remember you," she said. "Whatever happens. Even if we seal you away forever, I'll teach the songs, and I'll say your name right, and I'll tell children about the bell. That I promise you, and I don't promise easy."

The field exhaled. For one moment—one impossible moment—the grey broke, and Wren saw Aldermere whole: green hills running to a bright sea, white walls, blue shutters, smoke rising straight from chimneys, and everywhere, everywhere, the sound of a language being spoken by ten thousand living mouths.

Then it was grey again, and the voice was tired and grateful and faintly, terribly sad.

"Thank you," it said. "Now wake, child. Quickly. Because someone else is dreaming his way toward my door tonight, and he is not coming to remember."

* * *

She woke gasping, and the storeroom was already full of noise—Sable shouting from the stairwell, Ferro's maps sliding off crates as he scrambled up, Dominic pulling her boots on with the calm of a woman who had been dressed for catastrophe since evening fell.

"What?" Wren managed. "What's wrong?"

Sable reached the bottom of the stairs, pale as tallow, her thief's eyes wide. "It's Ilva's bread-seller. Message just came up." She swallowed. "The usurper's half-speakers finished translating a fragment tonight. Corvane's got it. They've got the opening clause, Wren—they've got the *first words of the sentence*."

Dominic was already at the threshold-stone, hand flat against the carved sigil, and Wren saw her feel it before she saw anything: the stone, cold for weeks, had begun—very slightly, very terribly—to warm.

"They're going to try to open it themselves," Dominic said quietly.

And from deep beneath the founders' stones, so faint that only Wren, in the hush after the dream, could hear it—the country began, softly, to sing.
