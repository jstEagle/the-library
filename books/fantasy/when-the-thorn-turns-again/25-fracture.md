## 25. Fracture

The cellar beneath the tannery had once held brine barrels; now it held the last pieces of a dead country, and they were cutting each other to ribbons.

Dominic stood with her back against the cold stone and watched it happen the way she had once watched ledgers go bad—line by line, entry by entry, the slow arithmetic of ruin. Twelve half-speakers around a single tallow lamp. Old Marta with her hands folded like a woman at prayer, except her knuckles were white. Tam Voss pacing, his boots leaving wet crescents on the floor. The twins from Saltrow, Ivo and Ilse, sitting shoulder to shoulder but turned away from each other for the first time in Dominic's memory. Nan Hollis's empty stool by the wall, where she'd sat every evening until her body finally decided, three days ago, that it was done carrying her.

And Wren. Small, sharp-eyed Wren, curled on a pallet in the corner with her knees drawn up, watching the adults come apart.

"—and what would we even *find* there?" Tam said, for the third time, or maybe the fourth. His voice had gone past anger into something rawer. "You've heard what comes through the dreams. You've felt it. That isn't a country waiting behind that door, Marta. It's a mouth."

"It is our *home*." Marta's voice was quiet, which somehow made it worse. Quiet voices in this room carried the weight of forty years of hiding. "My mother died singing songs she wasn't allowed to hum under her breath. My father was hanged in a market square for two words of greeting. And you want me to stand in front of the one door back and pull it shut?"

"I want us to *live*."

"So do I. There. We agree on something." Marta unfolded her hands. "The difference is what you think living means."

Dominic had heard arguments like this before—not these words, never these words, but this shape. Fifteen years ago she'd stood in a different room while older people with more scars had debated whether the guild should sell a certain key to a certain buyer, and the argument had worn this same groove: survival against principle, the living against the dead, and everyone certain they were the reasonable one.

She knew how those arguments ended. They ended with someone signing their name to something they couldn't take back.

"The usurper has thirty thousand men outside the walls," Ivo said. "He has Corvane. He has—" the twins' fragment of the language surfaced in his mouth like a fish rising, a word Dominic didn't know, guttural and old, "*—the ones who translate for him.* Half-speakers, working for the other side. So while we sit here deciding, they're assembling the sentence without us. And when they open that door—and they will open it—we'll be standing here having accomplished nothing except staying pure."

"Then we fight them for the door," Ilse said. "We get there first. We speak it first. We open it ourselves and we let the country decide what it wants to be."

"You didn't dream last night," Tam said flatly. "Or you did, and you're lying about it."

The cellar went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when someone has named a thing everyone is trying not to name.

Because they all dreamed now. Ever since the threshold beneath the Founders' Stones, ever since the door had stirred at the sound of the first clause, the dreams had come—grey wind, grey hills, a vast attention turning toward them through the stone like a lighthouse beam sweeping a dark coast. Not hostile. That was the worst part. It felt like being *wanted*, the way a starving thing wants, and hunger wears whatever face will get it fed.

Wren dreamed deepest. Wren came back from those dreams with sand in her hair and an expression Dominic couldn't read, and said things like *it remembers my grandmother's kitchen* in a voice too old for her mouth.

"It spoke to me," Tam said. "Three nights ago. In full Alderman, better than any of us can manage, because it's had four hundred years of practice and we've had scraps. It told me it only wanted to come home. It told me—" his jaw worked "—it used my little sister's voice. The one the soldiers took. And I believed it for a full minute, Dominic. A full minute I stood in that dream ready to say yes to anything." He looked around the lamplight, at each of them. "A country that borrows the faces of its dead to argue its case is not a country. It's a lure."

"It's *lonely*," Marta said. "There's a difference."

"There is, and it doesn't matter, because both of them will swallow you."

Dominic pushed off the wall. She hadn't meant to speak; she'd promised herself she'd let them argue it out, that this decision belonged to them, the scattered survivors of a language she'd helped bury. But her mouth had other ideas, the way it always did lately, the way it had since the oath flared back to life under the Founders' Stones.

"Both of you are right," she said, "and it doesn't matter."

Twelve faces turned to her.

"Tam's right that it argues with borrowed voices. Marta's right that it's lonely and that loneliness can be honest. I've stood in front of that door. I've felt it look at me. And here's what I know that none of you do, because you weren't there when it was sealed." She made herself keep going, made herself spend the coin. "It wasn't sealed to imprison Aldermere. Quillon's final page—the one Sable bled a piece of herself to recover—it says the exiles were let out. *Let out.* The door was closed behind them to keep something else from following. Whatever waits there now has been alone with that thing for four hundred years. Of course it's hungry. Of course it wears dead children's voices. It's been locked in the dark with a wolf since before any of your grandmothers were born, and it has forgotten which one of them it is."

"And so?" Tam's voice cracked. "So we leave it? Leave *them*?"

"So we don't open it blind. We don't seal it out of fear, and we don't open it out of longing. Either one feeds the same wolf." Dominic looked at them, at the ruined mosaic of a nation—Marta's grief, Tam's terror, the twins' fury, old Piet's silence, the others nodding or shaking their heads in the shadows. "We open it *guarded*. That was always the swordspeaker's work. Not to choose for the door. To stand in it and choose for whatever tries to pass."

"With what army?" Ivo demanded. "With what power? Your gift burned out holding the threshold for ten seconds. You could barely walk afterward. One word nearly killed you in the fish market. And the sentence needs a dozen voices we barely have, spoken perfectly, while thirty thousand soldiers try to dig through Ferro's tunnel to reach us—"

The bombardment chose that moment to underscore him. A shell struck somewhere above, near enough that dust sifted down through the floorboards and the lamp flame ducked and recovered. Everyone froze in the old practiced way, counting seconds, judging distance. Somewhere overhead, muffled by earth and brick, the city screamed and went on screaming.

"—while that," Ivo finished bitterly, "continues."

"We have Wren," Ilse said quietly.

Everyone looked at the corner.

Wren had gone very still. In the guttering light her eyes were huge, and Dominic saw—with a lurch of guilt so strong it felt physical—that the girl had understood every word. All of it. The debate about her language, her fluency, her usefulness. *We have Wren,* said the way you'd say *we have the key.*

"No," Dominic said. "That's not—"

"She's the only fluent speaker left," Ivo said. "You know it. The sentence needs native grammar, the cadences, the things no fragment-carrier like us can fake. Nan knew it—that's why she poured everything into her before she died. She's not just part of the answer. She's the spine of it."

"She's nine years old," Dominic said, and heard her own voice go flat and dangerous.

"And I was seven when the soldiers came to Saltrow," Ilse said, "and nobody asked my age before they made me watch. None of us got childhoods, Thackeray. That's the whole point. That's why we're here."

"That's the whole tragedy," Dominic snapped, "and you're dressing it up as strategy—"

"Don't you dare lecture us about tragedy." Marta had risen, and there was something terrible in how slowly she moved, like a glacier deciding. "You forswore the gift. You hid in ledgers for fifteen years while the rest of us hid in accents and married names and taught our children to cough instead of sing. You got to *choose* your silence. We were born into ours. And now the door is found, and the moment has come, and you stand there telling us to wait, to be careful, to guard—to *your* terms, *your* timing. Where was your care when your family signed the sealing? Where was your caution then, swordspeaker?"

The name landed like a blow. Dominic felt it in her ribs. Around the room, heads turned—some of them had known, some hadn't, and the ones who hadn't were learning it now, watching her with new arithmetic in their eyes. *Daughter of the sealing line. The betrayal runs in her blood.*

"My family sealed the door to save the exiles," Dominic said. Each word cost her. "And if I have to spend what's left of my gift standing in that doorway so that opening it doesn't end the world, then that's exactly what I'll do. But I won't feed a child to the threshold as the price of admission. Not her. Not anyone."

"She wouldn't be fed," Piet said from the shadows—he rarely spoke, and his voice was gravel and rust. "She'd be honored. Ask her what she wants."

"She's nine."

"Ask. Her. What. She. Wants."

Every eye in the room swung to the pallet in the corner.

Later, lying awake in the dark after everything had broken, Dominic would replay this moment again and again and hate herself for each version of it. Because she saw the trap a half-second before it sprang—saw that whatever Wren said, the room would take it as a verdict—and she opened her mouth to stop it anyway, and she was too slow, because Wren had already begun to speak.

"I'm not afraid of it," Wren said. Her voice was small in the big dark room, but steady, the way it got steady when she slipped into Alderman cadence even speaking Common. "It misses its kitchens. It misses the bells. It showed me the bell-ringer's hands, and the flour under them, and—" her breath hitched, caught, steadied "—and I think if everyone keeps shouting, nobody will hear what it actually needs, and then it will stop asking nicely."

"See?" Marta whispered.

"She's a child repeating what a hungry thing told her," Tam said, louder. "Gods above, are you all—"

"She speaks for it better than we speak for ourselves," Ivo said.

"She's *nine*—"

"You keep saying that like it means she gets to be spared. Nothing gets spared. Look outside!"

And that was the moment it stopped being an argument.

Dominic saw it happen in fragments, the way you see a cart crash—too fast to stop, clear only in retrospect. Tam crossing the room toward the pallet, not menacingly, he'd have sworn later, only to pick Wren up and carry her out of this madness, and Ivo stepping into his path, and Tam's hand going to his belt where he'd taken to wearing a knife since the ambush at the chandler's, and Ilse on her feet with her own blade out—where had Ilse even gotten a knife—pointed not at Tam but at the space between him and the child, and Marta crying out something in Alderman that was either a curse or a prayer, and Piet moving with shocking speed for a man of sixty to put himself between the blades and Wren—

"STOP."

The word came out of Dominic with the gift behind it. Just a fraction—a thread, not a torrent—but it hit the room like a dropped glass. Every candle flame stood still. Every voice died in every throat. For one heartbeat twelve people stood in perfect, ringing silence, staring at her, at the faint gold shimmer still fading off her tongue.

Then the backlash arrived, and her knees buckled, and she caught herself on a barrel stave, vision swimming, copper flooding her mouth. *Fifteen years of hoarding spent in one syllable,* some distant part of her noted clinically. *Well spent.*

In that heartbeat of silence, another sound cut through: small feet on stone stairs, fast, desperate, ascending.

"Wren—"

The cellar door banged open onto night air and smoke and the far-off orange flicker of the burning river quarter, and then it banged shut, and Wren was gone.

* *

They scattered like startled birds. Tam and Ilse out the delivery hatch toward the tannery yard, Ivo up the stairs after the girl, Marta standing frozen in the center of the room with her hands pressed over her mouth, whispering the same phrase over and over in the old tongue—*foolish children, foolish children*—though whether she meant the arguers or herself, Dominic couldn't tell.

Dominic ran.

Her legs were still shaking from the word; the stairs tried to pitch her headfirst twice and she took them on hands and feet, half climbing. Above her, through the tannery's sagging upper floor, the night was wrong—too bright, too loud, the siege guns keeping their patient rhythm, boom and crumble, boom and crumble, the free cities' oldest quarter coming down street by street while its oldest secret slept beneath it.

She burst out into the alley behind the tannery and the smell hit her—smoke, salt, blood, the sweet reek of the rendering vats—and the cold hit her, deep autumn wind funneled between the walls, and the noise hit her worst of all, because up close the bombardment wasn't rhythm, it was *weather*, it was the sky itself tearing.

"WREN!"

Ivo was already fifty yards down the alley, calling her name into the dark. Tam emerged from the yard gate, pale, knife still in his hand, looking down at it as if he'd never seen it before, and then he threw it away from himself, hard, into the drainage channel, and started running the other direction, shouting her name, shouting *I'm sorry, I'm sorry* to no one, to everyone.

Dominic stood in the alley mouth and did the thing she was best at, the ledger-keeper's trick, the thing fifteen years of hiding had honed finer than any swordwork: she made the chaos legible. Smoke drifting east meant the fires were west. West was the river quarter, the fish market where she'd first spoken, the streets Wren knew because Wren had walked them with her, with Sable, with Nan—Nan, who had died teaching her the last verse not six days ago, whose stool was still empty downstairs, and oh, oh gods, of course, *of course*—

The chapel of Saint Verity. The one place in the city Wren associated with Nan, where they'd sung together under their breath while the priest pretended deafness. Three streets west. Through the worst of the shelling.

Dominic ran.

The city at night under bombardment was a country she'd never mapped. Doors burst open spilling families with bundles and candle stubs; a man knelt in the middle of a crossroads praying over something covered with a coat; a woman dragged a handcart of squealing piglets through falling ash, and no one looked at anyone, because in this weather everyone was guilty of surviving. A shell struck the granary two streets over and the concussion picked Dominic up and set her down against a wall, ears screaming, world whited out, and she got up because stopping was dying, and kept running west.

Behind her she heard the others fanning out—Ivo's voice, Tam's, Marta's thin high call. Good. Let them search the safe streets. Dominic knew, with the sick certainty of the ledger-keeper balancing a column and finding it short, exactly where the missing figure had gone.

Saint Verity's stood at the top of Chapel Hill, small and ancient, its foundations older than the free cities themselves—Ferro had once traced its stones to the same quarry as the Founders' Stones below, though he'd never said aloud what he thought that meant. Its bell tower rose black against the firelight. As Dominic climbed the hill, lungs full of knives, she saw the chapel door standing open, and saw, framed in it, a small silhouette against the altar's single votive flame.

Wren stood in the aisle with her arms wrapped around herself, and she was singing.

Not loudly. Barely a thread of sound. But in the dead tongue, in the full flowing grammar that no fragment-carrier could manage, and the old stones of Saint Verity were doing what old stones sometimes did—they were *answering*. A hum, subsonic, felt in the teeth rather than heard, rising from the foundations, from the quarry underneath, from the deep places where Ferro's maps said the door lay sleeping. The votive flame stretched tall and thin.

And Wren was crying as she sang, great silent gulps between the phrases, and Dominic understood, standing wrecked and gasping in the doorway, that the girl wasn't praying. She was apologizing. To the country in her mouth, for the people who carried it. To the people who carried it, for the country. To Nan, most of all, whose stool sat empty a mile south while the last verse she'd given this child echoed off stones that remembered the language from before the sealing, from before everything.

"*Wren.*"

The singing stopped. The hum faded reluctantly, like a tide going out. The girl turned, and her face in the votive light was blotched and furious and terrified, nine years old and carrying four hundred years, and she said:

"They were going to make me decide. All of them. Even you. Nobody asked if I wanted to decide—they just waited to hear which way I'd point." Her chin came up, and it was Nan's chin, Nan's stubborn angle, learned in six months of stolen lessons. "So I'm not going back there until they stop needing me to be the answer."

Somewhere across the city, close now, horribly close, a shell shrieked down out of the dark—and this one wasn't random. Dominic heard it falling toward them, toward the chapel, toward the oldest stones and the smallest speaker, and she was already moving, already reaching for the last scrap of hoarded power in her burnt-out chest, when the tower above them began, very softly, to ring.
