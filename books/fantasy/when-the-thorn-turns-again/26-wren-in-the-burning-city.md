## 26. Wren in the Burning City

The first shell came down on the Tanner's Row aqueduct just after the evening bells, and the sound it made was not a sound at all but a hole in the world, a white silence that swallowed the bells mid-toll and then gave them back broken.

Wren had been carrying water. It was such an ordinary errand—Nan Hollis had sent her to the cistern on Copper Lane with two dented pails and instructions to mind her step on the wet cobbles—and she was halfway home when the sky over the harbor wall turned orange and everything she knew about where she was going stopped being true.

She remembered, later, that she did not drop the pails. That seemed important afterward, the way small useless things become important: she had stood in the middle of Copper Lane with two pails of water while the city came apart around her, and she had not dropped them until a man running past knocked her shoulder and spun her half around, and then they were on the ground anyway, the water spreading dark across stones already slick with ash.

*Get inside,* everyone was shouting. *Cellars, cellars, get below.*

But Wren stood still, because the language was rising in her throat.

It had been doing that more and more lately. The Aldermen said every speaker felt it differently—Ferro's mapmaker hands twitched toward drawing when the old words wanted out; Sable said hers tasted like copper; Nan said hers came as humming, a tune looking for its verse. For Wren it had always been a pressure behind the breastbone, like a word held too long underwater, straining for the surface. Dominic had taught her to press it down. *Not here,* Dominic said. *Not yet. A word spoken in fear is a blade swung blind.* Wren had been very good at pressing it down for most of a year.

But now the city was burning, and the word would not stay under.

It rose through her ribs like water finding a crack, and what it wanted to be, she realized with a strange floating calm, was not a weapon. The usurper's soldiers spoke their borrowed Alderman like hammers—words to break, to burn, to unmake. But the tongue was older than hammers. The tongue had been a country once. And countries, Wren thought, standing in the falling ash with her fists full of empty pails, countries know how to shelter people. That was the whole of what a country was: a place that stood between you and the weather.

She opened her mouth and let the word come up.

She didn't know its meaning the way she knew free-city words, one definition laid flat like a specimen on a page. She knew it the way you know your own name—whole, from inside. It was the word for *under*, and also for *within*, and also for the feeling of being held. She spoke it aloud, into the burning street, and gave it to the walls.

The next shell struck three houses down from where she stood.

It should have flattened them. Wren saw it come—the black arc against the smoke, the whistling—and she saw it strike, and she saw the blast bend. There was no other word for it. The fireball bloomed outward and then, at the edge of the lane, it leaned, the way tall grass leans away from a wind only it can feel, and the worst of it poured up the empty street instead of across the crowded doorways where families were pressing into cellars. Windows cracked. A shutter tore loose and cartwheeled into the cistern. But the people behind those doors—Wren could feel them through the stone, a warmth of heartbeats like embers under ash—were not touched.

The word left her and took something with it. Her knees went strange. She sat down hard on the cobblestones beside her spilled water, and the world rang like a struck bell, and somewhere above the smoke a voice was screaming orders in the usurper's tongue, and none of it mattered very much, because the walls were still standing, and the people inside them were breathing.

A woman knelt beside her. Wren knew her vaguely—one of the fishwives from the market, broad-faced, smelling of brine and woodsmoke. The woman's mouth was moving. Sound came back slowly, in pieces.

"—girl. Girl! Are you hit? Did you see—what *was* that?"

Wren looked at her own hands. They were shaking. "I don't know," she said honestly, in the free-city tongue.

But the woman was looking at the wall. At the scorch mark that bent. And Wren watched understanding move across the fishwife's face like weather—not awe yet, not quite, but the raw material of it.

"You spoke," the woman said. "I heard you. It wasn't any language I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "They say there's a child. Down by the Founders' Quarter. They say a child speaks and things obey." Her eyes came back to Wren, and Wren saw the danger arrive a heartbeat after the wonder, the way it always did. "They're saying it on the streets."

"Please," Wren said.

"I'm not going to tell anyone," the woman said—which was, of course, telling someone—but she helped Wren up, and pressed a heel of black bread into her hands, and pushed her gently toward the alley that led back to the safehouse. "Go home, child. Go home and stay there."

Wren went. But even then, even with her legs trembling under her, some small stubborn part of her noted that the bread was warm, and that the woman had given it freely, and that this was the second time since the siege began that a stranger had fed her without being asked.

* * *

The safehouse—a cooper's cellar behind the salt market, reached through a trapdoor under a pile of rotting barrels—was chaos.

Dominic was there, which was a relief so large it made Wren's eyes sting. Dominic was always the fixed point, the one who stood still while everything else moved. But tonight even Dominic looked frayed, her gray-streaked hair escaping its braid, a bandage wrapped hastily around one forearm, arguing in a low fierce voice with Ferro over a map weighted down with candlesticks.

"—the eastern stair is gone, Ferro. Collapsed or collapsed *on purpose*, we won't know which until someone goes down it, and I am not sending anyone down it."

"The tunnel under the tannery is still open—"

"And it funnels straight into the Founders' Quarter, which is exactly where Corvane wants us funneling—"

Then Dominic saw Wren, and stopped, and the whole argument stopped with her, the way weather stops when a colder front moves in.

"You were supposed to be back before the bells," Dominic said.

"The aqueduct fell."

"I know the aqueduct fell. I could *see* the aqueduct fall." Dominic crossed the cellar in four strides and took Wren by the shoulders, turning her, checking her with quick rough hands the way she always did—scalp, collarbones, ribs—as if counting her back into existence. "Copper Lane," she said. "You were on Copper Lane."

"How do you—"

"Because half the city is talking about Copper Lane." Dominic's voice went flat and careful, which Wren had learned was worse than shouting. "A shell fell on a full street and the street is still standing. A shell fell and the fire *bent*." Her hands tightened. "What did you do?"

Wren's throat closed. She looked at Nan Hollis, who sat by the single lamp mending a coat with fingers that shook now more than they used to, and at Sable in the corner shadow, watching with those eyes that missed nothing, and at Ferro, and at last back at Dominic, whose face held everything Wren feared seeing in it: not anger. Fear. Fear *for* her, which was heavier than fear of her.

"It wanted to come out," Wren said. "And there were people behind the doors."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, another shell landed, and dust sifted down through the floorboards like gray snow.

Then Nan Hollis laughed—a soft, cracked, wondering laugh—and set down her mending. "Bent the fire," she said. "In my grandmother's day they'd have called that a *halden*. A holding-word. It takes years of training to shape one, girl. It takes—" She stopped, and her old face went strange. "It takes love of the thing you're holding. You can't force a wall to care whether people live or die. The word has to mean it."

"I didn't decide anything," Wren said. "It just—it knew what it wanted to be before I did."

"That," said Sable from the shadows, "is either the most beautiful or the most terrifying thing I've ever heard, and I genuinely cannot tell which." She stepped forward into the lamplight, thin and sharp-faced, and looked at Wren with an expression Wren couldn't read. "The whole city will know by morning. Not just the whisperers. Everyone. And everyone includes the usurper's ears."

"Then we move her," Ferro said immediately, bending over his map. "There's the route under the tannery—"

"We are not discussing routes yet," Dominic said, and her hand was still on Wren's shoulder, and it hadn't loosened. She crouched then, so her eyes were level with Wren's, and when she spoke her voice was low enough that only Wren could hear all of it. "Listen to me. What you did today saved lives. I need you to know that I know that. Whatever else happens, whatever rules I give you after this—I know what you did, and why, and there isn't a person alive I'd trust with that word more than you." A breath. "And because I know that, I also know it wasn't the last time. The word found you once in a burning street; it'll find you again. So we don't pretend this away. We get ahead of it. Understood?"

Wren nodded. Her eyes were hot. "I wasn't trying to be brave," she said. "I was just—there was nowhere else for the word to go except through me."

Dominic's mouth did the almost-smile that was the most she usually allowed herself. "That's what brave is," she said. "Mostly. Nobody tells you that part."

Above them, the bombardment rolled on through the night, shell after shell, and the cellar filled with the smell of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, underneath everything, the sea.

* * *

By the third day of the shelling, the city had given the miracle a name, though not hers.

They called her the Ash-Child. Or sometimes the Little Saint of Copper Lane, though the fishwife's account had grown in the telling until the child in it was wreathed in light and the fire had parted like the sea. Wren heard the stories secondhand, passed along the bucket-lines by Sable, who made a profession of hearing things: a baker swore the Ash-Child had walked through the burning Tanner's Row without singeing her hem. A militiaman swore he'd seen her hold a collapsing doorway with her bare hands and a word no Christian mouth should know. A widow from the Salt Gate swore the Ash-Child had sung her drowned son's name back to her across the water, and the son had answered.

That last one wasn't true. Wren was nearly sure it wasn't true. But she'd learned that in a besieged city the truth of a story mattered less than what it did to the people telling it, and what these stories did was make people look up. Make them speak to each other in queues and cellars. Make them, sometimes, hum snatches of songs they didn't know they knew—old songs, Nan's songs, songs with maps folded inside them—because a story about a child speaking miracles had reminded the city that it had a tongue in its history besides the usurper's.

"They're not worshipping you," Sable observed, perched on a barrel in the lamplight, sharpening nothing in particular. "They're remembering themselves. You're just the excuse." She flashed her thin grin. "It's the best kind of theft. You stole their fear and left them their pride."

"I didn't steal anything."

"No. That's why it works."

Dominic hated the stories. Wren could see it eating at her—the way she scanned every face in the bucket-lines for soldiers, the way she doubled the watch on the safehouse, the way she said, each night, *not tomorrow, Wren. Tomorrow you stay below,* and each day the city found new reasons to need otherwise. On the fifth day a granary caught fire near the river gate with forty people trapped in the square behind it, and Wren was three streets away when she felt the word rise, and this time she didn't fight it. She spoke the *halden* into the wind itself, and the wind changed its mind, and the fire went sideways into the river, and forty people lived, and Wren vomited from the cost of it behind a warehouse and let Ilva—of all people—find her there.

She hadn't known it was Ilva at first. Just a soldier's silhouette in sergeant's grays, sword at her hip, standing very still at the mouth of the alley while Wren knelt heaving in the dark. Every story Wren had been told about Sergeant Ilva ran through her head at once: the conscript who'd led house searches, the woman who'd carried warrants for linguists and their harborers, the hunter who had turned, once, quietly, and never fully explained why.

"She's with us," Dominic had said, weeks ago, in her flat careful way. "As much as anyone is with anyone."

Now Ilva crossed the alley, and what Wren expected was suspicion, or arrest, or at least the wary distance soldiers kept. Instead the sergeant crouched, pulled a water flask from her belt, and held it out without a word.

Wren rinsed her mouth. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking. "You shouldn't be seen with me," she managed. "If your side sees—"

"My side." Ilva said it like she was tasting something spoiled. She sat back on her heels, and up close Wren could see what the war had done to her face—new lines, a healing cut along the jaw, eyes that looked like they'd stopped sleeping sometime around the edict. "You want to know something, Ash-Child? Three years ago I kicked in a door in Harlow's Reach because a neighbor reported singing. Singing. An old woman, teaching her granddaughter a lullaby her mother taught her. I put that woman in a wagon." Ilva's voice stayed perfectly level, which somehow made it worse. "She thanked me. That's the part I can't get out of my head. She thanked me, because she thought politeness might keep the girl off the wagon too. It didn't."

Wren said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be smaller than the silence.

"For two years I told myself the warrants were just paper. Words on paper, and paper doesn't burn people." Ilva laughed, short and airless. "Then I got assigned to Corvane's staff, and I read what the paper was actually *for*. Not the speakers. Not even the harboring. A child. One specific child, and a door, and a sentence—and suddenly every burned village makes sense, every wagon, every interrogation over a single word. We weren't hunting a language." She looked at Wren then, directly, and Wren saw that behind the soldier's steadiness there was something raw and unfinished, a decision still bleeding at the edges. "We were hunting you. And I have spent every day since asking myself what I'm for, if not for this exact moment."

"What moment?" Wren whispered.

"This one." Ilva stood, and drew her sword—not pointing it, just holding it, looking at it as if weighing it—and then drove it into the dirt of the alley between them, up to the crossguard, and let go. The steel hummed and stilled. "Corvane has the Founders' Quarter surrounded. He knows the door is under the city—he's known longer than we guessed, and he's closing the net tomorrow night. Everything I've done until now was hiding in plain sight. Falsified reports. Missed signals. Small treasons." Her jaw worked. "Small treasons are over. When I walk back into that camp tonight and report that I lost the Ash-Child in the alleys, I'm reporting the last lie I get to tell as one of them. After that I'm yours. All the way. If you'll have me."

Wren stared at the sword standing upright in the ground between them, gleaming dully in the firelight, planted like a flag.

"You could still walk away," she said. "Nobody would know."

"I would know," Ilva said simply.

So Wren did the only thing she could think of, the thing that felt truest, the thing Nan's songs had taught her without her noticing: she reached out and gripped the sergeant's forearm, warrior-fashion, the way the old Aldermen were said to seal a promise, and she said the word for *witnessed*. Just that. One syllable of a dead country, spoken softly in a burning alley, making a promise official in the only court that still had jurisdiction.

Ilva flinched—then, slowly, smiled, and it transformed her whole battered face. "My grandmother used to say that," she murmured. "When I was small. I never knew what it meant." She retrieved her sword, sheathed it, and became a soldier again in a single breath, spine straight, eyes moving to the alley mouth. "Get below. Stay below tonight if you can manage it—tomorrow night you won't be able to. And tell Yarrow—" A pause, and something flickered across her face that Wren would spend days trying to name. "Tell Yarrow that Corvane has offered terms. Publicly, at noon. Pardon and restoration, for one child delivered to the Founders' Quarter." Her voice hardened. "He thinks he's setting a trap for her mercy. Help her make sure it's a trap for his instead."

Then she was gone, bootsteps fading into the roar of the distant fires, and Wren stood alone in the alley with warm bread in her pocket and a dead word still tingling in her palm, understanding for the first time that the stories were right in one way and wrong in everything: she was not a saint, and she had performed no miracles. She had only, finally, stopped hiding. And the city—broken, burning, magnificent—had begun to stop hiding with her.

She turned toward the trapdoor under the barrels, and froze.

Someone was watching from the roofline opposite. A figure, still as a gargoyle, half-lit by the flames—and even at that distance, even through the smoke, Wren knew the cut of that coat, the stillness of that attention. Corvane's shadow had been following her longer than she'd ever known.

The figure raised one hand—not in threat. In salute.

And was gone.
