## 27. Corvane's Trap

The bread-seller's stall smelled of ash and scorched grain, and Ilva stood before it with Wren's hand in hers, feeling the small bones of the child's fingers like a bundle of kindling she had been asked to feed to a fire.

"You don't have to do this," the bread-seller murmured, not looking up from her loaves. Her name was Marta, or so she claimed; Ilva had never asked twice. "There are other ways."

"There aren't." Ilva's voice was flat, a sergeant's voice, the voice that made conscripts move. Inside her chest something was screaming in a register no one else could hear. "Corvane expects me at the third bell. If I come without the girl, he sends someone else to find her, and whoever he sends won't be me. Won't care if she comes back breathing."

Wren tugged her hand. Ilva looked down. The child's face was pale under the grime of the undercity, but her eyes were steady—too steady for nine years old, steady the way old soldiers were steady after they'd already decided how they wanted to die.

"I can do it," Wren said. In Alderman, then again in the trade tongue, practicing both, because Nan Hollis had drilled into her that a speaker must always know which language she is standing in. "I've said it a hundred times. *I am lost, please help me find my way.* That's all I have to say. That's all I remember of being found."

"That's not all you'll say," Ilva said. "That's the point. You'll say nothing else. You'll let them think you're more broken than you are. Can you do that? Can you be small?"

Wren considered this with the terrible seriousness of children who have watched villages burn.

"Nan says I'm very good at being small," she said. "She says it's the first thing they teach Alderman children. Be small, be quiet, keep the words folded up inside where the damp can't get them." A pause. "She cried when she taught me that. Did you know? She said it was the only inheritance we had left, and it wasn't even ours."

Ilva's throat tightened. She crouched, brought her face level with the child's, and did the thing she had sworn she would never do with any child of this war: she lied to it gently.

"Three days," she said. "I will have you out in three days. Whatever happens in between, however bad it looks—you hold that number. Three days. I built the schedule myself. I know every rotation Corvane keeps, every guard he trusts, every door in his headquarters that doesn't lock properly because the usurper's engineers cheapened the iron. Three days."

"And if it goes wrong?"

Ilva held the child's gaze and did not blink, because blinking would have been a confession.

"Then you run," she said. "You run toward the river, you say the water-words Nan taught you, and the fishwives will hide you the way they hid their own daughters during the last purge. Do you remember the water-words?"

"*The river remembers what the stone forgets,*" Wren recited. "*And it carries its children home.*"

"Good girl." Ilva straightened. Her knees cracked; she was getting too old for crouching in bread stalls, for treason, for all of it. She took a loaf from Marta's tray without asking and pressed a coin into the woman's palm anyway, because whatever else Ilva had become, she had not become a thief. "Let's go meet the envoy."

* * *

Envoy Corvane kept his offices in the customs house, which Ilva had always thought fitting: a man whose entire soul was an audit. He sat behind a desk of black walnut imported from a country that no longer traded with the free cities, writing with a silver-nibbed pen, and he did not look up when they entered, because making people wait was the first instrument in his orchestra.

Ilva waited. She had learned patience in fifteen years of service to a man she'd never met, taking orders that filtered down through Corvane like rain through a corrupt roof. She stood at attention and watched dust motes drift through the slatted light and felt Wren's presence beside her like a coal wrapped in cloth.

Finally Corvane set down his pen.

"Sergeant Ilva." His voice was pleasant. It was always pleasant. It was the voice of a man describing the terms of a loan. "And this is the famous child."

"She came to me," Ilva said. "Two nights ago. Begging at the east gate. She'd heard the proclamations—that speakers would be fed and sheltered—and she was starving."

"Was she." Corvane rose and circled the desk. He was a slight man, grey at the temples, with the soft hands of someone who had never carried anything heavier than a pen. He stopped in front of Wren and studied her the way appraisers studied gems, looking for flaws, for inclusions, for the telltale sparkle of forgery. "Child. What is your name?"

"Wren," said Wren, and shrank, and made herself small, and it was masterful, Ilva thought, watching the performance from the corner of her eye—the hunched shoulders, the dropped chin, the eyes that went everywhere except to Corvane's face. A broken little thing. A stray.

"And where do you come from, Wren?"

"Mara's Ford." Barely a whisper. "It burned."

"Mara's Ford burned four years ago," Corvane said mildly.

The silence stretched. Ilva kept her breathing even by main force. This was the moment—the trap within the trap, the question designed to catch a rehearsed story in its own net.

"The soldiers came back," Wren whispered. "They came back last month. They were looking for—" her lip trembled, a flawless piece of theater— "for grandmama. She knew songs."

"Did she now." Corvane crouched, unhurried, until his eyes were level with the child's. "Sing me one."

*No.* Ilva's heart slammed once against her ribs. This hadn't been in the plan. The plan was that Corvane would take custody, preen over his capture, report to his master, and spend two days composing the announcement while Ilva worked the rotations. He was not supposed to test the merchandise on the doorstep.

But Wren—small, clever, brave Wren—did not sing. Instead she began to cry, great gulping sobs, the genuine article drawn up from some well of real grief Ilva didn't want to imagine, and between the sobs she said, in a ragged whisper:

"He hit grandmama when she sang. He said singing was how they found us. Please, sir. Please don't make me."

Corvane held still for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has found exactly the flaw he was looking for, or believes he has, and Ilva could not tell which truth lay beneath it.

"There, now," he said softly. "No one will hit anyone. Not here. Here you'll be given warm milk and a soft bed and all the bread you can eat, and in exchange, Wren, you're going to help me with a puzzle. Would you like that? Children like puzzles."

Wren sniffled and nodded, wretched and small and perfect.

"Take her to the third floor," Corvane said to a guard by the door. "The comfortable room. And Sergeant—a word."

The guard led Wren away. The child did not look back at Ilva, and Ilva understood why, and it nearly broke her: looking back was a thing accomplices did. She stood at attention and waited for the word.

Corvane returned to his desk but did not sit. He picked up his pen and turned it slowly, end over end, the way other men turned knives.

"You've served the edict faithfully for fifteen years," he said. "House-to-house searches. Interrogations. Deportations. I've read your file. It is, frankly, spotless. Which is precisely what troubles me." He looked up. "A soldier with a spotless record in a dirty war is either a liar or a saint, Sergeant, and I have met perhaps two saints in my life and neither of them volunteered children."

"I volunteer her because she's worth more fed than dead," Ilva said, and let a fraction of contempt bleed through, the contempt a career soldier might reasonably feel for a clerk playing spymaster. "You want speakers. I found you one. File your gratitude or file a complaint; either way, sign for the delivery."

Something flickered in Corvane's eyes—amusement, maybe, or appetite. "Sign for the delivery," he repeated. "Yes. I believe I shall." He drew a paper toward him and wrote briefly, then held it out. "Your commendation. Two hundred crowns and a posting of your choosing when this business concludes. You've earned rest, Sergeant."

Ilva took the paper. Her hands did not shake. Fifteen years of practice kept them still.

"One more thing," Corvane said as she reached the door, and the pleasantness in his voice had acquired a new temperature. "The girl's grandmother. Mara's Ford. Songs. It's remarkable, isn't it, how often the details of these strays rhyme with the ones who escaped us in the spring. Remarkable, and convenient, and I am a man who distrusts convenience almost as much as I distrust saints." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat against skin. "Enjoy your crowns, Sergeant. Spend them somewhere far away. Somewhere you can't hear the bells."

Ilva walked out of the customs house into the grey afternoon with the commendation crushed in her fist, and understood that Corvane suspected everything, and had let her walk out anyway—which meant the trap was no longer hers. It had simply changed owners, and the bait inside it was nine years old and drinking warm milk three floors above the harbor.

Three days, she had promised. She now gave them forty-eight hours, and prayed to no god in particular that she was pessimistic.

* * *

The half-speakers arrived that evening, and Ilva saw them from the window of the requisitioned barracks across the customs yard: four figures in the grey livery of the usurper's linguistic corps, escorted through the gate like visiting dignitaries, because that was what they were. The usurper's own broken-tongued priests, his tame fragments of Aldermere, collected from refugee columns and prison carts and wherever else the hunt had scraped them up.

She had seen one of them before. Years ago, in the interrogation rooms—a man called Vess, hollow-cheeked and soft-spoken, who could read a captive's accent the way Ferro read coastlines. They said he'd sold his own brother's location for the right to keep speaking indoors.

Now Vess stood in the customs house yard with three others, and Corvane came down to greet them personally, and Ilva, watching from behind a shutter with a spyglass she'd liberated from a dead lieutenant, saw him hand over a leather folio thick as a brick.

She knew that folio. She had helped inventory it, months ago, among the effects seized from the guild when Quillon's world came down: the guild's fragment-ledgers, pages torn from forbidden folios, scraps of Alderman verse copied out by clerks who hadn't known what they were copying.

Ferro had told her once, in the safehouse, tracing the door-sentence across his maps: *"The sentence is whole in no single place. It lives scattered—in Nan's songs, in Wren's blood-memory, in a dozen broken mouths. No one fragment is dangerous alone. That is our whole hope, Ilva. Their ignorance is our wall."*

And now Corvane had gathered every scrap his agents had stolen and put the pile in front of the one man alive who could count.

Ilva lowered the spyglass. Her mouth was dry.

Below, Vess opened the folio, and even at this distance she saw the change in him—the way his shoulders squared, the way his head lifted, the way a starving man looks when the smell of bread reaches him from a window. He began to read aloud, quietly, testing syllables against each other, and the other three leaned in, and Corvane watched them with his pleasant face tilted like a man listening to an orchestra tune itself.

One of the half-speakers—a young woman with a burn scar crawling up her neck—said something, and Vess shook his head, and pointed at a line, and she corrected him, and he wrote it down.

They were assembling it. Right there in the yard, in the open, with the confidence of people who had spent years being punished for their language and had finally been ordered, rewarded, *paid* to speak it fluently. The usurper had done in one decree what fifteen years of persecution could not: he had made Alderman profitable, and tongues that had hidden in cellars for a generation were coming out into the light and finding each other.

Ilva thought of Dominic Thackeray's face when she'd heard the plan—*the gamble*, Dominic had called it, *is that they can't finish the sentence without Wren. Her fluency is the keystone. Fragments don't make grammar.* And Wren had believed it, or had pretended to, because believing was part of being brave.

But Vess had grammar. Vess had always had grammar. All he'd ever lacked was vocabulary, and vocabulary was exactly what a brick-thick folio of stolen guild pages provided.

Ilva counted in her head. Forty hours, maybe less, before Corvane no longer needed Wren at all—before the sentence assembled itself out of theft and collaboration, and the child in the comfortable room became not a key but a witness. Something to be disposed of, quietly, once her testimony stopped being useful and started being dangerous.

She set down the spyglass. She had promised three days. She had forty hours, a falsified duty roster, a bread-seller who owed her nothing, and a stairwell in the customs house that she had walked a hundred times and knew every loose plank of.

Below in the yard, Vess laughed at something one of the others said—the first laugh Ilva had ever heard from him—and the sound drifted up thin and cold through the evening air.

Somewhere in the walls of the free cities, under the founders' stones, a door waited for a sentence. And in a lamplit room above the harbor, four exiles were building it out of stolen pieces, syllable by syllable, while the true key slept a floor above them, dreaming in a language only she could save.

Ilva pulled her coat tight and went downstairs to write the message that would start everything moving at once.

*Forty hours. Bring everyone. Bring the songs. We are out of time.*
