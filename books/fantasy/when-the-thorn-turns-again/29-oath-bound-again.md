## 29. Oath-Bound Again

The undercity smelled of wet stone and candle smoke and the particular staleness of air that had not moved in three hundred years. Dominic stood in the chamber beneath the Founders' Stones with her hands at her sides and felt every eye in the room settle on her like a weight she was finally willing to carry.

There were fourteen of them. Fourteen half-speakers, gathered in a rough crescent around the sealed threshold, their faces lit by tallow and by the faint grey shimmer that leaked from the Door's seams even now, even shut. A fishwife whose Alderman grandmother had hummed to her in a tongue she'd been beaten for repeating. A customs clerk who'd spent thirty years transcribing numbers he didn't know were prayers. Two brothers from the salt flats who between them held perhaps forty words of the old language and guarded each word like coin. And at the crescent's center, small and solemn and watching Dominic with those too-old eyes, Wren.

Ferro had cleared the chamber of everything but candles. Sable stood near the stair with her arms crossed, her face carefully blank—the look she wore when something mattered so much she couldn't afford to show it. Ilva had posted herself at the top of the steps with two of her defectors, listening for boots on stone. Nan Hollis was dead nine days, buried on the ridge above the harbor with her feet pointing east toward a country none of them could see, and her absence filled the room more thoroughly than her presence ever had.

Dominic had asked for this. That was the strange part. Fifteen years of burying the gift, fifteen years of ledgers and silence and the careful, practiced nothing of her voice—and now she had asked to stand in front of strangers and swear aloud.

"You don't have to do it fancy," Ferro had said, when she told him. "The old oaths were plain. That's what made them hold."

Plain, then. She could manage plain. She had managed nothing for fifteen years; plain should be easy.

Wren came forward first, as they had arranged. The girl carried the iron key on a cord around her neck, and the sigil stamped into it caught the candlelight—the thorn-and-gate, the mark Dominic had known since childhood, since her mother's ring and her grandmother's ring before that, since the day she'd learned what her family name meant written in a forbidden ledger: *paid in full, sealed by oath.*

"Wren," Dominic said. "You'll speak the invocation. I can't—I won't be the one to open this. But if you'll call the words, I'll answer them."

Wren nodded. She had grown steadier since the market, since the burning streets, since the night the whole city began whispering about a child who spoke miracles. Steadier, and somehow younger at the same time—she had stopped pretending to be older than she was, which was its own kind of courage. She looked up at Dominic now without fear, which was the thing that undid her most.

"Say it however it comes," Dominic said quietly. "I'll follow."

Wren closed her eyes. When she spoke, it was in Alderman, and the sound went through the chamber like wind through wheat—not loud, but everywhere at once, touching every surface, every ear. Dominic did not need translation. The words lived in her bones, in the part of her she'd spent fifteen years starving. *Who stands? Who speaks? What is sworn, and to what end?*

The old challenge. The opening of every swordspeaker's rite, the question her mother had answered on her knees in a house that no longer existed, in a country erased from every map.

Dominic knelt.

The stone was cold through her trousers. She bowed her head, and for a moment—only a moment—the silence rose in her throat the way it always did, that familiar deadness where the gift used to live, the burned-out hollow left by one word spoken in terror when she was eleven years old. She had thought that hollow was who she was. She had built a life inside it, brick by careful brick, and called the building safety.

*Who stands?*

She lifted her head.

"I stand," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word because she had not said *I stand* as anything but an accounting entry in fifteen years. "Dominic Thackeray. Daughter of Maren Thackeray, sealing signatory of the Door of Aldermere. Last sworn of the Guild of Impossible Things." A breath. "Last swordspeaker."

Around the crescent, the half-speakers stirred. Old Marta, the fishwife, made a small sound in her throat—an Alderman word, soft, that Dominic recognized as a blessing for the newly named. Her mother had used it over her once, in another lifetime.

*What is sworn?*

This was the hinge. This was why they were all here, why Wren's hands trembled around the key, why Sable wouldn't watch and Ferro wouldn't breathe. Because the sentence needed an oath-breaker in the doorway—that much they understood now, from the fragments, from Nan's last verse, from Quillon's final page smuggled out of a burning guildhouse. And because the door demanded not the old oath renewed word for word but a new one, freely chosen, and no one had chosen anything freely in this story for a very long time.

"The old oath was to seal," Dominic said, and her voice found itself as she went, growing plainer and stronger, the way Ferro had promised. "My family swore it. Their families before them. We sealed the door to keep something from following the exiles out, and we paid for that seal with our voices, and I—" the word caught, and she let it catch, let the room hear it catch— "I broke mine. I killed with a word when I was a child, and I put my gift away like a knife in a drawer, and I told myself silence was penance. It wasn't. It was just silence."

Nobody moved. Candleflame bent in a draft from somewhere deep in the stone.

"So this is the new oath," Dominic said. "Not to seal. Not to open. To guard. Whatever comes through that door—hunger or harvest, song or sorrow—I stand between it and these people, and these people stand behind me, and neither side passes the other without my word. I will not close what is opened. I will not open what is closed. I will hold the threshold until I am relieved, or until I am nothing, whichever comes first." She looked at Wren. "And when I am gone, the oath passes to whoever chooses it next. Freely. As I choose it now."

*Freely,* Wren repeated in Alderman, translating not the words but the meaning, giving the assembled half-speakers the shape of it. *Freely, freely.*

Then Wren spoke the final line of the challenge, and Dominic knew it was coming, had known since Nan taught them the rite's bones, and still it landed in her chest like a fist:

*And what do you bring to the door?*

The old answer—the answer her mother gave, the answer carved into the ledger—was *my voice, in payment.* The swordspeakers had paid with their speech, had poured their gifts into the threshold to make it hold. That was the price. That was the trap. If Dominic answered the old way, she would be swearing herself back into exactly the bargain that had hollowed out four generations of her family, the bargain that turned her mother into a woman who hummed lullabies she couldn't finish and looked at her daughter sometimes like she was trying to remember how.

But the door was shut now. The price had been paid in full. What remained was not payment.

What remained was service.

"I bring my voice," Dominic said, "not in payment. In keeping."

The words left her mouth and the world changed.

It started as heat—not pain, not yet, just warmth blooming in the hollow place, the way blood comes back to a limb that has slept too long, terrible and sweet at once. Dominic gasped. Her hands pressed flat against the cold stone. Somewhere far away someone cried out—Sable, maybe, or Marta—and somewhere closer Wren said her name, but the sound reached her down a long corridor of ringing light.

Because the hollow was filling. Fifteen years of ash and silence, and underneath it all, impossibly, the gift had not died. Burned down to the root, yes—scorched, starved, buried—but roots are patient things. And an oath spoken truly is water finding its way down through cracked ground.

The warmth became pressure. The pressure became sound. Not in her ears—in her chest, in her teeth, in the stone beneath her palms, a deep harmonic thrumming like the Door itself clearing its throat after three centuries of sleep. The grey shimmer in the threshold's seams brightened. The candles guttered and stood back up. Every half-speaker in the crescent was on their feet now, and every one of them had gone pale, because they could feel it too—the language waking in the room like a sleeper turning over, vast and slow and aware.

"Dominic." Wren's hand on her shoulder. Small, warm, steady. "Breathe. You have to give it somewhere to go or it'll burn you again."

Somewhere to go. Yes. She knew this, dimly, from childhood—from watching her mother speak the small domestic miracles, the mended cup, the calmed horse, the fever cooled. A swordspeaker's power wanted motion. Held still, it ate its holder.

Dominic opened her eyes, found the nearest thing—Marta's shawl, frayed at the hem, salt-stiffened—and spoke.

One word. A mending-word, a low warm syllable that she hadn't shaped since she was a child and had never expected to shape again.

The shawl's frayed hem wove itself whole. The salt stiffness fell out of the wool like water. Marta made a sound that was half sob and half laugh and clutched the shawl to her chest, and the room exhaled, and Dominic sat back on her heels and shook.

It was such a small miracle. A shawl. Fifteen years ago she would have given anything to never feel that heat again, would have traded her right hand for assurance she'd never speak a working word. Now she knelt on cold stone with tears running openly down her face because a shawl had been mended, because the thing inside her had come home, because she had said *in keeping* instead of *in payment* and the difference between those two sentences was the difference between a cage and a post.

"It doesn't hurt," she said, mostly to herself. "It—it aches. Like a scar in rain. But it doesn't hurt."

"Scars ache when the weather changes," Ferro said from the edge of the crescent, his voice rough. "Means the weather's changing."

Sable crossed the room. She did it quickly, before she could decide not to, and crouched in front of Dominic, and for once her face wasn't blank at all. "Say something else," she said. "Anything. I want to see your eyes when you do it."

Dominic laughed—a broken, wet sound—and obliged her. She spoke a warming-word over Sable's ink-stained fingers, stiff from the vaults and the cold, and watched Sable's expression flicker through wonder and grief and something fierce and tender all braided together.

"There she is," Sable said quietly. "Fifteen years I've been trading in other people's memories, and I forgot you could just—make a new one."

The formal rite concluded with the old responses, each half-speaker speaking their fragment in turn, and it was clumsy and imperfect and some of them wept and some of them laughed and the two salt-flat brothers argued softly over whose turn it was to say which line, and it was, Dominic thought, the truest ceremony she had ever witnessed—far truer than anything performed in marble halls, because every person in it was risking something real.

When it was done, the Door had not opened. But its seams glowed steady now, faint as dawn through shutters, and the chamber no longer felt like a tomb. It felt like a held breath.

Ilva came down the stairs two at a time while they were still gathering candles. Her face told the news before her mouth did.

"Scouts on the ridge road," she said. "A full column behind them—banners I recognize from the vanguard. They'll invest the walls by tomorrow night." She paused, and her jaw worked. "And there's something else. A prisoner exchange happened at the Eastgate this morning. Corvane walked out with six of ours—half-speakers we'd been sheltering in the tannery quarter." She met Dominic's eyes. "He left a message. He knows about the rite. He says the usurper offers terms one last time, and he says—" her voice flattened with disgust— "he says to tell the keeper of the door that the country remembers her family's service, and rewards it."

Silence took the chamber, the bad kind, the kind with fear in it.

Dominic got to her feet. Her legs held. Her gift hummed in her chest, alive, awake, aching like a scar in changing weather—and she realized, standing there with candle smoke and enemy banners and a child's hand finding hers, that for the first time in fifteen years she was not afraid of her own voice.

"Tomorrow night," she said. "Then we have today. Wren—we've work to do. There's a sentence we haven't finished, and a door that's waiting to hear it." She squeezed the small hand in hers. "And tonight—" her voice softened, surprising her— "tonight we eat together. All of us. Before whatever comes."

Above them, muffled by three hundred years of stone, the bells of the free cities began to ring the alarm.
