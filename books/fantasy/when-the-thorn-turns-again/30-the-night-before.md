## 30. The Night Before

The safehouse smelled of mended wool and onion broth, and for one evening it smelled like a home.

Dominic Thackeray noticed it first as she came down the narrow stair: the particular warmth of too many bodies in too small a room, the good kind of crowded, the kind that pressed against her skin like a hand she had spent fifteen years refusing to hold. Below, in the lamplight, her strange family was doing nothing at all—and doing it together.

Sable sat cross-legged on the floor, stitching a tear in Wren's coat with the same deft fingers that could pick a memory out of a man's skull like a splinter. She sewed badly, Dominic noted, but she sewed anyway, tongue between her teeth, and every few stitches she glanced up to make sure the girl hadn't drifted out of reach. Ferro had spread his maps across two crates pushed together—a table now, a country now—and was arguing softly with Ilva about whether the Alderman road that ran beneath Tanner's Row had been paved or packed earth. Ilva argued because she loved to argue; Ferro argued because he loved his dead country; and underneath both arguments lay the same tenderness, the tenderness of people who had agreed, without ever saying so, that tomorrow they might not have this.

Nan Hollis sat by the fire in the good chair they'd carried up three flights for her, wrapped in two blankets despite the season, watching them all the way an old woman watches a garden she will not live to see harvested.

Wren was at her knee.

That was where Wren always was, these last weeks—knee-height beside the old woman, learning. The child's lips moved through the verses without sound, the way she'd been taught, the way speakers of a hunted language learned to carry words inside their mouths like smuggled coins. But tonight Nan kept saying, "Out loud, dove. Tonight you may be loud as you like."

Dominic stood on the stairs a moment longer, letting herself memorize it. She was good at memorizing; it was half of what a swordspeaker's discipline amounted to. The light on the copper pot. Sable's crooked seam. The way Ilva, still in her borrowed civilian coat after months out of uniform, sat with her boots planted like a soldier even when she was only drinking tea.

*Tomorrow,* said the cold voice at the back of her skull, *the walls come down.*

*Yes,* answered the newer voice, the one she was still learning to trust. *And tonight is ours.*

She came down the rest of the stairs and joined them.

* * *

The meal was not much. It never was, anymore—the city's granaries were under army requisition, the markets half-empty, bread sold from carts at hours set by rumor. But Nan Hollis had insisted on cooking, and none of them had been fool enough to stop her. So there was onion broth thickened with barley, and a heel of hard cheese Ferro had traded three days' cartography work to obtain, and apples gone soft at one end that Sable trimmed away without comment, and a jar of plum preserves that no one would admit to having hoarded and everyone quietly understood had come from Sable.

They ate sitting on everything—crates, stools, the floor, Nan's bed. Ilva tore her bread the way soldiers do, half and half, saving one piece. Wren ate with both hands and got broth on her chin and nobody minded, least of all Wren.

"Tell the one about the eel," Wren demanded, mouth full. "Nan, tell the eel one."

"I've told the eel one four times."

"Fifth time's the best one."

Nan laughed—a real laugh, deep in the chest, though it caught at the end and turned into something wetter that she disguised as coughing. Dominic saw Sable see it. Sable's hands went still over her bowl, and then resumed, and she said nothing, because some things are known best in silence.

"The eel one," Nan said, settling back. "Well. In Aldermere—in the true Aldermere, before any of us were born, mind—there was a fishwife named Berta who caught an eel so long it took three men to hold it. And the eel spoke to her. In those days everything spoke, if you knew how to listen. And the eel said, *Woman, I have swum the length of your river and I have seen your whole country, top to bottom, and I will tell you its shape if you let me go.*"

"And she let it go," Wren said, delighted, already knowing.

"She let it go. And the eel told her the country's shape." Nan held up her knotted hands and drew them slowly through the air, a long curve, a narrowing, a curl at the end like a question mark. "And Berta taught the shape to her daughters, and they taught it to theirs, and that's why we dance the eel-dance at harvest, hands just so—" her fingers curled, "—so the country remembers itself. A country is a body, dove. You keep it alive the way you keep any body alive. You feed it. You sing to it. You say its name."

"Its name," Wren repeated softly, and said it then—not aloud, but almost aloud, a breath shaped around the word, and the fire flickered once, gently, like something turning over in sleep. Everyone felt it. Nobody flinched. They were past flinching, most of them. They were learning to feel the language move in the room the way you feel weather change.

Ferro put down his cup. "I've mapped that curve," he said quietly. "Berta's eel. The eastern coast, where the land curls. I have it from a shipping ledger out of Corvess, 140 years old, cargo manifests from a port that isn't on any chart. I never knew why the coastline haunted me." He looked at his own hands, big and ink-stained. "Now I know I've spent my whole life drawing a place I'd never seen, and it turns out the place has been dreaming me back."

"That's how it works with countries," Nan said. "They're patient."

Ilva snorted—not scornfully; it was her version of agreement. "The usurper's not patient. His engineers have been surveying the north wall since dawn. I watched the mortar crews from the cistern roof." She said it plainly, the way she said everything, because Ilva believed that fear unspoken grew teeth. "One day, maybe two, before they bring down the Founders' Quarter gate. Maybe less if they don't bother with the gate."

"Then we'll be ready," Dominic said.

"Ready," Sable murmured, threading her needle, "is a strong word for twelve half-speakers, a thief, a mapmaker, a deserter, and a woman who talks to doors."

"It's the only word we've got," Dominic said, and to her own surprise she smiled when she said it, and Sable smiled back, and that was the whole of the argument, because they had all made their peace with the arithmetic long ago.

* * *

After the bowls were scraped, the confessions began—which is to say, nothing so formal as confession, but the loose, low talking that comes when people sense a threshold ahead and want to step across it unburdened.

It started, oddly enough, with Ferro, who was the shyest of them and therefore the bravest tonight. He cleared his throat twice, which everyone pretended not to notice, and then said, to the middle distance rather than to anyone, "When they struck me off the Cartographers' Register, I thought my life was finished. Eleven years I worked on Aldermere after that, alone, in a rented room, telling myself it was scholarship. It wasn't scholarship. It was grief with nowhere to go. I didn't know whose grief. My grandmother hummed songs I thought were just songs." He turned his cup in his hands. "You lot gave the grief somewhere to go. That's all. That's what I wanted to say."

Nobody made it awkward. Sable refilled his cup. Ilva bumped his shoulder with hers, once, hard, which from Ilva was an embrace.

Then Ilva herself, staring into the fire: "I arrested a baker in Marrowgate. Third year of my service. He'd taught his daughter a counting rhyme the man who wrote the report called 'suspicious phonetics.' I wrote the report." Her jaw worked. "The girl was six. I've thought about her face every day since I met Wren, and I'll think about it every day after tomorrow, whichever way it goes. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm saying it out loud so it stays said."

"You put yourself on the stair at the Founders' Stones," Dominic said, "with five people against a battalion. That's not erased. Nothing's erased. But it's weighed."

Ilva nodded once, accepting the sentence, whatever it was.

Sable, when her turn came—or rather, when she chose to take it, because Sable was never taken anywhere—was quieter than usual. "I've stolen a hundred memories," she said. "Maybe more. Faces, first loves, the names of children, the smell of someone's mother's kitchen. I told myself it was survival. Sometimes it was. Mostly—" she looked at the fire, and for a moment Dominic saw the girl Sable must have been, before the guild vaults got their hooks in her, "—mostly I stole because taking something from someone else meant I didn't have to look at what I'd sold of myself." She glanced sideways at Dominic. "I got it back. From the vault. You were there. But getting it back isn't the same as being done paying for it. I know that. Tomorrow, whatever happens—I want to spend the rest of mine buying things *back*. For other people."

"That's a guild worth founding," Dominic said.

"Don't make it sentimental," Sable said, but her voice cracked on *sentimental*, and Wren leaned over and put her head on Sable's shoulder, and Sable let her, and that settled it.

* * *

Later, when the others had drifted to their bedrolls and the fire had burned down to a red patience of coals, Nan Hollis called Wren to her chair one last time.

Dominic did not mean to overhear. She was keeping the watch by the window out of habit older than her oath, and the room was small, and Nan's voice had gone thin and clear as a wire.

"One verse left, dove. The hardest one. The one I've been saving."

"I'm ready," Wren said, and her voice was steady in a way that made Dominic's chest ache, because the child had earned steadiness the way soldiers earn scars.

"This verse isn't in any song I've sung you. My mother gave it to me the night before she died, and hers gave it to her the night before *she* died, and that's how it travels—from the dying to the young, because it's too heavy to carry long. It's the last clause of the opening sentence. The door won't wake without it. And it can't be spoken by just anyone." Nan's breathing rasped, evened. "Listen."

And Nan sang.

Her voice, which had been failing for weeks, gathered itself the way a wave gathers—out of depths nobody could see—and filled the little room with the old tongue, the true tongue, the language of a drowned country. Dominic did not understand the words. She understood the shape of them: a promise, a hinge, a handing-over. The coals brightened. The frost ferns on the window glass melted and re-formed into patterns like ferns, like feathers, like the curl of Berta's eel drawn in ice.

Wren sang it back. Once to learn it. Twice to hold it. The third time, she sang it alone while Nan listened, and when the last note settled, the fire stood up straight for a moment, every flame perfectly still, like soldiers presenting arms.

"There," Nan whispered. "Now it lives in you. Now I can put my pack down."

"Nan." Wren's voice wobbled for the first time. "Nan, I don't want—"

"Hush. None of that. Listen instead. When I'm done with this body, I want burying facing east-northeast. There's a hill above the harbor road, if the fighting lets you get there; if not, any high ground will serve. Face me toward Aldermere, dove. I've spent sixty years walking west. I'd like to walk home lying down." A dry chuckle. "And tell the ground what it's receiving. It likes to be told. Everything likes to be told—it's the whole secret of the world, and I've kept it sixty years and I give it to you free."

"What do I tell it?"

"Tell it: *here lies Hollis of the Hollis line, who remembered.*" Nan's hand found Wren's hair, stroked it once, twice, slowing. "That's all any of us ever were, in the end. Rememberers. The country couldn't be killed while even one of us hummed in the dark. You go on humming, dove. Loud as you like."

"Nan?"

But Nan Hollis had already begun the longest walk of her life, westward, eyes closed, smiling, her breath going out slow and unhurried as a tide, and it did not come back in.

Wren did not cry loudly. She climbed onto the arm of the chair and fit herself against the old woman's side and stayed there, singing the last verse over and over in a thread of sound, soft as a pulse, so that the room would not be silent, so that the remembering would not stop. One by one the others woke or came near—Sable first, then Ferro, then Ilva standing very straight with her fists at her sides—and finally Dominic, who knelt and closed the old woman's eyes and found them warm still, and thought: *sixty years of carrying, laid down in a chair, surrounded. There are worse ends. There are almost no better ones.*

They buried her at dawn, on the hill above the harbor road, exactly as she'd asked, because even a besieged city keeps its promises about the dead. The fighting had not yet reached that far. The ground received Hollis of the Hollis line, who remembered. Wren told it so, in the old tongue, loud as she liked, and the wind coming off the sea carried the words inland toward the walls, toward the undercity, toward the door.

They walked back down the hill into a city holding its breath.

At the bottom of the harbor road, a boy sprinted toward them, barefoot, wild-eyed, one of the runner-children Ilva had paid in bread to watch the northern works. He skidded to a stop, gulping air.

"The north gate," he gasped. "The usurper's sappers lit the charges early—they're bringing it down *now*. And there's more—" he swallowed, looked at each of their faces, "—they're not stopping at the walls. Torches in the Old Quarter. They've found the Founders' Stones. They're coming down."
