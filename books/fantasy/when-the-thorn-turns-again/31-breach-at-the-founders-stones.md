## 31. Breach at the Founders' Stones

The stair had been built to be defended.

That was the first thing Ilva had understood about it, three days ago, when Ferro had pressed his palm against the oldest of the founders' stones and shown her how the steps narrowed as they descended—each one a handspan tighter than the last, until at the bottom a single defender with a spear could hold back ten men. The free cities' first builders had known that someday someone would come down here hunting something they wanted kept. They had left the stair like a promise: *we thought of you. Hold.*

She was holding it now with six people and half a torch.

Above her, muffled through forty feet of rock and old mortar, the bombardment of the upper city went on like a heart that would not stop beating. The stone dust sifted down between the cracks in the vaulting with each impact, a fine grey rain that settled in her hair, in the corners of her eyes, in the wounds she'd bandaged on Tam and on the boy called Merrit who could not have been more than seventeen and who lied about it badly. Below her, past the landing where she stood, the threshold waited under its carved sigil—the Door of Aldermere, cold as riverbed stone, with Dominic Thackeray already standing in front of it like a woman reading her own gravestone.

And behind Ilva, up the stair, coming down through the breach Ferro's collapse had not been fast enough to seal, were soldiers of the usurper's Fourth Column. Her column. Her former column. Men whose names she knew, whose boots she'd marched beside for nine years, coming down into the dark with lanterns and grappling lines and warrants signed in a hand she used to salute.

"They've got the second landing," Tam said. He was crouched at the top of the narrow run, spear braced against the ceiling, listening the way old sappers listen—with his whole spine. "Two dozen, maybe. They're being careful."

"Careful is good," Ilva said. "Careful is slow."

"Careful with sappers isn't slow." Tam looked back at her, and in the guttering light his face was all shadows and one bright line of fear. "They'll come down through the roof of the lower gallery if they can't take the stair. Ferro said—"

"Ferro says a lot of things." She heard her own voice go flat, the sergeant's voice, the voice that had ordered conscripts into breaches for the better part of a decade, and she hated how easily it still came. "How many can we hold?"

"At this width? Three abreast, four if they're friends with each other."

"Then we hold it until it doesn't matter anymore. That's the whole order. That's everything I have for you." She moved up the steps past him, shouldering into the gap beside the youngest defector, a fisherman's daughter named Brey who held her blade like it was a tool she hadn't decided whether she trusted. "When they come, we don't chase. We don't shout. We let the stair do its work and we put steel where steel has to go. Nobody dies for pride down here. Understood?"

Nobody answered, which was the most honest answer she'd ever gotten from troops before a fight.

* * *

The first rush came with lantern-light swinging wildly, and it came stupid after all—which meant the careful ones were still setting their sapper charges somewhere above, and what hit the stair now was the impatient rank, the ones who'd drawn lots and lost and wanted it over with.

The stair did its work.

It was ugly and close and nothing like the battles in the songs, because battles in the songs happened somewhere you could see. This was a knife-fight in a chimney, lantern-glow strobing off wet stone, the man in front of Ilva so near she could smell the rosemary they rubbed in their hair against lice. He recognized her. That was the worst of it—he *recognized* her, and his face did something complicated, surprise and betrayal and then nothing at all because Brey's blade took him high in the chest and he folded backward into the men behind him, and his lantern went out on the steps, and they were fighting by the light of a fire that was drowning.

"Push them up! Push!" She didn't know if the stair could be held by pushing; she only knew that bodies tumbling uphill broke formations faster than blades did. For a hundred heartbeats it worked. The press above them gave, cursed, retreated to the second landing, and the defectors re-formed in the narrowing dark, bleeding, alive, three of seven still whole enough to stand a line.

Then the hammering started overhead.

Not boots. Not voices. A rhythm—*chunk, chunk, chunk*—patient as a carpenter, and Ilva's stomach dropped through the floor because she knew that sound from the other side of a hundred sieges. Picks. Iron picks, working through the vault crown of the lower gallery, opening the shortcut Tam had feared, the one that would come down into the open passage behind the stair and turn their beautiful killing-ground into just another corridor with soldiers at both ends.

"Ferro," she said, to no one. "Ferro, tell me you hear this."

"He hears it." The voice came thin and strange along the passage below, carried up from the mouth of the side tunnel where Ferro had spent two days with chalk lines and sacks of guild-powder, muttering numbers like prayers. "He has heard it since noon. He asks—" a pause, and Sable's voice finished the relay, arriving breathless at Ilva's shoulder, "—he asks how long you can keep them on the stair."

"Ten minutes if they're fools. Five if they're not."

Sable was quiet a moment. In the dark her face was mostly eyes, and the eyes were doing arithmetic Ilva didn't want to watch. "He said he'd need to know before the roof came down. He said—" She stopped. Started again, flatter, the thief's flatness she used when she was carrying something heavy and didn't want it seen. "He said to tell you the charges are set for the eastern spur, and the eastern spur is on his side of the gallery, and he's not going to have time to come back around once he lights it."

The words took a moment to arrange themselves into sense, and Ilva felt them arrange themselves the way you feel a floor give.

"No," she said.

"He asked me not to argue with you about it. He said you'd want to send someone, and everyone we have is on that stair, and the math is the math." Sable's voice didn't waver, but her hands, Ilva noticed, were closed hard around each other. "He said to tell Wren—" and there she stopped entirely, and stood, and for the first time since Ilva had known her, Sable the memory-thief had nothing to say.

*Chunk. Chunk. Chunk.* Above them the usurper's picks ate through the founders' city, patient as hunger.

"I'm going down there," Ilva said.

"You're going to hold the stair," Sable said. "Because that's the only thing standing between those men and a child, and because Ferro made me swear an oath I intend to keep, which is that nobody follows him, and nobody wastes his death by dying next to it." She reached out—quick, awkward, unpracticed—and gripped Ilva's forearm, mapmaker's calluses against soldier's. "He's been walking toward this his whole life, Ilva. You must know that. A man spends thirty years drawing a country that isn't on any map, and somewhere inside he's already decided the map is worth more than he is. All we can do is make sure he wasn't wrong."

Ilva stood in the dark with a dead man's errand in her ears and the sound of picks overhead, and she was a soldier, and soldiers know the math too, that was the whole obscenity of the trade—they taught you the math young so you'd never be able to unlearn it. The math said the stair. The math said five minutes. The math said that the only gift she had left to give Ferro Ash was to be exactly where he needed her to be when the world came apart.

"Hold the stair," she said, and her voice broke on the second word, and she let it break, because some things deserved to cost the person giving the order too.

* * *

They came again, and this time they were not fools.

Someone above had finally organized the assault properly—shields first, a wedge, the lanterns doused so the defenders' eyes would carry the darkness up the stairs toward them. It was textbook. It was, Ilva realized with a strange distant grief, *her* textbook; she'd written half the entries in the Fourth Column's assault manual during the marsh campaigns, and now she stood at the bottom of her own doctrine waiting for it to arrive.

The wedge came down and the stair drank it. Brey fought like someone remembering she'd done this before; Tam took a spear-graze across the jaw and didn't fall; Merrit, seventeen, killed a man older than his father and began to sob without stopping the work of his hands, which was the most soldierly thing Ilva had ever seen anyone do. Twice the wedge broke. Twice it reformed above them. And all the while the picks overhead went *chunk, chunk, chunk*, closer now, plaster sifting down like snow, and Ilva counted minutes against the roof and lost track and counted again.

Somewhere below, in the black of the eastern spur, a man who had spent his life redrawing a murdered country struck flint to powder fuse.

"Down!" she screamed—not at the enemy, at her own people. "Off the stair, down, DOWN—"

The world turned sideways.

There was no word for the sound. It was not loud so much as *total*, a single concussion that erased every other sound including the ones inside her own skull, and the stair beneath her feet bucked like a living thing, and dust came down in a solid wall, grey and total, erasing lantern-light, erasing the wedge of shields above, erasing the vault crown and the picks and the men holding the picks. The eastern spur had taken the gallery roof with it. The shortcut died unopened.

When the dust began to thin—minutes later, hours, she never knew—Ilva crawled back up the cracked steps on hands and knees with her ears roaring and found the second landing simply gone. Where the upper stair had run there was now a slope of rubble rising into darkness, tumbled stone packed tight, still warm through her glove when she laid her hand on it. Air moved somewhere deep in the mass but nothing else did. No picks. No boots. No voices calling for diggers.

The stair was sealed. From above. Which meant—

Which meant the way in was also the way out, and the usurper's column was entombed on the wrong side of forty feet of broken founders' stone, and so was the man who had put it there.

"Ferro," she said. Her voice came out of her ruined ears as vibration more than sound. She said it again anyway. She put both fists against the warm rubble and said it a third time, and the third time was when Tam caught her shoulders and pulled her back, gently, the way you handle someone you're afraid will shatter, and Brey stood aside with her helmet off and tears cutting clean tracks through the dust on her face, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing true left to say.

He had known. He had chalked his numbers and packed his powder and known, and he had lit it anyway, and the last thing the world had of Ferro Ash was thirty years of maps and a tunnel that would hold shut long enough for a child to live.

* * *

They came down from the stair slowly, seven of the nine who had held it, moving through a corridor that had become a different place—cracked vaulting, drifted dust ankle-deep, the sigil-carved threshold glowing faintly ahead where Dominic Thackeray stood exactly where she had stood an hour or a lifetime ago. The door was stirring. Ilva could feel it in her teeth, a low subsonic hum, the sound of something vast turning over in its sleep behind a slab of stone. Whatever was happening at that door had already begun, and it had begun because a mapmaker had bought them the time for it with himself.

Wren broke from the group gathered at the threshold and ran to Ilva before anyone could stop her, and the child looked up at her with the terrible directness children have, and asked, "Where's Ferro?" and Ilva discovered that she had prepared lies for this moment and could not use any of them.

"He sealed the tunnel," she said. "He sealed it with himself on the far side, and it's holding, and everyone on that stair is alive because of him."

Wren looked at her a long moment. Then she did something Ilva had not expected: she spoke a sentence in the dead tongue, low and formal, the syllables falling into the humming air like stones into deep water, and the whole undercity seemed to lean toward the sound. Nan Hollis would have known the words. Ilva didn't. But she saw Dominic at the threshold turn, and she saw the expression on the swordspeaker's scarred face—grief recognizing grief in a language older than either of them—and she understood that whatever Wren had said, it was the kind of thing you said over graves.

"It means *the road remembers who walked it*," Wren said, in the free cities' tongue, for Ilva's benefit. Then she turned back toward the door, toward the growing hum, toward whatever was coming.

Behind them, far above, muffled now through sixty feet of collapsed rock instead of forty, new sounds had begun. Not picks this time. Voices. Many voices, and the groan of machinery, and the unmistakable grinding of heavy stone being levered and hauled—the sound of an army deciding that if it could not come down the stair, it would remove the mountain the stair lived in.

And ahead, under the founders' stones, the Door of Aldermere began, for the first time in fifteen years, to open.
