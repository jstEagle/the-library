## 32. The Door Stands Open

The last word left Wren's mouth and did not fade.

That was the first thing Dominic noticed—that the language refused to end. Fifteen years of silence had taught her that words were like sparks: brief, bright, gone. But the opening sentence of Aldermere hung in the undercity air the way smoke hangs after a fire, and it went on hanging, and hanging, until she understood that it wasn't lingering at all. It was *arriving*. Somewhere ahead of them, something was hearing it for the first time.

The Founders' Stones chamber held its breath.

She stood at the center of a half-circle of half-speakers—an old woman with a cooper's forearms, a boy who had only ever known three words of his grandmother's tongue, Sable with her stolen memories folded tight behind her eyes, Nan Hollis's last verse still warm in Wren's throat. Twelve voices, some fluent, some broken, all of them trembling. And in front of them, set into the living rock beneath the oldest quarter of the free cities, the threshold.

It had been cold for centuries. Ferro's maps had promised as much; Quillon's final ledger page had confirmed it: *sealed shut, paid in full.* The stone door was carved with the sigil Dominic hadn't seen since childhood—the thorned circle, the root that crossed itself—and for fifteen years of her life it had been nothing but a rumor buried under other rumors, under guild secrets and burned villages and warrants for linguists and their harborers.

Now it was warm.

She could feel it from six feet away, a heat like a hearth through a wall, like skin remembered rather than touched. The carvings had begun to move. Not quickly—nothing about the door was quick—but the thorned circle turned the way a great wheel turns when the current finally takes it, patient and immense, and light came up out of the grooves of the sigil the color of dawn on grey water.

"Dominic." Wren's voice, very small. The girl's hand had found hers without either of them deciding it. "It's listening."

"I know," Dominic said.

And then the door opened.

* * *

There was no explosion. No thunderclap, no burst of light, none of the theater the ballads would later insist upon. What there was instead was a sound like a held breath released after four hundred years—a long, low exhalation that moved through the chamber and through the bodies standing in it, and every half-speaker wept at once, though not one of them could have said why.

The Door of Aldermere swung inward on hinges older than the free cities, and beyond it—

Beyond it was wind.

Grey country. That was the only honest way to describe what lay through the threshold: a land the color of unpolished pewter, rolling hills under a sky like wet slate, grass gone silver with distance, a river somewhere below glinting dull as old coin. Wind-scoured. Empty-looking. The kind of country that photographs badly and haunts you forever. Dominic's mother had sung about hills like those, back before the singing stopped, back before everything stopped, and hearing the memory now Dominic felt it land in her chest like a fist.

But it was the attention she felt next.

It came pouring through the open door the way cold pours through a broken window—not malicious, not yet, just vast and undirected and *hungry for shape*. Something on the other side had noticed them. Not soldiers. Not an army. The country itself—Aldermere, or what remained of Aldermere, starved of speakers for four centuries, worn down to hunger by the sealing oath her family had signed—was turning its whole ruined face toward the small band of people standing at its threshold.

Sable made a sound low in her throat. "Dominic. It's looking at us."

"It's been looking at us since Wren scratched at the guild door," Dominic said. "Now it can see."

The pressure of that gaze was physical. It pressed against her gift like a thumb against a bruise—the gift she had renewed two nights ago before the assembled half-speakers, swearing not to seal and not to open but to guard whatever came through, and felt flare back to life in her chest like a banked coal given air. She could feel the door's power moving through her now, could feel the sentence they'd spoken settling into the threshold like a keystone settling into an arch. The door was open. It would stay open exactly as long as something held it.

And the ledger page—the last thing Quillon had sent her, the truth he'd died clutching—said plainly what everyone had spent weeks refusing to say aloud:

*The door was sealed not to imprison Aldermere, but to keep something from following the exiles out.*

"Positions," Dominic said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Ferro, take the half-speakers back to the second landing. Wren—"

"No."

Wren stepped forward. Thirteen years old, salt-stung hair, iron key still on its cord around her neck, and the look on her face was one Dominic had seen once before—in a mirror, at seventeen, the night she'd sworn her first oath. "I opened it with you. I'm not going to the second landing."

Dominic looked at her for a long moment. Every protective instinct she owned told her to pick the child up and carry her up the stair herself. Every instinct except the one that mattered—the one that knew Wren was the last full speaker of Aldermere, and that this country, this vast starving attention, knew her name.

"Stay behind me," Dominic said. "One step behind me. Never beside."

Wren nodded and stayed one step behind her.

They walked to the threshold together.

* * *

Up close, the door was a cathedral.

The stone was warm as a living thing, and the sigil turning across its face threw slow shadows that were somehow also light, and through the gap between door and frame the wind of Aldermere came through carrying smells that broke people open: rain on slate roofs, woodsmoke, bread, a flower no one in the free cities had a name for anymore except Nan Hollis, who was dead eleven days and had asked to be buried facing this. Half-speakers farther back in the chamber were saying words to each other in the old tongue, testing them, tasting them, and the country beyond the door answered each one with a shiver in the silver grass.

Dominic stepped up onto the sill.

The threshold itself was a hand's breadth of carved stone, worn smooth in the center by feet that had crossed it four hundred years ago and never come back. When her boots touched it, everything changed.

The attention focused.

It came through the doorway like water finding a channel—all that vast, diffuse hunger narrowing suddenly to a point, and the point was her. She felt it read her: her name, her bloodline, the signature in the forbidden ledger that matched her family's, the oath broken and the oath renewed, the word she'd spoken at seventeen that killed, the fifteen years of ledgers and silence, the burning page, the choice in the fish market, all of it, everything, weighed in a single instant by something that had been waiting since before her grandmother's grandmother was born.

*Thief,* the country seemed to say. Or perhaps: *Daughter of thieves.*

"We didn't come to take," Dominic said aloud, and her swordspeaker's voice carried into Aldermere the way it carried into a room, and the silver grass bowed in waves. "We came to give you back your voice."

The wind stopped.

In the sudden stillness, Dominic became aware of how much sound the country had been making without her noticing—the grass, the far river, a bird-thing crying somewhere in the grey hills—and how completely it had silenced itself to listen. The attention gathered again, closer now, and she felt the question form before any words accompanied it, felt it the way you feel weather change:

*Why should I believe you?*

And underneath the question, so faint it might have been imagination, something else. A weariness. An emptiness like a granary after famine. Four hundred years of speaking to no one.

Behind her, Wren took her one permitted step forward.

"Because we're here," the girl said—in Alderman, in the dead tongue that wasn't dead, her young voice ringing off the ancient stone—and then she sang. Three lines of Nan Hollis's last verse, the map-song, the one that named the hills and the river and the flower, and the country beyond the door made a sound that Dominic would spend the rest of her life failing to describe. It came from everywhere and nowhere, from the grass and the sky and the stone under their feet, and it was not words, or if it had once been words it was past words now, and it was grief, pure and enormous, the grief of a thing that had been forgotten so long it had begun to forget itself.

Then the vastness moved.

Not physically—or not only physically. But Dominic saw it, because someone had to see it and she was the one standing in the doorway: the horizon shifting, the grey hills flexing, the whole country leaning toward the open door like a starving creature toward lit windows. The attention poured through the threshold in earnest now, thickening the air, pressing against the boundary of the arch, and Dominic understood with terrible clarity that the seal her ancestors had placed here had not been holding Aldermere *in*.

It had been holding Aldermere's hunger *out*.

"Ferro!" she called, not turning. "How long?"

"The usurper's sappers reached the upper gallery an hour ago," Ferro's voice came back, tight and flat. "Ilva's holding the stair. Minutes, Dominic. Maybe less."

Minutes. The country leaning in. The army coming down. And between them, a hand's breadth of carved stone, and her.

Dominic Yarrow closed her eyes, found the new-lit coal of her gift where it burned behind her sternum, and stepped fully onto the threshold.

* * *

The moment both her feet were on the sill, the door accepted her.

She felt it happen—a settling, a locking, the way a sword feels when it slides home into a scabbard made for it. Her gift, restored barely two nights and already scarred from the market, from the siege, from the sentence, rose up in her like water rising in a well, and the threshold drank it. Not cruelly. Almost gratefully. As though the door had been waiting four hundred years for someone to stand here and mean it.

She was the hinge now. She understood that without being told. The door would stay open exactly as long as she stood here and poured her gift into the gap—and she could feel already what that would cost, the way you feel the weight of a pack before you've lifted it fully onto your shoulders. Not death. Worse than death, maybe, or better, depending on the day: presence. Permanence. She would be able to leave this stone the way a keystone can leave an arch.

She'd known. She'd known since Quillon's ledger page, since the night she renewed her oath, since maybe the very beginning. Knowing didn't make the fear smaller. It made it hers.

"Dominic." Wren's hand on her sleeve, gripping hard. "What's happening? Your eyes—"

"Don't worry about my eyes."

"They're *glowing*, Dominic."

"Then the door is working." She smiled—it felt strange on her face, an expression from a previous life—and turned to look down at the girl one more time. Behind Wren, the half-speakers stood at the second landing, the old cooper-woman with tears running into her beard, the boy with his three words clutched like coins, Sable with her hand pressed flat over her heart as though holding something in. Beyond them, faint through the rock, the sounds of the battle on the stair had begun: steel, shouting, Ilva's voice roaring orders in two languages at once.

The usurper was coming. Aldous, the boy who fled first, the exile who wanted a kingdom instead of a homeland, was coming down through the dark with his stolen fragments and his borrowed half-speakers, and the door was open, and the country was leaning through it, hungry, and the only thing between all of it was a disgraced appraiser standing on a sill of carved stone.

"Dominic," Wren said again, and now her voice cracked, and she was thirteen years old again and not a speaker of miracles, just a child. "Don't do this."

"Wren." Dominic crouched—awkwardly, the threshold pulling at her like deep water—and took the girl's face in her hands. "Listen to me. Whatever comes through that door tonight, whatever happens to me—you are the last voice of Aldermere. Not its weapon. Its voice. Do you understand the difference?"

Wren's jaw set. It was Ferro's stubbornness and Nan's fire and something entirely her own, and it broke Dominic's heart and mended it in the same breath.

"Yes," Wren whispered.

"Then whatever I become, teach me my own name afterward." Dominic straightened, turned back to the doorway, to the grey and silver country waiting with its whole ruined face pressed close, and let her gift pour.

The threshold blazed. The sigil turned faster. The wind of Aldermere rushed through, and with it came seeds—she saw them clearly, small and dark and improbable, spinning through the golden light of the open door like snow falling upward—and songs, fragments of music threading the air, and memories, whole landscapes of them, offered freely now, spilling into the free cities' deepest foundations the way light spills under a raised curtain.

Something vast began to pour its attention through.

And Dominic Thackeray, last swordspeaker, keeper of the door, stood in the middle of it all and held.

From far above, down the long stair, through the ringing dark, came a sound she recognized even through stone and screaming: footsteps descending, many, and one set among them heavier than the rest, unhurried, certain, belonging to a man who believed the door had been opened for him.

He was wrong. She had minutes to make sure he learned it.

Dominic set her feet, filled her lungs with the wind of the country she had betrayed and was now becoming part of, and waited for the usurper to arrive.
