## 36. What Came Through

The door did not open so much as *exhale*.

Wren felt it in her teeth first—a pressure like the moment before a storm breaks, when the air goes sweet and strange. She stood beside Dominic in the threshold of the Founders' Stones, close enough that she could feel the older woman trembling. Not from fear. Wren had learned the difference by now. Fear shook outward, all elbows and flinching hands. This was something deeper, a shaking that started in the bones and worked its way up, the way a tuning fork hums after it has been struck.

Dominic had poured herself into the doorway. That was the only way Wren could describe it, though the words were inadequate—she was still there, solid and warm at Wren's shoulder, but part of her had gone *into* the stone and the wood and the old iron hinges, and that part would not come back out. The door breathed because Dominic breathed. When the wind moved through the undercity tunnels, it moved through her too.

And beyond the door—

Grey light. Wind-scoured country, exactly as they'd seen when the sentence first unsealed it: rolling hills the color of ash, a sky like pewter, the long grass bent flat by centuries of unattended weather. Wren's heart had clenched at the sight of it, because everything she knew of Aldermere came from Nan Hollis's songs and the fragments other half-speakers carried, and none of those songs described a country this *empty*.

But now, as the vast attention on the other side gathered itself—she could feel it looking, the way you can feel eyes between your shoulder blades—something began to come through.

Not soldiers.

Seeds.

They drifted across the threshold like snow falling upward, small dark things with papery wings, spinning lazily in the draft that flowed out of Aldermere into the free cities' deep earth. One landed on the back of Wren's hand. It was warm.

"Oh," she said, and then said it again in Alderman, because some words simply fit better in the old tongue: "*Aeth.*" Seed. But also beginning. Also the first syllable of every planting song Nan had taught her.

More followed. Seeds and then other things—dried stalks, curled leaves pressed flat as if they'd been sleeping inside books, a bird's feather grey as smoke. And threaded through it all, faint but unmistakable, a sound: singing. Not words yet, or not words she could parse, just the shape of voices, many voices, worn thin as river stones but still holding melody.

"It's not an army," Sable whispered behind them. Wren heard her shift, heard the soft clink of the memory-glass vials she carried everywhere now. "It's—it's luggage. It's what you pack when you've been evicted."

"No," Wren said slowly. The Alderman rose in her without effort, the language moving through her like water finding its channel, and she translated as she understood. "It's what you send ahead when you're coming home to a place that isn't there anymore." She looked up at Dominic. "It doesn't know where home is. It's been holding everything. All this time. Everything Aldermere was, it kept, because no one else did."

The hunger she had felt in her dreams—the starving voice, the country worn down to want—was still there, underneath. But now that the door stood open, now that someone stood witness on the near side, the hunger had changed its shape. A starving thing offered bread does not snatch. It weeps first.

"They have to be planted," Wren said. "That's what it's asking. Not passage. Not conquest." Her throat tightened. "Planting. It wants to be *grown* here."

*

They carried the first seeds up into the light in Nan Hollis's old flour sacking.

It should have been a solemn procession, and in a way it was—Ferro's maps spread over the worktable in the safehouse, Sable cataloguing each variety of seed with the reverence she usually reserved for stolen memories, Ilva standing watch at the door with her sword loosened in its scabbard. But there was also laughter, because one of the half-speakers, a stooped cooper named Bram who hadn't spoken more than ten words aloud since his village burned, took one look at the papery-winged seeds drifting around the lamplight and burst into tears and laughter at once.

"*Lirren-seed*," he choked out. "My mother grew lirren along our whole fence line. Blue flowers. Bees everywhere. I thought—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I thought I was the only one who remembered the name of the flower."

"You're not," Wren told him. "You never were."

There were eleven half-speakers now, plus Wren. Eleven broken pieces of a language, and somehow, together, they made nearly a whole. Each of them carried different words the way refugees carry different bundles—one had the words for rivers and boats, another for weaving and dye, another for the long liturgy of funerals. Put side by side, their fragments overlapped and locked, and whole sentences emerged that no single survivor had spoken in fifteen years. They spent evenings fitting themselves together like masons rebuilding a wall, marveling, grieving, arguing about pronunciation until Ferro threatened to map their throats.

Now they crowded around the table while Wren opened the sacking and poured out the seeds.

"Tell me what these are," Dominic said quietly. She stood apart, near the wall, as she always did now—as much of herself as always stood apart, anchored to the door below. Wren had stopped resenting that distance. You couldn't resent a hinge for being a hinge.

Wren picked up a seed with a spiral husk and held it to the light. The word arrived before she consciously sought it, rising out of the place where the language lived in her: "*Sorrel-ash.* Grows in burned ground. First thing back after a fire." She set it down and touched another, smooth and black as a beetle. "*Nightwheat.*" Another, tiny and round. "*Honeygrass.*"

One by one, the half-speakers named what they recognized, and the table filled with names, and the names filled the room the way water fills a dry basin. Ferro wrote them all down, cross-referencing against his marginalia-maps, his hands shaking. Sable recorded everything twice—once in ink, once in memory, and Wren saw her wince slightly at the cost, and love her for it anyway.

"These won't grow here," Bram said at last, gently, the way you deliver bad news to a child. "Different soil. Different sky. Aldermere is—" He stopped.

"Aldermere is right here," Wren said, surprising herself. "In this room. In us. The soil will learn. Soil always learns. That's the whole trick of soil." She looked down at the seeds, and the dream-knowledge stirred in her again—that vast patient attention on the far side of the door, watching through her eyes the way a grandmother watches through a window. "It knows," she added softly. "It's not asking us to rebuild Aldermere. It's asking us to let it try becoming something new. It says—" she paused, listening inward, translating feeling into the old tongue and the old tongue into the free cities' common speech— "it says a country is not a place. A country is a habit of care. And habits can be planted anywhere."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Nan Hollis's empty chair seemed very loud in its emptiness, and Wren understood that the ache in the room was grief changing direction—not the grief of loss anymore, but the harder, gentler grief of things that might actually be possible.

"We'll need somewhere with open ground," Ilva said practically, wiping her eyes with the back of one gauntleted hand as if she hadn't been crying at all. "Outside the walls. Which means outside the siege lines."

"The siege is breaking up," Ferro said, tapping his newest map. "Corvane's gone to ground since the duel. Half the usurper's army deserted when the word went round that their own commander couldn't speak anymore. There's a farmstead past the tanneries—abandoned since the bombardment. Good south-facing slope."

"Then tomorrow," Dominic said from her place by the wall, "we plant."

*

They planted in the grey hour before dawn, when the watchfires of the departing army smoldered low and the free cities smelled of rain and ash.

Ilva chose the hour and guarded the field. Sable scouted it three times. But it was Wren who knelt first in the cold furrow with the sorrel-ash seed cupped in both palms, and it was Wren who spoke the planting verse Nan had taught her in the safehouse kitchen, the one disguised as a lullaby, which turned out to be exactly what it had always claimed to be.

"*Sleep, little root, beneath the thorn,
the thorn turns green, the green turns corn,
and what was lost lies down to keep
its promise to the waking deep.*"

She pressed the seed into the earth. For a moment nothing happened, and her heart fell—and then she felt it, faint as a pulse, the attention on the far side of the door leaning closer, and warmth rose through the frozen ground like breath through a keyhole.

The seed split. A pale shoot unfurled, then a leaf, then two, impossibly fast, impossibly green against the ash-grey mud.

Around the field, eleven half-speakers gasped in eleven variations of the same dead tongue, and then they all began, clumsily, haltingly, to sing.

It was not good singing. Bram's voice cracked; the widow Maren knew the verses in a different dialect and they collided sweetly; a boy named Tam, who'd only ever had the counting rhymes, sang numbers where the others sang names. But the song was a map, and the map was a memory, and the memory was alive, and the ground drank it in. Wherever their voices touched, things grew—lirren shoots blue-tipped even before budding, nightwheat rising in silver-green rows, honeygrass spreading like spilled gold.

By full light, a quarter acre of Aldermere stood growing in free-city soil, and people were coming out of the ruined streets to stare.

Wren knelt in the middle of it, dirt to her elbows, laughing and crying at once, and felt the country's presence settle around her like a shawl—not hungry anymore, or not only hungry, but *grateful*, which was somehow more overwhelming. It pressed against the edge of her mind a single image, simple as a child's drawing: a fence line with blue flowers, bees everywhere, and a woman's hands in the dirt.

Bram's mother's fence. Sent through fifteen years of exile and imprisonment, across the threshold of a sealed door, delivered to the one person left who remembered it.

"It remembers us," Wren said aloud, to anyone, to everyone. "All of us. It kept everything."

Dominic stood at the field's edge, and though most of her was miles away and fathoms down, holding the door, enough of her remained to smile—the first true smile Wren had ever seen on her face, tired and crooked and real.

"Then we'd better give it plenty of new things to remember," Dominic said.

*

The planting became a ritual, and the ritual became a season.

Every few days they carried more seeds up from the door—Wren learned quickly that the country gave freely now, pressing gifts through the threshold whenever she came near, seeds and cuttings and once a small clay whistle that played a tune no living person had heard in fifteen years. They planted along the walls, in the bomb craters, in window boxes and graveyard corners and the cracks of abandoned streets. The free cities, so long grey, began to bloom in colors that had no common-speech names, and the half-speakers walked the neighborhoods teaching children the names, and the children taught their parents, and the language spread the way the honeygrass spread: patiently, everywhere, taking root.

Grief did not end. Wren still woke some nights reaching for Nan's voice and finding silence; they buried Nan's last verses deep in her heart and still they ran out at the worst moments, leaving her stranded mid-song. Bram still flinched at the smell of smoke. Maren set a place at her table every evening for a husband whose body had never come home. The country's gifts could not undo any of that, and Wren understood—they all understood—that this was not the point. The point was that the grief finally had somewhere to *go*. Every seed pressed into free-city soil was a mourning turned outward, a lament given hands, sorrow learning the shape of work.

On the first morning the lirren bloomed, the whole found family walked the rows together. Sable wore one blue flower tucked behind her ear and pretended she didn't know it was there. Ilva, off-duty for the first time in weeks, let Tam put a crown of honeygrass on her head and threatened dire consequences to any witness who mentioned it later. Ferro unrolled a fresh map on a board propped against the fence—his final map, he said, the one he'd been working toward his whole disgraced life—and began to draw the farmstead, the flowering rows, the city beyond, labeling everything in both tongues.

Wren walked to the middle of the field, where the growth was thickest, and pressed her palm flat against the warm earth.

*Thank you,* she said in Alderman, inward, the way she'd learned to speak to the presence beyond the door. *Rest now. We'll hold the remembering.*

The answer came gentle as the wind off the hills of home-that-was: a feeling like being gathered up, like a child lifted onto a hip, like the last note of a song held longer than it needs to be. Then it receded, contented, folding itself back toward the threshold where Dominic stood eternal, and Wren felt—for the first time since the burning of her village, since the salt-stung night she'd scratched at the guild door—the silence in her head was not an absence.

It was room to grow.

She straightened, brushed the dirt from her knees, and turned to call the others in for breakfast—and stopped.

At the field's edge, half-hidden by the new hedge of lirren, stood a girl of perhaps eight years, barefoot, mud-spattered, wearing the torn remnants of a soldier's coat far too big for her. Behind her, at a distance, a column of refugees snaked along the road from the north—dozens of them, then hundreds, drawn by rumor the way Wren herself had once been drawn. But the girl alone had crossed the ditch, and she was staring at the impossible blue flowers with an expression Wren knew from mirrors and dreams.

Her lips moved. What came out was Alderman—accentless, fluent, unmistakable—the voice of a native speaker of a country that had supposedly died.

"*Is this it?*" the girl asked. "*Is this Aldermere?*"

And Wren, with dirt on her hands and her family calling her name from the farmhouse, felt the future open before her like a furrow waiting for seed.
