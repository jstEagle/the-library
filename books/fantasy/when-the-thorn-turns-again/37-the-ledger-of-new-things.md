## 37. The Ledger of New Things

The guild Sable built stood where the old one had drowned.

Not literally drowned—the vaults beneath the Founders' Stones had flooded in the siege's last week, when Ferro's tunnels collapsed and the aqueducts burst—but drowned in every way that mattered. The Guild of Impossible Things had traded in stolen memories and sealed doors and other people's futures, and its corpse still sat behind barricaded doors on Candlewick Row while the city decided what to do with it. The magistrates talked about seizing it. The half-speakers talked about burning it. Sable talked about neither, because Sable had already gone in through a window nobody remembered existed, taken what mattered, and left a receipt.

The receipt was a joke. She'd written *paid in full* on a scrap of Quillon's own vellum, because even grief deserved good penmanship.

Her new guild opened on Thimble Lane, three doors down from Nan Hollis's old house, which Wren had claimed before the snow melted and filled with seedlings and singing children. The sign above Sable's door was not gilded and did not promise impossible things. It read:

**THE LEDGER OF NEW THINGS**
*Memories returned. Crafts mended. Nothing stolen.*

Ferro, when he was sober enough to carve—and he was sober more often now, though some nights the wrong side of the tunnel called to him like a tide—he'd added a small second line beneath, in letters so fine you had to crouch to read them: *except by consent.*

That was the whole business model, and it terrified her more than any heist ever had.

* * *

Sable had been stealing things since she was six years old. First purses, then secrets, then memories—that last trade learned in Quillon's back rooms from a woman named Vetch who smelled of vinegar and had hands cold as river stones. She knew the mechanics the way a pickpocket knows knuckles: where a memory lived, how it hooked into the meat of a person, what it cost to pry loose. Every extraction took something from the thief. A lock of hair's worth of her own past, traded away grain by grain until there were whole seasons of Sable's childhood that belonged to strangers.

She had assumed returning them would be different. That giving back would cost nothing, or would even fill her up somehow—the way stories said charity warmed the giver.

It did not work that way. Returning a memory meant holding it, and holding a memory meant living it, and some of what came back through her hands was heavy as ballast stone.

The first return had been an old cooper named Marta whose husband died at the breach on the north wall. Before the war, Marta had sold the memory of their wedding day to pay for roof repairs—a common enough transaction in those lean years; Quillon's clerks had called it *harvesting the sentimental surplus*, a phrase Sable intended to carve on his grave if anyone ever found enough of him to mark. Marta came to the new guild with coin she didn't have, because everyone assumed everything still cost.

Sable had made her sit down. Had made her tea. Then she'd opened the guild vault—not the ruined one under the city, but the modest cellar beneath Thimble Lane where the recovered memories lived in wax-sealed jars, each labeled in her own hand—and brought out the little clay pot with the blue thread tied round its neck.

"You don't owe me anything," Sable told her. "This was always yours. I'm just the courier."

Then she'd unsealed it, pressed her palms to Marta's temples, and let the wedding day pour home.

And Sable stood in a sunlit courtyard fifteen years dead, smelling orange peel and horse sweat, watching a young man with paint on his knuckles laugh so hard he couldn't finish his vows, and she felt Marta's joy like a struck bell and Marta's grief underneath it like the bell's shadow, and when it was done they both wept, and Sable went upstairs and put her head between her knees until the shaking stopped.

Devotion, she learned, was not a feeling. It was a practice. It was showing up with the jar in your hands day after day, knowing what the carrying would do to you, and doing it anyway.

* * *

Every morning, before the shop opened, Sable walked to the Founders' Stones.

The undercity stair had been rebuilt—Ilva's watch had seen to that, wide and honest, lit with lamps instead of secrets—and the Door of Aldermere stood at the bottom of it the way a mountain stands: without apology. Grey stone carved with the sigil Sable had once sold to a collector who thought it decorative. And in the threshold, where the hinge would be, stood Dominic Thackeray.

Sable had stopped trying to describe her to people who hadn't seen it. *She poured herself into the doorway* was true and useless. What visitors saw was a woman-shaped presence woven into the stone of the frame, visible from both sides, half in the free cities' lamplight and half in Aldermere's grey wind. Her eyes still moved. Her voice still came low and clear, though it sounded now as if it passed through a wall to reach you. She could not step forward. She could not step back. She had chosen the only place in two countries where she could belong to both, and she held it the way she'd once held oaths: absolutely, and alone except when company came.

Company came every morning at dawn, because Sable brought the ledger.

The ledger had been Quillon's once—his oldest folio, rescued from the raid—and then Dominic's, when she'd kept accounts of impossible trades in a hand so precise it looked printed. Now it was Sable's, and she'd ruled fresh pages in the back of it, and every evening she wrote down what the day had brought, and every morning she climbed down and read it aloud.

"The third of Greenmonth," she read, settling onto the stool Wren had dragged down years ago and that no one had ever moved. "Returned to Master Ellery Vance the memory of his mother's kitchen, which he sold in the famine winter to buy bread for his sisters. He cried in the shop for an hour and then bought a loaf from the baker next door and gave it away, which I think means it took."

Dominic's smile was a slow thing, like weather changing. "He'll keep doing that."

"I know. They all do, at first. Giving things away like they're making up for lost time." Sable turned the page. "Also: the Alderman weavers finished the second tapestry. The one with the thorn-rose border. Sold before it was off the frame—to a free-city merchant's wife, mind, not one of ours. She hung it facing the street."

"Good," Dominic said, and her voice carried that particular weight it got when the word meant more than the word. "Let them look. Looking is how remembering starts."

"Also also," Sable said, because the last item needed cushioning, "I had a visitor yesterday who wanted to sell something."

The wind through the door changed. From beyond the threshold, Aldermere exhaled its grey breath—grass and cold stone and something older underneath—and Sable felt Dominic attend the way she always did when trade crossed the line between countries.

"Selling what?"

"A memory. His own." Sable kept her eyes on the page. "A young man, maybe twenty. Free-city born, but his grandmother was Alderman—one of the ones who hid it, changed the name, the whole performance. He wants to sell me his memories of her. Says he can't afford to keep grieving her and pay rent at the same time." She paused. "He doesn't know what he's really asking."

"No," Dominic agreed quietly.

"He wants to forget her. But what he actually came looking for—" Sable closed the ledger on her finger. "What he actually wants is to remember her properly, and there isn't a jar in my cellar with that in it, because his grandmother took her language to her grave rather than pass it to a child she thought would be hunted for it. So he's got fragments. Smells. Half a lullaby. Grief with no shape." She looked up at the woman in the door. "I told him to come back Thursday and bring the lullaby. I've got a choir of grandmothers downstairs on Thursdays who can probably finish it."

For a moment Dominic said nothing, and the wind moved through her like she was the thing made of air and the world was the solid thing. Then: "You're trading in new things."

"That's the sign, isn't it."

"It is." And there was something in her voice Sable had spent months learning to hear—a frequency beneath the words, the sound of a woman watching the guild reborn as what it should have been all along. "Quillon used to say the guild trafficked in impossible things. He had it backward. Everything impossible was already true somewhere. We just moved it around and charged rent." A pause. "You're dealing in possible things. It's harder."

"Don't tell anyone. I'll lose my reputation."

* * *

Thursdays were the hardest days, and Sable loved them best.

The grandmothers came at midday: six Alderman women, four of them half-speakers, two who'd lost the tongue entirely and attended anyway because the songs still lived in their spines even where the words had rotted out. They sat in the workshop among the looms and the woodcarvings and the seed-jars, and they sang, and Wren—when she wasn't teaching the lane's children their letters in two alphabets—came to write down what surfaced.

Because that was the other trade now. Not just returning what had been stolen. Recovering what had been abandoned.

Every fragment a customer surrendered became material. The cooper's wedding-day joy had contained, in its background, a market cry in Alderman that none of the grandmothers had heard sung in forty years. A dockhand's memory of his father's boat carried the words for *tide-turn* and *safe harbor*, which had survived nowhere else. Even the painful returns—there were many, the siege had minted grief the way mints minted coin—even those sometimes held a scrap of the old country caught in them like a flower in ice.

Sable catalogued them all. Wax pots, blue thread, labels in her hand. And on Thursdays the grandmothers listened to whatever the week had yielded and pulled it apart like bread, and the language grew back a joint at a time.

It cost her. She never told them how much. Each memory she handled to extract its Alderman scraps took another sliver of toll from her own stock. She'd inventoried herself ruthlessly, once, early on: a list of what remained. Her mother's face: gone, sold at fourteen to buy passage. Her first theft: gone. The taste of the plum tree behind her childhood house: gone, and she didn't even know what plums tasted like anymore, only that she'd once loved them.

But some things remained. The smell of rain on Candlewick cobblestones. The sound of Nan Hollis laughing, which no one had thought to sell because no one had thought it valuable, which proved that Quillon's clerks had never understood the inventory at all. And one item she guarded past reason: the memory of a girl named Beatrix Yarrow—no, that was the novelist; the girl was Dominic Thackeray—aged eleven, standing in a guild corridor, swearing with tremendous ferocity that she would never, ever become a swordspeaker, and Sable aged nine watching her and thinking, with the absolute certainty of childhood, *she will.*

She'd stolen that memory herself, decades ago, meaning to sell it. She'd never managed to part with it. Some things weren't inventory. Some things were compasses.

* * *

On the first of Highsummer, a year and a season after the breach, Sable came down the undercity stair with the ledger under her arm and found someone already at the door.

Wren. Nineteen now, tall in the way of saplings, wearing the grey traveling cloak of the speaker-teachers who'd begun walking circuits between the free cities. She stood close to the threshold—close enough that the wind off Aldermere moved her hair—and she was talking to Dominic in the old tongue, low and fluent, and whatever she was saying made the stone around the doorframe glow faintly, like coals remembering fire.

Sable waited on the stair, not wanting to break it. She caught only the end: Wren finishing something long, and Dominic answering in the same language—her voice strange and beautiful in it, a river running through rock—and then both of them laughing, or something adjacent to laughter.

"She's teaching me," Dominic said, switching to common as Wren stepped back. "Properly. Fifteen years I carried this gift like a wound, and your generation is teaching me it's a garden."

"What did you just say to her? In Alderman?"

Wren's grin was quick and crooked. "Trade secret."

"Wren."

"I asked her what the door dreams about," Wren said, more softly. "She said it dreams about spring." She squeezed Sable's arm in passing and went up the stairs two at a time, cloak swirling, toward wherever the day's teaching waited, and Sable watched her go with the specific pride of a woman who had helped steal a future back from a usurper and considered it her finest job.

Then it was just the two of them, and the ledger.

Sable sat. Opened the book. Read the date, the entries, the small news: the cooper Marta had commissioned a cradle, Alderman-pattern, for a granddaughter due at harvest. The merchant's wife with the tapestry had sent her neighbors to look, and three of them had ordered their own. The young man with the lullaby had come back Thursday after all, and the grandmothers had finished his song for him, and he'd wept, and then he'd asked the question that Sable had saved for last.

"He asked," she said now, "if the door opens for the homesick."

The wind held still.

"Not for conquest," Sable went on, carefully. "Not for armies. He said—his grandmother used to tell him about a valley, orchards, a river with a particular sound. He said he'd give anything to stand in that river once. He knows she made it up, or mostly made it up, or remembers it wrong. He doesn't care. He asked if the door takes pilgrims."

Dominic was quiet a long moment. Beyond her, through the frame, the grey country breathed its wind across the threshold, and Sable had learned to read that wind the way sailors read water. It had moods. This one was listening.

"The closing sentence," Dominic said at last. "The blessing Wren and I spoke. It opens only for the homesick. That was the whole point—we didn't seal it, we tuned it." A pause, and something moved through her expression that Sable had no ledger category for. "I don't know what happens when someone walks through. No one has walked through yet. The exiles chose soil here. The country gives us seeds and songs and lets us plant them. Nobody's knocked."

"Somebody's knocking now."

"Yes." Dominic's voice dropped into the register she used for oath-language, the deep grain of it. "And here's the trouble, Sable. The sentence needs two voices to bless the opening. Mine"—the faintest motion of her shoulders, bound in stone—"and a speaker willing to stand witness at the threshold while it turns. Wren could do it. Wren should probably do it; it's her inheritance too. But if that boy walks through that door, he walks into a country that has been empty and starving and dreaming for fifteen years, alone, and whatever meets him there—" She stopped. Started again. "Whatever meets him will meet him with everything it is. All of it. At once."

"Is that dangerous?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. The door was sealed to keep something from following the exiles out. We opened it and learned the something was the country itself—hungry to be witnessed. A single grieving boy might be exactly the meal it's dreamed of. Or he might be exactly the guest it's dreamed of." Dominic's eyes found Sable's, steady as a keel. "I can't walk through and find out. You can't either—you'd need the tongue, and the toll it would take—"

"I know what things cost," Sable said. "It's the one subject I've always been expert in."

"So we choose. Turn him away and keep the blessing safe and untested. Or test it—with Wren witnessing, with the grandmothers singing the road-songs, with every safeguard we can build—and learn whether the door truly opens for the homesick, or whether we wrote a beautiful sentence over a trap." She smiled then, tired and real. "I know which answer the old guild would have given. Sell the information, hedge the risk, let the boy take the chance and charge admission. I know what Quillon would have written in this ledger."

Sable looked down at the open book, at her own ruled pages, at the entry waiting to be written. *Nothing stolen,* the sign promised. *Except by consent.* But consent to what, when nobody knew what waited on the far side of the turning?

She uncapped her ink.

"Tell Wren," she said, writing as she spoke, giving the words weight by making them permanent. "Thursday next. The boy comes with his lullaby, the grandmothers sing him to the threshold, and Wren stands witness." Her pen paused. "And write this in your half of the ledger, Dominic Thackeray, so it's on record: the first pilgrim to Aldermere goes with our love and our fear and our full permission, and whatever meets him, meets all of us."

Beyond the threshold, the wind changed—warmed, almost, like a held breath released—and far off in the grey country, faint as a memory of a memory, something that might have been an orchard in blossom stirred in the air between them.

Sable wrote the date, and under it, the entry that would start a new kind of account:

*One pilgrim, outbound. Paid for in longing. Change owed: everything.*
