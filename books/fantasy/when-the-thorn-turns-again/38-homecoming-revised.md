## 38. Homecoming, Revised

The first thing the free cities learned about Alderman spring was that it smelled of thorn-honey.

It came on a morning in late Thawmonth, drifting down from the terraces where the half-speakers had planted their first gardens, and by noon the whole of Saltmarket was asking what it was and where it could be bought. Old women leaned out windows. Fishwives argued whether it was a perfume or a pastry. And in the square outside the old customs house, a girl of perhaps eleven stood on an upturned crate with her arms spread wide and told them, in two languages at once, that you couldn't buy it — that it only grew where something buried had been allowed to come home.

Her name was Wren. By summer, every child in the lower city would know it.

* * *

Ilva Voss had commanded soldiers for nine years, and she had hated most of them.

Not the people — though there had been people worth hating — but the *shape* of soldiering: the marching, the counting, the way a squad became a fist and a fist became a thing that struck. She had led house-to-house searches through the free cities' oldest quarter with a warrant in one hand and a sick feeling in her stomach, and she had told herself, then, that orders were orders, and that the woman weeping over her burned books would have been weeping over something else if not books.

She no longer told herself that. Now she stood in the drill yard behind the salt granary at dawn, facing thirty volunteers who had signed up for something new, and she began each morning the same way.

"Say your name," she called out, "and say what you are guarding."

A young tanner's son went first, as he always did, because he always volunteered. "Piet. Guarding the east stair and everyone on it."

"Marla. Guarding the night market."

"Dossen. Guarding—" He hesitated. Dossen had been a conscript under the usurper, like Ilva herself once, and some mornings the words still caught. "Guarding my neighbors. From everything. Including me, if it comes to that."

"Including yourself," Ilva agreed, and moved down the line. It was a strange liturgy and she knew it — nothing like the roll-calls of the vanguard, where names were numbers and numbers were ammunition. But Quillon had kept ledgers because he believed that what was written down was owned, and Ilva had decided that if anything was going to be owned here, it would be the truth of why they stood watch. Not to hunt. Not to hold territory. To guard.

The usurper's edicts still hung nailed to the customs house door, rain-warped now, the ink bleeding until the word *linguists* was barely legible. Nobody took them down. Ilva had suggested it once, early, and Ferro had shaken his head. "Leave them," he'd said. "Let the children see what we came out of." So they hung there like a scar, and beneath them the city went on learning how to be a different shape.

The drills were unorthodox. Ilva taught spear-work and wall-walking, yes, but she also taught listening — how to hear a mob forming before it formed, how to tell fear-anger from hunger-anger, how to stand between two shouting groups and be the reason they stopped. She brought in the fishwife Brenna, who had talked down a bread riot single-handedly, to lecture on the architecture of crowds. She made her guards learn names. Every door on their routes. Every child. When the usurper's agents had hunted through these streets, she thought, they had seen houses as targets. She intended to raise a generation that saw them as faces.

On the last day of Thawmonth, a courier brought word that a column of the usurper's regulars had been sighted three days west, flying parley colors, carrying a sealed letter addressed — improbably, precisely — to *Sergeant Ilva Voss, Watch of the Free Cities.*

She read the title twice. Then she folded the letter into her breast pocket, unopened, and went back to the yard, and taught her recruits how to hold a shield line without breaking anyone beneath it.

That evening, alone, she broke the seal.

* * *

The school met in the back room of Sable's guild, which was not officially a school, just as Sable's guild was not officially a guild.

Officially it was a memory-brokerage, licensed under the new charters, trading in returned recollections and witnessed testimony — a strange trade, but the war had left so many people wanting to give back what they'd sold or lost that Sable never lacked for clients. Unofficially, the back room held fourteen children, a slateboard, and the last fluent speaker of a dead country, who was eleven years old and had strong opinions about grammar.

"No," Wren said, tapping the slate. "You're saying it like a command. *Vesh-alen.* That's 'give me the bread.' You want *vesh-aleth.* The *th* softens it. It's 'may there be bread.' See? Same words. Different heart."

The boy — Tammo, the cooper's son, quick and restless — frowned at his own chalk marks. "How can a letter change your heart?"

"How can a tone change your mother's mind?" Wren shrugged. "In Aldermere the language believed things about itself. My nan used to say the words were little doors. You could walk through the same sentence angry or kind, and arrive somewhere else entirely."

Sable, sorting memories at her desk in the front room, heard this through the wall and smiled despite herself. Little doors. The child had no idea, or every idea, how literally true that was.

Wren's methods would have horrified any schoolmaster in the free cities. She didn't teach vocabulary lists; she taught songs. She didn't correct errors; she made the class act out whole markets, weddings, arguments, until the language lived in their mouths instead of their notebooks. And twice a week she did the thing that drew the largest crowds and the most disapproving looks from respectable citizens: she taught them the words for weather, for planting, for mending nets — and the old blessing-shapes that used to go with them.

"They weren't weapons," she told a skeptical merchant who had come to complain that his daughter was learning "witch-tongue." He shifted under her gaze; it is difficult to feel authoritative when lectured by a child whose voice carries the cadence of a vanished nation. "They were tools. Hammers are tools too. You don't ban hammers because someone hit someone with one. You teach people what hands are *for*."

The merchant left unconvinced but quiet. His daughter stayed in the class. Within a month she was singing harvest rounds in perfect Alderman, and within two she had taught them to her grandmother, who cried the first time and demanded lessons the second.

By mid-spring there were three classes, then five. Children dragged their parents. Parents dragged their grandparents. An old shipwright turned up one grey morning, hat in his hands, mumbling fragments of a lullaby his own grandmother had sung before the purges — fragments no living speaker could complete — and Wren listened with her eyes closed, filled the gaps like water finding its level, and sent him home with a whole song. He came back the next day with his brother.

The language stopped being a relic. That was the miracle nobody had planned. It went around the city the way warmth goes through a house in winter — room to room, mouth to mouth — until shop signs appeared in two scripts, until dockhands counted cargo in Alderman because the numbers were shorter, until a baker named her new loaf *vesh-aleth*, may-there-be-bread, and sold out of it daily.

It was not universally loved. There were letters to the councils. There was a stone thrown through the guild's window one night, and after that Ilva quietly assigned two of her watch to walk the street after dark — guarding, not hunting — and the window was reglazed, and the classes went on.

But the current ran the other way. Everyone could feel it. Something long dammed up had found its channel.

* * *

Ferro marked it all on maps.

He had spent twenty years reconstructing a country from marginalia — trade tallies, sea-charts, the spacing of place-names in other people's poems — and now the country was reconstructing itself in front of him, in the streets, and he could hardly bear to sleep for wanting to record it.

His new atlas lay open across three tables in the guild's map room: *Aldermere Abroad.* Not the old country — that existed now only in the grey wind beyond the Door, tended by the keeper in the threshold — but its diaspora, blooming. Each page was a district of the free cities rendered in Ferro's fine obsessive hand, and onto it he plotted what the half-speakers planted and sang and built. A garden on the terraces where thorn-honey was made. The corner of Saltmarket where the bread was named. The well on Tanner's Row that the children had re-dedicated with a blessing Wren said was older than the well itself. Every point a pin of colored wax: gold for festivals, green for gardens, blue for songs recovered, red — fewer and fewer reds — for places where someone had been hurt for speaking.

At the start of spring the red pins had outnumbered all the others. Now the gold and green were winning. He measured it weekly, the way other men measured rainfall.

Nan Hollis would have loved the maps. Ferro worked best in the evenings, when the light was low and the guild was quiet, and sometimes he caught himself turning to ask her opinion of a coastline before remembering. They had buried her at the turn of the season, on the hillside facing east, exactly as she asked, with the last verse of the door-song carved on her stone in both alphabets. The half-speakers still walked up there on clear evenings. Some of them swore that from the crest, on certain nights, you could smell thorn-honey coming off the sea wind, though there was nothing east of the hill but water and distance.

Ferro didn't swear it. But he mapped the hill anyway, and marked it green.

* * *

The festival came at the full moon of Seedmonth, and nobody entirely agreed afterward whose idea it had been, which was perhaps the truest sign of belonging there was.

It braided itself together the way Wren said sentences did — same streets, different heart. The free cities' traditional Springtide fair had run for centuries: paper lanterns on the harbor, honey-fritters, the crowning of the May-warden, drunk sailors singing slightly ahead of the tune. Into this, without announcement or permission, the Aldermen wove their own rites. The lanterns gained a second layer — small clay lamps shaped like doors, set ajar, floated out on the tide to carry wishes to a country across the water. The fritters acquired a neighbor-stall selling *vesh-aleth* loaves stamped with the sigil. When the May-warden procession reached Saltmarket, the crowd simply began, without anyone leading it, to sing the sowing-song in two tongues, the lines folding into one another like hands being clasped.

Dominic would have noticed the seams. Dominic noticed everything, from her post at the Door — but Dominic was elsewhere, holding a threshold, reading her ledger aloud to the wind while Sable's clerk transcribed her voice into the daily entries. So the festival happened the way a family happens: imperfectly, generously, with arguments about the order of processions and a genuine crisis when the clay-lamp makers and the lantern-makers nearly came to blows over stall placement, resolved by Wren proposing they share a stall and inventing, on the spot, a hybrid rite that would henceforth be considered ancient tradition by everyone under twelve.

Tammo, the cooper's son, led the children's choir. Marla of the night market danced the reel that Nan Hollis used to hum, having extracted the steps from her grandmother over three weeks of bribes and patience. Old shipwrights who hadn't spoken Alderman since childhood wept openly into their ale and were embraced by strangers and were not embarrassed by it, because half the square was weeping too.

And at moonrise, Wren stood on the customs house steps — the same steps where the edicts hung above her, warped and fading — and gave the closing blessing of the night, the one she had composed herself and refused, all week, to let anyone preview.

"*Vesh-aleth, vesh-alore,*" she said, and the square repeated it, stumbling and then steadying, and the meaning rose through them like heat through floorboards: *May there be bread. May there be more than bread. May the table grow longer than the road was.*

Somewhere in the crowd, Ilva Voss — off duty, in plain clothes, holding a cup of cider she kept forgetting to drink — felt the words land in her chest like a hand pressed flat over an old wound. She had carried warrants through these streets. She had been the knock on the door at night. And now she stood among ten thousand people singing a dead country alive, and no one spat at her, and several people nodded to her, and one elderly woman pressed a honey-fritter into her hand and said, "You keep us safe, sergeant. Eat something."

Belonging earned, she thought. Never given. Never free. But given *anyway*, once earned — that was the discovery of that spring, and it undid her more thoroughly than any battle ever had.

She ate the fritter. It tasted of thorn-honey.

* * *

The letter from the west sat in her drawer for six days before she showed anyone.

When she finally laid it on Sable's desk — Ferro summoned, Wren hovering on a stool pretending not to read upside-down — it was with the expression of a woman handing over a live coal. "Parley colors. Regulars. Addressed to me by my old rank, which means someone in their staff remembers me, which I don't love."

"Open it properly," Sable said. "What does it want?"

Ilva read aloud:

*"To the officer commanding the Watch of the Free Cities. The Crown acknowledges the changed circumstances of the province and proposes terms of coexistence. In recognition thereof, the Crown offers formal cessation of hostilities, restoration of seized properties, and amnesty for all defectors, upon satisfaction of one condition: the surrender of the individual known as Wren, for examination by His Majesty's linguists, duration not to exceed one year, treatment guaranteed humane, return guaranteed thereafter."*

Silence.

Then Wren started laughing — not frightened laughter, something sharper, almost delighted. "*Return guaranteed thereafter.* Like borrowing a library book. Like I'm a—"

"We're not considering it," Ferro said flatly.

"I know we're not considering it." Ilva was already pacing. "I'm thinking about what it tells us. They've stopped calling her a criminal. No warrants, no edict-language. They've gone polite. Do you understand what that means? Polite means they've realized force isn't working. Polite means the Crown is *afraid* of an eleven-year-old."

"It also means," Sable said slowly, turning the letter toward the lamp, "that they know exactly where she is, and they chose a letter over soldiers. Someone at court has done arithmetic. Soldiers cost more than she's worth — unless they think she can be had cheaply." Her eyes flicked to Wren. "Which means somewhere in their machinery, someone believes you might *want* to go. Amnesty for defectors. Restoration of property. This letter wasn't written to threaten us, Ilva. It was written to tempt the city. To make our neighbors do the math too."

The silence that followed had a different texture. Outside, faintly, the harbor bells rang the hour, and someone in the street below was still humming the sowing-song.

Wren slid off the stool. She crossed to the window and looked east, past the rooftops, toward the dark line of the sea and the hill where Nan slept facing a country none of them could see. When she spoke, her voice had the cadence Ferro had learned to associate with the old songs — steady, level, older than her years.

"The usurper was an exile too," she said. "He wanted the door for a kingdom. We opened it for seeds and songs, and it worked, and the country came home to us instead of us going home to it. And now they write letters." She turned around, and there was something in her face that made even Ilva straighten. "They think we'll get tired. They think spring is a mood, and moods pass."

"And?" Ferro asked.

"And we should write back," Wren said. "But not to them. To everyone else. Because I know what comes next — I've sung the verses, I know the shape of the story — and the next part isn't a war." She looked at the letter, at the careful humane words, at the trap dressed as courtesy. "The next part is a choice. Theirs and ours. And somebody has to stand at the door when they make it."

Outside, the last of the festival lamps drifted out on the tide, small clay doors ajar, carrying wishes east across the water — and one of them, though no one saw it go, flickered once against the current, as if something on the far side of the horizon had answered.
