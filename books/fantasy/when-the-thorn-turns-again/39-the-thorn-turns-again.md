## 39. The Thorn Turns Again

The seasons had learned to pass through the door.

Wren noticed it first in the light. In spring, what came through the threshold was green-gold and smelling of rain on far-off hills; in autumn it arrived thin and silver, carrying the cold of a country that remembered winter differently than the free cities did. Now the light that spilled from the Door of Aldermere across Dominic's small stone chamber was the color of late harvest—amber and patient—and it lay over everything like a blessing that had forgotten to be dramatic.

Wren climbed the last of the stairs with a basket on her hip and bread wrapped in cloth against her chest, still warm enough that she could feel its heartbeat through the linen. The guards at the undercity gate had stopped saluting her years ago; now they just nodded, the way you nod at someone whose footsteps you know. Old Ferro's maps hung in the stairwell, replaced by copies because the originals lived behind glass in Sable's new guildhall, but Wren always touched the wall where the true ones used to be. Habit. Pilgrimage. The same thing, maybe.

Dominic sat where she always sat: in the doorway itself.

Fifteen years, and Wren had never once seen her anywhere else. The threshold had taken her the way a riverbed takes a stone—not cruelly, not as punishment after that first terrible year, but as a shape she had chosen and then grown into until the choosing and the shape were the same thing. Her feet rested on the free cities' side. Her shoulders and her folded hands rested on Aldermere's side. She was the hinge. She was the sentence's final clause, made flesh and patience, standing between a starving country and the world that had abandoned it, letting through only what could be carried gently: seeds, songs, memories, the occasional wind that smelled of heather and old stone.

She was grayer now. The lines around her eyes had deepened into something like riverbanks—worn there by constant passage, holding firm all the while.

"You're late," Dominic said, without opening her eyes.

"Bread takes as long as it takes." Wren set the basket down and unwrapped the loaf. "Nan's recipe. Well—one of Nan's recipes. The one that isn't secretly a map."

"Shame. I've been meaning to get lost."

Wren laughed, because it was expected, because it was true, because some jokes between them had been worn smooth as beach glass by fifteen years of telling. She broke the loaf and handed half through the threshold, careful to keep her fingers on her own side of the invisible line. Not that the line would hurt her. It simply wasn't hers to cross. The door opened for the homesick, for the grieving, for anyone who carried Aldermere inside them—but it kept its keeper, and everyone had agreed long ago that this was the price and the gift both, and no one spoke of undoing it except in the dark, alone, where such thoughts went to die of loneliness.

They ate in the comfortable quiet of two people who had run out of need for words but never out of pleasure in them.

* * *

Afterward, Wren did what she came to do every week, though neither of them called it a ritual anymore. She read to her.

Sable's ledger—the new one, the green one, bound in leather dyed the color of Alderman moss—lay open on Wren's knees. Each entry was read aloud at the door, because Dominic had asked for it once, early on, in a voice so carefully casual that Wren had understood immediately how much it mattered, and had never missed a week since.

"Entry forty-one," Wren said. "Marta of the Weavers' Quarter traded one memory of her grandmother's kitchen—for which she'd been saving since the bad years—to Ilva's watch, in exchange for nothing at all. Ilva said the memory was payment enough for the story of how to fix a leaking roof. Marta cried. Ilva pretended she hadn't."

"The watch is getting soft," Dominic murmured.

"The watch is getting *good*. There's a difference, and you taught it to them, even from down here."

"Entry forty-two?"

"Alderman barley, first sowing from seed that came through last spring. It took. Ferro's apprentice drew a map of the field for the ledger, which is ridiculous, because it's one field."

"Nothing Ferro ever drew was ridiculous. Overwrought, maybe. Obsessive. But the man mapped a country out of marginalia and lullabies. One field is a holiday for him."

Wren turned pages. Entries about returned memories finding their owners. About half-speakers teaching their fractured syllables to children who absorbed the language whole, the way children do, so that what had been broken into twelve pieces was becoming one voice again, younger and less afraid. About the festival last month when the free cities' bells and the Alderman songs had overlapped in the great square until nobody could say where one music ended and the other began.

And then, near the back, an entry Wren had written herself and not yet shown anyone:

"Entry sixty-three," she said, and paused.

Dominic opened her eyes. After fifteen years she could hear a hesitation the way other people heard thunder. "Wren."

"It's nothing. It's—" Wren looked at the page. At the words she'd inked three nights ago by candlelight, words that had been growing in her like the thorn-rose grew along the guildhall wall, slowly, thornily, inevitably. "It's a question I've been carrying. And you always said the door deserves honesty more than comfort."

"I say a lot of things. Most of them are about bread."

"Dominic."

Her name in Wren's mouth, in Alderman, in the old cadence Nan Hollis had taught her before the teaching became the last verse and the last verse became a grave facing east—all of it landed in the chamber like a struck bell. Dominic went still in her doorway. The amber light from beyond her shoulders seemed to lean closer, listening.

"Ask," Dominic said.

* * *

Wren set down the ledger and stood. She was twenty-two now, tall in the way of people who had been forced to grow up twice, once as a child and once as a symbol. She walked to the threshold's edge and stood on her own side of the line, close enough to see the fine scar on Dominic's palm where the gift had burned through her, the one she never hid and never explained.

"When I was nine," Wren began, "and you found me scratching at the guild door, I didn't only speak to you because I needed help. I spoke because your oath-bond burned when you heard me, and I felt it. Did you know that? Children feel things like that. The words in my mouth recognized the word-shaped wound in you. Like calls to like. A key hums near its lock."

"I knew," Dominic said quietly.

"And for fifteen years I've watched you stand here. Giving. Holding. Becoming the hinge so a whole country could pour itself gently into the world instead of breaking through. And every year I've thought: this is the noblest thing I will ever see, and also the loneliest, and also—" Wren's voice caught, and she let it, because Nan had taught her that a song without a catch in it hadn't been sung honestly yet. "Also, somewhere in the middle of all that nobility, you forgot something. We all let you forget it."

"Forget what?" But Dominic's voice had gone rough, and Wren knew she already knew.

Nan's proverb. The oldest one, the one from before the erasure, before the usurper, before any of it—back when Aldermere was just a country and not a ghost. Wren spoke it first in Alderman, the way it should always be spoken first, the way it had been spoken over cradles and grave-mounds and doorposts for a thousand years:

*"When the thorn turns again, it does not become a rose. It becomes the rose's root."*

Then, in the trade-tongue, for the woman who had spent fifteen years believing she was still, at bottom, a weapon being kept safely pointed nowhere:

"Do you understand what Nan was telling us, all those nights with the songs? A thorn isn't a failed flower. It isn't the part of the plant that went wrong and needs forgiving. The thorn is what protected the root through the frost. Through the burning. Through fifteen years of winter." Wren pressed her hand flat against her own chest, where the language lived. "You spent your whole life thinking you were the thorn—that your gift was a danger, that your silence was penance, that standing in this door was the best use a dangerous thing could be put to. But thorns turn, Dominic. That's what they're *for*. Everything you buried—the ledgers, the ledgers' worth of care you smuggled into the world through impossible trades, the girl who spoke a killing word and then spent her life making sure every word she touched afterward was a shelter—you didn't bury any of it. You rooted it. All of it. This door isn't held shut by a sacrifice. It's held open by a gardener."

The amber light trembled.

Or perhaps it was Dominic who trembled—a flicker, no more, a shiver passing through the keeper like wind through wheat. For fifteen years Wren had seen her weep exactly twice: once when Quillon's key had arrived with his dying note, and once the day Nan was buried facing east. Both times she had blamed the draft.

"This is the entry," Wren said softly. "Entry sixty-three. *The guild records that Dominic Thackeray, last swordspeaker, keeper of the Door of Aldermere, has been appraised. Final appraisal. Item: one thorn, long turned. Value:*" — Wren smiled through the blur in her own eyes — "*root of everything.*"

For a moment, nothing. The door breathed its slow breath of heather and rain-on-stone. Somewhere above them the city went about its afternoon, bells and carts and children shouting in two languages braided into one.

Then Dominic Thackeray—who had hidden behind ledgers, who had burned pages and lost ranks, who had stood unarmed before armies and unbroken before envoys, who had poured her very self into a threshold and called it peace—smiled.

Not the wry almost-smile she rationed out at good ledger entries. Not the grim baring of teeth she'd worn through the war years. A real smile, wide and crooked and startled out of her like a bird flushed from cover, the smile of the girl she must have been before any of it, before the word that killed, before the oaths—surfacing through fifteen fathoms of duty and grief to take one long breath of air.

"There she is," Wren whispered.

"Don't tell Sable," Dominic said, and her voice cracked clean in the middle of the joke, and she didn't try to mend it. "She'll put it in the ledger."

"Too late. Entry sixty-four. *The keeper smiled. Witnesses: one. The witness will testify to it annually, whether asked or not.*"

Dominic laughed then—actually laughed, the sound bouncing off the founders' stones, startling pigeons somewhere up the stairwell—and reached across the threshold as far as the hinge allowed, and Wren met her hand halfway, palm to palm, the scar against the living skin. The door hummed beneath them, that vast and patient attention on its far side turning toward them the way a field turns toward sun, and for a heartbeat Wren felt what Dominic must feel every hour of every day: the whole gray-and-green weight of a country leaning on one woman's faith that it deserved to come home.

It did deserve it. They had planted it here instead, and it had taken root, and the proof was in a hundred entries in a green book and one field of impossible barley.

"Thank you," Dominic said. Simply. As if fifteen years of gratitude could fit in two words. As if, Wren realized, it could—if the two words were spoken by someone who meant them in Alderman.

* * *

Wren stayed until the bread was gone and the light through the door shifted toward evening, going violet, the color Aldermere wore when it missed its people most. Then she gathered her basket and her ledger and stood to go, because even miracles observed proper visiting hours.

At the top of the stairs she paused. Turned back.

"Dominic. There's something else. It came through yesterday morning—Sable logged it, but I wanted to bring it to you myself."

The keeper's head tilted, the old hunter's attention waking beneath the peace. "Something came through?"

"Not through. *From.*" Wren chose the words carefully, the way Nan had taught her, the way speakers must when the language is alive. "A seed case. Sealed. With the sigil stamped in iron—the same sigil that was on my key. The same sigil that was on the seal your family signed. Except this one isn't stamped. It's *grown*, Dominic. The iron's been shaped like that from the inside. Whatever sent it made the mark the way a tree makes a ring."

Silence. The amber-violet light steadied, then quickened, like a pulse.

"What's inside it?" Dominic asked.

"That's the thing." Wren's heart was beating hard now, with fear or joy or that place where the two share a coat. "We can't tell. It doesn't open for me. It doesn't open for any of us. It's warm, and it hums the first line of the opening sentence, and it waits." She held up the ledger. "There's room on the next page for whatever it turns out to be. But I'm not writing anything down until you've seen it. Some entries, you make together."

Dominic looked at the threshold behind her—at the country she had held open with her whole self for fifteen years—and then at Wren, her found daughter, her fellow clause, her proof that thorns become roots.

"Bring it down," she said, and the door's light gathered itself around her like a mantle, and the smile hadn't left her face. "Let's find out what my country wants to say."

Wren went to fetch the seed case, taking the stairs two at a time, and behind her the Door of Aldermere breathed out the scent of coming rain.
