## 1. Small Charms

There are three rules to selling memories in Vell, and I have kept all three for twenty years, which in this city is roughly as impressive as keeping your fingers.

Rule one: never take a whole life. A memory without edges goes bad, like milk left in the sun. It curdles into something that wants to live inside you instead of the bottle. I've seen what happens to takers who get greedy — there's a woman down on Sallow Street who's been trying to remember her own wedding for thirty years because she sold every other day of her marriage piecemeal, and now the pieces don't fit together anymore. She buys a cup of it back from me every month, one hour at a time, and cries, and comes back. Grief has excellent repeat business.

Rule two: never take a name. Names are hooks. Take a man's name out of his head and he'll spend the rest of his life walking past doors that feel like they should open for him. Worse, names make memories traceable, and traceable is dead.

Rule three — my own rule, not the guild's, since the guild hasn't existed in twenty years: never let them see how deep the thorn goes.

The first two rules keep me alive. The third keeps me *me*.

* * *

The night market in Vell smells of fried onions, tallow smoke, and the particular sour sweetness of too many people remembering things they'd rather forget. My stall sits between a woman who sells luck by the pinch and a man whose eels can tell when you're lying, which makes him popular with wives and unpopular with everyone else. I sell bottled memories from a folding table with a striped awning, and if you didn't know better you'd think I was selling perfume.

Which is, in its way, exactly what I'm doing.

"First kiss," said the young man who came to me just after dusk, setting down three coppers with the solemnity of a man paying off a debt. "My gran's. She's dying. She keeps asking about a boy named Tomas and nobody knows who he was."

I had a dozen first kisses on my shelf, each stoppered in glass no bigger than my thumb, each glowing faintly with the color of whoever gave it away. Hers was rose-gold, shot through with rain. I'd taken it from her myself, eleven years ago, back when she still had most of a mind and wanted to keep only the best part of it somewhere safer than her own failing head. She'd laughed while I did it. Said it was like being kissed twice.

"That'll be five," I said, and he paid it without haggling, which told me everything about how much he loved her.

He uncorked it right there at the stall, because they always do, even though I tell them to wait, take it home, be somewhere soft when it lands. The rose-gold light poured up over his face and his eyes went distant and wet, and for four or five seconds he was an old woman in 1947 standing under an awning with rain coming down and a boy named Tomas saying something that made her laugh so hard she had to hold onto his sleeve.

Then it was gone, and he blinked, and pressed the stopper back in with hands that shook slightly.

"She looks happy," he said. "In there. Is she happy?"

"In there," I said, "she's always nineteen and it's always about to rain."

He thanked me. They always thank me. That's the part nobody warns you about — how much being thanked starts to weigh, after enough years. Every bottle I sell is somebody's grief, decanted and made portable, and I am the ferryman of small sorrows, and ferries don't get to weep.

* * *

Business was good that night, which should have been my first warning. Good business in Vell means either a festival or a funeral, and nobody was celebrating.

A widow bought back an hour of her husband snoring, because she'd sold it in a bad winter and discovered that silence is worse than noise. A stonemason wanted the taste of his mother's bread, just the taste, nothing else attached to it — I keep flavors separate for exactly these requests, little amber vials of pure sensory memory, the cheapest thing I sell and the thing people come back for most. A girl of maybe sixteen asked, very quietly, if I had anything for forgetting, and I lied and said no, because rule three applies to what I sell too: I don't deal in erasure. Erasure leaves holes, and holes get noticed.

It was near midnight when the child appeared.

She couldn't have been more than eight, barefoot, trailing a smell of river-mud, the kind of kid who belongs to the market itself rather than any family. She stood at the edge of my awning holding something folded and dirty, and she looked at me the way children look at dogs they've been told not to pet.

"You're her," she said.

"I'm afraid I'm someone," I said. "Can I help you?"

She unfolded the paper. Held it up.

I have imagined this moment perhaps two thousand times over twenty years, and in every imagining I handle it well. I laugh. I say something wry. I turn the sketch around and compliment the artist, offer to buy the drawing, send her off with a copper and a story about mistaken identity.

In practice, my hands went cold and I heard my own heartbeat, which is not a sound you want to hear.

The sketch was charcoal, smudged, clearly drawn fast by someone working from a description. It showed a woman with dark hair pinned back, sharp-cheeked, standing behind a table of small glass bottles. Underneath, in the blocky official hand used for tax notices and death warrants: **THORN-GIFTED. REPORT TO ANY GREY COAT. REWARD: TEN CROWNS.**

Ten crowns. Twenty years of careful living, priced at ten crowns. I tried to feel insulted and mostly succeeded.

"Where did you get this?" I asked. My voice came out steady, which I consider one of my life's genuine achievements.

"The men put them up on the fountain," she said. "By the temple steps. There's lots of them. Different ladies." She squinted at the paper, then at me, then back at the paper, doing the child's terrible honest arithmetic of faces. "That's your nose."

"It's a common nose."

"It's your nose." She wasn't frightened, which frightened me more. Curious, hungry, the look of a kid deciding whether this was worth ten crowns or whether knowing a secret was worth more. Market children learn early that information is currency. "Are you really thorn-gifted? Can you take my memory of the flood? Mam says I scream in my sleep."

And there it was — the old pull, the reason I do this work at all, the reason the gift is called a gift by anyone who's ever needed it. Here was a child with something drowning her, offering it up to a stranger with a price tag on her head, because where else in Vell does a poor girl go?

"No," I said. "Not tonight."

"I could pay later."

"Not ever, from me. Go home."

She went, but slowly, looking back twice, and I understood with perfect clarity that I had just become either a legend or a liability to her, and that eight-year-olds are terrible vaults.

* * *

I closed early. Packed the bottles in straw, folded the awning, counted my till with hands that remembered how to be steady about halfway through. Around me the market went on — the eel-seller packing his tubs, the luck-woman sweeping spilled pinches of fortune back into her apron — and above us all, on the temple steps, grey shapes moved among the crowds with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told *no* by the city.

Grey coats. Memory-hunters. For twenty years they'd been a story, a warning mothers told thorn-touched daughters, a bogeyman left over from the purges before the peace. For twenty years I'd told myself the same thing every careful person in Vell tells themselves: *they're not looking for me specifically. I'm small. I'm careful. I follow the rules.*

But the sketches were new. The sketches were *everywhere*. And someone had described my stall, my hair, my nose, closely enough that a child who'd never met me picked me out of a night crowd.

Twenty years, ending on a Tuesday.

I banked my coals, shouldered my basket, and did what I have done every night for two decades: I walked home through streets I know like the lines of my own palm, nodding to neighbors, buying a meat pie from Tomas the pie-man — no relation, probably, to the boy in the bottle, though I've wondered — and telling myself the oldest lie in the merchant's book:

*Tonight is ordinary. Tomorrow will be too.*

The pie tasted like nothing. Above the rooftops, from the direction of the temple, I could hear the criers' bells beginning to ring — not the festival peal, but the flat, repeated single stroke that means a proclamation is coming at dawn, that means the city should gather, that means *listen.*

In my basket, wrapped in straw, forty small glass bottles glowed softly against the dark. First kisses. Dead mothers. An hour of snoring. All the griefs I'd been trusted with, all the sorrows I'd promised to keep safe.

I carried them home through the ringing dark, and behind me, faint under the bells, I thought I heard a child's voice asking someone, very seriously, whether knowing where the memory-witch lived might be worth more than ten crowns after all.
