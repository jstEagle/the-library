## 2. The Proclamation

The trouble with selling memories is that you learn exactly how much a person can lose and still walk upright. Twenty years in Vell's night market had taught me the whole curriculum: grief comes in pints, love comes in thimbles, and guilt—guilt is the only thing people will pay full price for, because they'd rather carry someone else's than their own.

I was three bottles into the evening's trade when the criers came.

The first bottle belonged to a cooper whose wife had died in the spring fever. He wanted the memory of her voice saying his name—not the deathbed version, the good one, from when they were young and she still teased him. I keep those on separate shelves. The second was for a girl who wanted to forget her own wedding. That one I refused, gently, because forgetting is a knife that cuts both ways, and some things you're meant to carry even when they bleed you. The third—

The third was interrupted by bells.

Not market bells. Market bells ring in patterns, cheerful arithmetic. These were the long, flat strokes of the proclamation bell, the ones that mean the crown has something to say and would very much like you to stop living while it says it.

My hands went still over the cooper's bottle. Across the square, other stalls went quiet too—the pie-woman, the lamp-oil man, the fellow who sells lucky charms to people who should know better than to buy luck secondhand. We all turned toward the steps of the tithe-house like flowers turning toward an unfriendly sun.

A crier climbed up, grey coat buttoned to the throat, unrolled a scroll with the ceremony of a man who has rehearsed this in front of a mirror.

"By order of His Majesty's Regency Council," he announced, in the bored voice they train into them, "the practice of memory-craft, known as the thorn-gift, is hereby declared outlawed within the walls of Vell and all its liberties. All persons possessing such gifts shall surrender themselves for assessment. Any found concealing the gifted, or trafficking in stolen remembrance, shall be assessed likewise."

Assessed. The word sat in the air like a fish left out too long.

"The mercy of the crown," the crier continued, "is extended to all who come forward freely."

I have heard about the mercy of the crown my whole life. It comes with grey coats and a wagon, and it assesses you whether you ask it to or not.

The crowd did what crowds do—it made a noise like a held breath, then began pretending nothing had happened. The cooper paid me quickly without looking at the bottle, which told me he understood the situation better than the crier gave him credit for. The girl who wanted to forget her wedding melted into the alley. And I stood behind my little table of bottled first-kisses and dead mothers and did the arithmetic I'd been doing all my life, only now with different numbers.

Twenty years. That's how long I'd been careful. Never take a whole life. Never take a name. Sell small, sell sad, sell forgettable—a mother's lullaby here, a summer afternoon there. Nothing that would make a grey coat look twice. Nothing that would make anyone remember *me* too hard.

And now the crown had decided that the gift itself was the crime.

*They've always known,* said a cold little voice in the back of my skull. *This isn't new law. It's new courage.*

* * *

Marta the hedge-healer lived two doors down from my rooms above the chandler's shop, in a crooked house that smelled of comfrey and candle smoke. She wasn't thorn-gifted, not properly—her art was bonesetting and bitter teas and the particular magic of holding a stranger's hand while they hurt. But she knew things. She knew what a person's silence meant, could read a lie in the set of a shoulder. In Vell, that was close enough to witchcraft on any given Tuesday, depending on the mood of whoever was asking.

I heard the wagon before I saw it.

Wheels on cobblestone have a language. Market carts clatter; funeral carts roll slow and solemn. This one rolled with the heavy, deliberate patience of authority, and it stopped outside Marta's door just as the sky went the color of a bruise.

There were four of them in grey, and a fifth with a chain-of-office who read from a paper while two neighbors watched from windows with shutters open exactly a finger's width. Marta came out on her own, which was like her. She'd put on her good shawl. She'd combed her hair. When you're going to be dragged, apparently, you dress for it.

"I've done nothing but set legs and boil willow bark," she said, loud enough for the shutters.

"That will be determined at assessment," said the officer, in the tone of a man determining nothing ever again in his life.

She looked at the wagon, and then—and this is the part I'll carry until someone takes it from me—she looked directly at my window. Not searching. *Finding.* She knew where I stood in the dark, though there was no light near me and no reason for her eyes to land there.

What passed across her face wasn't fear and wasn't warning. It was apology. As if being arrested were a discourtesy she was committing against me personally.

Then they took her arm, and she went, and the wagon rolled off toward wherever assessments happen, and the shutters closed one by one like eyes deciding not to have seen anything.

I sat down on the floor of my room and discovered my hands were shaking.

Here's the thing about twenty years of caution: it feels like safety right up until it doesn't. I had rules the way a drowning man has straws. Never take a whole life. Never take a name. Keep your stock small, keep your customers grieving rather than powerful, keep yourself dull as dishwater. I'd built a fortress out of being boring.

But Marta hadn't been thorn-gifted at all, and they'd taken her anyway. Which meant the fortress had never been a fortress. It had been a story I told myself at night, and tonight the story had died in the street outside my window wearing a good shawl.

I got up. I lit the lamp. And I started to burn my stock.

* * *

You should understand something about destroying memories: it's not like burning letters. A bottled memory resists. It wants to be remembered—that's its whole nature—and when you break the seal over flame, the last of it goes up in a breath of something almost like scent. The cooper's wife saying his name. A sailor's last sight of a green coast. A woman's whole face when her son was born. Twenty years of small mercies, each one a little warm ghost that curled past my face on its way to nowhere.

I worked fast and I hated every minute of it, and if you think that's sentimentality, consider what it means to be the only person alive who remembers these things now. Every bottle was somebody's kept treasure, entrusted to me because keeping was my gift. Burning them felt less like destroying evidence and more like burying the dead twice.

By the time the sky went properly black, my table was bare and my lungs were full of smoke and grief-ghosts. I packed what mattered: coin, a knife, the plain cloak that makes a woman furniture. I was three steps from the door when the knocking started below.

Grey coats at the chandler's. Asking after the memory-wife. Asking after *me*.

Out the back, over the wall, into the alley where Vell keeps its garbage and its secrets. My heart performed feats of engineering. I went the way I'd gone a hundred times delivering quiet orders to quiet clients—down through Tanner's Row, along the drowned stair where the old city sinks into the new one, into the warren under the granary district where the rent is cheap and the questions are fewer.

That's where I found her.

An old woman lay in the lee of a collapsed doorway, half-hidden by her own blanket, and I'll be honest—I nearly walked past. The undercity is full of dying people, and a woman with a gift like mine learns early that you cannot catch everyone who falls. But her hand shot out and caught my ankle with a grip that had no business belonging to a body that far along, and when I turned, her eyes were bright and fixed and terrible.

"You're the thorn-girl," she rasped. "The one who sells small."

Word travels. Apparently even down here.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, because the rules say deny, deny, deny, and rules are what you cling to when everything else is burning.

Her laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing sounded like the end of something. "Don't waste your lies on me, girl. I haven't the time to appreciate them." She fumbled beneath her blanket and pressed something into my hands—cold glass, stoppered with black wax, sealed with a sigil I didn't recognize. A vial, no bigger than my thumb, heavier than it had any right to be. Memories have weight. This one had *mass*.

"No," I said, because I could feel it even through the wax—a memory vast enough to drown in, the opposite of everything I sold. "Take it to someone else. Take it anywhere else."

"There is nowhere else." Her fingers tightened on mine, and her voice dropped to the fierce whisper of the dying, who have earned the right to skip pleasantries. "The ones in grey are hunting it. They burned the archive at Corvane. They killed the archivist and his apprentices—all seven, and the youngest was fourteen." Her eyes bored into mine. "Whatever you do, whatever they promise you, however they make it hurt—do not let them have it. Swear it."

"I'm a merchant," I said stupidly. "I sell kisses and lullabies. I don't—"

"You swear it," she said, "or die knowing you wouldn't."

Somewhere above us, boots crossed the grate of the drowned stair. Grey coats, spreading through the undercity like ink through water. The vial sat in my palms, cold and patient and enormous, and the old woman's grip was failing, and there was no one else. There was never anyone else. That's how the world works: responsibility doesn't knock politely at the doors of the qualified. It grabs the ankle of whoever happens to be walking past.

"I swear," I said.

She smiled—actually smiled, relief moving across her ruined face like weather—and her hand fell away. "Good girl. Don't open it till you must. It bites." Her breathing slowed, then steadied into something quieter. "It was his, you know. At the end, he chose—" But the sentence went out of her like a tide, and she didn't come back with the rest of it.

*His*. Whose? I knelt there in the dark with a dead woman's secret in my hands and the word ringing like the proclamation bell.

*His.*

I tucked the vial deep inside my cloak, next to my heart, where I could feel its weight with every breath, and I ran—deeper into the undercity, away from the grey coats, away from my burned stall and my safe twenty years, carrying the one thing in Vell worth killing seven people over.

Don't open it till you must, she'd said.

I had a feeling I was going to find out exactly when "must" was.
