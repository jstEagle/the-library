## 3. What the Vial Holds

The undercity of Vell smells like a promise the city made to itself and then broke. Old stone, old water, old secrets seeping through the seams. I'd been down there three nights, in a dry corner of a disused cistern where the damp had given up trying, and I had not slept, and I had not opened the vial.

I want it noted, for whatever record survives me, that I resisted for three entire nights. That's practically sainthood, by my standards.

The vial sat on my knee like a small amber judgment. Sealed with grey wax—the color of the ones hunting me now, which felt less like coincidence and more like an old woman's last joke at my expense. She'd pressed it into my hands while her market stall burned behind us, her fingers cold as riverbed stones, and she'd said *keep it from the ones in grey*, and then she'd died the way people do when they've been holding something too long: all at once, like setting down a burden.

I'm a merchant. That was my whole identity, my whole clever little life. I sold bottled memories—first kisses, dead mothers, the smell of a summer that never actually happened because memory improves with bottling the way wine does. I had rules. Never take a whole life. Never take a name. Never take anything you couldn't carry in one hand and give back with the other.

And here was a thing someone else had carried, and died carrying, and handed to me with both hands.

"Right," I told the dark. The dark didn't answer. It rarely does, but down there it felt rude enough to try.

I broke the wax seal with my thumbnail.

* * *

You should understand how taking a memory works, since you're about to be taken along for this one. It isn't reading. It isn't watching. A bottled memory is a room, and when you open it, you walk inside and the door closes behind you, and for as long as the vial holds out, you are *there*. You wear someone else's eyes. You borrow their heartbeat. And if the memory is strong—if it was taken from someone who lived it rather than someone who merely witnessed it—you forget, briefly and completely, that you were ever anyone else.

This one was strong. This one was a cathedral.

The first thing I knew was sunlight, and laughter in my own throat—not mine, his—and the taste of honeyed wine and dust, and I was standing in a garden I did not know on a morning that had been dead for two hundred years, laughing at something a woman with thorn-marks silvering her forearms had just said.

The king. I knew it the way you know your own reflection, which is to say instantly and with a small shock of recognition. He was young. That was the first obscenity of it. Every portrait, every coin, every grim icon in every crown chapel showed the Last King as a stern gray man bearing the weight of a nation. But this man was maybe thirty, with ink-stained fingers—he'd been writing before he came out here—and he laughed like someone who had never once been interrupted.

He turned to the woman and said, "Say it again, Sera. Say the part about the pigeons."

"The pigeons," she said, patient as a saint, "have better claim to this city than your whole line, and they've governed more honestly."

"And what do we do about their superior government?"

"We pay them tribute, obviously. Bread crusts."

"Sensible." He bowed to a pigeon. Actually bowed. And the garden roared—I could feel the laughter around us like weather, dozens of people, gifted and unmarked alike, sitting on the grass together as though the world were a place where that was simply done.

I want to be honest with you: I fell in love with him in the first five minutes. Everyone does. That's what the memory is *for*, or what it became—it's not a recording, it's an argument, and arguments begin by making you love the thing you'll have to watch break.

The morning went on. He argued tax policy with a hedge-witch and lost gracefully. He let a child put a crown of dandelions on his actual crown, which sat beside him on the grass like a discarded hat. He asked a young man with storm-gray eyes what the gifted wanted, truly wanted, and the young man said, "To stop being a question," and the king wrote that down on his cuff like it mattered more than the tax ledgers.

I thought: *this is what they took from history.* Not the massacre. The pigeons. The dandelions. They burned the man and kept only the monument, because a monument can't make you grieve him, and grief is dangerous when the next thing coming is rage.

Then the light changed. That's how memories mark their turns—not with chapter headings, but with weather. The sun went behind cloud and stayed there, and the smell of the garden curdled into smoke, and the king was no longer sitting in grass. He was standing in a hall I recognized from paintings: the Long Chamber, where the Accord was signed, except there was no parchment on the table. There was a map, and there were red marks on it, and there were men in grey standing along the walls like furniture that had learned to stand.

His face had aged ten years in a breath. Grief will do that, or guilt; from the inside of his head, I couldn't tell which, and that ambiguity was the worst part.

"They refuse the registry," one of the grey men was saying. "Thornbury itself has closed its gates. Your majesty, they say the gift cannot be licensed."

"It can't," the king said quietly. "That's rather the point of it."

"Then it must be controlled."

He looked at the map a long time. I was inside his looking. I felt what he felt, and here is the thing I will spend the rest of my life not forgiving the memory for: I felt *reasonableness*. I felt a tired, decent man walking step by step down a road paved entirely of sensible arguments, each one small, each one defensible, none of them feeling like a cliff until the ground was already gone.

"If we cannot license the gift," he said slowly, "and we cannot permit it ungoverned, and the gifted will not surrender it—" He stopped. Even then, some part of him was still asking the garden's question, still hoping someone would hand him a fourth option. No one did. The grey men had seen to that, I understood suddenly; they had spent years closing doors and calling it counsel.

"Then it must be ended," he finished.

Not shouted. Barely above a whisper. And the grey men nodded, and one of them smiled very slightly, the way a man smiles when the trap he built finally closes on the animal he always intended it for.

The king ordered it himself. That was the second obscenity. Not a decree issued in his name by ministers—he stood in that chamber and gave the order in words so plain they sounded almost gentle. *Begin at Thornbury. Spare no one marked. Do it quickly, and do not tell me the numbers.*

Do not tell me the numbers.

I have sold the memories of murderers. Small ones, sad ones—a jealous husband, a panicked soldier. Murderers always describe their crime as happening *to* them, as though the knife moved on its own. This was worse. This was a good man doing a monstrous thing in full possession of himself, because he had been persuaded that the alternative was chaos, and because everyone around him had arranged to be more afraid of the gifted than of him.

The memory held me through it. Of course it did. It made me stay for the reports, the ash, the lists that grew too long to read aloud until someone suggested simply weighing them instead. It made me feel his hands shake and his conviction hold anyway, because by then admitting error cost more than continuing, and that, friends, is the whole engine of every horror ever committed: the arithmetic gets inverted somewhere and nobody stops to check.

The memory ended where the taker had chosen to end it—which meant someone had stood close enough to the king, in his final hour or near it, and pulled this out of him whole. Taken his best day and his worst day in one pull. That takes either enormous skill or enormous hatred, and the old woman's cold fingers came back to me, and I thought: perhaps both. Perhaps she loved him too.

* * *

I surfaced in the cistern gasping, on my hands and knees, my own name a stranger's word in my mouth for three full seconds. Astrid. Astrid Thornbury. Merchant. Neutral party. Keeper of small charms and careful rules.

Except.

Except that I had a thorn-gift myself, didn't I, the very thing the king had ordered ended, and I had spent twenty years pretending it was a shopkeeper's trick—bottling kisses, charging fair rates, keeping my head down so well I'd mistaken hiding for innocence. The grey coats weren't hunting me because I was dangerous. They were finishing the king's arithmetic. Two hundred years late, still working the same sum, still refusing to check it.

And the vial—oh, the vial. I sat against the wet wall and laughed, the hollow kind, the kind that echoes wrong in empty places. The crown wasn't hunting the gifted because we were a threat to peace. They were hunting us because this memory exists. Because somewhere in Vell an old woman had carried the proof that the throne was founded on theft—that the Last King stole the gift from the people it belonged to, murdered its keepers, and then built chapels on the graves and called it order. Destroy the memory, destroy the witnesses, and theft becomes heritage.

They didn't want the thorn-gifted gone because we were dangerous. We were dangerous because we remembered.

My careful little life lay around me in pieces, and I found I couldn't mourn it. Twenty years of neutrality, and neutrality, it turns out, is just a side you take slowly, without noticing, while other people pay for your comfort.

Somewhere above me, boots crossed the street grates—grey coats, walking their rounds over my head, hunting a merchant who no longer existed. And far off, deeper in the undercity, water dripped in a rhythm almost like footsteps, almost like someone who had been waiting down here a long time for me to finish screaming.

I pocketed the vial. Empty now of vision but heavier than lead.

"You could still run," I told myself. South, past the salt marshes, some village with no name worth stealing.

But even as I said it, I heard, soft and unhurried, the sound of someone clapping slowly in the dark ahead—and a voice like oiled hinges say, "Well now. The little charm-seller finally opened her inheritance."
