## 4. Grey Coats

The first thing you learn in the Inquisition is that everyone lies. The second thing is that the lies have a smell—or they do if you're cursed the way I'm cursed—and most of them smell like wet wool and fear-sweat and the particular sourness of a man who has already decided what he's going to say before you've asked.

Astrid Thornbury's lie smelled like rosewater.

I hadn't smelled it in twenty years, but a man doesn't forget the scent of the thing that ruined him. He carries it around like a splinter worked too deep to dig out, and one day he leans against a doorpost in the Ash Market and there it is again, faint as a hymn from a closed church: rosewater and thorn-ink and something underneath, green and growing, the smell of a gift being used.

She'd stood at that stall three days ago. She'd sold a bottled memory to a grieving widow—small charm, nothing illegal on its face, though the Proclamation had made even small charms a flogging offense—and then she'd walked away south toward the undercity with a vial in her pocket that half the grey coats in Vell would burn a district down to find.

I knew it was her the way you know a face across a crowded square after decades. Not because the sketch was good. The sketch was terrible. The sketch made her look like every other dark-haired woman in Vell, which is to say it made her look like a stranger.

But I wasn't looking for a sketch. I was looking for a girl who used to sit on the aqueduct wall with her skirts hitched up, eating stolen plums and telling me, with total sincerity, that memory was the only honest currency in the world because you couldn't counterfeit grief.

*You couldn't counterfeit grief.* I'd believed her. I'd been sixteen and stupid, and she'd been seventeen and beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful, and between us we'd managed to be wrong about nearly everything that mattered.

"Lord Inquisitor." Sergeant Vane at my elbow, breathless, holding a ledger like it might bite him. "The stallholder who rented the corner—the market clerk says she paid in old coin. Pre-Regency mint."

Of course she did. Astrid never paid with anything that could be traced, and old coin was untraceable by design, minted before the crown thought to put faces on money. She'd been preparing to run for years, maybe. Or she'd simply always been the kind of person who kept an escape route polished, the way other people keep their boots.

"Which direction did she go?"

"The undercity, sir. Down by the tanneries."

The undercity. Where the old guild halls rotted, where the memory-thieves had burrowed after the outlawry like rats into a granary. Where a woman carrying the last true king's stolen memory would go if she wanted protection—or if she wanted to sell it.

I told myself that was what she wanted. I told myself a lot of things over the next two days, and the trouble with telling yourself things is that eventually you start believing them, and then you're no better than the liars in your interrogation rooms.

* * *

My gift—I refuse to call it a gift; the church calls it Discernment, my mother called it a curse, and both of them were flattering themselves—works like this: I stand near a person, and their memories give off a scent only I can catch. Strong emotions linger longest. Grief smells like rain on hot stone. Guilt smells like copper. Love, when it's real, smells like woodsmoke in winter, and I have smelled it perhaps four times in my career, and hated every one of them.

It makes me very good at my work. It also means I can't attend a funeral without tasting ash in my throat for a week, and it means that when I walked through the tannery quarter at dusk with six grey coats behind me, the whole district screamed at me in scents. Terror hung off the drying-racks. Desperation pooled in the gutters. And threading through all of it, thin as wire:

Rosewater.

Fresher than the market. Hours old, maybe less. She was close.

"Spread out," I said. "Grey cloaks visible. We're not here to snatch—we're here to be seen. If she's smart, she runs. If she runs, we follow at distance. Nobody touches her without my word."

Vane gave me a look. Vane is a decent man, which is a liability in this profession, and his look said: *the Proclamation says seize or kill, my lord, it doesn't say follow politely.*

"The Proclamation," I said, "also says the memory is to be recovered intact. A dead courier helps no one."

Which was true. Which was also not the reason. The reason had no name I was willing to speak, so I dressed it in procedure and marched it around the district until it stopped looking at me.

We found traces instead of a person. A cellar door with a fresh-cut lock. A beggar who suddenly remembered he was blind when we approached. And in a doorway off Tanner's Row, chalked low on the stone where only someone kneeling would see it: a small spiral mark, a thorn curled inward.

The guild's sign. The Thieves of Memory, marking territory—or offering shelter. So she'd found them, or they'd found her. Either way, the clock was running. The guild didn't protect people out of charity; they protected assets. And an asset like that vial would be sold within the week to whichever rebel cell could pay, and then the war would come back to Vell wearing a hundred faces, and everything I'd spent twenty years holding shut would tear open.

That's what I told myself, kneeling in the doorway with my fingers on the chalk spiral. That it was about the city. About peace. About duty.

The chalk dust came off on my fingertips, and underneath the dust the stone smelled of her—not rosewater now, just stone and cold and the green ghost of her gift—and I stayed crouched there a moment longer than any competent inquisitor would have, breathing it in like a starving man outside a bakery.

* * *

Twenty years ago, before the outlawry, before the Proclamation, there had been a summer that lasted roughly forever, the way summers do when you're young enough to be fooled.

There was a field behind the orphanage wall where the gifted children were kept—quarantined, the matron called it, as if a fever were going around—and Astrid Thornbury was the worst of us. Worst behaved, boldest, the one who climbed the wall first and dared the rest of us to follow. She had a trick she'd do with plums: she'd hold one up and say, *close your eyes,* and when you opened them you'd remember eating it, sweet and cold, juice running down your chin, even though the plum was still whole in her hand. A memory of a plum, given free. First thing she ever stole for me, and the first thing she ever gave me, and I've never entirely sorted out which was which.

"You'll waste it," I told her once, meaning the gift. "Selling pieces of yourself. One day there won't be anything left."

"There's more of me than you think," she said, lying flat on the wall with the sun in her hair, utterly certain. "And anyway, Corvus—what's the point of keeping a whole self nobody wants?"

Somebody wanted hers. Somebody had always wanted hers. That was the tragedy of Astrid Thornbury: she gave away pieces of herself for coins because the world had taught her early that she was worth more in parts than whole.

Then the Purge came. The old king died, the Regency took the throne, and overnight our gifts went from curiosities to crimes. I remember the proclamation nailed to the orphanage gate. I remember the matron reading it aloud in a voice like a funeral bell. I remember Astrid turning to me in the crowd and saying, quietly, "They can't outlaw what's in your head, Corvus. They'll try. They'll fail. But they'll hurt a lot of people failing."

And I remember choosing. That's the part I don't tell anyone. There was a moment—a long one, a whole held breath of one—when she looked at me and I understood she was asking me to run with her, out past the wall, past the city, anywhere the grey coats weren't. And I was fifteen and frightened and I loved her in the dumb silent way boys love girls they think are above them, and I chose the gate instead. Chose the Inquisition's offer: serve, and your Discernment will be licensed rather than punished. Serve, and be the exception. Be the good monster among the bad ones.

She never asked me twice. That was the cruelest kindness she ever did me.

* * *

Two days later, a tanner's apprentice turned informant—paid, terrified, and therefore useless in equal measure—told us a story about a woman with a satchel who'd bought lamp oil and salt meat and asked after the flooded cisterns beneath the old temple district.

The cisterns. Not the guild halls. She was avoiding the guild, or she'd already refused them, and if she'd refused the Thieves of Memory then she was alone in the dark under Vell with the most dangerous object in the kingdom and no friends left anywhere.

I should have felt satisfaction. Duty confirmed, quarry isolated, recovery imminent. Instead I stood in the informant's smoky room and felt something that had no procedure attached to it, something that smelled—when my own treacherous gift turned inward—like woodsmoke in winter.

"My lord?" Vane said. "Shall I ready a full company? The cisterns flood at spring thaw; we'd want numbers."

"No company." I heard my own voice and didn't recognize its tone. "Six men. Quiet ones. If we come at her with an army she'll bolt into the deep tunnels and we'll lose the vial to the water."

All reasonable. All defensible. Every word of it a wall built over the same cellar.

Because the truth—the truth I carried the way she carried the vial, sealed and unspeakable—was simpler and far worse: I didn't want soldiers between us. I wanted to stand in front of Astrid Thornbury again, after twenty years, with no wall and no gate and no matron reading proclamations, and find out whether the woman who'd waited on the wrong side of it still existed somewhere inside the smuggler with the rosewater hands.

Justice required her capture. Justice required the vial. Justice required that I feel nothing but procedure.

But justice, I was beginning to suspect, had never once smelled like woodsmoke.

We went down at dawn, six quiet men and me, into the dark beneath the temple district, following a scent only I could catch—rosewater fading into stone, stone fading into water—and somewhere below us, in the drowned halls the gifted had blessed a thousand years ago, the trail ended at a flooded cistern whose black surface lay perfectly still.

Like a mirror. Like an eye. Like something waiting to be looked into.

I raised my hand, and the grey coats stopped, and the water didn't ripple at all.
