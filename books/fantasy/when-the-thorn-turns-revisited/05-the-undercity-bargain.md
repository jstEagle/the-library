## 5. The Undercity Bargain

The undercity of Vell smells like a candle that has been burning in a closed room for three hundred years—wax and old smoke and something faintly sweet underneath that you don't want to think about too hard. I'd been down there four days when the Guild found me, which surprised me, because I'd spent those four days being very good at not being found.

I should explain something about my trade. Memory-selling is a merchant's gift, not a thief's. I take what people offer me freely—a first kiss they're tired of carrying, a dead mother's laugh that hurts more than it heals—and I bottle it, and I sell it to someone who wants it. Consent on both ends, coin in the middle. That was always the difference between people like me and the Guild of the Hollowed Lantern, the memory-thieves who worked the undercity before the outlawry, taking whole lives out of heads like pulling teeth from a sleeping giant.

The crown dissolved the Guild twenty years ago. Officially. But the undercity keeps its own records, and its own residents, and apparently its own grudges.

They came for me at dusk on the fourth day, while I was eating cold eel pie in an abandoned tannery that smelled only marginally worse than the pie. Three of them, dropping down through the roof hatch with the soft confidence of cats who have never once been wrong about a room.

"Evening," said the one in front. She was older than me by a decade or so, silver-streaked hair cropped short, a scar across her jaw that looked deliberate—too straight to be accidental. "Astrid Thornbury."

"Not anymore," I said. "I'm nobody from nowhere."

"Nobody," she said, tasting it, "who's been paying rent to a smuggler named Fenwick for a cellar off Tanner's Row. Nobody who bought bread twice from the same baker, which nobody does if they're serious about hiding. And nobody who's been sitting very still for four days holding a sealed vial like it's a live coal." She smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "You're good, Thornbury. You're just not as good as you were, because you're scared now, and scared people develop patterns."

I set down the pie. There's no point finishing your last meal too fast; it only leaves more time to be hungry afterward. "And you are?"

"Maren Voss. Once of the Hollowed Lantern. Currently of nothing official, which is how we all prefer it." She crouched down to my level, which I resented, because it made her friendly instead of threatening and friendly is harder to refuse. "We've been watching you since the proclamation. You burned your stock—that was smart, though it broke my heart a little, some of those charms were lovely work. You didn't run east with the other merchants. You went *down*. Which told us you're not just hiding. You're keeping something."

"I'm keeping myself alive," I said. "It's a full-time occupation. I don't take commissions."

"We know what you're keeping." Her eyes flicked to my coat pocket, where the vial sat against my ribs like a second heartbeat. "The old woman. Sister Wren, they called her up top. Hedge-healer's grandmother, keeper of things. She gave it to you in the crowd while the grey coats were loading wagons."

My stomach did something slow and unpleasant. "You had eyes there too."

"Had? Child, half the crowd was ours. The crown thinks the Guild died. Let them. Dead guilds hear everything." She held out her hand, palm up. An old gesture. In the Guild's day it meant *trade offered in good faith*, or so I'm told. "Here's the bargain. Give us the vial, come below properly, and we'll keep you breathing. New face if you want it—I know a skin-singer who owes me. New name. Protection from Hale and his grey coats, because we know their routes better than they do. All we ask is the one small thing."

"The vial."

"The vial."

"And what happens to it after?"

Maren's smile widened by a degree. "Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously," I said. "That's rather the point of asking."

* * *

They took me to their meeting place to make the offer formally, which I suspect was also to intimidate me, and it worked, I'll admit that freely. The Hall of the Hollowed Lantern was a drowned chapel two levels below the tanneries, its nave flooded ankle-deep with water so black it reflected the lanterns like a floor of night. Maybe forty people stood in that shallow dark, and every one of them had hands that could reach into a skull and rearrange the furniture.

"You have to understand what you're carrying," Maren said, standing on the chapel steps so she could look down at me, which I also resented. Everyone down here arranged themselves vertically to their advantage. It was like being negotiated at by architecture. "Sister Wren wasn't a healer's grandmother. She was our archivist. Last of the old keepers. When the crown outlawed the thorn-gift, they didn't just want us gone—they wanted a particular memory gone, and Wren stole it right out from under them and sat on it for twenty years. Now she's dying, and she passed it to the only unaffiliated thorn-gifted soul in Vell who happened to be standing next to her. Fate, or bad luck. Same thing, usually."

"And you want to sell it."

"We want to *place* it," said a man beside her, round-faced, gentle-voiced, wearing the kind of shabby gentility that costs money to maintain. "There are people—good people, patient people—who have waited two decades for proof. Proof of what the throne did to earn itself. What the last king did to *us*."

"To you," I said.

"To everyone like us." His voice stayed gentle. That was the frightening part. Gentle men with floods at their feet are never gentle inside. "You've seen it, haven't you? You opened the vial. Don't lie to this room, Thornbury—we can smell a lie like blood in water."

I could have lied. Forty thieves and I considered lying anyway, mostly out of spite. But there was no percentage in it. "Yes," I said. "I've seen him. The king. Alive, laughing, young. And then—" My throat tightened around it still, three days later. "Then the order. The pyres at Silvermarch. His own gifted subjects, his own sworn thorn-speakers, burned on his word because they knew too much about where his crown came from. I've seen it. It's real."

The hall made a sound. Not cheering—something lower and hungrier, a sound like a tide deciding.

"That memory is worth more than gold," the gentle man said. "It's worth a war. The eastern factions will pay anything for it—not coin, they'll pay in protection, in territory, in a future where people like us aren't hunted for the crime of having heads. Give it to us, and you won't just be safe, Astrid Thornbury. You'll be *home*."

* * *

Here's the thing about home: I had one, once, and it was a stall in a night market and a set of rules I never broke. Never take a whole life. Never take a name. Never touch a memory that wasn't freely given. Twenty years of small, careful, honest work, and the entire architecture of my survival rested on one load-bearing principle: *stay out of other people's wars.*

I looked at the flood-black water and the forty faces above it, and I saw the trap laid out plain. If I gave them the vial, the rebels would use it to light the fuse, and the crown would answer with fire, and the fire would fall hardest on the undercity and the hedge-healers and every small gifted soul like the ones whose bottled kisses I used to sell. The Guild wouldn't survive it either—they were offering me protection from a storm they intended to personally summon.

But if I kept it, I was a target with a price. And if I destroyed it—

No. I hadn't let myself finish that thought yet. The vial was the last true account of the murdered, and destroying it felt like killing them twice.

"I can't give it to you," I said.

The tide-sound stopped. Forty faces went very still, the way a room goes still when everyone in it realizes the same dangerous thing at once.

"Think carefully," Maren said softly.

"I have thought carefully. That's the problem with me—thinking is most of what I do." I made myself stand straight in the ankle-deep dark, water soaking my hem, forty pairs of thief's eyes on me. "That memory belongs to the people it was taken from. Their descendants. This city. Not to a faction that wants to spend it on a war, and not to a crown that wants to bury it, and not to me, much as I'd love to be rid of it. Selling it makes you no better than the grey coats—you're just thieves with better politics."

The gentle man sighed, genuinely regretful, which somehow made it worse. "Wren chose poorly, then."

"Wren chose exactly right," I said. "She chose someone who'd say no to everyone. Including you."

Maren came down the steps slowly, her boots barely disturbing the black water, and stopped close enough that I could see the deliberate scar had been made by a thorn-gifted hand—a memory taken and carved into skin, some old punishment or some old devotion. "You understand what refusing us means," she said. "We can't have you walking around up there carrying that, unaligned. Hale will find you, or worse, someone will sell you to Hale, and then the vial goes into a crown furnace and twenty years of Wren's patience burns with it. We can't allow that. I'm sorry. Truly. You seem like a decent person, and that's such a rare thing down here."

"So what happens now?" I asked. "You kill me and take it?"

"No," Maren said. "Killing you might crack the seal—dead thorn-gifts do unpredictable things, and none of us fancy opening a vial by accident. We'll simply follow you. Everywhere. Until you slip, and you will slip, because scared people develop patterns. And when you slip, we take it, and you can go back to being nobody from nowhere." She stepped back. "Consider it a long negotiation."

They let me walk out of the drowned chapel alone, forty pairs of eyes tracking me through the dark like lanterns that had decided not to shine.

* * *

I didn't go back to the tannery. First rule after making enemies of professionals: never sleep where you've been found. I climbed instead, up through the bone-work of old aqueducts, and came out on a rooftop in the Dyer's Quarter where the wind smelled of dye-vats and freedom and, less poetically, ammonia.

Below me, Vell glowed its thousand ordinary lights. Somewhere up there in the lawful city, Corvus Hale was presumably reading reports, drinking tea, and constructing his case against me with that terrible patient precision he'd had even at fifteen, when he used it to organize our stolen apples by ripeness. Somewhere down below, forty thieves were arranging my capture with the same patience. And somewhere in between, the crown's grey coats were dragging hedge-healers into wagons because of what people like me carried in our heads.

Everyone wanted the same small glass object. The crown to destroy it. The Guild to weaponize it. Me to—well. To do what with it, exactly? I turned it over in my fingers, catching the lantern-light, watching the darkness inside shift like smoke in water.

Every road out of this ended with somebody winning and somebody burning, and I was so very tired of being the coin that everyone wanted to spend.

That was when I saw it—the light moving along the wall road above the Dyer's Quarter, steady and unhurried, the walk of a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry. A grey coat, alone, descending toward my quarter of the rooftops as if he'd received very specific directions.

Not the Guild's work, then. They'd promised to follow me, not to sell me.

Which meant someone else had already sold me first, and I had a horrible suspicion I knew exactly who wore that particular shade of grey, and exactly why his footsteps sounded like a memory I'd spent twenty years pretending I'd never taken.
