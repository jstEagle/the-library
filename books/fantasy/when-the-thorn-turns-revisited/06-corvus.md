## 6. Corvus

*Corvus*

I found her by the smell of rosemary and old water, which is to say I found her the way you find anything in Vell's undercity: by accident dressed up as skill.

The cistern beneath Tanner's Row was a cathedral once, or so the stonemasons had flattered themselves. Now its pillars stood knee-deep in black water that reflected nothing honestly, and the only light came from a crack in the vaulting where the city above leaked its lamplight down like a guilty secret. She stood at the far end of it with her back to me, which told me two things: she'd heard me coming for ten minutes, and she wanted me to know she'd heard me coming.

"Astrid Thornbury," I said, because procedure is a kind of armor.

"Don't." She didn't turn around. "Don't do the voice. The official one. You sound like a proclamation with a man attached."

I had rehearsed this arrest for six days. I had composed sentences on the ride down from the Grey House — *In the name of the Crown, surrender the vial and yourself* — and I had imagined her compliance, her resistance, her capture, all of it orderly as a ledger. What I had not imagined was standing in ankle-deep water while my heart did something undignified against my ribs.

"You're under warrant," I tried again.

"I know. I read it off your face the moment you walked in. You have a *warrant face*. You've always had a warrant face. When we were twelve you wore it when you caught me stealing plums."

"You were stealing plums."

"I was *liberating* them, and you ate half of them, so don't stand there dripping virtue at me." She turned then, and the light caught her, and twenty years fell off both of us at once and then climbed right back on, heavier than before.

She looked tired. Hunted women usually do, but on her it sat differently — like exhaustion she'd been carrying long before the grey coats came for her, something she'd been rationing out to herself in small doses since before I left Vell for the Inquisition. Her hair was shorter. Her hands were the same: quick, careful hands, hands that had held mine across a fence rail the summer everything ended.

"The vial," I said.

"Is that really why you came down here alone? No escort. No writ-bearer. Just you and your boots and your terrible stealth." She nodded at the water around my legs. "You splashed like an ox, Corvus. I could have put a knife in you twice."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because knives are for people who frighten you." Something crossed her face, fast as a fish. "You never frightened me. That was always our problem."

* * *

*Astrid*

He looked older. Everyone does; that's not news. But Corvus Hale had aged the way stone does — worn smooth in some places, cracked deep in others, and all of it in service of looking immovable. He'd been a boy made of elbows and certainty. Now he was a man made of grey wool and certainty, and I hated that the certainty was a lie on him now when it had only been innocence before.

He raised his hand toward his coat — slowly, the way you move around a spooked animal, which was insulting until I remembered he thought *I* was the spooked animal — and drew out not shackles but a small tin flask. He unscrewed it, drank, and offered it across the water.

"It's just cider," he said. "You look like you haven't eaten."

"I haven't. That's not a confession of weakness, it's a review of my finances."

He almost smiled. I watched him fight it and lose by a fraction, and I hated myself for cataloguing the victory.

We drank from the same flask standing four feet apart in a drowned church, which was either the stupidest thing either of us had ever done or the most honest, and I genuinely could not tell which.

"They'll notice how long you're down here," I said.

"They'll assume I'm being thorough."

"Are you?"

The silence went on long enough to develop opinions. Somewhere overhead, water dripped from the vaulting with the patience of something that had been counting the years longer than either of us.

"I keep telling myself yes," he said finally, and it cost him — I watched it cost him, watched the word come out bent. "Thoroughness. Procedure. Justice." He laughed, one dry note with no floor under it. "Do you know what they train us to say, in the Grey House? They train us to say the thorn-gift is theft. Taking what isn't yours. Every memory lifted is a wound, every charm a stolen good. And I believed it. Gods help me, I built a whole life on believing it."

"And now?"

"Now I keep thinking about the woman whose mother's laugh you sold her." My head came up. He saw it. "Yes, I know about your stall. I've known about your stall for years, Astrid. Do you think I let the Thornbury file gather dust out of incompetence?" He turned the flask in his fingers. "A widow bought three minutes of her dead mother laughing at a joke she'd forgotten. She came back the next week and asked if you had any more. You gave her one free. I watched you do it through a window like a lovesick fool, and I wrote in my report that the subject showed no signs of harmful practice, and I have hated myself with great consistency ever since."

"That's not mercy, Corvus. That's negligence. Your own law says so."

"My own law says a lot of things." His voice dropped, and the water seemed to carry it to me rather than the air. "My law says what you did to me was a crime."

And there it was. Twenty-two years old and laid out between us in the dark like a body.

* * *

*Corvus*

I hadn't meant to say it. Or rather — I had meant to say it every day for nineteen years, and had planned never to say it, and both of those things were true at once, which is the condition I understand best about loving someone.

Her chin lifted. Braced. Good. Let her be braced; I couldn't bear it if she weren't.

"The winter my father died," I said. "You remember."

"I remember."

"I came to you afterward. Sixteen years old, and I couldn't stop grieving. It wasn't sadness anymore, it was — weather. It was climate. I couldn't get out of bed, I couldn't eat, I kept hearing his cough in the walls. And you said you could help. You said you could make it quieter." I took a step forward and the water objected. "And you did. You reached into my skull with your little thorn-fingers and you took it. Not all of it. Enough. You took the sound of him dying and you left me the rest, and I woke up the next morning feeling *light*, and I thanked you. I thanked you, Astrid."

"Corvus—"

"And then a month later I realized I couldn't remember his face clearly. Not his face. I had his voice, his hands, his terrible jokes — but the face had gone soft, like a portrait left in the rain, and I knew. I knew what you'd taken to make room. You didn't take the grief. You took the *looking at him*. You took the part of me that kept his picture, and you called it mercy."

She was crying. Quietly, without drama, the way she did everything — as if tears were a household chore to be gotten through efficiently. It undid me more than shouting would have.

"Yes," she said. "That's what I took. Because grief was going to eat you alive, and a face is not a person, Corvus. A face is the wrapping. I left you everything that mattered and I took the thing that was killing you, and if you'd been anyone else you'd have thanked me and gone on."

"But I'm not anyone else."

"No." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at herself for the tears. "You're the one who became an Inquisitor specifically to hunt people like the girl who saved your life. I've wondered about that for nineteen years. Was it penance? Punishment? Did you join the grey coats so you'd have a lawful reason to hate me?"

"I joined because hating you was easier than the alternative," I said, "and the alternative required admitting that the mercy was correct."

The words fell into the water between us and sank. She stared at me.

"Say that again."

"No."

"*Corvus.*"

"I let you go out of duty to the truth," I said, louder, retreating behind the warrant-face where it was safe. "Understand me. There's a vial, and that vial contains something the crown will burn cities to bury, and you are carrying it, and every hour you carry it you are more dangerous than every gifted soul in Vell combined. I should take you in tonight. I am going to take you in. Tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever my nerve fails me again — but soon, and when I do, none of whatever happened in this cistern will matter. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you lying," she said softly. "You've always been a bad liar. Your ears go red."

My ears, I am certain, went red.

* * *

*Astrid*

He left the flask. He set it down on a dry ledge near the pillar like a man leaving flowers on a grave, and he said, "There's a patrol on Tanner's Row at midnight. Be anywhere else." And then he stood there a moment too long, water lapping at his boots, wanting — I would have sworn it on anything holy — wanting to say something else entirely.

"Corvus. The memory in that vial. If you saw it — truly saw it — whose side would you be on?"

"That's not a fair question."

"None of mine ever were. Ask the plum tree."

His mouth twitched. Then it settled into something older and sadder.

"I'm on the side of the law," he said. "It's the only side that ever took me in." And he turned and waded out through the black water, and the lamplight took him, and I was alone in a drowned cathedral with a king's worst sin in my pocket and a man's best confession floating on the tide.

I picked up the flask. Cider, gone warm, tasting of tin and apples and nineteen wasted years.

At midnight I was not on Tanner's Row. But I stood at the mouth of the tunnel and watched the patrol pass — six grey coats, lanterns swinging — and behind them, at the rear, walking like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to: Corvus Hale, counting his men, not counting me.

Which meant he was counting me.

And that, I thought, climbing back down into the dark with the vial cold against my breastbone, was either the most useful thing he'd ever given me —

— or the beginning of exactly the trouble I could feel coming, rolling toward us both like weather.
