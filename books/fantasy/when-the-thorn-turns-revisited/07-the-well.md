## 7. The Well

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the undercity, and I had learned to sleep inside it the way other people sleep inside blankets. It wasn't quiet, exactly. Water moves down there—always has, always will—and the old cisterns breathe through their vents like sleeping animals. But it was a silence with no one in it. No footsteps that meant my name. For six days after I walked away from the guild's offer, that was enough.

I want to be honest about those six days, because honesty is the only thing left in my inventory.

I didn't spend them being brave. I spent them being small. I found an alcove behind a collapsed wine cellar where a beggar had once chalked protective sigils on the stone—the thorn-gifted kind, from before the outlawry, back when we marked our doors to tell each other *you are safe here*—and I sat with my knees drawn up like a child hiding from a scolding, holding a sealed vial against my sternum and feeling it tick against my ribs like a second heart.

Because here is what I hadn't told the guild, and what I could barely admit to myself: the memory wouldn't leave me alone.

A bottled memory is supposed to sit still. That's the whole point of the bottle. You cork the past and you put it on a shelf and it waits for its buyer like a good little ghost. But this one pressed at the glass. At night I'd feel it warming against my skin, and when I dreamed—and I dreamed, oh, I dreamed—I dreamed in someone else's eyes. A hall hung with green banners. A king's laugh, bright as a struck coin, and then the same mouth saying *burn them all*, and the terrible calm of a man who believes he is saving something.

I had held ten thousand memories in my hands. First kisses. Dead mothers. The smell of a house before a fire took it. I knew how they behaved, the way a midwife knows babies. And I knew that this one was not behaving like merchandise. It was behaving like a debt.

On the sixth day, a boy came looking for me.

He couldn't have been more than twelve, barefoot, with the wet-lunged cough that undercity children wear like a uniform. He stood at the mouth of my alcove holding a lamp he clearly couldn't afford to burn, and he looked at me the way children look at things they've been told not to look at.

"You're the thorn," he said. Not a question. "The water lady."

"I'm nobody," I said, which was true in every way but the useful ones.

"Mad Sefa says you'd want to know about the pipes." He said it fast, like reciting a lesson he might be beaten for forgetting. "She says the thorn-gifted made the water sing, before. She says you'd know why the crown guards the deep cistern if you thought about it." He shifted his weight. "She says to think about who drinks."

Then he set the lamp down between us, turned, and ran, coughing his way into the dark like a small consumptive ghost, and I sat there thinking: *Sefa.* The hedge-healer from my street. They'd dragged her into a wagon three weeks ago and I had watched from behind my stall and done nothing, because doing nothing was my religion, twenty years of it, and I was very devout.

Except apparently Sefa had gotten a message out anyway. Or someone speaking in her voice had. Undercity saints work in strange ways.

I picked up the lamp and did what she told me to do. I thought about who drinks.

* * *

Here is a thing I knew and never once thought about, the way you know your own heartbeat: Vell runs on water. Every fountain in every square, every pump in every alley, the great public taps where washerwomen argue and children splash and the whole city comes each morning to fill its jugs—it all draws from below, from the Deep Cisterns, a honeycomb of vaulted chambers older than the crown itself. The masons' guild maintains the channels. The city guard keeps a bored pair of sentries at the access stairs, more to stop lovers and smugglers than anything else. Nobody thinks about it. Water is the one thing in Vell too obvious to suspect.

But I had been born in the undercity, and my grandmother had sung me the songs they don't put in primers anymore. Songs about the Blessing of the Waters, back when the gifted weren't outlawed but honored—or used, depending on which song you got, and my grandmother always sang both verses and let you choose. How the thorn-gifted had laid their palms on the stones of the cisterns and poured something of themselves into the rock, so the water would carry more than wetness. So the city would drink and remember what it owed.

And then the last true king burned his gifters, seized their halls, rewrote the songs into one verse, and founded a dynasty on the ashes. And the water kept flowing, blessing and all, unclaimed, unnoticed, running beneath the feet of every lie the crown ever told.

I sat in my alcove and felt the idea arrive in me the way a fever arrives—not gently, all at once, hot behind the eyes.

The vial wanted out. I had known that since the night I opened it. A memory that strong doesn't stay bottled; it leaks, it presses, it warms against your skin like a living thing begging to be born. Every day I kept it corked, it pressed harder against the glass, and every day I understood a little better why the crown hunted us so thoroughly: not because our gift was dangerous, but because memory is, and theirs was built on a hole.

They wanted the vial destroyed. The rebels wanted it sold—turned into a banner, a rallying cry, fuel for the next war, bought and hoarded and fought over by whoever could pay. The guild wanted its cut. Everyone wanted to own the truth.

And I sat there in the dark with a dead woman's dying wish ticking against my ribs, and I thought: *what if no one owns it?*

What if it went to everyone?

You can't steal a thing that belongs to everybody. That was the thought that lit the fever all the way up. The crown could seize every gifted soul in Vell, burn every sketch and proclamation, but they couldn't arrest a city. They couldn't drag forty thousand people into wagons. If the king's memory went into the water—if every man and woman and child in Vell drank their founding lie with their morning tea—then the truth would be in their bones. Unstealable. Uncorkable. Theirs.

It was a mad plan. I knew that too. I'm a merchant; I know the difference between an asset and a liability, and I was fairly sure I had just become the second. Dissolving a memory that size would take everything I had—I'd be pouring not just the king's past into the water but my own craft, my own strength, maybe my own self along with it. Memories that big don't dilute politely. They take the pourer down with them, or so the old songs said, and my grandmother had never lied to me in a song.

So there it was: my choice, finally shaped. Give it to the throne, give it to the rebels, give it to the fire—or give it to the city, and give myself with it.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in a collapsed wine cellar, loud enough that rats fled. Twenty years of careful rules. Never take a whole life. Never take a name. Stay neutral, stay small, stay safe. And now the safest thing I could do was nothing, and nothing was the one thing I couldn't do anymore, because a dying woman had put a vial in my hands and a healer in a wagon had seen fit to send a barefoot boy with a lamp, and somewhere in the last three weeks I had stopped being a merchant and become a custodian, which is a much worse job with much worse hours.

"All right," I said to the rats, to the dark, to Sefa wherever she was rotting. "All right. I'll think about who drinks."

* * *

I spent two days preparing, and I'll spare you most of it—wax, wire, a stolen crowbar, the long study of the guard rotations at the cistern stairs, which were exactly as bored as advertised. What matters is the third evening, when I went to say goodbye to the only person in the undercity I still half-trusted.

Old Tam ran a chandler's stall down in the Lampmarket that wasn't, selling candles to people who lived where the sun had never filed a complaint. He was guild-adjacent—everyone down there was—but he was old and mostly blind and he'd known me since I was small enough to steal wax ends. I brought him tallow scrap and gossip, and he paid me in warnings.

"Girl," he said, when I told him I was leaving Vell soon, going far, don't ask where, "you've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a person who's decided something." He trimmed a wick with fingers like knotted rope. "Decided people walk loud. Inside, I mean. You're walking loud inside, and the guild hears walking."

"They don't care what I do."

"They cared enough to make you an offer." He set down the wick. His clouded eyes found my face with unsettling accuracy. "And they cared enough to send that boy to you, which wasn't charity. Mad Sefa's message was real, but the delivery was arranged. Someone wanted you thinking about water."

The cellar went cold around me, though nothing moved.

"Why?" My voice came out flat. "Why would they push me toward a plan and let me keep the vial?"

"Because a plan needs a planner, and planners can be tracked." Tam sighed, and began packing wax ends into a sack with terrible patience. "Or because they figured you'd do their work for free—get the truth loose, let them scoop it up after. Or," and here his voice dropped to almost nothing, "because someone sold you the moment you walked out of their hall, girl, and grey coats pay better than rebels do."

I stood very still. Somewhere above us, the Lampmarket murmured on, hundreds of candles burning for people who believed light was simple.

"When?" I asked.

Tam shrugged, magnificent and useless. "Days ago. Maybe the hour you refused them. Maybe the hour you accepted the boy's message. Doesn't matter now. What matters is you've got perhaps a night, and the cistern stairs are the worst place in Vell to run out of time."

I kissed his forehead, because he was the closest thing I had to family and because he smelled like tallow and honesty, and then I walked out of the Lampmarket fast, mind racing, doing arithmetic I hated: the guards, the distance, the vial warm against my chest, and somewhere behind me—patient, professional, inevitable—a man in grey who knew my name and my face and, God help me, the taste of my childhood.

One night. One chance to pour a king's sin into a city's cup.

I turned toward the deep stairs and started to run.
