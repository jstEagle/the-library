## 8. Ash Wednesday Market

I have arrested forty-one thorn-gifted in my eleven years of service to the crown, and I will tell you what no inquisitor admits: it never gets easier. It gets quieter. That's worse.

On Ash Wednesday the market burned before noon.

Not by our design. The Grey Order does not burn things; fire is untidy, and untidiness leaves witnesses with opinions. But someone — later we learned a rebel cell calling itself the Kindred of the Well, because rebels have always had a gift for naming themselves after water they haven't touched yet — had set three fires along the tanners' row to draw the watch away from the square. So while Vell's finest were passing buckets like a village at a festival, I stood in the smoke-thick market with twelve grey coats and a piece of paper that told me where Astrid Thornbury would be.

The paper came from the guild. The memory-thieves' old guild, resurrected like a bad debt, and sold her location for a price I didn't ask about because I suspected the currency was something I'd disapprove of. Guilds survive everything — outlawry, purges, honest competition. It is their one talent.

"She'll run when she sees grey," said Sergeant Voss beside me. Voss is a good man with a dull imagination, which is the ideal combination for this work.

"No," I said. "She won't."

He looked at me. I did not explain. There are questions a man should not answer aloud even to himself, and *why do you know how she'll move* sits at the top of that list like a crow on a gallows.

* * *

I want to record, for whatever ledger keeps such things, that I went into that square intending exactly what the writ said: take her alive, take the vial, deliver both to the Chancery. Justice. Clean as a ledger line.

Here is what justice actually looked like: a burning market, ash falling soft as terrible snow, and a woman at a dead woman's stall wrapping bottles in wool with hands that shook only when she thought no one was watching.

Astrid Thornbury had not changed in twenty years except in every way that mattered. The girl who used to steal plums from Magister Corren's orchard and swear on her mother's grave she hadn't — the same mouth, now pressed flat with fear she was managing beautifully. The same eyes, which had once looked at me across a schoolroom desk as though I were a puzzle worth solving, and which now looked at me as though I were one.

"Hale," she said. Not Corvus. Never Corvus anymore. "You found me."

"The guild found you. I just paid the toll." I kept my hand on my cuff, where the writ was. "Give me the vial, Astrid. You keep the rest of your life."

"You mean the part you don't take."

"That's more than most get."

"It is," she agreed, terribly gently. "That's rather the whole problem, isn't it?"

And then the rebel came out of the smoke.

I never got his name. He was young, tanner's burns up both forearms, and he moved through the crowd toward her stall with the particular purposefulness of a man carrying something he believes is a weapon. I shouted the warning. Grey coats moved. And in the crush — in the screaming, shoving, ash-choked crush of a market coming apart — his bare hand closed around my wrist.

Skin to skin.

You understand what that means, for a touch-reader. For me. Eleven years I've worn gloves in public and told people it was an old burn, and eleven years the truth has been simpler and uglier: I read memory through touch, and I cannot bear it. Every handshake is a stranger's childhood poured down my throat. So I wear the gloves. So I am careful.

So I had never, not once, been touched by anyone who carried what this boy carried.

Because the boy had touched the vial. Or held it, or stood too close to its open mouth in some smuggler's cellar — it doesn't matter how. What matters is that when his skin met mine, the king's memory poured out of him and into me like water finding a crack.

* * *

Let me describe what I saw, because I need it written somewhere outside my own skull or I'll begin to doubt it, and doubting it would be the last comfortable thing I ever do.

I saw through the king's eyes.

Not the king of the histories — the marble face on the coins, the liberator whose statue stands in Chancery Square with his hand extended in eternal generosity. I saw a man on a hill above a valley town called Miren's Hollow, and I knew the name the way you know your own, and the town below was full of lights, ordinary lights, lamps being lit for evening because it was an ordinary evening. And the man felt —

This is the part I can't make clean. He felt *right*. That's the horror. He wasn't mad, or drunk, or deceived. He sat his horse and looked at the lights of the gifted families below — healers, menders, memory-keepers, children — and he felt the calm, cold certainty of a man doing arithmetic. *They can take what we remember,* he thought, in his own voice, in his own mind, and I was inside the thinking of it. *And what a man can take, a throne cannot trust.*

He gave the order. His captain hesitated — a good man, I could feel the goodness in him like warmth off a hearth — and the king repeated it, and there was nothing in him but the terrible tidiness of power defending itself.

Then fire. Then screaming that the memory preserved perfectly, every note, because memory does not flinch and men do. A woman running with a child and getting neither far enough nor fast enough. A memory-keeper, cornered in his own house, reaching out with his dying gift to *take* — to pull the horror out of his own head so he could die without it — and failing, because you cannot take what you're drowning inside.

And then, after. The king riding back through the smoke, and feeling — I felt it, God help me, I felt it as my own — not guilt. *Relief.* The relief of a debt paid. And beneath it, small as a seed, the first pale sprout of the thing that would become the official history: *It was necessary. They will understand, in time, that it was necessary.*

Twenty years of my life I have served the crown that grew from that seed. Twenty years I have hunted the descendants and inheritors of the people in that valley, and called it justice, and slept well, because I believed the lie the way you believe the floor.

The floor was the massacre all along.

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees in the ash. Voss was shouting my name. The boy-rebel was gone — fled or dragged, I couldn't say. My hands were bare; I'd lost a glove somewhere and didn't remember losing it.

And Astrid was crouched in front of me, having walked away from her own arrest to do it, looking at me with an expression I had spent twenty years making sure I'd never see again.

"You saw," she said. Not a question.

"Get back," Voss said, blade out, and two other grey coats had her flanked, and by every law I serve I should have given the order.

I looked at my own hands. They were shaking. Inquisitor Hale's hands do not shake; that is practically a job requirement. But they were shaking, and they would not stop, because they were still the king's hands somehow, still smelling of smoke that was two hundred years old.

"Corvus." Astrid's voice, low, only for me. Using the name like a key. "Now you know why I took yours. All those years ago. Why I took the bad ones out of you and left the good." Her jaw tightened. "I saw this. Part of it. Enough. And I couldn't leave it sitting in a boy who loved everyone he met."

"You stole from me," I said. The old grievance, worn smooth as river stone from handling. "For years. Without asking."

"Yes."

"I hated you for it."

"I know." She didn't apologize. She had never apologized, and watching her not apologize now, kneeling in the ash of a burning market with arrest on either side of her, I understood suddenly that she had been carrying the massacre alone since she was seventeen years old. Carrying it, and selling first kisses and dead mothers to grievers at a night market, and keeping the worst memory in the world sealed in glass so that no one else would ever have to hold it.

Alone. Twenty years, alone with it.

Voss shifted. "Inquisitor. The writ."

The writ. I had it in my hand. Take her alive, take the vial, deliver both to the Chancery — to the institution built on Miren's Hollow, funded by Miren's Hollow, armored in the lie of Miren's Hollow. Deliver the proof of the founding crime to the criminals, so they could burn it, and burn her with it, and go on burning the gifted until the thorn-gift was only a story told to frighten tax collectors.

"Astrid Thornbury," I heard myself say, formal, loud enough for the coats to hear, "you are under citation for unlawful keeping of restricted memory." I rose. My legs held. Barely. "Surrender the vial."

Her eyes flickered — hurt, then understanding, then something that might have been the ghost of an old smile. Because she knew. She knew I was performing the letter of my office while my entire soul stood in the ruins of itself, and she let me perform it, because she was kind, and kindness had always been her sharpest weapon.

"I surrendered it already," she said softly. "Hours ago. To someone who'll use it better than either of us could."

Every coat in earshot stiffened. Including mine.

"To whom?" Voss demanded.

But Astrid was looking at me, only at me, and what passed between us needed no touch to read: *the well, Corvus. The old cistern under the city. Where else would you put a truth you want everyone to drink?*

"Take her," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and Voss pretended not to notice because Voss is a kind man under the dullness. They bound her wrists with cord — not iron; even now, even after everything, I gave her that much and told myself it was procedure.

As they led her past me she stopped, close enough that her shoulder nearly brushed my bare hand, and murmured, so quietly it was almost ash:

"They'll come for the well, Corvus. The guild sold them the plan too. Whatever you decide to be — decide fast."

Then she was gone into the wagon, and I stood in the falling ash of Ash Wednesday market holding a writ that made me a servant and a memory that made me an orphan, and I knew — with the king's cold arithmetic rising in me one last time before it broke apart forever — exactly where I had to be before dawn.

The cistern. Before they reached her again. Before the vial reached anywhere at all.

I started walking, and I did not look back at the fire.
