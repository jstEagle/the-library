## 9. When the Thorn Turns

The water beneath Vell does not move the way water should. It moves the way memory does—slow, patient, going nowhere and everywhere at once. I stood at the edge of the ancient cistern with a vial in my fist and three kinds of people coming to kill me, and I thought, absurdly, *I should have charged more for my charms.*

Twenty years of rules. Never take a whole life. Never take a name. Never get between the gifted and the crown, never hold anything anyone would kill for. I had built myself a small, careful life the way you build a fire in wet weather—tinder by tinder, guarding every spark—and now I was about to throw the whole thing into a well.

Behind me, boots on the stair. Ahead of me, black water. Between them, Corvus Hale with a drawn blade and an expression I couldn't read in the lamplight, which frightened me more than either army did.

"They're coming down both shafts," he said. "Guild from the north, grey coats from the south. We have perhaps four minutes."

"We?"

His jaw did the thing it always did when he was deciding whether to lie to me. "I find," he said carefully, "that my loyalties have developed complications."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me."

"Astrid." My name in his mouth, and it still worked on me like a charm, damn him. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it."

* * *

Let me tell you what the vial held, one last time, because after tonight no one will be able to hold it again—not in a bottle, not in a skull.

King Aldric the Last, so called because there was nothing after him worth counting. In the memory he is young and laughing and he has mud on his boots, and he is standing in this very cistern while the gifted bless his coronation, and he swears—on the water itself, which is the oldest oath there is—that no child of the thorn will ever kneel who doesn't choose to kneel.

And then the years pass inside the memory like cards being dealt, and the laughter goes out of him, and he stands in a hall full of people who can take his memories and give them back changed, and he is afraid. That's the part nobody wants carved into their history: he was afraid of us. Not angry. Afraid. Fear made policy, and policy made the massacre, and the massacre made the throne, and the throne has been drinking from blessed water and calling it legitimacy for two hundred years.

The old woman who gave me the vial had carried it since before I was born. She'd held the king's dying hand and taken the truth out of him like drawing a splinter, and she'd spent her whole long life keeping it safe from everyone—the ones in grey, the guilds, the rebels, all the people who wanted the truth as a *weapon*. She gave it to me because I sold small charms and kept my hands clean, and she thought clean hands meant safe hands.

She was wrong about that. But she wasn't wrong about the rest.

* * *

The first grey coats came through the south arch at a run, lanterns swinging, and Hale turned to meet them with his sword up and his coat open, and I saw that under the grey he still wore the frayed blue scarf from the quarry years, the one I'd given him when we were both too young to know what outlawry meant. He hadn't worn it where anyone could see. He'd worn it where *he* could see.

"Corvus—" I started.

"Don't thank me yet." He set his shoulders. "You'll owe me a memory for this. A good one. Something embarrassing."

I turned to the water.

Here is the last secret, the one I haven't told anyone, the one I've been carrying since the flooded cistern when he cornered me and I almost gave everything away: I know how to unmake myself. Any thorn-gifted does, if they're honest about it. We can pour out. The gift runs both directions; that's the whole cursed point of it. You can put a memory into water, into glass, into another mind—but you can also climb inside the vessel yourself, all of you, and let go.

The old woman asked me to keep the truth from the ones in grey. She didn't say anything about giving it to everybody.

I pulled the stopper with my teeth.

* * *

Pouring a memory is easy. Anyone with a thorn scar can do it; children do it by accident. Pouring a memory *wide*—into moving water, into ten thousand throats at once—is not pouring. It's bleeding. The king's truth came out of the vial like smoke under a door, and the water received it, and then the water began to *change*, darkening from below as though something enormous had opened its eye at the bottom of the city, and I understood that if I stopped now—if I let the vial empty and walked away—the truth would sit in the cistern as a sediment, a rumor, a thing scholars would argue about for another two centuries while the grey coats burned us at their convenience.

It needed a courier. It needed someone to carry it out of the glass and into the flow.

Me. Obviously. There was never anyone else; I'd known since the well-square, since the hedge-healer's wagon, since the sketch on the wall. Every choice I'd made in twenty years of careful neutrality had been leading here, to the edge of black water, holding the last true king in my cupped hands like a bird with a broken wing.

Behind me, steel rang against steel. Someone shouted Hale's name. Someone else shouted mine, and got the pronunciation wrong, which even then struck me as funny.

"Last chance to talk me out of it," I said, not turning around.

"You already know I won't." His voice was ragged. Fighting and feeling at the same time—he'd never learned to separate them. "But I want it on record that I hate this."

"Noted for the record."

I stepped off the edge.

* * *

The water took me the way sleep takes you—not falling, but *arriving*. Cold first, then not cold. Then the current, the blessed current the gifted laid down two hundred years ago to carry health to a sick city, and I fed the king into it.

Not all at once. Memory isn't grain to be dumped; it's thread to be unwound. I gave the water the coronation first, the oaths, the mud on the young king's boots. I gave it the fear—I made sure to give it the fear, because a truth without its fear is just propaganda wearing better clothes. I gave it the order, and the screams, and the silence after, and the long gray years of a throne built on a grave pretending to be a foundation.

And the water carried each thread out through the channels, up through the pipes, into the wells of Vell. Into the morning kettles. Into the bathhouses and the breweries and the horse troughs. Into the mouths of bakers and soldiers and children and inquisitors, every single person who would draw water tomorrow and swallow the founding crime of their own government without knowing they were doing it, until they knew, all at once, together.

That was the trick of it. The old woman kept the truth safe by keeping it *singular*—one vial, one carrier, one throat it could be cut out of. I made it plural. You cannot seize a memory from everyone. You cannot arrest a city for remembering.

The vial emptied. The water went dark as spilled ink, and then darker, and then—this was the part I hadn't expected—it went clear. Cleaner than it had been in two hundred years. As if the blessing had only been waiting all this time for something honest to carry.

Somewhere above me, distantly, through fathoms and stone, I heard Hale shouting—not in pain. In triumph or grief or both; with Corvus the two sounds had always been twins.

And I felt myself thinning. Felt the edges of Astrid Thornbury going soft in the current like ink in rain. The memories of my mother's kitchen, my first customer, the child with the dead father and the bottled laugh, the boy at the quarry with the blue scarf—all of it loosening, all of it eager to go with the king's truth, to become part of the inheritance, to be drunk by strangers who would grieve my griefs without ever knowing whose they were.

I could have held on. Some part of me wants you to know—I want *me* to know—that I considered it. That letting go of your own past is not noble. It is terrifying, and it hurts in a place that has no name, and I did it anyway, because I had spent twenty years selling other people's losses and I finally understood what I'd been really selling them: permission to put a thing down.

I put it down.

* * *

What came after came in pieces, and some of the pieces are missing, and I have made my peace with the gaps the way you make peace with scars.

I remember surfacing, gasping, in the shallow channel at the cistern's rim. I remember Hale hauling me out by the collar, soaked to the bone, blood on his cheek, saying my name over and over like he was trying to pin it to me before the water finished taking it. I remember that I looked at him—at the frayed blue scarf, at the face I had loved since before I knew what loving cost—and felt... warmth. Recognition. Nothing more. A stranger's kindness, freely given.

I remember grey coats flooding the gallery and stopping, uncertain, because half of them had already drunk from their canteens that morning and the king's massacre was sitting in their stomachs like a stone, and you cannot raise a sword for the throne when you can taste what the throne is founded on.

I remember the guild-master of the thieves, up on the north stair, going white as chalk as the memory settled into him—his cut of the sale evaporating, his leverage dissolving, his whole rotten economy of stolen truth rendered worthless by a girl and a well.

I remember the water running clear.

Hale knelt over me, dripping, shaking me gently. "Astrid. Astrid, stay with me. What's your name?"

And here is the hook that the next days hung themselves on, the question I could not answer then and am still learning how to live without answering:

I opened my mouth, and honestly, truly, with no deceit in my heart—

I didn't know.
