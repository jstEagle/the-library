## 10. A Stranger to Herself

The trouble with giving away your entire past is that nobody warns you about the mornings.

I woke in a rented room above a chandler's shop on Tallow Street, and for three full seconds I didn't know whose hands were lying on the blanket. They seemed like good hands. Capable. A few old burns on the fingertips — candle work, probably, or maybe something braver. I decided I liked them. Then the name *Astrid* arrived in my head like a bill collector, and I remembered that I was the woman who had emptied herself into a well.

Three weeks since Ash Wednesday Market. Three weeks since I'd stood at the lip of the ancient cistern with Corvus Hale bleeding somewhere behind me and grey coats swarming the stairwell, and poured two memories into the dark water: a dead king's massacre, and my whole life besides. The king's memory because it was true and truths belong to everyone or no one. Mine because — well. Because a gift like mine was how all of this started, and I thought perhaps the kindest thing I could do with it was stop.

Also, if I'm honest, which is apparently a habit now: I wanted to know what it felt like to be free rather than merely careful. Twenty years of rules — never take a whole life, never take a name, never let them see the depth of you — and every rule had been a wall around a small frightened person. I burned the walls down and threw myself into the well after them.

The water had taken both offerings. The city drank its inheritance by morning.

* * *

You can't miss what you don't remember, which is either the great mercy of what I did or the great cruelty, depending on the hour. What I have instead of memory is evidence. The city testifies about me constantly.

The baker on Tallow Street gives me yesterday's loaves at half price and won't say why, just looks at me with wet eyes and presses the bread into my hands like an offering. A woman with a scarred jaw stopped me in the square last week, gripped my wrist, and said, "You gave us back our grandmothers," and then wept, and I stood there holding a stranger's grief like a package delivered to the wrong address. Children play a game in the gutters called *thorn-and-crown*, one side wearing crowns of twisted wire, and when they argue about who has to be the king they always lose interest halfway, as though even in play vengeance can't quite find its footing.

Because here is the thing nobody predicted, least of all me: the war did not come.

The rebels came out of the undercity expecting their banner-moment. They had songs ready. And then every man and woman in Vell woke up remembering the last true king ordering his own gifted subjects into the mass graves — not reading about it, not hearing it preached, but *remembering*, the way you remember your own mother's kitchen, with the smell of the bread and the sound of the door. You cannot be recruited into someone else's rage when the rage comes with the full weight of lived truth attached. The rebels wanted a weapon. What they got was a funeral. Even fury, when it belongs to everybody, gets tired faster than when it belongs to a faction.

The crown, for its part, discovered that hunting the thorn-gifted is poor politics when the entire citizenry shares a memory of why that hunt was shameful. The proclamations stopped. The grey coats still patrol, but they patrol like men waiting for orders that will never come, and I've seen two of them standing at the well in the evening, drinking from the public cup, faces unreadable, drinking anyway.

And the well itself — the blessed cistern, the old water — runs clearer now than anyone living remembers. The chandler's wife told me the water tastes the way her childhood tasted. She couldn't explain what she meant. She didn't need to. Everyone in this city tastes it and understands.

* * *

Corvus Hale found me on my fourth morning, because of course he did. He finds people. It's the only gift he never managed to hate properly.

He didn't arrest me. He sat down across from me at the soup stall without asking, which is the most intimate thing a former inquisitor can do, and said, "They're dissolving the Order. Grey coats are being folded back into the city watch."

"Congratulations on your career change," I said.

"I'm told I'm good at finding things." He turned his cup slowly. "It's the finding people part they're retiring." He looked at me then, and I watched him search my face for the girl he'd loved before the outlawry, and I watched him fail to find her, and I watched the failure land somewhere between grief and relief. "Do you remember the cistern?" he asked. "What you said to me there?"

"No," I said. "Should I?"

"It doesn't matter." And then, quieter, the self-deception finally finished cracking: "Yes. It matters. But it matters less now than it did, and I think that was your intention."

I had no answer for him. I genuinely didn't know what I'd said. But I knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a room you wake up in — some part of me had spoken to him with everything I had left, and whatever it was, it had been mercy-shaped. It always was, with him. That much survived the well. Some things apparently live below the waterline.

"You should leave Vell," he said. "Not because anyone will touch you. Because staying here would be like haunting your own house."

He was right, which was annoying, so I told him so, and something crossed his face that might once have been a laugh.

* * *

I spent another week settling accounts with a past I couldn't consult. The chandler let me work off my rent trimming wicks; my hands knew the work before I did. An old hedge-healer — the sister of the woman dragged away in the wagon, back now, acquitted by a city that suddenly remembered her fondly — found me at the market and pressed a sealed vial into my hands.

"One memory," she said. "Recovered from her effects. She'd want you to choose whether to keep it."

I held it up to the light. Inside, something silver moved like minnows. My handwriting on the label — I recognized it the way you recognize a scar. *First snow, mother singing.*

My mother. A woman who existed for me now only as a fact, like a date in a ledger. I turned the vial over twice. Keeping it would be a kindness to a stranger who happened to be me. Pouring it out would be finishing the job.

I walked to the well and gave it to the water, and the city drank my mother's singing with its breakfast, and somewhere in ten thousand kitchens, ten thousand people hummed a tune they didn't know they knew, and none of them grieved, and neither did I, and I stood at the wellhead feeling absolutely nothing where the largest thing in my life used to be, and it was — God forgive me — it was peaceful. That's the confession no one asks for: chosen forgetting feels wonderful. The guilt is supposed to come later, but I'd given that away too.

* * *

I left on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays the east gate is crowded with carters and nobody remembers faces in a crowd, least of all a face the city keeps trying to thank.

I carried almost nothing. A coat. Bread from the baker who wouldn't take payment. The burn-scarred hands and whatever lives underneath a name. At the gatehouse the guard asked my business and I opened my mouth to invent something and discovered I didn't need to — "Traveling," I said, and it was simply true, the first uncomplicated sentence of my life.

The road east climbs along the wall, and halfway up the switchback I looked back, because you do, even when the city holds nothing of yours anymore. Especially then.

Corvus Hale stood on the wall walk above the east gate. Not hiding. Not following. Just there, the way he'd been at the soup stall, present at the edges of my life like weather. He didn't raise a hand. He didn't call anything down. He just watched me go with the particular stillness of a man keeping a promise he made in a flooded cistern to a woman who no longer exists — and letting her go was the promise, and he was keeping it, and I understood that completely without remembering any of it.

I lifted one hand. He inclined his head, barely, the smallest bow a man can give, and then he turned away first, which was its own kind of mercy, and I loved him for exactly four seconds, cleanly, without history, the way you love a stranger's kindness on a long road. Then that went into the water too, wherever the water goes inside a person.

The road bent east. Vell dropped behind the hill with its clear well and its shared ghosts and its children playing thorn-and-crown until supper. Somewhere ahead there were towns I'd never sold charms in, rivers I hadn't blessed or poisoned, mornings I wouldn't recognize my own hands in — good hands, probably, capable ones.

I'd spent twenty years selling other people's memories so I could afford to avoid my own. Now I owned none and owed nothing, and the road didn't care what I'd been, and neither did I.

A carter heading the same direction offered me a ride as far as the crossroads. Asked my name, the way people do.

I considered the question honestly, which is more than most names ever get. Everything I might have answered belonged to the well now.

"Tell you at the crossroads," I said, and climbed up beside him, and the city of Vell — my witness, my debtor, my inheritance — let me go, and I let it go, and neither of us looked back twice.
