## 1. The Weight of Blank Pages

The ledgers of the Gilded Thorn weighed exactly as much as they should have, which was the first thing wrong with them.

Coral Vane had audited this collection for eleven years, and in that time he had developed a relationship with its weight the way a sailor develops one with the sea—not affectionate, exactly, but intimate in the way of long acquaintance. Forty-one volumes, bound in vellum gone the color of old teeth, each one calibrated to his hands. The early books were light; the guild had begun modestly, trading in small impossibilities—luck by the thimbleful, second chances sold at a discount to those who'd squandered their first. The later ones grew heavier as the inventory grew stranger: bottled grief, borrowed names, doors that opened only once and never again for anyone who passed through.

Tonight, on the eve of the centennial, he carried four of them up from the vault at a time, because five made him tilt like a listing ship, and Coral Vane did not tilt. He was thirty-four years old and built like a piece of furniture someone had made to be useful rather than admired—broad through the shoulders, narrow in his ambitions, with ink stains on his fingers so permanent they might have been tattoos if tattoos came in brown-black and smelled of iron gall. His hair he kept cropped close because long hair caught fire easily, and a mercenary's habits died harder than a mercenary did. In theory, anyway. He hadn't been a mercenary in any practical sense for over a decade.

In practice, he supposed, he was still under contract. That was the trouble.

He set the fourth volume on the counting table, aligned its spine with the others, and trimmed the lamp wick until the flame stood tall and steady. The ledger hall at midnight was the closest thing Coral had to a church. Long tables scarred by a century of transactions. Shelves rising into shadow where the oldest accounts slept. A single high window that let in nothing but the smell of rain when it rained, which tonight it did, softly, against glass that cost more per pane than most houses in the Tallow District.

He worked the way he always worked: left to right, top to bottom, column to column, checking the physical count against the recorded count against the weight of the collateral held in trust. It was tedious work. It was also, in his private opinion, the only honest work left in the building, and possibly the only honest work left in Vessia at all, though he would have phrased that more carefully aloud. Numbers didn't lie. People lied constantly, beautifully, inventively—but numbers, given a careful enough auditor, told the truth eventually, even when the truth was something the guild had paid very good money to bury.

By the second hour he had found three discrepancies, all minor: two grains of luck unaccounted for in the spring harvest trade, a borrowed name returned slightly worn—the client had used it to attend her own funeral, which always wore a name—and an entry in March where the clerk's hand trembled badly enough that Coral suspected the transaction had involved blood price. He flagged all three in red pencil. Red for irregularity. Black for balance. Green for paid in full, a color that appeared in these books roughly as often as green things appeared in the Saltmarsh.

It was during the third volume, somewhere past the middle, that he found the entry that should not exist.

He almost missed it. That was the second thing wrong. Coral did not miss things in ledgers; missing things in ledgers was how clerks ended up as collateral, and there were rooms beneath this building where former clerks were kept as examples. But the entry was written in a hand identical to his own—which was impossible, because he had not written it—and it sat in the margin of page 314, squeezed into a space no wider than a thumb, in ink so faint it might have been a shadow of ink rather than ink itself:

*Received in payment: one prayer, unanswered. Value: incalculable.*

Coral sat back. The chair creaked. Rain ticked against the high window.

He read it again. Then a third time, because the first rule of auditing was that the impossible was usually just the unfamiliar, and the second rule was that the unfamiliar was usually just somebody else's fraud. Fraud he could handle. Fraud had columns. Fraud reconciled.

This did not reconcile.

There was no date. There was no account number. There was no counterweight entered on the debit side, which violated the first law of the guild's founding charter: *nothing is given without something taken.* Every transaction in a hundred years of Gilded Thorn history balanced. Even the monstrous ones balanced, especially the monstrous ones—that was what made them bearable, what let the guild mistresses sleep at night. You took grief from a widow and gave her coin, and the scales dipped level, and level scales meant clean conscience, or at least the appearance of one.

A payment received with nothing owed in return wasn't a transaction. It was a wound.

Coral fetched the magnifier—a lens ground from a mirror-shard the guild had once bought from a dying oracle, which showed things as they were rather than as they appeared—and held it over the marginal line. Through the glass, the words did not resolve. They *deepened*, the way a footprint deepens when you step beside it, and for one disorienting moment he had the sense that the ink was not on the page but *under* it, surfacing slowly, like something rising through water toward air.

He put the lens down carefully, the way you put down a snake.

* * *

There were forty-one ledgers and he checked every remaining one before dawn.

He found six more of the margin-entries. Each in his own handwriting—or a handwriting indistinguishable from his own, which amounted to the same problem. Each without date or account. Each reading some variation on the same terrible formula:

*Received: one prayer, unanswered.*
*Received: one prayer, unanswered. Payer unknown.*
*Received: one prayer. Status: unanswered. Note: payer has stopped asking.*

That last one was in Volume 29, dated by position to roughly eleven years ago, and Coral looked at it for a long time without touching it. Eleven years ago was a year he kept locked in the same room of himself where he kept other things that didn't reconcile. He had gotten very good at not opening that room. He was, in fact, professionally excellent at it. Give him a heart to mend and he'd itemize it; give him a memory to sell and he'd weigh it to the dram; but ask him about the night his god stopped answering and he would look at you with the flat gray patience of a man quoting interest rates.

Vessel-of-Thorns. His god, once. The god of the thorn-hedged road, of the bargain kept past reason, of debts honored when honoring them ruined you. Coral had sworn to it at nineteen, kneeling in the mud outside a burning waystation, and the oath had been simple the way all true oaths are simple: *I will hold the line you draw, and you will hold me when I can't.* For eight years the god had answered—in the prickle of thorns along a coward's spine, in the sudden certainty that a bridge was rotten, in dreams where a voice like wind through brambles said *not this road, little clerk, not this one*—

—and then, on a night in autumn, mid-oath, mid-word, silence. Not death. Coral knew the feel of a dead god; the whole country knew it; Vessia's shrines had been going dark for generations, gods winking out like candles in a drafty house. This had been different. This had been a door closing from the other side. A voice cut off not at the end of a sentence but in the middle of one, and Coral had finished the oath alone, kneeling in the mud again, older now, saying words to something that no longer listened, because the alternative was to stop, and stopping meant admitting it was over, and he had built a life out of not admitting things.

So he had come to work for the guild that traded in impossible things, on the reasoning that a man whose god had vanished into impossibility might find it again among the stock-in-trade. Eleven years of ledgers later, he had found everything except that.

And now the ledgers were writing back.

He stood at the counting table as the sky behind the high window went from black to the bruised gray of early morning, and he laid the seven pages open side by side, seven impossible entries in seven different volumes spanning—by position, by binding wear—perhaps sixty years of guild history. Sixty years. Longer than his employment. Longer than his life.

Someone had been recording prayers. Unanswered ones. As payments received.

And the guild had accepted them.

Coral rubbed his eyes and thought, with the cold clarity that was his best and worst trait: *a payment implies a payee.* Someone had received these prayers. Someone had banked them. And the guild's whole architecture—the vaults, the scales, the cellar levels that Sable Quill pretended didn't exist below the third lock—was built on the principle that anything received must be stored.

Which meant that somewhere in this building, there was a room full of prayers nobody had answered.

He didn't like the shape of that thought. He liked even less the smaller thought nested inside it, the one that whispered in his own voice, in the handwriting from the margins: *you know who banks unanswered prayers. You've been making deposits for eleven years.*

* * *

Sable Quill found him there at first light, which was unlike her; the guild mistress kept merchant's hours and a merchant's appetite, arriving late and leaving later, ruling the Thorn between meals. She came down the ledger hall stairs in a dressing gown the color of dried wine, silver-streaked hair pinned up carelessly, and she stopped three steps from the bottom when she saw the pages laid out.

For a woman in her fifties who had spent a lifetime selling the unsellable, Sable had remarkably poor control of her face at unguarded moments. Coral watched the guard go up too late—the flicker of recognition, then calculation, then the smooth mask sliding home over both.

"Centennial tomorrow," she said. "You're here early."

"I'm always here."

"You're always here *on time*. Early is different." She descended the rest of the stairs and crossed to the table, looking down at the seven open volumes without touching them, which told Coral she already knew what they were. "Found something?"

"You could say that." He turned Volume 29 toward her, tapping the margin entry with one ink-stained finger. "Payments made in prayers. Seven of them across sixty years. No dates, no accounts, no counterweights. In my handwriting."

"Is it?" Sable asked mildly.

"That's the question I was hoping you'd answer."

"And instead you'll answer mine." She pulled out the auditor's chair—his chair, though she owned the building—and sat, arranging the wine-dark gown around her knees. Up close, in the flat morning light, she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Who else has seen these?"

"No one. I work alone."

"Mm." She studied him for a moment, and whatever she saw seemed to decide something. "Coral. Tomorrow we celebrate one hundred years of the Gilded Thorn. One hundred years of taking the unbearable and pricing it. Do you know why that works? Why people keep coming?"

"Because the alternative is carrying it themselves."

"Because the alternative is carrying it themselves," she agreed. "We are the world's pawnshop for pain. And a pawnshop survives on one principle above all others." She tapped the table, twice, precisely. "*Nothing comes in that we don't know the provenance of.* Every bottle of grief traced to its mourner. Every borrowed name logged to its owner. So if prayers have been coming into my ledgers for sixty years without provenance—" her voice stayed pleasant, which is how Coral knew she was frightened, "—then either my security has failed catastrophically, or the provenance is something I was never meant to trace."

"And which do you think it is?"

"I think," Sable said, rising, smoothing the gown, becoming the guild mistress again layer by layer, "that you are the finest auditor I have ever employed, Coral Vane, and that the wisest thing you could do before tomorrow's festivities is finish your count of the ordinary volumes and leave the extraordinary ones to their owners." At the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and added without turning around: "Some entries aren't errors. Some are seams. Pull a seam on a garment that's holding something in, and you find out what 'in' means."

Her footsteps went up and away.

Coral stood alone in the ledger hall with seven impossible pages and the smell of rain and ink. Outside, the city of Vessia was waking—cart wheels in the Tallow District, gulls fighting over the fish market, a hundred thousand people beginning a day that would end in tomorrow's centennial feast. Somewhere among them, he thought, people were praying. To dark shrines, to dead gods, to nothing at all. Prayers rising like heat off stone and going nowhere.

Going nowhere.

Or being received.

He looked down at Volume 29, at the entry dated by silence to eleven years ago. *Payer has stopped asking,* the margin said, in his own handwriting, and Coral Vane—who did not flinch at blood price, who had priced widows' grief by the ounce, who had spent a decade teaching himself that the ledger was the only thing in the world that would never abandon him—felt the floor of his competence tilt, just slightly, like a listing ship.

He closed the volume gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

Then, because routine was the only rope he had ever trusted, he picked up his red pencil and began the day's count—left to right, top to bottom, column to column—while behind him, in the margins of seven sleeping books, ink that was not quite ink waited for him to stop watching, and began, very quietly, to rise.
