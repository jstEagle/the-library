## 2. A God Who Stopped Writing Back

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Coral reached the bottom of the third ledger, and he found himself doing what he always did on nights like this—checking the ink for signs of life.

He ran his thumb along the margin of an entry dated nine years past. Dry. Of course it was dry. Ink did not breathe. Ink did not answer. He had learned that lesson eleven years ago, in the mud outside Harrowfen, and he relearned it every time he caught himself hoping otherwise.

*Contract: one god, retained. Terms: service until released. Payment: as sworn.*

That was how his own entry read, in his own younger hand, in the ledger he kept locked in the drawer beneath his desk—the personal one, not the guild's. He had written it at nineteen with the certainty of someone who believed contracts were sacred things, because to him they were. A contract was a promise made visible. You could hold it up to the light. You could point to it when the world went wrong and say, *here, this is what was agreed.*

His contract said *until released.*

It had never said anything about silence.

Coral set down his pen and flexed his fingers, working the stiffness out of them. Outside the ledger hall's narrow windows, Vessia was preparing for its centennial. Lanterns went up along the Gilded Row. Somewhere a fiddler was rehearsing badly. The Thorn would be full to bursting tomorrow night—guild mistresses in borrowed finery, debtors come to gawk at their betters, merchants trading in bottled thunder and secondhand courage. Sable Quill wanted the books flawless before then. Sable Quill always wanted the books flawless.

And so Coral sat alone among forty years of impossible arithmetic, auditing entries no honest accountant would believe: two coppers for a memory of rain; six silver for a name nobody else remembered; a favor owed to a woman who had been dead for a decade and whose estate still paid interest.

Professional work. Clean work. Work that kept his hands busy and his mind occupied and left no room for the thing underneath, the thing shaped like a man kneeling in the rain asking a question that got no answer.

He dipped his pen again. He did not look at the drawer where the old ledger slept. He was very good at not looking.

* * *

Eleven years ago it had rained the way it only rains in Vessia in autumn—not falling so much as arriving, all at once, like a debt called due.

Coral had been twenty-two, three years sworn to Vessel-of-Thorns, and he had thought he understood the shape of his god the way he understood the shape of his own hands. The Thorn-God was not a gentle thing. It answered prayers the way a moneylender answers pleas for credit: carefully, precisely, and always with interest. Its temples were small and mean and smelled of iron. Its priests wore crowns of woven bramble that drew blood at the hairline, because Vessel-of-Thorns held that nothing given freely was worth having, and nothing worth having came without cost.

Coral had been dying of a fever in a ditch outside Harrowfen when he'd made his bargain. He remembered the terms exactly—he remembered everything about that night, which was both the blessing and the wound of it. His voice, cracked and childish: *Save me, and I'm yours until you release me.* The presence that settled over him like a coat of nails, not cruel but exact. And then the fever breaking, and the mud, and the long walk back into a life he now owed.

Three years of service. Three years of carrying the god's small brutal mercies across the countryside—a harvest saved here, a tyrant's favorite horse lamed there, always priced, always fair, always signed in the ledger Coral kept strapped to his chest like armor. Vessel-of-Thorns spoke to him the way ink speaks: in margins, in margins of margins, in the space between one heartbeat and the next where a sentence could be written if you knew how to listen.

Coral had known how to listen. He had been good at it. It was, he suspected, the only prayer the god had ever truly wanted from him—attention. Payment in kind.

Then came the last night, and the last commission, and the silence.

* * *

In the present, the stub of candle guttered, and Coral trimmed the wick without looking up from his work.

Entry four hundred and twelve, centennial audit. *Bottled grief, vintage indeterminate, sold to the widow Maren Osta, price: her memory of her wedding day.* He wrote the valuation, checked it twice against the standard schedule, and initialed it. His initials were the same ones he'd used at nineteen. That was the trouble with names—they outlasted everything they belonged to.

He had a system for nights when the drawer pulled at him. Audit the ledgers. Balance the columns. When the columns balanced, start over with the oldest volume and check for transcription errors, knowing he would find none, because he never made any, because precision was the wall he had built between himself and the pit. Sable called him the best clerk the Thorn had ever employed. She said it like a compliment. Coral heard it correctly: *you are very good at staying busy, Coral Vane, and we are all grateful.*

He turned the page. The paper whispered.

Eleven years ago, the paper had screamed. That was the only word he had ever found for it. He had been kneeling in the mud outside Harrowfen a second time—not dying this time, just wet and tired and thirty miles from anywhere, holding the ledger open under his cloak while the rain tried to drown it. The commission had come through at dusk: a village elder praying for her grandson, lost in the fenlands, and Vessel-of-Thorns had accepted the prayer at its stated price—the elder's memory of her own mother—and sent Coral to do the collecting and the saving both.

Simple work. He'd done it a hundred times. Find the boy, pull him from the water, take the memory, sign the entry.

Except the boy had been dead already. Drowned three days, bloated gray, beyond even the Thorn-God's exacting reach. And so Coral had knelt in the rain beside a small drowned body, holding a ledger open to a blank page, and asked his god the only question there was to ask:

*She's paid. What do I give her?*

And the answer had begun to form—he felt it forming, felt that familiar pressure behind his eyes like a sentence being written through him—

And stopped.

Mid-oath. Mid-word. Coral would spend the rest of his life trying to describe what it was like, and the closest he ever got was this: it was like a hand letting go. Not a door closing. A hand. Warm, nail-studded, familiar beyond all reason, wrapped around his from the inside—and then gone, between one heartbeat and the next, leaving him holding a contract marked *until released* and a god who no longer picked up when he prayed.

He had knelt in that mud until dawn. He had prayed in every way he knew—in words, in ink, in blood from the bramble-crown he wove himself that night because the priests were too far away and somebody had to keep the forms. Nothing. Rain, and the dead boy's open eyes, and the blank page.

At sunrise he had walked to the elder's house and told her, with a clerk's flat mercy, that her payment could not be accepted, because the service could not be rendered, because the account was—his voice had actually done this, he remembered the shame of it—*temporarily suspended.*

Then he had gone back into the fen, buried the boy with his own hands because no one else would, and written in the ledger, beneath the voided commission, four words in his careful nineteen-year-old hand grown older:

*Balance outstanding. Awaiting instruction.*

He had been awaiting instruction for eleven years.

* * *

The candle died. Coral lit another from the lantern at the hall's end and told himself the chill that moved up his arms was only the draft from the window.

There was a ritual to it now, the checking. Every year on the anniversary—which was also, by ugly coincidence, three weeks before the centennial, so that grief and festivity shared a calendar the way they shared a city—he took out the personal ledger and read his contract aloud. Not as prayer. As audit. If a contract is a promise made visible, then reading it was simply verifying that the promise still existed, even if the promisor did not answer. He was not a man who abandoned accounts. Whatever else eleven years had made of him, he was not that.

*Service until released,* he read, quietly, to a hall full of sleeping books. *Payment: as sworn.*

Nothing stirred. No pressure behind his eyes. No sentence writing itself through him. Just the lantern flame standing very straight, the way flames do when nothing is listening.

Coral closed the ledger and locked the drawer and put the key back around his neck, where it hung against his sternum like a second heart, and went back to the centennial audit, because the centennial audit did not care about his grief, and neither did Sable Quill, and neither did the world, and there was a strange hard comfort in that. The world's indifference was the one invoice that never varied.

He worked until the sky outside went from black to bruise. He worked through the entries for miracle-stock, through the shelf-inventory of one-way doors (three remaining, all accounted for, none to be opened under any circumstances), through the quarterly reconciliation of the prayer-futures market, which had been quiet for a decade because prayers, in Vessia, had stopped being worth the paper they were promised on.

Gods had gone silent all over the country, people said. Not just his. The whole economy of the miraculous had dried up like a riverbed, which was why the guild had pivoted, decades ago, from brokering divine favors to bottling human ones—grief, memory, courage, regret. Human things. Things that stayed bought. Coral had always assumed the gods' silence was a general weather pattern, a drought of heaven, no more personal than rain.

But some nights, balancing columns that refused to balance because somewhere in forty years of entries there was a discrepancy shaped exactly like a hand letting go, he wondered.

He wondered whether a drought could be built.

He wondered whether a prison made of paper could hold something vast enough to be called a god—and if so, whether the accountants would necessarily know.

Then he wondered nothing, professionally, and initialed the page.

* * *

Dawn came gray over the Gilded Row, and with it came footsteps on the stairs—light, quick, arrhythmic, the walk of someone young and late. Pip. The guild runner, fourteen years old, indentured for a debt of four coppers that had somehow become eleven silver through the alchemy of interest, and cheerful about it in a way that Coral found either inspiring or deeply concerning depending on how much sleep he'd had.

"Master Vane." Pip skidded into the hall, breathless, holding a folded note. "Mistress Quill says—she says there's a client. Early one. Before opening, she says, and she says you're to process it personal."

"Personal," Coral repeated. Sable did not say *personal.* Sable said *routine* or she said *delicate*, and delicate meant she handled it herself, because Sable trusted exactly one pair of hands in this building and they were her own.

"That's what she said." Pip shifted, and lowered their voice, and looked, for the first time, uncertain. "She's crying, Master Vane. The client. Been crying since the gate. Says she wants to trade—" Pip swallowed. "Says she wants to sell her memory of her son. All of it. For passage money."

Coral looked at the note in Pip's hand. He did not take it yet. Through the high windows the first light was coming in thin and cold, touching the spines of forty years of ledgers, and somewhere in the cellar below—or nowhere; the cellar was just a cellar; he had checked it a hundred times—something he did not have a professional word for shifted in its sleep.

A mother selling her son. Priced per the schedule. Initials in triplicate.

"Tell Mistress Quill I'll be down directly," Coral said, and reached for his pen, and told himself—as he had told himself for eleven years—that a transaction was a transaction, and a clerk was a clerk, and the ink always dried eventually.

The ink always dried. That was the whole horror of it, though he didn't know that yet. That morning, standing in the ledger hall with the dawn behind him, Coral Vane still believed that dry ink was safe ink.

He was about to learn otherwise.
