## 3. The Client Who Paid in Memory

The Gilded Thorn opened its doors at the ninth bell, and by half past, Coral Vane had already refused three miracles.

The first had been a fishmonger who wanted his dead brother's laugh bottled so he could hear it on Sundays. The second was a boy of maybe fifteen who wished to borrow a nobleman's name long enough to walk through a gate he wasn't born behind. The third simply wanted to be loved, and there was no ledger line for that, not at any price, and Coral told him so with the flat kindness of a man reading a tariff schedule.

*We do not sell what cannot be weighed,* said the sign above the counter, in letters guilded with actual gold, because Sable Quill believed irony should be expensive.

Coral sat behind the intake desk with the day's ledger open before him, a cup of chicory gone cold at his elbow, and performed the only sacrament he still believed in: accurate bookkeeping. Ink, columns, dates. A thing written down could be trusted. A thing written down stayed where you put it. He had built eleven years of quiet on that foundation, stone by stone, and he laid another stone this morning, and another, and did not look too closely at the mortar.

"Next," he called.

The woman who came to his window was wet before she reached it.

Not rain—the morning was dry, the kind of pale gold autumn light that made even Thorn Street look almost honest. She was wet because she had been crying since some hour before dawn, and her face had given up on dignity somewhere around the third mile. She was perhaps forty, dressed in a widow's grey gone soft at the elbows, and she carried a bundle wrapped in linen against her chest the way other women carried infants.

Coral had processed four hundred and six transactions in his tenure as senior clerk. He knew, within a copper's breadth, what each grief would cost before the client opened their mouth. This one, he thought, watching her hands tremble around the bundle, would cost everything she had left.

"Name?" he asked.

"Maren." Her voice came out sanded down to the grain. "Maren Ostwick."

"Purpose of visit?"

She looked at the counter as if the answer might be written there among the ink stains. Then she unwrapped the linen.

Inside was a memory. Not metaphorically—this was the Thorn, and memories here were goods like any other, decanted into vessels of smoked glass no larger than a plum. But most clients brought theirs loose, held in cupped palms like water, shapeless until the decanters worked them into something storable. Maren Ostwick's memory had already been shaped. Someone had done the work for her, or she had done it herself over one long unbearable night, and what lay in the linen was a perfect sphere of amber light, and inside the light—

A boy. Perhaps seven years old. Standing ankle-deep in a river, sleeves rolled, holding up a fish with both hands and an expression of such triumphant astonishment that Coral felt it land in his own chest like a thrown stone. The memory turned slowly in its glass. The boy laughed. There was no sound—memories kept their sound for the owner alone—but Coral found himself supplying it anyway, some bright whooping note, because the boy's whole body was laughing, shoulders to heels.

"My son," Maren said. "Tam."

Coral picked up his pen. "What are you offering?"

"The day he caught the trout." She said it quickly, the way you pull a tooth. "The whole day. Morning to dark. It's—it's complete. I've kept it clean. I didn't go back to it every night like some do, wearing it thin. I saved it. You can see it's not worn."

It was true. He could see it. The sphere burned steady and unfrayed, colors deep as stained glass. A well-kept memory fetched triple the rate of a ragged one, and Coral's pen hovered over the valuation column while something in him that was not a clerk stood very still.

"And what are you buying?"

"Passage." Her hands flattened on the counter. "Out. East, they say, past the salt flats, to the coast, where the ships—" She stopped. Started again. "There's work on the coast. For widows. Laundry, mending. My sister went two years ago and she writes that there's bread, real bread, and the rent is honest."

"Vessia has bread," Coral said. It was not his place to argue with clients. He argued anyway, which was also becoming a habit, though he'd have denied it under oath. "The grain tariffs fell last spring. Bread is cheaper here than it's been in a decade."

"Bread is not the problem." Maren Ostwick looked at him then, straight on, and her eyes were the eyes of someone who had already died once and was merely waiting for the rest of her to catch up. "Everything else is. Every street I walk, I see him. Every door. He drowned in the Millbrook in April, master clerk. He drowned, and this city will not stop showing him to me. The river. His school. The cobbler's bench where he sat and watched the awl. I cannot buy a loaf without walking past my own heart breaking, and I have walked past it every day for five months, and I am done." She pushed the sphere forward an inch. "I want to go where he never was. That's all. That's the whole of it. Somewhere he never was."

The intake hall was quiet around them. The fishmonger had gone; the name-borrowing boy had gone; even the desperate lover had shuffled off into the gold-lit street. Just the two of them, and the ticking of the regulator clock Sable had bought from a man selling time he'd stolen from his own future, and the small turning light of a boy with a fish.

Coral wrote the valuation. *One (1) memory, intact, high fidelity: 40 crowns.*

Passage east, with fees and bribes and the coast road's tolls, ran thirty-eight.

He knew this because he kept those books too. He knew every price in this city the way a priest knows psalms, and so he knew, with terrible precision, that Maren Ostwick was selling the single best day of her life for two crowns of margin, and that the Thorn would resell that day to some barren merchant or grieving grandparent for three hundred, and that the arithmetic of the miracle trade was always, always the same arithmetic: the desperate pay in what cannot be replaced, and the comfortable pay in coin, and the house wins.

That was the business. That had always been the business. He had entered four hundred and six transactions into these ledgers and slept soundly after each one, or if not soundly, then at least asleep, which in Vessia passed for the same thing.

His pen touched the paper.

"Have you considered," he heard himself say, "a partial extraction?"

Maren blinked. "A what?"

"We can take the edges of the day. The journey to the river. The walk home after. Keep the center intact—the moment itself—and you'd lose perhaps a quarter of the value. We'd credit you twelve crowns. Combined with your savings—"

"I don't have savings." She said it without shame, which somehow made it worse. "I have this. I have him. And I can't—" Her voice cracked and she mastered it, visibly, hand over hand like hauling rope. "Master clerk. If I keep the center, I keep *him*. Don't you understand? I'm not selling a memory. I'm selling the last place he's alive. If any corner of it stays in me, I'll never get on that coach. I know myself. I'll stand at the gate and I'll turn around and I'll come back to this city and sit by that river until it takes me too. Take all of it. Take every minute. Please."

The clock ticked. The sphere turned. The boy held up his fish to a father who wasn't in the frame, or maybe was, maybe the father was the one holding the memory now, and that was why she'd kept it so clean, why she'd rationed herself, because it was the last photograph of a country that no longer existed and she was about to surrender its only border.

Coral Vane had taken an oath once. It was carved into the marrow of him, older than the guild, older than his employment, sworn to a god who had gone silent eleven years ago mid-sentence, and part of that oath—the part he could still recite, the part that hadn't rotted—was a line about weighing true. *Let every scale I hold weigh true, and let me bear the weight of what I measure.*

He had always read it as a promise about honesty.

Standing at the counter, pen in hand, he understood for the first time that it might have been a warning.

"Forty crowns," he said. "Intact memory, full extraction, standard fee waived." He heard himself add, from somewhere outside his own jurisdiction: "First-time client courtesy."

"There's no such courtesy," Maren said. "My neighbor sold a memory here in spring. She told me all the fees."

"Then she'll be pleased to know the guild has become more generous in its old age." He was already writing it, the false line item, the small fraud, his handwriting as neat as ever—as if neatness could launder a thing. "Sign here. Thumbprint below. The extraction takes a quarter hour; our decanter will see you comfortable. You'll feel a coolness. Like weather changing. Most clients describe it as a door closing gently in a far room."

"A door closing," Maren repeated. She pressed her thumb into the ink, then the page, and her print sat there beside his notation, red-brown, unmistakably human. "Will it hurt him? In the memory. Will he know?"

"No one in a memory knows anything," Coral said. "That's what makes them safe to sell."

He regretted it before the sentence finished landing. It was true, and it was cruel, and it was the kind of thing a clerk says, and he watched it hit her like a slap she felt she deserved.

"He won't know," she whispered. "No. No, he wouldn't, would he. He never knew anything, that boy. He thought the world was kind." She gathered the linen to her chest one last time, then set it down, then picked it up again, then set it down and stepped back with her hands pressed hard together as if holding herself shut. "Do it. Before I'm wise about it."

* * *

Sable Quill appeared the way she always appeared, which was to say she had probably been there the entire time.

Coral sensed rather than saw her: a change in the pressure of the room, the particular silence of the intake hall rearranging itself around a stronger gravity. When he turned, she was standing at the end of the counter, reviewing the day's ledger upside down, which she could read as easily as right-side up and considered a useful intimidation.

"Sable," he said.

"Guildmistress Quill," she corrected, mildly, out of ritual rather than pique. She was a narrow woman, silver-streaked, dressed in black so fine it drank the light, with a quill of actual bone tucked behind one ear—a relic from the guild's founding, she claimed, though Coral had long suspected it was goose, aged well. Her eyes were the color of ledger ink and about as warm. "Forty crowns for a full-fidelity childhood memory. Waiving the extraction fee. First-time courtesy." She tapped the fraudulent line with one lacquered nail. "Inventive. I don't recall authorizing it."

"You authorized discretion in matters of client welfare. Article Nine."

"Article Nine covers clients who faint, Coral, not clients who make you sad."

"It covers *welfare*," he said. "I interpreted broadly."

"You interpreted generously." She closed the ledger with one finger marking his entry. "Which is a vice in this trade. Generosity is how clerks start giving things away, and then the guild starts losing money, and then the guild starts dying, and then everyone who depends on the guild—every debtor we've forgiven, every pension we pay, every runner and decanter and night-watchman under this roof—loses their livelihood. Generosity isn't kindness, Coral. It's theft from people who aren't in the room."

"She's selling the last living image of her child for bus fare east."

"And we're the only house in Vessia that will give her fair weight for it, keep it safe, and sell it to someone who needs it more than she does. Do you know what the pawnshops across the river pay for memories? A third of our rate, and no guarantee the vessel holds. We are the *merciful* option. That's the joke of us. That's the whole rotten joke." Sable's voice never rose. It compressed instead, like coal becoming diamond, and the result was heavier than shouting. "You gave away eight crowns of margin this morning to feel clean. Fine. I'll allow it, once, because you've been faithful for eleven years and faithfulness earns a man one indulgence. But mark this, clerk: the day you start deciding who deserves mercy is the day you stop being a bookkeeper and start being a god. And gods, in my experience, are bad for business."

She said it lightly. She meant it completely. And Coral, who had served a god and knew exactly how bad for business they could be, said nothing at all.

Behind them, in the extraction room, the decanter's bell rang once—the soft chime that meant the work was done. A moment later Maren Ostwick emerged, and Coral watched her the way you watch a building after an earthquake, checking for collapse.

She looked lighter. That was the horror of it. Extraction took the weight and left the shape, and she stood there in her soft grey dress with her hands hanging easy at her sides, and when she glanced toward the counter where the smoked-glass sphere now sat in its padded drawer, her expression was politely blank. A stranger's souvenir. A pretty amber marble.

"Is it done?" she asked him.

"It's done."

"Good." She frowned slightly, searching herself the way you search a pocket for keys you've decided to lose. "I feel—I feel like I've forgotten an appointment. Isn't that strange? I feel like I was supposed to be somewhere this morning, and I've missed it, and it doesn't matter at all." She smiled at him, brief and unburdened, and it was the worst thing Coral had seen in eleven years of professional horrors. "Thank you, master clerk. Which way to the eastern coach?"

He gave her directions. He watched her go, upright, almost buoyant, a woman amputated and already adapting to the missing limb, and he stood at his counter until the gold light of the doorway swallowed her, and then stood longer.

"Coral." Sable's voice, quieter now. Almost gentle, which from Sable was more alarming than anger. "It gets easier."

"That's what frightens me."

She studied him for a long moment, head tilted, and something moved behind her ink-dark eyes—calculation, yes, but underneath it something older and wearier, a woman looking at a younger version of some choice she'd made herself decades ago. "Go take your meal," she said finally. "And when you come back, check the vault ledgers. All of them. The old ones, the ones nobody opens."

"Why the old ones?"

"Because this morning," Sable Quill said, and for the first time in the years Coral had known her, she hesitated, "three separate shrines in Lowmarket lit with answering fire, and a prayer for rain was granted in Harrow Fen before the priest finished speaking it, and I have been alive a long time, clerk, and I know what it means when the merchandise starts moving on its own."

She turned and walked back into the depths of the guild, black silk swallowing her, and Coral Vane stood alone at the counter beside a drawer containing a boy with a fish, feeling the first hairline crack run through the foundation of his quiet, orderly world—listening, without yet understanding why, for the sound of pages turning in a cellar no one had opened in a hundred years.
