## 6. The Cartographer of Nowhere

The map that ruined Ilex Voss had been drawn in her own blood.

Not metaphorically—she'd cut her palm with a penknife at nineteen and mixed the red into her ink because her master, old Corvane of the Surveyor's Guild, swore that the best maps were made by people willing to leave something of themselves in them. She'd thought it was a lesson about dedication. It turned out to be a lesson about evidence. When the Inquisitors came to burn her cartography hall, they traced the coastlines on her masterworks back to their source, and the source was her, and her blood remembered what her country was not permitted to remember: that Vessia existed.

That was eleven years ago. Since then she had been many things—a fugitive, a forger, a woman who sold "decorative charts" to merchants who never asked why every coastline looked slightly wrong, as if the ink itself flinched from the truth. She had learned to draw countries that weren't real so convincingly that customs men wept over them. She had learned to stop drawing the one that was real.

She had not learned to forget it. That was supposed to be impossible.

* * *

Ilex woke before dawn in the attic room above a chandler's shop in Lowmarsh, the river district of a city that had no name anyone could agree on, and did what she did every morning: she checked that she still remembered.

It was a ritual, not a test. There was no failing it, exactly—or rather, there was no way to know if you failed, which amounted to the same thing. She lay under the sloped ceiling with its smell of tallow and old rope, closed her eyes, and walked the border road in her mind. The white cairns along the Vessia marches, each carved with the surveyor's mark—a circle quartered by a line. The toll bridge at Ceres Ford where the water ran green with copper. The mountain called the Sleeping Abbess, whose peak wore snow like a wimple even in high summer. The salt flats. The orchards of the Vale of Nine Bells. Her mother's kitchen with its blue-painted door.

All present. All accounted for. All hers alone, as far as she knew, in all the world.

She got up, dressed, and went down to work on the forgery she was finishing for a spice merchant: a chart of the Amber Archipelago, which did not exist, rendered with loving precision, because lies paid better than truth and never sent Inquisitors to your door.

The knock came at midmorning, three soft raps, and when she opened the door a boy stood on the landing holding out an envelope sealed with black wax.

"Pip," said the boy, before she asked. "From the Gilded Thorn. You'll want to read it sitting down."

"I've read everything sitting down," Ilex said. "It's standing up that gets me into trouble."

But she sat, because the seal was real—the thorn-and-gilt sigil she'd seen exactly once before, on a document that had passed through her hands years ago and changed the direction of her life—and inside the envelope, folded twice, was a map.

She knew it instantly. Not the territory—the object. She had drawn it. Eleven years ago, in the last weeks before the burning, working from memory and desperation, she had inked a single copy of a survey of the city's mercantile quarter, and she had marked on it—not names, never names, she wasn't suicidal—but initials. A private index of everyone in the quarter whose ledgers showed strange entries. People who traded in things that couldn't be traded. She'd meant it as insurance, a map of the miraculous underground, proof that if she was going to burn for believing in Vessia she'd at least take useful company with her.

Her initials-index included a mark at the corner of Candlemaker's Row: two letters, *C.V.*, beside a small notation in her own handwriting: *keeps books. pays debts no one owes him.*

"How," she said to Pip, very quietly, "did the Gilded Thorn get this?"

"The guild buys old things," Pip said, with the shrug of a child who has delivered dangerous packages before and been told not to ask. "Attics. Estates. Dead men's trunks. This came in a lot last month. Mistress Quill says the map is wrong."

"The map is wrong," Ilex repeated.

"It shows a street that isn't there. And a name written next to it that nobody can read—it keeps coming off the page. They've tried copying it nine times. Every copy loses the name." Pip leaned forward, dropping his voice to a conspirator's register, clearly delighted. "Except yours doesn't. Mistress Quill says whoever drew this map remembers something. And she says you should come to the guildhall tonight, or she sells the map to the Silent Order by week's end, and then they come find *you*, because your blood's in it and their dogs can smell a maker."

He said it cheerfully, the way children deliver catastrophes, and then he waited on her landing while she stood very still among her false archipelagos and felt the floor tilt beneath the ordinary world.

The Silent Order. She had spent a decade staying ahead of them by being no one, drawing nothing true, leaving nothing behind. And now her oldest crime was sitting in a guild's strongroom with her blood in its fibers and her habit of hope written in the margins.

"Well," she said finally. "Tell Mistress Quill I'll come. Tell her also that if this is a trap, I've survived four, and the people who set them are all worse off than I am."

Pip grinned. "She said you'd say something like that. She said to tell you: 'Good. The fifth one's mine.'"

* * *

The Gilded Thorn did not look like a place where impossible things were kept. It looked like what it pretended to be: a countinghouse, respectable and dull, all dark paneling and brass fittings and clerks with ink-stained cuffs who moved through the halls with the hushed economy of people who handled money too large to discuss aloud. Ilex had forged documents in six cities and knew the architecture of institutional power when she saw it. Power liked to be boring. Boring was camouflage.

Sable Quill received her in a map room—which Ilex took first for a joke and then for a threat. Charts covered the walls, but every one of them was wrong in the same way hers were wrong: coastlines that hesitated, rivers that trailed off into blank vellum, cities labeled with plausible nothing. A gallery of the erased, hung like trophies.

"You're the cartographer," Sable said. She was a tall woman with silver-shot hair and the particular stillness of someone who had learned that motion costs money. "You drew a map eleven years ago that shouldn't exist, marking people who trade in things that don't exist, using blood that remembers a country that was never here. Do you understand how that sentence sounds?"

"I've had practice saying it to myself," Ilex said. "In mirrors, mostly. It sounds insane every time. It stays true anyway."

"And what country would that be?" Sable's voice was light, almost idle, and entirely a blade.

Ilex looked at the wrong maps on the walls and made the decision she'd been making every morning for eleven years, the decision to keep carrying the weight. But this time she set it down between two people.

"Vessia," she said.

Something moved behind Sable Quill's eyes—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Never heard of it."

"No," Ilex agreed. "Nobody has. That's rather the point."

* * *

They brought her to him in the ledger hall.

Coral Vane sat at a desk beneath a high window, and the afternoon light came down on him like something he was enduring rather than enjoying. He was younger than she expected—mid-thirties, perhaps—with the lean, weathered look of a man who'd been a soldier before he'd been a clerk, and the specific posture of someone whose work required him to sit perfectly still for hours. His sleeves were rolled. His hands were stained to the second knuckle. On the desk before him lay the map—her map, her blood, her decade-old act of treason—and beside it, a ledger open to a page that seemed to trouble him, because he kept looking at it the way a man looks at a wound he can't explain.

"Coral Vane," Sable said. "This is Ilex Voss. She drew your street."

His eyes came up, and Ilex watched him do the thing she herself did constantly: check a stranger's face for the flicker of recognition, the half-second where a person meets something they've lost. He didn't find it. Neither did she. Two strangers, then. But strangers aligned on some axis she couldn't yet name.

"Your map has my initials on it," he said. No greeting. She approved.

"It has everyone's initials. Yours happened to survive the erasure."

"Explain that word like I'm a client," he said. "Clients get the plain version."

So she pulled a chair up without asking—she'd learned that asking permission was how you ended up not getting it—and laid her hands flat on the map, on the streets she'd drawn from a life that had supposedly never been lived.

"Eleven years ago," she said, "every border, every record, every memory of a country called Vessia was removed from the world. Not destroyed—removed. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point. Destroyed things leave ruins. Removed things leave smooth edges. Ask anyone where Vessia was and they'll tell you there's never been anything there—just empty steppe between two neighbors who have always been neighbors, though if you press them on treaties or wars or marriages across that steppe, the histories go soft as wet paper. The maps show blankness. The chronicles skip a generation. Songs lose their middle verses." She tapped the vellum. "But paper holds what people can't. Ink doesn't forget unless you tell it to, and whatever unmade Vessia told *people*, not parchment. So somewhere in the world there are surviving documents—misfiled surveys, pressed flowers in old books, letters in dead relatives' trunks—that mention a place that officially isn't. I know because I've spent ten years buying them. And because I'm standing in one."

Coral had gone very still. Not skeptical-still. She knew skepticism; skepticism fidgeted. This was the stillness of a man hearing his own secret spoken in another voice.

"The street on this map," he said slowly. "Candlemaker's Row."

"Was real. The Row, the guildhall that stands on it now—no. Wait." She frowned, bent closer, tracing with one finger. "No, the guildhall isn't on the Row. The Row runs where the guildhall's eastern wing is. The building was built *over* the street. Or the street was built—" She stopped. Looked up. Looked at the ceiling, as if the floors above might resolve into cobblestones and shopfronts if she stared hard enough.

"Go on," Coral said quietly.

"When I drew this map, eleven years ago, the Gilded Thorn stood on Candlemaker's Row like every other business. A building among buildings. Now it sits on a street that doesn't exist, in a spot where the map says there should be open ground." Her finger trembled slightly; she flattened it. "Buildings don't migrate, Master Vane. But places that were erased can be... reoccupied. Anchored. Someone put this guildhall down on top of a hole in the world, the way you'd put a stone on top of something you didn't want crawling out."

The silence that followed had texture. Somewhere above them a clerk's footsteps crossed the floor, each one precise as a coin dropped.

"That's not the strangest thing," Coral said at last. "You should see the ledgers."

"I've heard about the ledgers."

"They're writing themselves," he said. "Entries surfacing in books that were blank an hour ago. Each one records a prayer answered somewhere in the city—and a price taken for answering it." He turned the open ledger toward her, and Ilex looked down at fresh ink that glistened like something recent and wet, like a scab, and read:

*Prayed for: rain for the Marrow Street gardens.*
*Paid: the petitioner's memory of her sister's laugh.*

And below it, in the same wounded hand:

*Prayed for: a son's fever broken.*
*Paid: the mother's name.*

"There are hundreds," Coral said. "They started three days ago. Whatever cage these books are, the lock has begun to turn."

*Cage.* He said it the way she'd once said *erasure*—a terrible hypothesis offered calmly, already believed, waiting only for confirmation. Ilex understood then why her initials-index had flagged him all those years ago. Some people kept books. And some people, given enough time and enough silence, became the kind of person who noticed what the books were for.

"My god used to answer prayers," he added, and his voice did something complicated and controlled. "Eleven years ago it stopped. Mid-oath. I've assumed since that it died."

"Eleven years ago," Ilex repeated. The number rang against the other number in her head and both chimed. "Vessia was erased eleven years ago."

They looked at each other across the bleeding ledger, and neither spoke for a moment, because there was a shape forming in the space between those two facts, and it was large, and it had teeth.

"If your god went silent the same season my country was deleted," she said carefully, "then maybe those aren't two tragedies. Maybe they're one tragedy with two symptoms."

"Or," Coral said, "maybe your country was the price, and mine was one of the payers."

The words dropped into the room and lay there. Ilex found she couldn't refute them. She thought of the smooth edges on the maps, the histories gone soft, the whole vast absence where Vessia had been—and she thought, with a vertigo she'd held at bay for a decade: *what if it wasn't stolen? What if it was spent?*

"I need to see the cellar," she said.

Coral's eyebrows rose. "There's no cellar on any plan of this building."

"Of course there isn't," Ilex said. "Nothing important about this place will ever be on a plan. That's how I know it matters." She stood, tucked her blood-map into her coat, and met his eyes. "Master Vane. I have spent eleven years being the only person alive who remembers a whole country. Do you know what that does to a mind? You start to doubt your own childhood. You start to think maybe the Inquisitors were right and you invented it, all of it, out of grief. I have wanted—one witness. One other person who carries an impossibility and hasn't drowned in it. And now I find a man whose god stopped speaking the day my homeland stopped existing, sitting in a guild that trades in bottled grief, over ledgers that bleed prayers." She exhaled. "I don't trust you. I want that on the record. But I believe you're real, and after eleven years, *real* is worth more to me than trust."

Something shifted in his face—not warmth, exactly. Recognition of a kind. The look of a man finding another survivor on a raft.

"For the record," he said, "I don't trust you either. But you drew my street before it disappeared, and I've been keeping accounts long enough to know a creditor when I see one." He closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a dying man's eyes. "Come on, then. Let's go find the cellar that doesn't exist."

* * *

They found it behind the wine racks, which was almost too perfect—Ilex would later decide the universe had a sense of humor and simply never used it kindly. A door that wasn't on any plan, black wood banded in iron gone green with age, and when Coral set his hand flat against it, his whole body shuddered like a struck bell.

Ilex heard nothing. But she *felt* something—a pressure, a hum below hearing, the sensation of standing near an enormous engine or an enormous grief—and she watched Coral's face as he listened to whatever was on the other side, and she saw hope arrive on it like an alarm bell, terror and joy wearing each other's clothes.

"It's alive," he whispered. "After eleven years. It's *alive*."

Through the black thornwood, faint as a voice carried across deep water, something answered him. Ilex couldn't make out the words. But she saw what they did to him, and she thought: *there's my second impossibility. First, a deleted country. Now, a caged god. And I have a bad feeling the two invoices are going to turn out to be the same invoice.*

Behind them, at the top of the cellar stairs, a floorboard creaked.

Coral went rigid. Ilex turned.

Sable Quill stood in the doorway, lantern raised, silver hair lit gold at the edges, looking down at the two of them—at the door, at Coral's hand upon it—with an expression Ilex had seen once before, years ago, on a magistrate reading a verdict he'd been ordered to deliver.

"Mistress Quill," Coral said carefully.

"You found it," Sable said. Her voice was level. Her knuckles were white around the lantern. "I was rather hoping you wouldn't. We need to talk, Coral. Tonight. Before the Order's scouts reach the city gates." Her eyes flicked to Ilex, cold and measuring. "Bring your cartographer. She should hear what her map cost the world."
