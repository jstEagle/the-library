## 11. First Prayer, First Price

Pip had stolen bread from the Gilded Thorn's pantry exactly forty-one times before the gods came back.

She knew the number because she kept a ledger of her own—a scrap of greasy paper folded into eight squares and hidden in the lining of her left boot, where nobody ever checked because nobody ever wanted to touch her boots. *Bread, one heel. Bread, two heels, owed to Tansy. Candle stub. Bread.* She wrote everything down the way Coral had taught her without meaning to, back when she'd first been assigned to carry his ink and sharpen his pens: *If it isn't written down, it didn't happen. If it did happen and isn't written down, someone will make you pay twice.*

That was the whole guild in a sentence, as far as Pip could tell. The Gilded Thorn traded in impossible things—bottled grief, borrowed names, doors that only opened once—and every impossible thing went into a ledger, and every ledger went into Coral's careful hands, and Coral's hands never shook.

Pip's hands shook constantly. That was why she was a runner and not a clerk. Also she was eleven, also she was three crowns into a debt she'd inherited from a mother who'd traded away her own name to pay a doctor, also she could fit through the gap under the cellar stairs, which made her more valuable than any clerk alive.

The night the bell began ringing—the urgent pattern, unprecedented-debtor, come-quick—Pip was already running. She'd been the one who heard it happen.

* * *

Here is what actually occurred, which Pip later wrote down on the eighth square of her paper, pressing so hard the pencil went through:

The kitchen of the Gilded Thorn fed maybe thirty people badly. Cook Marta stretched a sack of barley across four days and flavored it with whatever the eastern wing's debtors brought in trade, and everyone ate standing up, fast, before the bigger children got there. Pip had learned to eat with her elbows out like a street cat.

But that evening the pot had come up short. Marta scraped it and said what she always said, *there'll be bread tomorrow,* and there wouldn't be bread tomorrow, because the grain merchant had stopped extending credit the same week the shrines started flaring, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it.

Pip went out to the yard behind the kitchens to be hungry in private, which was a skill she'd perfected. And the strangest thing happened, which was this: she looked up at the sky—Vessia's sky, gray as dishwater, holding its breath—and without deciding to, without any thought at all, she prayed.

Not properly. Not kneeling, not with the words any priest would recognize. She just said it, out loud, to the air, the way you'd say something to a person who used to listen:

*"I'm hungry."*

Two words. A complaint, really. Not even a request. Her mother had taught her never to pray, had held Pip's face in both hands and said, *the gods of Vessia stopped answering a hundred years ago, love, and anything that answers anyway isn't a god, it's a bill collector.* But her mother's name had been traded away and her mother's face lived now in the memory of some paying client, and Pip was eleven and hungry, and the words came out on their own.

The air over the yard went gold.

Not bright, not loud. Just gold, the way butter is gold, spreading from a point above the well, and the smell arrived next—warm bread, real bread, the kind with a crust that crackles, and roast fowl, and honey, and apples, an entire autumn orchard of apples—and then the food itself, settling onto the washboards and the woodpile and the flat top of the well like it had been set there by invisible hands setting a table.

A whole feast. Enough for fifty people. Steam still rising off it.

Pip stood there with her mouth open and her hunger suddenly enormous and unbearable, because the smell was so much worse than nothing, and she did what anyone would do.

She yelled for the others.

* * *

They came pouring out of the Thorn like ants from a kicked hill. Runners first, then the child debtors, then the adults, then Sable Quill herself, drawn by the noise, standing in the doorway with her silver-topped cane and her face doing something complicated that Pip had no words for yet.

"Who paid for this?" Sable asked. Her voice was level. It was the levelness Pip would remember afterward, the terrible calm of a woman watching her life's work catch fire.

Nobody had paid. Nobody could say who had paid. Pip, standing at the edge of the feast she'd summoned, felt the whole yard's attention swing toward her like a compass needle, and she almost lied. She got as far as opening her mouth.

"I prayed," she said. "I just—I said I was hungry."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on your ears.

Then Tansy—fifteen, biggest of the runners, already holding a drumstick—said, "It's going cold," and that broke it. Because they were children of the Thorn, every one of them, raised on ledgers and interest and the doctrine that nothing is free, but they were also children, and there was a feast steaming in front of them, and the god or whatever-it-was hadn't asked for a signature.

Sable didn't eat. Pip noticed that. Sable stood at the edge with her knuckles white on her cane while the children of her guild tore bread and laughed and stuffed themselves, and her eyes moved over each of them like she was counting, or like she was taking inventory before a flood.

Coral wasn't there either. He was down in the ledger hall. Pip would think about that later too—how he hadn't come running at the shouting, how he must have been reading when the feast appeared, reading entries that were writing themselves, and how he had chosen not to come up. Chosen to stay with the books. At the time she just thought he wasn't hungry.

Pip ate three apples and half a loaf and a piece of honeycomb that tasted like sunlight feels, and she laughed until her ribs hurt when little Wren put an entire chicken bone in his pocket *for later*, and it was the best hour of her life, and she wrote it down that night in her boot-ledger in letters that wobbled:

*Feast. Free. No entry fee. (Check again tomorrow.)*

She should have checked sooner.

* * *

The forgetting happened the next morning, and it happened quietly, the way most prices are collected.

Her name was Marla. She lived in the crooked house against the Thorn's east wall with her husband and her loom, and she was the neighbor who pretended not to see the runners squeezing through the fence gap, and sometimes—maybe twice a year, on no schedule Pip could find—she left a basket of eggs on the step by the coal chute. Not charity. Marla would have bitten anyone who called it charity. Just eggs. Just because.

Pip had been sent to the chandler's on Cordwainer Row for tallow, six sticks, and she cut through the alley behind Marla's house because it saved ninety steps, and Marla was outside beating a rug.

Marla looked straight at her. Looked through her. And turned back to her rug.

"Mornin', Marla!" Pip called, because she was eleven and the world was still working.

Nothing. Another beat of the rug. Dust rising gold in the morning light—gold like the feast-light, Pip thought, and didn't know why she thought it.

Pip stopped walking. "Marla? It's me. Pip."

Marla frowned—not at Pip, never at Pip—but at the air near her, the way you frown at a draft. "Did you hear something?" she said, to the rug, to the alley, to no one. "Sounded like—" and then she shook her head, puzzled, and the puzzlement slid off her face like water off oilcloth, and she beat the rug again, and she had completely forgotten that anything had spoken at all.

Pip stood in the alley with six sticks of tallow getting heavy in her arms and understood, in the slow horrible way you understand things at eleven, that something had gone wrong with the shape of the world and that she was standing inside the wrong part of it.

She ran to the egg step. Empty—no, not empty, swept. The stone was swept clean, and had been for a long time, and there were no egg-baskets anywhere in the memory of the step, and when she knocked on Marla's door and Marla opened it, Marla said, "Can I help you, little one?" in the voice you use for strangers, for beggars, for children selling things you don't want, and her eyes were kind and utterly blank.

"Do you know me?" Pip said. Her voice came out small.

"I don't believe so," Marla said gently. "Should I?"

And then—this was the worst part, Pip would write, this exact part—Marla's brow creased, and she pressed a hand briefly to her temple, and she said, "Strange. I feel like I've lost something today. Like leaving a room and forgetting why." And she smiled apologetically and closed the door, and through the window Pip saw her go back to her loom, weaving, weaving, steady as a heartbeat, a woman missing something she couldn't name.

Pip sat down on the swept egg-step and cried the way children cry, all at once and gracelessly, and then wiped her face on her sleeve, and then took out the greasy paper from her boot and added a line, because Coral's rule held even here, *especially* here:

*Marla forgot me. The feast cost Marla. I ate the feast. My fault. How do you pay somebody back for being forgotten?*

Underneath, after a long time, she wrote: *Ask Coral.*

* * *

By noon, every child in the Gilded Thorn knew.

They gathered in the loft over the stables—the runners' territory, sacred ground, off-limits to clerks and mistresses—sitting on hay bales in a circle, the way they held their real meetings, the ones about which adults must never know. There were nine of them. Tansy, Wren, the twins Osk and Brindle, quiet Hesper who'd been sold to the guild at seven for her father's gambling debts, fat-cheeked Dunny, little Moss who still sucked her thumb, solemn Juno who read everything, and Pip.

And they compared notes, and the notes were bad.

Tansy's grandmother's sister—the one who sent the winter socks—no longer existed for Tansy's family; when Tansy mentioned her, her mother's face went smooth and confused. Wren's friend Col from Cordwainer Row had stopped waving back. Hesper's dog—Hesper had gotten a dog somehow, a yellow mutt named Turnip—had looked at her yesterday with a stranger's eyes and followed another boy home.

"It's a trade," Juno said. Juno always said it was a trade. "Every prayer answered takes something. That's how the old ledgers worked—that's what Coral says the ledgers *are*. You get the thing you asked for, and the price gets taken from around you, because the price can't be taken from you. The debtor has to stay solvent. So it collects from the edges."

"From the people who love us," Hesper said. She was looking at her hands.

"That's not fair," said Moss, who was seven, whose thumb was in her mouth, whose whole theology was fairness.

"No," said Tansy, who was fifteen and had been a debtor longest. "It's not."

The loft went quiet. Below them, a horse shifted in its stall. Through the slats, the light fell in dusty bars, and somewhere out in the city another shrine flared—you could see it even in daylight now, a golden smudge over the rooftops, third one that morning—and Pip watched all of them watch it, nine children of the Thorn, each one silently doing arithmetic.

Because here was the terrible knowledge, arriving all at once, curdling the innocence out of the room: they were hungry. They had always been hungry. And the gods of Vessia were answering prayers again, and answering was easy, and the price came out of the edges—from grandmothers' sisters, from dogs, from neighbors with eggs—and every single one of them could have a feast tonight if they just opened their mouths.

Moss was crying without knowing she was crying. "I miss Turnip," she said, though Turnip had been Hesper's dog. It didn't matter. It was all the same loss now, pooled together.

"So we don't pray," Dunny said. "Easy. We just don't."

"You didn't pray last time either," Juno said to Pip. Not cruelly. Just accurately. "You complained. The gods don't need us to ask anymore. They're *listening*."

That was the sentence that changed the room. Pip felt it land in everyone at once, the way weather changes. If listening was automatic, then wanting was dangerous. If wanting was dangerous, then—

"We stop wanting," Tansy said slowly, testing it. "Out loud. We stop saying what we need. Even to each other."

Nobody liked it. Everybody saw the sense of it. Nine children of the Gilded Thorn sat in a hayloft and made a vow that no adult had asked of them: they would be silent about their hunger, their cold, their loneliness, because speaking those things aloud had become a currency, and spending it spent other people.

It was the most grown-up thing Pip had ever done, and it felt exactly like dying a little.

* * *

She found Coral at dusk in the ledger hall, because of course she did, because he was always there, bent over the books with his sleeves rolled and his pen moving, a man built entirely out of careful habits. The lamps were lit. The great table ran the length of the room, and the ledgers lay open on it in their dozens, and Pip stopped dead in the doorway because the ledgers were *moving*.

Ink was surfacing on the pages like blood through a bandage. New lines, darkening, writing themselves in a hand that was almost Coral's hand but not quite—older, hungrier. Entry after entry. *Prayer answered: rain for the miller of Harrow Bend. Price extracted: the miller's daughter's memory of her own wedding day.* *Prayer answered: a fever broken. Price extracted: the physician's recognition of his own reflection.* Page after page after page, filling up with miracles and their costs, and Coral sitting among them reading every line, his face gray, his pen motionless for the first time Pip had ever seen.

"Coral," she whispered.

He looked up. Whatever he saw in her face made him push back his chair and hold out his arms, and Pip crossed the room at a run and hit him hard enough to knock the breath out of him, and he held on while she talked—all of it, the feast, Marla's blank eyes, the swept step, the loft, the vow—and she felt the moment his arms tightened, the moment the story reached Marla.

When she finished, he didn't say *it's not your fault.* Coral didn't lie. He was quiet a long time, one hand resting on her hair, looking at the self-writing pages as though they were a wound in the world.

"The ledgers are collecting," he said finally, half to himself. "Every answered prayer routes through the books. Which means the books decide the price. Which means whoever holds the books—" He stopped.

"Can we pay for Marla?" Pip asked. "Back? Can we buy her remembering me?"

Coral looked down at her, and Pip saw something cross his face that she'd never seen there before—something that looked like fear wearing the mask of calculation. "Everything has a price in this building," he said. "That's the problem, Pip. That's always been the problem."

He turned back to the bleeding ledgers, and Pip watched him read, and she saw the exact line that stopped his pen. She leaned in and read it too, in that older-hungrier hand:

*Debtor account: P. — minor, unregistered, debt inherited. Prayer received: sustenance. Price assessed and collected. Note:* —and here the ink seemed to press deeper, darker, deliberate— *the Order has been notified. Auditors dispatched. Hold all accounts.*

"Audit," Coral said softly.

"What's an audit?" Pip asked.

He didn't answer right away. Outside, far off across the city, bells began to ring—not the Thorn's runners' pattern, not any pattern Pip knew. Slower. Graver. From the direction of the harbor gates, where nothing ever came in at this hour, hoofbeats sounded on cobblestones, many horses, riding in formation, keeping perfect time.

Coral rose, blew out the lamp, and lifted Pip bodily onto his hip as if she weighed nothing—as if she were the only account he intended to protect.

"Pip," he said quietly, carrying her toward the door, toward the sound, "go wake Sable. Tell her the Silent Order is coming to count us."
