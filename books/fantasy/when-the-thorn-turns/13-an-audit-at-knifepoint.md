## 13. An Audit at Knifepoint

The knock came at dawn, and Coral knew before he opened the ledger hall's door that it would be trouble, because honest visitors to the Gilded Thorn never knocked at dawn. Honest visitors came at dusk, hooded, ashamed, wanting to trade something they couldn't bear to keep. Dawn was for officials. Dawn was for men with warrants folded in their coats like knives.

He opened the door anyway.

Three figures stood in the grey light of Ledger Street. Two were Silent Order enforcers in their bone-pale surcoats, faces wrapped to the eyes in grey wool, hands resting on the hilts of short blades that Coral had seen used exactly once before—on a debtor in Saltmarket who had tried to pray his way out of a contract. The third stood slightly forward, hands clasped behind his back, unhurried, as though the morning belonged to him personally.

High Warden Casserly was not what Coral had expected. He had imagined the head of the Silent Order as something carved from cruelty—a big man, a scarred man, a man whose face announced its work. Instead Casserly was slight, silvering at the temples, with the mild, patient look of a schoolmaster or an accountant. His eyes were the problem. They were pale grey, almost colorless, and they moved over Coral the way Coral's own eyes moved over a ledger page: reading, totaling, finding the entry that didn't balance.

"Master Vane," Casserly said. His voice was soft, courteous, and completely without warmth, like paper. "Forgive the hour. I'm given to understand the Thorn celebrates its centennial soon. An institution of that age invites inspection. The Crown takes an interest in institutions that trade in—" he paused, delicately, "—*goods*."

"The guild is licensed under charter," Coral said. "Inspections are conducted through the Office of Trade, by appointment."

"Yes." Casserly smiled faintly. "I am not the Office of Trade."

Behind him, one of the enforcers shifted, and the sound of his boot on the cobbles was very small and very loud.

Coral had spent eleven years learning what fear felt like in his own body so that he could file it away properly. It arrived now on schedule: cold in the fingertips, a tightening behind the sternum, the animal urge to run. He noted each symptom the way he'd note an irregular entry, entered it into the appropriate column, and let none of it reach his face. That was the first rule of the ledgers, and it turned out to be the first rule of lying to a Warden too: *record everything, reveal nothing.*

"Then you'll want tea," Coral said, stepping back from the doorway. "And you'll want Sable Quill, who holds the guild's charters. She's at the eastern wing. I can send for her."

"You'll do neither." Casserly crossed the threshold without being asked, and the two enforcers followed, and the door closed behind them with a click that sounded, to Coral's carefully calibrated ear, exactly like a lock. "Mistress Quill is being interviewed separately. By my colleagues. You and I are going to conduct a different kind of audit, Master Vane. A *sampling*. I select entries at random. You account for them. If your accounts are true, we shall be finished before lunch, and I will leave you to your centennial preparations with my apologies."

"And if they're not?"

Casserly looked at him with that mild, total attention. "Then we will be finished after lunch instead. And rather less pleasantly."

* * *

They sat in the small audit room off the ledger hall—the room where Coral did his most delicate work, where the windows were shuttered and the light came from a single lamp, because some transactions could not survive daylight. Casserly took the client's chair. Coral sat across from him with the current ledger open between them, and the two enforcers stood at his back, and he was aware of them the way a man is aware of weather about to turn.

"Entry four hundred and six," Casserly said. He hadn't touched the book; he recited from memory, which told Coral he'd already read it, which told Coral he'd been inside the guild longer than the knock at dawn implied. "Dated the ninth of this month. One vial of bottled grief, purchased by a client designated only as *the Widow Ashen*. Paid in full. Currency listed as—" his colorless eyes lifted, "—*one prayer, answered*."

Coral kept his breathing even. The entry was real. He had written it himself, three days before the shrines began to flare, back when a prayer answered was still an impossibility, back when the entry should have been flagged as fraud and wasn't, because Coral had checked the vault himself and found the vial genuinely filled.

"A clerical shorthand," Coral said. "Some clients insist on unusual payment terms. We record them as offered. Whether the payment was genuine is a matter for the appraisers, not the clerk."

"Was it genuine?"

"I don't know. I recorded the transaction. I didn't witness the prayer."

"But you verified the goods." Casserly's finger came down on the page, light as a moth. "Your own marginal note: *contents confirmed authentic*. So either a woman's grief was extracted and bottled—which your charter permits—or a prayer was answered somewhere in this building, which every law of God and Crown says cannot happen. Which is it, Master Vane?"

There it was. The trap wasn't in the question; it was in the space between the questions, the assumption buried in the phrasing. Coral had spent years catching clients in exactly these constructions—*you paid, therefore you received; you signed, therefore you agreed.* He answered the way he'd been trained to answer, slowly, precisely, giving nothing away in the architecture of the sentence.

"Both explanations assume the entry describes what actually occurred. Entries describe what was *recorded*. What was recorded was a claim of payment by prayer, verified against delivered goods. If the verification was mistaken, the error is mine and will be corrected. If the claim was fraudulent, the client defrauded the guild, and the guild will pursue recovery. In neither case has the guild answered a prayer. The guild doesn't have that power." He paused, then added, with the exact weight of bored honesty: "No one does. That's rather the foundation of our entire economy."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Behind him, one of the enforcers breathed.

Casserly studied him for a long moment, and then—he nodded, almost approvingly, and Coral understood with a lurch that the man had enjoyed that answer, the way a swordsman enjoys meeting a competent defense.

"Very good," Casserly said softly. "You lie like a well-kept book. Every figure consistent. Every cross-reference clean." He leaned back. "Let us try another. Entry five hundred and twelve. Three nights ago. *One borrowed name, returned damaged.* Explain the damage."

That one was worse. Coral had processed it himself—a client who had rented a dead man's name for a week and brought it back frayed at the edges, the name no longer answering when called. He had logged it, sealed it, and said nothing to anyone, because names returned damaged was the kind of entry that, since the shrines began flaring, made his skin crawl with implications he refused to examine.

"Wear," Coral said. "Names degrade with use, like any borrowed thing. The client exceeded the rental terms. We assessed a penalty."

"Against whom? The name's owner is dead."

"Against the client's deposit. Dead owners still hold title. That's guild law older than the charter."

"Is it." Casserly turned a page with one finger, though Coral was nearly certain he'd already read every word on it. "And here. Entry five hundred and nineteen. Dated last night. *One memory of a son, sold for passage money.* Client name withheld at her own request." The pale eyes rose again. "A weeping woman, I'm told. Came in during the evening rush. You handled it personally."

Coral's stomach went cold and stayed cold. *I'm told.* Someone had talked. Someone always talked eventually—that was the whole philosophy of the Silent Order, he realized now, the reason for their name: everyone else spoke, and the Order simply listened.

"She requested anonymity," Coral said. "Guild law protects it."

"Guild law." Casserly tasted the words. "An interesting phrase, Master Vane, from an institution whose founding documents I have been unable to locate anywhere in the archives of the Crown. A hundred years old, and no charter of record. No founding deed. No registration of trade. Do you know what that means, in law?"

"It means our charter predates the Crown's archive system."

"It means," Casserly said, still gently, still patiently, "that the Gilded Thorn does not legally exist. And yet here it stands. And here you sit, keeping books for a thing that isn't there, recording payments in a currency that cannot exist, verifying goods that ought to be impossible." He folded his hands on the table. "Do you know what I do with things that aren't there, Master Vane?"

"No," Coral said. "But I suspect you're about to tell me."

"I erase them." For the first time, something moved behind the mildness—not anger, but a kind of terrible earnestness, the shine of a man looking at his life's purpose. "Things that shouldn't exist are wounds in the world. Every impossible trade widens them. Every prayer answered cheapens every soul that ever prayed honestly. My Order exists to close those wounds. We have done so quietly for a very long time. And now—" his voice dropped half a tone, and somehow that was worse than any threat, "—the wounds have begun to bleed."

* * *

It went on for two hours. Entry by entry, Casserly worked through the recent ledgers with the tireless patience of a man counting someone else's money, and Coral answered, and answered, and answered—each reply measured, consistent, cross-referenced against the answers he'd given before, because Casserly remembered everything and would catch a discrepancy the way Coral caught a falsified signature. Twice Coral caught himself approaching a dangerous truth and swerved, smoothly, into a smaller lie that covered it. Once he caught Casserly testing him—asking about an entry Coral knew didn't exist—and denied it flatly, and saw the flicker of respect in the Warden's colorless eyes when the trap failed.

But the sampling was not the audit. Coral understood that around the second hour, sitting in the lamplight with his heart filed neatly away behind professional calm. The real audit was happening elsewhere in the building—Sable, interviewed by other Wardens; the cellar, perhaps, already searched while Coral sat here performing diligence. Casserly wasn't trying to break him. He was keeping him *here*, occupied, while his people found whatever they were going to find.

Which meant Coral's job was not to survive the questioning. His job was to buy time and give nothing away, and pray—gods forgive him—to whatever silent thing listened below the floorboards, that the cellar door looked like nothing at all.

"Last entry," Casserly said at last, and closed the ledger with a soft finality that made the enforcers shift behind Coral. "Not in your books at all. A question of inventory." He stood, buttoned his coat with neat, economical movements. "This guild houses a cellar. Your staff confirm it stores bulk goods. Yet the guild pays no storage tax on any cellar contents, and no manifest lists what's stored there. I sent two of my people down this morning to conduct a simple count."

Coral did not move. He had spent eleven years learning to keep his face still, and he spent all of that training now, every hour of it, in the space of a single breath.

"And?"

"They haven't come back up." Casserly regarded him with that patient, totaling gaze, and for the first time Coral saw something beneath it that he had not expected and had no column for: uncertainty. Faint, controlled, but there—the first crack in the Warden's perfect bookkeeping. "The door at the bottom of the stairs is thornwood, Master Vane. Black thornwood, older than this city. My men report that it *hums*. And when they laid their hands upon it, they heard—" he stopped, and chose his words with visible care, the first time all morning he had needed to, "—singing."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far beneath Coral's feet, faint as a heartbeat through stone, something sang on.

"My Order has records going back nine hundred years," Casserly said quietly. "We have catalogued every god in captivity, every prison, every lock. There is no cell registered beneath this building. And yet my men are standing in front of a door that sings, in a guild that doesn't exist, run by a mistress who trades in prayers, audited by a clerk who lies like scripture." He picked up his hat. "I am going to go and speak with them. When I return, Master Vane, you and I will continue this conversation. And I warn you—" the mildness returned, complete and terrible, "—I have ways of auditing that do not require your cooperation. Everyone balances, in the end. It is merely a question of how much red ink it takes."

He paused at the door, and looked back, and for just a moment the schoolmaster's face was gone and something older and wearier looked out.

"For what it's worth," Casserly said, "your answers were excellent. Genuinely. I've broken better liars with kinder questions." Then the door shut behind him, and his footsteps faded down the stairs toward the cellar, toward the singing, and Coral sat alone in the lamplight with his hands flat on the ledger and his heart finally allowed to beat.

They had minutes. Perhaps less. He was moving before the thought finished forming—snuffing the lamp, pulling the current ledgers from their shelf, mind racing through the arithmetic of escape routes and warning signals and the one person downstairs he trusted more than his own name.

Because the Warden had made one mistake, small and fatal, in an otherwise flawless audit.

He had told Coral exactly where he was going.

And Coral knew what waited behind that black thornwood door—and if Casserly's men had already heard it sing, then the Silent Order now knew the one thing in all of Vessia that could never be allowed to reach the surface:

His god was alive.
