## 19. A Village That Prayed Too Well

Ilex smelled the village before she saw it.

Not rot—she knew rot, knew the sweet-black stink of drowned cart horses and the sourness of a body left three weeks in summer heat. This was something else. Something like bread. Like fresh-cut hay, like beeswax and woodsmoke and the green breath of a well-tended garden, all of it layered too thick, too perfect, the way a corpse can be dressed in its Sunday best until the perfume becomes the worst part.

"Stop," she said.

Pip stopped. Coral did not—he walked two more paces before the word caught up with him, and then he turned back, one hand already resting on the strap of his satchel where the ledger rode against his hip. Even out here, three days from the Thorn's burning walls, he carried it like a limb. Sable's copy of the eastern accounts, rescued from the fire. Ilex had watched him pull it from the smoke with his bare hands and had thought, *he would sooner lose his fingers.*

"You smell that?" she asked.

"Bread," Pip said hopefully.

"That's what worries me." Ilex crouched at the crest of the hill and pulled the cracked spyglass from her coat. The road below them wound down through barley fields so golden they looked gilded—no, she corrected herself, they *were* gilded, every head of grain fat and heavy and identical, row upon row without a single blight or bird-pecked ear. Beyond the fields: whitewashed cottages with thatch like brushed hair. A millwheel turning. Laundry on lines, white sheets snapping in a wind that came from nowhere she could read, because there was nothing left to read. Her maps ended a thumb-width east of here. Everything past that line was blank paper, and blank paper was honest, at least. Blank paper didn't pretend to know things.

Through the glass she saw people. Dozens of them, moving through the village lanes with the unhurried purpose of the contented. A woman carrying water. An old man mending a fence. Children chasing a dog between the wells.

Her chest unclenched by half. Then it clenched again, tighter, because she'd been watching for thirty seconds and hadn't seen anyone look up. Not once. Not at the sky, not at each other, not at the dog. A boy ran full-tilt past the old man at the fence—close enough to touch—and neither of them turned his head.

"There," she said, and passed the glass to Coral.

He studied the village for a long moment. His face did what it always did, which was nothing—or almost nothing; there was a small tightening at the corner of his mouth, a tell she'd learned over two hundred miles of bad road, that meant the ledgers behind his eyes were turning pages fast.

"Harvest's wrong," he said.

"Explain."

"It's past midsummer. Barley should be going over, shattering at the heads. This is standing at perfect ripeness and has been, I'd guess, since—" He paused. "Since someone prayed it so."

*Since someone answered,* Ilex thought, but didn't say. Since Vessia's gods had woken from their century of silence and begun, in their terrible new fairness, to answer everything.

They went down anyway. There was no way around—the valley narrowed ahead into country her tattoos couldn't chart, inked skin or otherwise, and the Door of Unmaking lay somewhere beyond it, drawn in scar tissue along her ribs and the inside of her left arm. The route ran through this valley. Of course it did. She was beginning to suspect the erased geography of Vessia arranged itself specifically to make travelers walk through whatever the country most wanted them to see.

*This is lesson nineteen,* she thought bitterly. *Pay attention.*

* * *

The bread smell nearly knocked her flat at the first cottage. It rose from open windows and chimneys, warm and yeasty and endless, as if every hearth in the village had a loaf in it, rising forever.

A woman stood in the first garden they passed, cutting flowers. Her basket was already overflowing—roses, lavender, marigolds, more blooms than the small plot could plausibly hold—and still her shears moved, snip, snip, snip, adding stems to a pile that had spilled onto the path and kept growing.

"Good morning," Ilex said.

The shears kept moving. Snip. Snip. The woman smiled. It was a beautiful smile—Ilex could see that even in profile, the kind of smile that made strangers trust you, that got you invited in for supper—and it was aimed at nothing. At the middle distance. At some interior vista only she could see. Snip. Another stem joined the basket. Petals drifted from the overloaded pile and settled on the woman's boots, and she did not look down at those either.

"She's not deaf," Coral said quietly. He'd stopped a pace behind Ilex, and when she glanced at him his eyes were fixed on the woman's hands. "Watch. She hears us fine."

"How do you know?"

"Because she's smiling wider."

She was. It was subtle—a fraction of extra lift at the corners of her mouth, the way a candle flame leans toward a draft. But she never turned. Never spoke. Snip, snip, and the roses piled higher, and Ilex backed away with her skin crawling and her hand pressed flat against her coat where her maps lived under the cloth.

They found the whole village like that.

The old man at the fence mended the same three palings over and over—set the stake, tied the wire, stepped back, stepped forward, untied the wire, began again. His movements were smooth, unhurried, and utterly circular. Two women at the well drew water, poured it back, drew it again. The children chased the dog, and the dog—it took Ilex far too long to notice—ran the same loop of the lane, the same loop, the same loop, its tongue lolling in a happy grin.

And everyone, everyone, was smiling.

"Not husks," Coral murmured. They stood in the square now, in front of a shrine Ilex recognized by its shape if not its god—a thorn-crowned alcove, empty, its niche scorched clean. "That's what frightens me. Husks would be easier. Husks are dead things wearing bodies. These people are—" He searched for the word, and Ilex watched him fail, which frightened her more than anything else that morning.

"Full," Pip said.

They both turned. The child had wandered ahead—of course Pip had, because Pip treated danger the way water treats a slope—and stood in the middle of the square staring up at an old woman on a bench, who was smiling down at the child with those same bottomless pleasant eyes.

"They look full," Pip said. "Like cups filled all the way to the brim. So full there's no room left for anything else. You pour one more drop in and it just—" Pip made a small gesture, fingertips spilling apart, "—runs down the sides."

Ilex felt the observation land somewhere beneath her ribs. Out of the mouths of debt-children and guild runners, the gods delivered their sharpest audits.

"Pip." Coral's voice was gentle but it had edges. "Step away from her."

"But she's *smiling* at me."

"I know. Step away."

Pip stepped away. And the old woman's smile widened by exactly the width of a departing child, and her hands folded themselves in her lap, contented, complete.

* * *

They found the prayers nailed to the shrine.

It was Coral who noticed—because of course it was, because he was a man who read everything, who had spent eleven years reading the world the way other men read weather. Thin strips of parchment, hundreds of them, fluttering in the nonexistent wind, pinned to the scorched stone around the empty niche with iron pins gone brown with rust. Each strip held a few words in different hands. Some were elegant. Some were a child's scrawl.

*Let the harvest be enough for everyone.*

*Heal Marta's lung, please, she can't breathe at night.*

*Let my husband come home from the coast road safe.*

*Send rain before the week is out.*

*I don't want to be lonely anymore.*

Coral lifted the last one free with two fingers and read it aloud, and his voice, which could recite forty years of guild accounts without inflection, cracked on the third word.

*"Make me good. Make me stop wanting things. Take it all, take all of it, I'll give you everything if you just make me stop wanting."*

Silence in the square. Even the millwheel seemed to slow.

"That one's older," Ilex said. Her own voice sounded very loud. "Look at the rust on the pin. That prayer came first."

"And the rest?" Pip asked.

"The rest are what happened after." Coral smoothed the strip of parchment flat against his palm. "One person prayed to be emptied. The god answered. And then—" He gestured at the village, at the golden fields, the turning wheel, the smiling people walking their beautiful circles. "Then it never stopped answering. Every prayer after that was answered the same way. *Let the harvest be enough*—so it grew them a harvest no one eats. *Heal Marta's lung*—and somewhere in one of these houses there's a woman breathing perfectly, smiling at a wall. *I don't want to be lonely anymore.*"

He stopped. Ilex finished it for him, because someone had to say it and because saying it out loud was the only weapon she had.

"So it emptied her too. Can't be lonely if there's no one left inside to be alone." She looked around the square. The flower woman had come into view at the lane's end, basket overflowing, petals trailing behind her like the wake of a boat. Snip, snip. Still cutting. Still smiling. "This isn't a massacre. It's a *transaction*. Every entry balanced. Every prayer granted at cost."

"And the cost," Coral said quietly, "was everything the payer had."

* * *

They searched three houses before the fourth one broke Pip.

The first was tidy as a museum. Bread on every sill—Ilex touched a loaf and found it warm, impossibly, eternally warm, risen to perfection and never baked and never cooling, bread suspended in the moment of its own promise. A table set for six. Six chairs. No dust anywhere, because dust was disorder, and disorder was want, and want had been excised from this place down to the molecular level.

The second house held beds made with hospital corners and family portraits on the wall, and this was where Ilex made her mistake: she looked at the portraits. A farmer and his wife and four children, posed stiffly in a painter's studio, generations ago by the fashion of the clothes. She tried to match faces to houses and found she couldn't. Not because the people were gone. Because the faces in the paintings had *want* in them—had hunger and humor and worry, had the specific crookedness of real lives—and nothing in this village matched anymore. Whatever answered prayers here hadn't just taken the villagers' desires. It had taken the shapes desire carved into faces. Everyone under these roofs wore the same serene mask now, aged appropriately, gendered appropriately, but underneath: one smooth surface, smiling.

The third house had a crib.

Pip had run ahead into it, the way Pip ran everywhere, and Ilex followed the sudden silence and found the child standing very still beside a cradle that rocked gently, gently, with no hand near it. Inside lay a baby, pink-cheeked and sleeping, and smiling even in sleep—that same smile, that awful perfect curve—and above the crib hung a little wooden charm, the kind mothers hang to ward off colic, and burned into the wood in careful letters:

*Thy will be done.*

"Is it—" Pip's voice came out very small. "Is it alive?"

"Yes," Coral said from the doorway. He had appeared there the way he always did, silently, and Ilex realized she'd stopped being surprised by that and started depending on it, and decided not to examine what that meant. "Heartbeat's strong. It'll grow up. It'll learn to walk in circles and smile at strangers and cut flowers that never fit in the basket." He crossed the room and, very carefully, did not touch the child. "It'll live a long time. That's the part that gets me. Nothing here is dying, Ilex. That's the trick of it. This village is the healthiest place in Vessia."

"Stop," Pip whispered.

"What?"

"*Stop calling it a village.*" The child's fists were white. "It's a graveyard. It's a graveyard where the graves smile at you. Don't—don't stand there talking about how healthy it is, Coral, don't—" Pip's voice climbed, cracked, dropped. "My ma prayed like that. In the famine year. I remember. She sat up all night with her hands like this—" small fingers interlaced, knuckles straining, "—and she said, *gods, take anything, take my hunger, take my wanting, just let my children eat.*" Pip looked up, and there were tears on the child's face, furious ones. "If the gods had been awake that year—if they'd answered *that*—"

The sentence hung unfinished. It didn't need finishing. If the gods had answered, Pip would be standing in this square right now, smiling at strangers, chasing a dog in a circle, and some traveler three centuries hence would find the town and call it peaceful.

Ilex crossed the room and put her arms around the child, and Pip shook against her shoulder, and Coral stood by the crib with his eyes closed and his hand flat on the ledger-satchel like a man taking an oath.

"We leave the baby," he said at last, very quietly. "We can't carry what we can't wake. But we mark the village. On your map, Ilex, if there's any blank space left—here, we write it down. What it is. What happened. So the next traveler doesn't call it peaceful."

"There's space," Ilex said. "There's always space. That's the whole problem with erased countries." And then, because her mouth had always moved faster than her judgment: "You understand what this place is arguing, don't you?"

They both looked at her.

"This is the case *for* Casserly." She heard herself say it and hated it, and said it anyway, because a mapmaker's first duty was accuracy. "The Silent Order wants the gods caged. Wants the ledgers locked, wants miracles priced out of reach of desperate people. I've been thinking of them as the enemy since the day they burned my license. But you look at this square, you watch that woman cut her flowers forever, and you tell me the Order is wrong about what happens when prayer runs loose. Maybe a silent god isn't a cruelty. Maybe it's a mercy with better manners."

"No," Coral said.

It wasn't sharp. That was the thing about him—his refusals arrived with the calm weight of a ledger entry, and somehow that made them heavier than shouting. He opened his eyes and looked at her across the sleeping, smiling child.

"A silent god isn't a mercy. It's an unpaid debt. Eleven years I worked for a god that wouldn't speak, and I told myself every day that the silence meant discipline, meant wisdom, meant the silence itself was the lesson." His jaw tightened. "It was none of those things. It was a cage. And this—" he nodded at the window, beyond which the flower woman passed again, petals drifting, "—isn't what freedom looks like either. This is what a *bad contract* looks like. A prayer written by someone drowning, accepted by something that never once asked, *are you sure? Do you understand the terms?* The Order looks at this village and says the answer is fewer doors. I look at it and say the answer is honest ledgers. Terms in plain language. Prices marked before you sign."

"And when the price is everything?" Ilex asked. "When someone's drowning badly enough to sign anything?"

Coral was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the millwheel turned. Somewhere a child laughed—bright, hollow, endless.

"Then someone has to be standing next to them," he said finally, "who loves them enough to grab the pen."

* * *

They camped that night on the hill above the village, close enough to see the lights—not one of which flickered, because nothing in that valley wanted anything, not warmth, not safety, not morning—and Ilex sat up the first watch with her journal on her knees and her pen moving in the dark.

She drew the village first. Every mapmaker's habit: outline, roads, landmarks. She drew the shrine with its nailed prayers. She drew the well, the mill, the looping lane. Then, because the drawing was accurate and the truth demanded more, she drew the people. Small figures, dozens of them, and into each tiny face she set the same curved line—the smile—and beneath the drawing she wrote, in the cramped cipher she'd used since the Erasure taught her that plain text could be confiscated:

*Population: full. Wanting: none. Alive: technically.*

*Note to self:* she paused, pen hovering. Below the valley, the lights burned steady as buried stars. *Note to self: this is what we're racing toward. Not Casserly's army. This. The Door leads OUT of a country where gods answer prayers. Ask yourself why anyone built a door like that in the first place. Ask yourself who was desperate enough.*

*Ask yourself what Coral would pay.*

She wrote the last line before she could stop herself, stared at it, and did not cross it out. A mapmaker's first duty was accuracy.

It was deep in the night watch, near dawn, when she heard the footsteps on the road below—many of them, unhurried, keeping cadence like soldiers—and crawled to the crest of the hill with her heart hammering against her tattooed ribs.

Down in the valley, where the golden fields met the white road, torchlight moved in ranks. Banners she knew too well, gray as ash. And at the head of the column, riding a horse the color of old bone, a tall figure in the Silent Order's high collar lifted a hand, and the army halted, and a hundred helmets turned toward the hilltop where she sat.

Toward the hilltop where *all three of them* slept.

Ilex's hand found Coral's shoulder in the dark.
