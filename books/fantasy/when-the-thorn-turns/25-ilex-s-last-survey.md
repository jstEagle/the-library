## 25. Ilex's Last Survey

The anchor point was a mile back along the ridge, and Ilex had known it was hers from the moment she'd seen it — the way a surveyor knows a true bearing, the way a liar knows her own handwriting.

They'd made camp in the hollow below the cliff face, where the Door of Unmaking stood in its frame of black thornwood, patient as a held breath. From the hollow you couldn't see the Order's fires yet. From the ridge you could. That was the arithmetic of it, and Ilex had never been any good at pretending arithmetic said something else.

She spread her maps on the flat stone one more time, though she didn't need to. She knew every line of them. She'd inked them into her own skin, after all — the coast that no longer existed, the rivers with their traded-away names, the border that had been sold for a promise nobody alive remembered making. The route from the hollow to the anchor point ran along the spine of the ridge for a mile and then cut down through a fold of rock that wasn't on any map, because she'd drawn it there herself, three years ago, in a tavern in a city that had since forgotten it had ever been a capital.

Coral found her there at dusk. He didn't say anything at first. He just stood at the edge of her maps with his hands in his pockets, the way he did when he was auditing something and didn't like the totals.

"You've been standing there for ten minutes," she said. "Say it or don't."

"The anchor has to be held by someone who remembers the country," he said. "And there's exactly one of those."

"There's the god," Ilex said. "Behind the door."

"The god's not on this side of the door."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

The wind came up the ridge from the east, from the direction of the Silent Order, and it smelled of woodsmoke and cold iron and something else underneath — a smell Ilex had learned to dread, the smell of places being forgotten. Like a room after the furniture's been carried out. Like a name after the last person who knew it has died.

Coral crouched and looked at the maps properly, the way he looked at ledgers, and she watched him arrive at the same column of figures she'd arrived at an hour ago. He was good at that, Coral. He never flinched from a number. He just made you watch him read it.

"How long can you hold it?" he asked.

"Against an army of the forgotten, with a tattoo and a compass and a great deal of stubbornness?" She shrugged. "Long enough. That's the honest answer. Long enough."

"Long enough for what?"

"For you to open the door. For the god to come through. For everyone on this side of it to be somewhere else when the Order arrives." She tapped the fold in the ridge. "The route holds as long as the anchor holds. The anchor holds as long as someone with Vessia in their blood is standing on it, remembering. I'm the only Vessian left who remembers all of it. So."

"So," Coral said.

He didn't argue. That was the thing about Coral that had made her trust him in the first place, back when he was just a name on a map that shouldn't have existed and she was just a disgrace with ink under her skin: he didn't argue with true things. He'd sit with them until they stopped being bearable, and then he'd sit with them a while longer, and then he'd do what needed doing.

She'd have liked him to argue, just this once. She'd have liked it very much.

"You could draw the route into someone else," he said finally. "Pip. Pip could—"

"Pip is twelve years old and the Order would erase that child before supper." She rolled the maps closed with more force than the paper deserved. "Don't you dare put Pip in your mouth as an alternative. If you want to argue with me, argue with me. Don't hide behind a child."

"I'm not arguing. I'm grieving in advance. It's different."

That startled a laugh out of her — short, ugly, real. "Gods. You're the worst."

"I've been told."

* * *

She said her goodbyes at the fire, and she said them badly, which she'd decided in advance was the correct method. Sable had wanted a ceremony. Pip had wanted a promise. Coral had wanted, she suspected, a miracle, and she couldn't draw him one.

Sable came first, because Sable always organized her grief into an agenda. She sat down across the fire with her bandaged hand — the Order had taken two fingers in a cellar in Vessia's forgotten capital, and Sable had never once mentioned it since — and she said, "I've written to every contact I have left. If you hold the ridge, there will be people looking for you after. Cartographers. Smugglers. One extremely persistent nun."

"I don't need a rescue plan," Ilex said. "I need you to stop planning one, because it makes you stupid, and I need you sharp tomorrow."

"I'm always sharp."

"You cried into the stew last night."

"I seasoned the stew," Sable said with terrible dignity, and Ilex laughed again, and hated how easy Sable could still make it, and hated more that she'd only had this — the jokes, the fire, the found, stupid family of it — for a few weeks. A lifetime of being the woman who remembered a deleted country, and the universe had waited until the last possible minute to give her people worth remembering it with.

Sable reached across the fire and took her hand, the bandaged one, careful of the missing fingers. "Come back," she said. No agenda. Just that.

"I'll come back or I won't," Ilex said. "But I'll be honest about which, and that's more than most people get."

"That's a horrible goodbye."

"I know. I'm bad at them. I've had no practice. Everyone I ever said a proper goodbye to got erased."

Pip came next, and Pip did not organize anything. Pip just sat down next to her and leaned against her arm, all sharp elbows and unwashed hair, and said nothing for a long time, which from Pip was louder than a sermon.

"You're going to do the thing," Pip said at last. "The thing where you stay somewhere dangerous so we can go somewhere safe."

"Yes."

"And you're going to say it's fine."

"Yes."

"And then Coral's going to do the thing where he trades away a piece of himself to fix it, and then I'm going to be left holding a ledger with both your names in it and nobody to read it to."

Ilex looked down at the kid. Pip was watching the fire with the flat, old expression children get when the world has made them accountants of loss too early.

"Listen to me," Ilex said. "I tattooed a country onto my body so it wouldn't be forgotten. Do you know why?"

"Because you're mental."

"Because forgetting is the only death that's real. Everything else, you can walk back from. So here's what you're going to do, and I'm only saying it once, so pay attention." She turned Pip's chin toward her with two fingers. "You're going to keep your books. You're going to write everything down — the stupid things, the small things, what Sable puts in the stew, what Coral does with his pen when he's nervous. Because a record is an anchor too, Pip. A record is a map of a person. And if I don't come back, you're going to draw me one, and you're going to make it accurate, and you're going to make it unflattering, because flattering maps are how countries get lost."

Pip's eyes were wet and furious. "That's a horrible goodbye too."

"I told you. No practice."

Pip headbutted her shoulder — an affection, in Pip's currency — and stalked off to the far side of the fire to sulk with tremendous commitment, and Ilex watched the small hunched shape of the child and thought: *that one's going to be all right. That one's going to be more than all right.*

Coral came last. Of course he came last. He waited until the fire had burned down to coals and the others had gone to their bedrolls, and then he sat beside her on her bedroll's edge with his ledger across his knees, unopened, which for Coral was the equivalent of wringing his hands.

"I've been doing the arithmetic," he said.

"I know. I watched you do it. You do it with your jaw."

"The anchor needs someone who remembers Vessia. But it doesn't need all of Vessia remembered. It needs enough." He turned the ledger around. On the open page, in his small, precise hand, was a list. "I've been writing down everything you've told us. The route. The fold in the ridge. The bearings. The names of the rivers. If I stand at the anchor with this—"

"No."

"You haven't heard the—"

"I heard it. I heard it the moment you sat down." She took the ledger out of his hands and closed it. "Coral. You are the largest account in the largest ledger in Vessia. You are the debt this whole thing turns on. Tomorrow you're going to stand in front of a door that opens for a surrendered name, and you're going to need every gram of yourself you have left — every memory, every scar, every stupid childhood thing you sold to the guild when you were starving, all of it, because that's what the lock eats. You don't get to spend a drop of yourself holding a ridge for me. I won't carry that. I'd rather the route fail."

"The route failing means the Order takes the door."

"The route isn't going to fail." She tapped her own forearm, where the ink of an old coastline curled under her skin like a sleeping snake. "I've been erased from three maps and two memories, Coral. I'm the most difficult woman in this country to forget, because I'm the only one carrying the whole country around under her skin. An army of the forgotten is going to walk up that ridge and find a woman who remembers everything they've lost, and it's going to be like walking into weather. I will hold. I am very, very good at holding."

He was quiet for a while. The coals ticked. Somewhere down in the hollow, the Door hummed its low thornwood note, faint and constant, like a god breathing in a cellar.

"When this is over," he said, "if it's over and we're both—"

"When this is over," Ilex said, "you're going to buy terrible wine and complain about it, and Sable's going to pretend she isn't in love with you, and Pip's going to read us their ledger out loud, and I'm going to draw the first honest map Vessia's had in a hundred years, and you're going to stand behind me and point at the coast and say *I've been there* in that voice you use, and I'm going to say *no you haven't, it's erased, you absolute clerk,* and it's going to be a very good evening. That's what's over when this is over. That's the survey I'm running. Everything else is just terrain."

"That's not a goodbye either."

"I'm consistent."

He laughed — a rough, cracked sound, like a door that hadn't opened in years — and then he did the thing she'd been afraid he'd do and grateful he'd do, which was lean forward and press his forehead against hers, brief and hard, clerk to cartographer, debtor to the woman holding the map.

"Long enough," he said.

"Long enough," she agreed. "Now go to sleep. You have a god to free in the morning."

* * *

She climbed to the anchor point an hour before dawn, alone, with her compass and her knife and the country written on her body.

The fold in the rock was exactly where she'd drawn it, because she'd drawn it well. She wedged herself into the stone at the junction point — the place where the ridge's true bearing crossed the ghost of Vessia's eastern border, the place where a map that no longer existed still insisted on itself — and she opened her sleeves to the cold, and she began.

It wasn't magic, exactly. Or it was, but the honest kind: the magic of attention. She put her hand flat on the stone and she remembered, deliberately, in order, the way she'd taught herself over ten disgraced years. The coast first — the grey water at Sallow Bay, the smell of the fish markets, the particular way the fog came off the sea in November like a debt collector. Then the rivers, each with its traded name. Then the roads, the towns, the shrine at Marrow's Cross where her mother had prayed, the school where she'd learned to draw a true line. Then the capital, the tavern, the night she'd first understood that maps could be murdered and had sworn, drunk and furious, that she would be the country's last living witness.

The stone warmed under her palm. The route held. She could feel it the way you feel a held note — the ridge behind her singing faintly with bearing, the fold in the rock staying folded, the mile of path between her and the hollow staying *real* in a country that specialized in unreality.

The sun came up. The Order's fires, visible now from the ridge, began to break camp.

She watched them come for most of the morning. They moved the way the Silent Order did everything — without haste, without noise, a grey tide of the erased flowing up the valley with High Warden Casserly's banner at its center. Behind them, where they walked, the grass forgot itself in patches. She could see it even at this distance: little circles of grey where the world couldn't quite remember being a valley.

They'd reach the base of the ridge by midday. They'd reach her by late afternoon, if they found the fold, and they'd find the fold, because Casserly was thorough. She'd read his file — Sable's guild kept files on everyone — and thorough was the least of it. He was a man who believed, and believing men were the only ones who scared her.

So she settled in. She checked her compass. She ate the terrible travel bread Sable had packed, and found, annoyingly, that Sable had hidden a twist of dried fruit in the bottom, and that her eyes did something embarrassing about it, and that there was nobody up on the ridge to see.

She thought about the good evening she'd promised Coral. Terrible wine. The honest map. *I've been there.* She laid the memory out on the stone in front of her like a chart, and she anchored herself to it along with the coast and the rivers and the shrine at Marrow's Cross, because a person was just a country you carried, and she intended to be surveyed to the last inch.

By noon she could hear them. Not the army — the army was silent, that was the horror of it, thousands of feet making no more sound than snowfall — but the thing that traveled with them: a low, grinding hush, the sound of the world being asked to forget. The grey patches were climbing the ridge now. The grass at the fold's mouth went pale, then pale, then nothing, a blankness her eyes slid off like water off wax.

Ilex pressed her hand harder against the stone and began the coast again, from the beginning, louder this time, in the privacy of her own skull.

*Sallow Bay. Grey water. Fog in November like a debt collector.*

The blankness paused at the edge of the fold — and held, and could not come further, because a woman with a whole country under her skin was sitting in the stone and refusing, line by line, river by river, to let it go.

She smiled, alone on the ridge, with the army of the forgotten a mile below and the Door of Unmaking humming at her back.

"Long enough," she told it. "I only have to be long enough."

And far below, faint on the wind, she heard the thornwood note change pitch — the Door, beginning, at last, to open.
