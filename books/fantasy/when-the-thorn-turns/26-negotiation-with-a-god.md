## 26. Negotiation with a God

The Door of Unmaking was not what Coral had expected, though he could not have said what he had expected instead. Something grander. Something crueler. Not this: a door of black thornwood set into a cliff face above a grey sea, ordinary in its proportions, the kind of door you might find at the back of any counting-house in Vessia — if counting-houses grew out of living rock and hummed like a struck bell that never finished ringing.

He stood before it alone.

Sable was below, at the base of the cliff, doing what she did best: turning chaos into columns of figures. The debtors' revolt had followed them here — of course it had; misery is loyal — and she had spent the last day organizing the frightened into lines, the lines into watches, the watches into something that resembled hope with rations. Pip was with her, ostensibly helping, actually eating. And Ilex was on the ridge behind them, being a map, bleeding quietly from seven places where her tattoos had opened like seams so that the country around them would hold its shape long enough to matter.

That left Coral and the Door, and whatever waited on the other side of the thornwood, listening.

"You can stop pretending," he said. "I know you're awake."

The humming changed. It was subtle — a quarter-tone shift, the way a room changes when someone opens their eyes in it — but Coral had spent eleven years learning every silence his god could produce. He knew this one. It was the silence of attention.

"Eleven years," he said. "You went quiet mid-oath. Do you have any idea what that does to a contract? I've been in breach for a decade. Technically. Spiritually. There's no arbitration board for it. I checked."

The thornwood did not answer in words. It never began in words. Instead the door *showed* him — the way a ledger shows, ink surfacing without a hand to write it. Images rose in the grain of the wood like bruises coming up under skin:

A cage made of arithmetic. Columns of numbers stretching away in every direction, each column a chain, each chain a prayer priced and collected and filed. A god folded up inside the mathematics, pressed small, pressed patient. And threaded through it all — Coral saw this clearly, saw it with a sick lurch of recognition — the particular hand of the Gilded Thorn's founding ledgers. The same flourishes. The same double-entry discipline. Sable's handwriting, and Sable's grandmother's, and hers before that, all the way back a hundred years.

"I know," Coral said quietly. "I read the books. They bleed now, did you know that? Every entry names a prayer answered and a price taken. Your prison keeps its own accounts." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and cracked as an old seal. "I used to think I was keeping ledgers. Turns out the ledgers were keeping me."

The thornwood warmed beneath his palm. Not comfort — he was past mistaking it for that. It was the warmth of a thing gathering itself to speak plainly, which, in Coral's experience, was always worse than speaking beautifully.

*CORAL VANE.*

The voice came through the wood and through his bones and through eleven years of unanswered prayers, and it was exactly the voice he remembered, which was the cruelest part. Low. Thorned. Patient as interest accruing.

"You have no idea," Coral said, "how long I've rehearsed this conversation. Eleven years. I had opening remarks. I had grievances, itemized. I was going to be very professional about it." His voice betrayed him on the last word, fraying. "And now you're here and all I want to know is — why. Why you stopped. Why you let me stand at that altar mid-oath and say your name into nothing."

*THE LEDGERS CLOSED AROUND ME MID-SENTENCE,* the god said. *I WAS ANSWERING YOU WHEN THEY TOOK ME. YOU WERE THE LAST PRAYER I TOUCHED.*

Coral closed his eyes. "So it wasn't abandonment."

*I DO NOT ABANDON,* said Vessel-of-Thorns. *IT IS NOT IN MY NATURE. THORNS DO NOT LET GO. THEY ONLY HOLD ON TOO TIGHTLY.*

"That's not comforting. That's practically a confession."

*YES.*

Below, faintly, Pip's laugh carried up the cliff face — bright and unbroken, the laugh of a child who had been forgotten by a neighbor and fed by a miracle and had decided, in the way of children, to keep both wounds and feasts. Coral looked down at the small figure hauling water, and then at the ridge where Ilex sat holding a country together with her skin, and felt the full weight of what he had come here carrying.

"Tell me the terms," he said. "You didn't bring me across unmapped country and put me in front of this door out of sentiment. Gods don't do sentiment. They do contracts. So — terms."

The thornwood darkened. Ink moved in the grain, and this time it formed words, actual words, rising in the wood like entries surfacing in a bleeding page:

*THE LOCK IS THE LEDGER. THE LEDGER IS THE DEBT. THE DEBT HAS ONE ACCOUNT LARGER THAN ALL THE OTHERS COMBINED.*

"I know whose account that is." Coral's mouth was dry. "I've audited it. Every year, on the anniversary of my oath, I pull my own file and reconcile it, because I'm thorough, because it's the only conversation we still had. My childhood, sold to the guild at nine years old. My memories of my mother, my father, the village, the fever, the road. Eleven years of mercenary work held as collateral. Three years of joy traded last month to buy Sable out of Casserly's custody." He recited it the way he recited anything: flatly, precisely, the numbers doing the grieving so he didn't have to. "It's a large account. I'm aware."

*IT IS NOT LARGE,* said the god. *IT IS TOTAL. THERE IS NOTHING LEFT IN THAT ACCOUNT BUT THE NAME ITSELF. EVERYTHING ELSE HAS ALREADY BEEN SOLD OR TRADED OR SPENT. CORAL VANE — THE CHILD, THE SON, THE MERCENARY, THE MAN WHO LOVES THEM AND CANNOT ALWAYS REMEMBER WHY HE BEGAN TO — ALL OF THAT IS ALREADY THE GUILD'S, ALREADY THE LOCK'S. ONLY THE NAME REMAINS UNSURRENDERED.*

"And the name is the key."

*THE NAME IS THE ACCOUNT. AN ACCOUNT CAN ONLY BE CLOSED BY ITS HOLDER, FREELY. THAT IS THE ONE LAW EVEN THE LEDGERS OBEY — THE LAW OF FREE SURRENDER. NO ONE COULD TAKE YOUR NAME FROM YOU. NO GOD COULD SEIZE IT, NO WARDEN CONFISCATE IT, NO GUILD FORECLOSE ON IT. IT CAN ONLY BE GIVEN.* A pause, and the pause had weight, had texture, had almost — almost — gentleness. *THAT IS WHY I COULD NOT ASK YOU BEFORE NOW. THAT IS WHY I HAVE BEEN SILENT FOR ELEVEN YEARS RATHER THAN WHISPER THIS INTO YOUR EAR WHILE YOU SLEPT. A NAME GIVEN UNDER PRESSURE IS NOT FREELY SURRENDERED. THE LOCK WOULD KNOW. THE LOCK WOULD NOT TURN.*

"So everything hinges on me choosing it." Coral stared at the door. "Not being forced. Not tricked. Choosing."

*YES.*

"And if I refuse?"

*THEN THE DOOR STAYS SHUT. I REMAIN IN THE ARITHMETIC. THE ORDER BURNS THIS CLIFF BY NIGHTFALL AND EVERYONE YOU LOVE DIES WITHIN THE WEEK, AND THE LEDGERS GO ON COLLECTING PRAYERS UNTIL THE END OF VESSIA, WHICH, AS YOUR CARTOGRAPHER COULD TELL YOU, MAY NOT BE VERY LONG.*

"There it is," Coral said. "There's the fine print. You know, most gods at least try to make coercion sound like destiny."

*I AM NOT TRYING TO MAKE IT SOUND LIKE ANYTHING. I AM TELLING YOU THE TRUTH, WHICH IS WHAT I OWE YOU AFTER ELEVEN YEARS OF SILENCE. HERE IS MORE TRUTH:* The ink shifted, rearranged, became plainer. *IF YOU REFUSE, NO ONE WILL BLAME YOU. I WILL NOT BLAME YOU. A DEBTOR WHO PAYS EVERYTHING EXCEPT WHAT CANNOT BE SPARED HAS PAID HONORABLY. I RELEASED YOU FROM YOUR OATH THE MOMENT THEY CAGED ME. YOU ARE FREE TO WALK AWAY FROM THIS DOOR, CORAL VANE. THAT IS ALSO PART OF THE CONTRACT.*

Coral laughed again — really laughed this time, a short, disbelieving bark that startled gulls off the cliff below. "You're giving me an exit clause."

*A GOD OF THORNS LEARNED PATIENCE IN A CAGE MADE OF DOUBLE-ENTRY BOOKKEEPING. I LEARNED OTHER THINGS TOO. I LEARNED THAT EVERY CONTRACT IN THAT GUILD WAS WRITTEN SO THE DEBTOR COULD NEVER WIN. I WILL NOT BE A PARTY TO SUCH A DOCUMENT. IF YOU GIVE ME YOUR NAME, IT WILL BE BECAUSE YOU CHOSE TO, WITH YOUR EYES OPEN, STANDING IN FULL KNOWLEDGE OF THE PRICE. OTHERWISE KEEP IT. I WOULD RATHER ROT IN THEIR MATHEMATICS THAN BE FREED BY ANOTHER UNFAIR ENTRY.*

The wind came off the sea and moved through the thorns of the door, and they rattled — softly, softly — like a quill being shaken dry.

Coral sat down. Right there, cross-legged on the cold stone before the threshold, because this was going to take as long as it took, and he had learned long ago that important negotiations go better seated.

"Let's audit it," he said. "All of it. What happens when the name goes."

*YOU WILL BE FORGOTTEN,* the god said, without flinching, because gods of thorns do not flinch. *NOT FADED. NOT DIMMED. ERASED, AS VESSIA WAS ERASED. THE LEDGER DOES NOT ACCEPT PARTIAL COLLATERAL ON A FINAL ENTRY. EVERY MEMORY OF YOU IN EVERY MIND THAT HOLDS ONE WILL CLOSE. SABLE WILL NOT KNOW YOUR FACE. PIP WILL NOT KNOW YOUR VOICE. ILEX WILL FORGET THE ROAD YOU WALKED TOGETHER. YOU WILL REMAIN — FLESH, SKILL, HABIT, HANDS — BUT WITHOUT NAME OR HISTORY, A STRANGER TO EVERYONE, INCLUDING, IN TIME, TO YOURSELF.*

"And you? Will you forget me?"

The humming stopped entirely. For three heartbeats the Door of Unmaking was only wood, only black thorns, only a dead thing in a cliff. Then:

*I CANNOT PROMISE,* Vessel-of-Thorns said at last, and the honesty of it landed harder than any assurance would have. *MY MEMORY IS HELD IN THE SAME LEDGERS. WHEN THEY BURN BLANK, WHAT SURVIVES IN ME OF YOU WILL BE... UNCERTAIN. PERHAPS NOTHING. PERHAPS ONLY THE SHAPE OF THE LAST PRAYER YOU EVER SPOKE AT MY ALTAR, WHICH I HAVE CARRIED ELEVEN YEARS THROUGH THEIR ARITHMETIC LIKE A COAL I REFUSED TO DROP. I TELL YOU THIS SO THAT YOU CHOOSE WITH CLEAN BOOKS, CORAL VANE. I MAY NOT REMEMBER YOU EITHER.*

Coral looked at his hands. Clerk's hands, calloused wrong for a swordsman, right for a pen. He thought about how much of him was already gone — sold at nine, traded at twenty-six, spent at thirty-one. He had been liquidating himself his whole life, entry by entry, and had called it survival, and it had been, and there was no shame in it, and there was also no more of it left to do.

Except one line item.

"Sable will rebuild the guild," he said. It wasn't a question. He was thinking aloud now, doing what he always did with a difficult account — walking it line by line until it confessed. "She'll turn it honest. She's already started. Pip will grow up with food and letters and no debts. Ilex gets her maps back, her country back, her name back. The erased get their Door open both ways. All of that is on the other side of this lock." He looked up. "And none of it requires me to be in the picture afterward. That's the audit result, isn't it? I'm not load-bearing. I'm just the one holding the key."

*THAT IS THE CRUELEST TRUTH I HAVE TO OFFER YOU,* said Vessel-of-Thorns. *AND I OFFER IT BECAUSE YOU DESERVE CRUELTY DONE HONESTLY RATHER THAN KINDNESS DONE FALSELY. YES. THE WORLD YOU WANT DOES NOT NEED CORAL VANE IN IT. IT ONLY NEEDS SOMEONE TO PAY FOR IT. AND THE ONLY SOMEONE WHO CAN PAY IS THE ONE WHO OWES THE MOST — BECAUSE THE LOCK WAS BUILT OUT OF THE LARGEST DEBTS, AND ONLY THE LARGEST DEBT CAN UNMAKE IT.*

"My whole life," Coral said slowly, "has been a lesson in what things cost. The guild taught me prices before it taught me letters. I learned early that everything has one, and that the people who pretend otherwise are the ones charging the most." He got to his feet. His knees cracked. Thirty-four years old and his knees cracked like the guildhouse stairs. "So I appreciate that you didn't pretend otherwise. I appreciate the exit clause. I appreciate that you let me sit down and think, even though the Order is probably forming ranks on the ridge right now."

*THEY ARE,* the god admitted. *ABOUT FOUR THOUSAND STRONG. YOUR CARTOGRAPHER IS HOLDING THE RIDGE. SHE IS BLEEDING CONSIDERABLY.*

"Then I won't waste more time." Coral stepped closer to the door, close enough that the thorns nearly touched his chest, close enough to feel the heat of the imprisoned god like a forge behind a wall. "One condition."

*NAME IT.*

"Not for me. For them." He kept his voice level. "When the ledgers burn blank — every debt in them burns too. Every name held, every memory collateralized, every child indentured. Pip's file. The two hundred others like Pip. All of it ash. I'm not buying your freedom with my name while the guild's books survive to enslave anyone else. Total surrender or no deal."

The thornwood shuddered. And then, for the first time in eleven years, Coral heard his god laugh — a low, rough sound, like wind through brambles, like something fierce waking up.

*THERE HE IS,* Vessel-of-Thorns said. *THERE IS THE MERCENARY WHO KEPT MY OATH WHEN I COULD NOT KEEP MY HALF OF IT. YES. IT IS ALREADY WRITTEN. THE BURNING OF THE LOCK BURNS EVERY ACCOUNT IN IT. I SWORE IT TO MYSELF IN THE DARK YEARS, AND I RE-SWEAR IT TO YOU NOW. NO ONE ELSE PAYS. NO ONE ELSE IS HELD. YOUR NAME BUYS EVERY DEBTOR IN VESSIA THEIR FREEDOM ALONGSIDE MINE.*

"Good." Coral nodded once. Business concluded. Terms agreed. And now there was only the strange lightness, the terrible lightness, of a man who has reconciled his final account and found it balances.

He turned to go down to them. To see them one last time while he was still anyone.

"Coral Vane," the god said — and it was not the ledger-voice now, not the contract-voice, but something older and quieter underneath, something that sounded almost like a man kneeling. *I WILL TELL YOU WHAT I TOLD NO ONE IN ELEVEN YEARS. THE LAST WORDS I SPOKE BEFORE THEY CAGED ME WERE THE END OF YOUR OATH. I WAS SAYING:* and the thornwood rang like a cathedral bell: ***—AND THIS I SWEAR UNTO CORAL, WHO KEEPS MY THORNS: HE SHALL NEVER WALK ALONE.***

Coral stood very still.

"You were saying my name," he managed. "When they took you. I was the last thing—"

*YOU WERE NOT ABANDONED MID-OATH,* said Vessel-of-Thorns. *YOU WERE THE OATH. ELEVEN YEARS, EVERY LEDGER, EVERY PRICE — AND STILL I HAVE NEVER BROKEN MY WORD TO YOU. I SIMPLY HAVE NOT YET BEEN ABLE TO FINISH THE SENTENCE.*

Coral wiped his face with the heel of his hand, briskly, the way clerks correct errors.

"Then finish it," he said. "Tomorrow. After I sign."

He went down the cliff path toward the fires and the figures and the small bright laugh, moving quickly, because he wanted to arrive while he still had a name for them to say — and behind him the Door of Unmaking began, very softly, to glow through its thorns like a coal refusing, at last, to be dropped.

And far up the ridge, Ilex felt the anchor-points in her skin flare white-hot, one after another, as four thousand pairs of boots began to move.
