## 27. Pip Runs Away

Pip had stolen before. That was the whole of Pip's résumé, if you wanted to be honest about it—stolen bread, stolen coal, a stolen name that the Gilded Thorn had bought off a dead girl's mother for three coppers and never told anyone it was secondhand. Stealing was easy. Stealing was just walking out with something nobody was watching hard enough.

Walking *out* was the hard part.

Pip stood in the shadow of the Thorn's ruined eastern wing and watched the fire-black rafters breathe smoke into the gray morning. The guild had burned two nights ago, and the smell still hung over everything like a mourner who wouldn't leave. Sable was inside what remained of the ledger hall, arguing with maps. Ilex was at the window, pressing her fingers to her own tattooed arm, checking the route the way other people checked a pulse. And Coral—

Coral was sharpening his knife by candlelight even though it was morning, because that was what Coral did when he was afraid. He'd been doing it for three days. The blade was sharp enough to shave light off itself.

He was going to give his name away.

Pip had heard him say it to Sable through a door not built for keeping secrets, in the flat voice Coral used for numbers, which was how everyone at the Thorn knew he meant something more than numbers. *It has to be the largest account. It has to be freely given.* As if he were reading an entry aloud. As if he were auditing his own life and finding the sum acceptable.

Pip pressed both hands flat against their chest, over the place where the ledger-debt sat like a swallowed stone. Eleven years old, sold to the Thorn for a debt their father had run up on bottled courage, and nobody had ever asked Pip whether they agreed to the terms. Nobody had ever asked Coral either, probably. That was the thing about ledgers. They only ever recorded what was owed. Never what it cost.

*No,* Pip thought. *Not this one.*

They went back inside for supplies, because running away done properly was just a supply problem, and supply problems were solvable. Dried plums from the cellar stores—taken, not stolen; the distinction mattered to Pip more than anyone would ever know. A water skin. The stub of pencil they kept behind their ear and the half-burned page they kept against their heart, the one where Pip had started writing down everything about Coral Vane: *left-handed except when nervous. Hums when he audits. Says "hm" twice when a number is wrong. Once gave me his coat in the rain and said it was surplus inventory.*

If Coral was going to erase himself, then somebody had to keep the records. Pip had understood that since chapter one of this disaster, so to speak. But keeping records wasn't enough anymore. Records of a man weren't a man. Pip wanted the original document back.

And there was one person alive who might have the power to stop all of this without anyone erasing anything.

The High Warden. Casserly. The man with the cold voice and the army of the forgotten, camped three days east with enough Silent Order blades to make the horizon look diseased. Everyone said Casserly was the enemy. Everyone said the Order would kill them all and unmake the Door and end miracles forever, hallelujah, pass the dried plums.

But Pip had listened carefully—Pip always listened carefully; small people survive by listening—and here was what everyone missed: Casserly hadn't killed Sable when he had her. He'd cut her fingers, yes, and Pip had thrown up behind a barrel after seeing the bandages, but she'd come back *alive*, with her name intact and her memories mostly where she'd left them. The Order took prisoners. The Order made *offers*. Absolution, Sable called it, spitting. Betrayal-for-mercy.

Which meant Casserly dealt in bargains.

And nobody in the whole history of the Gilded Thorn had ever out-bargained a child debtor who had nothing left to lose.

Pip wrote a note. Three drafts, because the first was too apologetic and the second was too honest. The third said:

*Gone to fix it. Don't follow. I'm better at surviving than any of you think. — P.*

Then Pip crossed out *fix it* and wrote *do something about it*, because "fix" implied things were broken in a way that could be mended, and Pip was starting to suspect nothing broken in Vessia got mended cheaply.

They left the note under Coral's whetstone, weighted with a copper coin. Payment, technically, for the coat.

* * *

Getting out of the city was easier than Pip expected, which should have been the first warning. The streets around the Thorn were empty in the wrong way—not abandoned but *attentive*, as if the buildings themselves had turned to watch. Since the prayers had started being answered again, Vessia had developed a listening quality. Shrines flared at odd hours. Fountains ran backward. A baker on Tanner's Row had prayed for quiet and gotten it so thoroughly that no sound had come out of his shop for six days, and now people crossed the street rather than walk past the silence leaking under his door.

Pip prayed for nothing. Pip kept their head down and their wants folded up small, like contraband.

By noon they were past the last shrine-road and onto the moor road east, and by dusk the city was a smudge of woodsmoke behind them and the land had gone strange in the way Ilex had promised strange would go. No map held this country anymore. The hills rearranged themselves when you weren't looking. Pip passed the same leaning milestone four times before understanding dawned: it wasn't the road repeating. It was the road *refusing*. This stretch of world didn't want to be traveled toward the east, toward the Door, toward the ending of everything, and it was politely, persistently suggesting Pip turn around.

"No," Pip told the milestone.

The milestone leaned a little further, like a skeptical eyebrow.

"I'm eleven," Pip informed it, "and I've survived the Gilded Thorn, a fire, the Silent Order, and my father. You'll have to try harder than geometry."

Whether the road relented or simply got bored, the fifth time Pip walked east the milestone didn't reappear. Pip counted paces instead, the way Coral counted entries—one, two, three, each number a small brick laid down against the dark. Numbers didn't care if the world was unmapped. Numbers worked anywhere. That was the whole point of them.

Pip slept in a shepherd's bothy that may or may not have existed—the stones felt real, and the wool scraps in the corner smelled of real sheep, and Pip decided that was good enough. In the morning there was bread on the windowsill. Warm bread. Fresh bread, with butter already melting into it.

Pip stared at it for a long moment.

Someone had prayed near here. Someone's prayer had been answered, and the price had been paid by someone else, somewhere, invisibly—that was the new arithmetic of Vessia, grace on one side of the ledger and blood on the other, and you never got to see which column your breakfast came from. Pip thought of the neighbor who'd forgotten them after the feast, the way the woman's eyes had slid off Pip like rain off oilcloth. *Who are you? Have we met? No. I don't think so. Goodbye.*

Pip ate the bread anyway. Wasting answered prayers seemed worse than eating them.

*Don't pray,* Pip reminded themself, walking. *Whatever happens. Don't pray. Not even a little. Not even "please let this work."*

Because that was the trap, wasn't it? That was the beautiful horrible engine under everything. You couldn't even ask for help without spending someone else.

* * *

On the second day, Pip found the first forgotten one.

She was sitting by the roadside on a flat stone, very straight-backed, hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a coach that had stopped coming. She wore the remains of fine clothes—a dress that had once been dove-gray silk and was now more hole than silk, though it stayed eerily clean, as if dirt couldn't decide whether she existed either. Her face was pleasant. Forgettable. That was the horror of it: Pip looked directly at her and felt the details sliding away, the color of her eyes refusing to stick in memory, the shape of her chin smoothing over like water closing behind a stone.

"You can see me," the woman said. Not surprised. Wistful.

"You're sitting in the middle of the road," Pip said. "Hard to miss."

"Oh, people miss me constantly." She smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing Pip had ever seen, and Pip had seen Sable's hand bandages. "You must be touched by the ledgers. Or the Door. Something open in you." She tilted her head. "What's your name?"

"Pip."

"Just Pip?"

"Just Pip."

"Mm." The woman looked east, toward the far-off smudge that was the Order's campfires, visible now at night like a fallen constellation. "I had a name. A full one. Husband, two daughters, a house with a blue door." She said it evenly, reciting facts she'd rehearsed until they stopped hurting, or until she'd worn grooves in the hurt deep enough to stand in. "My neighbor prayed for her daughter's fever to break. It broke. Beautiful thing. And I paid for it, apparently. Some account of mine, some balance owed—I don't even remember what. The ledger reached over and subtracted me." She flexed her clean, ghost-clean hands. "My daughters walked past me in the market. My husband asked me to leave our garden. Politely. He thought I was a beggar."

Pip's throat had gone tight. "That's—" There wasn't a word. *Wrong* was too small. *Cruel* was too neat.

"That's bookkeeping," the woman said. "That's all it's ever been. Somebody's sums."

"The High Warden," Pip said slowly. "Casserly. He's collecting you? People like you?"

"He says we're going home." The woman's voice did something complicated. "He says when the Door is unmade, the answering stops, and no one else gets subtracted. He says the erased will be remembered again." She finally turned to look at Pip fully, and for one flickering moment her face resolved—brown eyes, a scar through one eyebrow, ordinary and human and *there*—before slipping away again. "Do you know what the terrible part is, child?"

Pip shook their head.

"I believe him." She smoothed her ruined skirt. "I've marched with the Order for nine days and every word out of that man is true. That's what frightens me. He means every word. And he'll burn down the world doing it, and mean that too."

Pip stood there with the wind pulling at their collar and thought: *I came here to bargain with a man whose promises work on ghosts.*

"Well," Pip said, with a bravado they absolutely did not feel. "Maybe he needs someone to point out the flaws in his arithmetic."

The woman laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and for a moment she was so vividly present that birds startled up from the gorse nearby, as if her existence briefly had weight again.

"Go east, then," she said. "And when you bargain, little Just-Pip—ask him about his sister. Ask him why he never prays."

* * *

The third day, Pip stopped hiding.

There was no point. The Order's pickets had seen Pip coming for miles—you could tell, because the watchers on the ridge didn't pretend not to watch, and because the road ahead had acquired that attentive quality again, except this time it was armed men providing the attention. When the first patrol finally stepped into the road, gray-cloaked and silent, faces wrapped below the eyes in cloth that drank the light, Pip stopped walking and put their hands where everyone could see them.

"I want to talk to the High Warden," Pip announced, before the patrol could decide whether to bother with questions. Voice steady. Mostly steady. Steady enough.

The lead warden looked down. Behind the eye-holes of the mask, something moved—surprise, maybe, or its professional equivalent.

"It's a child," said another.

"It's a debtor," said the first, and Pip felt those words go through them like a probe, some old trained instinct in the Order recognizing the ledger-stain on a soul the way a dog smells fear. "Where's your keeper, debtor?"

"Dead," Pip said. It wasn't true yet. It might be true soon, if Pip failed, and the thought arrived like a nail driven between the ribs. "That's rather the point."

They marched Pip into camp, and the camp was worse than the fires had looked from afar. Tents by the thousand. And among them—moving through the lanes, carrying water, tending cookfires, silent as snowfall—the erased. Hundreds of them. The woman from the road had been one of perhaps forty Pip could see from the ridge alone. Here they were everywhere, a whole population of people the world had declined to keep, and the living soldiers stepped around them with the careful courtesy of men who'd learned these ghosts could carry a blade when needed.

An army of the forgotten. Ilex had warned them. Pip hadn't understood until now that the warning had been underselling it.

At the center of the camp stood a command tent, black banners flanking the entrance, and outside it—reading dispatches by daylight, as calmly as a man reading a menu—stood the High Warden.

Casserly was taller than Pip had pictured and tireder. That was the detail that undid Pip's prepared speech for a moment: the man had shadows under his eyes like bruises, and his gauntlets, immaculate gray steel, were clasped behind his back in the posture of someone holding himself together by main force. He looked up as Pip was shoved forward, and his gaze did a quick, terrible audit—age, build, clothing, the Thorn's faded badge still sewn inside Pip's collar where Pip had forgotten to remove it.

"A runner," Casserly said. His voice was exactly as cold as the reports claimed, and underneath the cold, faintly, something exhausted. "From the Thorn. Alone." He crouched, unhurried, bringing his eyes level with Pip's, which was somehow more frightening than looming. "Children don't cross sixty miles of unmapped country to deliver messages. Children get carried. So either you were sent, which makes you a decoy—or you came yourself, which makes you desperate." A pause. "Desperate children are the most dangerous negotiators alive. They have no floor. Speak."

Pip opened their mouth.

And found, to their absolute horror, that the speech was gone—all those hours of rehearsing on the road, the arguments, the leverage, the clever offer—and what came out instead was small and raw and true:

"Don't let him do it. Don't let Coral give his name away. He's—he's the one who—" Pip's voice cracked and they hated it, hated it, pushed through. "He gives his coat away in the rain and calls it surplus inventory. He hums when he audits. If you unmake the Door, you don't save anybody, you just freeze everything the way it is, with all those people—" Pip jabbed a finger at the silent figures moving between the tents, at the woman with the blue door and the daughters, somewhere in that gray crowd. "With all of them still forgotten! Stopping the answering doesn't *unsubtract* anyone! You need the Door *open* for them to come home, don't you see, that's the only door out of a country that isn't there—"

"Enough." But Casserly hadn't moved. Hadn't blinked. And his eyes had gone, very slightly, to the crowd—to one figure in particular, a broad-shouldered ghost woman laughing at something another forgotten soldier had said, a laugh with no sound in it—and something in the High Warden's jaw shifted like a keystone deciding whether to fall.

"His sister," Pip pressed, reckless, remembering the roadside woman's advice, gambling everything on a stranger's rumor. "Ask him about—"

"*Enough.*"

The word cracked across the tent-line like ice giving way. Two guards flinched. Casserly rose to his full height, and for a long moment he simply looked at Pip, and Pip looked back, eleven years old and trembling and utterly unwilling to sit down first.

"You want a bargain," Casserly said at last, quietly.

"I want Coral Vane to stay real."

"And what," said the High Warden of the Silent Order, in a voice like a closing ledger, "could a child possibly offer me?"

Behind him, out past the black banners, the forgotten army stood among the cookfire smoke, and one broad-shouldered woman had turned, as if she'd heard her brother's anger carry all this distance, and was looking at the tent.

Pip drew a breath, and lied with everything they had:

"More than you think. I've read his ledgers."
