## 28. The Forgotten Army

High Warden Casserly had counted his army three times that morning, and each count came back wrong.

Not fewer, which would have been ordinary—desertions, sickness, the slow attrition of any march through unmapped country where the roads rearranged themselves overnight like a drunkard shifting in sleep. Wrong in the other direction. There were more of them than there should have been.

He stood on the rise above the camp with his ledger open across his forearm—the Order kept its own books, iron-bound and honest—and watched the columns come in out of the fog. They arrived without banners. They arrived without sound, most of them, their boots making no impression in the wet grass. They arrived, and then they were simply *there*, as if they had always been part of the army and Casserly had merely misremembered their absence.

That was how it worked now. That was what answered prayers cost, when the gods had woken hungry after a century of silence: people. Whole people, scraped out of the world's memory like wax from a seal. A woman prayed for her husband to stop drinking, and somewhere a man woke with no wife. A village prayed for rain, and a river town downstream forgot it had ever been founded. The erased did not die. Death left a body, a grave, a grief. Erasure left nothing but a shape in the air where a person used to fit, and the world closed over the shape like water.

And the erased walked.

Casserly had learned that in the first weeks, before he understood anything else. They drifted toward one another the way cold air drifts toward cold air. They could not be remembered by those who loved them, so they gathered into companies of mutual forgetting, an army of the unremembered marching on the last door out of a country that officially was not there.

"Two hundred and eleven new since dusk," said Second Warden Vos, appearing at his elbow with her slate. She was a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and the patience of a stone wall. "I've stopped trying to match faces to parishes. Most of them don't know their own names anymore."

"They don't need names," Casserly said. "They need a wall between the world and that Door."

Vos looked at him. She had served under him for nine years and she permitted herself exactly one skeptical silence per week, and this appeared to be the week's allotment. "And you believe that, High Warden."

It was not a question, so he did not answer it. He closed his ledger. Below, the fires of the camp burned with a strange cleanliness, giving off light but little heat, as if even the flames were half-erased. Somewhere beyond the ridge lay the cliff face and the Door of Unmaking, black thornwood in living rock, humming with the voice of a god that should have stayed silent.

One day's march. Perhaps two, if the country kept misbehaving.

"Hold the line at the eastern pickets," he said. "And Vos—if anyone comes out of the fog alone tonight, bring them to me. Don't let the ranks touch them."

"The ranks don't touch anyone," Vos said. "You've seen them."

He had. He saw it every time he closed his eyes: a sentry reaching to steady a stumbling recruit, and the moment skin met skin, both of them flickering, thinning, like ink held too close to a flame—one man losing his mother's face, the other losing whatever scraps he'd managed to keep. The erased were contagious with absence. Proximity to them wore holes in the remembered.

"I've seen it," Casserly said. "Bring them to me anyway."

* * *

The child came out of the fog two hours past midnight, and she was not erased.

Casserly knew it before the pickets called it—a small figure walking down the old pilgrim road with the particular stubbornness of someone who has decided exactly where she is going and dares the world to disagree. She was perhaps eleven. She wore a guild runner's sash, Gilded Thorn yellow gone gray with road dust, and she walked straight past two sentries as though they were fence posts, because children who grew up in debt-halls learned early which adults were furniture.

They brought her to his tent anyway, mostly because she let them, and because she announced, in a voice pitched to carry, "I'm here to see the High Warden. I have a proposition."

Casserly sat behind his campaign table with the iron ledger open and did not look up for the count of ten, because looking up too fast told people they mattered, and the first lesson of command was rationed attention. When he did look up, the child had already climbed into the chair opposite him, swung her feet, and was reading his ledger upside down with frank professional interest.

"You keep clean books," she said.

"Name," Casserly said.

"Pip." She said it easily, without hesitation, and something in his chest tightened, though he would have cut off his own hand before showing it. Names given freely. Names still owned. This child had all of hers.

"Pip," he repeated. "Do you know what I do to thieves of the ledgers, Pip?"

"Burn them. Or worse—you make everyone forget them first, and *then* burn them, so nobody mourns." She recited it with relish rather than fear. "Ilex says your Order invented that trick about four hundred years ago and has been very proud of itself ever since."

Ilex. The mapmaker. So the rumors were true; the disgraced cartographer had found her way to the Thorn, and her maps—her impossible maps, tattooed into her own flesh by all accounts—had shown them the road. Casserly set down his pen with great care. Around him the tent was quiet except for the wind worrying the guy-lines, and beneath the wind, faint as a pulse under floorboards, the hum of the Door carried even this far. He felt it in his back teeth. He had felt it for days. It sounded, God forgive him, like singing.

"You have a proposition," he said.

"I do." Pip leaned forward, elbows on his table, entirely at home. "Your whole army is made of people somebody prayed away. Right? That's what they are. The leftovers. The price." Her voice did something complicated on the word *price*—a child's voice learning, mid-sentence, the weight of an adult word. "Coral's going to open the Door and give himself away to free the god, and then all the answering stops being a theft and starts being fair, or that's the idea. But if you break the Door instead—" she swallowed, and for the first time she looked her age, a small girl in a large tent at the end of the world— "then none of them ever get fixed. My friend Sable. And the woman from the feast night. And everyone." She thrust out her chin. "So my proposition is: take me instead. Whatever bargain you're offering the god, offer it to me. I'm small. I don't eat much. And I owe the Thorn a lot of money anyway, so really you'd be doing everyone a favor."

For a long moment Casserly said nothing at all.

He had interrogated archivists who wept blood. He had watched Sable Quill's agents lie with such beauty it was nearly liturgy. He had stared down men who traded years of their lives like coin. Nothing had prepared him for a debtor-child offering herself up in trade with the bookkeeping instincts of a guild mistress and the theology of—well. Of a child.

"That is not how bargains work," he said finally.

"It's how *your* bargains work," Pip said. "Everything costs somebody. That's the whole point of you, isn't it? You think if nobody ever prays again, nobody ever gets erased again. Fair enough. Terrible, but fair." She hopped down from the chair. "But you should look at them first. Your army. Up close. Before you decide what they're worth."

"They are worth everything," Casserly said. "That is why I will not let the Door be opened."

"No," Pip said, standing in the tent flap with the fog glowing behind her. "You'll see." And she was gone into the white, and the pickets swore afterward that no child had passed them, though one of them was crying and couldn't say why.

* * *

Casserly did not sleep. At dawn he did something he had not done since the Order gave him the iron badge and the iron book: he walked his own lines.

Not the officers' review, not the inspection with colors flying. He walked among the rank and file of the Forgotten Army as the sun came up red through the fog, and he made himself look.

They were drilling, those who could remember drilling. Others stood in loose companies, facing nothing, wearing the uniforms of regiments that no longer existed from countries that had never existed. A man near the eastern pickets polished a sword over and over with a rag gone black, and when Casserly asked him his name, the man smiled with terrible sweetness and said, "I had one. Someone's using it now, I think. It fits them better."

A woman sat mending a coat that belonged to no one, sewing seams that unraveled as fast as she closed them, and she hummed a lullaby in a language Casserly almost recognized, in the way you almost recognize a face across a market crowd.

He had known, in the abstract. He had built his entire life in the abstract: the gods answer, the answer must be paid, payment is taken from the innocent, therefore prayer itself is violence, therefore the Door must fall and the answering must end forever. He had preached it from a hundred pulpits of cold morning air. He had believed it the way other men believed in the sunrise.

Then he turned a corner between the mess tents and saw the woman sitting alone on a supply crate, holding a wooden soldier.

She was perhaps thirty-five. Gray-shot hair pinned back with a bit of wire, because pins went missing in the fog-country. She wore a plain traveling dress much patched, and she was carving—had carved, was endlessly re-carving—the same small wooden figure, and she held it the way you hold a thing you cannot put down because putting it down means admitting it is all you have.

Casserly stopped walking.

He knew the way she held her hands. He knew the tilt of her head when she was concentrating, chin tucked, lower lip caught in her teeth. He knew that she whittled when she was worried and that she worried constantly and that her whittling had filled their childhood house with a fleet of tiny soldiers, tiny horses, tiny saints with their faces worn smooth by a brother's thumbs.

"Maren," he said.

The name came out of him before doctrine, before discipline, before nine years of the Order's training, which taught that the erased must never be addressed by name because names are hooks and hooks pull wounds open. The woman looked up.

She had his mother's eyes. Of course she did. They had both gotten their mother's eyes, and their father's stubborn jaw, and between them they had once split a single childhood the way siblings split an apple, each keeping half.

"Do I know you?" Maren asked gently.

It was the question he had prepared for. He had rehearsed this meeting for six years—not consciously, never consciously; the Order forbade its wardens from seeking lost kin, and Casserly had obeyed every letter of that law—but his mind had built this room long ago, the way a body builds a scar around a wound it refuses to name. In the rehearsed version, he said: No. And walked on. And the doctrine survived.

"No," he began.

"You look like someone," Maren said. She tilted her head, and the wooden soldier turned in her fingers. "Someone I can't—there was a boy. Wasn't there? There was a boy who used to hide in my room during storms, because he said my whittling sounded brave." She laughed a little, helplessly. "That doesn't make sense. I don't have a brother. I checked. I check every morning. There's just this hole where checking should stop, and I keep—" she held up the soldier— "carving him things."

The fog moved through the camp behind Casserly like a slow tide. Somewhere a sergeant was calling companies to order, and the calls sounded very far away, as if coming from the bottom of a well.

"What happened to you?" Casserly heard himself ask. "Tell me. All of it."

Maren shrugged, and began to talk, and the talking seemed to steady her, the way recitation steadies anyone holding themselves together by threads. "There was a fever. In our town—I can't remember the town, isn't that funny, I remember the kitchen but not the street. Everyone prayed. We prayed for weeks, all of us together, the whole parish, we prayed so hard." Her eyes shone. "And it worked. Oh, it *worked*. The fever broke all at once, like God reached down and wrung it out of us. Everyone lived. Every single person." She frowned. "Almost everyone."

"Who didn't?" His voice was a stranger's voice, level and cold, the interrogation voice, deployed against his own heart.

"That's the strange part. Nobody. Nobody died. But the priest's sermon the next Sunday was about gratitude, and he read the parish rolls, and my name wasn't on them. And my husband looked right through me at breakfast. And the boy—" her hand closed around the wooden soldier until the knuckles whitened— "the boy was just *gone*. Not dead. Gone. Like a tooth. You feel the gap where it was. You keep touching the gap." She looked up at Casserly, and her smile was the bravest thing he had ever seen, braver than any battlefield, and it cracked him down the middle. "Somebody paid for all those lives with me, I think. With us. Isn't that funny? I'm not angry. I just want to know if the boy got to stay."

Casserly knelt.

He did not decide to kneel; his knees decided for him, and suddenly the High Warden of the Silent Order was on the frozen mud of his own parade ground with his sister's eyes level with his own, and every certainty he had marched here on lay in pieces around him like dropped armor.

"He got to stay," Casserly said. "He's here."

Maren searched his face. For one suspended moment something moved behind her eyes—not memory, memory was gone, but the shape memory leaves, the dent in the cushion, the ghost-weight of a held hand. "You *do* look like him," she whispered.

"Maren." His voice failed and rebuilt itself. "In two days—perhaps one—an army will march on a door in a cliff. If the door opens, the ones who were taken come home. All of them. Every name goes back to its owner. Do you understand me? You would be remembered. The boy comes back. Everything comes back."

Her face lit, and it was the worst thing he had ever seen, because he knew what he had to tell her next.

"But the Order is going to destroy it first," he said. "I am going to destroy it. That is why we are here."

The light went out of her face slowly, like a lamp running out of oil, and she did not cry, and she did not curse him, because she did not know who he was, and that was the final cruelty: she received her death sentence from her own brother and could not grieve him, only the sentence.

"Oh," she said softly. She looked down at the wooden soldier in her hands. Then, with the practical courage of a woman who had already lost everything once, she held it out to him. "Will you give this to him? If you find the boy. He liked the ones with chipped noses best. He said perfect soldiers looked like they'd never fought anything."

Casserly took the toy. His hands were shaking. Sixteen years of iron discipline, and his hands were shaking like a novitiate's.

"I'll give it to him myself," he said. "I promise you, Maren. On the iron and on my name. He gets it from my own hand."

She smiled, satisfied, some knot in her loosening, and turned back to her endless mending, and dismissed him the way the forgotten dismiss everyone: kindly, completely, without a trace.

* * *

He found Vos at the command tent, going over the assault plans by lamplight, the approaches to the cliff drawn in careful ink. Casserly stood in the entrance a long time, mud on his knees, a child's whittled soldier in his fist, and Vos—nine years at his side, one skepticism per week—looked up and spent this week's, and next week's, and possibly the year's entire allowance, all at once.

"High Warden?"

"The plan stands," Casserly said. His voice was perfectly steady. He had built it to be. "We march at dawn. We breach the Door. We end the answering forever."

Vos waited. She was good at waiting.

"And afterwards," Casserly said, "when it's done—find out who was taken to pay for it. Every name the erasure ate. Write them in the iron ledger. Not as casualties." He looked down at the little wooden soldier with its chipped nose. "As debts. Ours."

He turned out of the tent into the fog, toward the cliff and the Door and the god humming behind it like a heart that refused to stop, and behind him, in the ranks of the Forgotten Army, ten thousand people he had sworn to save from miracles waited for him to save them from himself.

Dawn was coming. It would find them all there.

The first horn of the assault sounded while the sky was still dark—and it was answered, from beyond the ridge, by another horn, thinner, higher, blown by someone who had absolutely no business being anywhere near a battlefield.

Pip had not gone home.
