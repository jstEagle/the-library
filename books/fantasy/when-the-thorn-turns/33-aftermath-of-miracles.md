## 33. Aftermath of Miracles

The ledgers came back blank.

Not empty—blank. Sable Quill learned the difference in the first week, when she ran her thumb down the spine of the oldest book in the Gilded Thorn and found the leather smooth as a child's palm. Empty meant something had been taken. Blank meant something had been *given*, and she had not yet learned which word she could live with.

She stood in the ledger hall on the morning of the guild's one hundred and first year, in a building that smelled of scorched timber and new plaster, and she counted what remained. The eastern wing was gone. The cellar door was gone—there was a doorway of ordinary pine there now, opening onto an ordinary cellar that smelled of turnips, because the god had stepped through and the thornwood had remembered it was only wood. The debtors' rolls were gone. The prayer-entries were gone. The ink that had bled and written itself was gone.

What remained was Sable, and a guild with no product, and a country learning, day by day, that the miraculous had come back to market.

She had spent thirty years building an economy on silence. She had told herself it was mercy—the gods caged, the prayers unanswered, and therefore *priced*, and therefore fair. A woman could buy back her son's memory instead of losing it to a god's whim. A man could purchase a name instead of having it eaten. Everything in Vessia that the divine might have taken for free, the Thorn had sold at a markup, and Sable had called that civilization.

Then the ledgers had burned blank, and the gods had started answering, and Sable had discovered what her civilization had actually been: a dam. And dams, when they break, do not care how well they were built.

* * *

The first month was the worst.

Across Vessia, shrines flared and did not stop flaring. In Ashen Row a well began to weep clean water for anyone who asked it to. In the Saltmarket, a fishwife's curse landed on a cheating buyer like a thrown stone, and the crowd cheered, and three other people tried curses of their own, and one of them worked, and by evening there was a line outside the fishmonger's of people wanting small cruelties priced and paid.

Sable watched it happen the way she had once watched ledgers: with a pencil, and a cold stomach, and the professional conviction that everything could be accounted for if she was fast enough.

It could not. The gods were answering *everything*. That was the horror of it. Not the miracles—the miracles were, if anything, modest. A lame man's knee unswelled. A fever broke. A widow dreamed her husband's voice saying goodbye, once, clearly, and woke with the dream still warm. No, the horror was the *pricing*. Because the gods, freed, had not become generous. They had become honest. Every prayer was answered at exactly the cost of the answer, and the cost was always the same currency, and the currency was always *you*.

The fishwife's curse cost her the taste of her mother's cooking. The lame man paid the color of his front door, which he would never see again and could not name. The widow's dream cost her the ability to cry, which she said was fair, and which Sable, watching her say it, thought was the saddest sentence she had ever heard.

The guild's old clients came to her in those first weeks like refugees. The weeping woman who had once sold her son's memory for passage money—Maren, her name was, Sable had finally learned it, a month too late for it to matter—came to the Thorn's gutted hall and asked if she could buy the memory back.

"No," Sable said.

Maren's face did the thing faces did now, the flinch of people who had expected the old guild and were still learning there wasn't one. "I can pay. I have—"

"You can't pay for that anymore. Nobody can. It isn't for sale." Sable came around the counter she was building out of salvaged doors and said the rest of it, the sentence she had been rehearsing in the dark for a month and which still came out of her mouth like a bone she'd swallowed sideways: "But if you'll sweep floors, or keep accounts, or carry water for the shrine on Ashen Row, you can earn it back. The god that holds it now doesn't sell. It *restores*. Work for it. That's the whole price. Work, and time, and it gives things back."

Maren stared at her. "That's not how anything works."

"I know," Sable said. "I'm told that's the point."

* * *

She rebuilt the guild as a charity of last resort, because the alternative was to watch people learn the new economy by bleeding.

That was the phrase she used in the charter she wrote herself, in the ledger hall, on the first page of the first new book: *The Gilded Thorn exists to stand between the people of Vessia and the price of their own prayers.* Under it, because she was still Sable Quill and honesty had to start somewhere, she wrote: *Founded in sin. Refounded in debt. Both are ours.*

Pip found her there at midnight, still writing, and stood in the doorway of the hall with a lamp held too high, the way children held lamps, so that the light hit Sable's eyes and the page went shadowed.

"You spelled 'refounded' wrong," Pip said.

"It's a coinage."

"It's a mistake with confidence."

"Most of my life is that." Sable pushed the book across the table. "Read it. All of it. You're going to keep the books now."

Pip's face did something complicated. Pip had been a debtor in the old guild, a child bought against a mother's debt, and Sable had spent a year telling herself that keeping the girl fed and housed was the same as keeping her safe, and the Door of Unmaking had spent one afternoon teaching her the difference. "I don't know how to keep honest books," Pip said. "I know how to keep *your* books."

"Then you know more than you think. Honest books are just the same entries with nothing hidden." Sable rubbed her eyes. Her hands ached. They had ached since the Order's custody, since the fingers she'd bought back at the cost of three of Coral's remembered joys—joins she hadn't known he'd paid until he told her, flat and unaccusing, in the wreckage after Sable Unmade herself to him, and she had never found a way to carry that weight without her hands hurting. "Come at dawn. I'll teach you the parts I know. You'll have to teach me the rest."

"Which parts do you know?"

"How to write a number that doesn't lie." Sable looked at the blank ledgers stacked against the wall, forty years of them, waiting to be pulped or repurposed. "The rest I'm inventing as I go. That's what refounding means."

* * *

The trouble—the real trouble, the trouble that made her stop in the middle of staircases and stand there like a woman who'd forgotten why she'd entered a room—was that the new economy worked.

Gods answered fairly now. Priced transparently. A shrine-keeper in the Saltmarket posted the costs on a board, actual costs, in actual ink, and people read them before they prayed, and some of them prayed anyway and some of them walked away, and both choices were theirs. Vessia had never had that. Vessia had had the Thorn, which had priced the *silence*, and the Silent Order, which had enforced it, and between those two machines the country had gone thirty years without a single honest transaction with the divine.

Sable had built one of those machines.

She was rebuilding the other one now, out of the wreckage, into something with a different name, and some mornings she caught herself checking the new shrine-board prices against the old ledger rates to see if the gods were undercutting her, and she had to put her pencil down and go stand in the courtyard until the shame finished its work.

Ilex would have laughed at her. Ilex was not there to laugh. Ilex was at the map-anchor point at the edge of everything, holding the route open with her body and her ink, and the last letter Sable had from her was four lines long and said, in its entirety: *Still here. Army's gone. Door still open. Send ink, the good kind, and stop writing letters like I'm dying, I'm surveying.* Sable sent ink. She wrote the letters anyway.

And Coral—

Coral was in the ledger hall.

That was the trouble. That was the whole trouble, folded up small enough to fit in a room.

* * *

He had come back eleven days after the Door. Sable had been in the gutted eastern wing, directing men who were pulling charred beams, when a clerk from the Saltmarket shrine came running to say there was a man at the front of the guild asking for work.

She had gone down the stairs at a speed no mistress of the Thorn should have permitted herself, and there he was, standing in the doorway with the light behind him, and her heart had done the thing her heart still did, the lurch, the *there you are*—

And he had looked at her the way you look at a building you're thinking of renting.

"Good morning," he'd said. "I'm told this establishment hires clerks. I'm very good with accounts."

"You—" Sable had said, and then stopped, because the sentence she had was *you died for us, you unmade yourself, you are the reason there is a morning* and none of it was true for him, none of it had survived the Door, he had traded the name and the history and every year of them together and what stood in her hall was a man with his hands and his craft and none of his reasons.

"I'm told you pay fairly," the stranger had said. "That's rare, in Vessia, at present."

"It is," Sable had said, and her voice had held, because thirty years of holding her voice had not burned with the eastern wing. "We pay fairly. We keep honest books. Can you write?"

"I can write," he'd said, "like a man who was born holding a pen."

She had hired him on the spot, because the alternative was to stand in her own doorway weeping in front of a stranger, and she had spent the eleven days since learning the shape of the wound. It was not grief, exactly. Grief would have been simpler. Grief had an object, a grave, a name you could say into the dark. This was grief with the object *present*. This was loving a man across a counter, daily, while he totted columns in a hand she knew better than her own and had no idea why she sometimes watched him the way you watch a fire.

He held his pen wrong. That was the thing that undid her. He held it wrong, pinched too low, a clerk's bad habit, and she had watched him do it a hundred times in the old guild and teased him about it a hundred times, and he did it now, and it meant nothing to him, and it meant everything to her, and there was no one in Vessia she could say that sentence to without sounding mad.

Except Pip. And Pip had gone strange and fierce and secretive since the Door, and Sable had seen her, twice now, watching the new clerk with an expression Sable recognized from mirrors: the expression of a person counting something no one else could see.

* * *

On the first day of the guild's second century, Sable held court in the rebuilt hall and heard the city's accounts.

They came all morning. A man whose prayer for courage had cost him his fear of his own father, and who now could not remember why he'd ever flinched, and wanted to know if the guild could restore the flinching because without it he couldn't remember why he'd left home. A girl who'd prayed for her brother's fever to break, and it had, and the price posted on the shrine-board had been *one shared memory, chosen by the god*, and the god had chosen the day they'd buried their mother, and now the brother remembered the funeral and she didn't, and she wanted to know if that was fair.

"It's fair," Sable said, "and it's terrible, and those are allowed to be the same thing now. That's the whole new arrangement." She wrote the girl's name in the new ledger, in the column marked *Grievances the Guild Cannot Fix*, which was getting long. "What I can do is put you on the restoration rolls. Work for the god's shrines, or for us, and what was taken comes back. Not quickly. But it comes back."

"Does it?" the girl said. "Has anything come back?"

Sable thought of Maren, who had swept the hall for six weeks and woken last Tuesday weeping with joy because she'd dreamed her son learning to walk. She thought of the lame man from the first month, who'd worked the Ashen Row shrine through the winter and now, he wrote, could see the color of his front door again and had painted it a color he hated just to watch himself hate it.

"Yes," Sable said. "It comes back. That's the miracle nobody preaches."

* * *

In the afternoon the hall emptied, and the light came in long and gold through the new windows, and the stranger worked at his desk in the corner with the pen held wrong.

Sable stood at her own desk and watched him over the top of a column of figures and let herself have the four seconds of it she allowed herself daily. The set of his shoulders. The way he mouthed numbers. The small, unconscious gesture—there, he'd just done it—where he touched the page flat with two fingers before signing anything, a superstition he could not have explained because he no longer contained its explanation, and she knew, she *knew*, that it came from the oath, from the old god's contract, from a night eleven years ago when a mercenary had pressed his hand to a page and sworn himself to something that had gone silent—

"You're staring," the stranger said, without looking up.

"I'm auditing."

"Am I out of balance?"

"Constantly," Sable said, and went back to her figures, and the stranger smiled at his column, a small private smile, and she thought: *he was going to ask me to dinner. Before all this. He'd rehearsed it. He'd rehearsed it for a week and I pretended not to notice because I was afraid that if I noticed it I'd have to answer it, and I wasn't ready, and now the question is gone and the man is here and the question will never be asked again because he has never asked it.*

She thought: *I will spend the rest of my life being a stranger's employer.*

She thought: *I built a guild on the trade of impossible things, and the most impossible thing in Vessia is sitting in my hall doing arithmetic, and I cannot buy him back, because the one currency that purchase required, he already spent.*

* * *

Pip came to her at closing, when the stranger had gone out into the blue evening and the hall was theirs.

"I need a shelf," Pip said.

"You have a shelf."

"I need a *locked* shelf. In the back room. Where the old ledgers are." Pip stood with her arms crossed, fourteen years old and immovable, and Sable recognized the posture because she'd invented it. "And I need you not to ask what goes on it."

Sable put down her pen. "Pip."

"You said honest books. You said the whole guild runs on honest books now." Pip's chin came up. "These are the most honest books in Vessia. They're just not *ready* to be read yet."

"By whom?"

Pip looked at her for a long moment, and Sable watched the girl decide—watched her weigh it, this child who had learned accounting in a den of thieves and loyalty in a cellar full of god—and then Pip said, quietly, "By him. When he's ready. Which is not the same as when *you're* ready, so don't ask again."

She walked out. Sable sat alone in the gold light with her heart going like a drum in a parade.

She did not ask again. She was Sable Quill; she had spent thirty years knowing when a ledger was being kept from her, and for the first time in her life she had decided to let it be, because whatever Pip was writing in that locked back room, the girl's face when she wrote it was the face of a person doing the only right thing available to her, and Sable had learned, at the cost of everything, to recognize that face and stand out of its way.

But that night, locking up, she passed the back room and saw, through the door's high window, lamplight, and Pip at a desk, and the stranger's coat hung on a peg where Pip had asked him, casually, days ago, to leave it.

And on the desk, in Pip's careful hand, a new book. Thick already. Its first page headed in letters Sable could read even upside down and backwards through glass, because she had taught this child her own alphabet:

*The Counter-Ledger of Coral Vane.*

Sable stood in the dark hallway with her keys in her hand and did not go in.

She went home instead, through streets where shrine-lamps burned at honest prices, past a fishmonger's board listing what a curse cost, past a widow who could not cry feeding cats who had no idea what they'd been given. And at her own door she stopped, and said, to no one, to the city, to the blank ledgers of a god who had refused to keep what it was owed:

"He earned that name. Give it back."

And somewhere in Vessia, faint as a pen scratching in another room, something wrote the request down.
