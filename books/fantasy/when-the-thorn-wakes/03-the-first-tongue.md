## 3. The First Tongue

Maren did not sleep. That was the first thing Rafael noticed when they came down to the scriptorium at dawn — that Maren was exactly where they had left her eight hours ago, hunched over the child like a scholar over a dying flame, and that the candles around the table had burned down to puddles without her once moving to trim them.

The child sat wrapped in a guild blanket, a cup of untouched barley tea going cold in her small hands. She watched Maren the way a fox watches a snare it hasn't yet stepped in.

"Well?" Rafael said.

Maren held up one finger without looking up. She had a slate beside her and it was covered edge to edge in characters — some of them transcriptions of sounds the child had made, some of them attempts at correspondence, and some of them, Rafael saw with a cold slide in their stomach, crossed out so violently the slate had cracked.

"Maren."

"I'm trying to decide," Maren said finally, "whether to tell you something impossible or something worse." She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely alight. "Sit down, Rafe. Both of you. Sit down and don't touch anything."

Rafael sat. The child — Ilsa, though no one had given her that name yet; she had not been asked — drew the blanket tighter.

"Say it again," Maren said to the girl gently. "The part about the door."

Ilsa looked at Rafael first. That was new too. Last night she had looked through people as if they were weather. Now she studied Rafael's face with an attention that felt less like a child's curiosity and more like an auditor's — someone checking a figure against a column.

Then she spoke.

Rafael had heard it all night from the cot outside her room: that low, layered speech, vowels stacked on vowels, consonants arriving like stones dropped down a well. It should have been noise. It was not noise. Every syllable landed somewhere behind Rafael's sternum and stayed there, warm, like a coin held too long in a fist.

Maren wrote as the girl spoke, fast, her chalk shrieking. When Ilsa finished, Maren set the chalk down with exaggerated care, the way a person sets down a knife they'd rather throw.

"That sentence," Maren said, "contains eleven distinct morphemes I can isolate, and I can't parse nine of them. But here's what I can tell you." She tapped the slate. "The structure isn't agglutinative, isn't inflected, isn't isolating. It doesn't descend from anything. Every language we have — every dead tongue in this guild's cellar, every merchant pidgin, every church litany — descends from maybe four root families. This is none of them. This isn't a branch, Rafe. This is the trunk."

"You're saying it's old."

"I'm saying *old* is the wrong word. Old means it comes before something else. This doesn't come before anything else. Everything else comes before *it*." Maren pushed the slate aside and lowered her voice, which was absurd, because the only other soul in the room was seven years old and barefoot. "In the sealed stacks there's a fragment called the Ceren Gloss. Half a page. We've never been able to date it because the vellum won't burn and the ink predates ink. Three generations of linguists have broken their careers against it. The girl just recited its opening line. From memory. As if she were remembering it, not reading me."

The fire in the scriptorium grate popped. Neither adult moved.

"What does the line mean?" Rafael asked.

Maren's mouth worked twice before words came out. "*Before the counting, the counted.*" She laughed, a short unhappy sound. "Or possibly '*before the debt, the debtor.*' The grammar won't commit. And here's the worst part, Rafe — the truly worst part. Listen to how she says it. Not the meaning. The sound."

Ilsa said it again, patient, as if repeating herself to the slow.

And Rafael heard it — heard what Maren meant. The words didn't pass through the air so much as arrange it. For half a heartbeat the scriptorium felt drafted, planned, like a room in a house whose blueprints Rafael had suddenly been permitted to glimpse. The candle flames stood straighter while she spoke. The dust in the light hung instead of falling.

When she stopped, the dust remembered gravity all at once, and the moment broke.

"She's not speaking a language," Rafael said slowly. "She's speaking *the* language."

"Now you understand why I didn't sleep." Maren rubbed her face with both hands. "Every text that mentions the First Tongue agrees on two things. One: it predates kingdoms, predates gods, predates the idea of things being separate enough to have names. Two—" She hesitated.

"Say it."

"Two: nothing alive ever spoke it. Because by the time anything was alive, the language had already been used up. Spent. Like—" She glanced at the ledger shelves lining the walls, thousands of volumes recording ten thousand bargains, and her voice went dry. "Like currency. You don't spend the mint itself. You spend what the mint makes."

Rafael looked at the child. Ilsa looked back, unblinking, and then she did something worse than speak. She smiled — small, tired, knowing — and said, in accented but perfectly serviceable common trade-tongue:

"You're frightened. Don't be. He isn't angry yet."

Neither of them asked who *he* was. Some questions are doors you know better than to open before you've found the key.

* * *

Sorrel heard them out in her office with the shutters closed against the morning, which she never did. Sorrel liked light; she said a guildmaster who worked in shadow had something to hide, and Sorrel had spent forty years making sure she had nothing to hide except everything.

Now the shutters were barred and the lamps were lit and she sat behind her desk listening to Maren's report with her hands folded, still as a woman at her own trial.

When it was done she was quiet for a long time.

"The First Tongue," she said at last. "Spoken by a feral child who walked out of a dead-moon snowfall to our particular door, out of every door in Vellmarrow." Her eyes came up and found Rafael's. "You believe in coincidence, Nystrom?"

"No."

"Neither do I. Coincidence is a word poor people use for providence and rich people use for consequence." She rose and went to the window, as if she could see through the shutter-boards anyway. "How many people know?"

"In the guild? Maren. The night porter. Me."

"The porter's been paid off?"

"He's been made to forget. Standard entry."

"Good." Sorrel turned back, and now Rafael saw it — the thing under her composure, the way her thumb pressed white against her forefinger. Forty years Rafael had known this woman, since the night she'd pulled a gutter-frozen cutpurse off the streets and put a quill in their hand instead of a knife, and Rafael had seen her afraid perhaps three times. This was the fourth. "Here's what happens now. The girl goes below. The old wine cellar — the one behind the false rack, not the true one. No one sees her, no one hears her, no one speaks of her above stairs. Maren continues her work down there, alone, and keeps no notes outside her own head."

"Sorrel—"

"I'm not finished." The words came flat and fast. "No entries in any ledger concerning the child. None. Not acquisitions, not expenses, not the porter's fee. If it isn't written, it didn't happen — that rule has saved this guild more times than I can count, and today it saves her. Whatever she is, whoever sent her or whatever sent itself through her, the moment we write her down she becomes findable. Do you understand me? Our ledgers are the most legible objects in this city. Anything written in them belongs to the book."

"And if the book belongs to someone else?" Rafael asked quietly.

Sorrel's silence lasted four full seconds. Rafael counted them, because counting was what Rafael did instead of breathing.

"Then we'd best make sure it doesn't," Sorrel said. "Go. Move her now, before the household wakes properly. And Nystrom—" Rafael paused at the door. Sorrel was looking at her own hands. "Thank you for bringing her to me first. I know you considered otherwise."

Rafael hadn't, actually. There had been no decision at all, only the reflex of twenty years. But they heard what lived under the thanks: Sorrel was watching them, the way Maren watched a word she couldn't parse. Testing whether the structure would hold.

"It's what you taught me," Rafael said, and left.

* * *

That night Rafael dreamed of a crown.

Not worn — grown. It stood alone in a dark field, black iron thorns twisted into a circlet, and where gems should have been set there were seals, hundreds of wax seals, each one stamped with a signature Rafael knew better than their own name. Veth's mark. The silent god's sign, the one Rafael traced at the head of every honest page, the one that meant *witnessed, owed, true.*

The crown was singing. That was the horror of it — a thing of thorns and wax, singing in a voice like a struck bell that never finishes ringing, and Rafael understood in the dream's way that this was not music. It was speech. A voice so vast it could only be heard as tone, a sentence so long that centuries passed between its subject and its verb. And beneath the song, woven through it, a second sound: the dry whisper of a quill moving across paper, faster and faster, writing something that had no end.

Rafael woke with the taste of iron in their mouth and their ears ringing.

Not metaphorically. Ringing — a high, thin tone, like the after-hum of a temple bell, sitting inside both ears and refusing to fade. They lay in the dark of their cell and listened to it. Twenty years they had carried Veth's silence the way other faithful carried relics: the god did not answer, had never answered, and the faith was precisely in the unanswered prayer. Rafael had built a whole self on that foundation. *He is silent because he trusts us to keep the accounts without him.* It had been a comfort. It had been, if Rafael was honest, the only warmth in a life conducted entirely in numbers.

But silence, Rafael realized now, lying rigid in the dark, has a texture. And this was not it.

Silence is absence. This was presence — a sound being actively withheld, a note sustained just below hearing, the way a scream held behind clenched teeth is louder than the scream. The god was not quiet. The god was *muzzled*. And tonight, with a child asleep one floor below who spoke in the language reality was drafted in, the muzzle had begun — barely, faintly — to slip.

Rafael got up, dressed, and climbed the stair to the shrine.

It was a shame of a place, tucked under the eaves where the roof sloped too low for storage: a stone alcove, a single candle, a bowl for offerings that held only dust, because what do you offer a god who wants nothing? Rafael knelt on the cold flagstone, the way they had every seventh-day for two decades, and did what they always did. Said nothing. Asked nothing. Simply kept the appointment, because a debt honestly kept needs no words, and because somewhere in the marrow-deep accounting of their soul they believed — had always believed — that the keeping was heard.

Tonight the ringing filled the shrine like water fills a cup.

Rafael pressed their palms flat against the floor and felt it — actually felt it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall. Something below. Something vast and patient and *straining*, the way a debtor strains against a contract's terms, looking for the loophole, the grace clause, the line where the ink gives.

*I'm here,* Rafael did not say, because Veth's faithful do not say. *Whatever holds you — show me the entry. Show me where it's written, and I'll find the error in it. Every account has an error. You taught the world to keep books; your own book cannot be perfect.*

The ringing rose, crested —

— and stopped, cut clean, like a ledger slammed shut.

Down the hall, muffled by stone, a child began to hum. Low, wordless, the melody of a sentence too long to finish. Rafael knelt alone in the ringing dark and understood that they had just made a vow, out loud, in the only court that mattered — and that come morning, before anything else, before Sorrel, before sense itself, they were going down into the sealed stacks to find out what the Gilded Thorn's oldest volumes said about Mount Ceren.

Because debts, Rafael knew, do not disappear. They are only ever transferred.

And someone had transferred one to a seven-year-old girl.
