## 4. Ledger of Souls

The archive of the Gilded Thorn occupied the three floors beneath the counting room, and like most things in the guild it was organized by value rather than sense. The cheapest debts lived near the stairs—coughs sold by miners, a widow's fear of open water, a boy's talent for whistling. The deeper you went, the older and stranger the paper became, until at the bottom of the third stairwell you found yourself among volumes bound in leather so dark it drank the lamplight, and the ledgers there did not record trades so much as they recorded wounds.

Rafael had spent eleven years learning this archive the way other people learned a city: by getting lost in it repeatedly until the losing stopped. They knew which shelves leaned. They knew which books bit—there were two on the second landing that would take a drop of blood from a careless thumb, and Sorrel had never explained why, and Rafael had never asked, because asking questions about the deep collection was one of the many small ways people stopped being welcome in the Thorn.

Tonight they came down with a lamp, a slate, and an itch behind the eyes that had not left them since Ilsa spoke her first word in the doorway four nights ago.

*Veth,* the child had said—or something that sat in the ear the way Veth's name did, if Veth's name had ever been more than a shape everyone agreed to leave empty.

Maren had confirmed the language. That was yesterday's horror. Tonight Rafael wanted the older horror, the one underneath: what the First Tongue was doing in the mouth of a barefoot girl who looked six years old and might, for all anyone could prove, be younger than that in any way that mattered.

They started where forbidden things usually start: with the index that pretended not to exist.

It was a slim volume shelved spine-inward on the third level, catalogued under *Miscellaneous, Liturgical, Damaged*, and it listed nothing but omissions. That was its whole function. Every entry was a title with a reason struck through it—*burned*, *eaten*, *returned to sender*—and every so often, between the strikethroughs, a shelf mark. Rafael had found it in their second year and told no one, because the archive rewarded silence the way the guild rewarded everything else.

They ran a finger down the column of damaged liturgics until they reached an entry near the bottom:

*Codex Ceren — sealed by order of the Founding Hand — do not shelve — do not read — retained.*

No shelf mark. Just *retained*, which in the grammar of the Thorn meant one thing only: Sorrel kept it herself.

Rafael stood very still in the cold air and listened to their own heartbeat, which was doing something unprofessional.

Sorrel's private vault was behind her office, behind the false wall that held her good wine, and Rafael had opened it exactly twice in eleven years—once to inventory for the annual audit, once because Sorrel had been drunk on borrowed courage and wanted company while she looked at a letter she never answered. Both times, Rafael had seen the same thing: a long low cabinet of shallow drawers, and on top of it, a single book chained at the corner to an iron staple, as if the book were a dog someone loved and also feared.

Getting in tonight was simple. Getting in without being remembered was harder. The house was full of Sorrel's people, and half of them were awake, because the whole guild slept badly since Ilsa arrived, the way a kennel sleeps badly when something new has been put in the yard.

Rafael waited for the waning hour—the dead hour, the one the night clerk called the hour of unrecorded time—and took the back stair past the ink room, where the vats breathed in their sleep, and let themselves into the counting room with the key they were entitled to carry and resented carrying.

Sorrel's door was not locked. It never was. That was the message it sent, and everyone understood it: *you may enter, but you will feel me watching.* Rafael entered, felt watched, crossed the office in six steps, moved the wine rack, and stood before the vault.

The chain on the book was locked with a puzzle of seven rings, each ring marked with a debt-term in the old shorthand. Rafael read them upside down, then right way up, and laughed once, quietly, because of course. The combination was the founding contract of the Gilded Thorn itself—the terms Sorrel had accepted forty years ago when she'd bought the guild's charter from whatever passed, in those days, for the powers under Mount Ceren. Everyone in the house knew the founding contract. It was recited at every initiation, droned by every senior accountant until the juniors could say it asleep.

*Rent of one soul per decade, remitted. Interest forgiven in perpetuity, contingent upon faithful service. The Thorn endures; the account remains open.*

Seven rings. Seven clauses. Rafael turned them in order, and the lock sighed open like an old man grateful to stop pretending.

The chain fell away. The book did not look dangerous. That was the first lie it told. It was a ledger, standard format, black boards, the spine stamped in blind tooling with no title at all—which itself was a statement, because everything in the Thorn was titled, priced, and proud of it. A book with no name was a book that had paid to have its name removed, or had never been allowed one.

Rafael carried it to Sorrel's desk, set the lamp close, and opened it.

The early pages were ordinary, which unsettled them more than strangeness would have. Entries in a neat mercantile hand: purchases of charter, of premises, of silence from three separate constables. The founding contract transcribed in full, witnessed twice, the witness signatures smudged as though the witnesses had signed while looking over their shoulders. And then, beginning around the tenth leaf, the hand changed.

Rafael knew hands the way musicians know voices, and this one stopped them cold.

It wasn't beautiful. It was *certain*. Every letter placed as though the writer had decided, once and forever, how letters should go, and was merely reminding the page. No corrections anywhere. Not in forty years of entries—not one blot, one strike, one hesitation. People wrote like that only when they could not make mistakes, or believed they couldn't, and both possibilities made Rafael's mouth dry.

The entries themselves were worse.

Not trades. *Judgments.* Line after line of debts assessed, deferred, forgiven—forgiven, again and again, in a world where Rafael had been taught since childhood that forgiveness of a true debt was arithmetically impossible, that the number had to live somewhere, that this was not cruelty but physics. Yet here was a hand forgiving debts down entire pages, writing *remitted* beside obligations that should have outlived nations, and beside each remission, in smaller script, a cost noted and then—struck through. Paid. Absorbed. Swallowed somewhere out of sight.

*Who absorbs the cost?* Rafael thought, and the thought had a child's voice, and they hated it.

Then they reached the sealed page.

You could tell it was sealed because it wasn't. Someone had cut the stitching along the gutter, folded the leaves inward, and pasted a strip of plain paper across them with a wax stamp the size of a coin. The wax was the color of dried blood, and pressed into it was a sigil Rafael recognized without wanting to: a thorned circle, broken at the crown. Veth's sign, the sign every sworn soul in the guild wore scarred into the web of the thumb—but closed here, thorns meeting thorns, a circle shut around something.

Around a prisoner, some part of Rafael said, in Maren's voice. You seal a circle to keep a thing in.

The strip of paper said, in the certain hand: *Paid. Do not reopen. There is nothing owed here but grief, and grief is not transferable.*

Which was, Rafael reflected, the single most dishonest sentence ever written in a ledger, because grief was the one commodity the Thorn had never managed to keep off its shelves. They had bottled grief. They sold it by the dram.

They broke the seal.

The wax parted cleanly, obediently, as if it had been waiting eleven years for someone with bad judgment and good hands. The folded leaves opened.

The entry was dated forty years ago, to the month of the guild's founding. It was written in the certain hand, but the certainty had changed character—this was certainty under load, letters pressed deep enough to emboss three pages down. Rafael read it once at speed, then forced themselves to read it slowly, the way they audited, line by line, refusing to let comprehension arrive ahead of the words.

*A soul has been taken into custody below Ceren. Not purchased. Seized as surety against the debtor's default.*

A fragment. The entry used the word *fragment*, and used it the way appraisers used *carat*—precisely, clinically, a technical term for a portion of a whole.

*The fragment is drawn from Myself. It is the portion of Me that remembers being forgiven. It cannot remain above while the covenant stands, and the covenant must stand, therefore the fragment is held as collateral until the account closes.*

Below that, the buyer's mark—or the holder's mark, the entry didn't distinguish, and Rafael would understand later why no distinction existed. The mark was drawn large, half a page, in the same embossing pressure: a spiral of interlocking hooks, like thorns growing in a coil, and at the center of the spiral, a small closed circle.

Rafael stared at it.

Their body knew before their mind did. Their body went cold from the feet up, the old mercenary reflex, the one that said *you are standing on the edge of something and your legs already know the fall.* Because they had seen that mark before. Four nights ago. In candlelight, on the shoulder blade of a sleeping child, while Maren traced it with one careful finger and said, *birthmark, probably, they're often shaped like something, aren't they—*

A spiral of thorns. A closed circle at the heart of it.

Ilsa's birthmark matched the holder's seal on a forty-year-old entry recording the seizure of a divine fragment.

Rafael set the lamp down very carefully, because their hands had begun to shake, and shaking hands near open flame was a lesson every gutter child learned before they learned letters.

They read the rest of the page.

There wasn't much. Two lines, and a signature.

The lines: *Should the collateral be recovered above ground before the account closes, the covenant is void, and all remissions herein are recalled in full. Let no one call this cruelty. I forgave the world. The world required a pledge. The pledge is Mine, and it will sleep, and it will wake as someone small, and it will not know what it is, and that mercy also is Mine.*

And the signature.

Rafael had grown up in a country where the silent god's name was not spoken but *left*—a space at the end of prayers, a blank in contracts, an emptiness shaped exactly like a word. Every document in the Thorn bore that blank where a divine witness would stand. Rafael had signed beside it ten thousand times. They had never once seen anything written in it.

The signature on this page filled the blank.

It was not letters. It was a pressure—a mark like the impression a heavy thing leaves when it rests against soft ground for a very long time. Looking at it hurt in a way Rafael had no vocabulary for, a ache behind the sternum, homesickness for a place they'd never been. The god had not gone silent, some part of them whispered. The god had signed. This is what his voice looks like when it can only come out through a pen.

*Ilsa,* Rafael thought, and the name arrived whole, with a weight it hadn't carried four days ago. *She's not a vessel grown around a stolen soul. She's the stolen soul, grown a person around itself. He wrote it himself. He told us. He told anyone who ever broke this seal: she'll wake as someone small, and she won't know what she is.*

*And the moment the Court learns she's above ground, the covenant breaks, and every forgiveness in this book is called due.*

Every remission. Forty years of swallowed costs. All of it recalled at once, from whoever had been absorbing it.

From him. From Veth. From whatever was left of a god who had tried to forgive the world and been made collateral for his own mercy.

Rafael closed the book.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, and in its stillness Rafael heard, faintly, from three floors up, a sound that froze them worse than the signature had: a child humming. Ilsa, awake past the hour, singing to herself in the First Tongue—and now, having read the entry, Rafael could hear what Maren's transcription had only hinted at. The melody repeated. It resolved. It resolved the way a debt resolves, phrase answering phrase, balance finding zero.

She wasn't singing. She was *reconciling.* In her sleep-warm, six-year-old way, alone in a strange bed in a den of thieves, she was running numbers older than kingdoms, and the numbers were coming out even, and the evenness sounded, drifting down through floorboards and stone, almost like contentment.

Almost like someone humming while they worked, in a cellar, in the dark, for a very long time.

Rafael wrapped the codex, returned it to the staple, re-ran the seven rings, pushed the wine rack home, and stood a last moment in Sorrel's office with their palm flat against the desk, breathing the way they'd been taught to breathe before violence, back when they'd been someone who did that.

Because one more thing had been on the sealed page—one line Rafael had read and refused, until now, to think about. Small, in the margin, in a different hand: hurried, human, smudged. A reader's note, left by whoever had broken this seal before.

Forty years ago. Around the founding. Around the time Sorrel had pulled a starving child out of a gutter and given them a pen instead of a knife.

The note said: *He asked me to hide her when she comes. I said yes. God forgive me—I've told the Court where she'll be born.*

Rafael blew out the lamp and stood in the dark, listening to a child hum the arithmetic of a broken god, and understood that in the morning they would have to look Sorrel in the eye and decide which of them was lying.
