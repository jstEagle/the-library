## 6. The Broker's Visit

The broker arrived three days after the dead moon, on a morning so gray the city seemed to have been drawn in charcoal and never colored in.

I knew before anyone told me. I was in the counting room with the ledgers open before me when every drop of ink in the Gilded Thorn went still—not dried, not thickened, but *still*, the way a dog goes still when it hears something on the stairs that isn't a footstep. Ink is a living thing in this house. It breathes with the guild's business, rises and falls like a tide. And for the length of four heartbeats it stopped breathing entirely.

Then it started again, and somewhere below me, Tammerin's voice called up the stairwell: "Visitor. Says he's expected."

Nobody had expected anybody. That was the first lie of the day, and it wasn't even mine.

* * *

He stood in the receiving hall wearing a merchant's coat cut in the southern style, dove-gray wool, silver buttons polished to a shine that hurt the eye. He was of middling height and middling years, with a pleasant face—the kind of face you forget while you're still looking at it.

That was the problem.

I have spent eleven years keeping accounts for a guild that traffics in impossible things. I have learned to read faces the way Sorrel reads contracts: not for what they show but for what they're built from. A face is a ledger too. Every expression is an entry. Every smile has a source you can trace if you're patient enough.

His face had no sources. The smile sat where a smile goes, at the correct angle, with the correct number of teeth showing, but nothing moved behind it. It was like watching a puppet perform a man performing warmth. And there were small wrongnesses, the kind most people would file away without examining: his blink came half a beat too slow, as though he had to remember to do it. When he turned his head, the turn began at the shoulders and the face followed a moment later, reluctantly, like something being dragged rather than moved.

"Master Nystrom." He pronounced it correctly. Nobody pronounced it correctly on a first meeting. "Vessel, of the House Beneath. Your guildmaster will know why I've come."

"The House Beneath," I said, and made my voice into the flat, bored instrument I keep for exactly these occasions. "We deal with many houses."

"I'm sure." He smiled the smile again, the same one, identical down to the tooth. "But only one owes us."

Behind him, Tammerin caught my eye and shrugged—she'd checked the appointment book, found nothing, found no record of any Vessel ever having written to us. Which meant he hadn't come through channels. Which meant he'd come because he already knew what he wanted, and channels were for people who needed to ask questions.

Sorrel appeared at the top of the hall steps before I could answer, which meant she'd been listening, which meant she'd known he was coming too. She descended the way she always did in negotiations: slowly, letting the silence do half her talking.

"Vessel." Warmth in her voice, and under the warmth, something I'd never heard there before. Fear, maybe. Or its close cousin, deference. "You should have sent word. We'd have prepared a proper reception."

"Word travels," Vessel said, "both ways. I prefer to see things unprepared. Prepared rooms tell you what people want you to think. Unprepared rooms tell you the truth." His eyes—gray, ordinary gray, gray as dishwater—moved past Sorrel's shoulder and traveled the hall the way an assessor's eyes travel a warehouse. Cataloguing. Pricing. They snagged on the stairwell leading down to the cellars, held there a moment too long, and moved on.

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

Because Ilsa was in the cellars. Had been since the dead moon, sleeping in the linen room off the wine racks, guarded by a door lock Maren had warded with three languages and a threat. Three days we'd kept her hidden—Rafael the invisible, the careful, the man who had survived eleven years in this house by making himself furniture—and now a thing wearing a merchant's coat was standing in our hall, pricing our stairwells.

"We'll want to inspect your current holdings," Vessel said pleasantly. "Standard procedure when a house carries obligations of this vintage."

"Of course," Sorrel said. "Rafael will accompany you. He keeps our books. Nothing in this building exists that he hasn't counted twice."

It was the first time in my life Sorrel had used me as a shield, and she did it so smoothly that anyone else would have missed it. Anyone else would have thought: trusted clerk, safe pair of hands. But I heard the other message underneath, the one written between the lines the way Maren says the First Tongue hides meaning inside meaning: *Stay close to him. Watch what he looks at. Keep him away from the cellar.*

Or possibly: *Keep the cellar away from him.* With Sorrel, lately, I couldn't be sure they weren't the same instruction.

* * *

The inspection took four hours and felt like four years.

Vessel walked the vaults where we keep the bottled grief—rows upon rows of stoppered tears, each vial labeled in my hand with a debtor's mark and a date. He lifted one to the light, swirled it, set it back exactly where it had stood, within the width of a hair. He examined the shelf of borrowed years, tapping each jar and listening, actually listening, to the sound it made, the way a farmer thumps a melon. In the name-vault he stood for a long time before the cabinet where we keep names not yet spoken—the unborn ones, the ones waiting in their mothers' throats—and whatever he heard or felt there made his false mouth curve slightly upward.

"Fertile season," he murmured.

I wrote that down in the little book I carry, because writing things down is how I keep my hands from shaking, and because everything about this man belonged in a ledger. *Vessel, House Beneath. Inspected grief vault, 214 vials touched. Years, 31 jars. Names: 40 minutes' attention.* Somewhere in the margin of that entry, without deciding to, I also wrote: *face does not fit.*

"What are you writing?" he asked, without turning around.

"Inventory notes. Everything you touch gets logged. Guild policy."

"Good policy." He turned, and the turn did its terrible shoulder-first thing. "Accountability is the soul of trade. Most houses let sentiment rot their records." He looked at me then, properly looked, and I understood with total clarity that I was being assessed the same way the grief vials had been—lifted, swirled, priced. "You've been here long?"

"Eleven years."

"And before?"

"Gutters," I said. "The guild pulled me out of one."

"Mm." Something flickered across the borrowed face—not an emotion, but the memory of where an emotion would go, like seeing the outline of a picture burned into a wall. "They collect strays, don't they. Sorrel especially. There's a kind of debt in that, Master Nystrom. The deepest kind. Gratitude is just a loan you can never finish repaying."

"I wouldn't know. I don't borrow."

"No," he agreed mildly. "You lend. I've seen your hand in the books. Very disciplined hand. Very *clean*." He let the word sit between us like a coin on a table. "Clean hands are rare in this business. Almost suspicious, really. A man who keeps accounts for impossible things and never once smudges the page." He smiled the smile. Same teeth. Same angles. "What do you do with all that cleanliness, I wonder."

"I file it," I said, "under inventory," and walked past him toward the next vault, and my heart was hammering like a fist on a door, and beneath the terror—for the first time in eleven years—there was something else, bright and hot and completely unfamiliar.

Exhilaration. I was lying to the Hollow Court's own broker and he hadn't caught it, and some starved animal in my chest stood up on its hind legs and *howled*.

God help me. It felt good.

* * *

He lingered near the cellar stair for eleven minutes. I counted.

We had come to the end of the inspection circuit—the last vault, the archive room, and beyond its far wall the service passage that ran down to the wine racks and the linen room. Vessel stopped in the doorway of the archive and simply stood there, head tilted, in the attitude of a man hearing music from another room.

"You keep anything below?" he asked.

"Wine. Linens. Salt stock. The usual house stores." Each word placed like a coin on a scale, weighed beforehand.

"Mm." He took one step toward the passage. One step. The floorboard under his heel creaked, and I swear the sound bent downward at the end of it, like a note going sour. "House stores," he repeated. "May I?"

"Those aren't guild holdings. Stores are outside the terms of any inspection."

"Are they?" He turned his gray eyes on me, and for just a moment the mask slipped—not fell, slipped, the way a painting slips on a wall—and what looked out from behind the pleasant middle-aged face had no age at all and no interest in pretending otherwise. "Your guildmaster seemed to feel otherwise."

"Then take it up with my guildmaster," I said. "She's upstairs. I keep the books. The books say stores are excluded, and until the books say different—" I shrugged, mercenary-casual, gutters-and-grit, a man who couldn't be bothered. Inside, every nerve I owned was screaming.

The silence stretched. Down the passage, faint as breath, I heard—I would swear to my grave I heard—a child humming. Ilsa, awake, singing her wordless song to herself in the linen room the way children do, and the melody drifted up the stairwell and into the archive like smoke.

Vessel's head turned toward the sound with the smoothness of a weathervane finding wind.

And then Maren saved us, God bless her and her terrible curiosity. She came clattering down the archive stairs with an armful of folios and a scholar's absolute indifference to everything except her own errand, nearly walked into Vessel, looked up at his ill-fitting face with the mild annoyance of a woman interrupted mid-thought, and said: "Oh. You're blocking the shelf. The Ceren cartographies? Third shelf? I need them."

"Ceren," Vessel said softly.

"Mount Ceren. The old surveys. For a comparative study." Maren hefted her folios. "You're the visitor, yes? The one from under the mountain? Then perhaps you can settle a question for me—the deep galleries, are they natural karst or worked stone? The pre-kingdom texts disagree and it's *driving* me—"

"They are neither," Vessel said. "They are *written*." And he stepped aside from the shelf, and the moment broke, and he let Maren push past him in a cloud of ink-smell and impatience, and the humming upstairs had already stopped, and I could not tell whether Ilsa had gone quiet on her own or whether someone—or something—had asked her to.

* * *

He left at dusk.

At the door, in the last gray light, he paused beside me and produced from his coat a small card of black paper. On it, in ink that shimmered like oil on water, was written a single line:

*An account remains open. Settlement may occur at the holder's convenience.*

"A receipt," he said, "for the courtesy of a clean inspection." He pressed it into my hand. His fingers were cool and slightly too smooth, like river stones. "Keep it somewhere safe, Master Nystrom. Paper like this has a way of finding its way home."

Then he walked out into the street, and the fog took him at the second lamppost—not gradually, not the way fog takes anyone, but all at once, the way a page takes ink.

I stood in the doorway holding the black card until Tammerin came to bar the door, and then I climbed the stairs to the counting room with my legs feeling borrowed themselves, and lit the lamp, and opened the day's ledger to make the evening entries, because ritual is what holds a man together when the world has gone strange.

And there I sat, with the pen in my hand, looking at the line item that had to be written. *Visitor received. Inspection conducted. No irregularities noted.*

No irregularities noted. The truth was that a creature wearing a dead man's face had walked our halls, had listened to a hidden child sing, had left a receipt for a debt we all pretended didn't exist. The truth was that Sorrel had known he was coming and had hidden it from me, her keeper of all secrets, and had aimed me at him like a tool and thanked me for it.

The truth was that the books of the Gilded Thorn had never once, in two hundred years, contained a lie.

I thought about Veth. About the oath I'd sworn over the shrine bell, silent god, god who does not speak, whose whole covenant is that what is written stays written—that the record is sacred, that the account is honest even when the accountant bleeds. My whole life was built on that covenant. My whole self. The one clean thing the gutters never managed to dirty.

I thought about Ilsa, asleep in the linen room, humming in a language older than lying.

Then I dipped the pen, and my hand did not shake—it surprised me, how steady it was—and I wrote:

*Visitor received. Routine inspection. No irregularities noted. All holdings accounted for.*

The ink went down onto the page and the page accepted it, and nothing burned, no bell rang, no curse fell on my head. That was the worst part. The ledger took the lie as easily as a gutter takes rainwater. As though the books had been waiting eleven years for me to break, and had simply been polite about it.

I closed the volume and sat in the lamplight listening to my own heartbeat, and the exhilaration was still there, bright and awful, tangled through the terror like wire through rope.

Below me, faintly, Ilsa hummed in her sleep. Above me, in her tower study, Sorrel's lamp burned late—as it always did after dark dealings—and I wondered what entry she was making in whatever book she kept that I had never seen.

And on the table beside the ledger, the black card lay where I'd put it, and in the lamplight I noticed for the first time that the oil-dark ink had changed. New words were surfacing through it, rising slow as bodies through deep water, letter by patient letter, addressed—to my certain, sickening knowledge—to no one in this house but me:

*A clean hand, Master Nystrom. We remember clean hands. The House Beneath will call again.*

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, and did not sleep at all.
