## 7. What Veth Owes

The shrine to Veth stood in the oldest quarter of the city, wedged between a tannery and a house that had burned twice and been rebuilt twice more out of spite. It was a squat stone thing with a slanted roof and no door, because Veth required no threshold—you walked into his houses the way you walked into debt: uninvited, already owing.

Inside there was nothing but an altar and a silence so complete it felt manufactured. No icon. Veth's faithful had never made one. *How do you depict the one who listens?* the old catechism asked. *You do not. You sit where he can hear you.*

Rafael hadn't come here in eleven years.

They came now at dusk, when the tannery workers had gone home and the street smelled of lime and wet hide, and they stood in the doorway a long time before crossing it. Their body remembered the ritual even as their mind resisted it—left foot first, always left, because the right foot owed the world its weight and you did not spend what was owed.

*Superstition,* Sorrel would have said, laughing. *Veth is a ledger-keeper's god, Rafe. A god for clerks. He doesn't want your knees.*

But Sorrel had never sat through a dead moon night listening to a child speak the language that predated speaking. Sorrel had never watched ink crawl on a page like something trying to get away.

Rafael crossed the threshold on their left foot and knelt on the worn stone before the altar, and the silence closed over them like water.

---

They had prepared a prayer. That was the humiliating part—they'd drafted it that afternoon, sitting at their desk with Ilsa asleep in the locked storeroom below and Maren's notes spread out around them like evidence at a trial. They had drafted it the way they drafted everything: precisely, economically, with margins noted.

*Lord of Accounts, Keeper of the Silent Book, I bring you a discrepancy. A soul was sold beneath Ceren forty years ago, and the payment was entered in your hand. I ask you to confirm the entry. I ask you to tell me who signed against whom. I ask—*

And there the draft had stopped, because Rafael had realized what they were actually asking, and it wasn't confirmation. It was absolution. *Tell me my god didn't do this. Tell me the child wasn't collateral in some divine transaction I've spent my life serving without knowing.*

So they abandoned the draft. They knelt with empty hands and an empty mouth, which was, Maren had once told them, the only orthodox way to pray to Veth anyway. *He doesn't take petitions,* she'd said, back when they were both young enough to say such things over cheap wine. *He takes testimony. You don't ask him for anything. You tell him the truth and wait to see if he flinches.*

Rafael told him the truth.

Not aloud—the silence in the shrine didn't seem built for voices—but in the wordless way of long practice, laying facts on the altar like coins counted out one by one.

*A child came out of the snow speaking the First Tongue. She has a birthmark shaped like the buyer's seal from a contract sealed before her grandmother was born. She was grown around a stolen soul like a pearl grown around grit. The guildmaster knows. The guildmaster wants to sell her. And I have served this guild for nineteen years, kept your ledgers, kept your oaths, and I did not know what we were until she knocked on the door.*

*I believed you were silent because silence is your nature. The quiet at the end of every transaction. The pause after the pen lifts. I built a whole faith on that silence. It was the only part of religion that ever made sense to me—that somewhere, someone was not talking.*

*If you are silent because you chose it, then I am yours, and I will keep her safe in your name and pay whatever it costs.*

*If you are silent because someone took your voice—*

Rafael's hands had curled into fists on the stone without their noticing.

*—then tell me who. And tell me what you owe me for nineteen years of prayers into a deaf dark.*

That was blasphemy, probably. Asking a god what he owed. But Rafael was an accountant, and accountants know that every relationship is a ledger, and they had been running a deficit of faith for years and pretending otherwise, and the pretense had ended three nights ago when a barefoot child had looked up at them in the lamplight and spoken words that made the ink move.

They waited.

The silence waited back.

Minutes passed. The light in the doorway went from amber to grey to blue. Somewhere behind the tannery, a dog started up and stopped. Rafael's knees ached. Their breath fogged faintly in the cold stone air.

Nothing. Of course nothing. Eleven years of nothing, and why should tonight be—

The candle they hadn't lit lit itself.

No—that wasn't right either. There was no flame. But the altar was suddenly *lit*, the way things are lit in dreams, sourceless and certain, and Rafael understood with a lurch that the darkness in the shrine had not been absence all these years. It had been a held breath.

Then the vision took them.

---

They were standing on a mountainside under a sky the color of old pewter, and the mountain was Ceren—not the drawings in the guild's atlases, which showed a respectable peak with a respectable pass, but the *true* face of it, seen from inside, vast and hollowed like a tooth. Rafael's feet found no ground; they simply were there, in the way of visions, their body still kneeling on cold stone in a city shrine while the rest of them fell upward into the dark beneath the world.

Galleries opened below them, tier on tier, carved not by hands but by *recording*. Every surface was inscribed. Walls, ceilings, floors, the spiral staircases that descended beyond sight—all of it covered in script that Rafael's eyes could not read and yet somehow understood, the way you understand a scream without parsing its vowels. Contracts. Millennia of contracts. The whole history of everything anyone had ever traded away, written into the bones of the mountain.

And at the bottom—so far down that distance stopped meaning anything—at the bottom was a shape.

It might once have been a man. It might once have been a tree, or a door, or an idea. What it was now was *bound*: chains of a metal that wasn't metal passing through it at intervals, and where each chain entered, the script of the walls grew denser, as though the chains themselves were lines of text given weight. The figure was enormous even in its diminishment. Even shackled and folded small, it radiated the way a bank vault radiates—the sense of immense value made immobile.

Its mouth was open.

That was the detail that broke something in Rafael, then and forever after. The mouth was open, and had been open for a very long time, and nothing came out. Around the figure's throat, where the voice should have lived, was a collar of contract-stone, and from the collar ran conduits—Rafael could think of no other word—conduits that carried something away into the walls, into the galleries, into the deep archive of the Court.

The god's voice, siphoned off. Stored. Spent.

*Paid as consideration,* said the walls, in their unreadable legible script. *One voice, divine, in perpetuity. Received in full satisfaction of—*

And there the contract's purpose blurred, as though the mountain itself blinked, as though some clause was too large for a mortal mind to hold. But Rafael caught the shape of it, the way you catch the shape of a wave before it breaks:

*In full satisfaction of all debts owed to all debtors everywhere, which forgiveness the party of the first part did undertake to grant, and which grant the parties of the second part did refuse—*

Veth had tried to forgive everyone.

The realization arrived not as thought but as pressure, a fist closing around Rafael's heart. Every debt ever written down—and the guild's ledgers alone held thousands, and the guild was one guild among dozens, among hundreds, and beneath all of them lay this original book—every debt ever recorded, voided. Forgiven. Erased in a single stroke of divine mercy.

And the creditors had said no.

Not because the debts were just. Rafael knew with an accountant's certainty that they weren't—knew it from the inside, from nineteen years of entries like *one mother's name for a winter's bread* and *the memory of a daughter's face, term renewable at the holder's discretion.* The debts were monstrous. But they were *assets*, and the creatures beneath Ceren had built a kingdom on them, a court, an economy of souls with a Regent at its head, and a god who forgave all debts was not a savior to them. He was an extinction event.

So they had taken his voice as payment—for what? For the cost of *not* forgiving, apparently. They had billed him for his own mercy, and he had paid, because Veth kept his contracts, because that was the whole of him, because a god whose nature was the keeping of accounts could no more renege than the mountain could walk.

And then the world above had called him silent. Had built shrines to his silence. Had made a virtue of his mutilation.

Rafael knelt inside the vision and wept without tears, and the chained figure turned its head.

It shouldn't have been able to. The chains allowed nothing else to move. But the head turned, slowly, with a sound like pages turning, and Rafael found themselves looking into a face that was no face—a smoothness where features should be, the way a ledger page is smooth before anything is written on it—and yet they felt *seen*, felt known down to the last entry, every debt and credit of their small crooked life read at a glance and neither condemned nor pardoned, merely *acknowledged*, which was somehow worse and better than either.

The mouth moved. No sound came. But meaning arrived anyway, the way numbers arrive—not heard but understood:

*I did not abandon you. I am here. I have been here. I have heard every word.*

Nineteen years of prayers. Eleven years of silence since the last one. All of it received. All of it *kept*, filed somewhere in that impossible archive, and Rafael understood that Veth had answered none of it for the same reason a prisoner cannot answer letters—he had written back with his whole self, endlessly, and the mail had been confiscated at the throat.

*Why show me this?* Rafael asked, or thought, or screamed—it made no difference in the place where they were. *Why me? I'm nobody. I'm a clerk. I falsify nothing, I steal nothing, I keep the books and keep my head down—*

*Because you asked what I owe you,* the god said, and there was something in the understanding that might have been amusement, in another life, on another day, might have been warmth. *No one asks what a god owes. They only ask what they owe him.*

*Well?*

The chains tightened—or the vision tightened, Rafael couldn't tell—and the god's attention narrowed to a point, and the answer came with the terrible clarity of a final column summed:

*Owed: one accounting. Rendered by a hand unbound by contract. I cannot void what I signed. But a debtor's advocate—one who keeps the books, who knows the entries, who can find the errors—*

The vision shuddered. Somewhere far above, in the galleries, in the walls, in the conduits carrying the stolen voice, something had noticed the leak. The pewter sky flickered. The script on the walls began to crawl faster, agitated, like insects disturbed under bark.

*Find the original entry,* the god said, and the words came faster now, fraying at the edges as the vision began to tear. *Forty years past. My signature upon it. Find the error, Rafael Nystrom. There is always an error. Even gods make mistakes in the margins—*

*What error? What am I looking for?*

*The date,* said the god, and smiled, or the absence of a face did something that Rafael's mind translated as smiling, even as the mountain dissolved upward into dark. *Check the date against the child. Check when the soul was sold against when she was born. I signed for a soul that did not exist yet, clerk. You cannot sell what has not been made. Someone forged my mercy into a chain—find the forger's hand—*

The candle-that-was-not-a-candle guttered.

The shrine snapped back around Rafael like a lid closing: cold stone, aching knees, the smell of lime drifting from the tannery, the ordinary dark of an ordinary city night. Their breath came in ragged pulls. Their fists were still clenched on the altar, and when they pried them open, the palms were printed with the pattern of the stone, four red lines across each like ruled columns on a page.

They stayed there a long time, letting the shaking run its course, letting the fury arrive underneath it—because the fury did arrive, rising slow and total, flooding every channel the fear had carved.

The god had not gone silent. The god had been *silenced*, and bound, and billed, and then blamed for the quiet, and Rafael had built a faith on the wreckage of that injustice and called it devotion. And worse—the guild, the Gilded Thorn, Sorrel's beautiful ledgers, Rafael's own careful hands—worse, they were the *distribution network*. The Court wrote the contracts; the guilds moved the goods. Bottled grief, borrowed years, names not yet spoken. Forty years of Rafael's meticulous, honest, oath-bound work, feeding souls into a machine that had muzzled a god to keep itself alive.

*Ilsa,* Rafael thought. *The date. When was the soul sold against when was she born.*

The god had signed for a soul that did not exist yet. Which meant the entry was flawed. Which meant—if a clerk's logic held anywhere, even here, even against a Court of contract-devils—the debt was *voidable*. Not payable. Not negotiable. Voidable, if someone with access to the records could prove the flaw.

Someone with access. Someone who kept the books.

Rafael rose from the stone on legs that had gone numb and walked out of the shrine into the blue dark, and the city looked different to them now, thinner, as though they could see through it to the script underneath. Every shopfront a counter. Every neighbor a debtor or creditor. The whole world a set of subsidiary ledgers rolling up into one master book beneath a mountain, and somewhere in that master book, a forged line bearing a god's name.

At the corner by the tannery they stopped, and looked back once at the little doorless shrine, and made it formal, because they were who they were:

*Account opened,* they thought toward the dark inside it. *Investigation pending. I'll need the archives, the old volumes, the maps of Ceren. I'll need things the guild won't give me willingly.*

*I'll find your error, lord. And then we're going to talk about restitution—both directions.*

The cold air did not answer. But for the first time in eleven years, the silence felt less like absence than like someone leaning close, listening hard, waiting for the next entry.

Rafael pulled their coat tight and walked home through the waning-hour streets, already composing the requisition in their head: *maps of Mount Ceren, geological surveys, the old expedition folios from the vault.* Materials any senior accountant could request for audit purposes. Innocent. Documented.

Which meant stealing them from a vault that never slept, in a house where the guildmaster missed nothing, with a feral child hidden in the storeroom and hunters' patience wearing thin.

There would be a price for it. There was always a price.

Rafael smiled, thin and joyless, into the dark.

*Let the Court bill me,* they thought. *I know a god who'll countersign.*
