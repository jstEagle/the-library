## 8. Maren's Bargain

The map room of the Gilded Thorn occupied the third floor of the guildhouse, a long narrow chamber with slatted shutters and the permanent smell of lamp oil and old vellum. It was Maren's kingdom. No one entered without her leave, and no one had entered after midnight in forty years, because no one else was fool enough to want to.

Rafael stood in the dark between the atlas shelves, holding a candle stub they hadn't lit yet, and told themselves—again—that this was not theft.

It was retrieval. The maps were guild property. Rafael was guild. Sorrel had said so once, years ago, with her hand heavy on their shoulder: *Everything here is yours as much as mine.* She'd meant it as a kindness. Most of her cruelties arrived dressed as kindnesses too.

The problem was that Rafael knew exactly how theft worked. They had spent eleven years cataloguing it. Theft had a texture. A specific weight in the hands, like a coin you hadn't earned. And the rolled survey charts tucked under their arm—the ones showing the passes above Mount Ceren, the old pilgrim roads, the salt route through the Thornwood—had that weight. Heavy as sin. Lighter than a child's life, but sin didn't care about comparisons.

The lock on Maren's desk was a three-lever mechanism, older than the guildhouse itself, and Rafael had watched her open it four hundred times without ever appearing to watch. That was the accountant's gift: to see everything while seeming to look at nothing. The levers gave. The drawer slid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay what they needed—not just the surveys but Maren's own annotated copy of the *Ceren Ascent*, a book so rare the guild kept it under the same seal as the soul-ledgers. Her marginalia crawled up every margin in her small furious hand.

Rafael took both. Closed the drawer. Re-locked it by feel in the dark.

"You're doing the lever sequence wrong," said a voice from the doorway. "Third lever before second. You'll strip the pin."

Rafael did not jump. Eleven years of ledgers had burned the flinch out of them. But their heart dropped through the floor and kept going, down through the cellar, down past the cellar, into whatever dark place waited beneath the Gilded Thorn where all stolen things eventually came home to roost.

Maren stood in the doorway with a lamp in one hand and a half-eaten apple in the other. She was sixty if she was a day, thin as a stylus, with ink stains on her fingers so deep they looked like bruises. Her eyes went from Rafael's face to the roll of charts under their arm with the unhurried precision of someone appraising inventory.

"Well," she said. "Either you've developed a sudden passion for cartography at this hour, or I finally get to find out what you've been doing since the child arrived."

"Maren—"

"Don't." She bit the apple. Chewed. "Don't do the thing where you explain why I'm wrong. I've read your forged entries, Nystrom. All of them. Chapter and verse."

The word *forged* landed like a blade between the ribs. Rafael set the candle on the desk, slowly, the way you move when you've realized the room is full of something invisible and armed. "I don't know what you mean."

"Entry forty-one, the ninth. A 'provisioning commission' for a mission that doesn't exist in any mission log I keep—which is all of them, because I keep everything. Two hundred crowns drawn against the reserve fund with Sorrel's countersignature, which is very good, by the way, better than Sorrel's own hand some days. Except Sorrel was in Vell's Crossing on the ninth. I have her expense receipts. She signed nothing." Maren took another bite and spoke around it, conversational as gossip over tea. "You wrote two hundred crowns out of the guild's accounts into the dark, and then you winced when you sealed the wax. I saw the wince. Forgers always wince. Oath-bound scribes wince hardest."

*Ink and iron,* Rafael thought. *She has no idea how right she is.* Every false line they'd written had gone through them like a fishhook, dragging. The oaths woven into their writing punished each lie with a small precise agony, and they had been lying for six nights now.

"What are you going to do?" Rafael asked. There was no point in anything else. Not pleading, not charm. Maren had spent a lifetime around people whose entire trade was making lies beautiful; she was immune.

Maren set down the apple. Crossed to the window and opened the slats, letting in the grey pre-dawn and the sight of the city below, still asleep, smoke rising straight from ten thousand chimneys. "That depends," she said, "entirely on whether you say yes to my counteroffer."

"I'm listening."

"Passage." She turned. In the lamplight her face was stripped of its usual dry irony, and what was underneath startled Rafael—not hunger exactly, but something adjacent to it, the way a starving man looks at bread without reaching. "You're going to Ceren. Don't insult me by denying it. The maps alone tell the story, and the forged funds, and the fact that you asked Hesper in the stable about horses that can handle scree. You're taking the child to the mountain, or away from it, or into it—I don't know which, and frankly I don't care yet. What I care about is that you're going where the First Tongue comes from, and there is not a scholar alive who wouldn't sell a limb for that, and I am not alive in quite the ordinary way. I died years ago and got up and kept reading. So. Passage. Full participation. Access to whatever we find. And in exchange—" she held up one ink-blackened finger— "I don't take what I know to Sorrel."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the guild's night porter coughed.

"You'd blackmail me," Rafael said.

"I'd *contract* with you." Maren's mouth twitched. "There's a difference. Blackmail is theft. This is commerce. You of all people should appreciate the distinction—you've made an entire religion of it."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "Very little is. Ask the child."

Rafael looked at her for a long moment. Then they pulled out the chair at Maren's desk—the chair with the worn arms, the chair she'd sat in for decades—and sat down in it, heavily, feeling the absurdity of negotiating terms while caught red-handed inside another person's locked room.

"Why?" they asked. "Not the scholarship. The rest of it. Why help? You could report me and collect whatever favor Sorrel pays for loyalty these days. You could stay here, warm and safe, with your books."

"Safe." Maren laughed, short and unamused. "Nystrom, I confirmed the child speaks the First Tongue. Do you understand what that means? Not roughly. Precisely. It means reality has a draft copy, and it's sleeping in our attic, and everyone who knows what she is will come for her—Sorrel, the brokers under the mountain, every debt-collector between here and the sea. There is no safe anymore. There's only near and far. Far is better." She picked up the apple again and gestured with it, a professor's gesture, punctuating air. "Besides. You think I haven't noticed what you noticed? The ledger page. Forty years old. Sealed with the Court's mark. I filed it myself, back when I was young enough to still sleep well. I've wondered about that page for four decades. You want to go dig up the answer. I want to come along and write it down."

"The answer might kill us."

"Then I'll die informed," Maren said, "which is more than most people manage."

* * *

They talked until the sun came up.

It went easier than Rafael expected, mostly because Maren negotiated like she read—fast, thorough, skipping nothing. She wanted a formal entry in Rafael's private ledger, witnessed and signed: passage, protection, and first access to any texts recovered. She wanted veto power over burning anything. When Rafael hesitated on that last clause, she fixed them with a look.

"I know what you're thinking. That somewhere on that mountain there'll be a book so dangerous it has to burn. Maybe. But you'll want to burn things out of fear, Nystrom, and fear burns indiscriminately. Nothing gets destroyed without I've read it first. Even if it costs us."

"And if reading it costs us?"

"Then we'll have paid for knowledge at a fair price," she said. "Which is more than the Court can claim."

Rafael wrote the contract themselves, in their own hand, on Maren's good paper. The strange thing—the thing they would remember later, in darker rooms on the mountain—was how it felt to write. True words, freely given, terms agreed by both parties: the pen moved like water. No hooks, no drag, no punishment threading up their arm. After six nights of forgery, writing honestly felt like setting down a weight they'd forgotten they carried.

Maren signed beneath them, then studied Rafael's face across the desk with those flat scholarly eyes.

"It hurts," she said. "Doesn't it. When you falsify."

"Yes."

"How much longer can you sustain it?"

"I don't know." Rafael capped the ink. "Long enough. It has to be."

"Mm." She gathered the apple core, the lamp, the cold tea. At the door she paused. "One more thing. The child—Ilsa. Does she eat?"

The question was so ordinary it took Rafael a beat. "She eats. Barely. Like a bird."

"She hums at night. Did you know that? Third floor carries sound. Same seven notes, over and over." Maren's voice had gone quiet, almost gentle, which was somehow more alarming than the blackmail. "Those aren't a song, Nystrom. I transcribed them. They're conjugations. She's dreaming in grammar older than the mountain. Whatever she is, she isn't feral, and she isn't a vessel, whatever the old page says. She's a person standing in the middle of something enormous, and she's the only one of us who can hear it coming." She opened the door. "Get some sleep. We leave within the week, before Sorrel's patience runs out. I'll handle the horses."

"Maren."

She stopped.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She didn't turn around. "Just be worth it."

* * *

Rafael slept two hours and dreamed of nothing, which was its own mercy. When they woke, the light was late-afternoon gold, and the guildhouse was loud with ordinary noise—someone arguing in the counting room, the cook singing off-key, boots on stairs. The sounds of a home. Rafael lay still and let them wash over them, knowing, with the clarity that comes after a decision is truly made, that they were hearing all of it for the last time.

Downstairs, the guild was preparing for evening trade. Bottled grief gleamed in its case like dark honey. A client wept quietly at the counter over a jar of borrowed courage. Sorrel's voice drifted from her study, warm and low, soothing someone, selling something.

Rafael paused outside her door. Through the crack, they could see her at her desk, silver-haired, magnificent, turning a small object over in her fingers—a debt-token, black as a beetle's back. Even at this distance, even without touching it, Rafael could feel the pull of the thing. The same pull that lived now in the token hidden in their own coat lining, tugging south, toward the mountain.

Sorrel looked up. Their eyes met through the gap in the door.

For one suspended moment, neither of them moved. Sorrel's expression did the thing it always did—the warmth arriving a half-second after the assessment, the merchant's arithmetic running underneath the love like water under ice.

"There you are," she said. "I've been looking for you. Come in. Close the door."

And behind Rafael, at the top of the stairs, soft as falling snow, he heard Ilsa begin to hum.
