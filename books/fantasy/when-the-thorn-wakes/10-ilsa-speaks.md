## 10. Ilsa Speaks

The girl had been with us nine days before she said a word I could understand.

Nine days of the First Tongue pouring out of her in her sleep, in her waking, in the space between—little syllables that made Maren's hands shake when she transcribed them, made the ink in my ledgers crawl like something alive and unhappy. Nine days of Sorrel asking questions with a smile on her face and a knife behind it. *How is she settling? Is she eating? What did she say last night?* And me answering each one with a lie so small it barely qualified, learning that lying was like swallowing glass: it went down fine if you didn't think about the edges.

I had taken to sitting with Ilsa in the evenings, after the counting-house emptied. Not because it was wise. Because she looked at me.

Everyone else in the Gilded Thorn looked at her the way you look at a strongbox that might be trapped—at the value inside, at what it would cost to open. Sorrel looked at her like inventory. Maren looked at her like a manuscript. The enforcers didn't look at all; they'd been told not to.

But Ilsa looked at *me*, and there was recognition in it, and recognition is the most dangerous thing a person can give you when you've spent your whole life being careful not to be known.

So I sat with her. In the storeroom off the counting-floor where we kept the bottled griefs—the ones nobody bought anymore because grief went out of fashion the way everything does—and I did my evening reconciliation while she sat on a crate among the shelves, humming in a language older than debt.

That ninth night, she stopped humming.

I felt it before I heard it. The way you feel weather change. The air in the storeroom went still and attentive, the way it does at Veth's shrine, and every bottle on the shelves seemed to lean toward us.

"You smell like his promises."

Common tongue. A child's voice, thin and cracked from disuse, saying words no child should have cause to say. I set down my pen very carefully, because my hand had begun to shake and I did not want to blot the page. There were rules about blotted pages. There are rules about everything, when your god is an accountant.

"Whose promises?" I asked.

She considered me with those eyes that were too old for her face—not ancient, nothing so simple as ancient, but *unspent*. Eyes that hadn't decided yet what they were for.

"The quiet one's," she said. "The one who doesn't talk anymore." She wrinkled her nose, and for a moment she was just a child again, disgusted by a smell. "They're all over you. Under your skin. Like ink."

My oath-mark sits over my heart, a thorn wound closed in silver scar tissue, given to me the day I swore myself to Veth twenty years ago. It ached suddenly, deep and rhythmic, like a second pulse.

"You know him," I said. "Veth. You know who he is."

"I know what he tastes like." She hugged her knees. She was still in the borrowed wool dress Maren had found her, sleeves too long, bare feet tucked up—she refused shoes, always, even on stone floors in winter. "Promises taste like cold iron and someone crying far away. Did you know that? Everything has a taste if you're grown around it."

"Grown around it," I repeated. My voice came out flat. Accountant's voice. The voice I use when the numbers are bad and I need them to hold still while I look at them.

She nodded, matter-of-fact, terrible. "That's what I am. A growing-around. They put the seed in before I was borned, and then I grew, and the seed grew, and now there's two of us in here and only one of them is me." She tapped her own chest, twice, gently, the way you'd knock on a door you hoped wasn't home. "He doesn't talk much. The other one. He just remembers."

*Remembered what?*

I asked it aloud. She cocked her head, listening to something I couldn't hear—or maybe something I could, and had spent twenty years calling silence.

"He remembers being big," she said. "Bigger than the mountain. Bigger than the sky part where the birds argue. He remembers wanting to—" Her face scrunched, working at translation, at concepts arriving in her mouth already worn smooth by forty centuries of dark. "Wanting to let everyone stop owing. And then they cut him. And the piece that got cut fell for a long time, and then it landed in a mother, and the mother grew me around it, and when I came out I was already sold."

The words landed in the room like coins dropped down a well. I heard them go down and down and never hit bottom.

"Already sold," I said.

"Mm." She picked at a loose thread on her sleeve. "Before I had a name, I had a buyer. That's why I don't got a name, really. Ilsa is just the shape of the hole where one should be." She said it without self-pity, which was somehow infinitely worse. Children don't mourn themselves until adults teach them how, and nobody had taught her, and so she spoke her own erasure the way she'd report the weather.

I thought of the sealed page in the old ledger. Forty years ago. Payment recorded in Veth's own hand. A soul sold to the Hollow Court beneath Mount Ceren—and the buyer's mark that matched the birthmark over this child's heart, the thorn-shaped shadow I'd seen when Maren bathed her fever-wracked body three nights past.

Except it wasn't a match, was it. That was the thing I'd been circling for days without letting myself land. The mark on the page and the mark on her weren't two copies of one seal. They were two halves of one wound.

"What's your buyer going to do with you?" I asked. "When they get you?"

"They're not getting me," she said, with a flash of ferocity so sudden and so fierce that I nearly laughed, nearly cried. "Maren says I'm hidden. Maren says the door has three locks." Then the ferocity guttered out, because children cannot hold anger the way adults can; it burns through them and leaves them blinking. "But I hear the walls talking. Walls always talk. These ones say somebody under the mountain is hungry, and hungry things send people looking."

The bottles on the shelf nearest her fogged, faintly, as though breathing.

I closed my ledger. Some entries shouldn't be witnessed.

"Ilsa," I said. "Look at me." She did, and I found I didn't know what to do with being looked at, so I said the truest thing I had: "Nobody is selling you. Not while I'm keeping the books."

"That's a promise," she said immediately. "You just made a promise. I can taste it."

"And?"

"It tastes good." She smiled—a real smile, gap-toothed, six or seven years old, the first one she'd given anyone in the guild. Then it faded into something more careful. "But promises are how they got him. Somebody promised him everybody could stop owing, and then they wrote it down wrong on purpose, and the writing-down is what caught him. Words are teeth here." She pointed at my ledger. "Your book bites people. I can feel it from here."

I had sworn oaths on that book for twenty years. I had recorded griefs and years and names-not-yet-spoken, and told myself it was honest work, clean work, work that kept accounts balanced so the world wouldn't tip. And a barefoot child was telling me, in the mild tone of someone reporting that supper was ready, that my life's instrument was a jaw.

"Yes," I said. "It does."

"You could write different things in it," she offered. "If you wanted. Ink listens to whoever holds the pen. That's the first rule. Before kingdoms. Before gods. Ink listens."

*Maren would trade a finger for that sentence.* I filed it away and hated myself for filing it, for the reflex of acquisition, of turning even this—even her—into inventory in my head.

"It's late," I said. "You should sleep."

She slid off the crate but didn't leave. She stood there among the bottled griefs, small and grave, and reached out and touched my hand—two fingers, light as snow landing.

"When they come for me," she said, "don't be brave. Brave gets people caught. Be like ink. Go where you're not supposed to."

Then she padded out of the storeroom, barefoot on cold stone, humming again in the First Tongue, and I sat alone among the griefs with my heart hammering a rhythm I hadn't heard since I was young enough to be afraid of the dark for ordinary reasons.

*Born already sold. A vessel grown around a stolen soul.*

I had spent nine days thinking of her as collateral—a debt walking around in a child's body, a liability to be managed. Sitting there in the dark, I finally understood the difference between a concept and a girl, and the difference was that a girl knocks twice on your chest when she makes you promise, and a girl refuses shoes, and a girl knows exactly what she is because everyone else made sure of it before she could talk.

And the walls had talked. *Hungry things send people looking.*

I blew out the lamp and went to find Maren, because tomorrow we would need maps of Mount Ceren, and I had a feeling the ink was about to start listening to me whether I was ready or not.

* * *

I did not find Maren.

I found Sorrel instead, standing at the end of the corridor outside the counting-house with a lamp held high, and the light caught her face in a way that made it impossible to read—which, with Sorrel, meant she wanted it that way.

"Late night," she said pleasantly.

"Auditing," I said. Second lie. The glass went down easier this time. That frightened me more than anything the child had said.

"Mm." She turned the lamp slightly, and the light swung across the storeroom doorway behind me, across the shelved griefs, and settled on my ledger under my arm. "You know, Rafael, I've been thinking about our girl. About what a fortune she'd fetch, if a fortune were the point. And about what else she might be worth, if it isn't." Her smile was warm as ever. It always was. That was the horror of Sorrel; warmth was simply the material she was made of, the way other people were made of bone. "Sleep well."

She walked away down the corridor, lamp swaying, and I stood in the dark doing arithmetic I didn't want to do: how many hours since the broker's visit, how many miles to Mount Ceren, how long a message takes to travel ahead of you.

Ink listens, she'd said.

That night, alone in my room, I opened the ledger to a fresh page and wrote nothing at all. Just held the pen above it, and listened.

Somewhere beneath the city, beneath the mountain, beneath everything, something that remembered being bigger than the sky was waiting to hear what I'd write next.

I dipped the pen.

I began to plan a theft.
