## 12. The Salt Road

The road to Ceren was paved with salt.

Not metaphor—actual salt, crushed and raked flat by centuries of feet, so that on dry days the whole track gleamed white as bone under the sun, and when it rained the ground wept brine that stung any cut or blister you carried. Maren said it was runoff from the mountain's mines, that the Court's brokers had been pulling salt out of Ceren since before anyone thought to ask why salt. Ilsa just walked along the white line of it, barefoot despite my protests, and said the road tasted like remembering.

I had stopped asking how she knew things. It saved time and spared me answers I didn't want.

We had been three days out of the city when we started passing other travelers. Not merchants—merchants travel in wagons with their eyes on their goods. These people walked light, most of them, walking-light the way you do when you've sold what would have weighed you down. They moved in loose strings of two and five, heads bent against the wind off the plains, all drifting in the same slow tide toward the dark shoulder of Mount Ceren on the horizon.

"Pilgrims," Maren called them, and there was something wrong in how gently she said it.

"They're not pilgrims," I said. "Pilgrims go to pray."

"What do you call someone who walks a hundred miles to sell God a piece of themselves?"

I didn't have an answer. That was becoming a habit on this road, and I hated every instance of it.

The first one who spoke to us was a mason with dust in the creases of his knuckles. He fell into step beside me outside a waystation whose roof had been stripped for firewood years ago, and he talked the way lonely men talk—easily, gratefully, without being asked. His name was Doss. He'd been walking nine days.

"First love," he told me, apropos of nothing, apropos of everything. "That's what I'm carrying up. Well—" he laughed, and the laugh had a hole in it, "—carrying the memory of it, anyway. The feeling itself went three years ago. Paid the winter's coal with that. This is just the shape it left behind."

"You sold the feeling?" Maren asked, and I heard the linguist in her voice, hungry, cataloguing.

"Feeling goes cheap. Memory holds its price." He shrugged. "Broker at the foot station explained it like this: a memory's a thing. You can hand over a thing. A feeling's just weather. Can't sign for weather." He looked at me then, really looked, taking in the ledger-satchel at my hip, the ink stains I could never fully scrub from the side of my right hand. "You're guild," he said. Not a question.

"I keep accounts," I said.

"For the Thorn."

"For the Thorn."

He nodded slowly, and something in his face closed—not anger, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man confirming which family owned the house he'd been evicted from. "Then you know the forms better than I do. Tell me if I'm getting a fair rate. Nine days' walk, first love's memory, and they're offering remission of a debt of forty crowns plus interest accrued." He recited the figures the way you recite a prayer you no longer believe in but can't stop mouthing. "Is that fair?"

Every instinct I had said walk away. Every oath I'd broken to get onto this road said the same. But Ilsa had gone quiet beside me, watching him with those too-old eyes, and Maren was looking at me, and the honest answer was already halfway up my throat where it had apparently been waiting my whole life.

"No," I said. "The standard schedule values a first-love memory at sixty against principal, with interest forgiven as a signing courtesy. Whoever quoted you forty is pocketing twenty crowns of your past."

Doss stared at me. Then he sat down on the salt-white verge, very carefully, like a man setting down something fragile, and put his face in his hands.

"I knew it," he said into his palms. "I told Marta it felt short. She said don't be greedy, Rafe, it's charity, they're forgiving the debt at all." He looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and dry—a man who'd done his crying earlier, on some other stretch of road, and had none left to spare. "Marta. My wife. That's—I still have that one. Her name. Her face. Sunday mornings." He ticked them off on his fingers like inventory, and I recognized the gesture because I made it every day of my working life. "They took our harvest debt and turned it into this. Two bad seasons. That's all it was. Two bad seasons and a loan against next year's crop, and now I'm walking to a mountain to sell the only summer I ever loved."

Ilsa crouched down in front of him. She did this sometimes—approached griefing strangers with the directness of an animal and none of an animal's fear. She studied his face for a long moment and said, in her careful, accented common speech:

"It will not hurt when it is gone. That is the trap. You will not miss it. You will only be a man who is slightly less, standing in a house that is slightly emptier, and you will not know why the kitchen makes you sad."

Doss looked at her like she'd struck him. "How old are you, child?"

"Older than the contract," she said, and stood, and walked on, and left him holding that.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a ruined tollhouse, and I got out my ledgers because not getting them out had become impossible somewhere in the last week. My hands needed the work. The work was the only part of me that still knew what it was.

Except now the work had teeth.

I recorded Doss—not as a client, the guild would never see him, but in the private book I'd started keeping, the one written in my own cipher, the one that was turning into a confession. *Doss, mason, of Harrow Ford. Sells first-love memory to Court broker at foot station. Quoted 40 crowns against 60 fair value. Wife: Marta.* And then, because the pen seemed to move on its own: *He asked me if it was fair. He asked me because I was the first person in nine days who looked like he might know.*

Maren watched me write from across the fire. "You're keeping accounts of them."

"Somebody should."

"The guild keeps accounts of everyone. That's rather the point of a guild."

"This isn't the guild's book." I turned the page so she couldn't read it, though she couldn't have anyway; the cipher was mine alone. "This is the other kind. The kind that gets read aloud someday, by somebody, to somebody's face."

She considered that. Firelight moved in her spectacles. "In the First Tongue," she said, changing the subject the way scholars do when a subject cuts too close, "there's no word for 'debt.' Did you know that? I've been through every fragment Ilsa's given us. There's 'gift,' there's 'return,' there's 'owing-back,' which is more like a river flowing to its source. But debt—obligation held over a person, a weight you carry—the language doesn't have it. Either they never needed the concept." She paused. "Or it came later. After the Court started writing."

"After the Court started writing," I said. "I'd bet the ledger on it."

From the darkness beyond the firelight, Ilsa hummed. She did that at night now, more and more—little runs of melody in no scale Maren could transcribe, and lately Maren had taken to humming back, and the two of them would trade phrases like women calling across a valley. I lay back on my bedroll and listened to the sound of a dead god's language being sung over a salt road by a stolen child, and I thought about Doss's kitchen making him sad, and I slept badly.

* * *

The woman I interviewed properly—we'll call her that, interviewed, because the alternative word was *processed* and I refused it—came to us on the fifth day, at a shrine-shelter where the road bent around a dead orchard.

Her name was Vess. She was maybe fifty, weathered brown as saddle leather, traveling with a boy of nineteen who hovered at her elbow like a second shadow. They had a hand-cart between them loaded with nothing—literally nothing, an empty cart, which told me everything before she opened her mouth. People going up the mountain empty-handed were people whose cargo was internal.

"You're Thorn," she said. Not hostile. Weary, in the specific way of a customer approaching the counter of the shop that owns her.

"I was. I am. It's complicated."

"Everything's complicated. That's why they charge by the complication." She sat down heavily on the cart's rail. "My son here says you told a man at the last station he was being cheated. Forty against sixty. Word travels on the salt road faster than feet. So I want to ask you straight, accountant, and I want the true answer, not the kind answer, because the kind answer's what got me into this." She folded her hands. "I'm going up to sell my memory of my daughter. Fair rate or not?"

Behind her, the boy flinched. She didn't turn around, but she reached back without looking and found his wrist and held it, and I understood: he was the daughter's twin, or close enough. He was the reason she couldn't afford to keep it. Grief shared between two survivors doubles instead of halving; the memory of the dead sister lived in both of them, and every time one grieved, the other drowned. He'd begged her to sell hers so that he wouldn't have to watch her die of it by inches.

I did the arithmetic. It was reflexive, obscene, instant—the numbers assembled themselves in my head before my conscience could get a hand on them. *Memory of a child, primary-bond category, grief-saturated: high intrinsic value, the Court pays premium for saturated grief because it keeps well, bottles clean.* The Guild's own schedules priced such a memory at a hundred and twenty crowns against principal, plus forgiveness of ancillary obligations, plus—if the seller was savvy—a residual clause, a right of visitation, the ability to pay the memory back and reclaim it within ten years.

Nobody told the pilgrims about the residual clause. Residuals were how the brokers made their actual money: buy a soul's furniture cheap, rent it back forever.

"Fair rate," I said, "is a hundred and twenty against your principal, interest forgiven, and a residual clause—ten years, your right to buy it back at cost. If they offer you less than a hundred, they're robbing you. If they don't mention the residual, they're planning to own it outright and lease it back to grieving families as bottled comfort. That's the Thorn's best-selling inventory, madam. Bottled grief. We buy saturated memories at the mountain's foot for pennies and sell them in the capital to rich mourners who want to feel something authentic, and the margin would make you sick, and I wrote those margin reports for eleven years."

The silence after that lasted a long time. Wind moved through the dead orchard, and the branches clicked together like counting beads.

"You hate it," Vess said finally. Not a question either. Everyone on this road had stopped asking questions and started making observations; it was a road that taught you the difference.

"I'm good at it," I said. "Those aren't the same thing, but they live in the same house, and after a while you can't tell which one answered the door."

She nodded slowly, and then she did the thing I will remember until whatever's left of me stops remembering anything: she pulled a folded paper from inside her coat and handed it to me. A contract draft. Broker-standard form, the Court's seal stamped in wax that was black and faintly warm, as if the document had a pulse. "Read it," she said. "Tell me what they're not saying. You're the only one in a hundred miles who can."

And here is the moral vertigo, laid out plain: I read it. Line by line, clause by clause, the way I'd read ten thousand contracts, fluently, expertly, *lovingly*, and I found the poison in under a minute. Clause nine waived her residual. Clause fourteen assigned the Court rights to "derivative works"—meaning any memory adjacent to the sold one, meaning her son, meaning every future Christmas and birthday that touched the wound. Clause seventeen was the worst: silent assignment of her obligation to *any party the Court designates*, which is the hook they use to reel sellers into service afterward. Half the brokers' porters on that road were former clients, working off debts that had been quietly transferred into labor.

I told her all of it. I rewrote the clauses in the margin in my own hand—crossed out nine, amended fourteen, struck seventeen entire—and I signed the amendment as a guild accountant of record, which I had no authority to do anymore, which made it forgery, which made it the second forgery of my career in a week, and my oath-mark burned like a brand pressed fresh to my forearm while I did it, Veth's old sigil reminding me what I'd sworn and to whom and how far I'd already fallen from it.

Vess took the paper back and looked at my signature for a long time.

"They'll honor this?" she asked.

"The seal's valid. They care about form more than substance—it's their religion. A properly executed amendment binds them harder than their own greed." I hesitated. "One more thing. When they take it—when you're coming back down the mountain—you won't remember stopping to talk to us. You won't remember that a stranger fought for your clauses. Whatever's left will just feel like luck. I need you to decide now whether that's acceptable, because I can't fix it, and neither can you, and pretending otherwise is the kindest lie I could tell you."

She looked at her son. Her son looked at the ground. Somewhere behind us Ilsa was watching with her head tilted, and I could hear—not literally, but truly—that she was listening to something underneath Vess's words, some frequency below speech.

"Acceptable," Vess said at last. "It has to be. There's no version of this where I keep her and keep living. That's what nobody tells you about grief on an empty stomach. It's not noble. It's just heavy. And heavy things get sold." She stood, and shook my hand with a grip like a gate latch, and paused. "What was your name, accountant?"

And here my tongue betrayed me beautifully, because I opened my mouth to say *Rafael Nystrom* and what came out was: "No one's. I'm the no-one who reads the fine print."

She laughed—one dry bark, surprised out of her. "Then the no-one has my thanks." She took up the cart handles. The boy took the other side. They went on up the white road toward the dark mountain, shrinking by degrees, until they were a smudge, and then not even that.

Ilsa appeared at my elbow. She had picked a stone off the salt road, one of the grey ones from beneath the white crust, and she pressed it into my hand and closed my fingers over it.

"For the book," she said.

"It's a rock, Ilsa."

"It's a *witness*. Everything the road takes leaves a little of itself behind. That one belonged to her walk." She looked up at me. "You smell like his promises again. Stronger today. You're doing what he used to do, before they chained him. Reading for people who can't read. Standing in the door."

"And look where standing in the door got him."

"He's not angry," she said simply. "He's *proud*. Those are different smells." Then she wandered off to bother Maren about grammar, leaving me holding a stone that was warm on one side, in the shape of a grip, as if someone had been carrying it a long time.

I put it in the satchel with the ledgers. Of course I did. I was, whatever else had changed, still a keeper of accounts.

* * *

That night the wind shifted and came down off the mountain, and it smelled wrong—cold iron and hot wax, the scent of the broker's visit to our guildhall, the face-that-didn't-fit. I stood the middle watch because sleep had become a country I could visit but not live in, and I watched the road south, back the way we'd come, and twice I saw lights moving on it. Lanterns, traveling fast. Traveling in formation—three forward, two flank, one hanging back. That wasn't pilgrims. Pilgrims don't march.

Sorrel's enforcers moved exactly like that. I'd trained half of them myself, back in the mercenary years, before the oath, before the ledgers, before I'd decided that keeping accounts was a gentler way to hold a blade.

"Maren," I said quietly, and she woke the way scholars wake—completely, instantly, mid-thought. "Get Ilsa up. We're crossing the river at first light instead of noon."

She looked at the lanterns, and understood, and didn't waste breath on questions. Good. I loved her a little for that.

Below us, on the white road, six lights came on steadily through the dark—patient, unhurried, certain that distance and night were on their side.

They didn't know yet that I'd spent eleven years learning exactly how to make a ledger balance against violence. By morning, one way or another, the books would show it.
