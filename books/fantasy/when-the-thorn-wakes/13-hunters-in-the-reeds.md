## 13. Hunters in the Reeds

The river came on them at dusk, wide and brown and patient, the way rivers are when they have been carrying things to their ends for longer than anyone remembers.

Rafael smelled trouble before they saw it. Not smoke, not steel—something subtler. The absence of birdsong in a stretch of reeds that should have been loud with it. Twenty years of mercenary work had taught them that silence was never empty. Silence was full. Silence was men holding their breath.

They stopped at the top of the bank and pretended to study the crossing while their eyes did other work. A ferry house, shuttered. A rope strung across the water for a flat barge, half-sunk and listing. Reeds tall as a man on both banks, going gold in the last light. And there—a glint where no glint belonged, upstream among the cattails, like the edge of something polished catching the sun and being quickly sorry about it.

"Three," Rafael murmured. "Maybe four. Upstream, in the reeds."

Maren followed their gaze and her mouth went dry-looking. "Sorrel's?"

"Sorrel's doesn't leave reeds bent like that. Sorrel's people are professionals." Rafael's voice came out flat, which was how it always went when the old self started climbing up out of its grave. "These are just well-paid."

Ilsa stood between them with her hand in Maren's, watching the water with an expression far too old for her face. She'd been quiet all day. Quiet in the way of someone listening to something underneath everything else.

"They're waiting for dark," she said, in her careful common tongue, each word set down like a stone on ice.

"Probably," Rafael agreed.

"The tall one is afraid."

Rafael looked at her. "You can tell that from here?"

"He smells like fear smells." She said it without embarrassment, the way children say terrible things, because they haven't yet learned that terrible things require ceremony. "Like wet iron."

Maren made a small sound in her throat. "We could go around. There's a bridge two days north—"

"A bridge is a chokepoint. This is a choice." Rafael was already unbuckling the satchel, checking what they carried, hands moving with a competence that felt like reading letters written by a stranger who shared their name. Rope. Flint. The debt-token warm in its oilcloth wrapping, tugging gently southward toward Ceren like a hound against a leash. And at the bottom of the bag, wrapped in three layers of cloth because Rafael could not bring themselves to throw it away and could not bear to look at it: a short sword, guild-stamped, uncleaned since the day they'd sworn never to draw it again.

Seven years. Seven years since the oath at Veth's shrine, when Rafael had laid down violence the way a drunk lays down drink—one final time, meaning it, knowing they wouldn't mean it forever. *No more blood by my hand,* they had sworn, and the silence of the god had come down over them like snowfall, and they had believed, truly believed, that silence was acceptance.

Now they wondered if it had been warning.

* * *

They crossed at full dark, because Rafael decided that if the ambush was coming anyway, it should come on ground they chose.

The plan was simple enough to explain to a child, which it needed to be, because Ilsa refused to stay behind and Rafael had learned that arguing with Ilsa was like arguing with weather. Maren would take the girl into the ferry house and bar the door. Rafael would wade the shallows downstream, circle through the reeds, and do—whatever the moment required. That was the phrase they used, even in their own head. *Whatever the moment required.* It sounded like flexibility. It was actually cowardice with better posture.

The water came up cold past their knees, then their waist. The current pulled with the lazy strength of something that didn't need to hurry. Rafael moved slowly, feeling with each step, breathing through the mouth the way the old sergeant had drilled into them a lifetime ago—*breathe like you're bored, Nystrom, fear has a smell and everything in the dark hunts by it.*

That was the thing about old training. It didn't fade. It just waited, coiled in the muscle memory, patient as debt.

Fifteen meters from shore, Rafael heard it: a whisper of reed against leather, off to the left. Then another, closer. They were flanking the crossing too—which meant they expected the travelers to be foolish, or desperate, or both. Fair enough. Rafael had been both their whole life.

The first man stepped out of the reeds ten feet away and stopped, because he'd seen the wake Rafael's body made and understood his mistake a half-second too late.

He was young. That was the detail that would come back later, in the hours when Rafael lay awake doing arithmetic they couldn't balance. Young, maybe twenty-two, with a wispy attempt at a beard and a crossbow held wrong for close work. His eyes went wide. He opened his mouth—

—and Rafael was already moving.

It wasn't a decision. That was the horror of it, the thing they would spend the rest of the night refusing to look at directly. There was no decision, no weighing, no prayer to a silent god for guidance. There was simply the oldest arithmetic in the world, running itself: *he sees me, seen men call out, called-out men mean dead companions.* The knife came off their belt as if it had always been in their hand. Two steps closed the distance. The blade went in under the jaw, angled up, quick and clean and horribly kind, the way you kill a man whose death you don't want him to have time to consider.

He folded into the water. Rafael caught him before the splash, lowered him into the reeds, took the crossbow.

And then they stood there in the black river with blood thinning off their knuckles and felt absolutely nothing, and the nothing was worse than grief would have been. Grief meant some part of you still objected. This was the self they had buried rising up and putting itself on like a coat that still fit perfectly, and the fit was the obscenity. Seven years of ledgers and ink-stained fingers and careful invisibility, seven years of being someone who moved numbers instead of bodies—and the body remembered everything. The body had been waiting.

*One,* said the old counting. *Where's two?*

Two came out of the reeds on their right with a sword already swinging, a big man, bearded, competent—the professional Rafael had predicted and not found. The crossbow was useless at this range so Rafael threw it at his face, which bought exactly the half-second required, and then it was blades and breath and the ugly close geometry of killing, and Rafael was inside his guard before the man's weight settled, elbow to the throat, knee, knife twice into the thigh and once under the ribs, efficient as bookkeeping. The man made a sound like a question. Then he made no sound at all.

Rafael lowered him beside the boy. Two of them, side by side in the reeds, and somewhere in the world there were people who would wait for these men and never learn what the river knew.

*Stop,* Rafael told themselves. *This is the part where you stop.*

But the counting wasn't finished. Somewhere upstream, near the ferry house, a third man was moving toward Maren and Ilsa, and the fourth—the tall one Ilsa had smelled the fear on—was presumably with him, and every heartbeat Rafael spent standing in the water mourning was a heartbeat spent spending other people's lives. So they waded ashore wringing the river out of their sleeves and ran along the bank with the knife low and wet, and hated themselves with a hatred so clean and familiar it felt almost like comfort.

* * *

The third man died at the ferry house door, and his death was uglier because Rafael arrived angry.

Angry at Sorrel, who had sent these men. Angry at the Court beneath the mountain, which had made Sorrel into whatever she now was. Angry at Veth, silent in his chains, silent even now, silent while children were hunted and old women wept over sold memories and a god's own soul grew up barefoot in the snow. Angry most of all at the version of themselves who had believed an oath could fence off what they were.

The man heard them coming—Rafael wanted him to hear, wanted the fight fair, wanted something—and turned with a billhook raised, and the exchange lasted four seconds and cost Rafael a long burn of steel across the forearm before the knife found the gap under his arm. He went down against the doorframe and slid, leaving a dark stripe on the wood, and his face in the lantern light spilling through the shutter slats was ordinary, tired, a man who took contracts because winter was coming.

"I'm sorry," Rafael told him, and meant it, and knew the meaning changed nothing.

Inside, Maren had Ilsa behind a stack of salt barrels, a boat-hook gripped white-knuckled in both hands. When Rafael shouldered the door open she nearly swung it, and the look on her face when she saw who it was—relief collapsing immediately into alarm at the state of them—took years off her and added them somewhere else.

"You're bleeding."

"It's shallow." It was not nothing; the forearm burned with every flex. But it would keep. "Fourth man?"

"Gone." Maren pointed at the window. "I saw him run. Before it even started—he just ran."

Rafael closed their eyes briefly. A runner meant a report, and a report meant Sorrel would know within days exactly where they'd crossed, and the next party would not be four hired men in the reeds. The next party would be enforcers. The next party would be personal.

"Then we don't sleep here," Rafael said. "We take the barge."

"The barge is sunk."

"It's grounded, not holed—I checked at dusk. We bail it out, float it on the morning tide, and ride the current south. Rivers don't leave tracks." Rafael tore a strip from their shirt, wrapped the forearm one-handed, tied it with their teeth. Old motions. Mercenary motions. Each one landed like a key turning in a lock they'd sworn shut.

Ilsa emerged from behind the barrels. She looked at the blood on Rafael's sleeve, then up at their face, with that unnerving directness she had, as though she could read the ledger of a person the way Rafael read the guild's books.

"You killed them," she said.

"Yes."

"For us."

"Yes."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she reached up and took Rafael's wounded hand in both of hers, small and warm, and held it the way you hold a thing that might break.

"In my language," she said, "there isn't a word for what you did. Only words for what it costs."

Rafael's throat tightened. "What does it cost?"

"I don't know yet." She released their hand. "But the river does now. It keeps things."

* * *

They worked through the night. Maren bailed; Rafael patched the hull seams with pitch from the ferryman's shed and cursed softly in three languages. The bodies stayed in the reeds. Rafael could not make themselves bury strangers they'd killed, and could not make themselves leave without at least turning their faces skyward, and in the end did neither, and carried that failure out onto the water with everything else.

Near dawn, exhausted, they sat in the stern of the rising barge while Maren slept propped against the mast and Ilsa dozed against Maren's shoulder, and Rafael finally did the thing they had been avoiding since the first man folded into the water.

They prayed.

Not the formal prayers of the shrine—those had gone out of them somewhere on the Salt Road, worn smooth like coins passed too many times. Just words, raw and private, aimed downward through the water, through the stone, through however many miles of dark it took to reach the cellar where a god's voice sat in jars.

*I broke the oath. First blood in seven years. Three men. Do you hear me? Do you even—*

The silence answered. Of course it did. The silence was the only thing Veth had ever given anyone, and Rafael had spent years telling themselves it was peace.

But tonight, rocking on the brown river with blood drying under their fingernails and a child's words still lodged in their chest—*the river keeps things*—the silence felt different. It felt less like absence and more like attention. Like a listener holding very still.

And beneath the silence, faint as ink showing through cheap paper, Rafael thought they felt the debt-token warming against their breastbone. Not pulling toward Ceren the way it always did.

Pulling faster.

As if something down there in the dark had noticed the blood on the water, and the oath broken, and the old self risen—and was leaning forward.

The dawn came gray. The barge floated. Rafael took the pole and pushed them out into the current, southward, toward the mountain, carrying three deaths and a wound and a question they could no longer outrun:

If Veth had heard every prayer these seven years—then what else had the silent god been keeping count of?

Behind them, in the reeds, the river kept its things. Ahead, the current ran swift and dark, and it was running the same direction they were.
