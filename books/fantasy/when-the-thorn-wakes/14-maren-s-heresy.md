## 14. Maren's Heresy

Maren had spent forty years learning dead languages, and she had a private taxonomy for them. There were languages that died because their people died—plague, sword, famine, the usual. There were languages that died because they were murdered—conquered tongues strangled by imperial grammar. And there were languages that died because they were never meant to be spoken at all.

She'd always assumed the third category was empty. A scholar's fancy. The idea that some words were too structurally true for mouths to hold, like trying to pronounce a proof.

Then Ilsa started talking in her sleep.

Maren heard it first on the fourth night out from the city, in the lean-to they'd rigged against the rain on the Salt Road. She was awake because she was always awake—the old insomnia that came with age and with the particular guilt of a woman who had spent her life reading other people's secrets. Across the fire, the child lay curled against Rafael's bedroll, and her lips were moving.

The sounds were wrong. Not ugly-wrong. Wrong the way a perfectly tuned string is wrong when it plays a note you didn't know existed. Maren felt each syllable land somewhere behind her sternum, and the hair on her arms stood up, and she thought—with the cold clarity of the genuinely frightened—that the child was not speaking *a* language.

She was speaking *the* language.

* * *

By morning Maren had done what she always did with an obsession: she fed it until it stopped being able to hurt her. She sat Ilsa down on a flat stone by the road, produced a stub of charcoal and the back of a supply manifest, and said, "Say it again."

Ilsa looked at Rafael. Rafael looked at Maren with the expression of a man handing over a knife handle-first, which, Maren reflected, was more or less what this was.

"She won't hurt you," Maren said. "Words can't hurt you."

That, it would turn out, was the single least true sentence of her career.

Ilsa spoke. Slowly at first, then faster, the way children do when they stop performing and start playing. Maren transcribed phonetically, then broke the sounds into units, then began the work that would consume her for six days: finding the pattern underneath.

Here is what she found, and here is why she did not sleep again until the town that forgot:

The First Tongue had no tense. That was the first thing. Every verb root existed in a kind of permanent now, and time was added not by conjugation but by a particle that functioned like—she searched for the word for three hours before giving up—not like a marker of time but like a marker of *commitment*. You didn't say "the river flowed." You said "river" and then you said whether you were standing inside that statement or outside it, observing it, or—this was the part that made her hands shake—*drafting* it.

There was a grammatical mood for authorship.

No human tongue had a mood for authorship. Why would it? People don't write the world. But the First Tongue treated "is" as something you could choose to assert, the way a clerk chooses ink over pencil. And there were inflections—Ilsa produced them without knowing what they were—for *binding*, for *sealing*, for *witness*. Whole families of suffixes whose only function was to make a statement harder to unsay.

Maren sat with her charcoal notes spread across her knees and understood, with the slow vertigo of a woman standing at the edge of a shaft, that she was not looking at a language used to describe reality.

She was looking at the language reality was drafted in.

* * *

"You need to eat," Rafael said, on the fifth day, setting bread beside her elbow.

"In a moment."

"You said that yesterday. And the day before. You've eaten twice since the reeds." He crouched next to her, and up close she could see the toll the road was taking on him: the gray under his eyes, the way his hand kept going to the ledger he still carried even now, even fleeing, like a priest carrying relics out of a burning church. "What have you got?"

"I don't know how to explain it without sounding mad."

"Maren. I keep accounts for a guild that sells bottled grief. My madness threshold is generous."

So she told him. She laid out the charcoal sheets in order and walked him through it, the tenses that weren't tenses, the mood of authorship, the binding-suffixes. She showed him how Ilsa's nonsense-ramblings weren't nonsense—they were declensions, conjugations, a complete grammar running underneath the surface noise like water under ice.

"And?" Rafael said. "It's old. Old things are just old."

"It's not old. It's *prior*." She tapped the page. "Every word in this language has force built into its structure. When we speak, we describe. When this language speaks—" she stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and she wasn't sure she wanted it to be real yet. But Rafael was watching her with those patient accountant's eyes, waiting for the figure at the bottom of the column, so she gave it to him. "When this language speaks, it *enacts*. It doesn't record contracts. It *is* the contract. The words don't point at the agreement. The words are where the agreement lives."

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. Then: "The ledgers."

"What?"

"The Thorn's ledgers. The oldest volumes. Sorrel never let me read past the founding entries, but I've handled them. The script isn't any alphabet I know. I assumed cipher." His voice had gone careful, the way it went careful around dangerous inventory. "You're telling me it might be this."

"I'm telling you it might be descended from this. A dialect. A degraded copy." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, high and thin. "A merchant's pidgin off the draft of the world."

"And the Hollow Court?"

And there it was. The question she'd been circling for two days, the one she'd hoped Rafael wouldn't ask because answering it meant admitting what she actually believed.

"I think," Maren said slowly, "that the Court didn't learn this language. I think the Court is where it's been *kept*. Think about what we know. Contracts that bind gods. Debts that survive death. Souls held as collateral for forty years without decaying. None of that works under natural law. All of it works if the agreements are written in the drafting-language—if the contract doesn't describe your obligation but literally constitutes it. You couldn't break such a contract any more than you could break your own bones by wishing."

"So the Court writes in the First Tongue."

"I think the Court *is* writing. I think beneath that mountain there is a text, or texts, or—" she groped for it—"a continuous act of writing, centuries long, and everything the Court does is a stroke of the pen. We aren't being hunted by brokers, Rafael. We're being edited."

* * *

That night Ilsa had a fever dream, and Maren, sitting up with her, heard the child use a construction she hadn't encountered before: a long compound verb-form, layered with binding-suffixes three deep, wrapped around a single name.

Veth.

Maren wrote it down. Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady; it was one of the few facts about herself she had ever liked. But her heart was going like a bird in a box, because she recognized the structure now, having lived inside the grammar for six days. It was a covenant-form. An oath-shape. And the meaning, as far as she could parse it through a child's fevered simplification, was something like:

*the-one-who-spoke-and-it-held, silenced-by-his-own-signature.*

Silenced by his own signature.

She stared at the transcription until the charcoal blurred. Because that was impossible. Gods didn't sign. Gods *were*—they preceded grammar, they predated obligation, they were the raw material the drafting-language worked upon. That was theology 101, the first thing every student learned: the divine stands outside the text.

But if Veth had signed something—if the silent god had once put his own name in the binding-mood—

Then he was inside the text. Bound by it. Subject to it like any mortal debtor, and unable to speak himself free, because speech itself was the medium of his chains.

"Oh," Maren said aloud, to the dark, to nobody. "Oh, you poor magnificent fool."

* * *

She should have stopped there. She knew that even then, in the way you know a ladder is rotten while you're still climbing it. There is a line in scholarship, an unwritten rule older than any guild law: some questions are safe to ask because answers exist, and some questions are safe because no answer exists, and the dangerous ones are the questions where an answer exists but was buried on purpose. Maren had spent her whole life teaching students to tell the difference. She had written a monograph on it, for pity's sake. *On the Ethics of Recovery.* She'd argued, in print, that certain texts deserved their obscurity.

On the seventh night she took out her notes, all of them, every charcoal sheet, and she began to compose a sentence.

Just one. A test. Something harmless—she chose it carefully, the most benign construction the grammar allowed, a simple declarative with no binding-suffixes at all: *the fire burns.*

She spoke it in the First Tongue, using Ilsa's pronunciation, checked three times against her transcriptions.

The fire leaned toward her.

Not much. A finger's width of flame bending against the wind, like a dog hearing its name. Then it righted itself, and the night went on, and nothing else happened, and Maren sat absolutely still for a very long time with her heart slamming and tears standing in her eyes—not from fear, though the fear came later, and stayed.

From awe. Pure, ruinous, professional awe. Forty years among corpses of languages, and she had just heard one answer.

Then the fear arrived, exactly on schedule, wearing her own monograph's argument as a mask. *Some texts deserve their obscurity.* Because if she—a deaf old woman with charcoal and insomnia—could bend a flame with one unbound sentence, then what had the Hollow Court written with ten thousand years and infinite patience? What was waiting under Mount Ceren, drafted and sealed and *witnessed*, in a grammar whose every structure was a lock?

And worse, the question under the question, the one she would carry like a stone from here to the mountain and beyond: if reality was a draft, then who held the pen? And what happened to the world when someone started writing in it who shouldn't?

* * *

In the morning she burned half her notes.

Not all. She wasn't a saint, and she'd be lying to herself forever if she claimed otherwise. She burned the test-sentence and its transcription. She burned the covenant-form around Veth's name—because that was the most dangerous thing she owned, a loaded weapon disguised as linguistics, and she could feel it wanting to be used. She kept the grammar. Of course she kept the grammar. She was Maren; the grammar was the first interesting thing in thirty years, and she was not strong enough to burn it, and she suspected, dimly, that she was going to need it before this road ended.

Ilsa watched her feed the pages to the cookfire with the grave attention children give to incomprehensible adult rituals.

"Why?" the girl asked. In common speech—her common speech was coming fast now, the way a flood comes fast.

"Because knowing things is safe," Maren said. "Using them isn't."

Ilsa considered this. Then she said something in the First Tongue, one short sentence, unbound, gentle—and Maren felt it land in her chest like a warm hand.

She didn't translate it. She couldn't have; her hands had gone unsteady for the second time in her life. But she understood it anyway, the way you understand a mother's tone across a crowded room, and the meaning was: *you're afraid of the wrong thing.*

Later, when Rafael asked what the child had said, Maren lied to him. First lie she'd told him in eleven years of friendship, and it came easily, which frightened her more than anything under the mountain could.

"She said the fire was pretty."

Rafael nodded and went back to the ledger, and Maren sat with her surviving notes hidden against her ribs, and looked south, toward the smudge of Mount Ceren on the horizon, and thought about pens, and who was holding them.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told Rafael. Here was the heresy, the actual one, the one that would get her burned in any sane century: she had parsed enough of the grammar now to recognize what Ilsa's ramblings really were. Not fragments of memory. Not echoes of a stolen soul.

Dictation.

The child was reciting. Somewhere far below them, something was writing—and Ilsa, born already sold, grown around a stolen soul like pearl around grit, was reading it aloud in her sleep.

And the passage she'd been reciting for seven nights, the one Maren had finally decoded at dawn, the one now folded against her heart where the burned pages used to be, was dated.

Three days hence. Beneath the mountain. A signature line, left blank, awaiting a name.

The First Tongue had no future tense. But it had something crueler: a mood for inevitability. And whatever waited under Ceren had drafted the next three days in it already.

Maren looked at Rafael's bent head, at Ilsa humming softly over her breakfast, and began, quietly, to plan how to change a text that did not believe in change.
