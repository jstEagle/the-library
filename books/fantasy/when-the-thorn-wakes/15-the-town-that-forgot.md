## 15. The Town That Forgot

The village announced itself by its silence.

Not emptiness—Rafael had walked through empty places before, burned keeps and plague towns where the quiet had a texture like ash. This was different. Ashford-in-the-Vale, if the crooked sign at the crossroads could be believed, was full of people. Smoke rose from chimneys in a dozen thin columns. A dog trotted down the lane with the self-important air of a dog on an errand. Laundry hung between windows, snapping in the cold wind off the hills.

And nobody spoke.

They nodded as the three travelers passed. They smiled—the smiles of people who remembered that smiling was done, the way you remember a dance step from childhood without remembering the music. A woman beating a rug paused to wave. An old man mending a fence touched his cap. But their mouths stayed closed, and when Rafael met their eyes he saw something that made his ledger-keeper's soul flinch: nothing behind them. Not vacancy, exactly. Worse. Rooms swept clean and furnished with the memory of furniture.

"They've all got it," Maren murmured, falling into step beside him. She'd been talking since dawn—Ilsa's night-songs transcribed by firelight, her theories piling up like snow against a door—but now her voice dropped to the hush she saved for texts too old to argue with. "The look. I've read about this. Famine villages, forty years back, maybe fifty. The whole eastern vale starved one winter."

"I know the story," Rafael said. "Grain failed. The Court's brokers came through offering terms."

"Everyone knows the story." Maren's laugh was dry as chalk. "Nobody remembers the *terms*."

Ilsa had gone still. She stood in the middle of the lane with the wind pulling at her hood, barefoot on frozen mud because no boot they bought her would stay on, and she was looking at the village the way Rafael looked at a ledger page that wouldn't balance. Her lips moved. The sounds that came out were soft and round and wrong for a child's mouth—vowels that seemed to bend the air around them, consonants like stones dropping into deep water. The First Tongue. She spoke it the way other children breathed: constantly, unconsciously, and mostly to herself.

But now she wasn't speaking to herself. Now she was looking at a woman standing in a doorway—a young woman, hands flour-white, staring out at the road with that swept-clean smile—and Ilsa said something long and low, and the woman's face changed.

Only for a moment. Only a flicker, like a candle guttering in a draft. The smile cracked along some seam Rafael couldn't see, and behind it, for half a heartbeat, was grief. Vast, unsheltered, animal grief. Then the woman blinked, smoothed her apron, and went back inside, and the smile settled over her face again like a lid over a well.

"What did she say?" Rafael asked. "What did you say?"

Ilsa looked up at him. Her eyes were gray as slate and older than any child's eyes had a right to be, and when she answered it wasn't in the First Tongue. It was in the small hoard of common words she'd begun to spend carefully, one at a time, like coins she didn't trust.

"Empty," she said. Then, after a struggle, pressing her small fist against her own chest: "Full of empty."

* * *

They took a room above the village's only inn, which was called the Shepherd's Rest and was warm and clean and staffed by a landlord who communicated entirely in gestures and a kind of cheerful humming. That was another thing Rafael noticed as the evening wore on: the humming. Everyone hummed. The barmaid hummed as she poured. Two farmers by the fire hummed at each other over a board game, laughing soundlessly when one of them won. It was as if speech itself had been part of the price—as if they had sold not just a day but the voice that belonged to it, or perhaps they simply had so little left to say that they filled the silence with melody instead.

Maren sat by the window with her notes spread around her like fallen leaves, writing by the last gray light. "It was called the Tithe Winter," she said without looking up. "Before your time. Before mine. The famine hit the whole vale, but here—" she tapped the page— "here it hit hardest. Crop blight, then the early frost, then the roads washing out. By midwinter they were boiling bark."

"And the brokers came."

"The brokers always come." She finally looked up, and there was something raw in her scholar's face that Rafael hadn't seen before. Something almost afraid. "Here's what the chronicles don't record, because the chronicles were written by people who could pay in coin instead. The Court doesn't buy memories wholesale. It buys *days*. One day, taken whole, roots and all. You don't lose the fact of the thing—you keep knowing it happened somewhere, the way you know a story. But you lose the living of it. The weight of it. The morning-after of it."

Rafael turned his cup slowly on the table. "Why a day? Why not take everything? They could. We both know they could."

"Because a whole life is worth more than one day," Maren said. "And because one day is enough." She held his eyes. "You've audited the guild books, Rafael. You've seen bottled grief. Have you ever wondered why grief keeps? Why it doesn't spoil in the glass?"

He hadn't wondered. He had catalogued it—*grief, bottled, vintage various; handle with care; do not shake*—the way he catalogued everything, and moved on to the next line. That was the craft. That was the oath. You recorded what was, not what it meant. Veth demanded accuracy, and accuracy was a mercy, because meaning was where the bleeding started.

"It keeps," Rafael said slowly, "because it's already been lived. Grief is just love with nowhere to go. It doesn't need the day anymore. The day can be cut away and the grief stays ripe."

Maren nodded once, grimly, and went back to her notes.

Upstairs, through the floorboards, Rafael could hear Ilsa humming along with the village below.

* * *

The truth came out the way truth does in forgotten places: sideways, in fragments, from the person least able to carry it.

Her name was Bess. Rafael found her the next morning in the churchyard—or what had been a churchyard, though the church itself was a roofless shell of blackened stone, abandoned even before the famine, its bell long since sold. Bess was kneeling in the grass, pulling weeds from around a row of small stones. She did this, Rafael guessed, every morning. He watched her work for a while before approaching, and noticed that she weeded with great tenderness and no particular pattern, tending stones at random, missing some entirely, returning to others she had already cleaned.

There were eleven small stones.

He counted twice to be sure. Eleven graves, each no longer than his forearm. Each marked with a name worn nearly smooth, and dates that clustered within a single season of one terrible year.

"Good morning," Rafael said.

Bess looked up and smiled her swept-clean smile. "Morning," she said, and seemed faintly surprised to hear her own voice, as if she'd found a coin in a coat pocket.

"You tend these graves."

"I do," she agreed, pleased. "Someone has to."

Rafael crouched beside her, slow, unhurried, the way you approach any creature that might bolt. "Whose are they? Do you remember?"

Bess frowned—not in pain, that was the terrible thing, but in mild puzzlement, the frown of a woman asked to recall where she'd left her spectacles. "Children," she said. "From the Tithe Winter. Everyone knows about the Tithe Winter. Terrible year. We sold..." Her brow creased deeper. "We sold what we needed to sell."

"A day," Rafael said gently. "The Court bought a day from each of you."

"Yes! That's it exactly. One day. Fair terms, all things considered." She said it with real warmth, with gratitude, even—the rehearsed gratitude of someone who has been told many times how lucky she was. "We'd have died otherwise. All of us. The brokers were very kind, under the circumstances. Very professional."

"Which day did they take?"

Bess opened her mouth. Closed it. Her smile flickered—that same seam showing, that same guttering candle—and Rafael saw her grip her own knee hard enough to whiten her knuckles, chasing something down through the swept rooms of herself and finding every door locked.

"I don't..." she began, and stopped. Started again. "It was a day. In the winter. There was—" Her voice thinned. "There was singing, I think. Or maybe that's the humming. We hum now. Did I mention we hum?" She laughed, bright and awful. "We hum because—"

She stopped.

She looked at the eleven small stones.

And Rafael watched it happen: the moment the arithmetic caught up with her. Not the memory—the memory was gone, sold, corked in some jar beneath a mountain—but the *shape* left behind, the negative space where the day had been cut out. Eleven graves. One season. A woman who weeded them every morning without knowing why her hands shook.

"Oh," Bess said.

Very quietly. Like a door closing very far away.

Then her face smoothed over again, the lid settling back onto the well, and she smiled at Rafael with perfect, vacant kindness and said, "Lovely weather we're having," and went back to her weeds, and Rafael had to walk to the far side of the churchyard and stand with his hand pressed against the cold church wall until he could breathe properly.

He was an accountant. He had spent twenty years recording the prices of things. He had written entries for bottled grief and borrowed years and names not yet spoken, and he had told himself, every night, kneeling at his travel-shrine with his palms flat on the wood, that the ledger only recorded—that the guilt belonged to the buyers and the sellers, and Veth's silence was a silence of neutrality, and numbers were clean.

Eleven stones. One day. *Fair terms, all things considered.*

He thought of the entry he would write, because he was who he was and his hands itched for the pen even now: *Ashford-in-the-Vale. Population, three hundred and six. Debts held by Hollow Court, one per household, matured. Assets remaining: all.* And beneath it, in the margin, where honest accountants note what the figures conceal: *they do not know what they paid. they will never know. they weed the same graves every morning and cannot tell you whose they are.*

He thought of Sorrel teaching him the trade in the back room of the Gilded Thorn, her hand warm on his shoulder: *The ledger never lies, little bird. That's what makes us honest. Everyone else is selling stories.*

The ledger never lied. It just stood there, immaculate, while the world bled.

Behind him, small feet crunched across frost-killed grass.

* * *

Ilsa knelt among the eleven stones, and she wept.

Not the way children weep—not for hunger or fear or a scraped knee. This was something else. She knelt in the frozen grass with her bare feet tucked under her, her small hands flat on the earth, and the sound that came out of her was low and rising and structured, syllable folding into syllable, and Rafael understood with a jolt that she was not crying. She was *speaking*. She was weeping in the First Tongue, grief rendered directly into the language reality was drafted in, and the air around the graves was doing things air should not do. Frost bloomed outward from her knees in spirals. The worn names on the stones darkened, as if wetted, as if the carved letters were drinking.

Maren appeared at Rafael's shoulder, notebook forgotten, hanging open in her hands. "She's naming them," Maren whispered. "Look. Look at the stones."

The letters were filling in. Not changing—*restoring*, as though the weathering of fifty years were running backward. Rafael read the nearest stone as clarity spread through it: **TAM, BELOVED, THREE WINTERS**. Beside it: **LILY AND ROSE, TWINS**. Another: **JORY**. Another. Another. Eleven names surfacing like drowned things rising to be counted.

"She's giving them back," Rafael said. His voice came out wrecked. "Their names. The village forgot the day but the stones kept—"

"No." Maren's whisper was fierce. "Don't you see? The stones kept the names, but names are nothing without the day. A name on a stone is just a word. What Ilsa is doing—" Maren's breath caught as the First Tongue rose toward some crest, as every chimney in Ashford began, unconsciously, to hum along, hundreds of voices finding a melody they didn't know they knew— "what she's doing is *mourning*. Properly. In the grammar grief was originally written in. The Court can buy a day out of a human heart, but the First Tongue doesn't recognize the sale. As far as the language underneath everything is concerned, those children were loved yesterday."

The villagers had come. Rafael became aware of them gradually—first the landlord, then the barmaid, then the farmers, then dozens, the whole population drifting out of their houses and down the lanes with their hands empty and their faces turned toward the churchyard, humming. The swept rooms behind their eyes were rattling. Doors straining at locks. Bess stood at the churchyard gate with her mouth open and tears running freely down her face, and when Rafael reached her and asked, softly, "Do you know why you're crying?" she shook her head and laughed and sobbed at once and said:

"Not at all. Not at all. Isn't it wonderful?"

And it was, and it was unbearable, and Ilsa sang on among the graves with frost feathering her hair, mourning fifty years of unmourned dead in a tongue no living throat should know, and Rafael Nystrom—who had sworn an oath to a silent god, who had kept accounts for the traffic in impossible things, who had told himself for twenty years that the ledger only records—knelt down in the frozen grass beside a child born already sold, and wept too, in plain common words, because it was the only language he had.

* * *

They left at dusk. The villagers lined the road to see them off, humming, waving, smiling their lid-over-a-well smiles, and Bess pressed a loaf of bread into Rafael's hands and said, "Come back in spring. Bring the little one. I feel—" she touched her breastbone, frowning at her own fingers— "I feel like I owe her something. Isn't that strange?"

"No," Rafael said. "That's the truest thing anyone's said to me all year."

Three miles east, in the last light, Maren finally broke her silence. "I've finished the transcription. Last night's song, and today's. The lament for the eleven." She hugged the notebook to her chest like a shield. "Rafael, the grammar is consistent. Across every song, every fragment, every word she speaks in her sleep—it's all *one system*. A complete language. And a complete language means a complete set of rules, and rules mean contracts can be *drafted* in it, not just read."

"I know," Rafael said.

"You don't understand. If the Court writes its bargains in this tongue—if that's what contract-stone *is*—then whoever speaks it fluently isn't carrying a child who knows secrets." Maren's voice dropped. "They're carrying a pen. A pen that can rewrite the terms."

Rafael looked back down the road, at the last smoke of Ashford rising straight and thin into the darkening sky, at a village full of people who had paid a fair price and would spend the rest of their lives humming over graves they couldn't grieve.

Behind them, far back along the salt road, a lantern swung in the dark. Then two. Then four—riding in formation, moving fast, closing the distance they'd gained at the river crossing days ago.

Sorrel's riders. Coming for the pen.
