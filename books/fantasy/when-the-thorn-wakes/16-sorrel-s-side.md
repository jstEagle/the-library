## 16. Sorrel's Side

Sorrel found the forgery on a Tuesday, over cold tea, the way she found most things that would ruin her.

She had not meant to audit Rafael's books. That was the joke of it, the small cruel joke she would carry to whatever grave the Court eventually dug her: she had opened the ledger out of habit, the same way she'd opened ledgers for forty years, and there it was on page three hundred and eleven, an entry so clean it practically confessed.

*A mission commissioned by the guildmaster. Provision of escort, cartographer's services, and road-writs for one Maren Voss, linguist, and party of two. Destination: Mount Ceren. Purpose: acquisition of pre-contractual texts.*

Sorrel had never commissioned any such mission.

She sat very still in her office above the counting floor, listening to the sounds of the guild going about its evening below—the clink of scales, the murmur of brokers closing their day-books, someone laughing in the stairwell. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a house that did not yet know it had been robbed.

The entry was good work. That was what stung first, before the fear, before the grief—professional admiration, sharp as a splinter. Rafael had matched her hand almost perfectly. Almost. The descenders on the *g* were a half-weight too light, and the date had been written left to right in a single unbroken stroke instead of the careful two-stroke four she'd taught them herself twenty years ago, back when Rafael's hands shook too badly to hold a pen and she'd had to curl their fingers around it one at a time.

*Two-stroke fours,* she thought. *I taught you everything I know about lying on paper, and you still got the fours wrong.*

She turned the pages backward with the flat of a letter knife, not touching the ink. There were others. A debt forgiven that hadn't been—a vintner in Lowmarsh who owed the Thorn eleven years of his life, wiped from the book as if he'd paid it. A standing withdrawal against the guild's reserve of bottled grief, drawn down in amounts just small enough not to trip the weekly reconciliation. Road-writs. Stable fees. Bribe-money to the gate watch, dated three nights ago.

Three nights ago. They were already gone.

Sorrel set the knife down and looked at her own reflection in the dark window, and what she felt—not what she showed, because she'd forgotten how to feel anything without showing it to no one, but what she *felt*—was pride.

That was the horror of it. Her first honest emotion, reading the proof that her best, her only, the child she'd pulled half-drowned out of the Sallow gutter with lice in their hair and a mercenary's dead eyes, had committed the one unforgivable sin of the Gilded Thorn and done it beautifully: her first feeling was pride.

Her second was envy.

Because Rafael had done the thing she had never been able to do. Rafael had picked up a pen and written against the guild, against the Court, against the whole vast machinery of debt, and walked out the door into the snow. Sorrel had been writing in the Court's favor for thirty years and had never once had the courage to stop.

She poured the cold tea out. Then she went downstairs to find her hunters.

* * *

They gathered in the map room, the five of them she trusted least and used most: Brannoc with his scarred knuckles, the twins Isla and Isolde—who had named themselves after each other as a joke and now could not remember which came first—quiet Pell, and young Corvin, who still flinched at loud noises and was therefore her best interrogator.

"I'll be plain," Sorrel said. "Nystrom has forged the books and fled south with the linguist and the girl."

No gasps. She'd chosen them for that too.

"The girl is Thorn property," Brannoc said. "Guild law says—"

"Guild law says nothing about this." She heard her own voice go flat, the voice she used when the truth was too expensive to tell whole. "What I need from you is not capture. Nystrom is not to be harmed. Do you understand me? Not a hair. If they fight, you withdraw. The linguist either. I want the girl brought to me alive and untouched, and if the choice comes between losing all three and touching one of them, you lose all three and come home to explain it to me."

Corvin frowned. "Then what are we actually doing?"

"Following." She spread the salt road map across the table and weighted the corners. "At a distance. You do not close. You send word by wren every second night, and you follow until I tell you to stop. That's all. That's the entire job."

Pell, who noticed things, was looking at her with those pale eyes of his. "And if someone else closes on them first?"

The room went quiet. Sorrel smoothed a crease in the map that wasn't there.

"Then you get to the girl before whoever it is does," she said, "and then you run."

* * *

Afterward she climbed the tower stairs to the reliquary room, which was not called a reliquary room, which was officially a records annex for sealed instruments, and which contained exactly one thing worth sealing.

The contract hung on the wall inside a frame of black iron, and it had hung there for thirty years, and in thirty years she had touched it perhaps a dozen times, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

It was written on skin. Her mother's, though she'd never been able to prove that to herself one way or the other, and had stopped trying around the tenth year. The language was First Tongue, which she could not read, but the terms had been explained to her slowly and kindly by the broker with the face that didn't quite fit, thirty years ago, in a cellar smelling of wet stone while her children slept upstairs:

*One future per child, held in trust. Two futures total. Interest accruing annually. In the event of default, collection may be made in kind.*

In kind. She had signed because the winter had been killing them—because Tomas's cough had gone green at the edges and little Wren had stopped growing, stopped even crying, and the broker had come to the door like an answer to a prayer she hadn't known she was making, offering bread and medicine and coal for the stove in exchange for something abstract. Futures. Who misses a future? You can't hold one. You can't watch it starve.

She had learned, since, exactly what a future weighs.

The interest had compounded quietly, the way interest does. By the fifteenth year the Court had begun asking small favors. Look the other way on this shipment. Record this debt in our favor. Keep your ears open, Guildmaster, for souls of unusual quality—and oh, by the way, should a child ever come to your door speaking the old tongue, we would consider it a matter of great mutual interest.

Thirty years of small favors. That was how the Court bought people. Not with knives, not with threats—with patience, and arithmetic, and the slow accumulation of a person's compromises until the pile was taller than the person and they couldn't climb over it anymore. She sometimes wondered whether Rafael suspected. Whether, all those nights bent over ledgers together, her hand guiding theirs through the grammar of obligation, they had ever noticed that the guildmaster always wrote certain entries facing away from the light.

Probably not. Rafael had a gift for not seeing what survival required them not to see. It was why she'd loved them. It was why loving them had been so useful to the Court, and she had known that too, and let it be useful, and hated herself in the specific efficient way she'd gotten very good at, the way you get good at anything you practice daily for thirty years.

Now Rafael had run straight toward the mountain with the one soul the Court wanted more than anything it had wanted in centuries, and Sorrel stood alone in a room with her mother's skin on the wall and understood, with perfect clarity, what she was going to do.

She was going to warn the Court.

Not capture. Not betray Rafael to the hunters—she'd already made sure her hunters were hounds without teeth. But the Court would learn anyway; the broker had eyes everywhere, and if the Court learned that the Gilded Thorn had known and withheld, then the contract on the wall would move from trust to default, and collection in kind meant her children. Tomas, who was thirty-one now and had a daughter of his own, a fat-cheeked solemn baby named after nobody. Wren, who had survived that winter and grown up strange and gentle and was teaching himself the lute in some city Sorrel sent money to and never visited, because visiting meant questions, and questions meant the Court noticing him.

Default meant futures collected. She had seen a future collected once, early on, shown as a courtesy, the way you're shown the instruments before the surgery. A man in his forties who simply began to fade—not die, fade, his choices narrowing one by one, the woman he loved forgetting him, the job he excelled at dissolving, until he was a gray smudge of a person sitting in a doorway, waiting for nothing. The broker had said, *You see how gently we collect.* And the worst part, the part that lived under her ribs like a swallowed stone, was that it had been gentle. That was the Court's real genius. Not cruelty. Gentleness, weaponized. Kindness with compound interest.

So: warning. She could frame it as diligence. She would frame it as diligence, and the broker would smile its ill-fitting smile, and somewhere beneath Mount Ceren an entry would be made in a book older than kingdoms, and Sorrel Ashgrave, guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn, would have sold out the only person she had ever loved purely, cleanly, without profit—for her children. For her children, who did not know she existed as anything but a name on letters containing money.

Rafael would understand, she told herself. That was the lie she chose, out of all the lies available, because it fit best in the hollow where her conscience used to sit. Rafael, who kept accounts for impossible things, who knew better than anyone alive that every payment has a source—Rafael would read the ledger of her life someday, if they lived, and see the entry, and understand that love and betrayal were the same transaction recorded in different columns.

She didn't believe it. But she'd believed worse things for longer.

* * *

The message took her an hour to compose and four words to say.

She wrote it in trade-cant first, the guild's working cipher, then realized her hands were shaking and started over in plain script, because plain script could not be misread, and this needed to arrive intact. She did not write *they are coming to you*. She did not write *stop them*. She wrote what a diligent guildmaster would write to a valued counterparty, informing them of market movement:

*Inventory of interest has left my house, traveling the salt road toward Ceren. Party of three. My people shadowing at distance, under orders not to engage. Consider this notice given freely, in hope of continued good relations. —S.A.*

Freely. The word looked wrong on the page. Nothing in her life had been freely given in thirty years; everything carried freight, interest, a lien. She read the note six times, adjusted nothing, and sealed it with the thorn sigil.

There was a drop for Court correspondence behind the shrine of Saint Aldric in the Ropewalk, a loose votive brick that she had been feeding for three decades. She went herself, at the waning hour, wearing a porter's coat. The city was asleep under new snow. Her breath came out white and fast. At the shrine she knelt as if praying, worked the brick loose with two fingers, and slid the packet into the dark.

Her hands wouldn't leave the brick.

She stayed kneeling on the frozen ground with her fingers in the wall of the shrine, and she thought about Rafael at seven years old, feral and silent in the gutter outside the guildhouse, watching her with the eyes of something deciding whether she was predator or weather. She had crouched down and held out her hand and said, *I pay wages and feed daily, and I don't ask where you've been.* And Rafael had taken her hand, and that had been—she could admit it now, kneeling in the snow—that had been the last free thing she ever did. The Court had learned of the foundling within the month. Had noted it. Had filed it away, one more asset in the guildmaster's portfolio, one more thread in the web.

Maybe that was why Rafael had run. Maybe the child had smelled the mountain on her, all these years, the way animals smell weather.

She pushed the brick back into place and stood, and her knees cracked like green wood, and she laughed—one short ugly syllable that scared a cat off the shrine steps—because here was the sum of her: a woman who had sold her children's tomorrows to buy their todays, and then sold her soul's one friend to keep the purchase from being repossessed. Every column balanced. That was the discipline she'd taught Rafael, the first rule of the craft: *every column balances*. Nothing appears from nowhere; nothing vanishes without trace. Love, mercy, a child saved from a gutter—all of it entered somewhere, in some book, and drawn against later, with interest.

She walked home through the snow, and at the guildhouse door she stopped, because the night porter had left a lamp burning for her in the window the way they did, the small ordinary kindnesses of the house she'd built, and the light lay yellow on the snow, and for a moment she allowed herself to imagine a different ledger. One where she had burned the contract thirty years ago and paid whatever came. One where she'd torn the black-framed thing off its wall and fed it to the stove and told the Court to come collect, and let Tomas and Wren take their chances in an honest world rather than a safe one.

But the world wasn't honest. That was the first lesson of accounting and the last lesson of life, and she had learned both at the same desk.

Inside, she climbed to the reliquary room one more time. The contract hung in the dark, patient as stone. She stood before it and pressed her palm flat against the iron frame—she permitted herself that much, the metal, not the skin within—and she spoke to it the way she had never dared speak aloud in thirty years, quietly, in the voice she used for balancing books at midnight.

"When this is done," she said, "when the girl is delivered and the account is clear, I am finished. Whatever's left of me, I'm taking back. I don't care what it costs. I've paid enough to know the difference between a price and a punishment, and I'm done confusing them."

The contract said nothing. Contracts never do. That was the whole trick of them.

She took her hand off the iron, banked the lamp, and went to write the orders that would send her hunters down the salt road at dawn—slowly, at a shadow's distance, forever two days behind whatever was coming from beneath the mountain, moving faster, with better maps, and with thirty years of her own borrowed time riding at the front of it like a figurehead.

Two days, she calculated, watching the candle gutter. Perhaps three, if the passes were bad.

She blew out the flame and sat in the dark and began, for the first time in her life, to pray to a silent god.
