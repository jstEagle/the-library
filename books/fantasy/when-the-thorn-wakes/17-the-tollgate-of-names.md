## 17. The Tollgate of Names

Mount Ceren did not look like a mountain from up close. It looked like a decision that had never been unmade.

Rafael had seen the sketches in the guild's forbidden maps—Maren's stolen cartography, inked in a hand too steady for a woman who claimed she only wanted to *look*—and none of them had prepared them for the way the mountain simply stopped being geology and started being architecture halfway up its lower slopes. Stairs cut into basalt. Windows with no rooms behind them. Doorways sized for something taller than any living person, their lintels carved with script that made Rafael's teeth ache to glance at.

Ilsa walked between them with her hand in Rafael's, humming.

She had been humming since dawn. Not a tune—Rafael had tried, twice, to find the melody's repeat and failed both times. It was more like a key turning slowly in a very old lock. The debt-token in Rafael's pocket had gone warm three days ago and had not cooled since, tugging at the fabric like a fish on a line whenever they drifted off the road. Now, with the mountain filling half the sky, it pulled so insistently that Rafael had stopped pretending to navigate at all and simply let it lead.

"You're doing the face," Maren said. She walked behind them, burdened under her satchels, breath fogging in the thin cold air. "The one where you're counting things you don't want to count."

"I'm always counting things."

"Yes, but usually you enjoy it."

Rafael didn't answer. The truth was that they were counting—had been counting for hours—and every sum came out wrong. Days of travel: nine. Meals remaining: enough, if you counted generously. Names left: one.

That last figure kept snagging like a burr in wool.

They crested a shoulder of black rock as the sun went down behind them, and the Tollgate of Names stood before them in the last red light, exactly as ugly and inevitable as a bill presented at a funeral.

* * *

It was a wall across the entire throat of the pass. No gate in it—that was the first obscenity Rafael's accountant's eye registered. A tollgate with no gate, no portcullis, no way through or around, just a sheer barrier of grey stone forty feet high, running from cliff to cliff, seamless except for a single archway at its center. And in the archway, framed against whatever dimness lay beyond, sat the gatekeeper.

It was small. That was the second obscenity. Rafael had expected a giant, a troll, some monument of muscle and menace. Instead there was a figure the size of a child—smaller than Ilsa—seated on a stool of black wood, hands folded in its lap, wearing robes that might once have been fine and were now the color of old dishwater. Its face was smooth as an egg. No eyes. No mouth. Just a faint suggestion of features, like a sculpture someone had abandoned at the sketching stage, and yet Rafael felt, with absolute certainty, that it saw them. All of them. Every version of them, perhaps, going back to the gutter and forward to whatever waited under the mountain.

Above the archway, carved deep into the stone, ran words in the First Tongue. Rafael couldn't read them. But Ilsa could—Ilsa read them the way a bell reads being struck. Her humming stopped. Her small hand tightened around Rafael's fingers.

"What does it say?" Maren whispered.

Ilsa's voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a child reciting something grown-ups had told her never to recite. "*All who enter owe. All who enter pay. The coin is what you are called.*"

"Charming," Maren said. "And here I'd budgeted for gold."

Rafael stepped forward. Ten feet from the gatekeeper, the air changed—the way air changes over a grave, or inside a vault. It wasn't cold. It was *accounted*. Every particle seemed to have been weighed, entered into a column, approved by some authority older than authority. Rafael's oath stirred in response, the vow to Veth that lived somewhere beneath the breastbone, and for the first time since the shrine it felt less like a comfort and more like a rope around the waist, paying out slack toward something dark below.

"We seek passage," Rafael said. "Down. Into the Court."

The gatekeeper did not move. Did not breathe, so far as Rafael could tell. But the sense of attention sharpened to a point, and then—

"*Name.*"

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere. From the wall. From the stones underfoot. From inside Rafael's own skull, spoken in a register that made the ledger-ink of their soul want to crawl to the margins. One word, and it was not a question.

"We don't give our names," Rafael said carefully, "to strangers under mountains."

The egg-smooth face tilted, very slightly. Somewhere in the blankness of it, Rafael felt amusement arrive and depart, like a clerk noting a joke without laughing at it.

"*Name,*" it said again. "*Or turn back. The mountain does not bargain. The mountain only tallies.*"

Maren came up beside Rafael, satchels clinking. "I've read about this," she murmured, low, urgent. "Old pilgrim accounts. Third-hand, fourth-hand, mostly nonsense—but the consistent part is the toll. They say the gate takes your name. Your true name. Not what people call you. What your mother called you, before anyone else got a vote." She paused. "And they say you don't get it back. They say you walk down the mountain missing it, like a tooth, and you spend the rest of your life tonguing the gap."

"Cheerful reading, Maren."

"I skipped the parts about what happened to the ones who refused."

"And those are?"

"The accounts end." She adjusted her spectacles with a hand that wasn't entirely steady. "Which I take to be the answer."

Rafael looked back at Ilsa. She stood a few paces behind, snow-dusted, hollow-eyed, wrapped in a coat two sizes too large that Sorrel's own seamstress had made for her in another life, back when the guild was still home. She was looking at the gatekeeper with an expression Rafael had learned to dread over nine days of travel: recognition. The way you look at a door you've been through before, or a debt you've already signed.

"It wants our names," Rafael said to her, gently. "Do you understand?"

Ilsa nodded. Then, in her careful, flattened common tongue: "Mine is not mine to give."

Rafael's stomach dropped. "What do you mean?"

But Ilsa only pressed her lips together, and the humming started again, low, defensive, and Rafael understood that this was one ledger entry even the child wouldn't open. Not here. Not in front of the thing that tallied.

* * *

They argued about it, quietly, ten yards back down the pass while the light died.

"There has to be another way," Maren said. "There's always another way. There are forty chapters of guild precedent proving there's always another way—bribery, forgery, misdirection. You've falsified an entire guild mission, Rafael. Forge a name."

"That's the problem." Rafael crouched, drawing lines in the snow with a bare finger because their hands needed to do something. "You can't forge this. Think about what it's asking. Not a word. Not a label. It wants the true name—the one woven into me before I could choose anything. If I offer it a lie, it will know. And worse—" they hesitated, hating the shape of the thought, "—worse, if I *believed* the lie even a little, it would take the lie. It would peel away whatever part of me actually answers to it. I'd come down that mountain having lost something real anyway, and I wouldn't even know which piece."

"So we lose something real on purpose instead," Maren said bitterly. "Wonderful accounting."

"It's honest accounting. There's a difference." Rafael stood, brushed the snow from their knee, and found their eyes drifting back to the archway, to the small seated figure waiting with the patience of stone. "Besides. I've been thinking about what it costs all day. All week, if I'm honest. And I keep arriving at the same number."

"Which is?"

"A name my mother gave me." Rafael's voice came out rougher than intended. "She gave me two things in my whole life. That name, and a fever she caught nursing me through winter, that took her by spring. Everything else I have, I took, or traded for, or stole. The name is the only gift I ever carried that I didn't put a price on." They exhaled, watching the breath hang white in the dark. "Seems fitting that the mountain should ask for exactly that."

Maren said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its edge. "You know what happens after. In there. People forget themselves. Pilgrims' accounts talk about it—the ones who paid, wandering the galleries, trying to remember why they came. Some of them never leave. They just stay, reading the walls, looking for their names among the contracts, hoping someone wrote them down somewhere."

"Then I'll write myself down," Rafael said. "I'm good at ledgers. It'll be the first entry I've ever made in my own favor."

* * *

Rafael paid first.

They walked to the archway alone, boots crunching, the debt-token burning in their pocket like a coal, Veth's oath pulling taut beneath their ribs. Up close, the gatekeeper smelled of nothing at all, which was somehow worse than any smell. It raised its smooth face. It had no mouth, and still Rafael heard the smile in it when it spoke.

"*Name.*"

Rafael closed their eyes.

The name was there, of course. It had always been there, folded small in the deepest pocket of them, past *Nystrom*, past the gutter-name they'd worn at eight years old, past everything Sorrel had built on top of it. It was the sound their mother had made over a squalling infant in a room that smelled of tallow and snowmelt, the first word ever addressed to them, the first credit extended to them in a world that would spend decades collecting on everything else. Rafael had not spoken it aloud in thirty years. Had not let anyone speak it. Had kept it the way misers keep a single coin: touched nightly, spent never.

They opened their mouth and gave it to the dark.

Saying it felt like opening a vein. The syllables came out strange in their own voice, too large, ringing off the stone, and the instant the last one fell, the gatekeeper moved for the first time—one small hand rising, palm open, patient as a collection tray.

And the name began to leave.

Rafael had imagined many ways it might feel. Torn out. Cut. Bled. It was none of those. It *peeled*, the way bark peels from a branch, the way old wallpaper peels from a wall in long slow sheets, taking the plaster with it. Rafael felt the name lift free of the marrow of them, layer by layer, and each layer took a little more than the name. It took the memory of being small and called safely through a doorway. It took the warmth of hearing it whispered over a cradle. It took the shape of the woman's mouth that made the sound, so that even as Rafael stood there, they could feel her face going soft and featureless in memory—not gone, never gone entirely, but *smoothed*, like the gatekeeper's face, like a coin rubbed blank by centuries of handling.

They did not cry out. They were proud of that, later, in the way you are proud of the smallest possible things in the worst moments of your life.

When it was done, the gatekeeper closed its empty hand, and the name was inside it, and Rafael stood in the archway trying to remember what they had lost and finding, where the name had been, a gap with clean edges. A tooth-shaped absence. They knew something had been taken. They knew it had mattered. They could no longer say how.

"*Paid,*" said the mountain, and the word rolled away up the pass like distant thunder.

Rafael turned back. Maren and Isla stood watching, and their faces were wrong—no. Their faces were right, and it was Rafael who was wrong now, Rafael who looked at these two people they would die for and felt the knowledge of them slide slightly, like furniture on a tilting floor. *Maren*, they reminded themselves. *Linguist. Steals maps. Laughs at her own jokes. Chose us.* The facts held. But they held the way a rope holds—taut, effortful, requiring constant grip.

*Ilsa. The girl. The soul. Ours to save.* Grip. Hold on.

"Your turn," Rafael said, and their voice sounded like a stranger's to their own ears, and they could no longer remember whether that was new.

* * *

Maren fought it longer than Rafael had. She stood before the gatekeeper with her chin up and her arms crossed and tried, gloriously, absurdly, to negotiate.

"I'll offer an alias. A professional designation. I'm known in four universities as the Grey Quill, surely that constitutes—"

"*Name.*"

"A title! Titles have legal weight, I've drafted charters, I know what—"

"*Name.*"

"I am not finished negotiating—"

The gatekeeper extended its open hand, and the mountain said, with terrible gentleness, "*All who enter owe. All who enter pay.*" And Maren looked at Rafael, and Rafael looked back, and something passed between them that neither of them would afterward be able to describe, because describing requires shared ground and the mountain was busy confiscating theirs.

Maren paid. She gave her name in a rush, like tearing off a bandage, and then she made a sound Rafael had never heard from her—a short, wounded, furious noise, half gasp and half laugh—as whatever her mother had hung around her neck thirty-some years ago peeled away and left her lighter and emptier. She staggered. Rafael caught her elbow. For a moment they stood holding each other up, two gutted books, pages fluttering loose.

"Did it hurt yours?" Maren asked, gray-lipped.

"I can't remember mine well enough to say."

"Gods." She laughed again, wetly. "We're going to be insufferable company for the next hundred miles. Neither of us will be able to finish a sentence about ourselves."

"We'll manage. We mostly talked about you anyway."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

And it was, and the laughter helped, and both of them knew it was the kind of help that doesn't last, and both of them took it anyway.

Then there was Ilsa.

* * *

The child walked to the archway alone. Rafael started to follow and Maren's hand closed on their sleeve—*wait*—and Rafael waited, fists clenched, hating every second of it, because there are debts a guardian cannot pay for and this was one of them.

Ilsa stood before the gatekeeper, small as it was, smaller now, and looked up into the eyeless face for a long time. The humming had stopped. The mountain itself seemed to lean inward, every carved line of the First Tongue above the archway seeming to brighten, attentive.

"*Name,*" the mountain said.

And Ilsa answered—in the First Tongue, a stream of syllables that made the debt-token in Rafael's pocket go hot enough to scorch, that made the carved script flare like coals breathed upon. Rafael caught none of it. Understood none of it. But they watched the gatekeeper's smooth face change—watched something move beneath the surface of it, a ripple, almost a flinch—and watched the small hand rise and hover, open, and then, slowly, close again without taking.

Ilsa said something else. Shorter. Final.

The mountain was silent for the space of ten heartbeats. Twenty. Then:

"*Pass.*"

Not *paid*. *Pass.* As though the toll did not apply. As though the account were already settled—or already owned. Ilsa turned and walked back to them through the archway's shadow, and her face was old, old, unbearably old, and young again by the time she reached them, and she slipped her hand into Rafael's and said, in her flat careful common tongue:

"He already knows my name. He's had it since before I was born."

Rafael's blood went cold. "Who? Who's had it, Ilsa?"

She looked up the pass, past the wall, toward the black bulk of Ceren swallowing the stars, and her hand tightened.

"The one who sold me."

* * *

The wall let them through.

There was no gate even now; the archway simply ceased to be a wall, the way a held breath ceases to be held, and beyond it the mountain opened into a descending gallery lit by veins of pale luminescence running through the stone like sap through a leaf. The air was warm. It smelled of dust and iron and something faintly sweet, like paper left too long near a candle.

Maren paused at the threshold, staring down into the glowing dark, and said what Rafael was thinking:

"No turning back after this."

"There was no turning back in chapter six," Rafael said. "This is just where the mountain admits it."

They descended together—two nameless souls and a child who belonged to something older than names—down stairs worn concave by a million payments, past walls that Maren's eyes kept dragging toward and Rafael's kept sliding off. Behind them, faint as a closing book, the Tollgate sealed itself into seamless stone.

And far below, deeper than sound should travel, something vast shifted its weight in the dark, and Rafael felt the debt-token pulse once against their heart—like a knock.

Like an answer.

Like a summons.
