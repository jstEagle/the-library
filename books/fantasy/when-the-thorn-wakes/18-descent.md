## 18. Descent

The debt-token stopped pulling and started *dragging*.

Rafael felt it through the leather of their glove, a slow insistent tug toward the black seam in the mountainside where the pilgrim road simply ended—where the road had always ended, if the old maps were honest, which they weren't, because no cartographer who drew past this point ever came back to correct the proof.

"We walk from here," Rafael said. "Horses won't go."

The horses had already made their opinion known. They stood at the treeline with their ears flat, sweating despite the cold, and when Maren untied the lead rope they backed away as one animal, as if a single mind lived behind four sets of eyes.

"Good instincts," Maren said. She shouldered her satchel of stolen maps and copied pages. "I've decided to share them."

Ilsa said nothing. She hadn't spoken much since the Tollgate of Names, since she'd watched Rafael pay their mother's name into the iron mouth of the gatekeeper and seen it peel away like bark. But she took Rafael's hand without being asked, and her grip was small and cold and certain.

*You smell like his promises.* That was what she'd told them, weeks ago, in another life. Rafael wondered what they smelled like now, with most of a name gone and the rest fraying.

They went in.

* * *

The first gallery was ordinary rock, and that was almost worse—the ordinariness of it. A tunnel wide enough for two abreast, walls slick with meltwater, air that tasted of stone and old iron. Rafael's boots found steps cut by no chisel they knew, each one slightly wrong, slightly too deep or too shallow, as though the mountain had grown stairs the way it grew teeth.

Then the light changed.

Not brighter. *Different.* The darkness ahead acquired a faint gloss, like ink drying on good paper, and Rafael lifted their lamp and understood that the wall was not wall anymore.

It was writing.

Contract-stone. Maren had described it in her whispering, feverish way during the last week of travel, translating fragments from texts the guild kept chained shut: galleries of the Hollow Court where every bargain struck beneath the mountain was cut directly into the living rock, self-inscribing, self-maintaining, older than any kingdom that had ever claimed the surface. Rafael had filed it under the same shelf as everything else Maren insisted was true—interesting, improbable, survivable.

Standing in front of it, they understood they had been an idiot.

The words were not carved. They were not painted or burned. They simply *were*, in the way that veins are in marble, running through the stone in lines of darker dark, and when Rafael moved the lamp the letters seemed to stay lit a half-second after the light left them, like afterimages pressed into the eye.

Maren read the nearest one aloud before Rafael could stop her.

"*Let it be recorded: Ossa of the Fen Countries sells her third memory of her mother's face, being the memory of her mother laughing, to the bearer, for forty years of unbroken health—*"

"Don't," Rafael said.

"—*payable immediately, nontransferable, irrevocable until the death of all parties.*" Maren's finger hovered over the stone. Her voice had gone strange, reverent and horrified in equal measure, the voice of a woman watching her life's work assemble itself out of the dark. "Rafael. This is dated before the Fen Countries existed. The name is older than the country it names."

"The Court doesn't make mistakes in its records."

"No." Maren laughed, short and unhappy. "No, it doesn't. Which means either history is wrong, or this is what history was written *on*."

Ilsa tugged Rafael's hand.

She was looking up. Rafael followed her gaze and felt their stomach drop through the floor of the world.

The ceiling was covered too. Every inch. Bargains stacked on bargains, clauses nested inside clauses, some passages so dense the script collapsed into something like woven cloth, like basketry, like the crosshatch of scar tissue. And it went *up*—the gallery rose in tiers, each tier lined with more of it, spiraling away overhead into darkness that the lamp couldn't reach and the eye refused to extrapolate.

"How far does it go?" Rafael asked, though they already knew the answer was a question about depth rather than distance, and that neither number would be survivable.

"Further than light," Maren said. "Further than names. This is just—" she turned a slow circle, lamp held high, her shadow wheeling across millennia of other people's desperation— "this is the *foyer*, Rafael. This is where they keep the small stuff."

* * *

They walked because stopping felt dangerous. Rafael couldn't have said why, exactly, only that the galleries exerted a pressure against stillness, the way deep water pushes against eardrums. The contract-stone thickened as they descended. Whole walls became single agreements—one sale of a river's future fish, spanning forty feet of script; a lease on someone's grief, renewed annually for six generations, each renewal countersigned by heirs who had never met the original mourner.

Rafael did what they always did with ledgers. They audited.

It was reflex, discipline, armor. Terms, consideration, signatures, witnesses. Clause by clause, wall by wall, they read the Hollow Court the way they'd read ten thousand pages of the Gilded Thorn's books, looking for errors, for fraud, for leverage. And the terrible thing—the thing they'd carry out of here if they carried anything—was that the contracts were *good*. Airtight. Elegant, even. Whoever drafted these had a command of language that made Rafael feel like a child scratching sums in dirt.

Every entry was fair. That was the obscenity of it. Fair prices, freely given, clearly stated. No one down here had been cheated. Everyone down here had been *paid*.

"Here." Maren had stopped at a stretch of wall where the script ran thin, sparse entries, long gaps. "Look at the dates. There are gaps, Rafael. Decades where nothing was recorded. What happens to a court that stops taking business?"

"Maybe business was bad."

"Nobody's bad at this kind of business." Maren traced a gap with one finger. "Unless the court was closed. Or—" her finger stopped— "or the *clerk* was."

Rafael looked at the gap. It was very old. Older than the guild. Older than Sorrel, probably, though Sorrel had a way of making age seem negotiable.

At the end of the gap, a single new entry began, and its opening line was different from all the others. Not a sale. Not a purchase. Rafael leaned close and read it twice, three times, their lips moving without sound:

*Let it be recorded, against all precedent: the undersigned offers his voice entire, in perpetuity, as surety against the forgiveness of all debts presently outstanding within these halls.*

There was no price listed. There was no buyer named. And where every other contract in the gallery bore a signature—a mark, a seal, a smear of blood—this one bore only a single word, pressed into the stone so deeply the rock around it had fractured in a starburst pattern:

**VETH.**

The lamp flickered. Somewhere below them, faint as a pulse in a sleeping wrist, Ilsa hummed.

* * *

She'd been humming for an hour before Rafael noticed, and once they noticed, they couldn't stop hearing it.

It wasn't a tune. It wasn't quite music. It moved the way the script moved—intricate, layered, the same phrase returning altered, a word bent slightly each time it came around. Maren stopped dead in the corridor when she finally caught it, satchel slipping off her shoulder.

"That's the wall," she whispered. "That's the *Ossa contract*. She's singing it. Note for note, clause for clause."

"Ilsa." Rafael crouched to her level. The girl's eyes were half-closed, unfocused, fixed on nothing. "Sweetheart. Stop."

She didn't stop. The humming deepened, gained harmonics it shouldn't have been able to hold in such a small chest, and along the wall beside her the contract-stone *answered*—the dark lines brightening, just barely, pulsing in time with her like text remembered by the tongue that wrote it.

"Maren." Rafael's voice came out flat and careful, the voice they used over open wounds. "What happens if she reads herself?"

Maren didn't answer. Maren had gone white and was staring past Ilsa at the section of wall she'd unconsciously drifted toward—and Rafael saw it too. The script there was different. Newer, relatively speaking, and smaller, cramped, urgent, and every line of it was in a language that predated kingdoms and gods, the First Tongue, the language reality had been drafted in—

And Ilsa was reading it. Fluently. At speed. A child sounding out her own indictment.

Rafael put both hands on the girl's shoulders. "Ilsa. Look at me."

Her eyes focused. The humming faltered, caught, died. She blinked up at Rafael, and for a moment she was only a tired eight-year-old in a borrowed coat, three days hungry, feet blistered from a road she'd never chosen.

"He's sad down here," she said. "All the words. He's sad in all of them."

"Who's sad?"

But she only pressed her face into Rafael's coat, and the wall dimmed slowly back to sleep, and Maren stood with her hand pressed flat against the contract-stone like a woman taking a pulse.

"She's a key walking through a lock," Maren murmured. "Rafael—we can't let her touch the walls again. Whatever's in her, the Court wrote it down somewhere, and if she finds the entry—"

"If she finds the entry, she learns what she is."

"Yes."

"And if she learns what she is, the Court knows we know."

Maren nodded. Above them, miles up, the entrance seam was a hairline of grey. Below them, the galleries went on turning downward, tier under tier, bargain under bargain, the whole weight of every transaction ever struck beneath Ceren pressing down through the stone like a hand learning patience.

Rafael took Ilsa's hand again and walked on, auditing the dark, counting the exits there weren't.

* * *

They camped in a side alcove where the walls were mercifully blank—ordinary granite, unclaimed, the only silence in hours. Rafael built a fire out of oil-soaked rations because wood was a joke down here, and the flame burned strangely, straight and blue-edged, giving off heat but little light, as if even fire had signed something and agreed to terms.

Maren slept first, curled around her satchel like it was a child. Ilsa fought sleep the way she always did lately, eyes darting to the dark mouths of corridors, listening for whatever children listen for. Rafael sat between her and the main gallery with a knife across their knees and did the accounts.

That was the phrase they used, privately, for this particular prayer: *doing the accounts*. Veth had no rites. Veth had no temples worth the name, no priesthood, nothing but shrines going green with neglect and ledger-keepers like Rafael who'd sworn themselves in exchange for nothing they could name. You served by keeping honest numbers. You listened to the silence. You balanced what could be balanced and you marked the rest unpaid and you trusted that silence meant assent.

Except it hadn't been assent. It had been a man with his throat full of stone.

Rafael looked at their hands in the blue firelight. Hands that had forged a guild's books to fund this trip. Hands that had killed at the river crossing, quick and clean, old skills reopening like scars. Hands that had paid a mother's name into an iron gate without flinching, and hadn't yet decided whether that made them empty or lighter.

"You're doing the faces," Ilsa said softly.

"What faces?"

"When people think hard. Their faces do sums." She crawled over and settled against Rafael's side, fitting herself into the space under their arm like she'd done it for years instead of weeks. "In my head there's a room with numbers on the walls. I don't remember learning it. It was already there when I learned anything."

Rafael's arm tightened around her before they could decide to allow it.

"What's on the walls, Ils?"

"Payments." Her voice was drowsy now. "Big ones. One payment is so big it isn't a number. It's just—" her hand made a shape in the air, opening, letting go— "*gone*. Everything, paid out, and then the room locked." She yawned. "He wants to know if you'll finish reading him."

Rafael went cold from the boots up. "Who wants—"

But she was asleep. Children could do that—fall through the floor of a conversation into dreams without apology. Rafael sat very still, holding a warm, breathing girl who spoke in her sleep with someone else's grammar, while the blue fire burned its disciplined flame and the contract-stone waited beyond the alcove's mouth, patient as arithmetic.

Somewhere below, in the master ledger from which all ledgers descended, there was an entry with a fractured signature and no buyer's name. A god who had tried to forgive everyone and been made collateral for his own mercy. A voice in jars, maybe—Maren's texts said jars, or cells, or wells; the translations disagreed.

*He wants to know if you'll finish reading him.*

Rafael had spent thirty years finishing other people's sentences. Auditing their evasions. Closing their open accounts. It was, they had believed, the only prayer they knew.

Now the prayer had a body, and it was asleep against their ribs, and it was humming even in its dreams—soft, intricate, descending—like a hymn being sung from the bottom of a well by someone who could hear footsteps on the stairs.

Rafael banked the fire, woke Maren for second watch, and lay down facing the gallery's black throat.

They were nearly asleep when the stone began, very faintly, to glow—not the whole wall, just one passage, one thread of script perhaps fifty yards down the main corridor, brightening like a coal breathed upon. Rafael rose without waking the others and walked to it, boots silent, heart loud.

The illuminated passage was fresh. Wet-dark. Days old at most. And it was written in trade-script, the common contract-hand, legible to any guild accountant in the world—which meant it had been written *for* someone like Rafael, someone who could read it without Maren's help:

*Let it be recorded: the vessel approaches as scheduled. The Regent extends welcome, and reminds the escort that delivery honors all prior obligations, and that obligations, unlike travelers, always arrive.*

Below it, in the space where a counterparty signs, the stone was blank.

Waiting.

Rafael stood a long time in the dark, reading the empty line. Then they drew their knife, and—knowing it was mad, knowing the walls watched, knowing ink answered ink down here—they pressed the blade's edge to their own forearm, and wrote beneath the Court's neat invitation a single word in their own blood, in their own hand, in the plainest trade-script the Gilded Thorn's ledgers had ever used:

*Contested.*

The stone shivered. The glow guttered out like a snuffed wick. And from far below—farther than lamps, farther than names, deeper than the galleries had any right to go—something vast and patient turned its attention upward, and every contract on every wall for a hundred yards in every direction brightened at once, all together, like ten thousand held breaths,

and Ilsa, in her sleep, began to hum.
