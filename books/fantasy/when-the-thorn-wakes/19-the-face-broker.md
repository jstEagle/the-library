## 19. The Face-Broker

The gallery of faces found us before we found it.

We had been following Ilsa's humming down a corridor of contract-stone for what felt like hours—the walls inscribed so densely with bargains that the script blurred into texture, like bark, like skin—and the humming changed pitch. Went soft. Almost sweet. Ilsa stopped walking and looked up at me with an expression I'd learned to dread on her small face: recognition.

"He's here," she said. "The one whose face doesn't fit."

Maren's hand went to my sleeve. "Rafael."

"I know."

I didn't know. That was the trouble with the Hollow Court. You never knew anything until it had already been entered into the record.

The corridor opened into a chamber shaped like the inside of a bell, smooth-walled, lit from no source I could name by a light the color of candle wax left too long in the sun. And there he stood, exactly as I'd last seen him in the Gilded Thorn's receiving room: the broker from beneath the mountain, wearing his borrowed face like a coat buttoned wrong. The jaw sat a half-inch too far left. The eyes were brown when the light made them green. When he smiled, the smile arrived a moment before the mouth agreed to it.

"Ledger-keeper," he said. His voice hadn't changed either—warm gravel, the voice of a man who had talked a hundred desperate people into signing things they couldn't read. "You've been difficult to reach. The mountain is very large, and you walk it like a man trying not to leave footprints."

"Old habit," I said. "Guards at my back tend to make me quiet."

"Ah." He tilted his head, and the tilt was wrong too, a few degrees past comfortable. "Yes. Sorrel's people crossed the Salt Road behind you. Two of them, I'm told. One of them is still alive, which speaks well of you or badly of him, depending on how one keeps accounts."

Maren made a small sound. I kept my eyes on the broker. "You keep accounts?"

"Everyone keeps accounts, ledger-keeper. Some of us are simply honest about the ledger." He spread his hands—an open gesture that his shoulders refused to join. "I'm not here to take the girl. Not today. I'm here because the Court believes in settlement. Amicable terms. A contract both parties can sign without weeping. We get so few of those."

Behind me, Ilsa's humming had gone silent. I could feel her pressed against Maren's side, a small heat, a small weight. Forty years ago someone had written her soul into a page and sealed it, and she had been born around that absence like a pearl grown around grit. Every step she took toward the heart of this place, the absence pulled harder.

"You inspected our guild," I said. "Months ago. You stood outside her hiding place and smiled at the wall. Why didn't you take her then?"

"Because taking isn't collecting." He began to walk—not toward us, but along the curve of the chamber, his footsteps making no sound at all, which in a space this quiet was louder than boots. "A child dragged screaming from a cellar in Veth's city? That's theft. Theft creates grievance. Grievance creates inquiry. Inquiry creates *witnesses*, and witnesses are the one commodity the Court cannot buy back cleanly." He paused beside a vein of contract-stone and ran one finger down it almost fondly. "No. The girl must be delivered. Handed over freely, by her guardian, under proper terms, with signatures. Then the entry is clean. Then no one in your guild, your city, your god's shrinking congregation, can ever say the Court did anything but honor its paper."

"And if delivery isn't offered freely?"

He turned. The lamplight-colored glow caught his mismatched eyes. "Then the Court waits. The Court is very good at waiting. It has been waiting forty years for this particular account to mature." Something moved behind his face then—something I want to say was hunger, though hunger is a living thing's word. "And the account, ledger-keeper, has matured beautifully."

* * *

Here is what I should have done in that moment. I should have taken Maren's arm, taken Ilsa's hand, and walked out of that chamber with whatever dignity a man can carry while fleeing. I knew what the offer would be. I had known since the broker first appeared at our door wearing a face that fit him the way a glove fits a hand two sizes larger. The Court does not send negotiators to deliver threats. Threats are cheap. Negotiators are sent only when they believe you have something to trade.

But I asked anyway. Gods forgive me—I asked, because part of me wanted to hear it priced.

"What terms?" I said.

The broker's smile finally caught up with his mouth.

"Sit," he said, and there were chairs. There had been no chairs before; I was nearly certain there had been no chairs before. Three of them, arranged in the curve of the wall, upholstered in a dark leather I recognized because I had sat in its twin every evening for eleven years in the Gilded Thorn's reading room. My chair. Or close enough that my body knew it, and lowered itself into the middle one before my mind gave permission.

Maren remained standing. Ilsa stayed pressed to her side. Good, some distant part of me noted. Let them stand where they can run.

"The terms," the broker said, settling across from me with the ease of long ownership, "are these. First: the girl comes to the Court, tonight, unresisted. No blades drawn, no corridors fled through, no enforcers of yours or Sorrel's dying in the dark on her behalf. She walks in on her own feet, and the transfer reads as surrender, not seizure. In exchange—"

"Get to it," I said.

"—in exchange, the Court restores what was taken from you at the tollgate."

My hand went to my chest before I could stop it. An old instinct. The name my mother gave me had been peeled away at the gate below—a sensation I still could not describe honestly except to say it was like having a tooth drawn from somewhere behind the ribs. I had been Rafael Nystrom for twenty years, the gutter-name, the mercenary-name, the name I chose. But underneath it, in the place where names root, there was a socket that ached on cold nights. The gatekeeper had taken the first name and filed it among ten thousand others in the toll-archive, and I had told myself it was a fair price, a necessary price, a price I'd pay again.

I had told myself a great many things.

"Second," the broker continued, watching my hand at my chest with what I can only call professional satisfaction, "the Court settles your standing with the Gilded Thorn. Sorrel's warrant on your head—withdrawn. Your forged entries—amended, not exposed. Your exile—commuted. You return to the guild as guildmaster's right hand. Not clerk. Not auditor. Her second, with authority over all ledgers north of the river, a residence above the counting floor, and a pension written in the guild's charter itself, binding on her successors." He let that sit. "Sorrel has already agreed to the arrangement."

The words landed in me one at a time, each one finding an old bruise.

*Above the counting floor.* I knew that room. Third window from the corner, the one with the view of the shrine bells, the one Sorrel had joked about for years—"when you stop running, Nystrom, that one's yours." I had laughed. I had wanted it. That was the filthy truth of it: I had wanted it the whole time, wanted the warmth and the routine and the smell of ink and tallow, wanted to be the man who belonged somewhere so thoroughly that belonging became invisible. Eleven years I had told myself I lived carefully, invisibly, by choice. And here was a stranger in a stolen face holding up the mirror and showing me that my careful life had been a waiting room.

"That's a lie," Maren said quietly. "Sorrel sent hunters after us. She'll have sent word ahead. Whatever she 'agreed' to, it's bait."

"Of course it's bait," the broker said pleasantly. "Bait is simply an offer made to prey. This offer is made to a partner." He leaned forward, and his borrowed face did something terrible—it softened. Became, for a moment, almost kind. Almost the face of a man who had once pulled another man out of a gutter himself. "Let me tell you what I see in your ledger, Rafael Nystrom. I see a boy who sold his sword-arm to a silent god because silence was the only thing that never lied to him. I see a man who kept other people's debts faithfully for eleven years and was never once late with a payment of his own. I see a man who erased three entries two nights ago and hasn't slept since, because the oaths in his hands screamed at him while he did it." He tilted the wrong-jawed head. "I see a tired man. The Court respects tired men. Tired men make the best settlements."

"And the girl?" My voice came out flatter than I intended. "In your telling, she's just an entry maturing."

"She is an entry," the broker said. "So are you. So am I, for that matter—I wore a different face before this one, and I signed it away willingly, and I have never once regretted it, because regret is for men who misjudge their contracts." He rose, unhurried, and walked to where Ilsa stood. He did not touch her. He crouched instead, bringing his ill-fitting face level with hers, and spoke in a voice pitched gentle as a lullaby.

"Little vessel. Do you know what happens to you if the ledger-keeper refuses? Nothing tonight. Nothing tomorrow. But the Court accelerates, and acceleration is not gentle, and the soul inside you is pulled out the way a tooth is pulled—quickly, when it must be, but never painlessly." He straightened. "Whereas if he accepts, you sleep in a warm bed tonight, in the galleries of the Court, attended and fed, and the extraction happens in spring, properly, with ceremony. Months more of being a person. Months you would otherwise spend running." He looked at me over her head. "I am, in my way, offering you mercy for the child. Refuse it if you like. But weigh it."

Ilsa looked up at me. She didn't cry. She had stopped crying somewhere on the Salt Road, after the town that forgot, and I understood why: she had seen what people looked like on the far side of grief, and she was conserving hers.

"You smell like his promises," she said to the broker. Then, to me, in the First Tongue, four syllables I had learned by heart because she'd taught them to me on the road as a joke: *don't sign, don't sign.*

* * *

The chamber waited. That was the worst of it—the patience of it. The broker returned to his seat and folded his hands and became, in effect, furniture, and the bell-shaped walls held their silence around us, and the light neither brightened nor dimmed, and I understood that this room had no clock in it because the Court did not need one. Deadlines were for debtors.

I made myself think it through the way I'd audit any ledger. Line by line. Debit, credit.

The name. Twenty years I had worn Rafael Nystrom like a coat bought secondhand, and it had mostly fit. But the mother-name—that was the one spoken over me before I could speak, the one that meant something in a language only one other living person had known, and she was ash in a pauper's field. To have it back would be to have the first hour of my life returned to me. No amount of chosen names replaces the one you were given before choosing was possible.

The seat. Sorrel. I turned that coin until I could read both faces. On one: the woman who had fished a half-dead mercenary out of the Ropewalk gutter, set a ledger in front of him, and said, "Your killing days are over if you want them to be. Counting is cleaner. Counting is *kind*, if you do it honestly." Eleven years of tallow-smoke and winter tea and the specific grace of being trusted with a guild's secrets. On the other face: hunters on the Salt Road, a warrant with my description on it, and—if Maren was right—word already sent ahead to this very Court, meaning Sorrel had chosen her older debt over me before I ever reached the mountain. The seat she'd supposedly offered wasn't hers to give. It was the Court's, dressed in her kindness, the way the broker's face was a man's face dressed on something else.

And underneath both coins, the third thing, the one the broker had named aloud because he was skilled enough to know that naming a man's weakness disarms him more than hiding it: I was tired. Bone-tired. I missed ink that didn't scream. I missed doors that locked from the inside. I missed being a man whose worst sin was a rounded number.

That was the crystallization, right there, in the wax-seal quiet of that bell-shaped room. The Court wasn't offering me a bribe. Bribes are paid to strangers. The Court was offering me my own life back—the exact life, the warm one, the one I'd have had if a barefoot child had never knocked at the waning-hour door. All it cost was everything the new life had made of me.

"Counteroffer," I said.

The broker's eyebrows rose, genuinely or in imitation of genuine, impossible to say.

"I keep the girl. I keep my gutter-name. And in exchange, I'll bring you something worth more than her soul." I heard myself say it and hated the fluency of it—eleven years of negotiating on the guild's behalf, the skills of the trade turning traitor in my mouth. "Veth's silence. Its cause, its location, its terms. The Court has kept the god's voice in jars for centuries, but it has never known who else knows, or how the knowledge moves, or where the old shrines still hold listening-wards. I do. I've spent months mapping them. That intelligence, freely given, signed and sealed—"

"No," Maren said sharply.

"—is worth more than one matured entry," I finished. "Especially an entry the Court can collect later anyway. You said it yourself: the Court is good at waiting."

It was a bluff built on a lie built on a truth, and I watched him weigh it, and I knew—he knew I knew—that the truest part was the most damning: *an entry the Court can collect later anyway.* If we failed, if the mountain closed over us, Ilsa would be gathered regardless, and the Court would lose nothing by pretending to bargain. Which meant the offer on the table wasn't really an offer at all. It was a courtesy. A formality. The signature page at the end of a contract whose terms had been drafted decades before I was born into it.

The broker studied me for a long moment. Somewhere in the stone, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, I thought I heard humming—not Ilsa's; hers had stopped—something older, coming up through the contract-veins, a sound like a thousand voices remembering the same song.

Then he smiled, and this time the smile came all at once, all of it, every borrowed tooth.

"There it is," he said softly. "There's the man Sorrel described. 'He'll refuse,' she told my principal, 'because he loves the child, and because he is mine, and because even broken, he keeps faith.' She wagered against you, ledger-keeper. She wagered *for* you. I find I've lost money either way." He stood, and the chairs were gone as silently as they'd come, and I was on my feet without knowing I'd risen. "Keep your counteroffer. File it. The Court honors all filings." He was moving toward the corridor mouth now, and the light bent strangely around him, and his voice came back to me with the warmth of a closing door. "You have until the moon sets. After that, the Regent stops negotiating and starts collecting. I'd decide what you're willing to become, quickly. The mountain is very large, ledger-keeper—but the cellars are very deep, and one of them is calling your god by name."

He stepped into the dark of the corridor and was gone, and his footsteps made no sound going either.

For a moment none of us moved. Then Ilsa took my hand. Her palm was hot—hotter than it should have been, hotter than a child's, as if something under her skin had begun to stir.

"He lied," she said. "About spring."

"I know," I said.

"He also didn't lie," she added, with the terrible precision children keep in reserve for the worst truths. "About the pulling. It started an hour ago. I can feel it now, all the time. Like a hook." She looked up at me, and in the wax-colored light her eyes had flecks in them I had never seen before—flickers, almost, like light through jar-glass. "Rafael. I don't think we have until the moon sets. I think we have less."

Behind us, far up the corridor we'd come by, Maren raised her lamp—and its flame bent sideways, toward the heart of the mountain, though no wind moved.

Something down there had opened its eyes.
