## 20. Refusal

The broker's gallery was warm.

That was the first obscenity of it. Everything else beneath Ceren had been cold—the salt road, the tollgate, the descent through galleries where contract-stone drank the heat out of your breath—but the broker's hall glowed like a hearth-room in a rich man's house. Lamps burned behind amber glass. The floor was polished to a mirror sheen, and Rafael's own reflection walked beside them, warped and flattering, a version of themselves that had never slept on stone or forged a line of ink.

The broker had made them wait an hour in an antechamber lined with portraits. Faces, hundreds of them, each one rendered with terrible tenderness: a woman mid-laugh, a boy holding a dead bird, an old man whose eyes had been painted so honestly they seemed embarrassed to be looked at. Maren stood before them with her arms crossed and her jaw tight, doing the thing she did when she wanted to read something but knew reading it might cost her. Ilsa pressed close against Rafael's side and did not look at the faces at all.

"She keeps looking at the doors," Maren murmured. "Counting them."

"Good," Rafael said. "So am I."

Three doors. One they'd come through, sealed now—Rafael had heard the lock turn with a sound like a bone setting. Two others, flanked by guards who were not quite men. They wore the guild livery of no house Rafael knew: grey cloaks, iron collars, faces smooth as eggs. They hadn't blinked since the party arrived. Rafael had started counting their breaths just to stay calm, and had stopped counting because there was nothing to count.

The inner door opened without being touched.

"Come," said the broker, wearing his borrowed face like a coat buttoned wrong.

* * *

The gallery proper was a long room built around a table, and the table was the point. It was cut from a single slab of pale stone, and its surface was covered in writing—not carved, not inked, but *grown*, lines of script rising from the rock like frost feathers, dense as ledger-work and older than any hand Rafael had ever studied. Maren made a sound low in her throat, involuntary, the sound of a scholar seeing scripture.

The broker gestured to chairs that had been arranged for exactly three guests. He had known how many of them would come. That, more than anything, told Rafael what kind of night this would be.

"Wine?" the broker asked.

"No," Rafael said.

"It's very good. Grown in the fourth gallery, from grapes paid for honestly." The broker smiled, and the smile sat on his face the way a mask sits slightly proud of the cheekbones beneath. "Everything here is honest, Nystrom. That's what your guildmaster never understood to tell you, or perhaps understood too well. We are the only honest market in the world. Every price posted. Every term fixed. No haggling, no fraud." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Fraud is such a vulgar word for what you've been doing in our books."

Rafael kept their face still. Somewhere under the mountain's stone, pages they had rewritten in the Gilded Thorn's ledgers were probably screaming.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you do." The broker poured wine anyway, three cups, unhurried. "You erased eleven debts last month. You invented a mission charter with the seal of a regional factor who died two winters ago—you used his mark beautifully, by the way, the slight leftward drag on the terminal stroke; we noticed, we simply admired it. And then you walked out of Vellmaris by the corpse-gate with a child who smells like an unpaid balance and two companions whose names you bought back at our tollgate with money that didn't exist until you wrote it into being." He set down the decanter. "You are either the worst accountant the Thorn ever employed or the best, and after considerable discussion, we have decided it is the latter. Hence this meeting instead of, say, a cell."

Ilsa's hand found Rafael's sleeve. Her fingers were ice.

"You have her," the broker said, and now he wasn't smiling at all, and the face he wore seemed briefly too small for whatever was using it. "Or rather, she has *it*. A fragment of soul, sovereign-weight, cut from the debtor himself forty years past and seeded into a vessel. She has been carried toward us across half the world by the only person stubborn enough to make the journey interesting." He folded his hands. "We would prefer not to take her. Taking is expensive. Guards bleed, galleries crack, and souls in transit bruise if handled roughly—a bruised fragment makes poor collateral. So. An offer, freely posted, terms fixed, price honest."

He slid a sheet of contract-stone across the table. It was thin as vellum and warm to the touch, and the words surfaced as Rafael watched, arranging themselves in the First Tongue above a plain-tongued summary, the way a coin shows both faces at once.

Maren leaned in before Rafael could stop her. Her lips moved as she parsed the elder script, and Rafael watched the color leave her face in stages, like a lamp running down.

"Read it aloud," the broker suggested kindly. "For the record. Our galleries keep records of everything. It's rather our whole nature."

"I'll read it," Rafael said, and took the stone.

The terms were simple. The Court valued simplicity the way surgeons valued clean cuts.

*Item the first:* the vessel—Ilsa—to be delivered intact, willingly, to the Court's custody within one day's turning.

*Item the second:* in consideration thereof, the debtor known as Rafael Nystrom shall have restored to them the name surrendered at the tollgate of names, together with all records of debt, fraud, and flight expunged from every ledger in which they appear, including those beneath Ceren.

*Item the third:* the said Rafael Nystrom shall be seated at the right hand of Sorrel Ashvane, guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn, in perpetuity, with authority second only to her own.

*Item the fourth:* the linguist Maren Corvane shall be granted safe passage home and shall speak of nothing she has seen, this condition enforced by standard means.

There was a fifth item, smaller, in finer script, and Rafael read it twice because the first time they refused to believe it.

*Item the fifth:* the vessel shall not be harmed. She will be fed, warmed, and taught. She will live a long life in comfortable rooms. She will simply never again be consulted about anything, including herself.

"They're kind cells," the broker said softly, watching Rafael read. "I want you to understand that. You've been imagining dungeons because that's what people imagine. But she'd have a garden. There's a gallery where the light comes down through a fault in the mountain and things grow—white moss, silverfruit. Children raised here are gentle and strange and mostly content. We have had vessels before." He tilted his head. "You're wondering what happened to them. Everyone wonders. The answer is that they grew up, and they were useful, and when they were no longer useful they were retired, which is a kinder word than the accurate one, and I am offering you the accurate one because you strike me as someone who respects accuracy even when it bleeds."

Rafael set the stone down. Their hands were steady. They noted that distantly, the way you note weather—*steady hands, cold morning*—because some part of them had been a mercenary for a long time before it was an accountant, and that part had come back over the weeks of the road like a dog returning to a house it hated, and it was taking inventory now. Two doors. Guards who didn't blink. A table of grown stone between them and the thing wearing a face.

"And if I refuse?"

The broker sighed with genuine regret, and the regret was the most human expression he'd managed all evening, which told Rafael everything about where his humanity lived.

"Then item the third becomes void, item the second becomes void, item the fourth becomes—" he glanced at Maren almost apologetically, "—amended. And the vessel comes to us regardless, within the day, because the Regent has already signed the extraction order and my refusal to hurry is courtesy, not permission. You cannot steal from us, Nystrom. You can only choose how much it costs everyone you love when we collect."

The lamps hummed. In the walls, faintly, Rafael could hear the contract-stone breathing—that dry whisper of script growing, millimeter by millimeter, every bargain in the world accreting itself into the dark. Forty years ago a god had signed something in this room, or a room like it, and the whole world had been paying interest ever since.

Rafael thought about the shrine. About the cracked jar in the cellar of voices—no. Not yet. That came later, or never, depending on the next ten seconds.

They thought about Sorrel pulling them out of a gutter with a hand like a manacle and a voice like a door closing: *You'll work for me. Numbers don't lie, and neither will you, if you know what's good for you.* They thought about the warmth of the guild kitchen, the weight of small debts, the careful invisible life they had built out of other people's bargains.

Then they thought about a barefoot child in the snow saying *you smell like his promises*, and about white moss and silverfruit and a girl growing up gentle and strange in a garden she could never leave, useful until she wasn't, retired.

"No," Rafael said.

The word came out quiet. They hadn't planned it quiet. It came out the way Veth's silence must have been—total, structural, a thing with weight-bearing properties.

The broker waited. Perhaps he expected elaboration. Bargainers always do; they think a refusal is just an offer turned inside out, something to be unfolded and re-priced. Rafael gave him nothing. No speech. No justification. Just the word, sitting on the table between them like a coin that couldn't be spent.

"May I ask why?" the broker said at last, and there was real curiosity in it, the first true feeling Rafael had seen on him. "You are trading a name you already sold, a seat you claim not to want, and the safety of everyone present for—for what, precisely? The vessel is not your child. The linguist is a colleague of six weeks' acquaintance. The god whose fragment she carries abandoned his post of silence centuries ago and let a thousand lesser gods pick up his worship like dropped coins. What do you imagine you owe any of them?"

And Rafael, who had spent the whole descent telling themselves they were doing this for the child, for the god, for justice, for the ledger's oldest rule finally broken on purpose—Rafael heard the answer come out of them from somewhere below accounting, below mercy, below the gutter where Sorrel had found them:

"Nothing. I don't owe anyone anything. That's the whole point. Somebody has to be able to say no down here, or none of the yeses mean anything."

Silence. Even the walls stopped whispering, or seemed to.

The broker looked at Rafael for a long moment with an expression that was almost—almost—wistful. "The Regent said you'd say something like that. It's why the order specifies you die owed." He rose. "Take the linguist."

* * *

It happened fast, the way bad things do—not with drama but with logistics.

The egg-faced guards moved, and they moved *wrong*, joints bending in orders that made Maren scream before they even touched her. Rafael got a chair up—one of the elegant gallery chairs, solid as a shield—and caught the first guard across the shoulders with it. The chair shattered. The guard didn't. It turned, unhurried, and its smooth face split along a seam Rafael hadn't seen, and what was underneath wasn't a mouth.

"Maren, *down*—"

But they weren't reaching for Maren to hurt her. That was the horror of it. They lifted her off the floor with terrible gentleness, four hands, her feet dangling, her notes spilling everywhere—her precious notes, months of cipher-work, pages fanning across the polished stone like wounded birds—and one guard gathered the pages up, every scrap, deliberate as a clerk. They were confiscating her. All of her.

"Let her go!" Rafael had a shard of chair-leg in their fist now, and old muscle memory set their stance without asking permission, weight forward, blade-hand low. "She knows nothing that matters—"

"She knows the grammar," the broker said. He had not moved. His voice had gone flat and administrative, and somehow that was worse than menace. "Grammar is property here. Unlicensed scholarship is theft, and theft is a debt, and debts are collected." He nodded at the guards. "She'll be kept in comfort pending review. You may visit. Once. If you deliver the vessel within the day."

Ilsa screamed.

Not a common-tongue scream. It came out of her in the First Tongue, raw and uncontrolled, syllables that bent the lamplight, and Rafael felt the words in their teeth the way you feel a bell struck near your skull. The amber lamps flickered. Frost-feathers of script surged up the table's edge, answering her, growing wild, and the broker took one step back—*one step*, the first fear Rafael had seen on him—and snapped something sharp in the elder language.

Ilsa clapped her hands over her own mouth. Too late. The words kept coming, muffled through her fingers, her eyes huge and wet and terrified of the sound coming out of her, of the way the room *leaned toward her* like a plant toward sun. She shook. The stolen soul was stirring under her skin, waking because she was afraid, and every terrified syllable made the contract-walls grow faster.

"*Stop,*" Rafael said, kneeling, taking her wrists gently, pulling her hands away and holding them. "Look at me. Just me. Ilsa. Look at my face."

Her eyes found theirs. The words faltered, stuttered, thinned.

"There you are," Rafael said. "Breathe. In through the nose. Nothing you say can hurt you. Nothing you say belongs to them. Say nothing else tonight if you don't want to—but breathe."

She breathed. She was shaking like a wire, but she breathed, and slowly the wild script on the table stilled, and the lamps steadied, and in the sudden quiet Rafael became aware that the guards had frozen mid-motion, waiting, because everything in this room waited on the broker, and the broker was watching Rafael kneel in the wreckage of a gallery chair with a trembling child's wrists in their hands.

"Fascinating," the broker said quietly. "You know what you just did? You calmed a sovereign fragment by touch. Do you understand how few living creatures could do that?" He shook his head slowly. "It doesn't change the terms. It only confirms them. The Regent will find that very interesting."

"Where are you taking her?" Rafael demanded, nodding at Maren. "Tell me where."

"The Gallery of Standing Offers," the broker said. "Third corridor past the weeping wall. You know the way; your debt-token does." He was moving toward the far door now, the guards flowing around him with Maren suspended among them like a banner. She had stopped struggling. She caught Rafael's eye over an egg-smooth shoulder, and she didn't cry out, didn't plead. She tapped two fingers against her own collarbone—her old classroom signal, Rafael realized, the one scholars used to mean *memorize this, I'll recite later*—and then she was gone through the door, and the door sealed itself with that bone-setting click, and the gallery was very large and very warm and held only Rafael, Ilsa, scattered notes, and a broken chair.

* * *

They gave Rafael one hour. A servant-thing made of grey cloth and polite posture brought water and bread and a slate, and said, in a voice like a well, "The standing offer remains open until the moon-turn," and left. Outside the antechamber, faintly, the mountain went on breathing its slow script into the dark.

Rafael sat on the floor with Ilsa's head against their shoulder and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one prayer they had left.

One day. Deliver the vessel, get Maren back, get the name back, get the seat, get the soft life, watch a girl grow up gentle and strange in a garden until she was no longer useful. Or refuse—which they had done, irrevocably, gloriously, stupidly—and spend the day finding another way, with no map, no ally, no name of their own to sign anything with, and a deadline measured in a child's coherence, because every hour the stolen soul stirred harder and Rafael had seen, in the wild growth on the table, what happened to stone when Ilsa spoke too long. What would happen to a girl.

"We'll get her back," Ilsa whispered. Common tongue, hoarse, her first voluntary sentence in hours. "Maren. We'll get her back."

"Yes."

"You're lying the way you write," she said. "All straight lines. So nobody sees the wobble."

Rafael laughed—a cracked, awful sound—and kissed the top of her head. "Smartest person under this mountain," they said. Then, because the hour was burning: "Can you feel it? The thing inside you. Is it louder down here?"

Ilsa was quiet a while. When she answered, her hand drifted to her sternum, pressing lightly, the way you press a bruise to check it's still yours.

"It's not loud," she said. "It's *homesick*. It wants to go deeper. And Rafael—it's humming the way to somewhere, and I think..." She looked up, and her eyes had a light in them that wasn't hers, or wasn't only hers. "I think it's the same place the debt-token pulls. I think it's where *he* is."

Rafael reached into their coat and drew out the debt-token, the sliver of contract-stone that had dragged them across half the world like a compass needle. It was hot now. Hotter than the gallery's false warmth could explain, and as they held it, faint lines woke across its surface—new script, growing, and Ilsa read it upside down before Rafael could stop her, her lips shaping the elder strokes like a song she'd always known.

"What does it say?" Rafael asked.

Ilsa's voice came out very small, and very certain.

"It says: *the cellar of voices. Before the moon turns. Bring my pen.*"

Outside, somewhere far up the throat of the mountain, a bell began to toll the waning hour, and under its ring, deep beneath the galleries, something silent for four hundred years shifted its weight against its chains—and Rafael Nystrom stood up, and picked up the pen.
