## 22. Audience with the Regent

They came for me at the hour when even the contract-stone seemed to hold its breath.

Two guards, if you could call them that—tall shapes in gray livery whose faces were smooth as unwritten pages. They did not speak. They simply stood in the gallery outside my cell until I understood that standing there *was* the summons, and rose, and followed.

Maren gripped my arm as I passed her. Her knuckles were white around a stub of charcoal she'd been using to transcribe wall-inscriptions onto scraps of her own clothing. "Rafael. Whatever it offers—and it will offer—you hear the terms twice before you answer once."

"I'm an accountant," I said. "It's the first rule of the trade."

"It's the only rule," she said, "and it's the one this place was built to break."

Ilsa was sleeping in the corner of our shared cell, curled against the cold stone like a cat against a hearth that wasn't there. In her sleep she was humming—not the First Tongue exactly, but something underneath it, the melody beneath the language, the way a river has a sound beneath its rushing. The guards' page-faces turned toward her for one long moment, and I felt something in my chest go tight and hot.

*If you touch her,* I thought, and was surprised to find the thought had no words attached to it after that. Just weight. Just the old mercenary arithmetic: what I would do, and how far.

Then I followed the guards down.

* * *

The Court's galleries descended in spirals, each ring older than the last. Maren had catalogued them in her feverish way—the outer rings inscribed in languages dead before the first kingdoms, the inner rings in scripts that predated writing itself, and somewhere at the center, she believed, the original book. The contract-stone hummed under my palm as we passed. Every bargain ever struck down here had left a residue, and the deeper we went, the more the air felt thick with *terms*. You breathed in obligations. You exhaled interest accrued.

The guards stopped before a door that was not a door.

It was an archway of black stone, and set into it—rather than a lock or handle—was a single face. A real one. Weathered, human, eyes closed, mouth slightly open as though interrupted mid-sentence four hundred years ago. As I approached, the eyes opened.

"State your business," the face said, in a voice like mine would sound after drowning.

"The Regent summoned me," I said. "I didn't state anything."

The face almost smiled. "That," it said, "is why you were summoned." And the archway opened onto darkness, and the guards did not follow me in.

* * *

The Regent's chamber was round, and lit by nothing I could name—a light without source, shadowless, the kind of illumination that made everything look like it had been photographed rather than seen. The floor was a single slab of contract-stone so vast and so old that its inscriptions had worn to smoothness, legible now only to whatever read them.

And at the center stood the Regent.

I had expected a monster. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the one currency I've never been able to stop trading in. I had expected something like the broker with his ill-fitting face, only vaster—teeth and hunger and scale. What stood before me instead was *crowded*.

The Regent was assembled from faces. Dozens of them. Hundreds. They covered it the way scales cover a serpent or feathers cover a bird—layered, overlapping, each one oriented differently, each one alive. An old woman's face on what might have been a shoulder. A young man's across the chest, eyes moving, reading the room. A child's face low on the body, near the ground, looking up at me with an expression of such plain curiosity that my stomach turned over. They blinked out of rhythm. They breathed out of rhythm. The whole assembly shifted and resettled continuously, like a crowd pressed too close together, like a market square that had learned to stand.

"You are Rafael Nystrom," it said. The voice came from all of them at once, and none of them—a chord of voices an inch apart from harmony. "Ledger-keeper of the Gilded Thorn. Sworn to Veth, who does not speak."

"I'm sworn to him," I said. "Whether he speaks is between him and whoever took his voice."

Something rippled through the faces. It took me a moment to identify it as amusement—or the memory of amusement, worn smooth by use, the way an inscription wears to smoothness.

"Sit," the Regent said.

There was no chair. But the floor was warm where I stepped, and when I sat cross-legged on the ancient stone, the inscriptions beneath me pulsed faintly, reading me back. I kept my hands visible. Old habit. Old life.

"Do you know what I am?" the Regent asked.

"A collection," I said. "Of surrendered faces. People who paid."

"People who *settled*." One of the faces—an old soldier's, gray-bearded, positioned high on what served as the Regent's head—spoke alone, briefly, before being folded back into the chorus. "There is a difference, ledger-keeper, and you of all people should honor it."

"I know the difference," I said. "I've written it ten thousand times. Settlement is not payment. Settlement is what happens when someone can't pay and the creditor decides a piece of them will do."

"And yet," the Regent said, and every face turned toward me at once, which is an experience I would not recommend, "here you are. Descended into my court to do precisely what? To steal back a settlement?"

"To return stolen goods," I said. "The soul inside that child isn't collateral. It's a *fragment*. You cut it off a living god and grew a girl around it, and now you're calling her a debt instrument. I've read enough contracts to know when the paper is dirty."

The silence that followed had texture. The faces rearranged themselves slowly, settling into something like attention, the way a courtroom settles.

"You believe you understand what Veth attempted," the Regent said.

"I know he tried to forgive everyone. All debts. Every entry in every ledger, voided. And you couldn't allow it, because—" I gestured at the walls, the galleries above us, the whole descending architecture of the place. "Because forgiveness unmakes you. If every debt is forgiven, there's no Court. There's no trade. There's nothing to broker, nothing to collect, nothing to *be*."

"Yes," the Regent said. "That is approximately correct, in the way that a child's drawing of a storm is approximately weather."

One of the lower faces—the child's—giggled. It was the worst sound I had heard since entering the mountain.

"Then tell me properly," I said. "You clearly brought me here to explain. Brokers always want the counterparty to understand the terms. So explain."

* * *

The Regent moved for the first time—not walking so much as *pouring*, the mass of faces redistributing as it crossed the chamber to stand over a section of the floor where the worn inscriptions ran deepest. It gestured, and the stone brightened, and I saw what was written there.

Numbers. Dates. Not dates as I knew them—older, vaster, the accounting of ages.

"When the world was drafted," the Regent said, "it was drafted in obligation. This is the first truth, and the one your linguist is already halfway to. Nothing exists free. Every breath is borrowed from the atmosphere; every moment is taken from time, which keeps accounts. The First Tongue is not merely language. It is *the grammar of exchange*. Reality is a contract, ledger-keeper, and it has been performing flawlessly since before there was anyone to sign it."

"Poetry," I said.

"Bookkeeping," the Regent replied. "You flatter yourself that poetry is the deeper thing. It isn't. Poetry is what people say when they can't read the numbers." A face near its middle—middle-aged, tired, kind around the eyes—continued alone: "Veth understood the numbers better than any god before or since. That was his dominion. Debt. Obligation. The great web of what-is-owed that holds the world in tension. Every promise kept, every vow honored, every price paid—all of it sang through him. He was not silent then. He was the most *articulate* thing in creation."

I thought of the shrine in the Gilded Thorn's cellar, the little alcove where guild members left coins they'd never take back. I thought of forty years of silence.

"And then?" I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended.

"And then he read the whole ledger," the Regent said softly. "Every god reads their own domain eventually. Most stop early—it is kinder that way. Veth read to the end. And he found the entries no one talks about. The debts that could never be paid. The widow who owed her grief to a war-lord because her husband's death had been purchased in advance. The farmer who sold his daughter's laughter and spent thirty years listening to her silence and calling it a temper. The pilgrims on your salt road, selling their first loves for bread. He read the arrears of the entire world, Rafael Nystrom, and he discovered that most of what was owed could never be collected without breaking the debtor entirely."

The tired face looked at me with something that was almost compassion, and I hated it more than I have hated anything in my life, because it was *my* expression. It was the look I wore doing audits late at night, cataloguing bottled grief, telling myself the trade was honest because the books balanced.

"He decided to forgive," I said. "All of it."

"All of it," the Regent agreed. "A general amnesty. Every debt voided, every bond released, every obligation dissolved retroactively to the signing of the world. He began drafting it in the First Tongue—the only tongue in which such a covenant could be written. Do you understand what would have happened if he finished?"

The chamber seemed to lean in.

"A debt forgiven is a structure removed," the Regent said. "Remove one debt, and the space it held collapses gently; the debtor stands up straighter; the world adjusts. Remove *all* debts—" the faces spread wide, an awful bloom, "—and you remove the tension that holds form apart from form. The contract that says this is *you* and that is *the mountain* and this is the *price of bread* and that is the *length of a life*. Obligation is not a burden laid upon the world, ledger-keeper. Obligation *is* the world. Forgiveness on that scale is not mercy. It is unmaking. He would not have freed anyone. He would have dissolved everyone—every soul, every stone, every story—back into the undifferentiated dark that existed before the first term was struck."

"That's your interpretation," I said.

"It is not an interpretation. It is arithmetic." For the first time, the chorus sharpened into something like anger, dozens of mouths shaping the same words a half-breath apart. "We watched the draft take form. We felt the edges of things going soft wherever his pen moved. The Court did not imprison your god out of malice, Nystrom. The Court imprisoned him the way you dam a river—not because you hate water, but because water does not care what it drowns."

"And his voice," I said. "The jars. A century of speech in each one. Was that damming too?"

The Regent was quiet for a long moment. When it answered, several of the faces had closed their eyes.

"His voice was the pen," the Regent said. "A covenant written in the First Tongue requires a signature of sovereign weight. While he could speak, he could always begin again. So we took the instrument. We did not destroy it—we are not destroyers, whatever your guild's stories say. We *filed* it. Archived. Each jar holds what he said during a century of captivity, preserved perfectly, accruing nothing. It is the most respectful imprisonment ever devised. Ask yourself, ledger-keeper: if we were monsters, why does he still live?"

"Maybe because a dead god makes a poor collateral," I said. "Or maybe you keep him alive the way a lender keeps a debtor alive. Alive means the account stays open."

More ripples through the faces. This time I couldn't read the expression at all.

* * *

"You love the child," the Regent said.

It was not a question, and I refused to treat it as one.

"She was grown around the fragment," the Regent continued, when I said nothing. "Forty years of cultivation. A vessel shaped by patience. And now she walks and speaks and weeps for villages full of mourners who cannot mourn, and you have decided she is a person, and therefore the fragment must be returned to its origin, and the god restored, and everything my Court has built for nine thousand years torn down in a single act of—what do you call it? Mercy? Justice?" The tired face again, the kind eyes. "You are about to commit the largest default in history, and you think of it as balancing the books."

"Here's what I think," I said, getting to my feet. My legs were steadier than I deserved. "I think you've got beautiful logic and rotten premises. You say obligation is the world—but I've walked the Salt Road. I've seen what your world looks like. Women selling the memory of their children's faces so they can eat. That's not order. That's order the way a tumor is growth."

"The alternative is dissolution."

"The alternative is *choice*," I said. "Debts freely made and freely forgiven. Gifts. Grace. Things given without expectation of return—which your grammar doesn't even have a word for, does it? Because if it did, you'd have to admit the whole system is smaller than it pretends to be."

For a moment—one heartbeat, two—I thought I saw it. A flicker across the assembled faces, dozens of expressions shifting toward something that was not amusement and not anger but *recognition*, as if somewhere inside the crowd of the surrendered, someone remembered having once thought exactly this, before they settled.

Then it was gone, smoothed over like ink dried into stone.

"You are going to attempt it," the Regent said. "The covenant. The amnesty. I can see it in you the way I see everything—every entry, every intention. So hear my final term, and understand that it is not a threat but a disclosure, because disclosure is the one law even we must keep."

The chamber dimmed. The inscriptions under my feet went dark except for one line, freshly cut, glowing like a wound:

*A covenant requires a signature of sovereign weight.*

"Veth cannot sign," the Regent said. "He has no voice. Ilsa is a fragment, not a whole—a fragment's signature binds only the fragment. And you, ledger-keeper—" every face smiled at once, gentle and terrible, "—are sworn to him. Your oath-bond is the last sovereign instrument in this mountain. If you mean to finish what he started, you already know what the signature will cost. The Court does not need to take it. You will *give* it. That is the trap of grace, Rafael Nystrom: it is always the giver who bleeds."

"I've read the terms," I said.

"Then we are concluded," the Regent said, and began to turn away—and paused, and one face alone spoke, the child's, looking up at me from near the ground with that unbearable plain curiosity. "The little one hums in her sleep. Did you know? She hums the amnesty draft. He wrote it into her before we ever took his voice—the ending, the part he never got to sign. She has been carrying the last verse of a god's unfinished sentence for forty years, and she thinks it is just a song."

The archway opened. The guards waited.

"One more thing," I said, standing in the doorway, not looking back. "When this is done—when the debts are voided and the Court comes down—what happens to them? The faces. The ones you're wearing."

The Regent was quiet long enough that I did look back. And for just a moment, all those hundreds of eyes were open, and all of them were looking at me, and all of them were afraid.

"We do not know," the Regent said. "No one has ever survived being forgiven before."

* * *

They walked me back up through the galleries, past the contract-walls, past Maren's cell where she pressed her face to the bars and read my expression like a ledger entry and went pale. Past Ilsa's door. Through the rings of older and older bargains, each one humming its small eternal note.

At the threshold of our cell block, one of the page-faced guards stopped. And then, in a voice like dry paper turning, it said—quietly, so quietly I knew it was not authorized—

"She hums it wrong, you know. The last verse. The vessel's throat can't shape the sovereign grammar." A pause. "But there are jars below that hold a century of the true pronunciation. Jars that crack, sometimes. Jars that whisper."

I stared at it. The smooth blank face gave back nothing.

"Why are you telling me this?"

The guard tilted its head, and for half a second I saw behind the page—saw the ghost of features, someone's features, surrendered long ago and still, somehow, in there. Still counting.

"Because I was a debtor once," it said. "And no one has ever read my terms aloud."

Then the face smoothed shut, and the guard turned away, and I went to sit beside Ilsa while she slept, and listened to her hum the ending of a god's sentence—and began, in the dark, with a stub of charcoal and the inside of my own forearm, to learn the tune.
