## 23. Maren's Choice

Maren had spent forty years learning languages the way other people learned sins—one at a time, each one leaving a mark she couldn't show in polite company.

She had learned Old Vessic from a smuggler who charged her in teeth. She had learned the trade-tongue of the Salt Road from merchants who thought she was a boy and paid her accordingly until they found out, at which point she'd learned a third language: profanity. She had learned dead scripts from gravestones, liturgical grammar from bored priests, and the click-and-hiss of the deep-market cant from a woman in Kettering who had taught her everything except how to say goodbye.

But the First Tongue—

The First Tongue was different. The First Tongue was not something you learned. It was something that learned *you*.

She sat in the gallery with her notes spread across her knees, pages she had filled by candlelight over eleven days of descent, and she felt them looking back at her. Not metaphorically. She had written Ilsa's words down phonetically, sixty-three pages of them, and somewhere around page thirty the ink had started doing things. Drifting. Settling into patterns she hadn't drawn. On page fifty-one, a word she had transcribed wrong—she was sure she'd transcribed it wrong—had corrected itself overnight, and when she read it aloud to test it, the torches in their camp had all leaned toward her at once, like flowers toward sun.

Rafael had watched that happen and said nothing. Rafael was good at saying nothing. It was practically a religious discipline.

Ilsa had said, matter-of-factly, chewing on a strip of dried pear: "It likes you."

Nothing liked Maren. Nothing had ever liked Maren. Things tolerated Maren. Things found Maren useful. This was the arrangement she had made with the world and she had never once resented it, because usefulness was the only love she'd ever been offered and she had taken it the way a starving woman takes bread off a stranger's cart—with gratitude and no questions.

Now the Regent's herald stood in front of her holding a book.

* * *

They had been summoned. That was the word for it, though no one had said it aloud. A door in the contract-stone wall that hadn't existed an hour before now gaped open onto a stair going down, and standing at the top of the stair was the herald—a tall figure in robes the color of wet slate, its face smooth as an unfired mask, waiting.

"You," it said to Maren, and its voice was every voice she had ever studied layered one atop another, "have been taking dictation."

"I'm a linguist," Maren said. Her mouth was dry. "It's what we do."

"It is not what you do." The herald tilted its head, and the gesture was almost human, almost curious, and that was worse than menace. "What you do is copy. What you have been doing for eleven days is *drafting*. You have written words in the First Tongue that were not spoken to you. Do you know what that makes you?"

"Unemployed, if the Gilded Thorn finds out."

The herald did not laugh. Nothing down here laughed. But something behind the smooth face shifted, the way light shifts under ice.

"The Court offers you the Grammar," it said. "The full declension. Every root, every binding form, every tense including the ones your kind has no names for—the tense in which a thing is always about to have happened, the tense in which a debt is already paid regardless of payment. The language reality was drafted in, complete. No mortal has held it since the god went quiet."

Maren's hands went still on her notes.

Forty years. Forty years of fragments, of reconstructions, of arguing in journals with men who spelled her name wrong, of dying languages she could only ever half-hear through the gauze of time. And here it was, whole. Offered. Every question she had ever asked, answered at once, forever.

She thought about what she could write with it. Contracts that would hold across kingdoms. Grammars that would outlive empires. Her name carved into the discipline itself—not Maren the guild functionary, not Maren who kept the Thorn's translation room and was thanked by nobody, but Maren, the woman who brought the First Tongue back into the world.

She thought about it for four full breaths, which in the galleries beneath Mount Ceren is a very long time, because the walls are listening and they take notes.

"And the cost?" she said, because there was always a cost; she worked for the Gilded Thorn, cost was the entire industry.

"The Court does not sell," the herald said gently. "The Court trades. The Grammar for your services. You would remain below, drafting. The Court has been short-handed since the god stopped signing."

"And Ilsa."

"The child is not part of this negotiation."

"The child is the only reason you're negotiating."

For a moment the herald's face did something terrible and small—it rippled, like a reflection disturbed—and Maren understood, suddenly and completely, that underneath the smoothness there were faces, many faces, and they were all tired.

"The child," the herald said, "is inventory."

* * *

They gave her until the third bell—which was strange, because there were no bells below the mountain, but she heard one anyway, tolling somewhere in the stone, counting down.

She sat with her notes in a side gallery where the contract-walls receded enough to let her pretend she was alone. Sixty-three pages. Eleven days of work. The best scholarship of her life, and she knew it—knew it with the cold clarity of a woman reading her own eulogy—that these pages were worth more than everything else she had ever produced combined.

Ilsa found her there. Of course she did. The child moved through the Court like water finding cracks.

"Maren." Ilsa crouched beside her, studying the pages upside down, which was how children read everything. "You're doing the face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you're counting things you don't want to count." Ilsa picked up the pear she'd been carrying around for two days and offered half of it, solemnly, the way you feed something wild. "In my dream there was a lady made of pages. She wanted me to come live inside her book. She said it was warm."

"What did you say?"

"I said books aren't warm, they just hold heat other people made." Ilsa shrugged, wise and seven and utterly unbothered. "She got angry. Books do that."

Maren looked at the child—at the birthmark like a buyer's seal, at the soul folded up inside her small body like a letter in an envelope, at the thing wearing a girl the way a girl wears a coat—and felt something crack open in her chest that she recognized, with horror, as tenderness.

She had never wanted children. She had said so, many times, usually unprompted, usually defensively. Children were noise and need and they grew up to be disappointments or corpses, and Maren had chosen knowledge instead, knowledge which asked for everything but at least told you the price up front.

And here was this child, offering her half a pear, and Maren understood that the Grammar was being priced against exactly this moment. That was the trade. That was always the trade. The Court didn't want her scholarship. The Court wanted to teach her that everything she loved could be converted.

"Listen," Maren said. "Ilsa. If someone offered you every answer in the world, but you had to stay down here alone forever, would you take it?"

Ilsa considered this with the seriousness of a judge. "Alone?"

"Alone."

"But you'd be gone?" Her lip wobbled. "And Rafael?"

"Gone too."

"Then no," Ilsa said, as if it were obvious, as if the greatest linguist of her generation hadn't just spent four breaths failing the same test. "Answers are just words. Words are cheap. I know *all* the words." She tapped Maren's knee with one sticky finger. "You have to learn which ones are true. That's the hard part. Nobody can give you that."

The bell tolled the second hour.

* * *

Maren burned her notes at the edge of a cold hearth in the side gallery, using a taper she'd pocketed from the brokers' hall, and she did it methodically, page by page, because she was a scholar and even arson deserved rigor.

Page one: Ilsa's first night, the words that made the ledger ink crawl. Burned.

Pages nine through fourteen: the conjugation of the verb *to owe*, which had no first person singular, because in the First Tongue you cannot say *I owe*, only *it is owed through me*. Burned, and the flame turned briefly green, and something in the walls sighed.

Page fifty-one: the self-correcting word. She saved that one for last. She read it aloud once more, quietly, so the stone could hear it—*veth*, the old root, the buried root, the name-shaped hole in the world—and then she fed it to the fire and watched the flame go white and stand perfectly still, like a held breath, before collapsing back to ordinary orange.

Sixty-three pages. Forty years of wanting. Ash.

She felt lighter. She also felt gutted. Both things were true, and she was done pretending only one feeling got to happen at a time.

When she walked back into the main gallery, the herald was waiting where the stair met the stone, and Rafael was there too, and Rafael's hand had drifted to the knife at their belt in that way Rafael had of being violent in complete silence.

"Maren," Rafael said. Low. Warning or welcome; with Rafael it was often both.

"I'm fine." She held up her empty hands, palms out, ash still smudged along one wrist like a bruise. "Better than fine. I've just committed the most expensive act of self-sabotage in the history of philology."

The herald regarded her. The smooth face gave nothing away, but the air around it thickened, the way air does before a signature.

"The Court offered," it said. "You refused."

"I burned it, actually. Refusing seemed insufficiently dramatic."

"That was not yours to burn." For the first time, something like anger moved through the herald's voice—all those layered voices snagging on the same jagged edge. "That grammar exists once. It was drafted into your notes the moment you transcribed truly. You did not destroy it. You destroyed our claim on it."

"Then we're both disappointed."

"No." The herald stepped closer, and Maren held her ground, mostly because her knees had locked. "The Court remembers refusals. They accrue. You will walk out of here, linguist—you will climb, and see the sun, and grow old in whatever dusty room you crawl back to. And on the day of your death, when your account comes due as all accounts come due—" the smooth face leaned down, close enough that she could smell old ink and cold stone, "—we will collect. Everything you ever learned. Every language. We will take the knowing out of you, and we will keep it, and you will die illiterate in your own life. Marked."

Somewhere behind her, Ilsa made a small sound. Rafael's knife came out of its sheath with a whisper.

"Don't," Maren said, without turning around. "Rafael. Don't. It's not a threat, it's an appointment. There's a difference, and you of all people know the difference."

She looked at the herald, and she found—to her genuine surprise—that she wasn't afraid. Or rather, she was afraid the way you're afraid of weather. Vast, impersonal, already arriving.

"One amendment," she said. "To your marking."

The herald waited.

"When you take it—when you strip every language out of me—I'll have one left. Just long enough." She smiled, and it hurt her face, because smiling was a muscle she'd let atrophy. "The one Ilsa taught me. The one that means *friend*. You can't unteach a person the word for friend, herald. It's not vocabulary. It's scar tissue. So when I die knowing nothing, I'll still know that one word, and it will be about her, and you will have handed eternity a gift you don't understand."

The herald stared at her. Then, slowly, it inclined its head—not mockery, not deference, something older than either—and stepped aside from the stair.

"The marked may pass," it said. "Once."

* * *

Later—or what passed for later, in a place with no sky—Rafael found her sitting with Ilsa asleep against her shoulder, and sat down beside them without asking, which from Rafael was practically an embrace.

"You gave up the Grammar," Rafael said.

"I gave up a cage with very good lighting."

"You'll die owing them."

"I was always going to die owing somebody. At least this way it's on purpose." Maren shifted carefully so Ilsa's head settled into the crook of her neck. "Besides. Someone has to survive you two long enough to write all this down honestly. In a language the Court can't touch." She paused. "I'm thinking Common. Badly punctuated."

Rafael huffed—almost a laugh, filed under breath for later use—and looked out across the gallery where the contract-walls glowed faintly with ten thousand years of bargains, and Maren saw their jaw set in the particular way it set when a plan was forming that would horrify her.

"They're moving tonight," Rafael said quietly. "The Regent. I heard the brokers talking. They're not waiting for the moon. They're going to start extracting early."

Ilsa stirred in her sleep and, without waking, began to hum—a small looping melody in the First Tongue, a wordless phrase that Maren's trained ear caught and translated before she could stop herself, and the meaning of it landed in her chest like a dropped stone:

*Help me. Help me. Help me.*

Maren closed her eyes, held the child closer, and started, silently, to draft a plan of her own—in plain, stubborn, badly punctuated Common.

Because the Court had marked her for collection.

But nobody, in forty years, had ever accused Maren of paying her debts on time.
