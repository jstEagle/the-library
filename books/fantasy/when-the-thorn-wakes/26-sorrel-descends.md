## 26. Sorrel Descends

The mountain had been waiting for her, Sorrel decided. That was the only explanation for how easily the road opened. No avalanches, no rockslides, no wolves—just a smooth dark throat of switchbacks descending into Ceren's roots, as though the stone itself remembered her name and made way.

She rode at the head of six enforcers, and she hated every one of them for being there, because their presence made the thing honest. A delegation. A delivery. She had dressed it in guild language—*recovery of misappropriated assets, protection of the Thorn's standing*—but the ledger didn't care about language. The ledger cared about weight. And what she carried in her saddlebag was the heaviest thing she'd ever owned: a debt-token older than her gray hairs, older than the guild itself, humming against the leather like a trapped wasp.

Forty years she'd kept it buried under the floor of her counting room. Forty years pretending that burying a debt was the same as paying it.

"Guildmaster." Dain reined up beside her, the one whose face she'd broken with a chair leg when he was nineteen and stupid. He'd forgiven her. They always forgave her. It was one of the many things about herself she could not forgive. "The gate ahead. Same one Nystrom's party would have come through."

"I know which gate."

"You want us to check for sign?"

"No." She heard her own voice go flat, the voice she used over ledgers and coffins. "They're already inside. If Rafael wanted to be caught on a road, they'd have stayed in the city."

Dain nodded and fell back, and Sorrel looked at the gate—a seam of pale stone in the black cliff, carved with names so old they'd worn down to suggestion—and felt something she hadn't felt since she was young enough to still believe the world could be argued with.

Fear. Clean, uncomplicated fear.

Good, she told herself. Fear means you haven't finished dying yet.

* * *

The gatekeeper did not ask for her name.

That was the first wrong thing. She knew the toll—it was written in the token's fine print, in the First Tongue she couldn't read but had spent forty years dreaming in. Every traveler pays their true name. But when her party reached the threshold, the broker was already there, waiting, wearing his face like a coat buttoned slightly off-center, and he simply bowed.

"Guildmaster Sorrel. You are expected."

"Expected," she repeated.

"The Court does not forget its agents." He gestured, and the gate opened—not swung, not ground apart, just ceased to be closed, the way a mouth ceases to be shut. "Your people may wait outside. What happens below is between you and your account."

Her enforcers looked at her. Six faces, ranging from loyal to frightened, and not one of them she would trust past the second gallery, because she'd trained them never to be trusted that far by anyone, including her.

"Dain. If I'm not out in three days, take the guild. Don't look for me. Don't avenge me. Just—" She almost said *keep the books clean*, and heard how pathetic that was, and stopped. "Just keep the books."

He saluted. She walked through the gate alone, and behind her she heard the mountain close its mouth, and the sound was exactly the sound of a door locking from the inside, which was the only way doors had ever locked for her.

* * *

The galleries were worse than she remembered.

She'd been down here once before, forty years ago, a younger woman with two children starving in a rented room above a tannery and a broker's offer folded in her pocket like a knife. She'd sworn then that she would never come back. She'd built an entire life on top of that promise—the guild, the ledgers, the power, the careful kindnesses she distributed like a woman paying interest on something invisible—and she'd told herself the life *was* the payment. Look what I built. Look who I saved. Doesn't that count?

The walls answered her now. Contract-stone, Maren had called it in her reports, and the reports hadn't done it justice. Every surface was inscribed, and the inscriptions moved—not flickering, nothing so dramatic, just shifting the way text shifts when you reread it and understand more than you did. She passed bargains three thousand years old. She passed one signed last week. She did not read them. Reading them was how the Court got into you; she'd known that since the first time, and knowing it had never once helped.

What broke her composure was the humming.

A child's voice, threading through the caverns like water through limestone. Off-key. Stubborn. Singing a song that made the contract-stone around Sorrel shiver, made forty-year-old ink lift its edges like grass in wind.

Ilsa.

Sorrel stood in the dark gallery with her hand pressed flat against a wall of other people's ruin, and listened to the girl sing, and understood—for the first time, completely, without any of the softening fictions she'd wrapped around it—that what she had come to deliver was a child. Not collateral. Not an asset. Not the Court's property temporarily wearing a body. A child who hummed when she was nervous and had once, in the Thorn's kitchen, eaten an entire jar of candied ginger and lied about it with crumbs still visible on her chin.

Rafael loved this child. That was the whole catastrophe, wasn't it. Rafael, whom Sorrel had pulled out of a gutter with both hands and half her fortune, had looked at this impossible girl and done the unforgivable thing: they'd cared. And now everything was complicated, and complication was the one currency the Hollow Court did not accept.

"Guildmaster." The broker's voice, beside her though she'd heard no footsteps. His face smiled. The smile arrived a half-second after the rest of him, like thunder after lightning. "The Regent will receive you. Bring the token."

"And if I don't?" she said. An old habit, haggling. It rose in her like bile, professional to the end.

The broker tilted his head, and for one moment the face slipped—not changed, just settled wrongly, the way furniture settles in an empty house—and beneath it was something patient and vast and very tired of her.

"Guildmaster. You have been coming here for forty years. In dreams, in debts, in every signature you've ever forged on your own behalf." He extended one hand, palm up, courteous as a dance partner. "There was never a version of tonight where you turned back. We only waited for you to know it."

She gave him the token. Her hand shook. She noted it, the way she noted all entries, and followed him down.

* * *

They brought her to a chamber where Rafael was already waiting, and Sorrel's heart did something complicated and painful, because they were sitting on the floor with their back against the master ledger's pedestal like a clerk sleeping off a long shift, and they looked up at her with no surprise at all.

"Sorrel."

"Rafael." She kept her voice level. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other accountants."

Despite everything—a war below the world, a god in jars, a deadline measured in a child's dissolving—despite all of it, Sorrel felt her mouth twitch. That was the tragedy of Rafael Nystrom, she thought. Even now. Even here. Still making jokes across the ledger line, still refusing to let the numbers be only numbers.

"They told me you'd be here," she said.

"I asked them to bring you to me first. Before the Regent." Rafael stood, slowly, like someone whose body had been renegotiated recently and not entirely in their favor. "Sorrel. Don't do this."

"Do what? Say it plainly. We're both too old for the dance."

"Don't hand her over."

"She was always going to be handed over." Sorrel heard the words leave her and hated their shape—smooth, rehearsed, the speech of a woman who had practiced this conversation in the dark for decades. "That was decided before either of us met her. Before you were born, maybe. The Court doesn't collect late, Rafael. It collects *eventually*. All I've ever controlled is the manner of the collection."

"Manner." Rafael laughed, and there was blood-anger in it. "You want to talk about manner. Forty years, Sorrel. You sat across from me at that table for forty years and taught me that a ledger is a moral document. That every entry has a face behind it. You taught me—" Their voice cracked, and Sorrel flinched, because she had never once heard Rafael's voice crack, not in the gutter, not in the ambushes, not at any funeral. "You taught me the whole religion of it. Was that real? Any of it?"

And there it was. The question she had been carrying down the mountain like ballast.

"Yes," Sorrel said. "It was real. That's the worst part. Sit down, Rafael. You'll want to be sitting."

* * *

She told it plainly, because plainly was the only mercy she had left to give.

"Winter. Forty-one years ago. My daughter was four. My son was two." She kept her hands still on her knees, clerk's discipline, because if she let them move they would do something she couldn't take back. "Their father had died in the spring—fever, cheap coffin, the whole ordinary tragedy. By midwinter I'd sold everything that mattered and most of what didn't. There was a famine tax and a landlord and I remember sitting on the floor counting coins by tallow-light and arriving at the same number eleven times, hoping arithmetic would change if I watched it long enough."

Rafael had gone very still.

"A broker came to the door. Of course he did. They find you at exactly that hour—I know that now; I didn't know it then. He offered me a trade. Simple terms. My children's futures—specifically, the futures they would have had, the lives they would have lived, the whole unwritten book of them—in exchange for enough gold to survive the winter, plus a standing arrangement. Protection. Credit. Access." She smiled, and it felt like a wound smiling. "I told myself I was buying them their childhoods. Bread instead of hunger. A roof instead of the frost. I told myself the futures were hypothetical anyway—who knows what any child becomes? Maybe I'd sold nothing. Maybe I'd only sold smoke.

"But I knew. Even then, signing, I knew. You can't sell a future without selling the person inside it. My son grew up directionless and angry and left at sixteen and I never learned why he was angry, because the reason had been sold before he could walk. My daughter—my daughter stayed. Sweet, capable, hollow in a way I could never name. She died last autumn, Rafael. Fever again. The family disease. And at her bedside she held my hand and said, *Mother, I always felt like I was waiting for my life to start.*"

The chamber was silent. Somewhere far off, through miles of stone, Ilsa was still humming.

"So yes," Sorrel said. "I became the Court's agent. Because the arrangement didn't end with the gold. The gold was bait; the hook was that I would spend the rest of my life finding them what they wanted. Souls mostly, at first—small ones, desperate ones, the usual traffic. Then stranger things. And eventually the Court told me what they were really growing toward. A vessel. A child born around a stolen soul. They showed me the contract. Veth's own signature on it, forty years old, and the payment clause, and the mark that would appear on the vessel's skin.

"And I said yes. Because saying yes was the only way to keep the arrangement alive, and the arrangement was the only thing keeping the wolf from my door, and by then—" her voice finally failed her, and she let it, and did not wipe her eyes because clerks do not weep during an audit, "—by then the wolf lived in my house. Had been living there for decades. Slept in my bed. Signed my correspondence. I wasn't the Court's agent because I was wicked, Rafael. I'm not even sure I was the Court's agent because I was afraid. I think I was the Court's agent because I had become a person for whom the trade was the only shape left. You build your cage and then you live in it so long you mistake it for a spine."

Rafael sat down on the floor again. Not dramatically. Just—the strength went somewhere else for a while.

"All those years," they said finally. "All those lessons. The ethics of the ledger. You were teaching me the rules of the thing that ate you."

"I was teaching you the rules of the thing that ate you so you wouldn't get eaten." Sorrel looked at her hands. "It was the closest thing to repentance I could afford. Repentance is expensive here. Everything is expensive here. That's the entire architecture."

"It doesn't have to be." Rafael leaned forward, and their eyes had the light in them that Sorrel had seen only twice before—once in the gutter, once the day they'd found the sealed page. The light of a person about to commit fraud against reality. "That's what I came down here to prove. Debts can be voided. Forgiven. Written off. The god tried it once, and they caged him for it, but the attempt proves it's possible. Forgiveness exists, Sorrel. It's just been imprisoned."

"Child." She used the word gently, and meant it. "You cannot forgive what the Court holds. I tried. Gods know I tried. There is no entry in any of my books, in forty years of clever accounting, that un-sells a child's tomorrow."

"Then we'll write a new kind of entry."

* * *

The Regent's summons came as a pressure rather than a sound—the whole cavern inhaling—and the broker appeared at the doorway with his ill-fitting smile.

"The Regent receives the guildmaster now. Alone. The debtor will wait."

Sorrel rose. Straightened her coat. Forty years of posture assembled themselves around her spine, the armor of a woman who had never once let the room see her count the cost.

At the doorway she stopped. Turned back.

"Rafael. When I pulled you out of that gutter, do you know why I did it?"

"You've never said."

"Because you were keeping accounts. In the mud, in the rain, with a stick—tracking who owed you what, who had stolen your coat, who had given you bread. Everyone else down there was surviving. You were *auditing*." Her throat tightened. "I thought: this one understands. This one will learn what money really is. And you did, and you became better at it than me, and kinder with it than me, which was never difficult." She paused. "If you truly believe a debt can be forgiven—if you can find a way to write it—then write mine too. Not because I deserve it. Because if forgiveness is real, someone should test it on the worst case first. Consider me your proof of concept."

Then she walked through the doorway, toward the Regent assembled from surrendered faces, and behind her she heard Rafael say, quietly, to the dark:

"Already done, old woman. Already done."

And Sorrel, who had spent forty years learning to hear what was not said, carried those two words down into the Regent's chamber like contraband—like the first honest coin she'd owned in decades—and did not yet know that she was going to need them sooner than anyone planned.
