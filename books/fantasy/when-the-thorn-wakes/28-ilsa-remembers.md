## 28. Ilsa Remembers

The soul comes up like a bubble through dark water.

Ilsa is sitting against a contract-wall in the deep gallery, knees pulled to her chest, listening to the stone hum. The walls have been humming all night. Rafael says it's the mountain settling. Maren says it's the contracts reading themselves. But Ilsa knows what humming means. Humming is what you do when you are holding yourself together with sound.

She has been humming since she was very small. She never knew why.

Now she knows.

It starts as warmth behind her ribs — not fever-warmth, not the burning that came when the Regent's servants dragged her toward the extraction room and Rafael killed two of them to get her back. This is different. This is old. It feels like a door in her chest that was painted shut, and someone on the other side has finally found the seam.

*Ilsa,* something says, and it does not use her name right. It uses it like a coat that doesn't fit. *Little piece. Little cut-away. Come home.*

She opens her eyes.

The gallery is dim. Maren sleeps curled around her satchel of burned notes, ashes still dusting the corners of her mouth like she fell asleep mid-confession. Rafael sits three paces away with his back to the wall, ledger open on his knees, pen moving in the dark without light. He writes even when there is nothing to write. She has watched him do it for weeks. He writes because if his hands stop, his thoughts start, and his thoughts are mostly fear wearing an accountant's face.

*Rafael,* she almost says. *Rafael, something is happening to me.*

But the words come out wrong.

They come out in the First Tongue.

She doesn't decide it. Her mouth simply opens and the language pours out, syllables like stones dropped into still water, and the contract-walls around her *answer*. Every inscription within twenty paces brightens, ink waking under skin of rock, and the whole corridor shivers the way a dog shivers when it hears its name.

Rafael's pen stops.

"Ilsa?" His voice is careful. She knows that care. It's the care he uses when he thinks she might break, which is funny, because he is about to learn that she was never whole enough to break. "What did you just say?"

"I don't know," she says, and her common-tongue comes back ragged, like cloth pulled through a thornbush. "It said itself. I didn't say anything."

He sets down the ledger. That frightens her more than anything else could. Rafael without his ledger is a man standing in a river with no rope.

"Say it again," he whispers.

And she tries — she reaches for the words the way you reach for a dream before waking — and this time when they come they bring everything with them.

* * *

Here is what remembering is like, when the thing you remember is bigger than your body:

It is like pouring the sea into a cup.

The first drop is a hillside. Not any hillside she has walked — she has walked so few, and all of them gray with snow and fear — but a hillside green past sense, grass so alive it seemed to be singing at frequencies only roots could hear. She is standing on it. Except she is not standing on it; she is *under* it and *through* it and *of* it, the way a word is of a sentence, and every blade of grass is a debt being forgiven, and forgiveness moves through the world like dawn moving across an ocean, country by country, heart by heart—

She remembers forgiving.

That is the second drop, and it nearly drowns her. Because forgiveness, she learns in the space of a heartbeat, is not gentle. Forgiveness is a *force*. It is the loudest thing there is. When a god decides that every debt ever written shall be void — every theft unthefted, every cruelty uncruel, every bargain struck in desperation returned to the desperate hands that struck it — reality does not thank him. Reality screams. Contracts are load-bearing. Tear them all out at once and the house of the world comes down.

He knew that. She remembers him knowing it. He did it anyway.

*I am the god of debts,* he had said, to whom? To the dark? To himself? His voice had been vast and tired and kind, the way winter is kind to things it intends to let sleep. *And no debt is paid until it is forgiven. So I will forgive them all. Let the Court howl. Let the ledgers burn. What is order, next to mercy?*

And the Hollow Court had answered.

The memory turns, and she wants to look away and cannot, because it is not a story she is hearing anymore. It is a surgery she is feeling.

They came for him beneath Mount Ceren, where his own first shrine stood, where he had gone to write the Great Absolution in the First Tongue — the language reality was drafted in, the only script strong enough to hold a forgiveness of that size. And the Court, which exists because debt exists, which is made of debt the way fire is made of hunger, could not allow it. They could not kill him. You cannot kill the thing that keeps the scales balanced; kill Veth and every debt in creation comes due at once, and the world ends not in fire but in *collection*.

So they did worse.

They took his voice.

Not his mouth-voice. His *binding* voice — the part of him that signs, that seals, that speaks terms into the bones of things. They cut it away as collateral, one fragment of his own self, held against his good behavior, and they wrote the seizure into the First Tongue where even gods must obey grammar, and they chained what remained of him in a cellar of jars while his stolen speech sat in their vaults earning interest.

And the fragment — the piece they cut away, small enough to hide, too dangerous to destroy —

They grew it a body.

*Ilsa.*

The word arrives and she is back in her own chest, her own small ribs, gasping on stone floor, and Rafael is holding her shoulders and Maren is awake and shouting her name and neither of them can hear what she can hear, which is herself, everywhere, enormous, pressed flat into the shape of a seven-year-old girl.

"No," she says. In common tongue. Then in First Tongue: *no, put it back, put me back, I was — I was the hillside —*

"Ilsa." Rafael's face is very close, gray in the dark, eyes wide. "Talk to me. What's happening?"

She looks at him and the memory gives her one last gift, cruel as all its gifts:

She sees him from above. From the height of a god looking down at a gutter in a city whose name she suddenly knows — Corvane, the year of the salt famine — watching a child kneel beside a dying man and swear an oath in the silence after, an oath with no words because the god of words had been silenced, an oath that Veth heard anyway, because a god does not need a voice to listen.

*That one,* the great torn remnant of him had thought, looking at Rafael Nystrom kneeling in filth. *Keep that one close to my shrines. Keep that one writing. If I cannot speak, let someone keep the books honestly, so that when I wake, I will know what the world owes.*

Then the memory closes like a fist, and Ilsa is only Ilsa again, shaking, soaked in sweat, a girl who now knows she was never born.

She was *made*. Grown around the stolen piece like a pearl grown around a wound. A vessel with a heartbeat. Collateral with dimples. Forty years the Court had waited for the vessel to ripen, and Sorrel had signed for them, and somewhere along the way everyone — the brokers, the guildmaster, the Regent with its coat of borrowed faces — everyone had forgotten to ask whether the payment could feel cold.

Everyone except, apparently, the payment.

"Ilsa." Rafael's hand is warm on her cheek. His thumb wipes at wetness she didn't know was hers. "You went somewhere. Where did you go?"

She opens her mouth to lie, because children learn lying early when their lives depend on adults staying calm.

She finds she cannot do it. Not to him. Not to the man her other self chose in a gutter forty years ago.

"I remembered," she says.

Maren makes a sound like a hinge. Rafael goes very still.

"Remembered what?"

"Being him."

The word lands in the gallery and the walls take it up, inscriptions flaring faintly, and far below — floors down, in a cellar full of jars — something shifts against its chains, and the hum in the stone changes key.

* * *

They sit her by the small fire Rafael allows, though smoke rises toward vents that breathe like lungs and he hates the risk. Tonight he allows it because his hands were shaking too badly to stop her.

Maren crouches in front of her with the desperate gentleness of a scholar who has just realized the text she's been studying has been studying her back. "Slowly," Maren says. "As slowly as you can. What do you remember?"

Ilsa holds her hands out over the flames. Her fingers don't look different. That is the strangest part. Inside she is a hillside and an ocean and a severed voice, and outside she is small and grubby and missing a tooth on the bottom left, and the mismatch makes her want to laugh and scream in equal measure.

"I remember grass," she says. "Green grass. Real grass — not like stories. I remember forgiving people. Thousands of them. Millions." She frowns, trying to carry the number in a child's arithmetic. "More than stars. Every debt. He was going to erase them all, and the Court caught him halfway through the writing."

"Veth," Rafael says. It isn't a question. His voice has gravel in it.

"The god of debts." She nods. "He tried to forgive the world. That was his crime." Her lower lip trembles and she bites it, hard, because she refuses — *refuses*, with a stubbornness that is somehow both entirely her own and entirely ancient — to cry about this. "So they cut off the part of him that forgives. His voice. His yes-and-no. And they kept it. Down here. Earning interest."

"And you," Maren says softly, "are the part they cut off."

"I'm the jar," Ilsa says, and is surprised at how steady it comes out. "They just made the jar walk."

Silence. The fire pops. Somewhere far off in the galleries, contract-stone ticks as old bargains settle.

Rafael has his head in his hands. When he looks up, his eyes are wet and furious and lit from underneath by something she recognizes now, having seen it once from the inside: it is the exact expression of a god deciding to commit mercy regardless of cost. It runs in the family, apparently. Whatever family means, when you are a splinter of a silenced deity growing in a stolen body.

"Forty years ago," he says. "The sealed page. The sale recorded in Veth's own hand." His jaw works. "It wasn't a sale. It was a seizure dressed up as one. The Court made him sign for his own mutilation."

"He signs everything," Ilsa says. "Even this. Even me." She touches her own sternum. "There's a signature in here. I can feel it. Under my heart. Like a scar shaped like handwriting."

Maren breathes something in Old Corvic that translates roughly to *impossible* and then, catching herself, adds in First Tongue: *entirely possible, we simply lacked the nerve to imagine it.* Ilsa understands the old language fully now, effortlessly, the way you understand your own pulse. It is, she realizes, not a language she learned. It is the language she is *made of*. Words were the raw material. Someone built her out of grammar.

"Why didn't you tell us?" Rafael asks, and there is hurt in it, and she loves him for the hurt, because it means he forgot, for one moment, that she is a payment — he spoke to her as a person who keeps secrets.

"I didn't know until tonight," she says. "The Regent moved up the extraction. It's pulling me apart faster. I think—" she searches for the accounting word, because Rafael taught her that everything true can be said in accounting, "—I think the pulling *tore the wrapping*. And what's inside got out."

"What's inside," Maren repeats. Not a question either. A confirmation. Scholars need to say things aloud or they don't believe them.

"A god," Ilsa says. "A little one. Me."

She watches them absorb it. Maren sits back on her heels, lips moving, already composing the heresy she'll never write down. Rafael stares into the fire with the face he wears when balancing a book that will not balance, and Ilsa realizes — with the strange double vision that is hers alone, seeing as a child and as a fragment simultaneously — that he is doing arithmetic with souls. Hers. His. The covenant he's been drafting in secret for days, the forgery she has felt him hiding in his ledger like a knife in a coat.

"You're going to trade yourself," she says. "For me."

His head snaps up. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody." She shrugs. "You smell like his promises. You always did. And now I know what his promises smell like from the inside."

Rafael stands abruptly, turns away, presses both fists against the contract-wall as if the stone could hold him up where his legs won't. The inscriptions under his palms glow faintly, responding, the way they respond to her. Everything down here responds to her. She is, after all, the oldest creditor in the room.

"It doesn't matter what I was going to do," he says finally, not turning around. "It matters what happens next. The Regent accelerates the extraction tomorrow night. After that, there's no you to save. Just a soul in a jar and a body full of ink."

"I know." She picks at a thread on her sleeve. "I can feel myself unraveling. At the edges. Like a sock." She looks up. "Rafael. I remembered something else. Something important."

He turns. Maren leans in.

"When he was cut," Ilsa says slowly, feeling for the words the way you feel for stairs in the dark, "he wasn't angry. Isn't that strange? A god, chained, mutilated, and he wasn't angry. He was *sorry*. For the Court. Because he could see what they were — they're not monsters, they're *debts that learned to walk* — and he was sorry for them, and he was sorry for Sorrel, and he was sorry for the broker with the wrong face, and he was sorry for me." Her voice catches. She pushes through. "And sorry isn't nothing. Sorry is *currency*, down here. The strongest kind. He couldn't finish the Great Absolution because they stopped his hand. But the forgiveness he'd already written — the part that got into the world before they seized him — it never expired. Debts have been quietly forgiving themselves for forty years and nobody noticed. Little ones. Kindnesses. People paying forward instead of back and never knowing why the weight lifted."

Maren has gone white. "The absolution is *live*," she whispers. "Half-written and live. The covenant Rafael is drafting — it wouldn't be starting the forgiveness. It would be *finishing his sentence*."

"Yes," Ilsa says. "And I know how it ends. Because I'm the part of him that was cut off in the middle of signing it." She spreads her small hands, palms up, an accountant's gesture she stole from Rafael months ago and never returned. "The last stroke of the Great Absolution needs a sovereign signature. His voice. Which is me. Which means—"

She stops. Because saying it makes it real, and real things can be taken away, and she has only just learned she exists.

Rafael crosses the space between them and kneels, so their eyes are level, and takes her hands in his scarred ones, and waits. That is the thing about Rafael nobody understands, the thing the ledgers never record: he knows when *not* to write.

"It means," Ilsa says, "that they don't need to take me to the extraction room. They need to bring me to the master ledger. I'm not just the payment." She swallows. "I'm the pen."

The hum in the walls changes again — deeper now, resonant, and beneath it, unmistakable, comes a sound none of them has ever heard and all of them recognize: a single note of a voice, filtering up through floors of stone and centuries of silence, cracking a jar somewhere in the dark.

Rafael's grip tightens on her hands.

"He heard you," Ilsa says, smiling through the tears she finally lets fall. "He's been listening the whole time. And Rafael — he says hurry."

Far below, in the cellar of voices, the first jar splits clean down the middle, and a hundred years of stolen speech comes home.
