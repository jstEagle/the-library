## 29. Running Out of Time

The change came first as a smell.

Rafael had spent thirty years with their nose in ledgers, and every book had a scent—tallow, iron-gall ink, the faint sweetness of paper drinking damp. Contract-stone smelled different. Older. Like a cellar where something had been weeping for centuries. But when the tremor moved through the galleries at what Rafael guessed was dusk, if dusk still existed down here, that smell sharpened into something else: hot copper and burnt sugar, the exact stench of an entry being torn out of a book.

They knew it because they'd made that smell themselves. Nine days ago, in the guildhouse, forging Sorrel's mission into the ledger, each false line had burned like this.

Ilsa screamed.

Not loudly. That was the worst of it. She made a sound like a kettle taken off heat too late—a thin, exhausted note—and folded forward against Maren's shoulder as though her bones had been quietly subtracted.

Maren caught her, lowered her to the stone. "Her hands." The linguist's voice was steady in the way Rafael had learned meant the opposite. "Look at her hands."

Rafael looked. Ilsa's fingers were translucent at the tips. Not pale—translucent, the way vellum goes when held to candlelight, veins showing through like watermarking. And moving beneath the skin, faint as breath on cold glass, were lines of script. Fine, looping, patient.

Contract-ink. Writing itself into her.

"No," Rafael said, and heard how useless the word was, and said it again anyway. "No. We have until the ritual. We have—"

"We had until the ritual." Maren pulled Ilsa's sleeve back. The script had climbed past the wrist. "The Regent's not waiting anymore. It's accelerating the extraction. It doesn't need us gathered in its gallery—it can draw her out wherever she stands, slow as bleeding or fast as a cut throat. Right now it's choosing slow. A message."

"To us."

"To you." Maren looked up. In the gloom of the outer galleries, lit by the phosphor-veins running through contract-stone, her face was all hollows and eyes. "It wants you to watch. It wants you to feel every hour go by knowing you did this to her by coming here."

* * *

Rafael carried Ilsa.

She weighed almost nothing, which was itself terrifying; three days ago she'd been solid as any child, complaining about the dark, humming her terrible beautiful songs along the walls. Now she was light the way a page is light. Rafael walked the galleries with her cheek against their collarbone and felt her lips move against their throat, whispering First Tongue in her sleep, and tried not to think about the fact that each whispered word was a syllable leaving her, being called home ahead of schedule.

The walls watched them pass. That was not a figure of speech. Every surface was inscribed—bargains struck across millennia, ten thousand years of want written in a script older than kingdoms—and Rafael had learned, in the long descent, that some contracts were still *live*, and live contracts noticed things. As they passed, the lines nearest Ilsa brightened, faint gold waking in the grooves, like dogs lifting their heads at a familiar footstep.

"She's a payment walking past the till," Maren murmured. "Of course they want her."

"Can they reach her? Here?"

"Here we're in the anterooms. Outer galleries. The Court proper—the Regent's hall, the master ledger, the cellar of voices—is deeper. There are thresholds between. Old ones. Things even the Court honors." Maren adjusted her satchel, the one full of notes she should have burned and hadn't, because burning them would have meant admitting what they cost. "We're standing on a technicality, Rafael. It's the only ground we own."

A technicality. Rafael almost laughed. Thirty years an accountant, and in the end their whole war was going to be fought on technicalities. Perhaps that was fitting. Veth was the god of debts; his faithful had always fought with fine print.

They found a side gallery—a narrow vault off the main passage where the walls were so densely inscribed they'd gone black, layer over layer, contract atop contract, until the writing became texture, became simply darkness. A dead end. Private. Somewhere no live contract bothered to watch.

Rafael laid Ilsa down on their bedroll and sat with their back to the wall, and for a while none of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't arithmetic, and the arithmetic was obscene.

One night. Maybe less.

* * *

"So we do it tonight."

Sorrel stood at the mouth of the vault, half in shadow. She had arrived two days ago expecting to hand a child to the Court and settle a forty-year debt; instead she had stood in front of Rafael, wept once, briefly, like a woman lancing a wound, and turned her face against everything she'd built. She still wore her guildmaster's signet. She hadn't been able to take it off. Rafael understood. Some debts you pay by wearing them.

"It takes time," Maren said. "The covenant. Ilsa has to teach Rafael the strokes—she's been doing it, but the grammar isn't learnable in an evening, it's learnable in—" She stopped. Did the math she didn't want to do. "We needed three days. Conservatively."

"We have one night," Sorrel said.

"Then we write faster."

"And if the strokes come out wrong?" Sorrel came further in, boots silent on stone. "This is reality's draft language, Maren. You told me yourself—one miswritten clause and the covenant doesn't void the debts, it *ratifies* them. Seals them forever. Or worse, it reads the signer as the subject. Rafael signs one glyph crooked and they're not voiding anything—they're volunteering."

"I know what I told you."

"Then don't say 'faster' like it's free."

Rafael listened to them argue with half their attention. The other half was on Ilsa's hand in theirs, watching the ink climb. It had reached the second knuckle now. It wrote without hurry. That was the horror of it—contracts never hurried. They knew exactly how much time they had, and it was always more than you had.

*Ilsa,* Rafael thought. *Hold on.*

As if the thought were audible—as if, down here, thought and speech were neighbors sharing a wall—the child's eyes opened.

"You're loud," she rasped. "When you worry. You're very loud."

Rafael's chest seized. "Hey. Hey, little thorn. Save your voice."

"You save yours." She tried for her usual scowl and got maybe a third of the way there; dissolution was eating even her expressions. "The man under the mountain says you're going to do something stupid."

"The man under the mountain."

"The god. He talks in my sleep now. He says—" Her brow creased with effort. "He says the pen remembers what the hand forgets. And he says you shouldn't be afraid of signing wrong. Because you already signed the worst thing you could ever sign, years ago, and it worked out."

"What did I sign?"

"You'll know it when you write it." Her eyes drifted closed. Then opened, furious with herself for closing. "Rafael. Listen. If I start unraveling before you finish—you finish anyway. Promise me. Don't you dare stop to hold my hand. He said that too. He said you'd want to stop and hold my hand and he said that would be the one kindness that kills everyone."

"I promise," Rafael lied, gently, the way you lie to children and the dying.

She knew it was a lie. She closed her eyes anyway and let herself sleep, and the ink crawled another knuckle's length, patient as winter.

* * *

They planned in whispers among the contract-walls, because loud voices woke the live clauses.

Maren spread her notes—heretical, priceless, worth more than the guildhouse—across the floor and weighted the corners with stones. "The covenant needs four things," she said. "A text declaring the debts void. That part Ilsa can give us; she's recited fragments in her sleep every night since we entered. A substrate that outranks the Court's own records—that's the problem. You can't void the master ledger using anything the master ledger recognizes as valid material."

"So we don't use its materials."

"We use *him*." Maren tapped the wall, the black layered stone. "Veth's signature is on every page down here. His authority runs through the whole system like water through limestone. If the covenant is written on a surface he's already bound to—"

"His prison," Rafael said. "The jars."

"The cellar walls. Yes." Maren's eyes had the fever-brightness Rafael dreaded and relied on. "Which means the ritual happens *there*. In the heart of the Court. Which means we walk past everything the Regent owns to get there, carrying a child who is dissolving into the most valuable substance in existence."

Silence. Even Sorrel had nothing to add to that.

"There's more," Rafael said finally. "The signature. Sovereign weight. The covenant is worthless unless someone signs it whose soul the system recognizes as collateral of sufficient rank." They flexed their right hand. It ached constantly now, had ached since the Cellar of Voices, since a cracked jar had whispered their name. "That's me. My oath-bond. Veth himself holds my sworn soul as surety—I've known it since I was nineteen years old and knelt at his shrine. It's the only signature I have that the Court must honor."

"You don't know that it must honor it," Sorrel said. "You're gambling your—"

"Yes. Obviously. That's the entire point of a signature, Sorrel. It's a promise the other party can collect on." Rafael heard their own voice come out flat and clean, the accountant's voice, and was grateful for it. "I've been reading other people's collateral for thirty years. I know exactly what mine is worth. It's enough. It's barely enough. And it has to be given freely, fully, without reservation—which is why I'm telling you both now, in front of witnesses, in a room full of contracts: whatever happens tonight, however it looks, I am choosing this. No one forced me. Write that down somewhere, if the walls will let you."

Maren wrote it down. Of course she did.

Sorrel stared at Rafael for a long moment, and Rafael watched forty years of guildmaster discipline fight something older and lose. "You sound like him," she said at last. "Veth. When I met his priests, before—before everything—they talked like that. Flat. Clean. Like the price was just a number." Her jaw tightened. "I hated them for it. It made the selling look easy."

"The selling was easy. That's what I'm undoing."

* * *

They divided the night into watches and tasks, because desperation without a schedule is just panic wearing a coat.

Maren took the grammar. She sat cross-legged beside the sleeping child with her notes and a stub of charcoal, transcribing the First Tongue glyphs from Ilsa's murmured dreams, checking each stroke three times, building Rafael a copybook of sentences that could unmake the world. Occasionally Ilsa surfaced enough to correct her—"No. The hook goes *under*. It's a hook. It catches the debt and pulls"—and then sank again, lighter each time, the ink past her elbow now.

Sorrel took the route. She had spent decades mapping the Court through intermediaries and bribes, and she drew the passages from memory on the back of a guild requisition form, which Rafael thought was the funniest thing they'd seen in weeks and did not laugh at. Two routes. The long one, through the broker's galleries, wide and fast but watched. The short one, through the archive of forfeited names, narrow, unwatched—because even the Court avoided that place—but requiring each of them to walk a corridor lined with everything they'd ever lost, and keep walking.

"The long way," Maren voted.

"The short way," Sorrel said. "Watched corridors are corridors with guards. Guards are people who can be bought, and we have nothing left to buy with except the thing they're trying to take."

Rafael took the fourth task, the one no one assigned because no one wanted it: sitting with Ilsa, teaching the covenant's final clauses into their own hand, stroke by stroke, while the child faded. It was the cruelest kind of lesson. Every glyph she taught cost her something visible—a little color, a little weight, a little more translucence creeping up her arms—and every glyph Rafael learned went down into them like a nail, because the First Tongue did not sit on the page the way common script did. It settled. It *agreed to be there.*

Halfway through the night, Ilsa looked up from the practice strokes Rafael was cutting into a slate shard and said, suddenly lucid, entirely present:

"When this works. When he wakes up. What happens to me?"

Rafael set the stylus down very carefully.

"The fragment comes home to him," they said. "That's what the texts say. You're a piece of Veth, little thorn. When he's whole, you—"

"I know what the texts say. I'm asking you."

And there it was. The question underneath all the others, the one Rafael had been not-asking since Chapter after chapter, since the Salt Road, since the reeds, since the town that couldn't mourn. What happened to the girl when the payment came due in reverse?

"I don't know," Rafael said. It cost them everything to say it plainly, and they said it plainly. "You might stay as you are. You might become something new—you'd hardly be the first thing remade in this mountain. Or you might go home to him the way a river goes home to the sea, and there wouldn't be an *Ilsa* left to walk out of here." Their throat closed. They made it work anyway. "So I'm going to tell you the truth and then I'm going to beg. The truth is I would burn every ledger in creation to keep you, and the begging is: please hold on until the last possible moment. Give him every piece he's owed. But make him wait for the last piece. Make him wait until the covenant is signed and sealed and the Regent is dust. Can you do that? Can you be stubborn for one more day?"

Ilsa considered this with the grave, unhurried attention she gave to everything, the attention of a child who had been old before she was born.

"He waited four hundred years," she said. "I can be stubborn."

Then she taught Rafael the clause for *void upon waking*, and her voice was steady, and Rafael's was not, and neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

In the last hour before they moved—if hours meant anything down here; Rafael measured it in the ink reaching Ilsa's shoulder—Sorrel came and sat beside them at the vault's mouth, watching the phosphor-veins pulse in the far walls like a slow heartbeat.

"I've been trying to remember," she said, "when I decided you were mine. The gutter-child. Do you remember it?"

"You pulled me out of a drainage channel behind the tanneries. Winter. I'd stolen a ledger."

"A ledger. Of all things. Half-dead of cold with a stolen account book under your shirt." Something that was almost a laugh moved through her. "And you wouldn't let go of it. I pried your fingers open and you started crying—not from the cold, from the *book*. So I let you keep it. And I thought, well. There's the rarest thing in the city. Someone who loves the record more than the silver." She turned her signet ring around and around on her finger. "I've been thinking all night about whether I saved you or recruited you. Whether there's a difference. Whether I ever once did a kind thing in my life that wasn't also a purchase."

"You did," Rafael said. "Just now. Standing with us."

"That remains to be seen." She stood, and rolled her shoulders like a woman going to a negotiation she intended to win. "Short way, then. Through the names. Keep walking, whatever you see. And Rafael—" She paused at the edge of the shadow. "If it comes to holding her hand or finishing the line, you finish the line. That's not an order. Guildmaster's dead; I killed her two days ago in a corridor. It's a request. From someone who knows exactly what it costs to stop halfway."

Then the vault filled with light.

Not lamplight. Not phosphor. It came up from the floor itself, from the contract-lines in the black stone, every groove in the gallery flaring gold at once, and the whole mountain seemed to inhale. Ilsa cried out—a small, tearing sound—and Rafael was at her side in a heartbeat, gathering her up, feeling how much less of her there was to gather.

Across her collarbone, above her heart, the contract-ink had finished a sentence. Rafael couldn't read First Tongue unaided yet. But they didn't need to. They were an accountant. They knew a demand for immediate payment when they saw one.

Maren read it aloud anyway, voice shaking. "'Extraction commences at the turning of the watch. All parties bearing claims may present themselves at the Hall of the Regent to witness settlement.'" Her eyes came up. "It's summoning us. It's done hiding. It wants us in the room when it takes her."

"Then it's made a mistake," Rafael said.

They stood, and shouldered their packs, and checked the covenant pages one last time—text, substrate, signature, all present, all ready. One night had become no nights. Somewhere above them, or below them, or in every direction the mountain owned, the turning of the watch was approaching, and a child was coming apart in their arms, and the god who had waited four hundred years was waiting on them too.

Rafael lifted Ilsa. She weighed what a promise weighs.

"Keep walking," they said—to Sorrel, to Maren, to themselves. "Whatever we see in there. Whatever it shows us. Keep walking."

And the three of them stepped out of the dead-end vault into the blazing galleries, toward the Hall of the Regent, with the whole Court watching and one hour left on the clock.
