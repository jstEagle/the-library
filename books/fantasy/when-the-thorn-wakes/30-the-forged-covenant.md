## 30. The Forged Covenant

The contract-walls of the outer galleries hummed at night. Not sound exactly—Rafael felt it in their teeth, a vibration like a struck tuning fork that never quite stopped. Maren said it was the stone reading itself. Ten thousand years of bargains, checking their own arithmetic in the dark.

Rafael had not slept properly since the cellar of voices. They sat now in the hollow they'd claimed—a shallow alcove where three contract-stones met at an angle, sheltered from the patrols of face-brokers who drifted through the galleries like smoke given errands. A scrap of tallow candle. A knife. And on a flat slab of stone worn smooth by older hands than theirs: the page.

It was not paper. Nothing down here was. It was vellum made from something that had once argued about being flayed—Ilsa had told them that much, translating with the flat calm of a child reciting weather—and it drank ink the way living things drank water. The ink was worse. Rafael had ground it themselves from char and gall and a single drop of their own blood, because every honest forger knew you signed your work whether you meant to or not. Better to choose the terms.

Ilsa knelt across the slab from them, barefoot as always; her boots had been lost at the river crossing and she'd declared shoes a form of lying anyway. In the candlelight her birthmark—the buyer's mark, the Court's mark—stood out dark against her throat like a thumbprint left by someone very large and very patient.

"Again," she said. "From the first stroke."

"There are four hundred strokes," Rafael said.

"And you have done them wrong eleven times." She held up small fingers. She could not count past ten without starting over, but she counted wrongness fine. "The tongue doesn't forgive. That's the whole point of it. Common tongues forgive. You say a thing crooked and people still know what you mean." She tapped the vellum. "This isn't saying. This is *making*. If the line is crooked, the debt is crooked. If the debt is crooked—" she shrugged, a child's shrug, terrible in its simplicity— "it eats whoever wrote it."

"Cheerful."

"I'm not here to cheer you. I'm here to teach you how to forge reality before they come take me apart." She smoothed the ruined attempt aside—Rafael's eleventh failure, its lines already curling black where the vellum rejected them—and laid down a fresh sheet. "First stroke. The one that means *beginning*. Show me."

Rafael took up the stylus Maren had cut from a shard of contract-stone, and drew.

The stroke was supposed to descend like a root—straight, then branching, then straightening again into certainty. Rafael's hand knew ledgers. Rafael's hand knew the clean verticals of numbers and the confident horizontals of sums, and it wanted to make this stroke *tidy*. It came out tidy.

Isla's breath hissed through her teeth. "Tidy is a lie. Beginnings aren't tidy. Do it again and don't be ashamed of the mess."

* * *

They worked while the mountain slept, if mountains slept, if the thing coiled beneath them did. Maren kept watch at the mouth of the alcove, her burned notes reduced to ash in the pouch at her belt—she would not explain why she still carried the ash, and Rafael had learned not to ask. Some people kept their scars where they could see them.

On the third night—or what Rafael chose to call night, the galleries' light dimming to a bruised amber on no schedule anyone could name—Ilsa finally let a full clause stand.

Rafael read it back the only way they could, sounding out meanings Ilsa had hammered into them stroke by stroke: *Let all debts written beneath this stone be counted as paid when the silenced one speaks.*

"That's it," Ilsa said quietly. "That's the heart of it. Say it again."

"Let all debts written beneath this stone be counted as paid when the silenced one speaks."

"You didn't stumble."

"I've been stumbling for three nights. My feet learned."

"No." She was looking at the clause, not at them, and her expression was one Rafael had never seen on a child's face before: recognition. "It's not the words. Words don't care who says them. But the *strokes*—they're holding. They're not eating you." She looked up. "Do you understand what that means? The covenant wants to be written. It's been waiting for somebody to write it. All this time, all these walls of contracts, and underneath every one of them there's a blank space shaped exactly like forgiveness, and nobody ever filled it in."

Rafael set down the stylus. Their hands were shaking—not fear, or not only fear. An accountant knows a certain feeling: the moment the columns balance, the moment the sum that should be true *is* true, and the whole ledger snaps into alignment like a bone setting. They were feeling it now, except the ledger was the language reality had been drafted in, and the sum was a god's freedom.

"It needs more than clauses," they said. "You said it yourself. A covenant isn't a contract. A contract binds two parties. A covenant—"

"—remakes everyone who touches it," Ilsa finished. "So it can't be signed by two parties. It has to be signed by weight." She pressed her palm flat against the vellum, over the clause, and Rafael watched the ink brighten faintly under her skin like a coal remembering fire. "Sovereign weight. The signature of someone whose word outranks the walls."

"The Regent signs everything down here. Its mark is on half these stones."

"The Regent's word is borrowed. It wears surrendered faces and speaks surrendered oaths. Borrowed weight doesn't sign a covenant—it *is* collateral." Her nose wrinkled. "Maren explained it better. Something about promissory notes."

"She would." Rafael rubbed their eyes. The candle had burned low enough that its flame was more memory than light. "So we need a king. Or a god."

"We need someone whose promise, once made, cannot be taken back by anything in this mountain." Ilsa's voice went very small. "There's one person I can think of. He's in jars."

Rafael had known this was coming. They had known it since the cracked jar whispered their name in the dark, since Veth's fractured voice told them the law that ruled him: *a debtor can void a debt by paying it in full, or by dying owed.* They had spent three nights drafting clauses and letting themselves not think about the last line of the document, the way a merchant lets himself not think about the storm cloud past the horizon.

"He can't sign," Rafael said. "He's bound. His contracts hold him faster than they hold anyone. That's the whole cruelty of it—he's the most honest signer in existence and honesty is his cage."

"He can't sign *as himself*." Ilsa lifted her hand from the vellum. Where her palm had rested, the clause glowed and faded. "But he's not only himself anymore, is he? Forty years ago they cut a piece off him and grew me around it." She smiled, and it was the worst smile Rafael had ever seen, brave and eight years old and doomed. "A fragment of a god has whatever weight the god had. Just... less of it. Like a coin cut into halves. Each half is still silver."

"You'd sign it."

"I'd *start* it. My mark opens the covenant. But my weight alone won't turn the key—the Court grew me small on purpose. They measured me to fit a debt, not a throne." She traced the empty space at the foot of the draft where a signature belonged, and her finger left no glow there at all, and Rafael understood that she had tested this already, alone, in some hour when neither of them was watching. "It needs someone above me. Someone sworn to him. Someone whose whole soul is a signature already."

And there it was. The last line of the ledger, patient as arithmetic.

Rafael Nystrom, sworn soul of Veth. Oath-bound since the gutter, since Sorrel's hand and the shrine bell and the words spoken over candlelight: *my word is yours, my death is yours, my account is yours to close.* Every entry Rafael had ever written in the guild's books carried that oath woven through it like thread through cloth. That was why forgery hurt—that was why each false figure had felt like driving a nail through their own palm. The oath wasn't a belief. It was a lien. Veth held first claim on everything Rafael was.

Which meant Rafael's signature was not just a signature. It was collateral transfer. Signing the covenant wouldn't bind Rafael to Veth's cause.

It would spend Rafael the way a seal spends wax.

* * *

Maren found them staring at the blank space. She folded herself down onto the stone beside them without asking—she had stopped asking permission for anything around the second day below, as though the mountain's enormity had burned some courtesy out of her—and studied the draft in silence for a long time.

"It's good work," she said at last. "Better than good. I've read forty years of guild documents and six thousand years of wall, and I'm telling you: this clause structure is cleaner than anything the Court itself has produced since the Regent consolidated. Whoever taught you taught you true."

"A child taught me. Between asking for supper and falling asleep."

"Then the child is the finest grammarian alive, which we knew." Maren touched the edge of the vellum, delicately, the way she touched everything down here—everything was either scripture or ordnance, and often both. "The signature space is the problem."

"Yes."

"Not the finding of a signer. There's a candidate asleep in this alcove wearing your cloak." Maren's eyes flicked to Ilsa, curled against the wall, breathing slow. "The problem is that a signature given under compulsion weighs nothing, and a signature given in ignorance weighs less. The covenant will test the signer. It will ask, in whatever way covenants ask: *do you know what you are giving?* If the answer is no—if the signer hasn't looked straight down into the well—they'll fail, and the failure will eat them, and probably us, and certainly the girl."

"So I tell them. Whatever it costs."

"Do you even know what it costs?" Maren's voice was gentle, which was how Rafael knew she was about to be merciless. "You're an accountant. You think in entries. Soul, debited; covenant, credited; balance achieved. But I've read the walls, Rafael. I've read what happens to souls spent into contract-ink. They don't die. Dying is what happens to bodies. A soul paid into a covenant gets *distributed*. Spread across every line it seals. Forever." She let that sit. "Somewhere in the walls of this mountain, there might be a fraction of you for as long as stone lasts. Listening to debts get collected. Unable to look away. You of all people should understand what that would be."

Rafael was quiet for a while. The humming in the stones filled the silence, ten millennia of bargains murmuring their own terms.

"I watched a woman on the Salt Road sell her memory of her son's laugh," they said finally. "She cried the whole time and didn't know why. That's what the trade looks like from outside. From inside, from the ledger side—" their hands moved, helpless, the old accounting gesture— "it's just numbers. Clean columns. You write the entry and the entry is *true*, that's the horror of it, nothing you record is ever false, and so you never have to feel anything, because truth is a kind of anesthesia." They looked at the blank signature space. "I've been numb for twenty years, Maren. I thought it was professionalism. I think it was preparation."

"Don't dress suicide in bookkeeping."

"I'm not." And they weren't, and the knowing of it settled in their chest like a struck bell. "A man who jumps off a cliff to escape something makes an escape. This is different. This is—" they searched for the word and found it in the First Tongue, a stroke-shape rather than a sound, and translated badly— "*payment rendered willingly at value received.* I'm not running from anything. For the first time in my life I'm running *toward* the number."

Maren looked at them for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and cracked, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand.

"Gods help me, you actually believe that. You actually believe it, which means the covenant will too." She stood, knees popping, and gathered her ash-pouch and her stone-stylus. "Then finish it. Tonight. Because I've been counting patrols, accountant, and I count fewer than yesterday, and fewer the day before, and in my experience when guards thin out it's because they're concentrating somewhere. The Regent isn't losing interest in us. It's gathering its grip."

* * *

They woke Ilsa gently. She surfaced from sleep the way she always did—instantly, completely, eyes open and focused before her body finished moving, the feral child never far beneath the scholar of impossible grammar. When she saw the finished draft spread on the slab—all save the final line—she went still in a way that made Rafael's chest ache.

"It's done?" she whispered.

"All but the signature."

"Read it to me. All of it. In common, and then I'll check the strokes."

Rafael read. *Let all debts written beneath this stone be counted as paid when the silenced one speaks. Let the jars break. Let the faces remember their names. Let the walls forget what they were told. Let the first debtor rise and walk out of his own handwriting, owing nothing to anyone, forever.*

Ilsa checked every clause twice, her lips moving along the strokes. When she finished, she sat back on her heels and was silent for so long that Rafael began to fear the draft had failed some final hidden test.

"It's beautiful," she said at last, and her voice broke on the word. "It's the most beautiful thing anyone's ever written. Do you know why? Because nobody's ever written *paid* before. Four thousand years of walls and not one of them contains the word *paid*. Only *owed*, and *owing*, and *transferred*, and *forfeited*. The Court doesn't have a word for finished." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and eight years old. "You invented a word tonight, Rafael Nystrom. You put *finished* into the language reality was drafted in."

"Your god invented it. I just copied his handwriting." Rafael turned the vellum toward the empty space at the bottom. "Now the hard part. The signature needs sovereign weight, and mine will do—but only if I give it truly, knowing what it costs, wanting to give it. So I need you to tell me the rest. What happens to a soul paid into a covenant. All of it. Don't soften it. If you soften it and I sign anyway, the signing fails, and we all die in the dark for nothing. So: everything."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. She was quiet long enough that the candle guttered and steadied. Then, in the flat clear voice she used for translations, she told them.

She told them about distribution, about being spread thin across sealed lines. About the listening-that-cannot-stop. About the way a spent soul does not end but *attends*, forever, the way a signature attends every document it ever sealed. She told them that the pain of the writing was nothing—a hangnail—compared to the pain of the paying, which was not pain at all but *unraveling*, the slow honest loosening of every knot that made a self. She told them that some part of the signer persists in the covenant's first clause always, and that clause was *let all debts be counted as paid*, and so some small piece of whoever signed would spend eternity inside the word *paid*, which was, she said, either heaven or hell depending on things she didn't have the grammar for yet.

When she finished, the alcove was very quiet. Even the walls seemed to lean in.

"Well," Rafael said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. Twenty years of numbness, and here at the end, the numbness parted like a curtain, and behind it was not terror but something almost like relief. "That's the price. I've seen worse terms. I once watched a man sell his daughter's wedding day for a sack of flour, and the flour was moldy."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." They picked up the stylus. "Ilsa. One more thing, and then we do this. If it works—if he wakes—he'll be free, and you'll be free, and the fragment in you goes home to him. You understand that. You stop being a vessel. You become just—" their voice caught, and they pushed through it— "just a girl. No mark. No weight. No crown of thorns in your dreams. Just Ilsa."

She nodded. Then, fiercely: "Just Ilsa. Who learned the First Tongue before she learned common. Just Ilsa, who taught an accountant to forge. I'll still be dangerous. Don't you dare pity me."

"Wouldn't dream of it." Rafael set the stylus point against the vellum, at the head of the empty signature space, and felt the oath stir in their chest like a sleeping animal turning over. Somewhere far above, in a cellar behind a broken jar, a silent god waited with his ear pressed to the dark. Somewhere closer, boots were gathering in the galleries, concentration tightening like a noose. The candle burned. The walls hummed their ten thousand years of *owed*.

"I'm going to write it in his name," Rafael said. "*Veth, who listens.* Sovereign weight, freely given: my sworn soul, entire, in payment of the covenant. Witnessed by the fragment and the forger." They looked up once, at Ilsa, at Maren, memorizing them the way you memorize a page you'll never see again. "Any last objections, speak now. After this stroke, the document starts taking offers seriously."

Ilsa opened her mouth—

And the candle died.

Not guttered. Died, all at once, snuffed by a pressure that rolled through the gallery like a door closing somewhere enormous, and out in the dark of the corridors came a sound Rafael had hoped never to hear again: the wet, unhurried footsteps of a broker wearing a face that didn't quite fit, and behind it, many more.

"They know," Maren breathed. "They're coming *now*."

Rafael looked down at the blank signature space, at the stylus trembling in their hand, at the covenant waiting with its mouth open.

"Ilsa," they said. "Teach me the last stroke fast. We're out of nights."

Her hand closed over theirs in the dark, small and burning hot, guiding the point back to the vellum. "Follow my voice. Stroke one—"

From the corridor, the face-broker called out, sweet and wrong, in a voice like a coin dropped on marble:

"Accountant. The Regent invites you to renegotiate."
