## 34. The Regent's Last Contract

Sorrel had always believed she would die in the guildhall, in her chair by the window, with a ledger open on her lap and the smell of ink and beeswax around her like a shroud she'd chosen herself. A comfortable death. An accountant's death. She had arranged for it the way she arranged everything—carefully, in advance, with contingencies.

The caverns beneath Mount Ceren were not that death. But standing there, watching the Regent come apart at its seams, she understood with terrible clarity that this was the one she had been walking toward for forty years. Every step since the gutter, every kindness and every cruelty, all of it had been a road, and the road ended here, in a hall of contract-stone where a god's voice was flooding through the dark like water through a broken dam.

She could not stop smiling.

* * *

It had started as unraveling. That was the only word for it. The covenant Rafael signed had gone up in white fire, and the fire had not stayed contained—it had run along the walls of the gallery like flame along a fuse, and everywhere it touched, the contracts burned their terms into light and then into nothing. Debts voiding themselves. Millennia of bargains unwriting in a single breath.

The Regent stood at the center of it and screamed.

Sorrel had never heard it scream before. In forty years of correspondence—letters that arrived sealed in skin that wasn't leather, delivered by brokers whose faces didn't quite fit—the Regent's voice had always been smooth as poured wax. Reasonable. Patient. The voice of an institution, not a creature. Institutions did not scream.

But the Regent was screaming now, because the Regent was made of faces, and the faces were remembering.

She watched them surface one by one as the god's voice washed over the assembled features: a young man's jaw suddenly weeping with a young man's grief; an old woman's eye blinking open and looking, for the first time in centuries, at what it was attached to; a child's mouth opening and saying, in a small cracked voice, *Mama?*—to no one, to everyone, to the dark.

The Regent clawed at itself. Its hands—they had hands now, or still, she'd never been sure which—tore at the collar of its robe as if the robe were strangling it, but the robe was part of it too, woven from surrendered things, and every thread was waking up.

"You cannot do this," it said, and the voices came out braided together, dozens of them, a choir forced into one throat. "The contracts are the world. Without the ledger, nothing holds. Nothing *holds*."

"Maybe," said Maren from the corridor mouth, her arm pressed against her ribs where the guard's blade had caught her, "nothing is supposed to hold."

Sorrel almost laughed. The linguist. Of course it would be the linguist who found the courage to say it plainly. Sorrel had spent forty years dressing the same truth in silk so she never had to look at it naked.

* * *

The Regent found her. That was how she thought of it afterward—not that it looked at her, but that it *found* her, the way water finds a crack. Its remaining coherent eye fixed on her across the burning gallery, and the screaming stopped, and the smoothness came back for one last performance.

"Sorrel Vane."

Her name in that voice. Forty years, and it had never once used her name before. She had been *the agent*, *the hand*, *the account*. Names cost something here; she knew that better than anyone. It spent her name now like a miser's last coin, and she understood what that meant.

It was dying, and it wanted to bargain.

"You hold an anchor-debt," it said. The faces rippled beneath the surface of its own—the child-mouth mouthing *Mama* again, the old woman weeping silently, all of them visible now through the thinning wax of the Regent's composure. "The oldest debt in your line. Your signature, given freely, witnessed and sealed. While it stands, I have purchase in this place. While it stands, I can bind myself to it. Rebuild. Endure." The eye brightened, wet and hungry. "You would keep your guild. Your position. Everything you built. All you must do is refuse to pay."

There it was. The final offer, laid out as clean as a balance sheet. Refuse to pay, and live rich. Pay, and—

"And if I pay," Sorrel said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "What does paying mean?"

The Regent smiled with a mouth that belonged to a drowned sailor three hundred years dead. "You know what it means. You've known since the day you signed."

* * *

She had signed it in this mountain, in a room much smaller than this one, twenty-two years after the famine took everything. She had been young—not young the way Rafael had been young in the gutters, feral and furious, but young the way a woman is young when she has buried two children and discovered that grief has a weight and the weight has a price and someone, somewhere, is buying.

The broker had worn a kind face that day. That was the horror she'd never told anyone: it hadn't been monstrous. It had been *kind*. It had sat with her while she cried and offered her tea and explained, gently, that her children's futures—those unwritten years, those unlived days—were valuable precisely because they would never be used. Unspent time. Clean collateral. And in exchange: enough grain to carry the village through winter. Enough for everyone. Not just her.

She had thought about the other mothers. The ones with living children, hollow-eyed, boiling bark for soup. She had signed.

She told herself for decades that it was sacrifice. That she had traded her dead children's ghosts for living strangers' bodies, and any honest arithmetic came out in her favor. And when the Court came back years later and said, *the signature is useful to us, serve as our hand in the world above*, she told herself that too was service—that inside the machine she could soften it, slow it, save a few.

And then she'd pulled a half-dead mercenary out of a gutter because the mercenary reminded her of no one, of everyone, of the children she couldn't picture anymore—and she had taught Rafael the ledgers, and fed them, and loved them in the wordless way she was capable of loving anything—and every year the Court's demands got heavier and every year she told herself the same story, thinner each time, until it was gauze, until it was air.

Rafael had seen through it in the end. Of course they had. Nobody kept books like Rafael Nystrom. You couldn't hide a running deficit from an auditor like that.

* * *

"I want terms," Sorrel said.

The Regent's eye narrowed. Around it, the gallery burned. Somewhere down the corridor, she could hear Ilsa humming—that strange, sweet, ancient melody that was apparently the sound of reality being drafted—and the humming was getting louder, and the contracts were dissolving faster, and whatever the Regent meant to do, it was doing it quickly now.

"The debt is mine alone," she said. "Not my line. Not my heirs—I have none. Not my guild. The moment I pay, every obligation connected to my signature ends. All of them. Including the ones the Court holds against people I—" she stopped, adjusted, "—against members of the Gilded Thorn. Against Rafael Nystrom. Against the girl."

"The girl is not yours to bargain for."

"The girl is collateral on a contract written in a god's hand, and that god is currently unchaining himself in your cellar," Sorrel said. "Everything is mine to bargain for, if the price is high enough. And I am about to offer you the highest price there is."

Silence. Even the burning contracts seemed to lean in.

"My life," she said. "Not my soul—you don't get my soul, that's spoken for, it's been Veth's since I first wrote a ledger in his name and meant it. My *life*. The years I have left. I'm sixty-one years old, I drink too much plum brandy, and my heart has been unreliable since the famine. But it's years. Real ones. Freely given, willingly paid, no coercion, no fraud—which I believe your own contract-stone requires, doesn't it? Consent has to be genuine or the entry won't hold." She smiled at it, sharp as a paper edge. "I learned that from your brokers. They were very thorough teachers."

The Regent's faces churned. The drowned sailor's mouth opened and closed. When the voice came, it was smaller, and it was not one voice but many, and some of them sounded afraid.

"Why?"

And there it was. The question under everything. Why would she pay? What did she get?

Sorrel looked past the Regent, down the corridor, to where Rafael stood with blood on their sleeves and a dead god's covenant still smoking faintly off their skin, and where Ilsa leaned against them, small and blazing, and where Maren held her ribs and grinned like a woman who had just read the ending of the world's oldest book.

"Because I sold two futures to feed strangers," she said, "and I have spent thirty-eight years telling myself it balances. It doesn't. It never did. Debts of that shape don't balance—they compound. Every year I didn't pay, the interest was *me*. Who I was becoming. What I was willing to do." Her throat tightened. "You want to know why? Because somewhere in your cellar there are jars with a century of silence in each one, and a god listened to all of it and asked for nothing, and I am so tired of asking for something. I am so tired of the asking."

She stepped forward onto the gallery floor, between the burning walls.

"So write it. Write it now, before your stone stops holding ink. Sorrel Vane pays the anchor-debt in full: one life, freely rendered, against all obligations arising from her signature. All of them. Void. Paid. Done." She held out her hand, palm up, the way she'd taken payment ten thousand times across ten thousand counters. "And then you can go to whatever hell is left for a thing made of other people's surrender, and you can know that the last entry in your whole rotten history was a debt *cleared*."

* * *

The Regent hesitated. She saw it weigh her—saw the calculation move behind the drowning faces, the institution reaching for one more term, one more loophole, one more clause. It had survived millennia on loopholes. It would reach for one now, even now, even burning.

Then the humming swelled, and the wall nearest them went white, and the contract-stone began to soften like candlewax, and the Regent understood—institutional, ancient, doomed—that its medium was dying faster than its patience.

It took her hand.

Its grip was cold and wrong, too many palms layered in one, but she did not pull away. She felt the entry write itself—not on paper, not on stone, but into her, into the marrow-deep ledger every living thing keeps without knowing it. *Paid in full.* She felt her children's futures come loose from wherever the Court had kept them, all those unlived years released like birds from a snare, going—where? Nowhere. Everywhere. Back into the world's great unwritten stock of maybe. Free. At last, free.

"Oh," she said, because it hurt, and because it didn't, and because both at once was more than language was built for. "Oh, that's—"

The warmth started in her chest and moved outward, gentle as brandy, final as snowfall. She sank, and the gallery floor was cold stone, and someone was shouting her name—Rafael, of course it was Rafael, running toward her with their terrible stubborn face, still trying to audit the situation, still looking for the error in the entry.

"There's no mistake," she told them, when they knelt and caught her shoulders. Her voice was getting hard to push. "Check the book. It's clean. First clean entry I ever... made."

"Don't." Rafael's hands were shaking. Forty years of ledgers and they had no column for this. "Don't you dare close the account. We're going home. You owe me—"

"I do," Sorrel agreed, and smiled, and meant it as absolution: *owe me nothing, that's the gift, that was always the gift.* "Tell the children—" She stopped. There were no children. There had never been anyone to tell. Except.

Except.

She turned her head, with the last of the turning she had, and found Ilsa standing over her, the stolen soul blazing gently in her small body like a coal wrapped in silk. The child looked down at her with eyes older than kingdoms, and Sorrel thought: *hers. Some of the futures I bought back are hers. Some of those unlived years are pouring into her right now.*

"Yours," she whispered to her. "Whatever I freed. Some of it's yours. Spend it better than I did."

Ilsa knelt. Took her hand, the way you take payment. And said something in the First Tongue—three soft syllables, round and warm—and though Sorrel couldn't translate it, she didn't need to. Some entries require no literacy. She felt it land the way grace lands: undeserved, unearned, total.

"That's the one," she breathed. "That's the... yes. Put that one in the book."

The warmth reached her fingers. The last thing she saw was Rafael's face, and the last thing she thought was not of ledgers or debts or the Court or the god, but of a winter morning long ago, a gutter, a scrap of a person half-frozen and glaring up at her with murder in their eyes, and how she had looked at that scrap and thought, without knowing why: *this one will matter.*

She'd been right. Accountants love being right.

It was enough. It was finally, at the very end, exactly enough.

* * *

The gallery went quiet in stages. First the screaming stopped—had stopped, she realized later, before she ever knelt; the Regent had made no sound at the end, only folded inward like a document being burned from the edges, its borrowed faces stepping free one by one into the god's returning light, confused, blinking, *people* again, staggering off into the dark to find out who they'd been. Then the contract-stone finished softening, and the walls became just walls, ordinary rock sweating ordinary water, and the vast pressure of the Court's attention lifted off the mountain like a hand withdrawn.

Maren sat down hard against the corridor wall and laughed—high, disbelieving, edged with tears. "She bargained it to death. She *bargained* it. The most powerful entity in creation and Sorrel Vane closed it out with a handshake and a settlement clause."

"She was good," Rafael said.

They were still kneeling where Sorrel knelt. Their hands were still under her shoulders, as if holding her in place might hold the moment in place, might give them time to find the flaw in the entry, the error, the thing that could be corrected. There was no flaw. There never was, with Sorrel. Her books had always balanced.

Ilsa stood beside them, still holding the hand that was cooling, and hummed—softly now, a different tune, one Maren had never heard and would spend the rest of her life trying to transcribe. A mourning song. Or perhaps a founding one. In the First Tongue, the two were sometimes the same word.

High above them, far up the mountain's throat, something vast exhaled—a sound like a bell the size of the sky, struck once. The shrine bells across the world, every one of them silent for centuries, rang in answer.

Rafael bowed their head over the body of the woman who had pulled them from the gutter, and did not pray, because prayer was no longer necessary. He was listening. He was right there.

"We should go home," Ilsa said, in plain trade-common, her second sentence ever, her voice small and certain in the settling dust. "Someone has to write it all down."

And somewhere in the dark below, in a cellar full of shattered jars, a god picked up a pen.
