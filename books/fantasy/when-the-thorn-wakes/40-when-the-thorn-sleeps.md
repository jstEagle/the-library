## 40. When the Thorn Sleeps

The bells of the lowland shrines had been ringing for three years now, and Rafael still woke some mornings convinced it was a dream they'd invented to comfort themselves on the road.

Not today. Today the sound came through the open shutters of the schoolhouse in Thornhollow — a real town with that name now, though the guild had never owned land here — and it rang in the particular key of the shrine at Ceren's foot, which Maren had tuned herself, arguing with two bellfounders for a week about harmonics no one else could hear. Rafael lay in the narrow bed above the counting room and listened until the last note faded into the ordinary sounds of morning: cart wheels on gravel, a dog complaining, Ilsa already downstairs doing something she'd been told twice not to do.

They rose. Their knees announced themselves these days, an indignity they had decided to treat as evidence of survival rather than decline. In the mirror, the face that looked back was older by eleven years and lighter by one name. It still startled them sometimes, how little grief there was in it.

Downstairs, Ilsa had rearranged the lesson room.

She did this every few weeks, and every time Rafael pretended not to notice for at least an hour, because watching her think she was being subtle was one of the quiet pleasures of their life. She had moved the tall desk — Rafael's desk, the one Sorrel had once sat behind — to the wall, and dragged the small tables into a circle instead. A circle said: we are equals here. A circle said: no one keeps the books *over* anyone. She was nineteen now, tall as Maren and twice as stubborn, her dark hair braided back the way the salt-road pilgrims wore theirs, a habit she'd picked up somewhere and never explained. The birthmark on her wrist had faded to a pale silver crescent, like a scar from a wound that had healed so long ago even the body had forgotten it.

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"I'm assessing my student's insubordination."

"My *rearranging*." She set two inkwells on the center table, then a third, then frowned and took one away. "If we're going to teach the new practice, the room should look like the new practice. You can't teach gift-economics out of a room shaped like a debtors' prison."

"The Gilded Thorn was not a prison."

"It had cells."

"They were storage." Rafael hung their coat on the peg by the door. "For bottled grief. Which is worse, actually. Point taken."

Outside, the bells rang again — noon came early in autumn, or perhaps Rafael was simply slower than they used to be — and down the lane the first students were arriving. That was the other thing that still felt like a dream: that people *came*. Not desperate people, not yet, though the desperate found their way too and were never turned away. But farmers with surplus grain who wanted to record its giving. A widow who had inherited a house too large for her and wanted the transfer witnessed properly, in a book that would hold. Two brothers who had quarreled for a decade over their father's forge and come, red-faced and sheepish, to ask whether forgiveness could be entered in a ledger the way a debt could.

Rafael had told them the truth: that the new books recorded what was given, not what was owed, and that no one could be made to give anything. They had stood in the doorway a long time. Then the elder brother had put his hand out, and the younger had taken it, and Rafael had written the entry while both of them cried and pretended they weren't.

That entry — *one reconciliation, given freely, witnessed under the thorn* — was still the most-read page in the public book. People came from three towns over just to look at it, the way pilgrims used to visit relics. Maren said it was because hope needed physical evidence. Rafael thought it was simpler than that. People wanted to know the world kept records of kindness, that it didn't just evaporate unwitnessed the way it always seemed to when you needed proof most.

Ilsa had finished with the room. She stood in the middle of her circle, hands on hips, waiting for the verdict.

"It'll do," Rafael said.

"You hate it."

"I love it. Sit down. Ink first, philosophy after."

* * *

The morning's lesson was column-keeping, which sounded dull and was, but only the way scales are dull to a musician. Rafael had learned it in a gutter-side office forty years ago from a woman named Sorrel Vane who had pulled them out of the mud, fed them stew, and put a pen in their hand with the words *"Everything true can be counted, and everything counted can be trusted. Start with what's true."*

Sorrel had been wrong about half of it. But the half that was right was the half that mattered, and Rafael taught it now with her name spoken plainly, without flinching, because the second book in the schoolhouse shelf — the one bound in grey leather, the one no student touched without asking — was Sorrel's ledger, recovered from the ruins beneath Ceren, water-stained and honest in the way only a ruined thing can be. Her debts were all crossed out now. Not paid. Forgiven. There was a difference, and Rafael made sure every student learned it.

"Columns tell you where the weight sits," Rafael said, walking the circle while six students bent over their pages. "A number alone is dead. A number in a column has neighbors. Neighbors tell you if it's lying."

"Can numbers lie?" asked Tam, the ferryman's son, who asked this question every lesson and always would.

"Numbers can't lie. People lie *with* numbers. Your job is to make the page a place where lying gets hard." Rafael tapped the edge of his work. "Look at your grain totals. What's wrong?"

He stared. His neighbor leaned over. Somewhere in the middle rows, a whisper, a recount, the small electric silence of arithmetic catching someone out — though this time the culprit was the exercise itself, a planted error Rafael seeded in every practice book, because a student who has caught an error once never fully trusts a clean-looking column again.

Ilsa watched from the corner, arms folded, wearing the expression she wore when she was letting Rafael teach and saving her corrections for later. She was better than Rafael at the First Tongue now — obviously, inevitably; it lived in her bones, part of her the way the silver crescent was — but accounting was Rafael's tongue, and she knew it, and she had never once tried to take the pen. She had her own work. She was translating the contract-walls of the Hollow Court, or what remained of them, into plain speech, so that the bargains struck across millennia could be read at last not as scripture but as history — as the long, sad record of what frightened people will trade when no one offers them anything kinder.

Maren said the translations would take a lifetime. Ilsa had said, "Good," in a voice that made it clear she intended to have one.

The bells rang again as the lesson ended, and the students filed out into the cold-bright afternoon, and Rafael stood at the window and listened to the sound roll down the valley toward the mountain.

Ceren was only a mountain now. The Court's galleries had collapsed into ordinary stone the night the thorn woke, and in the years since, miners had begun working the outer seams again, carefully, under charter, finding nothing stranger than silver. Sometimes a crew came up pale and quiet, saying they'd heard singing in a sealed gallery, and Maren went down with a lamp and came back saying it was resonance, geology, acoustics — and then she'd stand outside Rafael's door for a while in the evening, not knocking, before walking home.

Rafael knew what the singing was. So did Maren. Some things a god says are meant for everyone, and some are meant for one person at a time, and Veth had eleven centuries of listening to make up for, and he did it patiently, one jar-crack, one gallery, one sleeping merchant at a time. He had never appeared to Rafael. He had never spoken to them directly since the caverns flooded with his waking voice. He didn't need to. He paid attention, and attention, Rafael had learned, was the whole of what a god owes anyone.

But on certain mornings — today was one — Rafael woke with the sense of having been watched over all night, kindly, the way a parent watches a child sleep, and they had stopped calling it imagination years ago.

* * *

In the afternoon they walked up the hill behind the schoolhouse, as they did every week, to the graveyard.

It was a small one. Thornhollow was a young town. Three stones bore names Rafael had carved themselves: **SORREL VANE**, who slept beneath a rowan because she'd once said, drunk, that rowans were the only honest tree. **THE CHILDREN OF THE TOWN THAT FORGOT**, whose names no living soul remembered but whose day Ilsa had written out entire in the First Tongue on a slab of slate, so that the mourning that had been stolen from that village finally had somewhere to go. And the third stone, newest, read only **HERE LIES WHAT WAS OWED**. Beneath it, unmarked, were buried the shattered jars of the Cellar of Voices, gathered and carried out by hand, because Rafael could not bear to leave even the broken vessels of a god's stolen centuries in the dark.

Ilsa met them at the gate, as she always did on Thursdays. She carried wildflowers, badly picked, stems uneven, wrapped around a river stone to hold them straight.

"You're late," she said.

"Bells ran long."

"Maren says you slow down on purpose on Thursdays so you can complain about being late."

"Maren," Rafael said, taking the flowers, "talks too much."

"Maren says that too."

They walked the rows together. At Sorrel's stone, Ilsa knelt and pressed her palm flat against the granite the way she always did — a gesture Rafael had never asked about and she had never offered to explain, though Rafael suspected it was something she'd learned in the deep places, some old courtesy of the First Tongue, a way of greeting what remains of someone. Rafael set the flowers in the iron ring and stood a while.

"I forgave her a long time ago," Rafael said, which was not entirely true and both of them knew it.

"You forgave her. You just haven't finished *believing* it yet." Ilsa looked up. Nineteen years old and she still had the feral child's directness in her eyes, undiluted by anything. "There's a difference. You told me yourself. Debts and gifts."

"When did I tell you that?"

"The night we got home. You don't remember. You were crying."

"I don't cry."

"You cried like a cracked jar." She stood, brushed off her knees, and slung her arm through Rafael's — she had done this since she was twelve and had never once asked permission, and Rafael had never once objected — and they walked back down the hill toward the lights of the town.

Below them, the schoolhouse windows glowed. Through them, faintly, came the sound of Maren's evening class — adults this time, the ones who worked days — reciting something in ragged unison. Not the First Tongue. Plain trade-gloss, the simplified script Maren had drafted for reading the old contracts: *this was given, this was taken, this remains.* A language for honesty, built out of the wreckage of a language for ownership.

"Next month," Ilsa said, as they reached the door, "I want to run the lessons."

"All of them?"

"All of them. You sit in the corner and catch errors. Tam says you give him the answers with your eyebrows."

"My eyebrows do no such thing."

"He's started writing down your eyebrow movements. He calls it 'the Nystrom Codex.'"

Rafael laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that had taken them a decade to relearn — and held the door.

"One month," they said. "And if the columns wobble—"

"They won't wobble."

"—if they wobble—"

"*Fine*, you can fix them. Go inside, old man. You're letting the heat out."

Rafael went inside.

* * *

Late that night, when Ilsa had gone up to bed and Maren's lamp had gone dark across the lane, Rafael sat alone in the counting room with a new book open before them.

It was the custom now — Rafael's own invention, and they admitted freely that it was a vanity, and kept it anyway. Every year on the anniversary of the waking, they began a fresh ledger. Not because the old ones filled up. Because a blank first page is a promise, and promises should be renewed out loud, in ink, where the world can see them.

This year's book was bound in green leather, stitched by a tanner in the valley who refused payment and had therefore become, by the new rules, the holder of a recorded gift — one binding, given freely, witnessed under the thorn — which pleased him so much he'd had the entry copied and framed above his workbench.

Rafael dipped the pen. The ink caught the lamplight, black and patient.

On the first line, in a fair hand grown steadier with age, they wrote the heading they had written eleven times before:

**Ledger of Gifts Given Freely. Year Twelve of the Waking.**

Then they paused.

Through the shutters, far off, the midnight bell at the Ceren shrine rang once — soft, unhurried, a sound like a hand laid gently on the shoulder of the valley. Rafael closed their eyes and let it pass through them, and in the ringing's wake there was the familiar presence, wordless as ever, attentive as ever: the god who listened, keeping his vigil, keeping his accounts in the only currency he had left and the only one that had ever mattered.

*I'm here,* Rafael thought, the way you think at someone across a quiet room.

The silence answered. It was not empty. It had never been empty again.

Rafael opened their eyes, touched pen to the blank page below the heading, and waited — for tomorrow's reconciliations, for the widow's house, for Tam's eyebrows and Ilsa's circle of desks, for whatever small, uncountable kindnesses the year would bring and demand be written down.

The page waited.

So did they.

Together, as all honest books begin.
