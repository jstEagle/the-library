## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bees don't know I'm lying to them.

That's the thing about keeping hives in a valley where magic comes to die: the bees are the only creatures honest enough to judge you, and they've decided not to. They go about their business with the single-minded devotion of monks who've never once questioned the monastery. I lift the lid off Hive Three and they hum their low brown note at me — not anger, not welcome. Just acknowledgment that I exist, which is more courtesy than most days deserve.

"Morning," I tell them, because ten years in exile will make you talk to insects and call it companionship.

Smoke from the tin puffer drifts across the comb, and the bees fold into themselves like a congregation sitting down. I work the frame loose. Good cap on the honey, no sign of the chalkbrood that took Hive Two last autumn. Small mercies. The valley is full of small mercies and empty of everything else.

Let me describe where I live, since no map bothers to. Hollowmere sits in a bowl of hills two days' walk past the last place anyone would bother naming, ringed by a ridge line so unremarkable that travelers cross it by accident and forget they did. That's not poetry. That's the function. The valley *forgets* itself outward — roads slide out of memory, letters go unread, promises made to visit quietly dissolve. People who leave for supplies sometimes don't come back, not because anything ate them, but because somewhere on the ridge they remembered a cousin they meant to see and turned around and never thought of us again.

The old-timers who were here when I arrived called the veil. I call it the lid.

Nothing magical survives here. Wards gutter. Enchanted blades rust overnight. A hedge-witch came through my first winter carrying a charm against bad dreams, and by morning she was weeping over a lump of dead silver and couldn't tell me why she'd been crying. Whatever you bring in, the valley eats. Which is precisely why they put me here.

I set the frame back, gentle as an apology. My hands are steady. They're always steady now; I've had ten years of practice at steadiness, the way a man who's burned his hand once practices holding things carefully forever.

*You never meant to do it.*

That's what I say some mornings, to the fog, to the bees, to nobody. It's true, which is the problem. If I'd meant it — if there'd been malice in it, ambition, even ordinary spite — then I could have made peace with being a monster. Monsters get tidy stories. But I was nineteen and frightened, and something old woke up in my blood, and the sky over Ashenford opened its throat, and when the rain finally stopped there wasn't a village anymore. There were foundations. There was mud. There were forty-one names read aloud at a funeral in a town I wasn't permitted to attend.

So: Hollowmere. Population eleven, plus me, plus however you count the bees. Exile dressed up as mercy. *We won't hang you,* said the Magistrates of the Conclave, in their grey robes with their grey voices, *we'll simply send you somewhere your kind of weather can't happen.* And I thanked them. That's the humiliating part. I got down on my knees in front of the whole conclave and thanked them for the privilege of being buried alive somewhere quiet.

I close Hive Three and stand there a moment with the smoke curling off the puffer, watching the morning burn off the eastern hills. It's beautiful here. I want to be clear about that, because I'm about to complain, and it's only fair to admit the prison has good light. The hills go gold then green then blue as the sun climbs. The stream talks to itself all day. In autumn the rowans along the lane go the color of embers, and if you didn't know what the valley did — if you didn't feel it pressing gently, patiently, on the inside of your skull like a thumb smoothing out a wrinkle — you'd call it paradise.

I know what it does. Some mornings I wake up and can't quite remember the shape of my mother's hands, and I have to lie very still and dig for them, and when I find them it's because I wrote a description down years ago and hid it under a floorboard like contraband. Left hand broader than the right. A scar on the thumb from a paring knife. She held her needles wrong and knitted anyway. Write it down, Wendell. Before the valley smooths it out too.

The valley doesn't just eat magic. It eats edges. Grief, mostly, but grief is only one flavor.

* * *

There are three mirrors in Hollowmere, and I avoid all of them on principle.

The first belongs to Marta Tull, who keeps the washing yard and is seventy percent elbows and thirty percent opinions. The second hangs in the trading post, behind the counter, angled so that customers can check whether the hat makes them look foolish (it always does). The third is mine, inherited with the cottage, and lives face-down under my bed like a body I'm hiding.

Here's why: mirrors remember differently than the rest of the valley does.

Everything else in Hollowmere goes soft. Reflections don't. When I pass Marta's wash-water or catch the trading post glass at the wrong angle, the static gathers — faint, blue-white, crawling at the edges of my reflection like frost forming on a warm window. It moves when I move, half a heartbeat late, and some mornings it takes a little longer than others to catch up, and I try very hard not to think about what that means about which of us is the copy.

Ten years ago there was lightning in that static. Ten years ago it was a door. Now it's just a scar itching, and I have learned to walk past mirrors the way you walk past a dog you once saw bite someone: politely, without running, while sweating.

"You're doing the thing," said Tam Broadhollow yesterday, catching me angling away from the post's mirror.

"I don't know what you mean."

"The thing where you look at yourself like you owe yourself money." He weighed a sack of barley meal onto the counter and pushed it toward me. "One day you're going to explain that."

"One day I'll explain a great many things, and you'll wish I hadn't."

He laughed. Tam laughs easily; he's lived his whole life here and the worst thing the valley ever took from him was a sweetheart who left for the coast and forgot him within a season. He thinks my oddness is city manners. Let him. City manners are the least incriminating explanation available for a man who flinches at his own face.

The truth is I don't hate the reflection. I hate what it proves. As long as the static gathers, the storm isn't gone. It's sleeping. It's been sleeping for ten years in whatever room of me I nailed shut, and every night I go to bed in this soft, forgetting valley and trust a locked door to keep holding.

* * *

Afternoons I mend things. It's the closest work to prayer I've found.

Today it's the schoolhouse roof, because Widow Cade cornered me at the well and deployed the sigh she keeps holstered for exactly this purpose. So there I am on the ladder with slate and nails, three stories of gossip drifting up from the yard below, the children reciting harvest sums inside in a chant that rises and falls like weather.

"—and he *never* says where he's from, not properly—"

"Marta says he paid for his cottage in coin that looked minted yesterday."

"Coin's coin."

"Coin from a man with no history is a question, is what it is."

I hammer a nail. From up here I can see the whole valley laid out: the crofts, the lane, the stream stitching through it all, the ridgeline standing patient as a wall around us. Eleven chimneys. Nine of them smoking already, though it's barely past noon and the cold hasn't committed yet. Winter comes early to Hollowmere. The valley pulls the season in around itself like a blanket, like it wants everything tucked down and drowsy and dim.

It occurs to me, not for the first time, that I could name every person down there, every bee in every hive, every stone in every wall I've mended — and not one of them could name the color of my mother's eyes. I wrote those down too. Grey-green, like the sea deciding whether to be cruel. Under the floorboard, with the hands.

Contentment, I've discovered, is mostly a matter of lowering your standards slowly enough that you don't hear them hit the ground.

I tell myself I'm content maybe forty times a day. I have it down to a reflex. Bees need tending? Contentment. Roof needs mending? Contentment. Someone asks how I sleep, and I say *well enough*, which is contentment with a straight face. It works the way a poultice works: it draws nothing out, but it covers the wound and stops you looking at it, and after enough years you almost forget there's anything underneath except a normal amount of sadness, the kind everyone carries, the kind that doesn't level villages.

Almost.

* * *

The feeling starts at dusk, and it does not stop.

I'm walking home from the hives with the empty puffer slung over my shoulder and the last light going copper on the ridge, and it arrives the way a change in pressure arrives before weather — not a sound, not a sight, just the animal certainty of *something oriented toward you*. You know how a field of sunflowers all face the same way? Imagine finding out you're the sun.

I stop on the lane. The valley holds its breath with me. Even the stream seems to lower its voice.

Nothing. Copper light, long shadows, a crow working the hedgerow with theatrical indifference. The ridgeline, blank and blue and utterly ordinary.

But the feeling stays. It stays the way a splinter stays — not painful, exactly, but *present*, a small hot insistence under the skin of the evening. And here is the detail I will spend the night turning over like a stone with something under it: the static feeling is one I know. Ten years ago, in the hour before Ashenford died, the air tasted exactly like this. Charged. Attentive. Like the world had turned its head.

Magic doesn't survive in Hollowmere. That's the entire architecture of my sentence. Nothing crosses the veil willingly; nothing magical persists; the valley smooths and dims and forgets. So whatever is pressing its attention against the inside of my evening, it is either not magic —

— or it is standing outside the veil, looking in.

I make myself walk home at a normal pace. This is a discipline I have practiced: fear at parade tempo. I feed the stove. I eat bread and hard cheese and the pickled onions Tam trades me for hive work. I wind the clock. I do all the small domestic liturgies that add up, collectively, to *an ordinary man lives here*, and I perform them with the focus of an actor who knows the audience is hostile.

Then, before bed, I kneel and drag the mirror out from under the bed.

I shouldn't. I want to be honest about that: every rational instinct I own votes no. But the feeling hasn't faded, and there's a voice in me — the same reckless, stupid, nineteen-year-old voice that called the storm without meaning to — that says *check. Know your enemy. Look.*

I turn it over and prop it against the wall and hold the candle up.

My face. Thinner than it was at nineteen, grey coming in early at the temples, the lines around my eyes carved deep by squinting at bees and pretending at peace. Ordinary. Deliberately, expensively ordinary.

And at the edges of the reflection, the static gathers.

Except tonight it isn't faint. Tonight it isn't the lazy frost-crawl I've spent a decade learning to ignore. Tonight the blue-white shimmer runs along the mirror's rim like a fuse burning, quick and eager, and my reflection's eyes — half a heartbeat late, as always — come up to meet mine.

And my reflection smiles.

I don't. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept in the lives of men who talk to bees: I did not smile. I sat on the floor of my cottage holding a candle with a shaking hand while the thing wearing my face smiled at me out of the silvered glass, slow and wide and knowing, the way you'd smile at someone you'd been waiting a very long time to see.

The candle gutters. The flame bends sideways though every window is shut.

Then the static settles, the smile drains out of my face like water out of a basin, and it's just me again — exhausted, kneeling on cold flagstones, alone in a valley built specifically to ensure that nothing like this could ever, ever happen.

I put the mirror face-down. I nail the floorboard back over the description of my mother's hands. I lie in the dark and listen to the clock tick and tell myself, forty times, fifty, the reflex firing like a heart that won't quit:

*I am content. I am safe. Nothing crosses the veil.*

Out past the window, far off at the treeline where the copper light had gone, something in the dark stood very still — and stayed.
