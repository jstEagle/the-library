## 2. A Stranger at the Treeline

The morning the man died in my yard, I had been arguing with a bee.

Not arguing, exactly. Negotiating. Her name was Agatha—don't ask how I know, you spend ten years talking to insects in a valley that forgot how to listen and you start assigning personalities—and she had decided that the fourth hive box belonged to her alone, on principles known only to queens. I had my glove halfway off my hand and was explaining, in the reasonable tone one uses with small tyrants, that we could all prosper under an arrangement of mutual respect, when the air changed.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I have left. The air *changed*. Not the wind—the valley doesn't get wind so much as it gets suggestions of wind, polite drafts that apologize on their way through. No, this was something older and wronger, like the moment before a sneeze stretched out into eternity, or the silence between lightning and thunder when the lightning is still deciding.

Agatha felt it too. The whole hive went quiet at once—not the drowsy quiet of evening but the held-breath quiet of prey. Ten thousand wings stilled. I stood in the yard with my bare hand half-gloved and felt every hair on my arms rise like they'd been paid to.

Then he came through the treeline.

I need you to understand what that means, because it took me a moment to understand it myself, standing there with honey on my sleeve. Nothing crosses into Hollowmere. That's not a saying; it's a law of nature, the same way rivers run downhill and debts come due. The veil around this valley isn't a wall—it's a forgetting. Travelers reach the ridge, look down at our little bowl of green, and find their eyes sliding off it like rain off wax. They remember roads elsewhere. They decide, with great confidence, that there's nothing down there worth the climb. I've watched merchants do it from my window, turning away mid-thought, their mules placidly agreeing. The valley keeps itself. It kept *me*, which was rather the point.

So when a man staggered out of the pines—out of the pines, past the ridge, down the slope where no foot had printed snow or mud in ten years—my first thought was not wonder. My first thought was that the world outside had finally remembered us, and that remembering is never gentle.

He was tall, or had been. Grief and distance have a way of shrinking people in memory, but even doubled over as he was, I could see he'd been built long, like a door someone had leaned against too hard. His coat told a story in three acts: once fine, then road-worn, now ruined. Grey livery gone greyer, silver thread at the cuffs tarnished to dull lead. And blood—gods, the blood. It soaked his side in a wide dark bloom, dripped from his fingertips, painted a stuttering trail behind him across the grass like a sentence being written by a dying hand.

"Hello," I said, because I am an idiot. Because what do you say? *Welcome to Hollowmere, population me and some bees*?

His head came up. He had a face like a ledger—all sharp lines and columns, a nose broken and reset slightly crooked, eyes the grey of old coins. He looked at me the way a drowning man looks at a rope: with hope, yes, but also with the terrible arithmetic of someone calculating whether the rope will hold.

"You're real," he said. His voice was rough, scraped thin. "The valley's real."

"That depends who's asking," I said, and immediately hated myself for it, because here was a man bleeding out in front of me and I was doing *door policy*. Ten years of solitude does that. You get so practiced at keeping the world out that you forget how to let anything in, even when it's leaking.

He took another step and his leg went out from under him. I caught him—or caught enough of him—and together we performed the ancient undignified dance of the dying: two steps, a sag, two more. He was heavier than he looked and lighter than he should have been, as if parts of him had already been subtracted.

"The veil," he wheezed against my shoulder. "It let me through. It *opened*."

"It doesn't open."

"It did." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing came up red-flecked. "For me. Or—" and here his coin-grey eyes fixed on me with an intensity I didn't yet have the context to dread, "—for what I carry. Maybe both. Maybe the same thing."

We made it to the bench beside my door, the one I'd carved myself in year three when the loneliness got loud enough to require projects. I sat him down and he slumped there like a marionette whose puppeteer had stepped out for a drink.

"I'll fetch water," I said. "There's a poultice—"

"Don't." His hand shot out and closed around my wrist, and his grip was astonishing, iron wrapped in ice. "Don't waste it. Listen. We don't have time for you to be kind. Kindness takes time."

That, I have since learned, may be the truest thing anyone has ever said to me.

"My name is Corvin," he said. "Corvin Ashe. I'm a courier."

"Of what?"

"Of things that shouldn't be carried." He smiled, and it was a horrible smile, gallows-humored and self-aware, the smile of a man who had already read his own ending and found the prose wanting. "And I've been carrying one too long."

*

Here is what I knew about him in those last minutes, catalogued the way I catalogue everything now, because observation is safer than participation:

His hands were a courier's hands—scarred knuckles, ink stains in the creases, calluses from years of riding and writing. But there were other marks beneath them. Old ones. Round scars, neat as punched holes, dotting the inside of each forearm. I've seen branding like that exactly once before, on the wrist of a trader who passed through Gravenholt and wouldn't show his palms to anyone. Debt-marks, he called them, when he thought I wasn't listening. Marks of ownership.

His boots had walked a very long way very recently. The mud on them came in layers: red clay, then ash-grey, then black loam that smelled of deep places, of underground, of soil that has never met the sun. I would think about that mud later. I would think about it a great deal.

And his coat—his ruined, beautiful grey coat—had livery stitching at the collar. A sigil worked in tarnished silver thread: a mountain inverted, its peak driven downward, crowned with a circle of thorns.

I didn't know the sigil. I want that recorded clearly, for whatever court eventually judges my choices: I did not know what it meant. I only knew that looking at it made the back of my teeth ache, the way they ache before a storm breaks, and I hadn't felt that ache in ten years. In the magic-dead valley, storms are theoretical. Nothing aches here. That's the whole selling point.

"Water," I said, because he was grey around the lips now, and because fetching water gave my hands something to do besides shake. "You can refuse the poultice, but you'll take the water."

"Take the water," he agreed gravely, "and give you the map. Fair trade. Everything's a trade, Wendell Dravenmoor."

The world went very narrow and very loud.

I hadn't heard that name spoken aloud in ten years. I'd shed it like a snake sheds skin, left it coiled somewhere back on the ridge, and become simply *Wendell*—the beekeeper, the quiet one, the exile who asked no questions and drew no attention. My heart slammed against my ribs hard enough that Agatha's hive, thirty feet away, hummed a warning note in sympathy. Bees feel these things. Bees always know.

"How," I said, "do you know that name."

"Because I looked for it." He drank when I pressed the cup to his mouth, mechanically, like a man fulfilling a contract clause. "Three years, I looked. The Undercourt has files on everyone worth owing, and yours was thick. Thick files mean old debts. Old debts mean—" he gestured vaguely at everything, at himself, at the bleeding, at the general catastrophe, "—this."

"The Undercourt."

"Later." His voice was fraying now, words coming apart at the edges like wet rope. "First, the map. Take it. Don't burn it—you'll try, it won't work. Don't bury it—it comes back. Don't give it away, because whoever holds it next inherits the whole arrangement, and you seem like a man who'd hate to do that to a stranger." Another of those awful smiles. "Which leaves holding it yourself. Sorry about that. Truly. If it helps, I picked you because you seemed like the type who survives."

"It doesn't help."

"No," he said. "It wouldn't."

From inside his coat he brought out a folded square of parchment, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with handling. Even folded, even wrapped, I felt it. There's no honest way to describe the sensation except to say the map was *aware*. Not alive, exactly—alive things have warmth, breath, the modest dignity of decay waiting for them. This was aware the way a trap is aware, the way an open eye in the dark is aware. The hair on my arms stood up again, and this time they didn't lie back down.

"Blood-ink," Corvin said. "Signed in mine, witnessed in worse. It's a contract and a compass and a leash, depending on which end you're standing at. Right now you're standing at the worst end, and I am so sorry, but the alternative was letting them win, and I've spent three years learning exactly what letting them win looks like."

"Corvin—"

"Listen." The iron grip found my wrist again. His skin had gone cold, cold as well-water, and still that grip held. "They sold my soul. That's not poetry. There's a ledger, under Mount Vhalen, with my name written in it in my own hand, dated and sealed, and on the winter solstice they collect what's written. What's collected doesn't come back. It goes into the walls. Into the lights." His eyes flicked upward, toward the pale empty sky above the valley, and I saw genuine horror there, the specific horror of a man who has seen the afterlife and found it architectural. "I got out. I ran. Nobody runs. But I got out long enough to choose where to die, and I chose here, because here is the one place the collector's writ runs thin, and because—"

He stopped. Coughed. The sound was wet and final.

"Because why?"

"Because you're a Dravenmoor," he whispered, "and I thought maybe the storm still remembers you."

I should have dropped the map. I should have thrown it into the fire, buried it under the beehives, hurled it over the ridge into the forgetting. Any sane person would have. But he pressed it into my hands—folded it closed over my fingers with both of his, like a priest administering something—and the parchment was warm, blood-warm, *his*-warm, and I am ashamed to say I held on. After ten years of holding nothing, I held on.

"The solstice," he said. His voice had dropped to almost nothing, a secret shared with the ground. "Find it before the solstice. Before they seal the ledger. Whatever else you do—whatever it costs—" His coin-grey eyes found mine, and for one clear moment the pain in them receded, and what lay underneath was not fear. It was apology. Enormous, unforgivable apology. "Don't let them keep singing."

"What?" I leaned close. "Singing what? Corvin—"

But the light had already gone out of him the way a lamp goes out—not gradually, but with a small inward collapse, the flame suddenly nowhere. His grip loosened. His head tipped back against the bench I'd carved in my loneliest year, and Corvin Ashe, courier of things that shouldn't be carried, died in my yard on an ordinary grey morning with honey still on my sleeve.

The bees stayed silent for a long time afterward. Even Agatha.

* * *

I buried him at dusk, under the rowan at the edge of the property, because rowans keep things in and keep things out and I didn't know yet which direction I needed. Digging took hours. The valley's soil is stubborn—it doesn't much care for being disturbed; it prefers to forget disturbances quickly—and by the time I finished, my hands were blistered and my back was a single complaint.

I said words over him. I don't remember which words. Something about rest, and debt, and the road. It felt insufficient. Men who die apologizing deserve better liturgy than a tired beekeeper improvising.

The strange part—the part I keep circling back to—is how ordinary the evening remained. The light did the usual slow amber slide down the western rim. The hives resumed their murmuring. Somewhere a thrush rehearsed its four-note argument. The valley absorbed a violent death the way it absorbs everything: completely, indifferently, smoothing it over like water closing over a stone. By tomorrow, the grass would grow back over the grave-site. By spring, no one would ever know.

That was supposed to be comforting. For ten years it *was* comforting—the valley's merciful amnesia, the way it erased tracks and griefs and the particular shame of being exiled from your own life. Sitting on my doorstep that night, I realized with a lurch that the comfort had curdled. I didn't want to be forgotten anymore. I wanted the opposite. I wanted someone to know a man named Corvin had died here, that it mattered, that the ledger they'd write him into wasn't the only record of his existence.

I was, apparently, willing to be responsible for that. Which frightened me more than the map did.

The map sat on my table where I'd left it, folded in its oilcloth shroud. I had put it down three times since sundown. Three times I'd walked away, made tea, checked the hives' night-weights, stared at the ceiling. Three times I'd returned to find it exactly where I'd left it—which sounds right until you notice that the third time, the fold faced a different direction than I'd left it. As if it had turned over in its sleep.

I didn't open it. Some instinct older than sense advised against opening it in the dark. Instead I sat across the table from it like a duelist awaiting dawn, drinking tea gone bitter, listening to the valley be beautifully, suffocatingly silent.

Around midnight, I noticed the static.

My reflection in the darkened window had always been slightly wrong—faint shimmer, like heat-haze, like the ghost of the storm still clinging to me after all these years. Tonight the shimmer was stronger. In the glass, my outline crackled at the edges, faint blue-white threads crawling along my shoulders like frost forming in reverse. Like a garment being fitted.

Like a crown being measured.

"Stop that," I told my reflection, the way I'd told it a thousand times over ten years. "You don't exist. Neither do I."

The reflection, for the first time ever, didn't obey. It looked at me—really looked, with my own eyes but none of my exhaustion—and its mouth moved. No sound came through the glass. But I read the shape of it easily enough. Two syllables. Familiar ones.

*The solstice.*

Outside, at the treeline, something stood very still among the pines.

I tell myself I imagined the details—a shape in grey, patient as winter, positioned precisely where the veil begins. I tell myself the valley's law held, that it couldn't cross, that the forgetting would do its work by morning. But I also noticed that the bees had gone silent again. All of them. Every hive, every field, the whole valley holding its breath.

And the map on the table unfolded itself, just one corner, just enough to show me the first line drawn in dried brown ink:

*A debt is a door. Doors open from either side.*

Tomorrow, I would go to the trading post. Tomorrow I would find someone who knew what blood-ink meant, someone who dealt in impossible routes and charged accordingly. Tomorrow I would begin paying for a stranger's dying wish with a currency I didn't know I possessed.

But tonight, I sat in the dark with a dead man's contract, while far beneath the world, I somehow knew, a pen was being dipped.
