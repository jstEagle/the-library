## 3. Ink That Bites

I buried the courier at dawn, which felt like the responsible thing to do, and then I spent the rest of the morning trying to destroy his map, which felt like the sane thing to do.

The burial went fine. The valley soil is soft and indifferent, the way everything here is, and I dug the grave with the same spade I use for turning the compost. There's something bracing about giving a man a hole in the ground with a compost spade. It says: you are part of the world now, friend, right down at its least glamorous level. I marked the spot with a flat stone because I had no name to carve into anything better. He'd never told me one. He'd spent his last breaths on the word *solstice*, and you can't put *solstice* on a headstone without it sounding like a threat.

The map was the problem.

Here's what I knew by mid-morning: it would not burn. I want to be precise about this, because precision matters when you're losing your grip. I didn't fail to burn it. I burned it *thoroughly*. I held one corner over my cookfire until the flame had wrapped the whole folded thing and sat there chewing on it for a full minute — long enough to reduce a letter, a love note, a confession of crimes. The fire turned orange, then green, then a color I don't have a word for, and then it guttered out entirely, and when I poked through the ashes with a stick, the map lay there at the bottom like an egg at the bottom of a nest. Unburnt. Unwarm. Slightly smug, if parchment can be smug, and I've come to believe this particular parchment can.

So then I tried tearing it.

You'd think that would be simple. Paper tears. That's practically its job description. I gripped two corners and pulled, and the map stretched — actually *stretched*, like wet leather, like something breathing — and then snapped back with enough force to sting my palms. I tried scissors. The blades skated off it as though it were oiled glass. I tried my wood-axe on the chopping block, which in retrospect was the moment I stopped being a man destroying a document and started being a man dueling one. The axe bounced. The map didn't have so much as a crease where the edge had landed.

I stood in my yard, axe in hand, sweating in the cold, and addressed the map directly.

"Fine," I said. "You win. But understand that I'm not happy about it."

The map did not respond, which I counted as a mercy.

Now, before you judge me — before you sit comfortably in your chair and think, *well obviously he should have just left it alone* — let me remind you of the situation. A dying stranger crossed a veil that nothing crosses willingly, bled out in my kitchen, pressed this thing into my hands, and died whispering about the solstice as though it were a debt collector's appointment. Which, I would learn later, is exactly what it was. At the time, all I knew was: magic doesn't happen here. That's the entire point of this valley. It's the whole reason I was sent to live in it. Ten years of nothing — no sparks, no whispers, no storms answering my worst moods — and now, suddenly, a piece of paper in my possession that treats fire as a mild inconvenience.

Either the world outside had changed, or I had brought something across the line that shouldn't have been able to cross it.

Neither option made me feel warm inside.

* * *

By afternoon I'd moved from destruction to containment, which is what people do when they've accepted defeat but haven't admitted it yet. I wrapped the map in oilcloth. I tied it with three knots. I put it in an iron box — an old ration tin, the closest thing to iron I owned — and carried the tin to the far corner of my cellar, set it behind the pickle crocks, and stacked two sacks of barley on top of it.

Then I went up to check on the bees, because bees are honest work and honest work keeps a man from thinking.

It didn't work.

I stood there in the cold with my bare hands near the hives — the bees sleep deep in winter and don't mind me hovering; we have an arrangement, the bees and I, built on mutual indifference — and all I could think about was the tin behind the barley. Not *what was in it*. I knew what was in it. A map. Ink. Blood, probably, given how the courier had looked when he arrived. What I couldn't stop thinking about was the fact that I could feel it.

Not hear it. Not see it. *Feel* it. The way you can feel someone standing behind you in an empty room. The way you can feel a gaze settle between your shoulder blades. The tin was thirty feet away, underground, behind pickles, and I could feel it the way a tooth feels a cavity: a low, patient attention, waiting for me to bite down.

"Ten years," I told the hives. "Ten years of being nobody, and it takes one bleeding stranger four minutes to ruin it."

The hives, being bees, offered no counsel.

I lasted until sundown. I want that on the record. I cooked supper — barley porridge, because irony — I ate half of it, I washed the bowl, I banked the fire, I lay down in bed and closed my eyes and performed the entire ritual of a man going to sleep. And then I got up, took a lamp down to the cellar, moved the barley, opened the tin, and unwrapped the oilcloth, because the not-knowing was worse than any knowing could possibly be.

Here's the thing about the map, now that I finally allowed myself to look at it properly: it was beautiful, in the way that wounds are sometimes beautiful. The ink wasn't black. Up close, in lamplight, it was the color of old blood gone dark, a rust-brown that seemed to shift when I wasn't looking directly at it. It showed mountains I didn't recognize and rivers that ran in directions rivers shouldn't run, and at the center of it, rendered in careful, obsessive detail, was a peak — Mount Vhalen, though no name was written beneath it, just a symbol like a crown drawn upside-down, like a cup, like something meant to be filled.

And in the margins — this is the part I'll swear to until I die — in the margins, the ink was still moving. Tiny script, too small to read, crawling along the borders of the map like ants along a fence rail. Writing itself, or rewriting itself, or simply unable to hold still.

"You're a long way from home," I said to it.

My hand, entirely without my permission, reached toward it.

I caught myself. Actually caught my own wrist with my other hand, like restraining a stranger. My fingers had been about to touch the margin-script, and some animal part of me — the part that knows to pull back from a snake, from a cliff edge, from a door that's been left open in a house that should be locked — that part had screamed loud enough to override the rest.

That was the second warning I failed to heed properly, if we're counting. The first was letting the courier die holding my hand instead of asking him questions while he still had breath. I'm telling you this so you understand I'm not a fool by nature. I'm a fool by increments. Most of us are.

I rewrapped the map. I retied the knots. I put it back in the tin, behind the barley, and I climbed the cellar stairs, and I stood in my kitchen in the dark feeling profoundly stupid, because I had just realized something that should have occurred to me hours ago:

I hadn't been able to put it down all day either.

Not physically — I mean I'd *tried*, twice, to leave it places. Once on the table while I fetched water. Once on the porch rail while I saw to the hives. Both times, when I came back, it was closer to me than I'd left it. Inches, only inches, and I'd told myself I'd misremembered where I'd set it, because that's what a man tells himself in a valley where nothing magical has happened for ten years and his sanity depends on keeping it that way.

But the tin. The tin had been shut. The oilcloth had been tied.

I went back down to the cellar.

The tin was open. The oilcloth lay beside it, neatly folded, knots undone, arranged with a tidiness that no draft could account for. And the map —

The map was gone.

For three full seconds I experienced the purest relief of my adult life. Gone. Gone means gone. Gone means I can go back to bed and tomorrow I'll tell myself I dreamed the whole —

It was in my pack. The travel pack that hangs on the peg by my front door, the pack I haven't used since the day I arrived, because exiles don't take holidays. I found it there in the morning-grey of the kitchen, sitting in the bottom of the pack, folded small and neat as a love letter, tucked beneath the flap like it lived there. Like it had always lived there. Like it was *home*.

"I don't want you," I said out loud, to a piece of paper, alone in my kitchen at midnight. If you're keeping score of my dignity, mark that moment down.

The map, being paper, said nothing. But the static feeling — that toothache attention, that held breath — settled deeper, and I understood, in the bone-deep way you understand things at midnight, that wanting had nothing to do with it. You don't get to want or not want a leash. You just wear it.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the dead fireplace with the pack on my knees and the map inside it and I watched the window go from black to grey to the pale washed-out blue of a valley winter, and when the light was strong enough to see clearly, I made a decision that every sensible instinct I own voted against.

I would carry it to the veil and throw it out.

Not burn it — I'd learned my lesson there. Not tear it. Just give it back. Toss it over the boundary, past the stones, into the world where things like blood-inked maps belonged, and let whatever wanted it come collect it from the wilderness instead of from my kitchen. Clean hands. Simple solution. The kind of solution a man devises after a sleepless night, which is to say: a bad one.

The veil isn't dramatic, if you've never seen it. From inside, the valley just — ends. A ring of old standing stones, moss-eaten, leaning like tired shepherds, and beyond them a shimmer you mostly can't see unless the light hits wrong. I walked the hour to the nearest stone with the pack heavy on my shoulders and my breath smoking, and the whole way the feeling in my chest grew louder, that patient attention tightening like a hand closing around something soft.

At the stones I stopped. Stood there. Felt the line of it pass through me — the boundary of the only place in the world I'm permitted to exist — and raised the map above my head to throw it.

I couldn't let go.

I don't mean I hesitated. I mean my fingers would not open. I stood under the grey sky gripping a folded piece of parchment with both hands, prying at my own fingers like a child trying to unclench a fist around a forbidden sweet, and the map pulsed once against my palms — *pulsed*, warm as a heartbeat — and somewhere in my skull a voice that was not quite a voice said, very calmly, in the tone of a clerk correcting a clerical error:

*No.*

I dropped the map. It landed face-up in the frost. I stumbled backward three steps, shaking my hands like I'd touched a stove, and stood there gasping in the cold while the map lay in the grass being innocently rectangular.

That's when I saw him.

At first I thought it was one of the standing stones — a figure among figures, tall and narrow, at the far side of the veil. Then it moved, the way smoke moves, which is to say continuously and without visible effort, and I understood it had never been a stone at all.

It stood exactly at the boundary. Not past it. Not near it. *On* it, the way water sits on glass — and I watched it press forward, once, testing, and the air along the veil lit faintly silver where it pushed, and the figure recoiled with a smoothness that was somehow more frightening than violence would have been. Because it didn't rage. It didn't claw. It simply stopped pressing, straightened, and waited.

It wore grey. Livery grey — the grey of uniforms, of official business, of documents served and doors knocked upon. I couldn't see a face. I'm not sure there was a place for one. Where the head should have been there was a smooth pale suggestion, like an egg seen underwater, and tilted slightly, the way a person tilts their head when reading a difficult passage.

Reading me. I understood that immediately and completely. It couldn't enter — the valley wouldn't have it, the same way the valley wouldn't have the storm I'd once called, the same way the valley won't have anything — but it could look. And it looked at me the way I imagine a creditor looks at a debtor through a shop window: noting the goods, assessing the terms, unhurried, because unhurried is what you get to be when the debt isn't going anywhere.

The map pulsed again against the frost, and the figure's head tilted a fraction further.

"Oh no," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable. "No. No, no. He gave it to *me*. I didn't ask for it. I didn't sign anything."

The figure did not answer. Of course it didn't. But I felt — and I will defend this perception to my grave — I felt its attention shift from the map to me with the weight of a ledger opening, a fresh page turning, a pen being dipped.

I backed away. Slowly, the way you back away from dogs, though nothing in its bearing suggested speed. When I was twenty paces off I turned and walked — did not run, I want credit for not running — back up the valley track toward my house, my bees, my pickle crocks, my small gray life. Behind me I could feel it the whole way, that pressure between the shoulder blades, patient as winter.

At my door I stopped. Looked back. From this distance the figure was just a smudge at the treeline, motionless, exactly where the veil ran. It would be there tonight. It would be there tomorrow. It could not come in, and I could not throw the map out, and somewhere south of us a mountain held a court that kept books, and solstice was coming whether I liked it or not.

I looked down at my pack, where the map had crawled back to sit against my spine like a passenger.

"Well," I said to it. "I hope you're proud of yourself."

There's only one person in this valley who might know what that thing was — who deals in roads and boundaries and the prices of crossing them. She sells maps herself, at the trading post, and she looks at everyone like she's already priced them.

God help me, I was going to have to talk to Isolde.
